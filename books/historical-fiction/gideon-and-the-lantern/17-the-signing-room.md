## 17. The Signing Room

The courier door of the Ministry of Peace was a servant's entrance once, before the war made servants of everyone. It stood in the alley shadow behind the marble, its brass handle worn to the shine of an old coin, and above it — freshly painted, the paint still tacky in the cold — a sign: *AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. ARMISTICE DAY. NO ADMITTANCE WITHOUT CREDENTIALS.*

Xanthe had entered through this door forty-one times. They knew the number because their handler had made them count, early on, back when counting deliveries was a game and not a ledger of complicity. Forty-one times carrying paper that moved men like weather moves wheat. This would be the forty-second, and it would carry nothing but memory.

They stopped at the mouth of the alley and became someone else.

It took perhaps ninety seconds. They had rehearsed it against Gideon's mirror that dawn while he held the lantern up and watched with the terrible attention he gave everything — census taker's attention, the kind that saw people as lists and then, somehow, as people anyway.

"Slower," he'd said. "You're walking like you're late. Couriers are never late; couriers are *scheduled*. Lateness is guilt."

So now: shoulders rounded not with fear but with fatigue, the honest fatigue of a functionary who had been awake since three. Coat buttoned to the collar — the coat itself was the credential, Ministry grey with the dispatch loop at the shoulder, taken from the body of a courier who no longer needed it. Satchel riding the left hip. Papers visible in the outer flap, real papers, requisitions Gideon had forged overnight from his own census stock, stamped with a ministry seal he had been quietly counterfeiting for two years because, he said, accuracy was a habit you kept even when lying.

And the walk. Widow's walk. Scheduled, not guilty.

A sentry stood at the door now, which was new, which was the whole war in one sentence. He was young enough that his helmet sat on him like a borrowed pot.

"Courier," Xanthe said, and let the boredom show. "Dispatch section. I've got signatures wanted upstairs and if they're not there by ten it won't be my head, so." They lifted the satchel flap so the requisitions showed, and did not lift it further, and did not meet the sentry's eyes, because important documents were boring and boring things were safe.

The sentry glanced at the papers, at the grey coat, at nothing else. "East wing's sealed. Signing hall. Nobody in without a blue pass."

"Then I'll die in the corridor and someone else can carry them," Xanthe said, already moving through the door, and the sentry, trained by two years of bureaucracy to fear nothing so much as being handed another man's problem, stepped aside.

Inside, the Ministry smelled as it always had — floor wax and ink and, beneath both now, the cold stone breath of a building whose boilers had gone unlit since January. But the corridors were wrong. Empty corridors were wrong; the Ministry ran on foot traffic the way a heart runs on blood. Today the east wing had been cleared, and cleared wings echoed, and every echo sounded like footsteps behind.

He was still behind. Xanthe had seen him twice since the square — once reflected in a shop window on Cordwainer Street, black coat unhurried among running porters; once crossing the far end of Registry Row while Xanthe waited out a checkpoint in a doorway, heart slamming, face arranged into the blank patience of a woman waiting for a tram that would never come. The handler's man. Not the handler himself; the handler would be upstairs polishing the surrender for his own signature. This one was quieter. This one was worse.

*You have eleven minutes,* Gideon had said, tapping the face of a stolen watch. *The signatories convene at ten. The clause must be spoken before the pen touches paper or it dies in ink.*

Nine minutes, then, by the clock above the stairwell. Xanthe climbed.

* * *

The signing hall had been the Treaty Room once, back when Vessany signed things it meant. It was a long room, white and gold, hung with portraits of chancellors dead so long their names had worn off all but the plaques, and down its center ran a table so polished it looked like dark water frozen mid-ripple. Along one wall, tall windows gave onto the square, where the crowd had gathered without anyone organizing them — thousands of grey coats and headscarves standing in the falling snow, quiet the way ruins are quiet, watching the building that was about to sign away the country they were standing in.

At the table sat six men and one woman, and before each of them lay a folder bound in ribbon the color of dried blood.

Xanthe knew four of the faces from dispatches. The woman was Chancellor's office; her signature appeared on orders Xanthe had carried to scuttle the fleet. At the table's head sat the handler — Chancellor's Undersecretary Veyra Marchand now, the promotion three weeks old, the title sitting on him like armor over a wound. He was speaking low to the man on his right, and his hands were perfectly steady, and Xanthe thought: *you burned it. You fed my clause into the stove in front of me and your hands didn't move.*

And at the table's foot, apart from the others the way a stone sits apart from a river's current, sat Aldous Renner.

Everyone in the courier service knew Renner, and everyone feared knowing him. Signatory for the Eastern Provisional Districts — the last bloc whose votes the armistice technically required, the last man whose name on the paper meant the paper was legal rather than merely true. Twenty years ago he and Marchand had fought a feud through ministries like a fire through a dry season: Renner had opposed three of Marchand's appointments, blocked two of his treaties, and once, famously, walked out of a signing in protest of terms he called "surrender dressed as settlement." He was seventy-one years old. He had a reputation for two things: incorruptibility, and spite. Of the two, spite was the more reliable.

He was also, Xanthe had learned in a basement two nights ago from a shaking junior clerk, the only signatory whose initialing was required on the *annexes* — the schedules, the clauses, the fine print where countries actually died. Marchand could sign the main instrument alone if he chose. The annexes needed Renner's hand.

Which was why Marchand had spent three weeks burying the delay clause. Which was why it existed only in the head of a courier standing now in the servants' corridor outside the hall, listening to the murmur of the machinery about to close, with snow melting in their boot seams.

Seven minutes.

There was a protocol for couriers at signings. There was a protocol for everything; the Republic's last functioning organ was its love of procedure. Documents were carried to the table by authorized dispatch personnel, logged, initialed, returned. A courier could walk to that table. A courier had walked to that table forty-one times.

But Xanthe carried no document. And when they spoke, they would not be speaking as a courier. They would be committing, in front of six witnesses and a window full of a dying nation, the act their handler had already tried to kill once — reciting state paper from memory, which was either treason or testimony depending entirely on whether the room chose to hear it.

Their hands, they noticed distemperately, were shaking. Gideon's voice arrived in memory, calm as arithmetic: *Fear isn't the enemy. Fear is just information. You're afraid because this matters. Good. Let it make your hands careful.*

Xanthe pressed their palms flat against the cold wall until the shaking became stillness, and thought of Mother's voice two nights ago, thin as paper and twice as strong: *My life is spent, love. Yours is not. Spend it well.*

They straightened the grey coat. They lifted the satchel. They opened the door.

* * *

The hall swallowed them the way deep water swallows sound.

No one looked up at first — that was the mercy and the horror of being invisible; the great rooms of the world are built never to see the hands that feed them. Xanthe walked the left side of the table with scheduled steps, past the Chancellor's woman, past two generals whose faces they knew from fleet orders, past the foreign liaison with his neutral grey suit and his eyes like filed glass, toward the table's foot where Aldous Renner sat reading annex pages with a fountain pen capped beside his hand like a weapon holstered.

Marchand saw them first. Of course he did. His gaze came up mid-sentence, found the grey coat, found the face beneath the cap brim, and something crossed it — not surprise; Xanthe had expected surprise — but a cold, rapid recalculation, a man doing sums and disliking the total. His hand moved a half-inch toward the bell on the table.

"Dispatch for the Eastern signatory," Xanthe said, loud enough to be procedural, dull enough to be dismissed. "Requires receipt signature."

"Couriers wait at the door," Marchand said. "Nothing is received during session."

"This one's marked priority annex correction, Undersecretary." Xanthe let the faintest pause fall. "Annex corrections go direct to the signatory initialing. That's regulation 114."

The room had begun, grudgingly, to listen. Regulation was the one language everyone present spoke fluently. Renner looked up. His eyes were pale, watery-blue, old — and quick underneath the age, quick as a trap.

"Annex correction," Renner said. His voice was gravel poured slowly. "I've received no correction schedule."

"It's not on the schedule, sir." Xanthe reached the table's foot and stopped at the exact distance regulation prescribed — close enough to hand over, far enough not to loom. "Because it was removed from the schedule."

The words dropped into the room like a stone into still water, and every ripple ran outward at once. The liaison's pen stopped. One of the generals turned his head. Marchand said, very quietly, "That will be all," and it was not addressed to Xanthe.

Renner had not moved. But his attention had changed quality — from the bored scan of an old man at a long table to something focused and fine, a lighthouse beam swinging around. "Say that again," he said.

"Sir, I shouldn't—"

"*Say it again.*"

Xanthe set the satchel on the table edge. Their hands were steady now; the fear had done its work and burned down to fuel. Somewhere beyond the windows the crowd in the square shifted, a low sound like the sea heard from far inland.

"The annex on evacuation scheduling," Xanthe said, "as drafted on the ninth contained a clause — Annex Twelve, Subsection Four — providing for a forty-eight-hour interval between ratification and border closure, for the purpose of opening a civilian corridor through the eastern docks. That clause was struck from the final text before distribution. Not amended. Struck. By order of the Undersecretary's office." They looked at Marchand, and did not look away. "I know because I carried the original. And because I have carried documents for this government for six years, I can repeat the clause verbatim."

"You're a courier," Marchand said. His voice was silk laid over a blade. "Couriers do not read what they carry."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "We don't. We memorize it, though. It's the first thing they teach us — that paper burns, and heads are harder to burn." A beat. "Though some people try."

Someone at the table made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Marchand's composure held, but Xanthe watched the cost of holding it arrive in his jaw.

"This is nonsense," Marchand said to the room. "An unauthorized person, making unverifiable claims, disrupting—" He gestured at the folders, the ribbons, the whole solemn machinery. "Guards can return this individual to the dispatch office, and we can proceed."

"Sit down, Veyra."

It was Renner. He had not raised his voice; he simply set the words down between them like a card played face-up, and the table went quiet in a way that had twenty years of feud inside it. Renner rose — slowly, one hand on the chair back, an old man's rise — and looked at Xanthe with those trap-quick eyes.

"If I ask you to recite this clause," he said, "and you recite it, and it matches the drafting record — which I will have fetched, because unlike some people in this room I keep records — then someone has committed fraud against the armistice instrument. Do you understand what you're alleging?"

"Yes, sir."

"Do you understand what happens to you if you're wrong?"

"Yes, sir." And then, because the moment demanded truth and not theater, Xanthe added: "I've understood that for several days, sir. I came anyway."

Renner studied them for a long moment. Outside, the crowd murmured; a church bell somewhere rang the three-quarters hour. Ten o'clock. Minutes, now. Perhaps five.

"Recite," Renner said.

* * *

Xanthe closed their eyes, and the clause rose in them the way a psalm rises in someone who has prayed it their whole life — because they had. Three years of carrying other people's words had taught their memory to hold text the way a hull holds water out: not as effort, but as shape. They had recited it to Gideon in the lighthouse with shells exploding across the harbor until he could say it back to them syllable-perfect, and Gideon had wept, briefly and silently, and then checked them again.

"Annex Twelve, Subsection Four. *Interval of Application.* In recognition of the civilian population remaining within the capital district and contiguous eastern wards, a period of forty-eight hours shall intervene between ratification of the present instrument and the closure of land and maritime borders, during which interval the occupying authority shall permit unmolested passage through designated eastern dock corridors for all persons presenting themselves, without requirement of documentation, permit, or berth certificate; such passage to be supervised jointly and conducted with due regard to safety of life at sea."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears. Snow ticked faintly against the tall windows. Out in the square, ten thousand people stood unaware that their next two days were being argued over a polished table by seven people and a courier.

"A berth certificate," Renner said quietly, when Xanthe finished. "Without requirement of berth certificate." He turned his head, very slowly, toward Marchand. "You cut the one paragraph that didn't require paper. You cut the paragraph for people with nothing left but their feet."

"The interval was operationally unworkable," Marchand said. "The occupation commission would never—"

"The occupation commission," Renner said, "will be told what the instrument says, because I am going to read it to them. Fetch the drafting record." This to a secretary frozen by the wall, who fled. Then Renner looked back at Xanthe, and something in the old face had shifted — not warmth, exactly; Renner did not look like a man who traded in warmth. Respect, maybe. Or recognition. One stubborn thing seeing another.

"You know what happens if this passes," he said. "Two days. Two days of boats. And two days during which every man in this room has to stand in front of the occupiers and explain why the surrender they bought is late."

"Yes, sir."

"And you know that when they come looking for whoever cost them those two days — and they will come — the trail ends at a courier who claims to have memorized a document that no longer exists." Renner picked up his fountain pen and uncapped it, unhurried. "There'll be no paper to prove you ever carried it. No receipt. You've made yourself evidence with nowhere to file it."

"I'm accustomed to being unfiled, sir."

For the first time, the corner of Renner's mouth moved. It was not quite a smile; it was the ghost of a smile that had died young. "So am I," he said. "That's why I'm still invited to things."

The secretary returned breathless with a leather folio — the drafting record, the ninth day's text — and Renner paged through it with an old man's deliberate slowness that was, Xanthe realized, entirely tactical, because Marchand had begun to speak — objections, procedural motions, the word *irregular* deployed like cavalry — and Renner simply kept turning pages until he found it, and put his finger on the line, and turned the folio around to face the table.

"Annex Twelve, Subsection Four," Renner said. "As drafted. As delivered to this table by a courier who carries nothing but a working memory and, apparently, a spine. As struck by—" he glanced up, mild as arsenic, "—someone. Now. We can proceed to sign an instrument whose annexes have been altered in secret, and I will take my districts home, and you can explain to the occupation why your own signatories won't initial. Or we can restore the clause, delay the pen by one hour, and sign the document that was actually written." He capped and uncapped the pen once, a small precise click. "I have waited twenty years, Veyra, to watch someone try to bully me into bad bookkeeping. Choose quickly. The courier's given us a country's worth of reasons to hurry."

Marchand looked around the table — at the generals who would not meet his eye, at the Chancellor's woman already reaching for her pen, at the liaison whose filed-glass gaze had gone carefully, diplomatically, anywhere else — and Xanthe watched him understand, in real time, that the room had left him. His hands, those perfectly steady hands, settled flat on the table, and when he spoke his voice had gone quiet and stripped, the voice under all the silk, the one Xanthe had heard once before in a basement, making offers.

"Restore it," Marchand said. "Initial the restored text. And then—" his eyes came up and found Xanthe's, and held them, and there was no heat in them at all, which was the most frightening thing in the room, "—we will want a full accounting of how classified instruments leave this ministry in people's heads."

"Then it passes?" Xanthe asked Renner, and their voice cracked on the second word, and they let it.

"It passes," Renner said, and set his pen to the restored page.

Outside, the bells began to ring ten, and beneath them — rising through the stone, through the glass, through the floorboards — came the sound of the square: ten thousand people learning from a shouted bulletin that the signing had stalled, that the stalls meant boats, that boats meant morning after tomorrow and the morning after that. It was not cheering yet. It was louder than cheering. It was the sound of a city inhaling.

Xanthe stood at the foot of the table while pens scratched, invisible again, unfiled again, evidence with nowhere to be kept — and through the tall windows, beyond the crowd, at the mouth of the alley across the square, a figure in a black coat stopped, looked up at the lit windows of the signing hall, and turned away into the snow.

They had an hour's head start. Maybe less. Xanthe was already calculating the route to the harbor when Renner, without looking up from his initials, said quietly:

"Courier. Walk out through my office. There's a back stair, and a man named Dorrit keeps the coach gate, and he owes me a debt older than this war. You've bought ten thousand people two days." The pen paused. "Don't waste yours standing in the front hall."
