## 18. Forty-Eight Hours

**Xanthe**

The clause passed at eleven minutes past four in the afternoon, and Xanthe knew it before any runner came, because the bells began.

They had not rung in Vessany for a year. The occupiers had ordered them silenced — bells summoned crowds, and crowds were dangerous — and so when the great cracked bell of Saint Aurelia's began to swing, its voice rusty and off-key, half the city stopped in the street and simply listened, faces tilted upward like people hearing rain after drought.

Then the bell finished its first peal and every other bell in the district took it up, and the sound broke into shouting, and the shouting broke into running, and Xanthe stood in the middle of Cordwainer Square with the cold biting through their coat and understood that forty-eight hours had just begun.

Signorale Vessieri had risen from his chair at the head of the signing table, slow as an old ship coming about, and read the clause aloud in a voice like gravel poured through a sieve. *Article Nine, provision deferred.* Two days. Not peace, not rescue, not mercy — just two days, bought with one courier's memory and one old man's stubbornness, and Xanthe had watched the handler's face across the room as the words went into the record. Ferro Lask had not moved. He had looked at Xanthe the way a man looks at a ledger that will not balance, and then he had looked down at his own hands, and Xanthe had known, in the way you know weather coming, that it was not over.

But it was over enough. The corridor was open.

Now the square was becoming something else entirely. Word traveled the way fire travels along a fuse — the quay, someone shouted, go to the quay, boats at the quay — and the crowd's center of gravity shifted, and Xanthe let themselves be carried with it, ribs bruised by elbows, boots treading on their heels, everyone in Vessany suddenly moving toward water.

*Forty-eight hours.* They did the arithmetic as they walked, because arithmetic was what they had instead of prayer. Forty-eight hours minus the time to reach the docks. Minus the queue. Minus whatever the occupiers decided at the last moment to take back. It came out to almost nothing, spread across a hundred thousand people, and still they ran, because almost nothing was more than they'd had yesterday.

The snow had turned to gray sleet. Somewhere behind them artillery spoke twice, distantly, like a door slamming in another house.

* * *

**Gideon**

Gideon Hale stood on the ministry steps with his census ledger under his arm and watched the exodus of a nation.

He had spent three years counting the people of Vessany — doors knocked, names entered, lies noted in his neat, betraying hand — and now all of them were pouring past him toward the harbor, and he found he could not stop reading them. A woman carrying a birdcage, the bird gone, the cage full of spoons. An old man pushing a handcart stacked with photograph frames, the glass already cracked, the faces already fogged. Children asleep upright against their mothers' coats, swaying with each step like small drunkards. Every one of them a line in his book. Every one of them now a number in an evacuation that existed because a courier had walked into a signing room and refused to die quietly.

He should have felt triumph. What he felt was closer to vertigo.

"Records office is done," said the clerk beside him, a boy named Pell who had survived the war chiefly by agreeing with everyone. "Ministry says burn everything sensitive by midnight. That's us, sir. We're sensitive."

"I know what I am," Gideon said.

He did not burn the ledgers. He told Pell he would, and watched the boy hurry off toward his own family, and then Gideon stood in the empty records room with its shelves of lives and thought about which pages mattered now. The false identity trail he had built for Xanthe — three names, four addresses, a paper ghost walking ahead of the hunters — those pages would hang them if anyone official ever cross-referenced. He fed them into the stove one at a time and watched the ink curl brown, and felt nothing but relief, which frightened him more than fear would have.

The last page he kept was the Thornbury household entry. Mother, bedridden. Daughter, occupation listed as *seamstress*, which was the lie he had written himself and then spent a season suspecting. Son, age fourteen.

One berth. He had heard it confirmed at midday, before the bells: the corridor manifest allowed supplemental berths for dependents of certificate holders, but the original certificates — the real ones, the ones issued before the rot set in — there was exactly one left unclaimed in the whole administrative wreckage of Vessany, and it was folded inside a lighthouse lantern in his coat pocket, and it had been burning a hole through the wool for weeks.

He had promised himself he would give it away without flinching. He discovered, standing in the warm ash-light of his own burned secrets, that he had lied to himself, and that the flinching was already underway.

* * *

**Tam**

The harbor did not look like anything Tam had imagined, and Tam had imagined it constantly, drawing boats in the margins of his schoolbooks until the margins ate the lessons.

It looked like grief organized by queue numbers. The mole stretched out into the slate water and on it stood ten thousand people in the sleet, packed shoulder to shoulder, breathing steam, and beyond the mole rode the ships — gray hulls, black hulls, a hospital boat with its red crosses smeared by rain — and between the shore and the ships moved launches and fishing smacks and one paddle steamer that Tam recognized from summer excursions, its decks now rail-to-rail with bodies. Soldiers held the gangways. Officers with clipboards called districts in a flat shout: *Harbor Ward! Cordwainer! The Hill, The Hill only!* And at each call a section of the crowd surged forward and was stopped, and sorted, and mostly turned back.

Two days was nothing. Tam could see that now. Two days was a cup of water passed down a line of ten thousand.

He found his sister near the customs shed, where the certificate desk had been set up under canvas, and the sight of her nearly undid him — Xanthe, who was always smooth and unreadable, stood with snow melting in their hair and their hands shaking so badly they couldn't work the button of their coat, and Tam pretended not to notice, because that was the family trade, pretending.

"They'll call Cordwainer within the hour," Xanthe said. "Maybe less. The lists are backed up."

"Then we're near the front."

"You're near the front." Xanthe looked at him, and Tam saw the thing in their face that he had been dreading for a week, arriving at last like a train you hear on the far side of the hill. "Tam—"

"No." He said it fast, before the sentence could land. "No, whatever it is, no."

"There's one berth. One. Gideon's certificate — it covers one principal, and the manifest amendment might stretch to a dependent, might, if the desk clerk is merciful, and merciful clerks are—" Xanthe's breath came out ragged. "Mother won't take it. She's made me promise. She made me promise, Tam, and I can't break it and I can't keep it, and that leaves—"

"Me." His voice came out very small under the noise of ten thousand people wanting to live. "It leaves me."

"It leaves you."

"And you stay. And Gideon stays." Tam looked past his sister, across the sleet-gray mole, at the ships that were not for them. He was fourteen years old and he had spent the war hoarding scrap metal and learning which adults lied and which ones merely performed it, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like being struck, that he was being handed the entire future of his family in the shape of a boat ticket, and that accepting it meant carrying it alone across open water to a country he had never seen.

"I don't want it," he said.

"I know."

"That's not fair. Nobody asked me if it's fair."

"Nobody asked any of us," Xanthe said. "That's what war is. It's the asking being over."

Behind them the officers began calling Cordwainer Ward, and the crowd moved like a single animal, and Tam felt his brother-sister-sibling's hands on his shoulders, turning him toward the gangway, and he dug in his heels for exactly one second — one honest second of refusal, of fury, of being a child — and then he let himself be turned.

* * *

**Ferro Lask**

In a third-floor office above the emptied ministries, Ferro Lask signed his last documents and waited for the knock he had been expecting since four o'clock.

It came at dusk. Three knocks, even spacing, ministry pattern. He did not say come in; there was no point. The men who entered wore the armbands of the Ministry's own provost detachment, and the senior one had been at his wedding, years ago, back when Vessany still pretended it had decades left.

"Undersecretary Lask." The man's voice was careful, almost kind. "You are relieved of your commission and remanded to ministry custody pending inquiry. Article Nine has... complications. There are questions about the disposition of certain dispatches."

"There are questions about the disposition of a country," Lask said. "I tried to answer them quickly. Someone answered them slowly. History will grade on a curve."

He rose, straightened his coat, and paused at the window. Below, the mole glittered under work lamps, and the boats went out loaded and came back empty, again and again, like the tide learning a new job. He had meant to protect his family. He had meant, in the end, to protect himself, and told himself the two were the same thing, which was the oldest lie an official could tell. Somewhere on that quay was a courier who had chosen worse arithmetic and better people.

"Your family has been granted provisional berths," the provost said, not meeting his eye. "Ministry discretion."

Of course. His arrest was the ministry's receipt; the berths were its apology, cheap because they cost the signatories nothing but space. Lask nodded once, took his hat, and walked out between the men with his spine straight and his hands cuffed loosely in front of him, and if any of them noticed that the Undersecretary was weeping silently, none of them reported it.

* * *

**Xanthe**

The berth clerk was not merciful, but she was tired, and tired clerks sometimes confuse lines on a form.

Xanthe stood at the canvas desk while she examined the lantern-pass — Gideon's certificate, genuine, pre-war issue, the embossed seal still crisp — and then the household registry page, and then Tam, who stood at attention like a soldier twice his size trying to look indispensable.

"One principal," the clerk said. "Dependents require supplementary authorization. I can't—"

"The dependent clause, paragraph six." Xanthe kept their voice level, bored, bureaucratic; they had delivered enough dispatches to know how paperwork wanted to be spoken to. "Minors in sole custody of a certificate holder's designated household. It's in the corridor manifest amendment. Signed yesterday."

The clerk looked at the amendment, which did exist, and at paragraph six, which did not quite say what Xanthe claimed, and at Tam's face, and at the line of two thousand people behind them, and stamped the page.

"Boarding gate nine," she said. "Next."

Walking to gate nine took eleven minutes and lasted a lifetime. The launch was loading now, a squat gray tug with children already wrapped in blankets along its gunwale. Sleet had soaked through Tam's coat. His chin was trembling, from cold or otherwise, and he kept his fists jammed in his pockets like a man holding shut a door.

"Mother's ring." Xanthe pressed it into his palm and closed his fingers over it. "She wanted you to have it. She said—" Their voice failed, once, briefly. "She said you'd know what it's for. When you're older. She says infuriating things like that."

"Come with me," Tam whispered. "There's room in the engine coal, I've seen stowaways, I could—"

"No."

"Then say when. Say when I get to come back. Say it's a when and not an if."

Xanthe knelt on the wet planking so their faces were level. Around them the queue pressed and the officers shouted and somewhere a woman was singing, low and wordless, a hymn or a lullaby or both. "When," Xanthe said. "Spring crossing, if the corridor holds. I'll be at the lighthouse. You'll see it lit from the water — Gideon and I will keep it lit, every night, so you can't miss us. That's a promise with an address attached."

Tam laughed, horribly, wetly, and hugged them hard enough to crack a rib, and then an officer was pulling him up the gangway, and then he was at the rail, and then — this was the part Xanthe had not prepared for — he was simply a face among faces, getting smaller, getting grayer, getting gone.

The tug pulled out into the current. Tam stood at the stern the whole way, one hand raised, and Xanthe raised theirs, and the space between the shore and the ship filled with everything neither of them could shout across water.

Then the sleet thickened, and the harbor swallowed him, and it was done.

Xanthe stood on the mole long after the shape was lost, hand still in the air, feeling the exact dimensions of the hole. No clean wins. That was the lesson of the whole cursed republic: you saved one and mourned him immediately. Behind them, the queues went on calling districts. Ahead, out at the harbor mouth, the first shells of the evening barrage fell short and threw up white columns of spray.

A hand settled on their shoulder. Gideon, coat collar up, census ledger gone at last, his face exhausted and open.

"He'll make the crossing," Gideon said. "The water's calm for the season."

"You can't know that."

"No," Gideon agreed. "But I can say it, and you can choose to believe me, and that's most of what love gets to do tonight." He turned his collar against the wind. "The lighthouse?"

"The lighthouse."

They walked back up the mole together, two small figures against ten thousand, and behind them the boats filled and the guns spoke and the two days ran down like sand, and overhead, faint through the sleet, the first star of evening appeared and vanished, appeared and vanished, like a lantern being tested.

At the edge of the quarter, a figure detached itself from a doorway and fell into step thirty paces behind — heavy boots, ministry-issue greatcoat, and moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who had all night and only one destination. Xanthe felt the gaze between their shoulder blades before they heard the footsteps, and tightened their hand around Gideon's.

"Don't look back," Xanthe murmured. "We're followed."
