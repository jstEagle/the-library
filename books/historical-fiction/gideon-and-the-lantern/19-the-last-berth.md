## 19. The Last Berth

The corridor had been open for thirty-one hours when I finally understood that the arithmetic was done.

Forty-eight hours. That was what the clause had bought — two days in which the guns stayed quiet and the harbor gates stood open and the boats came in like patient animals to be loaded. I had spent a year of my life learning to measure time in sealed packets and dead drops, and now I stood on the quay at Corvenn Dock measuring it in gangplanks: one lowered, one raised, one being winched into place even as the gulls screamed over the cranes. Every plank that rose carried four hundred people out of the Republic of Vessany. Every minute it stayed down left four hundred more standing in the snow.

The snow itself had turned filthy with traffic — boot-printed, cart-rutted, gray at the edges where the coal smoke settled into it. Refugees moved along the quay in lines that did not so much advance as exhale forward, bundles on their backs, children roped to their parents' wrists, old men carrying portraits wrapped in oilcloth like relics. The occupiers' observers stood at intervals along the pilings in their slate-gray coats, clipboards in hand, counting. They had agreed to the corridor. They had not agreed to make it comfortable.

I found Tam at the end of the third queue, exactly where Gideon's forged trail of identities said he would be — because Gideon had written him there, three days ago, with a census pen and a steady hand, building a boy named Tobias Marle out of ink and crossed-out names. My brother stood with his hands jammed in his pockets and his chin up, the way he'd held it since he was six years old and fell off the harbor wall trying to see how far the water went. His coat bulged oddly at the seams. Even now, even here, he had scrap metal sewn into it. I could see the shapes of washers and hinge-pins through the wool, a boy armored against a world that no longer wanted what he knew how to trade.

"You're late," he said.

"The line from the ministry was—"

"I know." He looked past me at the boat. "Is it true? Two days?"

"Two days. Then the gates close and they don't open again. Not for anything."

He nodded slowly, and I watched him do the thing he always did — take the fact, turn it over, find its weight. He was fifteen. He had bartered his way through two winters, lied to census takers, run from liaison officers, and never once cried where I could see it. I thought: he is the bravest person I know, and he is fifteen, and I am about to break his heart in front of four hundred strangers.

"Tam." I took his shoulder so he'd have to look at me. "Where's your bundle?"

"In the pile." He nodded at the baggage heap under the tarpaulin. "Why?"

"Because you're going aboard."

For a moment he just stared at me, and I watched the sentence arrive in pieces — the word *aboard*, then the word *you're*, then the absence of any *we* attached to it. I watched him assemble it the way he assembled everything, methodically, refusing to reach the conclusion until every part was accounted for.

"No," he said.

"Tam—"

"No. Where's yours? Where's Gideon's?" His voice cracked upward, the adolescent pitch of it betraying him. "The berth's for one. You told me the berth was for Mother, so either she changed her mind or—" He stopped. He understood. Of course he understood; he'd understood everything since he was eleven years old, and simply hadn't let himself say it. "She told you not to bring her. Didn't she."

"She made her own decision," I said, and my voice held, which surprised me. "This one's mine. And Gideon's making his."

"Gideon can tear up whatever he wants, that doesn't mean—" Tam's fists came out of his pockets. He grabbed the front of my coat, actually grabbed it, right there in the queue, and nobody noticed because everybody in Vessany was holding somebody else's coat this week. "You don't get to stay. That's not how it works. The whole point — the clause, the corridor, all of it — the point was that someone *gets out*."

"The point was that thousands get out." I put my hand over his fist, gently, the way you calm an animal. "Look at the line, Tam. Look behind you. Every one of those people gets on a boat because a piece of paper didn't die in a fireplace. Mother gave me her life and told me not to waste it on a berth. I'm not going to waste it on a gangplank instead."

"That's not noble, that's stupid—"

"Yes," I said. "Probably both."

* * *

Getting him to the gangplank took twenty minutes and every trick I owned.

I lied to him first — small lies, logistical lies, the kind that sound like process: we have to check your manifest, we have to confirm the Marle registration before the observers do, come this way, stand here. He knew he was being managed. He let it happen anyway, because underneath his fury was the same current running under everyone on that quay: the animal knowledge that the boat leaves whether you board it or not, and the tide does not negotiate.

At the foot of the plank a ministry clerk checked his paper against Gideon's false ledger, and for one long moment the whole architecture of our deception hung on a bored man with ink on his cuff comparing two names that did not exist. Then he waved Tam through, and my brother was standing on the plank with nothing between him and the deck except forty feet of swaying wood, and he turned around.

"Mother's ring," he said suddenly. "Did she—"

I had forgotten. God help me, in the chaos of the last two days, in the signing room and the snow and the sirens, I had forgotten, and my hand went to my collar where I'd sewn it three nights ago while Mother slept, and Tam saw my face change and knew before I said anything.

"You have it."

I pulled the thread with my teeth. The ring came loose into my palm — thin gold, worn nearly to wire on one side from sixty years of hands, the garnet in it cloudy as an old eye. She had pressed it into my hand the last time I sat with her, without ceremony, without even lifting her head fully from the pillow, the way she did everything important: quietly, as if it were housework. *"Give it to the one who crosses the water,"* she'd said. *"Not the ones who stay to sweep up. Staying people don't need rings. They need brooms."*

Even dying she'd been funnier than any of us.

I climbed three steps of the gangplank so we were eye to eye, and I took his hand — his chapped, scraped, metal-hoarding hand, the hand that had followed an informer through the market at twelve and a liaison office at fifteen — and I pressed the ring into his palm and closed his fingers over it.

"She said you carry it," I said. "Across the water. She said the one who crosses keeps the family, and the ones who stay keep the city, and neither job is finished until the other one comes home."

"Xanthe—"

"Listen to me." I gripped his wrist hard enough that he'd feel it for hours, and I meant him to. "You get off that boat in wherever it lands — Harrow Point, Selvane, wherever they're processing — and you find the registrar, and you give them the name Tobias Marle, and then you give them the name Thornbury, and you tell them there are two more coming. Because there are. Do you hear me? There are two more coming. I don't know when. I don't know how. But if you believe one thing, believe that the story isn't finished, because I memorized a clause that wasn't supposed to survive, and I delivered it, and it worked. Things work, Tam. Against every expectation, things work."

His eyes were streaming now, openly, and he wasn't even fighting it, and I realized with a jolt that this was the bravest thing I'd ever seen anyone do — cry on a gangplank in front of occupying observers and board anyway.

"I hate you," he said, wrecked.

"I know. It's mutual."

"I'm going to write to you. Every week. I'm going to write so much you'll beg them to close the border just to stop the mail."

"Do that," I said, laughing and crying in the same breath. "Do exactly that."

I stepped back down onto the quay. I watched him climb — small against the sky, one hand fisted around the rope rail, the other fisted around our mother's sixty years. At the top he stopped once, looked back, and I raised my hand, and he raised his, and then the sailor pulled the plank in behind him like drawing a stitch closed, and my brother was on the deck of the *Meridian Star* among four hundred strangers, and he was ours, and he was safe, and he was gone.

The whistle blew. The lines began to cast off. I stood at the edge of the quay and felt something tear loose in my chest — not cleanly, not painlessly, but tearing nonetheless, like a root coming out of frozen ground — and I understood that this was what Mother had meant. Letting go was not losing him. Letting go was keeping him in the only direction keeping was still possible.

* * *

Gideon was waiting by the bollards at the end of the pier, hatless in the snow, a folded paper in his hands.

I saw the paper before I saw his face, and I knew what it was, because I had spent a year learning to read documents at a glance: heavy stock, ministry watermark, the printed border of an evacuation permit. Exit papers. His exit papers — real ones, honestly earned or corruptly bought, it didn't matter; they were passage, and he was holding them the way you hold a bird you're deciding whether to release.

He didn't look at the boat. He looked at me.

"Berth's used, then," he said.

"Tam's crossing. With Mother's ring." My voice was still unsteady. "Your papers — Gideon, those are real. That's a real permit. You could walk up that gangplank in twenty minutes."

"I know."

"There'll be nothing after this. When the corridor closes tomorrow night, that's it. The armistice signs, the borders seal, and whatever happens after happens to whoever's inside it. You've seen the terms. Everyone with a clearance has seen the terms."

"I have," he said. "I copied some of them myself." A ghost of the census-taker's dry smile, the one that had once terrified me across a kitchen table. "That's rather the point. I know precisely what staying costs. That's why I know it's not the question."

"Then what is?"

He unfolded the papers. He smoothed them flat against the bollard cap, once, carefully, as if honoring them. Then he tore them in half. Then halves again. Then again, and again, until the snow around his boots was littered with confetti bearing the watermark of a government that had eight more hours of existence, and the wind came off the water and lifted the pieces and scattered them out over the harbor like a strange, brief snowfall going the wrong way.

"Why?" I asked, when I could speak.

"Because I spent eleven months pretending to count people," he said, "when what I was doing was looking for the ones worth staying for. And I found them. Both of them. One just boarded a boat, and one is standing in front of me asking why." He took my hand. His fingers were freezing; mine weren't much better. "A lantern-pass buys one crossing. A person who knows how to register the displaced, who has lists, who has a pen and a table and the patience of a bureaucrat — that person isn't worth one crossing. That person is worth whatever comes after. There are going to be thousands inside these borders when they seal, Xanthe. Thousands with no papers, wrong papers, forged papers I wrote myself. Someone will need to make them legible to the new masters so they aren't disappeared. I can be that someone. I'd be useless across the water. Here, I'm — " he paused, and corrected himself, quietly — "here, I'm home."

"And me?"

"You," he said, "memorized a clause under a burning lamp and walked it into a room full of men with seals and stamps, and came out having saved four thousand people with nothing but your voice. What you are worth across that water is beyond calculation. What you are worth here—" he squeezed my hand "—is also beyond calculation. So it isn't arithmetic anymore. It never was. It's just choice. I'm choosing this. Are you?"

I looked at the harbor. The *Meridian Star* was already moving, low in the water, black with souls, trailing gulls and smoke. Somewhere on its deck a boy with a gold ring fisted in his hand was watching the city he'd never seen shrink, and I sent him everything I had across the widening water — silently, uselessly, completely.

"I registered a family yesterday," I heard myself say. "Reunited a mother with two children through the displaced-persons rolls. Used a cipher key to do it. The same codes that moved armies." I laughed, wetly. "It turns out paper is just paper. It matters whose hands it's in."

"That's a yes, then."

"It's a yes."

Gideon nodded, and then — in front of the observers, in front of the queues, in front of the whole dying light of the Republic — he put his arms around me, and I let him, and I held on. Around us the corridor kept exhaling: the next queue shuffling forward, the next plank lowering, the machinery of mercy grinding on for nineteen more hours. Above the harbor the lighthouse stood dark on its point, waiting.

"We should get back," I said into his shoulder. "The registry reopens at seven. There's a family from the Salt Quarter on the list, and half their records burned."

"After you," he said.

We turned from the water together. Behind us the last whistle of the last boat we could see faded over the gray sea, carrying my brother and my mother's ring toward whatever came next — and ahead of us the snow was still falling, patient and indifferent, on a city full of people who needed counting, and naming, and keeping.

We were halfway up the quay when a runner in ministry gray caught my sleeve, breathless, and pressed a folded dispatch into my hand.

"Courier?" he panted. "They said — find the courier. It's marked urgent."

I opened it. The paper was thin, cheap, the kind used for things meant not to last. Three lines. And by the second one I had stopped feeling the cold entirely.

The armistice delegation has moved the final signing forward. Not tomorrow night. Dawn.
