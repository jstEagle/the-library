## 20. What the Corridor Carried

*Dear Xanthe,*

I have started this letter eleven times. I know because I counted the crumpled pages on the floor of the boarding house in Saltmere before the landlady made me sweep them up. Eleven attempts, and every one of them failed for the same reason: I kept trying to write about the corridor, and the corridor is not the thing I need to tell you about. The corridor was water and wire and two days of grace. What I need to tell you about is the people.

So let me start there. Let me start with the woman who held my hand while we waited for the gates to open, because she is the reason I finally understand what you did.

Her name is Marta Voss, though she told me this only after we had been at sea for six hours, when names stopped being dangerous things to say out loud. She was a schoolteacher in the Marsh Quarter. She taught arithmetic to children who are now scattered across three coastlines, and when the shelling started she went back into her classroom one last time to collect the register, because — she said, laughing in that way people laugh when the alternative is weeping — someone ought to know which children had made it out and which had not. She carried that register across the whole city in an oilcloth satchel, and she carries it still. On the boat she read me the names like a litany. Forty-one children. Nineteen accounted for. She reads it every morning. She says someday the list will be all checkmarks or all grief, and either way it deserves to be read until then.

You should know that when the armistice stalled — when those forty-eight hours opened like a crack in a wall and the whole city poured toward the docks — it was people like Marta who came through. Not soldiers. Not ministers. Teachers and dockhands and grandmothers and a man named Osric Pell who had spent the war selling counterfeit ration cards and spent the evacuation giving away real ones he'd somehow come by, refusing to explain, grinning like a thief who had finally found something worth stealing honestly.

That is what your clause carried, Xanthe. Not a delay. A city's worth of ordinary lives, given two days' head start on oblivion.

* * *

I keep thinking I should tell the crossing properly, so you can see it, because I know you'll never leave and so you'll never have a crossing of your own, and there is a part of me that grieves that even as I admire it.

We boarded at the East Quay in the grey hour before dawn, the second day of the corridor. There were nine hundred of us packed onto a coastal steamer built to hold four hundred, and the crew did not turn anyone away. They just kept saying *below, below, find room below*, and people folded themselves into every hold and stairwell like letters crammed into an overflowing satchel. I stood at the rail because Tam from the tenements had learned long ago that the best bartering spot is wherever nobody else wants to stand, and nobody wanted the rail. Too exposed. Too close to the water that might carry mines. I wanted it precisely because it was honest. You could see everything leaving from there: the harbor walls, the cranes standing over the empty quays like gallows, the smoke of the artillery line beyond the eastern hills where the corridor's boundary was drawn in shells that were, for those two days, forbidden to fall.

They fell anyway, once. At midmorning a battery somewhere south of the river broke the truce — whether by accident or appetite I never learned — and three rounds came down on the Fishmarket district. We heard them from the rail. The sound arrived a full breath after the flash, and in that breath the whole ship went silent, nine hundred people holding nine hundred lungs, and then came the noise, and then came the crying, and then came Marta Voss's hand finding mine without either of us looking, the way you find a banister in the dark.

The ship did not turn back. That is the thing I keep returning to. The captain — a broad, sunburnt woman everyone called Skipper Aune — rang the engine order telegraph to full ahead and said, loud enough for the whole deck to hear, "They get one mistake. We don't give them two." And we went on through the marked channel, past the buoyed lane, past the gray hulls of the occupier's patrol boats riding watch at the corridor's edge like dogs ordered to sit, and nobody fired, and by noon the guns behind us had fallen back into their grumbling silence, and by evening Vessany itself had sunk beneath the horizon the way a stone sinks — slowly, then all at once gone.

I didn't cry at the rail. I want you to know that. I saved it for later, in the hold, wedged between sacks of seed grain, and even then I cried quietly, because there were children sleeping against those sacks and I have learned from you that a person's grief is his own business unless he chooses to make it yours.

* * *

There are other people I should tell you about, because you gave up more than Mother's ring to put me on that boat, and I think you deserve to know what your ring purchased.

There was a boy called Fen, maybe eight years old, who traveled alone. He had been separated from his family in the crush at the West Gate and he refused to speak for the first day — just sat in the corner of the hold with his knees up and his eyes going from stranger to stranger like a man counting exits. It was Osric Pell, the card-forger, who cracked him. Osric sat down beside him and dealt him a hand of cards and cheated shamelessly, losing every round on purpose, and by the fourth hand Fen was accusing him of throwing the game and by the sixth Fen was winning actual biscuits off him, and by the time we sighted the far shore Fen was asleep against Osric's shoulder, and Osric sat absolutely rigid for three hours so as not to wake him. I watched him do it. A man who had looked out for number one through an entire war, holding still as a statue because a boy trusted him.

There was Dr. Ilse Renn, who set two broken arms and delivered a baby in the ship's galley during the worst of the swell, working by lamplight with the cook holding the lamp and the whole hold listening to the first cries and cheering so hard the sailors came down to see what we'd won. Her name was Anemone. Born at sea, in a corridor bought by a clause one courier refused to let die. They will tell her someday where she came from, and the story will be terrible and beautiful in equal measure, and it will begin with you.

There was an old man, name of Corin, who had been a lamplighter in Vessany for forty years, back when the streets still had lamps worth lighting. All through the crossing he kept looking back at the horizon where the city had been, and on the second night he said to me, "Boy, do you know why they retired the lamps?" I said no. He said, "Because the electric came, and it was cheaper, and nobody asked the lamps how they felt about it." Then he laughed until he wheezed, and I laughed too, and it struck me that he wasn't talking about lamps at all. He was talking about himself, and about all of us, and about how a thing can be replaced and still have been worth keeping. I think you will understand that better than anyone.

* * *

Now I have to tell you about the berth, because I know what it cost you and I have not been honest enough yet about what I did with it.

I could have sold it. You know that. In the crush at the East Quay, berths were changing hands for sums I cannot write down without embarrassing both of us — a man offered me a year's wages and a coat with a fur collar, and for one ugly moment I actually considered it, because I was sixteen and furious and my mother was dead three weeks and my sister had stayed behind on purpose, and it seemed to me that the world owed me something warm to wear.

But then I saw the berth for what it was. Not paper. Not passage. It was your refusal, printed on a ministry form. It was Mother saying *my life is spent and theirs is not.* It was Gideon tearing up his own exit papers on the dock — yes, I saw that too, from the gangway, and I will carry the sight of it until I die: a man who spent the whole war pretending to count people finally showing what he had counted them *for*. Every person who ever tried to buy that berth off me was trying to buy a piece of you, and I decided none of them could afford it, and then I decided the only way to spend it rightly was to give it away entirely.

So I did. I gave it to Marta Voss.

Not immediately. Don't think I'm a saint; I am not, and Marta would be the first to tell you. I gave it to her on the second night, after she read me the register again and I asked her about child number twenty-three, a girl called Bettina, and Marta's face did something I had seen once before — on your face, the night you told me the fleet was scuttled. That particular stillness. Grief arriving early, before there is even a body to grieve. And I understood that if Marta reached the far shore, nineteen children had a teacher waiting to find them, someone who knew their names and their reading levels and which ones lied about doing their sums. If I reached the far shore, I had myself, and a ring, and a fury I was mostly using for warmth.

"You'll need somewhere to stay," she said, when I pressed the paper into her hands. "This is yours."

"It's my brother's," I said, "and my brother is staying," and she looked at me for a long moment the way Gideon used to look at doors, seeing more than the surface showed, and then she said, "Then I'll owe him a bed and a table for as long as it takes," and that is how I come to be writing this letter from her spare room in Saltmere, with a real mattress under me and bread with actual butter on the table, which I eat slowly and suspiciously, like a cat handed a fish.

She has already found four of the forty-one. Two in the camps at Harrow Point, one working kitchens in a port town two days south, and one — this made her cry, the only time I've seen her cry — one teaching *other* displaced children their letters in a church hall, because apparently that is simply what Miss Voss's students become. Four checkmarks on the list. Thirty-seven to go. She says she will not stop until the register is finished, whatever finished turns out to mean, and I believe her, and I am helping. I have developed a useful talent, it turns out, for finding people who don't want to be found. I got it from watching a census taker work, and from watching a courier evade one.

* * *

I should tell you what the papers here are saying, since you get nothing over there but rumor and artillery. They're calling it the Vessany Corridor, capital letters and everything, as if it were a place instead of a promise. The ministry communiqués claim credit for the delay clause, naturally — the same ministry whose handler burned the original in front of your eyes, though I notice they do not mention *that* part, nor the arrest, nor the way he reportedly wept when they led him out, though I confess I enjoyed hearing it anyway. There is talk of an inquiry. There is always talk of an inquiry. But there are also ships — eleven so far, more promised — running the marked channel while the armistice holds its strange, negotiated breath, and every one of them is full, and every one of them carries somebody who would not be breathing if a courier in a forgettable coat had done the forgettable thing.

Sometimes I try to do the arithmetic you must have done, standing in that signing room with the only copy of the clause living behind your teeth. Nine hundred souls to our boat alone. Eleven boats. Call it eight thousand, call it ten. Against that you set one berth, one brother, one life that could have been lifted clean out of the ruin and set down safe in Saltmere with butter on the bread. Any sensible clerk would tell you the math is obvious. But I know you, Xanthe, and I know it did not feel like arithmetic to you. It felt like Mother. It felt like the only honest answer to the question she left you: *whose life is not yet spent?* Yours wasn't. Ours weren't. None of theirs were. So you answered like a woman who had been raised right, and you refused to choose, and in refusing to choose you chose everybody.

I am not going to tell you I'm glad you stayed. I'm sixteen and I'm allowed to be selfish in a letter if nowhere else. I am going to tell you that I understand it now — that I understand it the way you understand a tide, by being moved by it — and that understanding it is somehow worse and better than being glad.

* * *

The last thing I have to report is small, but I've saved it because I think it's the thing you'll want most.

On our third night out, when we were well clear of the corridor and the sea had gone calm and black, old Corin the lamplighter came up on deck carrying a storm lantern he'd brought aboard in his kit — smuggled it past inspection wrapped in his spare shirt, the old fool, as if the inspectors would confiscate fire from a man who'd tended it for forty years. He hung it from the aft rail and lit it, and he stood beside it facing back the way we'd come, toward a horizon with no city on it anymore, and he kept it burning all night. When I asked him why, he said, "Habit, boy." And then, after a while, he said, "And because there's others behind us. And a light says: this way. It says: someone kept it lit."

I thought of you and Gideon then. I thought of the lighthouse on the point, dark these two years, and I thought: if anyone in Vessany relights that lamp, it will be those two, out of pure stubborn tenderness, because they are the sort of people who answer darkness with a flame and call it work.

So here is my request, and consider it payment for the berth, which I know you'll wave off but I'm insisting anyway: write to me when the letter can reach me. Tell me the lamp is burning. Tell me the corridor still carries people. Tell me what you are becoming over there in the ruins, because I suspect it is something the histories will not have a proper category for, and I want to hear it from you before the historians get their hands on it.

Marta sends her love and a promise: when the register is finished, she is coming back across the water herself, checkmarks and all, to teach in whatever school rises from the ashes — and to shake the hand of the courier who bought her thirty-seven more chances to find her children.

I send my love too, and Mother's ring is safe on a cord around my neck, and I wear it not as mourning but as marching orders.

Light the lamp, Xanthe. Someone is watching the horizon for it.

*Your brother,*

*Tam*
