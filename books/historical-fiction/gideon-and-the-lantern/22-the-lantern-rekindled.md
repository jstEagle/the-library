## 22. The Lantern Rekindled

**Xanthe**

Spring came to Vessany the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and not all at once, and never quite complete.

The ice broke up in the harbor in the first week of March with a sound like distant artillery, and everyone who heard it flinched, and then laughed at themselves for flinching, and then didn't laugh at all. By April the rubble along the Quay of Sighs had grown its first green: weeds forcing their way up between paving stones that had been cracked by shell fragments rather than frost. Xanthe walked past them every morning on the climb to the lighthouse and thought, every morning, that they had never seen anything so stubborn or so beautiful.

The city had fallen in May of the year before, quietly, the way Mother had died — not with a bang but with a long exhale, the last resistance leaking out of it like warmth from cooling stone. There had been no final battle. The occupiers marched in through gates that were already open, past civilians who stood in doorways with the flat, exhausted expressions of people who have survived something by outlasting it. The armistice that should have sealed the borders forever had been delayed — forty-eight hours, then seventy-two, as the clause Xanthe carried in their head was argued over line by line in a room full of men who had expected no one to speak it aloud.

Forty-eight hours. It sounded like nothing. It was eleven thousand people.

Xanthe knew the number because Gideon counted them. That was the strange arithmetic of the peace: Gideon Hale, census taker, keeper of lists, had spent the winter after the fall doing what he had always done — writing down names — but now the names went into registers of the displaced, matched against registers of the evacuated, cross-referenced until families torn apart by the corridor could find each other again on paper if nowhere else. He worked out of a requisitioned schoolhouse three days a week and out of the lighthouse the other four, because the occupiers' clerks did not trouble themselves with the peninsula's far edge, and because the lantern needed tending, and because — though he would only ever say it obliquely, in the language of practicality he used for tenderness — the light mattered.

Boats still came through the evacuation corridor. Not the great transports of those first desperate weeks, but smaller craft: fishing boats from neutral harbors, coastal traders running sanctioned relief routes, sometimes a private vessel whose captain had once eaten bread in Vessany and remembered what it tasted like. The channel markers had been destroyed in the last artillery exchanges, the buoys sunk or dragged away, and the naval charts sold by the occupying administration were wrong in ways that might be incompetence or might be design. A boat coming in at night, in bad weather, with a nervous pilot who did not trust his own charts, needed one thing above all others.

It needed a fixed light on the point, burning steady, exactly where the old charts said it should be.

So on this evening in April, a year after the fall, Xanthe Thornbury climbed the hundred and eleven steps of the decommissioned lighthouse with an armful of split kindling and a can of lamp oil, and thought about Tam.

The letter had come two days ago, carried in the satchel of a relief boat out of Corvane, addressed in handwriting that had changed — broader now, more confident, the letters of someone who had been made to write a great many forms in a new country and had grown into his own name in the process. *Dear X,* it began, which was new; Tam had never shortened anything in his life, too afraid of wasting ink. Xanthe had read it four times and still could not read it steadily, and so had folded it into their coat — the coat, the old courier coat with the lining finally emptied of codes — and brought it up the stairs, meaning to read it again in the lantern room where there was nothing to do but watch the light and think.

*They are asking me to teach the younger ones arithmetic,* Tam had written. *Me. I told them I learned my figures bartering scrap metal in a war zone and the schoolmistress said that was the best qualification she'd heard all year. The ring fits Mother's finger better than it ever fit mine, which is to say I keep it in a drawer and take it out when I need to remember that someone loved me enough to stay behind. Which is often. Which is most days.*

*I met a woman on the crossing who said her brother was pulled from the water at Point Aldery because he saw a light where there was supposed to be nothing. She didn't know whose light it was. I knew. I didn't tell her. Some things you keep like coal against winter.*

Xanthe reached the lantern room and stood a moment in the doorway, letting the wind off the sea push at them, reading the last lines without taking the letter out again, having already committed them to the memory that had once held a surrender clause:

*You asked once whether belonging was a place or a people. I think I know your answer now. Tell Gideon the light reached us before we ever saw land.*

The lamp wanted trimming. Xanthe set down the kindling and went to it with hands that had learned the work in a single winter — trim the wick, check the oil feed, wipe the condensation from the lower curve of the glass where the sea breathed against it at night. The mechanism that rotated the lens had been broken since before the war; the light did not sweep the horizon the way a proper lighthouse light should. It simply burned, fixed and steady, a single unwavering point. Gideon had worried about that, at first — *a fixed light reads as anchored, they'll trust it less* — until the first boat came in during a gale and its pilot told them afterward, shouting over the dock noise with tears freezing in his beard, that in all that chaos of wave and dark, the one thing that hadn't moved was the light. Everything else in the world shifted. The light stayed.

People trusted what stayed.

Footsteps on the stairs — the particular rhythm of them, half a beat heavier on the left foot, that Xanthe had known for a year now and could pick out of any crowd. They did not turn around. There was a period, early on, when turning around had felt necessary every time, as if the world might yet take him back; now it was enough to listen to him climb.

"You've lit it early," Gideon said, emerging into the lantern room with his collar up and salt spray silvered in his hair. He was carrying a parcel wrapped in waxed cloth and looking pleased with himself in the restrained way that was all he permitted himself.

"Clouds coming in off the water. I want it visible before the rain kills the horizon." Xanthe nodded at the parcel. "And that is?"

"Flour. Actual flour, not the chalk-and-sawdust mixture the relief office sells. Marta Voss owed me a favor from the register — I found her sister's family in the Corvane listings in February." He set it down by the stairs and came to stand beside Xanthe at the glass, close enough that their shoulders touched, which after everything was still a thing Xanthe noticed every time, and expected someday to stop noticing, and hoped they never did.

"Marta Voss," Xanthe said. "She's on the list of the ones who stayed."

"She's on the list of the ones who stayed," Gideon agreed. "Which is getting longer."

That was true, and it was the quiet miracle neither of them had predicted. In the first months after the fall, the staying had looked like defeat — the remnant, the left-behind, the ones without papers or passage or luck. But spring had come, and the occupiers had turned out to be administrators before they were monsters, bored rather than brutal in most districts, and people had begun filtering back. Not many. A trickle. Carpenters assessing roofs. An old woman who had crossed the corridor to Corvane and returned because, she said, she had buried two husbands in the churchyard at Vessany and did not intend to make them wait alone. Families with children born across the water who spoke Vessani words with foreign accents and needed, their parents said, to know where the words came from.

"Renna's boy is registered for school in the autumn," Gideon said. "I put his birthplace down as Vessany and his mother cried in my office for ten minutes. Good tears. I checked."

"I would hope your professional instincts extend to distinguishing types of crying."

"It's taken years of practice." He said it lightly, but Xanthe heard the weight under it — the years of the other kind of practice, the practiced menace, the questions that weren't on any official form. They had talked about that winter often enough now that talking about it had worn smooth places in the pain, the way a riverbed stone goes smooth. But some parts never wore smooth. The chalk mark on the doorframe. The basement office. The handler's face as the delay clause burned.

"The letter came," Xanthe said.

Gideon turned. "From Tam?"

"Two days ago. He's teaching arithmetic. He has Mother's ring in a drawer and takes it out when he needs remembering." Xanthe recited the rest from memory, evenly, the way they had once recited clauses — and stopped at the last line, and could not be even about it after all. "He says the light reached them before they ever saw land. He means you, mostly. He means us. He—" Xanthe stopped, and looked out at the graying sea, and felt the year's whole weight arrange itself gently around this one small fact: that Tam was alive and safe and thinking of them, that the distance between them was water and paperwork and nothing else, that everything they had paid had bought precisely what they had paid it for.

Gideon put his hand over Xanthe's on the lamp housing. His palm was warm from the climb, rough with the pen calluses that had never gone away.

"He knows," Gideon said. "What you chose. What it cost."

"He knows better than anyone alive. That's what frightens me about him growing up — he'll understand everything. There's no lying to a boy who watched you give away the last berth on the last boat."

"Would you take it back? If you could stand at that dock again?"

Xanthe considered it honestly, because honesty between them had been earned at such a price that spending it cheaply would have been a desecration. The dock. The snow. Tam's thin wrists and his furious, hopeful eyes. Mother's ring, warm from her hand because she had pressed it there only minutes before, upstairs, in the bed where she had refused the berth with the same calm she had once refused second helpings.

"No," Xanthe said. "I'd do it slower. I'd hold his face a moment longer. But I'd do it." They turned their hand under Gideon's, laced fingers. "Would you tear up your exit papers again?"

"I tore them because leaving was the only thing I couldn't do and still be the person you'd decided to trust." He shrugged, the smallest shrug, the one that meant *this matters enormously*. "The papers were never the point."

Out past the point, the clouds they had been watching all evening arrived, folding the horizon away, and the first drops hit the lantern glass and ran down in long silver threads. Below them the sea went invisible. Somewhere out in all that darkness — Xanthe pictured it, could not stop picturing it — a boat was perhaps finding its way, a pilot leaning over wrong charts, a child asking how far, and above all of it one fixed light refusing to move.

* * *

**Gideon**

Later, when the rain had settled in for the night and the stairs were too dark to descend safely anyway, they spread blankets on the lantern room floor and shared bread from Marta Voss's flour, baked into flat rounds on the little iron plate Xanthe had carried up the cliff path on their back like a traitor to convenience. The lamp hummed above them. Rain drummed the dome of glass. From outside, Gideon knew, the room they sat in would look like a single warm star hanging on the black shoulder of the peninsula — the same sight, presumably, that had met the eyes of pilots and refugees and one woman on a Corvane-bound transport who told her story to a thin boy with his mother's ring in his pocket.

Gideon lay on his back with his hands laced behind his head and watched the light play across the ceiling, and conducted, as he did most nights now, his private accounting.

Before the war he had believed, with the whole smug certainty of a man good at his work, that a list was a form of power. You wrote down who belonged and who did not, who was home and who had fled and who lied about either, and the writing-down was itself a kind of owning. He had been very good at it. He had been good enough at it to terrify a courier with ink-stained fingers and a forgettable face, and good enough to know, even then, in the basement of himself where men keep what they cannot afford to display, that the terror he produced was a symptom of his own — that the watcher watching the Thornbury window across the square was watching the only life in Vessany he wished he could have lived.

Now the lists served differently. Now a list was a rope thrown across water. This morning he had reunited a father in the displaced registry with a daughter in the Corvane records — nine years old at separation, thirteen at reunion, a gap in a life the size of a war — and the man had gripped Gideon's hand hard enough to bruise and said nothing at all, because there was nothing language was equal to, and Gideon had walked home along the quay feeling the bruise like a medal.

"Your face is doing something," Xanthe said from beside him, not opening their eyes.

"Accounting."

"Balanced?"

"For today." He found Xanthe's hand in the blanket-dark. "Ask me on a bad day and get a different answer."

"There won't be bad days. We've used up our share."

Gideon laughed — really laughed, the unguarded bark of it that still surprised them both, because neither of them had laughed much in the years when laughter could have killed them — and Xanthe opened their eyes at the sound and looked at him with the expression that had, over the course of a winter and a spring, become the truest thing in Gideon's world: not desire exactly, and not merely affection, but *recognition*. Two liars at a bread line, each certain the other was the enemy, each correct, each wrong. Two people who had spent their whole lives being unreadable and had finally, impossibly, been read.

"The letter," Gideon said. "Read me the part about the ring again."

Xanthe recited it without the paper. *Someone loved me enough to stay behind.* Their voice caught on the last word, just barely, the same place it caught every time, and Gideon rolled toward them and kissed the corner of their mouth where the catch lived.

Outside, the rain thickened. Inside, the light burned.

Somewhere in the middle of the night Gideon woke to silence — the rain ended, the clouds torn open, stars out over a sea gone mirror-flat — and lay listening to Xanthe breathe and thinking about the question the boy had answered in his letter. Whether belonging was a place. Gideon had spent his career cataloguing addresses, and he knew now that he had never once catalogued the right thing. A house could be requisitioned. A district could be occupied. A country could exhale its last breath and be gone. But a lantern room with two blankets and a loaf of real bread, a hand that found yours in the dark without searching, a light kept burning on the exact spot where the old charts promised one —

Home was not the place you fled to. Home was the evidence you left behind that someone had refused to abandon you. It was written in light across the water, in lists kept faithful, in a ring in a drawer in another country taken out on hard evenings and held until morning.

He slept again. Toward dawn, the tide brought in a boat.

* * *

**Xanthe**

They heard it first as a sound out of place: an anchor chain, where there should be only water on rock. Then voices, thin with distance, and the creak of a hull kissing the old landing below the cliff. Xanthe was up the stairs from the sleeping loft before fully awake, coat over nightclothes, hair wild, taking the hundred and eleven steps at the speed of a person who had once run dispatches through occupied streets and had not entirely let that person go.

The boat was small — a fishing ketch with patched sails, riding low in the water. Eleven people crowded her deck, and even from the top of the cliff path Xanthe could see the shape of them: the shape that had become legible to them over this year the way other people learned to read weather. Exhaustion sitting on some, vigilance on others, and on two children wrapped in one blanket, a terrible unguarded sleep, the sleep of bodies that had finally been told they could.

Gideon came down the path behind Xanthe, lantern in hand, boots on the wet stone. Together they stood at the landing while the ketch's captain — a gray-braided woman with a fisherman's forearms and an administrator's tired eyes — called up in a hoarse voice:

"We're making for Corvane. Water's foul, food's gone, and the little ones need— we saw your light. Charts say this coast is dead."

"Charts are wrong," Xanthe called back. It was, they reflected, the sentence their entire life had been training them to say. "Come up. There's bread. There's a register if you're looking for people. There's—"

They stopped. At the rail, helping a girl over the side onto the landing stones, stood a young man Xanthe did not know — and beneath the exhaustion, beneath the salt-crusted coat, Xanthe saw it: ink stains on his fingers. Courier's ink. Dispatch blue, the shade that had once marked Xanthe's own hands in a mirror in a rented room in another life.

The young man looked up at the light burning in the tower above them, and then at the two figures at the landing, and something moved through his face — recognition of a kind, the look of a person meeting survivors of a shipwreck he thought he'd drowned in alone.

"They told me in Corvane there were still couriers on this coast," he said. "People who move things. Letters. Names." His voice shook and steadied itself with visible effort. "I have a list. Forty families scattered across four harbors. I've been carrying it for eight months and I don't know where to put it down."

Xanthe looked at Gideon. Gideon was already unrolling the oilcloth that protected the register, already clearing space on the landing's flat stone, already — in the methodical, unhurried way of a man who had found at last what his meticulousness was for — preparing to write down names.

"Put it here," Xanthe said, and took the list from his stained fingers, and above them, against the paling sky, the lantern burned on.
