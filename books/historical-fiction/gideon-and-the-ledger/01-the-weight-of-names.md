## 1. The Weight of Names

The trouble with war, Sebastian Halloran had decided somewhere around the third winter of it, was that it was terribly badly organized.

Not the fighting — the fighting, God help everyone, ran with the precision of a clockworks built by a madman. Bullets arrived on schedule. Ambulances did not. The wounded came in waves, and between the waves there was always this: the quiet hours, when a rear-echelon hospital at Saint-Ambrose-sous-le-Front settled into itself like an old dog by a fire, and the only sounds were the hiss of the stove, the moan of a man dreaming of home, and Sister Amity's clogs on the flagstones, keeping time with everything and agreeing with none of it.

Sebastian moved down Ward C with a lamp in one hand and a wet cloth in the other, and made their rounds the way they made all their rounds — thoroughly, silently, and slightly out of step with everyone else, because Sebastian had learned long ago that being overlooked was a kind of armor.

"Halloran." Private Denny Okafor, both legs bandaged to the hip, propped up on his elbows as Sebastian passed. "Tell me honest. Am I dying?"

"You have a splinter of shrapnel in your thigh the size of a plum stone, which we removed, and an infection we are winning against," Sebastian said. "You are in more danger from your own snoring than from anything the enemy has to offer. Corporal Weiss two beds down has lodged three formal complaints."

"He snores *himself*."

"And yet he complains. War makes strange alliances."

Denny laughed, then winced, then laughed anyway, because that was the sort he was. Sebastian wrung out the cloth, wiped his brow, and let him catch their wrist.

"Halloran. Honest, though. Will they write my name down? When the dispatch comes? My mum — she reads them all. Every list. She's got them pinned up on the kitchen wall, names of lads from our street." He said it lightly, the way soldiers said things that mattered most. "I'd hate for her to be reading lists I'm not on."

"You will be on the list," Sebastian said. "I'll see to it myself."

And there it was again, that small tightening behind the breastbone, the one that came every time Sebastian promised something they could not fully control. Dispatches were written by officers. Officers wrote what flattered them. A boy's name was only ever ink until someone with rank decided whether it deserved paper.

*History happens elsewhere,* Sebastian's old instructor used to say — meaning the great decisions, the treaties, the maps redrawn in rooms with carpets. It was meant as comfort. You, little nurse, need not carry the weight of it. But Sebastian had taken it differently. If history happened elsewhere, then here — in the wards, in the mud between the tents, in the space between a soldier's last breath and the officer's pen — here was where history was *lost*. And someone ought to be standing in that gap, picking up what fell through.

* * *

The ledger lived under the false bottom of Sebastian's instrument case, beneath the forceps and the rolled bandages, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. It was not a beautiful object. It had begun life as a stationer's sample book, half blank pages and half printed specimens of fine copperplate that no one at a field hospital would ever need — *Specimens of Commercial Hands, No. IV* — and Sebastian had won it off a quartermaster in a card game so one-sided that the quartermaster still, two years later, greeted them with narrowed eyes and the word *"cheat"* delivered with genuine warmth.

Into it, in a hand much neater than anyone would expect of a person who slept four hours a night, Sebastian wrote the names.

Tonight there were three.

The first was Denny's — precautionary, since Denny was alive and likely to stay so, but Sebastian had learned not to trust the machinery of officialdom even with the living. The second was Corporal Ansel Marchetti, who had died on Tuesday of wounds to the abdomen and whose death, according to Captain Brice's morning report, had been recorded as "one (1) expired, name withheld pending confirmation." Pending confirmation. As if a man could be unconfirmed after eleven months of service, after letters home in a schoolboy's hand, after a sweetheart's photograph tucked into his cap band. Sebastian had confirmed him. Sebastian had held his hand while he went, and told him a lie about spring, which was the oldest lie in nursing and the kindest.

The third name was harder.

Private First Class Rowan Petty. No — not Petty. That was how the intake clerk had written it, mishearing, and Sebastian had spent a full hour of a very tired evening establishing that the boy's actual name was Rowan Petrosian, that his family had come over the mountains two generations back, that his grandmother called him Hrach by rights and "my sparrow" by love. He had died in the triage tent before dawn, seventeen years old at the outside, and no officer had troubled to get his name right even once.

Sebastian dipped the pen, and wrote:

*Rowan Petrosian, called Hrach, called sparrow by his grandmother Meline Petrosian, late of Harrow's Cross. Died 14th of the Frost Month, wounds of the chest, in the third triage tent, holding my hand. Asked if the sky was clear. It was not; I said yes. Seventeen years, or near enough. He laughed twice that evening — once at a joke of Denny Okafor's, once at nothing, which is the best kind of laugh.*

Then, as always, the small line beneath, the ritual line, the line Sebastian had written now one hundred and forty-one times:

*Recorded here because nowhere else.*

It was not heroism. Sebastian was firm with themselves on this point. Heroism happened under fire, forward of the wire, in places Sebastian had been carefully, deliberately kept away from for three years. What this was, was bookkeeping. The universe was sloppy; somebody had to keep the accounts.

There was warmth to be found in the practice, too, if one looked — and Sebastian had become an expert looker, because the alternative was despair and despair did not change dressings. There was warmth in Denny's jokes and Amity's terrible tea and the way the whole hospital smelled faintly of carbolic and bread, because the cook, bless her implacable heart, refused to surrender the ovens to the war. Warmth in the small stubborn dignities: a man washed properly before burial, a letter finished for a hand too ruined to hold a pen, a name spelled right.

"You're at it again."

Sebastian did not jump — jumping wasted candle — but closed the case a shade quickly, which amounted to the same confession.

Sister Amity stood in the doorway of the supply alcove, sixty years old, forearms like a stevedore's, expression arranged into its usual setting of mild disapproval that fooled nobody who'd seen her carry a dying man a quarter mile on her back. She looked at the instrument case. She looked at Sebastian. She said nothing about either, which was its own kind of eloquence.

"The stove in Ward B wants wood," she said. "And you want sleep, though you'll pretend otherwise, and I haven't the patience tonight to argue you into honesty."

"In a moment, Sister."

"Mm." She lingered. Her eyes went to the case once more, then to the window, where the eastern dark was beginning to thin at its edges. "Front's quiet," she said, in the tone of a woman who did not believe quiet, having been lied to by it before. "Too quiet, my mother would have said. Though my mother also said that about Tuesdays, so."

"I'll fetch the wood, Sister."

"See you do." At the door she paused, and her voice dropped into the register she reserved for truths. "Whatever you keep in there, Halloran — keep it dry. Paper rots. And this winter everything rots."

Then the clogs, fading down the corridor, keeping their own account of the hours.

* * *

The wood was where the wood always was, stacked under the lean-to between the laundry and the dead-house, and Sebastian carried an armload back through the cold, breath smoking, boots crunching the iron crust of the snow. The hospital sprawled before them in the grey almost-light: the long ward-sheds, the surgery hut with its one good window, the tents beyond, all of it hunched under the hills like something trying to make itself small.

From the ridge, on a clear day, you could see the front — a smear of smoke on the southern horizon that never quite went away, like a stain on a ceiling in a house you couldn't afford to leave. The papers called it the Kellin sector. The men who came back from it called it other things, quietly, and then changed the subject.

That was the arrangement of the world, as far as Sebastian had ever been permitted to see it: the war happened *there*, and the accounting of the war happened *here*, and the accounting had developed a curious habit of coming out shorter than the war. Men went in. Lists came out. The lists were lighter than the men. Nobody official seemed to find this troubling, and so Sebastian had appointed themselves the discrepancy — the difference between what happened and what was written, kept safe in a stolen sample book under a false bottom, in a hand neat enough that someday, someone might read it without a magnifying glass.

Someday. Sebastian believed in someday the way other people believed in weather. It would come or it wouldn't, and either way there was dressing to change.

Inside Ward B, Sebastian fed the stove, checked the two sleeping men nearest it, straightened a blanket that did not need straightening, and allowed themselves the small vanity of reviewing the night's entries one final time before sleep. Marchetti. Petrosian. One hundred and forty-three names now, counting Denny's hopeful one. Each with a date, a wound, a family where known, a detail — because details were the soul of proof, and proof, someday, would want a soul.

One hundred and forty-three. And those were only the ones Sebastian had personally witnessed or verified. Multiply by every hospital along every sector, by every column of refugees whose dead were never admitted anywhere at all, and the true ledger — the honest arithmetic of this war — would fill a cathedral.

Well. One began with a sample book and a stolen candle-end. Cathedrals were simply stones that had waited.

Sebastian banked the fire, set their boots by it to dry, and lay down on the narrow cot in the nurses' quarters with the instrument case tucked against the wall, within reach of one hand, as always. Outside, the wind worried at the tent lines and the sentry coughed at his post, and the great machinery of the war turned somewhere south, grinding out material for future entries.

Sleep came slowly, and it brought dreams of kitchens — always kitchens, warm ones, bread ones — which Sebastian chose to interpret as the mind's insistence that such places still existed somewhere, despite all evidence.

They woke, later, to Amity's clogs and the smell of burnt porridge and a grey dawn pressing at the shutters, and everything was ordinary, everything was exactly as they had left it, the whole careful world intact.

Except that when Sebastian crossed the yard to the laundry door at first light, taking the shortcut past the hospital's back entrance, they stopped.

There was a bundle on the step.

A child's blanket, army-grey with a general's star stitched at the corner — a general's star, which made no sense at all — wrapped around something small and breathing. And on top of the blanket, weighted down with a stone against the wind, a note in a hurried hand, three words long:

*Forget the face.*
