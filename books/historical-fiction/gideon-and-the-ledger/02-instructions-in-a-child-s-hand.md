## 2. Instructions in a Child's Hand

Sebastian Halloran had learned, over nine years of war, that dawn at a rear-echelon hospital smelled of three things: carbolic, cold porridge, and the particular loneliness of men who had survived the night and were only now beginning to be afraid of it. The first smell was Sebastian's to manage. The second belonged to the cook, Marta, who defended her porridge like a fortress. The third was nobody's, and so it drifted through the wards unclaimed, settling into the blankets of soldiers who stared at the ceiling and rehearsed the letters they would never send.

Sebastian rose before the third smell arrived. That was the arrangement: rise in the dark, boil water, count the bandages twice, and walk the perimeter of the building while the frost still held its shape—checking, always checking, that the world outside had not grown teeth overnight.

It was on this walk, at the back door where the slop buckets went out and the milk came in, that Sebastian found the child.

The door was ajar. It should not have been. Sebastian stood very still for a moment, one hand on the frame, running through the inventory of plausible explanations—a wind, an orderly gone out early, a cat with ambitions—and then pushed the door wide.

The child sat against the wall beneath the lean-to, tucked between a stack of firewood and a barrel that had once held pickled onions and now held nothing but regret. A boy, perhaps seven, perhaps younger; it was difficult to tell with children, who wore their ages loosely and grew out of them faster than anyone could keep track. He was wrapped in a blanket of astonishing quality—deep green wool, gold cord at the edge, the kind of blanket that had never in its life touched a hospital. His eyes were open. He did not cry. He looked up at Sebastian with the flat, patient attention of someone waiting for a train that has been delayed a long while.

"Good morning," Sebastian said, because one says good morning, even to mysteries.

The child considered this. "I'm hungry," he said.

Of all the words he might have chosen—the ones Sebastian would later imagine in the dark, *help me*, or *they left me*, or *don't let them find me*—he chose hunger. It was, Sebastian would come to understand, the most honest word available to him.

"You'd better come in, then," Sebastian said, and heard, even as the words left their mouth, the sound of nine careful years cracking down the middle like river ice.

* * *

Protocol, in the matter of found children, was clear enough that even the newest orderly could recite it. Unaccompanied minors were to be reported immediately to the commanding officer, Major Grell, who would refer the matter to the garrison provost, who would enter the child into the civilian rolls, which were kept by persons whose sympathies lay, as everything in this war lay, with whoever signed their pay. A child entered into the rolls became a line. Lines could be crossed out. Sebastian knew precisely how much weight a crossed-out line carried, because Sebastian spent every night writing lines of their own—the names officers omitted from dispatches, recorded in a ledger kept under the loose floorboard beneath cot fourteen, where the floor sagged just enough to make a secret comfortable.

Sebastian had built a life on the principle that the safest thing to be was unremarkable. A nurse of no faction and no family, competent, quiet, forgettable in the way that furniture is forgettable—useful precisely because no one looks at it twice. For nine years Sebastian had been invisible on purpose, and invisibility had kept them alive through three offensives, two changes of command, and one epidemic of typhus that had taken half the staff and, mercifully, none of the ledgers.

And now here was this child, sitting on a flour sack in the linen room, eating bread and dripping that Marta had brought without asking questions, because Marta's questions were reserved for matters involving her porridge.

"Slowly," Sebastian advised. "Bread swells."

The child slowed. His hands were small and clean, which was wrong—children who traveled did not have clean hands unless someone had cleaned them recently, and recently meant within hours, which meant someone had stopped along the road, deliberately, to wipe away something. Mud. Blood. Evidence.

The blanket was folded over him with military precision.

"Where did you come from?" Sebastian asked, gently, the way one asks about the weather.

The child chewed. Swallowed. "A car," he said. "Then the road. Then the door."

"Before the car?"

He shrugged, the universal answer of childhood, and ate his crusts, and looked around the linen room with frank curiosity, as though it were a place he had been told about. His gaze snagged briefly on Sebastian's face and stayed there a moment too long—not staring, exactly, but studying, the way one studies a portrait to decide whether one knows the subject.

"What?" Sebastian asked.

"Nothing," said the child. "You have kind eyebrows."

"I—" Sebastian touched one eyebrow, absurdly. "Thank you."

"Mama says eyebrows tell you who to trust before mouths do." He said it simply, without weight, the way children say the heaviest things. Then he yawned, enormously, and leaned against the flour sacks, and was asleep between one breath and the next, the way only children and cats can manage.

Sebastian sat on an upturned crate and watched him sleep and understood, with a clarity that felt like standing in cold water, that nothing about this was simple.

Someone had brought this child here on purpose. Someone had dressed him in a general's blanket and cleaned his hands and walked him to a hospital's back door in the last hour of dark, and had left him with instructions. Because there had been instructions. Sebastian had found them.

They were in the child's pocket, folded twice, written on paper far too good for a note and in handwriting far too controlled for haste:

*Forget the face.*

Four words. No name, no date, no signature. Sebastian read it six times while the child slept, and each time the words rearranged themselves into something worse. *Forget the face* was not a plea. Pleas asked for help. This was an order—an order issued to whoever found him, preemptively, by someone accustomed to being obeyed. It said: this child was never here. It said: whatever you saw, unwitnessed it.

And underneath the fear, quiet as a coal under ash, something else stirred in Sebastian's chest. Something that felt uncomfortably like recognition. Because Sebastian had spent nine years doing the opposite of forgetting, in secret, in a hand small enough to hide, and here was a person—seven years old, with kind opinions about eyebrows—who had been made into the same kind of entry Sebastian spent every night refusing to allow. A name omitted from a dispatch. A face ordered forgotten.

"Well," Sebastian said aloud, to the sleeping child, to the room, to the nine careful years lying in pieces on the floor. "That settles that, doesn't it."

* * *

The trouble with breaking protocol, Sebastian reflected through the long morning, is that it must be done in the gaps between people, and a hospital is mostly people.

Sebastian moved the boy three times before noon. First from the linen room to the convalescent ward's back corner, behind a screen usually reserved for the dying—which was grim logic, since no one looked behind those screens unless invited—but Private Ansel, who occupied the nearest bed and suffered from a head wound that had loosened his filter entirely, kept saying "There's a child back there" in the wondering tone of a man announcing weather, and so the boy was moved again, to the storeroom off the pharmacy, and then, when the pharmacist came early, to the attic above the laundry, which was hot, low-beamed, and reached by a stair everyone had agreed for years to pretend did not exist because climbing it was somebody else's job.

By afternoon the boy—still unnamed, still unentered in any roll—had eaten two meals, drunk an alarming quantity of Marta's barley water, and formed an alliance with the laundry women, who passed him sweets through the attic hatch with the conspiratorial ease of women who had smuggled letters, liquor, and one memorable goat past every regulation this army possessed. Sebastian did not ask what they had been told. The laundry women's discretion was legendary and entirely fictional, but their loyalty was real, and real loyalty does not require accurate stories.

"What do I call you?" Sebastian asked, perched on a beam with a cup of tea that had gone cold from neglect.

The boy thought about it seriously, chewing his lip. "Wren," he said at last.

"Wren. Is that your name?"

"It's a bird," he explained patiently, as though to someone very slow. "Birds are small and they get away. I'd like to be one of those, please."

"Please," Sebastian repeated, and laughed despite everything—a short, surprised laugh that hurt the ribs. "Well. Wren it is. Temporarily."

"When will it stop being temporarily?"

And there it was, the question Sebastian had been circling all day, delivered with a child's terrible aim. Sebastian looked at him—at the clean hands, the general's blanket, the four words folded in Sebastian's breast pocket like a splinter working toward the heart—and gave the truest answer available.

"When I've worked out how to keep you safe. Not before."

Wren nodded, satisfied, as though this were the sort of promise adults made every day and kept. Perhaps, Sebastian thought grimly, it was the sort of promise they ought to make more often.

The major would have to be handled. That was the arithmetic Sebastian ran through while pretending to restock bandages: Major Grell was a bureaucrat of the old school, more frightened of paperwork than of shells, and a report of a found child would travel upward within the hour, gathering endorsements and requisitions the way a rolling ball gathers string. By evening the boy would be a file. By week's end, God knew. And somewhere above it all, someone had already decided this child should be forgotten—which meant the machinery that processed reports and the machinery that issued orders like *forget the face* were, if not the same machine, at least driven by the same engine.

For the first time in nine years, Sebastian Halloran lied by omission to a superior officer. Major Grell, making rounds, paused at the end of the ward, sniffed the air—heavy with laundry steam, blessedly—and said, "Oddly domestic in here today, Halloran."

"The women are boiling the lice out of the winter blankets, sir. Smells like my grandmother's kitchen."

Grell shuddered at the memory of lice and moved on, and Sebastian stood among the cots with their heart going like a drummer boy's, understanding that the crack in the ice had opened all the way through. There was no version of this, now, in which Sebastian returned to being furniture. The child upstairs knew their eyebrows. The child downstairs—in every sense—was Sebastian's.

* * *

Night came, and with it the ritual.

Sebastian waited until the ward's breathing settled into its rhythms—Ansel muttering at his dreams, young Corporal Vey snoring with the enthusiasm of the truly exhausted—then knelt, eased up the board beneath cot fourteen, and took out the ledger.

It was a plain book, quarter-bound, bought three wars ago from a stationer who assumed it would hold recipes. Inside, in Sebastian's small square hand, it held everything the dispatches did not. Names. Dates. Circumstances, stated dryly, because grief reads better dry. Page after page of men the war had used up and the record had declined to mention.

Tonight Sebastian dipped the pen and hesitated over the blank line.

There was no protocol for this either. No regulation governed the recording of a living child whom no one was permitted to remember. But the ledger had never been only a record of the dead; it was a record of refusals. Every name in it was a small act of insubordination against forgetting, and if ever a creature needed inscribing against erasure, it was the boy asleep in the attic with a bird's name and a general's blanket.

Sebastian wrote:

*This morning, before light, a child was left at the south door. Seven years, perhaps. Clean hands, kind judgment of faces. Wrapped in a general's blanket. Note in the pocket: 'forget the face.'*

*I decline.*

The pen scratched. The ink dried. Somewhere above the ceiling beams, Wren slept, and below the floorboards the names slept, and outside the frost took hold again over a country where, in high offices with good stationery, the history of this war was being tidied for publication—loose threads trimmed, awkward pages removed, faces forgotten by decree.

Sebastian put the ledger back beneath the board and pressed it flat, and was straightening when boots sounded on the gravel outside—measured boots, unhurried, the boots of someone arriving rather than passing. Through the ward's dark window Sebastian watched a staff car idle at the gate, its lamps cutting two white swords through the fog, and saw by the markings on its door that it had come from headquarters itself.

Nobody drove out from headquarters at midnight to discuss porridge.

Sebastian pulled the floorboard down until it whispered shut, straightened apron and face into perfect unremarkability, and went to answer the knock.
