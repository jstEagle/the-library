## 3. The Adjutant's Questions

The child had been with Sebastian for two days when the motorcar came up the hospital road.

Sebastian heard it before they saw it — a sound like an animal too large for the landscape, grinding through the spring mud where nothing heavier than a mule cart had any business going. They were folding bandages at the receiving tent's front table, and their hands went still on the linen, the way a bird goes still when a hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

*No one drives to a rear-echelon hospital in spring mud unless someone has told them to.*

"Halloran." Sister Amity appeared at their elbow, smelling of carbolic and the boiled-sweet she was never without. Her voice was pitched low, conversational, entirely for Sebastian alone. "Gentleman at the gate says he's from headquarters. Says he wants a tour."

"At dawn?"

"He says he's been driving since dark. He says—" Amity paused, and the boiled sweet clicked against her teeth, which it only did when something was genuinely wrong. "He says he's looking for a child who may have wandered off from a refugee column. Polite as you please. He thanked me twice."

Sebastian folded the last bandage with more attention than the task deserved. Two days. The child — who ate like a small wolf and slept like one too, curled into the general's blanket with her back against the wall of the supply closet behind the linen store — had been fed, washed, taught the hand signals Sebastian used with the wounded who couldn't speak, and not once reported to anyone. Two days of a decision compounding interest.

"Wandered off," Sebastian said. "From a column. In war country. At seven years old."

"That's what he said."

"And headquarters sends a staff car and an adjutant to look for one lost child."

"I did wonder," Amity said dryly, "whether we'd all mislaid a prince."

*If only you knew how close.* Sebastian untied their apron. "Where is she?"

"Behind the flour sacks, reading your list of names. I gave her a biscuit and told her it was a very important game to be very quiet." Amity's eyes, pale as well-water, held Sebastian's. "I don't know what you've gotten yourself into, Halloran. But I've been a nurse for thirty years, and I know what it looks like when a man asks questions he already knows the answers to. So I'll say this once: whatever you're doing, do it boringly. Nothing survives boredom."

It was, Sebastian would think later, the wisest thing anyone ever said to them, and they failed to follow it within the hour.

* * *

Adjutant Corvane was not what Sebastian expected, which was itself unsettling; Sebastian had expected either a thug or a fop, and got instead neither. He was perhaps forty, tall without being dramatic about it, dressed in a uniform so correct it seemed less worn than installed. His face was pleasant the way a locked door is pleasant — smooth, symmetrical, communicating nothing except its own closedness. He stood at the gate holding his cap and thanking Private Doss for directions that Doss had plainly invented.

"Halloran?" Corvane extended a hand. His grip was dry, brief, calibrated. "Adjutant Corvane, Fourth Division headquarters. I'm grateful to you for the time. I know how scarce it is back here."

"We manage." Sebastian heard their own voice come out level, unhurried, the voice they used for delivering bad news to strangers, and was obscurely grateful to it. "How can the hospital help headquarters?"

"A missing child." Corvane produced a notebook but did not open it, which Sebastian understood immediately to be a performance; a man like this remembered everything he needed. "A girl, approximately seven. She became separated from a civilian column near Marle ten days ago. Her family is — understandably anxious. We've been checking every facility along the corridor." He smiled, and the smile reached every feature of his face individually, in order, like soldiers presenting arms. "Yours is the fourth hospital. May I walk through?"

"Of course." Sebastian began walking before their heart could finish its next beat. *Boringly,* Amity had said. "We're mostly surgical here. You'll want to excuse the smell."

"The smell of healing," Corvane said pleasantly. "Not at all."

He walked through the receiving tent with his hands clasped behind him, pausing here and there to compliment a clean instrument tray or ask a convalescent private whether his wound was treated promptly, and each pause lasted exactly long enough to seem courteous and no longer. Sebastian trailed him, narrating bed counts and supply statuses, and discovered that terror, fully arrived, felt remarkably similar to concentration. It focused the mind wonderfully. They had never noticed so much in their own hospital: the gap under the surgery table where a cat sometimes hid, the loose board by the dispensary, the way Corporal Enns slept with one eye open and would sell anything to anyone.

Corvane stopped at the laundry line.

"You have children here?" he asked.

Sebastian's stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution. Then they followed his gaze and saw what he saw: small clothes drying between the sheets. A shirt sized for a boy of eight, patched at the elbow. Socks far too small for any soldier.

"Laundry comes in from the village school," Sebastian said. "The schoolhouse was requisitioned in March. The matron washes for us; we wash for her students. It's cheaper than coin." This was true. It had been true since March. Sebastian had never once been grateful for it until now.

"Efficient," Corvane said. "And humane. One doesn't often find both in the same ledger."

*Ledger,* thought Sebastian, and kept their face still through what felt like a full minute and was probably two seconds.

He completed the tour in eleven minutes. At the gate he stopped, turned back, and said, "You didn't ask me her name."

Sebastian blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"The missing child. Most people ask the name first. You asked how the hospital could help headquarters." He was smiling again, mildly, with real warmth in it, or something wearing warmth's coat. "I find I notice things like that, Nurse Halloran. Occupational habit. Names are my business — I keep the general's correspondence, you understand, and a name out of place is like a tooth out of line. Small thing. Everything notices."

"If you'd told me the name," Sebastian said, "I'd have told you we haven't seen her. We see four hundred men a week, Adjutant. Children come through with refugee columns and go on again. If one had stayed, Sister Amity would have reported her, because protocol exists precisely so that people like me don't have to make decisions." They heard themselves say it and marveled somewhere underneath the words: *that is a lie. Every clause of it is a lie, delivered smoothly, to an officer, in wartime.* Their careful life — the life of signatures in the proper place and names spelled correctly and grief recorded privately, invisibly, where no one could punish it — tilted on its axis and did not fall.

Corvane studied them for a moment. Then he laughed — a short, genuine, startlingly human laugh — and put his cap back on.

"Well," he said. "I believe you. Isn't that funny? I believe you absolutely." He reached into his breast pocket and produced a card, cream-colored, heavy stock, and held it out. "Headquarters will be expanding its medical administration after the war. Records, chiefly. There'll be a senior post — registrar of field casualties, good pay, a desk in a building that isn't leaking, and the extraordinary luxury of never having to hold anyone down while the morphine goes in. I've read your file, Halloran. Your penmanship alone qualifies you. Think about it. When I return this way — and I return this way often — I'd take your answer gladly."

Sebastian took the card because refusing it would have been interesting, and interesting was fatal.

"Thank you, sir," they said.

"One more thing." Corvane settled the cap. His voice remained perfectly pleasant; that was the horror of it, that nothing about him ever changed register, so that menace and courtesy became the same weather. "If a child does turn up — wanderers do, they turn up in chimneys, in ditches, in the backs of carts, it's remarkable — the kind thing, truly the kindest thing, is to send word to me first. Before the commandant, before the chaplain, before anyone. Because families are suffering, Halloran, and bureaucracy is slow, and I am not." Another smile, complete and orderly. "Forget the paperwork. Come to me directly. Would you do that? As a favor between people who understand records?"

"I would do that," Sebastian said.

"Lovely," said Corvane, meaning none of the words, and drove away through the mud, leaving ruts like a signature.

* * *

That night the hospital ran its usual gauntlet — a convoy at dusk, nine men off a train, two of them shouting in fevers, one of them a boy of nineteen asking repeatedly whether anyone had written to his mother and being assured, repeatedly, by everyone, that yes, yes, of course, first thing tomorrow — and it was past eleven before Sebastian made the rounds of their private rituals. Lamp trimmed. Instruments counted. And then, last, the linen store, where they sat on a crate with the ledger on their knees and added the day's names in pencil: Private Ansel Doory, died of his wounds at 6:40 in the evening, though the dispatch would record only "casualties consistent with engagement strength"; Gunner Pell, whose name the sergeant had misspelled on the ward book and whom Sebastian had therefore written correctly, because somewhere a mother owned a Bible with that spelling in it.

Wren watched from her nest of blankets, knees hugged to her chest, the way she always watched — solemn, unblinking, cataloguing. Two days old to Sebastian's care and already she had learned the hospital's rhythms, when to be quiet, when the screaming meant pain and when it meant fear and when it meant nothing at all.

"Is that the book of dead mens?" she asked.

"Their names," Sebastian said. "It's a book of names."

"Why?"

And there it was, the question Sebastian had answered for themselves a hundred times in the dark and never once aloud. They looked at the child — at the face the note had ordered forgotten, sharp-chinned, grave-eyed, a smudge of biscuit at the corner of her mouth — and understood with sudden, nauseating clarity that the adjutant had believed them today exactly as long as it suited him. A man who offered a promotion before finding proof was a man assembling proof. The tour had taken eleven minutes. He had looked at everything.

"Because," Sebastian said slowly, "when people try to pretend something didn't happen, writing it down is how you refuse. That's all the book is. A refusal."

Wren considered this with the seriousness of seven years. "Does it work?"

"It worked today," Sebastian said. "A man came asking after you, little wren. And I lied to him."

"To keep me?"

"To keep you."

She nodded, satisfied, and burrowed deeper into the blanket. "Good. Mam said you'd come. She said wait for the one who writes names."

Sebastian's pencil stopped above the page.

"What else did your mother say?"

But Wren was already asleep — children sleep like falling, all at once, mid-sentence — and Sebastian sat in the lamplight with the ledger open on their knees and the cold arriving through the stone at their back, understanding that the question had just changed shape entirely. Not *how do I hide a child.* Something older and heavier, pressing up through the floorboards of the world:

*What did her mother know? And who, at headquarters, was afraid of it?*

Tomorrow, Sebastian decided, they would open the ledger properly — not adding to it, but reading backward, entry by entry, name by name — because a registrar-to-be keeps records, and somewhere in those pages, if the child was right to wait for them, was the reason a general's daughter had been left at a hospital door with instructions to forget her face.

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off toward the front, the guns kept their own kind of ledger, and Sebastian blew out the light and sat a while longer in the dark, no longer invisible even to themselves.
