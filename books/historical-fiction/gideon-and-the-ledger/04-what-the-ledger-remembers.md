## 4. What the Ledger Remembers

The hospital slept the way old soldiers slept—badly, and in shifts. Somewhere down the corridor a man with a fever muttered his sister's name over and over, as if repetition could summon her through the dark. The lamps had been trimmed low for the night, and their light lay along the records room shelves like something poured.

Sebastian Halloran sat at the small table in the corner with a candle of their own, because the ledger deserved better than borrowed light.

It had been four days since the child arrived at the back door wrapped in a general's blanket. Four days of hiding Wren in the linen store between Sister Amity's inspection rounds, of teaching the boy to fold bandages badly and to whistle only when whistled to, of feeling—every time boots rang on the gravel outside—the particular cold that lives at the base of the spine. But tonight the hospital was quiet, the child was asleep under three blankets with one ear cocked even in dreaming, and Sebastian had run out of reasons not to do what they had been avoiding since dawn.

They were going to read the ledger properly. Not as ritual. As evidence.

For six years the book had been a devotion, not a document. Every night, after rounds, Sebastian wrote down the names the dispatches left out. The officers' reports said *casualties light*; the ledger said otherwise. It said Private Ansel Mott, nineteen, who died asking whether anyone had seen his gloves. It said Corporal Devereaux, who was buried twice because the first grave flooded. It said the things that did not survive translation into official language: the smell of a field hospital in August, the way a man's hand goes heavy before the rest of him does.

Sebastian had never thought of it as dangerous. Dangerous things were loud. This was quiet—a slim volume bound in canvas reclaimed from a stretcher, kept beneath a loose floorboard under the cot, written in a hand small enough to be mistaken for nothing at all. History happened elsewhere, in ministries and headquarters tents, among men who used words like *annihilation* and *decisive engagement* without ever smelling either one. Sebastian had made peace with that. Had built a whole life on it, in fact, like building a house on a hill far from the flood line.

Then a child had washed up against that house with a note pinned to him saying *forget the face*, and the flood had begun moving uphill.

Sebastian unlocked the drawer, lifted the floorboard, and set the ledger on the table beside the candle.

"Right," they murmured to it. "Let's see what you know."

The early entries were almost embarrassingly innocent. The first page, from six years ago, recorded a single name in handwriting so careful it looked printed: *Jem Harlow, shot for straggling; report lists him as died of dysentery.* Sebastian remembered being furious about Jem Harlow—not hot fury, but the slow kind, the kind that curdles into method. They had stolen the canvas that same week.

The pages turned. Names accumulated. The war grew teeth as the entries went on, and the omissions grew larger too, until entire engagements were described in dispatches with numbers that could not possibly be reconciled with the number of men Sebastian had personally watched stop breathing. There was a grim arithmetic to it. Dispatch arithmetic. Subtract the shameful, round down the dead, carry the glory forward.

Sebastian reached the autumn two years past and slowed.

There were three entries clustered within a week of each other, all marked with the same place name, and Sebastian's own marginal notes—written in the urgent shorthand of someone recording faster than comfort allowed—suddenly read less like grief and more like testimony.

> *Kellin Bridge, third day of the retreat. Men brought in say the bridge was held, but ask them how and they stop answering. One boy—I think he was a boy, fourteen maybe, wearing his dead brother's coat—kept saying "we pushed them onto it" before the fever took the words and then him. Recorded: Pvt. Emeric Soll (d.), unrecorded: the boy, whose name no one knew.*

> *Kellin Bridge again. Wagonloads, not walking wounded—wagonloads. A sergeant with his hands bandaged told me the crowd on the bridge was not soldiers. He said it twice, very carefully, the way men say things they will later deny having said. Then he asked me to write down that he had said it, and then he asked me to cross it out, and I did neither. I am writing this instead.*

> *Third entry, Kellin Bridge. Forty-one names collected from the dying so far. Officers' dispatch reports the action as a heroic stand, General Vane commended, bridge "denied to the enemy at great cost." The cost I have counted is not the cost they mean. Collected names appended. Also appended: order papers found on a dead courier, signed A.V., instructing that "the crossing be cleared of all civilian obstruction by any means." By any means. His own phrase, copied here exactly.*

Sebastian sat back.

The candle guttered and steadied. Down the corridor, the fevered man had finally gone silent, or asleep, or somewhere in between.

*A.V.* Aldous Vane. The name was on every wall in three provinces, chiseled above the word *Savior of Kellin Bridge*. Schoolchildren could recite the story: the general, outnumbered, holding the crossing with a handful of veterans while the enemy streamed toward him, turning retreat into legend. There was a painting. In the painting Vane stood tall on the span, sword raised, sky behind him arranged in suitably dramatic golds.

No civilians in the painting. Sebastian had looked, once, standing in the provincial capital between train changes, and there were no civilians in it anywhere. Just soldiers and sky and glory.

And now, in the linen store of a rear-echelon hospital, slept a seven-year-old wrapped in a blanket embroidered with the Vane crest, delivered with instructions to forget his face.

"Oh," Sebastian said quietly, to nobody, which was how they said most things. "Oh, you clever, terrible man."

Because it fit. It fit with the awful click of a lock whose key has been in plain sight all along. A general rewriting the war's ending needed more than dispatches—he needed no loose threads. And what thread is looser than a bastard son? What proof of a man's private life more inconvenient than a child with his jawline? Sebastian had assumed the note meant the boy was embarrassing. Shameful. A scandal to be hushed.

But scandals get paid off. Scandals get sent to distant aunts in distant provinces. You do not pin a note to a scandal's coat that reads like an instruction to an assassin—or worse, like a plea—and abandon it at a hospital door in a war zone.

Unless the face itself was evidence.

Sebastian turned back to the third Kellin Bridge entry and read the appended names. Forty-one. Then, further down the page, in ink slightly different because the candle had been replaced mid-entry, a forty-second line:

> *Names of the living who saw, kept separately: Sgt. Bram Wilcott (m.u.), Cpl. Tessa Rell (transferred), Pvt. Odile Ferran (m.w.—whereabouts unknown). If anyone ever asks, these are the ones who can say it was not heroism. God help whoever asks.*

Sebastian had written those lines two years ago as a kind of private prayer, filed under *unlikely*. Now the prayer had a deadline. Historians were coming with the armistice—everyone whispered it—to write the war's official account, and General Vane's faction intended to hand them a finished manuscript with the massacre sanded out of it. The witnesses would die of fevers and misadventure. The order papers would burn, conveniently, in some records-room fire. The boy would be forgotten so thoroughly it would be as if he had never drawn breath on a bridge his father had cleared with fire.

The child wasn't just a charge. He was the last living exhibit.

Sebastian pressed the heel of a hand against their eyes and laughed once, short and disbelieving. Six years of believing history happened elsewhere. Six years of thinking a ledger full of dead men's names was a shield, a private chapel, a way of staying clean at one remove. And all along they had been sitting on the one object in the province that powerful men would burn a hospital to destroy. The flood hadn't come uphill. Sebastian had been living in the riverbed the entire time, calling it a hill.

* * *

Wren appeared in the doorway sometime past two in the morning, dragging his blanket, hair standing in directions hair had no business achieving.

"You're still up."

"I'm working." Sebastian closed the ledger on reflex, then felt foolish—could the boy read cursive by candlelight from across a room? Could anyone?—"Go back to bed."

"I heard the paper." Wren came closer, peering at the table with the frank curiosity of children and cats. "What is it?"

"It's a list."

"I hate lists." He considered. "Except the ones with my name."

Something went sideways in Sebastian's chest. "Your name isn't in it."

"Could it be?" He climbed onto the bench opposite, folded his arms, and fixed Sebastian with a look of tremendous seriousness, the sort adults usually reserved for bank negotiations. "You should put my name in it. If it's important."

"It's not—it isn't that kind of list, Wren."

"What kind is it?"

And there it was, the question, arriving at two in the morning from a person who still tied his laces wrong. Sebastian looked at the ledger, and at the boy, and understood that lying to him would require a skill Sebastian had only just discovered four days ago and did not wish to practice.

"It's a list of people who are in danger of being forgotten," Sebastian said. "I write down their names so that somebody, someday, knows they were real."

Wren absorbed this with the gravity it deserved, which was more gravity than most adults gave it. "Did you forget anybody?"

"No. That's the point. That's the entire point."

"Then it's a good list." He nodded once, decisively, as though the matter had been ruled upon by a competent court. Then, yawning: "My mother kept a list too. Of songs. She said if she wrote them down they couldn't take them." He slid off the bench, trailing blanket. "She was mostly wrong about things like that. But she kept it anyway."

He padded back down the corridor. Sebastian listened until the linen store door clicked shut.

*So they couldn't take them.* A woman with a song-list and a general with a burning order and a nurse with a stolen piece of stretcher canvas, all keeping records against the dark for exactly the same reason and entirely different ends. Sebastian wondered, not for the first time and not gently, what had become of the mother. The boy spoke of her the way soldiers spoke of home—frequently, briefly, and with a studied lightness that fooled no one who had learned to read the pauses.

* * *

By lamplight's last inch, Sebastian did something they had never done in six years of keeping the ledger.

They read it as an enemy would.

Every entry about Kellin Bridge. Every name of every witness, marked alive or dead or whereabouts unknown. Every copied signature, every date, every scrap of corroborating detail that Sebastian had recorded out of stubbornness rather than strategy. Taken as devotion, the ledger was a memorial. Taken as evidence, it was a case file—and a damning one, assembled by accident, organized by nobody, indexed by grief.

Aldous Vane had ordered civilians driven onto a bridge so that its destruction could be blamed on the enemy and its defense turned into scripture. Sebastian had forty-one names that said otherwise, plus a sergeant, a corporal, and a private whose whereabouts were unknown, plus a boy asleep thirty feet away whose existence contradicted the official version in ways no dispatch could argue with. The child's face was the one document Vane could not forge and therefore had to erase.

And Sebastian Halloran—quiet, careful, invisible Sebastian Halloran—was currently sitting on all of it, in a hospital with a chaplain who reported upstairs, waiting for the adjutant to come back with better questions.

"We are in trouble," Sebastian told the ledger conversationally. "Deep, historic, archival trouble. The kind they teach schoolchildren about, eventually, if we win."

The ledger, being a book, said nothing, which Sebastian found restful. Most of the best conversations of their life had been conducted this way.

But rest was a luxury with a shelf life, and the shelf was tilting. Corvane would return. The chaplain's sermons had already begun aiming themselves. And somewhere in a headquarters tent, a general who had turned a massacre into a monument was wondering why a nurse hadn't yet handed over his son.

Sebastian blew out the candle and knelt to return the ledger to its hiding place—and stopped, hands on the canvas cover, struck by a thought so obvious it must have been hiding behind all the others, embarrassed to speak up.

The floorboard was the first place anyone would look. Not tonight, perhaps, but soon. A book this valuable needed to be somewhere no one would search—which meant, logically, it needed to be duplicated, carried, dispersed, made un-erasable. Which meant trust. Which meant people. Which meant everything Sebastian had spent six years avoiding.

"Well," Sebastian said to the dark, wry despite everything, "isn't this a fine education for a person who only wanted to be left alone."

They slid the ledger under the floorboard, pressed the wood home, and lay listening to the hospital breathe. Outside, far off toward the front, artillery muttered in its sleep like the whole war dreaming of its own ending.

Two years ago, in a different ink, Sebastian had written a prayer into the margins: *if anyone ever asks.* Someone was going to ask. Soon. With authority.

And when they did, the answer would have to travel—out of this hospital, past Corvane's couriers, all the way to Verenne, to the neutral archive, to the historians before the record closed.

Sebastian stared at the ceiling and began, unwillingly, to count allies.

The count did not take long.
