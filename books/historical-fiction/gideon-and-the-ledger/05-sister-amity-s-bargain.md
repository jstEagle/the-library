## 5. Sister Amity's Bargain

Sister Amity had been a head nurse for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned three things with absolute certainty: that fever always broke before dawn or after noon, never in between; that the men who complained loudest about their wounds were always the ones who would outlive her; and that nothing, absolutely nothing, stayed hidden in a hospital for longer than it took a rumor to walk the length of one corridor on two broken legs.

So when little Orderly Pell came sidling up to her at the medicine cupboard on Tuesday morning, wearing the expression of a boy who had been told to carry a message and was hoping the message would dissolve before it reached its destination, Amity set down her mortar and pestle and said, "Out with it, then."

"Nothing, Sister."

"Pell. You have the face of a man who has seen a ghost and taken notes."

He glanced up and down the ward — empty but for Halloran's sleeping fevers and one snoring sergeant who could have slept through the Last Trump — and lowered his voice to something barely above the sound of the wind worrying at the shutters. "It's just... there's a child in the linen store. Behind the third shelf. Been there since Sunday, they're saying. Halloran's been feeding it porridge out of the invalid bowl."

"A child," Amity repeated.

"Yes, Sister. A girl, maybe. Small. It's got a blanket with gold braid on it, which is how you know it isn't anybody's from around here, because nobody around here owns anything with gold braid unless they stole it off a corpse, and nobody steals off corpses in this hospital because Halloran would know." He paused, gathering courage for his final report. "Also it sneezed."

"Sneezed."

"In a very incriminating manner, Sister."

Amity thanked him, told him to go fetch clean water and stop looking like a conspirator because he did it terribly, and stood for a moment with her hand flat on the medicine shelf, thinking.

A child. Since Sunday. Hidden by Sebastian Halloran — of all people. Halloran, who corrected other nurses' charting in pencil so gently the correction looked like an apology; who had once refused, politely and immovably, to let a colonel's aide take morphine stocks "off the books," and had stood there with that mild, unyielding face until the aide went away and did not come back. Halloran did not break rules. Halloran bent them, occasionally, the way a willow bends in wind, and only ever toward mercy.

Well. Amity had been a nurse long enough to know that some rules deserved bending, and some children deserved porridge out of the invalid bowl.

She finished her rounds first. That was not stalling; it was simply that a head nurse who abandoned her rounds for anything short of fire was a head nurse whose authority leaked away like a poorly bandaged wound. She changed the sergeant's dressing, listened to his chest, endured his joke about the army's idea of a mattress, and promised him, as she promised every man, that she would pass on his regards to no one at all, since his regards were always to someone he oughtn't be writing to.

Then she washed her hands, tied her apron, and went to the linen store.

* * *

The linen store smelled of soap and camphor and, faintly, of porridge. Amity closed the door behind her, waited for her eyes to adjust, and said to the third shelf, in the voice she used for waking patients gently:

"I know you're there. Both of you."

Silence. The particular quality of silence that meant breathing had stopped.

"It's all right," she said. "I've come alone. Nobody followed me, and I sent Pell to the far ward with instructions to be useless there for an hour, which is his natural condition."

There was a rustle, a soft thump of small feet, and then Sebastian Halloran rose from behind the shelving with a child attached to their skirt like a burr that had decided, after long deliberation, that this particular plant would do. Sebastian's face was composed — it was always composed; Amity suspected the face had been assembled from spare parts belonging to somebody much calmer — but their hands were folded too tightly, and their eyes went past Amity to the door, counting exits.

"Sister," Sebastian said. "If you're here to—"

"I'm here to see what has been living behind my linens for two days and eating my invalid porridge," Amity said. "Move aside, and let me look at her."

The child was small even for seven, which was Amity's guess at her age; thin in the way of children who had recently learned that food was a thing that arrived rather than a thing that existed; dark-eyed, watchful, with a smudge of porridge at the corner of her mouth that she was clearly pretending did not exist. She wore clothes that had been good ones once, cut down by someone who could sew but had been hurried, and around her shoulders — clutched now, both fists full of the wool as though someone might try to pry it loose — was a blanket with a general's gold braid at the hem.

And under the grime, under the wariness, under the terrible stillness of a child who had learned that stillness was safety, there was a face. Amity looked at it for a long moment and understood, with a cold settling in her stomach that had nothing to do with the season, why someone had wrapped this particular face in a general's blanket and left it at a hospital door with instructions to forget it.

"Well," Amity said briskly, because briskness was a tool like any other in her kit, and now was the hour for it. "She's half-starved, she's got a cough coming, and she hasn't been washed properly in a week. Halloran, what in God's name have you been doing?"

Sebastian's chin came up a fraction. "Keeping her alive."

"Yes, and doing it badly, because you can't do this alone and you knew it, which is why you've been walking through the wards these two days looking like a woman carrying water in a sieve." Amity crouched down to the child's level, unhurried, letting the child study her the way children studied everything now — for signs. "What's your name, love?"

The child looked at Sebastian. Sebastian looked at Amity. Some negotiation occurred across that glance, conducted entirely in eyebrows, and apparently the terms were favorable, because the child said, in a voice rusty from disuse:

"Wren."

"Wren," Amity repeated. "Good strong name. I knew a wren once that nested in my mother's boot for a whole spring. Stubborn little creature. Built its house where nothing could reach it and sang anyway." She held out her hand, palm up, and waited. She was very good at waiting. Nineteen years of night shifts taught a person to wait the way stones teach rivers. "I'm Amity. I'm the one who runs this place when everyone senior is pretending to run it from headquarters. Which means the stores, the schedules, the laundry, and the comings and goings at every door all answer to me. If anyone is going to hide in my hospital, it will be done properly or not at all."

Wren looked at the offered hand. Then, with the gravity of a magistrate, she put her own small one into it.

* * *

They moved Wren within the hour — properly, as Amity had promised — from the linen store to the small room off the dispensary where recovering orderlies napped between shifts. It had one window, high and narrow, that looked onto the herb garden and nothing else; it had a door that locked from the inside; and most importantly it sat directly across the passage from Amity's own quarters, so that anyone approaching it would first have to walk past a head nurse who slept badly, woke instantly, and kept a candle stub and a rolling pin by her bed, the latter acquired during a supply crisis and retained ever since for reasons she described as sentimental.

While Wren slept — truly slept now, the boneless sleep of a child who had decided, provisionally, that the room's two adults could be trusted with her unconscious body — Amity and Sebastian sat on either side of the narrow bed like bookends, and Amity said:

"Now. Tell me all of it. From the beginning, and don't leave out the parts you think will frighten me. At my age, Halloran, very little frightens me, and what does frighten me is usually the part somebody tried to spare me."

So Sebastian told it: the back door at dawn, the note, the blanket. *Forget the face.* The decision — made in perhaps ten seconds, Sebastian admitted, ten seconds that had reorganized their entire understanding of themselves — to say nothing to the commanding officer. The days of concealment. And then, hesitating over it as though the words weighed more than the child did, Sebastian reached into their tunic and brought out a small, much-handled book, bound in scuffed leather, and laid it on the counterpane.

"You should know what else I keep," Sebastian said.

Amity read it while Sebastian watched her — or didn't quite watch her; watched the window instead, the way a person watches a door when they are expecting either absolution or arrest. Page after page of names. Not the names from the dispatches — Amity knew those, everyone knew those, they were read aloud at staff meetings in a tone reserved for inventory — but the other names. Men listed as missing who had died. Men listed as deserters who had died holding a line nobody official would admit existed. Dates, wounds, last words where there were last words, next-of-kin where there were next-of-kin, and where there were none, small annotations: *wrote letters himself, kept them in his left breast pocket. No one to send them to.*

At Kellin Bridge the entries changed. They stopped being soldiers' names.

Amity closed the book and sat with her hand on the cover for a moment, feeling the weight of it differently than she had felt the weight of any ledger in nineteen years of nursing administration.

"My sons," she said, "were called Edmund and Silas."

Sebastian went very still.

"Edmund died at Harrow Fen in the second year. Officially. There is a stone with his name on it, and a letter that arrived four months later, written by a chaplain who never met him, describing his 'peaceful end.' Silas died in the third year at a place that does not appear on any map I've ever been shown, and his name does not appear on any list, because the action he died in was never acknowledged. I wrote eleven letters asking. I received two replies, neither of which answered me, and one of which suggested, kindly, that I might be confusing him with another soldier of similar name." Her voice stayed level. Nineteen years of night shifts taught a voice to stay level. "I stopped writing letters and started keeping this hospital running, because grief that has nowhere to go will eat a woman alive unless she gives it work."

Sebastian's hand came to rest, very lightly, on the ledger. "Sister—"

"Don't thank me yet," Amity said. "Because here is my bargain, and it has teeth in it. First: I help you. Properly. Wren eats from the kitchen, not from stolen bowls; she is entered nowhere, but she is known to me, to Pell, and to old Marta in the laundry, who is deaf in one ear and selectively deaf in the other, which makes her the safest confidante in this province. Second: you tell me everything from now on. No more solitary heroics, Halloran. I've buried enough lone brave people. Third—" and here she leaned forward, and let the level voice drop to something quieter and harder, "—you keep that book away from Father Merrin."

"The chaplain?" Sebastian frowned. "Merrin does his rounds, blesses the dying—"

"Merrin does his rounds, and then once a week he takes the afternoon coach to headquarters, and he does not visit the cathedral when he gets there. He visits an office. I know because I was in that office once, in the first year, asking after Silas, and I saw Merrin's coat on the rack and heard his voice through the partition, cheerful as Sunday, giving somebody a report on morale in the rear hospitals." She tapped the counterpane with one finger. "Whatever Corvane is, whatever he wants with this child, he will not need to search this hospital. He will ask Merrin. And Merrin will tell him, not out of wickedness — Merrin believes, you understand, he sincerely believes that peace is coming and that remembering is the disease and forgetting the cure — but out of faith, which is worse, because faith cannot be bribed or frightened. Only avoided."

Outside the window the light was going amber over the herb garden. Somewhere down the corridor the sergeant was snoring again, a homely, ordinary sound, and Wren turned in her sleep and pulled the general's blanket higher under her chin.

"So we avoid him," Sebastian said.

"We avoid him," Amity agreed. "We are pleasant to him, we take his blessings, we compliment his sermons, and we tell him nothing. And Halloran—" she paused, and the humor that lived permanently at the corner of her mouth, dry as good sherry, surfaced for a moment. "You should also know that your porridge theft has been noticed. Invalid bowl number six has been missing two days. Cook believes the ghost of a hungry corporal is haunting us, and has begun leaving out extra bread for it. I have not corrected her."

For the first time in two days — perhaps, Amity suspected, for the first time in years — Sebastian Halloran laughed. It was a small laugh, rusty as Wren's voice, but real.

"There," Amity said, satisfied. "That's better. Now kiss the child goodnight, go sleep in an actual bed, and tomorrow we begin doing this properly." She rose, gathered her keys, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch. "Oh — and one more item for your consideration. Merrin takes his coach Thursday. But this evening he asked me, ever so casually, whether the hospital census had changed lately. Whether we'd taken in anyone unusual."

She saw the fear move through Sebastian like weather, quick and cold, and she met it steadily, the way she had met nineteen years of nights.

"He's already asking, Halloran," Amity said quietly. "Which means we have perhaps two days to make sure that when he stops asking and starts telling — the only thing he can tell Corvane is nothing at all."
