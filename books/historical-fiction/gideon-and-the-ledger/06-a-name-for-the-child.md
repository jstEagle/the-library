## 6. A Name for the Child

The child had been with us nine days before I realized I had learned her footsteps.

That is the sort of thing a nurse notices about patients — the drag of a bandaged foot, the hitch in a gait that says the morphine has worn off — and I told myself that was all this was. Clinical observation. The soft, quick step on the stairs to the linen loft at six each morning, pausing on the fourth stair because the fourth stair creaked, meant I had a small shadow who had already mapped the hospital better than some of the orderlies.

Her name, as far as anyone knew, was nothing. The note had said *forget the face*, and whoever wrote it had not troubled themselves with a name, which told me something about how they thought of her: as an object to be misplaced, like a key or a letter. I could not call her *nothing*. So I called her Wren, because she was small and brown-eyed and made a great deal of noise for her size when she decided the porridge was an insult.

"You're frowning at it," she informed me that morning, "like it owes you money."

"Where did you hear that?"

"Tomas says it about his boots."

Of course he did. Tomas Reyes could make friends with a fence post, and apparently had. This was the trouble with letting people near you: they leaked into the child's vocabulary, and then the child said things like *it owes me money* at breakfast, and I found myself laughing into my tea, which was becoming a habit, and habits are dangerous. Habits are how people find their way under your skin.

I put down the cup. "We have work today."

"We always have work."

"Yes, but today's work is letters."

* * *

It had started, as most of my mistakes do, with the ledger.

I keep it in a oilcloth sleeve inside the false bottom of my instrument case, and every few nights I take it out and copy fair entries from my pocket notebook, because ink fades and grief should not be allowed to fade with it. That night Wren had crept down from the loft — the fourth stair again; I would have to oil it, or perhaps I would not, since it functioned as a rather effective alarm — and stood watching me write by candlelight with the particular patience of a child who has decided not to ask questions only because the questions are queuing up.

"What are those words?"

"Names," I said.

"Whose names?"

"People who died." I should have said *soldiers*, which was true enough and smaller. But she looked at the page with such seriousness, chin propped on the edge of the table, that the tidy lie would not come out of my mouth. "People whose names might otherwise be forgotten."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Like if you forget where you put your boots?"

"Worse than boots."

"Much worse," she agreed solemnly, and then, because she was seven and not a magistrate: "Can I draw on it?"

"No."

"Can I draw on *something*?"

And that was how it began. I tore a sheet from the back of an old supply requisition — Sister Amity would say the army owed me far more than one sheet of paper — and wrote the letter A at the top, and beneath it, because I am who I am, I did not write *apple*. I wrote *Ardan Fell*. Private, 4th Rifles. Died of his wounds on a Tuesday while I held his hand and the chaplain was busy elsewhere.

Wren traced the letters with a stub of pencil, tongue between her teeth. "Who's Ardan Fell?"

"A man who liked chess and complained about the coffee."

"Did he win at chess?"

"Never once."

"Then maybe forgetting him isn't so bad."

"He'd argue with you," I said. "Loudly. For hours. He argued with everyone about everything except the coffee, which he accepted as his cross to bear." And then I heard myself — the past tense, the fondness, the whole shape of a man rebuilt out of memory in front of a child — and understood what I was doing.

I was teaching her the alphabet out of a book of the dead.

Any sensible person would have stopped there. I have been accused of many things in my life, mostly by myself, but never of being sensible, so instead I turned the page and wrote B, and beneath it *Bren Okafor*, and told her about the harmonica.

* * *

We fell into a routine, which is another dangerous habit. Mornings were porridge and grievances; afternoons, while the ward slept off its dressings, we sat in the linen loft among the smells of soap and starch, and Wren learned her letters from the ledger's margins.

I want to be honest about why. Not the noble reason — the noble reason arrived later, dressed respectably, and took credit for everything. The first reason was simpler and worse: I enjoyed it.

I had spent eleven years keeping people at arm's length, and I had gotten very good at it, the way a man gets good at any wall he builds high enough. Arm's length is a safe distance. At arm's length you can hold a dying man's hand without it costing you sleep; you can know four hundred names without loving one of them. It is a professional arrangement, arm's length, and I had signed it gladly, because the alternative — the alternative was Ardan Fell arguing about chess until two in the morning, and Bren Okafor teaching me three chords on the harmonica badly, and all of it ending the way it ended, every time, in a grave marked with someone else's name or no name at all.

But a seven-year-old does not respect arm's length. A seven-year-old will simply walk under your arm and stand inside it, looking up, waiting for the next letter, and there is no protocol in any nursing manual for removing her.

"C," she said. "C is hard. It pretends to be a K sometimes."

"It's a coward's letter," I agreed. "Full of disguises."

"Like spies."

"Exactly like spies."

"Was Cephas Lorne a spy?" She had memorized the entry, of course. Children memorize everything; it is adults who carefully forget.

"Cephas Lorne was a farrier. He shoed horses and sang off-key and once gave his boots to a boy whose feet were frozen, and walked barefoot through the snow himself, which is why—" I stopped. Which is why he lost two toes, and later the fever, and later everything. She waited, patient, pencil ready. "Which is why we remember him," I finished.

"That's not why we remember him," Wren said. "We remember him because of the boots."

Out of the mouths of babes, as the chaplain likes to say, generally while meaning something else entirely. She was right, though. The ledger recorded that Cephas Lorne died of fever, February the ninth. It did not record the boots, because the ledger records facts and I had believed facts were the point. Sitting in the dust of the linen loft with a child's pencil in my hand, I began to wonder if I had been keeping half a book all these years — the skeleton of all those men without the warmth that made them men.

So I started adding things. Small things. *Bren Okafor: could not carry a tune in a bucket, carried it anyway.* *Private Hollis: afraid of thunder, ashamed of it, no longer.* Wren dictated some of them, having opinions about what mattered. Under her direction, the ledger of the dead became, page by page, a strange and tender document — part testimony, part eulogy, part catalog of harmonica players and boot-givers and men who owed their boots money.

Tenderness, I have since decided, is the riskiest activity there is. Bullets are straightforward. Tenderness gets inside you and rearranges the furniture.

* * *

Sister Amity caught us at it on the fifth afternoon.

I heard her on the stairs — the fourth stair betrayed her too; the loft had no secrets left — and I did not panic, exactly. Panic implies speed. What I felt was more like the slow settling of a house when its foundations shift. Amity stood in the doorway in her grey habit, hands folded, looking at Wren bent over the requisition paper, tongue between teeth, copying *Dulcet Anselm* with a D that leaned drunkenly leftward.

"Hm," said Amity.

"Sister—"

"I taught letters for six years before I took orders," she said. "Your D needs a spine, child. Like so." She knelt, took the pencil, drew the stroke. "There. Now it stands up straight even when it's tired."

"She's learning from a list of names," I said, because apparently I intended to confess everything, right away, like a fool.

Amity looked at me over her spectacles for a long moment. Whatever she saw in my face seemed to satisfy her. "Better a list of names than a blank page," she said. "My mother taught us our letters from the parish register. Births, marriages, deaths. We thought it was dull as ditchwater." Her voice went quiet and even, the way it went when she spoke of her sons, which was rarely and never fully. "Now I'd give a great deal to be bored by it again."

She stayed for the rest of the lesson. She corrected Wren's E and told a story about a boy in her village who had spelled his own name wrong for a year and grown attached to the mistake, and Wren laughed, and Amity laughed, and I sat there holding the ledger against my chest like a shield that had quietly become something else.

Afterward, in the corridor, Amity touched my sleeve. "You know what you're doing, Halloran?"

"Teaching letters."

"Mm. You're writing yourself into the book." She said it kindly, which somehow made it worse, and went off down the corridor with her keys jingling, leaving me to consider that I had spent eleven years standing outside history, taking its notes, and had just been caught sitting inside it, in the dust, laughing.

* * *

That night I copied the day's additions into the ledger proper, and Wren — who was supposed to be asleep in the loft — appeared at my elbow on silent feet, having defeated the fourth stair by jumping it entirely.

"Read me one," she said. "A good one."

"They're all good ones."

"The best one, then."

I turned pages. It is a strange thing, choosing a favorite among the dead; it feels like a sin and probably is one, and I did it anyway. "Bren Okafor," I said. "Corporal, 11th Infantry. He played the harmonica. Badly. He played it anyway, every night, and the first week we all complained, and by the second week if he stopped playing we couldn't sleep, because the silence was worse than the noise."

"Why was the silence worse?"

Because the silence was where the shelling lived. Because the noise meant someone was still alive enough to be terrible at music. I didn't say either of those things. "Because we'd gotten used to him," I said. "You can get used to almost anything. That's the good news and the bad news."

Wren chewed on this. Then she said, in the small, level voice children use when they have been carrying a question for days: "Is my name in there?"

The candle fluttered. Somewhere down the ward a man moaned in his sleep and was quiet again.

"No," I said. "You're not in there. You're not dead."

"But I don't have a name anywhere else." She said it without drama, the way she reported the porridge situation. "The note didn't have one. You call me Wren but that's a bird, not a name. So if nobody writes me down somewhere—" she shrugged, a small, terrible shrug, seven years old and already fluent in erasure— "then I'm like the silence."

I set down the pen. I am not a demonstrative person; my hands know how to bind wounds and turn patients and little else. But I took both of hers, pencil-stained, warm, and held them flat on the table beside the open ledger.

"Listen to me," I said. "You are going to have a name. A real one, written properly, in a book that doesn't close. And when you're older you'll read it yourself and know that somebody refused to let you be the silence. Do you understand? Whoever wrote that note told the world to forget your face. Well. The world has me to deal with now, and I have never forgotten anything in my life. Ask anyone. It's my worst quality and my best one."

She studied me with those brown eyes, deciding, the way children decide — completely, instantly, without appeal. Then she nodded once, business concluded, and pointed at the ledger.

"Write it down, then. Wren. So it's official."

"It's not your real name."

"It's my *real-est* name so far." She pushed the pen toward me. "Write it. Before the candle dies."

So I did. Right there in the ledger, between the fallen of February, I wrote: *Wren. Alive. Learning her letters.* It broke every rule I had built the book on — the living had no place in it; it was a ledger of the omitted, not a diary — and my hand shook slightly doing it, and I have never regretted an entry less.

"There," I said. "Official."

"Now teach me W. It's my letter. It has to be the best one."

"W is four strokes and an attitude," I said. "Watch closely."

* * *

We were on our third attempt at the attitude when the sound came from below — wheels on gravel, hooves, the raised voice of the night orderly — and then knocking at the hospital's front door, urgent and arrhythmic, the knock of people who have traveled badly and fast.

Wren froze, pencil in the air. "Visitors?"

"At this hour?" I blew out the candle and moved her behind me toward the loft stair, heart beginning its old cold arithmetic. Visitors at midnight were never visitors. They were casualties, or they were trouble, and lately trouble wore a uniform and asked courteous questions.

Through the loft window I could see lanterns in the yard, and stretcher-bearers lifting a litter from a cart, and a woman's voice — thin, cracked, repeating something over and over — and the word that reached me through the glass, faint but unmistakable, was *Kellin*.

Kellin Bridge. Where no survivors were supposed to exist.

I tucked the ledger into its oilcloth, kissed the top of a small head that smelled of pencil shavings and starch, and went down the stairs, skipping the fourth.
