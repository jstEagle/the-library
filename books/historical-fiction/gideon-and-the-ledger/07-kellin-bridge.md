## 7. Kellin Bridge

The pain came and went like weather. That was the first thing Odile Ferran decided about her leg: it had opinions, and it intended to share them.

The second thing she decided was that this hospital smelled honest. The forward aid station had smelled of iodine and fear in equal measure, but mostly fear, layered on thick as creosote. This place — a converted wool warehouse with a loft full of cots and a floor that creaked like an old ship — smelled of carbolic soap and boiled linen and, underneath it all, somebody's stew. It was impossible to be certain you were dying in a place that smelled of stew.

"Back with us?" said the nurse.

Not Sister Amity — she'd been by earlier, brisk and kind, tucking blankets with the efficiency of a woman who had buried precision-work of her own somewhere and never spoken of it. This was another one. Dark-haired, quiet, with ink stains on their fingers that no amount of scrubbing quite removed. Halloran. Odile had heard the name whispered on the transport up from the railhead: *if you wake up at Halloran's station, you woke up.*

"I'm back," Odile said. Her voice came out like paper. "Though I can't speak for where I was."

"Somewhere warmer, I hope."

"A road." She closed her eyes against the memory of it. "A very long road."

Halloran checked her pulse without asking permission, which Odile appreciated. There was a courtesy in not pretending to ask. "Fever's down. You'll keep the leg, if you let it alone."

"I've never once let anything alone in my life."

"So I'm learning." And there — the smallest pause, the eyes going still. "You were brought in saying a word over and over. Do you remember?"

Odile did not answer. She looked at the ceiling instead, at the water stain shaped like a running horse, and thought: they told us there were no survivors. They put it in the dispatch. *No survivors recovered.* Neat as a ledger line. She had spent eleven days being no survivor, crawling ditches with a shattered knee, eating raw turnips out of a farmer's flooded field, and every step of it she had thought: *I am the mistake in the report. If I exist, the report is a lie.*

"You said Kellin," Halloran said quietly.

"Yes," said Odile. Because lying to someone with ink-stained fingers, in a room that smelled of stew, seemed suddenly beyond her strength.

Something moved across the nurse's face — not shock. Recognition. As if Odile had recited the first line of a song whose verses Halloran already knew by heart.

* * *

They came back after evening rounds, when the lamps were turned low and the ward had settled into its nighttime economy of groans and snoring and one man in the corner who talked in his sleep about horses, always horses, in the fond exasperated tones of a man giving orders to animals that ignored him.

Halloran pulled a stool up beside her cot. Set down a cup of broth. Did not set down anything else — and yet Odile had the distinct impression that something else was there, in the pocket, in the oilcloth smell of the coat, waiting to be asked about.

"You should sleep," Odile said.

"In a moment. First I'm going to ask you something improper, and you're going to have every right to refuse."

"That's usually how my best conversations start."

Halloran almost smiled. It was a careful smile, kept in reserve, like coins sewn into a hem. "Kellin Bridge. The official account says our Third Column held the crossing against enemy regulars. A heroic stand. Commendations issued. I've read it four times."

"And?"

"And there are things in my keeping that don't agree with it." The voice stayed level, but Odile heard the effort of the levelness, the way you hear the strain in a rope holding weight. "I need to know what you saw. Not for the ward book. Not for anyone's book that has an official stamp on it."

Odile looked at the broth. Steam rose off it and bent the lamplight.

"You understand," she said, "that if I tell you, I stop having survived. Right now I'm Private Odile Ferran, invalided, lucky. The moment I say what happened at Kellin Bridge, I become something else. Something they send men with warrants for."

"I do understand."

"You say that so calmly."

"It's recently become personal," said Halloran, and did not elaborate, and Odile, who had learned to read silences the way other soldiers read terrain, understood that elaboration would cost them both more than either could afford tonight.

She drank the broth first. All of it. There was no sense testifying on an empty stomach; her mother had said something similar about funerals, and her mother had attended enough of them to be an authority.

Then she began.

* * *

"We reached the bridge at dusk on the ninth," she said. "Third Column, two hundred forty effectives, plus the stragglers we'd gathered on the march — maybe sixty more. Refugees, mostly. Farmers from the Kellin flats. Women, old men, children. We'd been telling them to follow us because the roads south were mined and the bridge was safe. Our own engineers' word. *The bridge is held, the bridge is safe.* You could put that on a poster."

Halloran's hands were folded very still in their lap.

"The enemy wasn't across the river yet. That's the part nobody says. There was no assault. No heroic stand, because there was nothing to stand against — not yet, not that night. We cooked. Some of the refugees had a fiddle. I remember thinking the water sounded nice under the planks." She stopped, and found, to her surprise, that stopping didn't break her. She started again. "Then Colonel Merrick's staff car came up the north approach, and everything got loud, but none of it was the enemy."

"Merrick," Halloran repeated, and Odile watched the name go into whatever book lived behind those eyes.

"He walked the span. Calm as a man inspecting his orchard. And he gave the order — I was close enough to hear it, God help me, I'll hear it until I die — he said, *Clear the crossing. All of them.* And Captain Deverel asked him, clear to which side, sir, and Merrick said, *It doesn't matter which side. It matters that they aren't here tomorrow when the photographers come.*"

"The photographers," Halloran said softly. Not a question. A confirmation of something already suspected.

"There were going to be photographs of the stand. Of the defense. A clean story needs a clean stage, and the refugees weren't clean. So the guardsmen pushed them. Bayonets flat at first, then not flat. Toward both ends and off the sides, into the water. And the ones who wouldn't run—" Odile's voice did something she hadn't authorized, cracked down the middle, and she hated it, and went on anyway, because a soldier finishes the patrol. "The ones who wouldn't run were made an example of on the span itself. Where the next wave of refugees could see. So they'd jump rather than argue."

The horse-man in the corner murmured to his horses. Somewhere below, a door shut.

"The water took most of them," she said. "The river was high. By morning there was hardly anything at the banks. Merrick wrote his dispatch sitting in the staff car with the engine running — I saw him through the window, writing, while the planks were still wet. *Held the crossing against superior forces. Casualties light. No survivors among the irregular personnel recovered.* Irregular personnel. That's what sixty farmers and their children were. Personnel. Irregular."

Silence. The lamp fluttered.

When Halloran finally spoke, the voice was different — stripped of its carefulness, raw at one edge. "How many?"

"Three hundred, near enough. Counting the stragglers. I counted. I counted the whole time, on the road after, in the ditches — I counted them because if nobody counts them, then the number is whatever Merrick says it is. Zero." She looked at her own hands, filthy-nailed, cracked. "Eleven days I carried three hundred people in my head. My knee is smashed and my head is full, and I have been so afraid that the head was the only place any of it would ever live."

Halloran stood. Went to the door. Looked both ways down the corridor with the practiced ease of someone who had done it many times before, and came back, and from the coat brought out oilcloth, and from the oilcloth a book.

It was not beautiful. It was a ledger, army-issue, the kind used for ration tallies. But it had been filled edge to edge in a small, disciplined hand, and when it opened, Odile saw columns of names. Names with dates. Names with regiments. Names with small annotations — *left a sister in Marle*, *owed me two sous*, *hummed while the surgeons worked* — that no official record on earth had ever bothered to carry.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"My ledger," Halloran said. "Of everyone the dispatches leave out. I've kept it three years. Every omitted name I could catch." A pause. "There's a page I couldn't fill. Kellin Bridge. I knew the official count was a lie — I could feel it, the way you feel a bone set wrong — but I had no names. Only a lie with a hole in it." They looked up. "Private Ferran. Will you help me fill the page?"

And Odile Ferran, who had crawled through flooded fields eating raw turnips, who had decided somewhere around day six that she would survive specifically and precisely out of spite, felt something in her chest unlock — a door she had barred from the inside.

"Start at the beginning of the column," she said. "I'll give you the names in marching order. That's how I remember them. That's how I promised myself I'd remember them."

* * *

They worked past midnight.

Halloran wrote; Odile spoke; the ward breathed around them, horses-horses-horses, and the lamp burned low and was trimmed and burned low again. Names crossed from a drowned place into ink: Anouk Pell, wheelwright's wife, red shawl. Old Teodor, who had carried his bees' hive-box eight miles. The Vasseur twins, aged five, who had marched holding a string between them so neither would get lost, and neither had gotten lost, not until the bridge. Odile gave each name and each detail she had, and some of the details made no sense — a hatpin, a joke about goats, a woman who sang a lullaby in a language Odile didn't know — and Halloran wrote every one down without once saying *is that relevant?* Because it was relevant. It was all relevant. That was the whole point of the thing.

Near the end, Odile named the officers. Merrick. Deverel. Lieutenant Sass, who had wept while obeying, which she noted did not excuse him and possibly indicted him further. Halloran's pen pressed hard enough at those entries to emboss the next page, and Odile found she liked knowing that — liked the idea of Merrick's name leaving a dent in paper he'd never see.

"There's one more thing," she said, when the page was nearly full. "There were children on that bridge who didn't drown. A handful got driven off the far end, north, back toward the flats. I saw two taken up in a staff vehicle. Officers' discretion, someone said. Which means someone wanted them gone quietly — witnesses, or worse, evidence." She rubbed her eyes. "One of them was a little girl, maybe seven. Dark hair. She was holding the string from the Vasseur twins. Somebody cut it."

The pen stopped.

For a long moment Halloran did not move, and Odile watched a whole campaign happen behind those eyes — retreat, rally, decision.

"Thank you," Halloran said at last, carefully, the way you set down something heavy. "That's — thank you."

"You've gone pale as the linens. What is it?"

"Nothing. Get some sleep, Private Ferran."

Which was, Odile reflected, watching the nurse walk away with the ledger hugged to their chest like a rescued child, the least convincing lie she had ever heard delivered by anyone with such steady hands. *Nothing.* Nobody went pale over nothing. And nobody, she thought, pulling the blanket up, hugging a book of the dead to their ribs at midnight unless the dead had just acquired living stakes.

She slept anyway. Testimony, it turned out, weighed less once shared.

She woke once, briefly, in the grey before dawn, to voices in the corridor — Halloran and the head nurse, low and urgent — and caught only fragments: *a child*, and *since Tuesday*, and then, sharper, from a direction she didn't expect, footsteps in the chapel wing and the sound of someone practicing a sermon in a carrying, righteous voice, the kind of man who believed the Lord had personally commissioned his filing system.

The sermon text drifted under the ward door like smoke: *thou shalt not bear false witness against thy superiors.*

Odile lay still in the dark, three hundred names newly lightened from her chest, one name newly heavy upon it, and thought: the past doesn't stay buried. It crawls eleven days through ditches and arrives at your hospital smelling of stew, and it starts talking.

By breakfast, she intended to find out exactly whose face that little girl had, and why Nurse Halloran had gone white as sheets at the mention of a string.
