## 8. The Chaplain's Sermon

The chapel at St. Aubry's Rear Hospital had been a granary before the war, and it had never quite forgiven the conversion. The smell of rye still haunted the rafters, the pews were grain crates planed smooth and set in rows, and the bell in the tower — such as it was — had once been rung to warn of threshing fires rather than to call anyone to prayer. Father Lucien Aubert, the hospital's chaplain, liked to say the building remembered its purpose: to hold what men had gathered and keep it from burning.

Sebastian had never found this comforting.

On the morning after the records room incident — no, Sebastian corrected themselves, there had been no incident yet; the morning was innocent, the morning knew nothing — Sebastian sat in the third row from the back with Sister Amity on their left and a sleeping convalescent named Piotr slumped against their right shoulder, drooling gently onto the sleeve of a borrowed dressing gown. The chapel filled slowly, the way soup fills a bowl, from the edges in. Wounded men came on crutches and in wheelchairs. Orderlies came because attendance was noted. Nurses came because Father Aubert noticed absence the way a hawk notices a field mouse — from a great height, and without mercy.

Wren was not in the chapel. Wren was in the linen room, under a mountain of folded sheets, with a biscuit, a slate, and strict instructions to be a piece of furniture. Sebastian had rehearsed the instructions twice. *You are a very small table. Tables do not giggle.* Wren had nodded with the gravity of a child accepting a knighthood, and then, as Sebastian reached the door, had asked, "Do tables get biscuits?"

"Only very good ones."

"I am the best table," Wren had said, and disappeared under the sheets.

Now Sebastian sat with their hands folded and their heart going like a laundry mangle, and told themselves that a chapel full of people was the safest place in the world to be invisible. Sebastian had been invisible for eleven years. It was a craft, like nursing. You learned where the light fell and you stood elsewhere. You learned which questions were invitations and answered only the ones that were not.

Father Aubert mounted the crate that served as a pulpit. He was a tall man gone soft at the edges, with a voice like warm oil and a face that seemed assembled for kindness — broad brow, gentle mouth, eyes that were, Sebastian had always thought, rather like the eyes of the portrait of a saint: painted on, and watching you from a fixed and unblinking angle.

"Brothers, sisters," he began. "Friends. Gatherers of the wounded harvest."

That was new. Sebastian felt Amity stiffen slightly beside them, a movement so small it might have been a draft.

"I have been thinking," the chaplain said, "about the ninth commandment. *Thou shalt not bear false witness.*"

A cough from the back. Piotr snored and resettled against Sebastian's shoulder, heavy as a sack of wet sand.

"It is a commandment we imagine belongs to courts and marketplaces," Father Aubert continued, his voice rolling out with the ease of long practice. "To liars in the dock, to cheats with false weights. But I tell you, false witness wears finer clothes than that. False witness can wear a nurse's apron. It can keep a tidy ledger." He paused, and let the word *ledger* fall into the room like a coin into a well. "It can smile. It can be, above all things, *sincere*."

Sebastian kept their eyes on the chaplain's collar button, which was brass, slightly tarnished, and entirely innocent of any knowledge of ledgers.

"For what is false witness, in the end?" the chaplain asked, spreading his hands. "It is the setting of one's own memory against the memory of one's betters. It is the private account that contradicts the public one. And the ninth commandment does not say *thou shalt not lie*. It says *thou shalt not bear witness falsely*. Witness, brothers and sisters, belongs to authority. A private man who remembers differently is not a witness. He is a *gossip*. A private woman who keeps her own record—" and here his gaze came down from the rafters and traveled, slowly, along the rows, "—is not a historian. She is a *thief*. She has stolen from the truth its only lawful custodians."

His eyes passed over Sebastian the way a searchlight passes over a wall. It did not stop. It did not need to. The whole sermon had been addressed to the third row from the back, and everyone in it knew it except Piotr, who was asleep, and possibly the chaplain, who had the gift of making menace sound like pastoral care.

Amity's hand came to rest on Sebastian's forearm. Her grip was light and her knuckles were white.

"The war is ending," Father Aubert said, and his voice gentled into something almost tender, which was somehow worse. "Peace is coming, and peace has needs. Peace needs a clean page. And I say to you: what a mercy that will be. What a mercy it is, that the Lord in His wisdom has given the writing of history to generals and not to nurses. For nurses see too much, and generals see *whole*. Let us pray."

They prayed. Sebastian prayed too, though their prayer was less a devotion than an inventory: *Wren under the sheets, ledger in the floor of my footlocker under the winter stockings, Odile in the fever ward, Tomas on the morning cart run, Amity's hand on my arm, the chaplain's collar button, the chaplain's eyes, the door, the door, the door.*

After the blessing, as the crates scraped and the wounded shuffled out, Father Aubert descended from his pulpit and worked his way along the rows, shaking hands, asking after fevers, being — Sebastian had to admit — genuinely kind to the men, most of whom loved him. He reached the third row. He took Amity's hand. He took Piotr's hand, and Piotr woke, said "Amen, Father," to a man who had not asked him anything, and sat up looking pleased with himself.

Then the chaplain stood before Sebastian.

"Nurse Halloran," he said warmly. "You look tired."

"I look like every other nurse in this hospital, Father."

"Yes," he said, smiling. "That is what is so remarkable about you." He held the handshake a half-second past comfortable. "The Lord sees the sparrow, you know. Even the sparrow that believes itself invisible. Especially that one. He has a particular fondness for sparrows who imagine they have escaped His notice."

"How kind of Him," Sebastian said.

"Kindness," said Father Aubert, releasing their hand, "is the whole of my vocation." And he moved on down the row, laying hands on the fevered and the frightened, a good shepherd counting his flock, and — Sebastian could not shake the thought — counting them twice.

* * *

The rest of the day passed in the ordinary liturgy of the ward: dressings changed, fevers charted, a young gunner named Beck wept quietly while Sebastian cleaned shrapnel from his shoulder and told him, truthfully, that he would play the fiddle again, and Beck said he didn't play the fiddle, and Sebastian said, "Well, then you have a whole life of not playing the fiddle ahead of you, which is more than some of us get," and Beck laughed, which was the entire purpose of the remark.

Sebastian checked on the linen room at intervals. The mountain of sheets was undisturbed. From beneath it, at one point, came the sound of very careful scratching — Wren, practicing letters on the slate. Sebastian lifted a corner of the sheet-mountain and looked in. Wren looked back, biscuit crumbs on their chin, eyes enormous.

"Table," Wren whispered, by way of identification.

"Very convincing," Sebastian whispered back, and let the sheets fall.

It was Odile who said it first, in the fever ward, in the flat voice she used for things she had decided not to feel. She was propped against her pillows, one arm splinted, her burns healing into a map of Kellin Bridge across her collarbone.

"Your chaplain," she said. "The one with the oil voice."

"What about him?"

"He came through the ward this afternoon. He didn't bless anyone. He walked the rows like a man counting sheep, and he stopped at every bed where a soldier had been at Kellin." Her eyes fixed on the ceiling. "There are three of us left here who were there, and he stopped at all three beds, and at yours, and he didn't say a word to anybody. He just looked. Then he left."

"Perhaps he was making a pastoral visit."

"Perhaps," Odile said, in the tone of a woman who had seen what happened at Kellin Bridge and did not believe in perhaps anymore. "Sebastian. When a man like that walks a ward without speaking, he isn't visiting the sick. He's taking attendance."

Sebastian sat with that through the afternoon, and through the change of shift, and through supper, which was barley soup and the heel of a loaf, eaten standing up in the corridor because there was no time to sit. Tomas found them there, balancing his bowl on a windowsill, and he was his usual self — quick, warm, complaining about the cart horses with the passion of a man who had been personally betrayed by every horse in the army.

"You missed the excitement," he said. "Chaplain had the quartermaster open the storeroom this evening. Wanted candles, he said. For the office."

"Since when does the chaplain take candles from the quartermaster instead of the vestry?"

"Since tonight, apparently." Tomas shrugged, spooning soup. "Quartermaster was sore about it. Said the vestry candles were right there. Chaplain said the vestry candles were spoken for." He grinned. "Maybe he's planning a very long prayer. Maybe he's converting to darkness."

"Maybe," Sebastian said, and smiled, and the smile cost more than the soup.

Because Sebastian had done the arithmetic, and it would not come out. A man who preaches on ledgers in the morning does not fetch candles at night. A man who walks a ward in silence is not counting the sick. And the storeroom, as every soul in the hospital knew, shared a wall with the records room.

* * *

Night came down on St. Aubry's the way it always did — slowly, and then all at once, the lamps in the wards dimmed to coals, the night nurses took their rounds, and the building settled into the creaking, coughing, dreaming life of a place full of hurt men. Sebastian made their own round. They checked the linen room: the sheet-mountain breathed. They checked the footlocker: winter stockings, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, the ledger, patient as a seed. They stood a moment in the dark of the nurses' quarters with their hand flat on the locker lid, feeling their own heartbeat come up through the wood, and told themselves the old catechism: *You are invisible. You have always been invisible. The light falls elsewhere.*

Then they went to the corridor outside the records room, and stood in it, and did nothing, which is the hardest work there is.

The records room was a narrow stone cell at the end of the east wing, holding the hospital's official books: admissions, discharges, deaths, the tallies that went up the chain to headquarters, where other tallies were made of them, and other tallies of those. Sebastian had copied from those books for eleven years — copying was permitted; *keeping*, apparently, was theft — and knew the room the way a farmer knows a field.

The corridor was dark. Sebastian stood in the dark beside the linen press and waited.

They did not wait long. Sometime past the second bell — Sebastian would never be sure how long, because fear keeps its own clock and it runs fast — there were footsteps. Two sets. Unhurried. The particular unhurried tread of men who have every right to be where they are.

A key turned in the records room lock. The door opened. Lamplight spilled into the corridor, thin and yellow.

Sebastian did not breathe. This was not a figure of speech. Their body, which had been through shelling and triage and eleven years of war, simply declined the service, and Sebastian stood in the linen press's shadow as empty of air as a glass.

Father Aubert's voice, low and pleasant, said something Sebastian could not catch. A second voice answered — and Sebastian knew it, and the knowing was a stone dropping through them: Quartermaster Brandt, the one who had been sore about the candles, the one who had complained to Tomas. His tone now had no soreness in it. It had the flat obedience of a man who had been shown something, or told something, or offered something.

They went in. The door stayed open a hand's width. Lamplight moved inside the room in slow sweeps, the way a lamp moves when a man carries it along a shelf, reading spines. Paper rustled. A drawer rolled on its runners, and rolled again, and a third time, patiently, thoroughly.

Sebastian stood in the dark and understood, with the clarity that comes only to people standing in dark corridors, that the institution had chosen. Not the war, not the army, not some distant headquarters — *this*, the hospital, the granary-chapel, the ward where Sebastian had held the hands of the dying for eleven years. It had a chaplain with an oil voice and a quartermaster with a key, and it had chosen the clean page. The building that had been built to keep what men had gathered from burning was going through its own records by lamplight, deciding what would be permitted to survive.

The lamp swept again. A page turned. Father Aubert murmured something that might have been a prayer, or might have been a list.

Then, from the far end of the corridor, from the direction of the linen room, came a sound.

Small. High. Unmistakable.

A child's laugh, quickly stifled — the kind of laugh a seven-year-old produces when they have been a very good table all day and have forgotten, for one glorious second, to be furniture.

Sebastian's blood went to ice water. The lamplight in the records room stopped moving.

The chaplain's voice, warm as ever, warm as oil on water, said clearly enough to carry down the stone corridor:

"Was that a rat?"

And Quartermaster Brandt answered, and his voice, too, had gone pleasant, and that pleasantness was the most frightening thing Sebastian had ever heard: "No, Father. Rats don't laugh."

The lamp came out of the records room. It came down the corridor. And Sebastian, pressed into the linen press's shadow with their heart slamming and their mind already running — *the sheet-mountain, the biscuit crumbs, the slate with letters scratched on it in a child's hand, letters copied, God help them, from the ledger* — did the only thing left to do.

They stepped out of the shadow, into the light, and said, "Looking for something, Father?"
