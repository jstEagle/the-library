## 9. Terms of Surrender

Adjutant Elias Corvane had a theory about kindness, and it was this: kindness was a discipline, like drill or bookkeeping. Anyone could feel generous on a fine morning. The real work of mercy was done in bad light, in bad weather, by men willing to be misunderstood.

He dressed carefully that morning — the grey uniform, the silver braid at the collar polished but not gleaming (gleam invited resentment), his boots brushed to the exact standard of a man who had better things to think about than boots. He ate half an apple and gave the other half to the sentry outside headquarters, who looked as though he hadn't been given fruit since the war began, which was probably true, because Corvane had requisitioned the orchards himself.

"You'll want to write your mother," he told the boy. "The post goes out Thursday."

"Sir?"

"The war is ending. Write her before the censors get sentimental." He smiled, which he knew made him look trustworthy, and went out into the rain.

* * *

The hospital at Marle sat in what had once been a convent school, and it still smelled faintly of chalk and floor wax under the iodine and the other smell, the one no one named. Corvane had visited forty-one hospitals in three years. He could rank them blindfolded by the sound they made: the good ones were quiet in a working way, a hum of purpose; the bad ones were quiet in the other way.

This one hummed. He approved of it. He intended, insofar as his intentions mattered anymore, to keep it humming.

Sister Amity met him in the corridor with the expression of a woman who had buried two sons and one husband and had therefore run out of patience for adjutants somewhere around the second funeral.

"Matron," he said warmly. "You look well."

"I look tired, Adjutant. So do you. Shall we skip the part where we pretend otherwise?"

He liked her enormously. He would have liked to recruit her. "Nurse Halloran," he said instead. "I'm told they're on the ward."

"They're always on the ward. It's rather their defining feature."

"Then I'll find them there."

She did not move out of his way so much as stop obstructing it, which was a distinction Corvane noted and filed. People thought Amity was merely blunt. He suspected she was a fortress with excellent manners, and fortresses were only ever taken from within or around, never through.

* * *

Sebastian Halloran was changing a dressing on a boy of nineteen whose leg was a subject best not discussed at table. They worked with the economy of someone who had done ten thousand dressings and resented none of them, and when Corvane stopped at the foot of the cot, Sebastian looked up with the mild, unbothered face of a person interrupted mid-sentence by weather.

"Adjutant," Sebastian said. Not *sir*. Not *welcome*. Just the word itself, set down like a tray.

"Nurse Halloran. Might I borrow five minutes? There's a supply office down the corridor that nobody's using."

"There's a supply office down every corridor nobody's using. That's how you can tell it's an army."

Corvane laughed — genuinely, because it was funny — and gestured toward the door. Sebastian wiped their hands, told the boy on the cot he'd be back before the morphine wore off and not to let anyone tell him the soup was improving, and walked with him down the hall. Their stride was unhurried. Corvane had watched soldiers, priests, and one archduke try to read Sebastian Halloran's face, and he had concluded long ago that there was nothing to read. It was like trying to interrogate a closed book by staring at its spine.

He respected that. He also intended to open the book.

* * *

The supply office contained a desk, two chairs, a window with rain on it, and a crate of oranges that had gone hard as cannonballs. Corvane shut the door, offered Sebastian a chair, took one himself, and did not open with pleasantries, because he had learned that people trusted you more when you skipped them.

"I'm going to tell you something I've told four people," he said, "and then I'm going to make you an offer, and then you're going to say no, and then I'm going to explain why you shouldn't. Does that order suit you, or would you prefer to say no first and save time?"

"That depends," Sebastian said, settling into the chair with the ease of someone prepared to stay all afternoon, "on whether the something is interesting."

"The armistice is signed. Or it will be by Friday. The delegations are arguing over the punctuation, but the substance is done." He watched for a flicker — relief, grief, anything. Sebastian's face remained a closed book, though Corvane noticed the hands go still in their lap, which in Halloran was roughly equivalent to another man weeping. "The historians arrive within the month. The victors' historians. Ours, if you like — the ones who will write the account your grandchildren will be taught, assuming your grandchildren are taught anything, which given the state of the schoolhouses is optimistic."

"And the offer?"

"Passage home. Real passage — a staff car, papers, a discharge with honors. Immunity from any inquiry touching your service here, which I should mention will be thorough, because thoroughness flatters the victors. And a pension backdated two years, because I checked the pay ledgers and this hospital has been robbed systematically since 1911 and someone ought to be embarrassed about it."

Sebastian blinked. It was, Corvane thought, the most expression he'd seen from them all year. "That's a great deal of money for whatever you're about to ask."

"It's a fair price for what I'm asking. I want the ledger, Nurse. And I want the child."

* * *

There it was. He had expected denial — the polite, baffled *what ledger?*, the *what child?* — and he had prepared three graceful exits from each. Instead Sebastian simply looked at him, and the silence stretched long enough that Corvane found himself filling it, which annoyed him, because filling silences was a concession and he knew it.

"You know," Sebastian said at last, "most people lead with the accusation."

"I don't accuse. Accusation implies I need you to confess. I don't. I need you to cooperate, and confession is optional."

"And if I say there's nothing to give you?"

"Then I'll say that a child of approximately seven years arrived at your back door on the morning of the fourteenth, wrapped in a blanket bearing General Vane's monogram, and that no such arrival appears in any register, and that Sister Amity's laundry counts have been wrong by exactly one small shirt for eleven days, which she'd know if she weren't covering for you — and she is covering for you, incidentally, and doing it badly, because matrons are excellent nurses and poor criminals." He leaned forward. "And then I'd say that a ledger exists — I have a witness who saw it, years ago, a clerk with debts and a memory — and that it contains names. Names that officers chose to leave out of dispatches. And that when the historians come, they will not ask gently."

Sebastian's hands had not moved. But something behind the eyes had shifted — not fear, Corvane realized. Calculation. He had spent three years among men whose faces performed everything and felt nothing, and here was the opposite: a face that performed nothing and felt, he suspected, a great deal.

"You're offering me a bribe," Sebastian said.

"I'm offering you a rescue."

"From what?"

"From being right at the wrong time." Corvane stood, because standing helped him say the next part, and went to the window where the rain was writing its own ledger down the glass. "You think I'm Vane's creature. I understand. I wear his colors, I carry his orders, I once shot a mule for him because it had gone lame and he couldn't bear the noise of it suffering, and I have regretted the mule more than any man I've killed. But hear me, because I am going to be honest with you in a way that will cost me if you repeat it."

He turned. Sebastian had not moved.

"This war is ending, and the men who won it have decided what it meant. They decided it in committee rooms in the capital while you were holding boys' hands in the dark. Kellin Bridge will be a heroic stand. The retreat at Sorrel Field will be a strategic withdrawal. The winter of '13 will be a hardship nobly endured. And the names — the ones you've been collecting like a miser collects coins — those names will simply not appear. Not because anyone hates them. Because a history with every name in it cannot be taught in an afternoon, and the victors have an afternoon."

"And you believe that's right."

"I believe it's *kind*." He said it plainly, without apology, because he had turned the sentence over for three years and it had never once failed him. "You want to know what I saw at Kellin Bridge, Nurse? I arrived after. I counted. Do you know what counting does to a man? I have counted every night since. If the ledger comes out — if your names come out — every widow in the province learns the precise manner of it. Every mother learns that the heroic stand was a slaughter ordered by a general who then had the bridge repainted for the photographers. Is that mercy? You'd be trading a clean story for a true one, and the true one buys the dead nothing. They're dead either way. The living get to sleep."

"The living," Sebastian said quietly, "don't get to decide what the dead were."

"No," Corvane agreed. "But they get to decide what the dead are *said* to have been, and that's the only afterlife anyone actually manages. I'm offering you the chance to give these people a good one." He came back to the desk and laid it out flat, the way he laid out everything: terms, dates, signatures. "The car comes Saturday. The papers are already drawn. The child goes to a family in the south — a good one, I've selected it myself, a childless couple who farm apples and have never read a newspaper. In ten years Wren — yes, I know what you call the child, Nurse, I pay attention — in ten years Wren is a farmer's daughter with freckles and no history at all. No general for a father. No bridge for a birthplace. Nothing to testify to, nothing to be hunted for. Forget the face, the note said. I am offering to do exactly that, thoroughly, and kindly, and forever."

* * *

For a long moment the only sound was the rain and, distantly, the ward — someone laughing, low and cracked, the way the wounded laugh when the morphine finds the joke before they do.

"You rehearsed that," Sebastian said.

"Every word. For six days."

"It's a good speech. The apple farmer was a nice touch. Did you invent the freckles just now?"

"Freckles are statistically likely in southern children."

"And the pension?"

"Genuine. Backdated. I told you, the pay ledgers are a scandal."

Sebastian stood. They were not tall, and they did not loom, and yet Corvane had the distinct sensation of being the shorter one in the room. "Here's my trouble with your kindness, Adjutant," they said. "It's very tidy. Everyone gets what they need except the truth. The widows sleep, the child farms apples, the historians finish by supper. And the only person asked to give up anything is me — my ledger, my risk, my name. You've built a mercy where all the cost lands on the one person who kept the receipts." A pause, and then, gentler, which was somehow worse: "Has anyone ever offered *you* a tidy mercy, Adjutant? Or do you only administer them?"

Corvane opened his mouth and found, to his genuine surprise, that he did not have an answer prepared. He had answers prepared for everything. He had an answer for the mule.

"Saturday," he said instead. "The car comes at dawn. If the ledger and the child are on it, no one is ever troubled again. If they aren't—" He shrugged, and hated the shrug even as it happened. "Then I stop being able to protect you from what happens next. And what happens next is not me. What happens next is men who don't count bridges afterward. Men who'd burn this hospital with everyone in it and file it as a boiler accident. I am, I promise you, the gentlest thing coming down that road."

"I believe you," Sebastian said, and it was worse than an insult. "That's the terrible part. I believe every word. Go check on your patient in bay four, Adjutant — the one pretending to sleep. He's lying about his pain because he thinks complaining is cowardice, and someone should tell him it isn't."

They left. The door clicked shut with the soft finality of a ledger closing.

* * *

Corvane stood alone in the supply office for some time, watching the rain. He thought about the mule. He thought about counting. He thought, absurdly, about freckles, and whether he had invented them or remembered them from somewhere — a village, a photograph, a face at a window on the road out of Kellin Bridge, one of the hundreds of faces he had counted and catalogued and never once written down.

That was the difference between him and Halloran, he supposed. He had counted. They had *written*.

He collected his hat, nodded to the sentry — who was, he noted with satisfaction, writing a letter to his mother — and went out into the rain to report his failure to a general who would not take it gently.

Behind him, in the ward, Sebastian Halloran was already pulling a young private named Odile Ferran into the linen room, closing the door, and saying words Corvane would not hear until it was far too late:

*"I need you to carry something to Verenne."*
