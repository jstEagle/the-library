## 10. Odile's Choice

The linen room smelled of carbolic and cold starch, and Private Odile Ferran had been in enough trouble in her nineteen years to know that small rooms with closed doors were where trouble did its best work.

"I need you to carry something to Verenne," Halloran said.

Odile laughed. It came out wrong — too high, too fast, the laugh of a person whose hands had not stopped shaking since Kellin Bridge, which was eleven days ago and also, some mornings when she woke, happening right now. "Verenne. That's — that's across the front, Nurse. That's a hundred and forty miles through a country that's on fire."

"It is," Halloran agreed. They had that way of agreeing with true things as though true things were difficult and deserved respect. "It's about a hundred and forty miles."

"You said it like it was around the corner."

"I didn't want to prejudice you against the distance before you'd met what it's for."

And then Halloran held out the package, and Odile saw that it was not a package at all, really. It was a book. A plain book, ledger-sized, bound in the grey cloth of an account-book, wrapped twice in linen and tied with surgical tape because apparently even the string in this hospital was requisitioned by the medical corps.

"What is it?"

"Names," Halloran said. "Two hundred and six of them. Some from Kellin Bridge."

Odile's hands remembered themselves all at once. The shaking stopped. It was the strangest thing; for eleven days her body had been a badly packed kit-bag, rattling, and now it went still and heavy as a stone dropped in water. "The official report says three hundred were saved at Kellin Bridge," she said. "Heroic stand. I've heard them read it out. Sergeant read it twice."

"The official report doesn't mention the civilians driven onto the bridge ahead of the assault," Halloran said. "Doesn't name them. Doesn't count them. This does." They touched the grey cover once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't want to wake. "There's a duplicate. This one needs to reach the neutral archive at Verenne before the victors' historians close the record. After that, anything not in the record never happened."

"And if it gets there?"

"Then it happened," Halloran said simply. "Forever."

Odile looked at the ledger. She thought about the bridge — she had been trying very hard not to think about the bridge, and failing, because thinking *about* not-thinking was still thinking — and she heard the water under it, and the singing, which was the worst part, the singing, the woman who sang while the shells came down because her daughter was frightened of thunder and had been taught that songs meant safety —

"No," Odile said.

Halloran didn't move. Didn't sigh, didn't press, didn't do any of the persuading things that persuaders do. They just stood in the linen room among the folded sheets and looked at Odile with those grey, patient eyes, and waited, which was somehow worse than arguing.

"No," Odile said again, louder, as if volume were courage. "I'm sorry. You're asking me to — I'm not — Nurse, look at me. Look at my hands."

Halloran looked. Odile held them up, and they were shaking again now, fine as wire.

"Those carried stretchers for nine hours at Kellin Bridge," Halloran said. "After everything else about you had gone."

"That's different. That was just — forward and back, forward and back. Nobody asked me to decide anything. Nobody asked me to be brave in a *direction*." Odile heard her own voice crack and hated it. "I saw what people do to each other over a bridge. I'm not going to carry the reason across a hundred and forty miles of it. If they catch me with that book, they won't shoot me. They'll do worse than shoot me. And I can't — I've got nothing left to be worse'd *with*, Nurse. I'm empty. I ran out eleven days ago and I've been walking around ever since like a kettle on an empty stove."

She hadn't meant to say that last part. It was too honest. But Halloran only nodded, slow, as though Odile had made a good point in a seminar.

"All right," Halloran said.

"*All right?*"

"You can't carry it empty-handed into a fire. Nobody can. It isn't cowardice, Private Ferran, it's arithmetic." They set the ledger down on the folding shelf between the pillowcases, and left it there, which Odile understood later was the whole trick: they didn't take it away. "If you change your mind, you know where I am. If you don't, I'll ask someone else, and I'll think no less of either of us."

"That's a terrible way to recruit people."

"It's the only way that works," Halloran said, and opened the door, and the noise of the ward came in like weather, and Odile went back to her duties with her heart banging and the ghost of the grey book sitting on her shoulder like a bird she couldn't see.

* * *

For two days she did not change her mind, and she told herself so firmly and so often that Sister Amity commented on it.

"You're humming," Amity said, on the second evening, while they changed a dressing together. "You haven't hummed since you came in. Last time you hummed was — "

"Don't," Odile said. "Please."

"All right." Amity smoothed the bandage with a thumb, gentle as rain. "But whatever it is you're not deciding, Private, I'd decide it soon. Deciding is like bleeding. Best done early and somewhere clean."

That night Odile sat on her bunk after lights-out with her boots half off, and tried to pray, and got as far as *Lord, make me an instrument* before her own voice in her head sounded so much like the chaplain's that she stopped out of pure spite. The chaplain had preached on Sunday about false witness against one's superiors. He had looked directly at her during the third verse, she was nearly sure of it. Or maybe he'd been looking at the pillar behind her. Everything since Kellin Bridge had this quality: eyes might be on her, shells might be falling, the floor might open. A girl could go mad trying to check every *might*.

So instead she did what she had learned to do on bad nights in the ward, which was to go and stand where the work was.

She padded down the corridor in stockinged feet, past the sleeping rows, to the small side bay where the hospital kept its oddments: the convalescents who needed quiet, the ones nobody knew what to do with yet.

And there, in the narrow bed by the window, slept the child.

They called her Wren. Odile had seen her before, always briefly, always at a distance — a thin wisp of a girl of seven or so who appeared at mealtimes and vanished afterward, who had been officially recorded nowhere, who moved through the hospital like a draft through a doorframe. Tonight she had fallen asleep sitting up, mid-something, a pencil still hooked in her fingers, a piece of paper pinned under one small hand.

Odile crept closer. She knew she shouldn't. She crept closer anyway.

On the paper, in a laborious child's hand, letters marching uphill and downhill with no particular respect for margins, was written:

HALLORAN  ODEIL  FERRAN

And below it, smaller, more careful, copied letter by letter from something:

FERRAN, ODILE, PVT., KELLIN BRIDGE, CARRIED NINE HOURS.

Odile stood very still.

The child slept with her mouth open, one cheek pressed to the blanket, utterly surrendered the way only children and soldiers on leave ever fully manage. Her hair had escaped its plait. Her fist stayed curled around the pencil even in sleep, holding on.

*Copied from something.* From the ledger. From the grey book that Odile had refused, that was sitting somewhere within thirty feet of this bed, containing her own name, written down by someone who believed that a name written was a name that existed. Halloran had written *her*. Not the report of her, not the number of stretchers, not the commendation that would never come. Her. Ferran, Odile, Pvt., Kellin Bridge, carried nine hours. As if that were a fact of history, permanent as a mountain, worth preserving against fire and forgetting and generals.

And this child — this erased child, smuggled in a blanket, hidden from a general, the one person in this whole war whom everyone agreed must not exist — this child had learned her letters from the names of the dead, and the first thing she had done with the power to write was write *Odile*.

Well. All right. Two names, actually. But Odile was one of them.

She didn't cry. She'd used up crying somewhere around hour six on the bridge. What happened instead was quieter and worse: she felt herself fill up. Like a kettle, she thought wildly, like a kettle on a stove somebody had lit without telling her, filling slowly, and the water rising toward something.

Wren stirred, smacked her lips, and murmured — words, almost. Odile leaned down. The child was singing, barely, a thread of a tune, half-swallowed by sleep. Something with a rise and fall to it, a lullaby-shaped thing, old and worn smooth.

Odile knew the tune.

She had heard it on the bridge, sung by a woman with her arms around her daughter while the world came apart, sung precisely *because* the daughter was frightened, sung as if a song could build a roof over anyone.

The child had been there. Of course she had. The child had been there, and had lived, and had been carried away and erased, and she still sang it in her sleep, and somewhere in a grey book there were names of people who had died on that bridge so that this small throat could go on making that sound, and the men who ordered it all wanted the song and the names and the face forgotten, forgotten, *forget the face* —

Odile put out one finger and very gently unhooked the pencil from the sleeping child's grip, so she wouldn't roll onto it.

Then she straightened up, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, businesslike, and went to find Halloran.

Courage, she discovered, standing there in the dark ward smelling of carbolic and sleep, was not a thing you had. It was a thing you decided. And apparently — she gave a small wet laugh at the ceiling — apparently she was going to have to keep deciding it, every mile of a hundred and forty, because a decision made once was just an opinion, and opinions didn't survive contact with checkpoints.

The kettle inside her came gently to the boil.

* * *

Halloran was awake, of course. Odile suspected Halloran slept the way cats did, in shallow installments, one ear open. She found them in the records room with a lamp turned low, mending the spine of a register with thread and a curved needle, and when Odile shut the door behind her and said, "I'll carry it," Halloran didn't even look surprised. They finished the stitch first. Odile respected that enormously.

"On conditions," Odile said.

"Name them."

"One. When it's done — when Verenne has it — you write me in that book. Properly. What I did and why. Not the medal version. The true version."

Halloran set down the needle. "Private Ferran, if you get that ledger to Verenne, you may write your own entry. I'll hold the pen steady for you if your hands are tired."

Odile blinked at that, and found she had no answer, so she moved on. "Two. I want to say goodbye to the little one properly. Not sneaking. She copies names out of that book; she deserves to know one of them is leaving on purpose."

"She already knows yours."

"I saw," Odile said. "You spelled me *Odeil*."

"The second E comes and goes," Halloran admitted. "We're working on it."

"And three." Odile took a breath. "Tell me how to lie. I've never done it. I got through the checkpoint at Marle Road last month by being so obviously harmless the sentry felt sorry for me, but that was before I was carrying evidence of atrocities, so I feel the strategy needs revision."

Something happened to Halloran's face then that Odile had never seen on it. It took her a moment to recognize it as a smile — a real one, crooked and warm, arriving like sun through a dirty window.

"Lesson one," Halloran said, pulling the grey ledger from its drawer and beginning to wrap it in fresh linen, quick and exact. "The best lie is mostly true. You are a discharged nursing auxiliary traveling west to her aunt's. That's true, if we make it true. Tomorrow I'll discharge you — properly, papers and stamp, 'deemed unfit for further service owing to nervous condition.' No one will question it. Half this army has a nervous condition; it's practically uniform issue."

"They'll believe I'm mad?"

"They'll believe it *gratefully*," said Halloran. "It confirms what everyone suspects about everyone else. Lesson two: never carry the thing you're hiding where a search looks first. The ledger will travel in the lining of your coat collar — a stiff collar explains itself, and no sentry in history has ever searched a collar, because collars are above their pay grade. Lesson three—"

"Wait." Odile stared. "In the *collar*? A hundred and forty miles? In this weather?"

"You wanted the true version of yourself written in the book," Halloran said mildly, threading a long needle with waxed thread in three efficient passes. "This is the version that earns it."

Odile groaned and sat down on the records-room stool. "Fine. Collar. What's lesson three?"

Halloran drew the linen tight and began to sew, small neat stitches, and for a moment didn't answer. The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the corridor a man coughed in his sleep, and another voice murmured comfort to him.

"Lesson three," Halloran said at last, quietly, "is that you don't do this alone. There's a refugee column forming at the Marle road, moving west ahead of the armistice. You travel in it. There's a woman named Gaunt who runs the rear of it — big as a barn, mean as vinegar, absolutely trustworthy. Tell her the ferret sent you."

"The *ferret*?"

"It's a long story involving Sister Amity, a stolen ham, and the winter of '09," said Halloran, "and you'll hear it around a fire on the road, if there's any justice. Which brings me to the last thing, and it isn't a lesson." They tied off the thread, bit it clean, and held out the coat — Odile's own coat, she realized, taken sometime in the night from the drying line, its collar now mysteriously thick and proud. "When you get to Verenne, ask for the director. Don't argue with him, don't explain, don't tell him the whole history of the war. Just put the ledger on his desk and say: *the record isn't closed yet.*"

"The record isn't closed yet," Odile repeated.

"He'll understand. He's spent forty years being afraid of exactly that sentence." Halloran set the coat in her lap. It weighed hardly anything, and everything. "One more week, Private Ferran, and the historians arrive at the front. Whatever reaches Verenne before they finish is true forever. Whatever doesn't—" They shrugged, lightly, and did not finish, and did not need to.

Odile put the coat on. It hung the same as ever, except that when she moved her head, the collar moved with it, solid, present, alive with its secret cargo of two hundred and six names. She thought of the bridge. She thought of the singing. She thought of a child's pencil marching uphill: *ODEIL.*

"How long do I have to keep deciding?" she asked. "To be brave, I mean. Before it sticks."

Halloran considered this with the seriousness they gave to everything.

"In my experience," they said, "it sticks around the ninetieth mile. Give or take."

* * *

Odile left before dawn, in the blue-grey hour when the hospital smelled of porridge and antiseptic and the sentry at the gate was yawning over a letter to his mother. Her discharge papers said what Halloran had promised. Her collar rode firm against her neck. At the gate she produced her papers, was waved through with the sublime indifference of authority toward anything boring, and walked out onto the west road feeling like a fraud, a heroine, and a kettle, all three at once.

At the top of the first rise she stopped and looked back.

The hospital lay below in the last of the dark, its windows lit here and there — the ward, the kitchens, and one other window, low and small, where a lamp still burned. The records room. Halloran, probably, writing. Adding. Refusing to subtract.

Odile stood there a moment longer than she should have — a hundred and forty miles, and she was dawdling at mile zero — and it seemed to her, in the way that small premonitions come to people who have learned to trust the hair on their arms, that the lamp behind that particular window flickered oddly. Orange, where it should have been white. Restless, where Halloran was never restless.

Probably nothing, she told herself. Drafty old building.

She pulled her new-stiff collar a fraction higher over the ledger's weight, turned west, and started to walk — and behind her, faint on the morning air, she thought she caught the first sour breath of smoke.
