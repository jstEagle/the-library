## 11. Fire at the Records Room

The night the records room burned, Sebastian was teaching Wren the letter K.

*K* had turned out to be a difficult letter. It stood, in Sebastian's primer of ledger names, for Kellin—Kellin Bridge, Kellin village, all the Kellins of the dead—and Sebastian had hesitated over it longer than over any other entry they'd shown the child. In the end they had chosen Private Anselm Ketterle, age nineteen, drowned not at the bridge but in the flooded millrace two days after, and they had drawn the letter slowly on the back of an old supply requisition while Wren sat cross-legged on the storeroom floor with a blanket around her shoulders like a small general reviewing troops.

"K," said Wren. "Like *kit*."

"Like kit," Sebastian agreed. "Like kettle."

"Like—" Wren considered. "Like *kicked*."

"That is a very complete education in the letter K."

Wren giggled, and the sound did what it always did to Sebastian, which was to make the storeroom smell less like carbolic and mildew and more like somewhere else entirely, somewhere neither of them had ever actually lived.

Then the bells started.

Not the morning bell, not the mess bell, not any of the dozen small domestic bells that ordered their days. This was the alarm gong from the east corridor, and beneath it came the sound Sebastian had learned in the war's first year and never unlearned: shouting with running underneath it, the particular rhythm of people moving toward something they hoped wasn't as bad as it looked.

Sebastian got Wren up by one hand and opened the storeroom door. The corridor beyond was already orange-lit at the far end, light jumping along the whitewash like something liquid.

"The records room," said Sister Amity, materializing out of the smoke-haze with her sleeves already rolled. She had a way of appearing fully informed about everything within four seconds of its happening, which Sebastian had once attributed to long experience and now suspected was simply a form of second sight granted to women who had outlived three head surgeons. "It's gone up like a harvest field. They're saying a lamp was left burning."

"A lamp." Sebastian heard their own voice come out flat.

"There is no lamp in that room. There hasn't been an open flame permitted in that room since the winter of the shelling, when young Halloran here"—Amity nodded at them without heat—"made such a scene about the paper that the colonel himself wrote the regulation. I remember because he misspelled *inflammable* and we were too polite to correct him."

They were moving as she talked, Amity sweeping Wren onto her hip with the ease of a woman who had carried sons, Sebastian keeping pace, and the corridor opened out into the courtyard where half the hospital stood in the cold in various states of undress, faces lit from below by the fire in the east wing window.

The records room burned the way paper burns: fast, total, strangely beautiful if you refused to think about what it was. The windows glowed solid amber. Sparks went up in a column. Orderlies passed buckets mostly for the look of the thing, because there was nothing in that room to save and everyone standing in the courtyard knew it—the admission books, the death registers, the dispatch copies, four years of the war's official memory for this whole sector, going up in a column of gold into a sky that had no business being that clear in November.

"Stand back from the wall," Amity said generally, to no one and everyone. "If the roof comes down it will come down *outward*, and I have buried enough of this army without adding volunteers."

Wren's hand found Sebastian's. Her grip was very small and very deliberate, the grip of a child who had learned that fires meant leaving places quickly.

"It's far away," Sebastian told her. "It can't reach us."

"I know." Wren watched the sparks. "But the names are in there."

Sebastian went still. "What names?"

"The ones you read me. The book with the names." Wren looked up, matter-of-fact, seven years old and precise as a clerk. "Is that the fire taking them?"

"No," said Sebastian, and knelt down so they could say it at her own eye level, and found their voice was steadier than they had any right to expect. "That book is safe. That book sleeps with me, under my coat, every night. What's burning in there is the other kind of record. The kind written by the people who forgot the names in the first place."

Wren thought about this. "So the wrong book burned."

"*Exactly* the wrong book burned," said Sebastian, and had to turn away for a moment and pretend great interest in the bucket line, because there are moments when a child says a thing so cleanly that laughing and weeping sit too close together to risk either.

* * *

By midnight the fire had eaten itself out, having found nothing left to eat, and the courtyard smelled like a library that had died violently. The roof of the east wing held. A black wet scar marked the corridor's end, and the door of the records room stood open on a chamber of ash, the shelves reduced to charcoal fingers, the registers to brittle grey fans that crumbled when Sergeant Bray, poking gingerly with a poker, tried to turn one.

"Lamp left burning," Bray reported, with the confidence of a man repeating orders rather than observations. "That's what the captain says. Lamp left burning, curtains too near, bad luck all round."

"Curfains," said Amity pleasantly, at his elbow. "There were no curtains in that room, sergeant. There has never been a curtain in that room. There was a shelf, a table, four chairs, and a padlock the size of your fist. But do go on about the curtains."

Bray retreated with the expression of a man who had stepped on something in the dark and decided not to investigate.

Sebastian stood in the doorway of the ruined room. The heat still came off the ash in waves, faint and animal. Here had been the admission ledger with Wren's arrival logged—or not logged; Sebastian had seen to it that no such entry existed, which was perhaps why the room's destruction struck them now with a strange double grief. Four years of careful institutional memory, and Sebastian felt its loss the way one feels the loss of a rival: the enemy of a lifetime, dead, and the world suddenly emptier for it.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing that had been growing in Sebastian's chest since the first alarm bell and now came to full flower in the ash-smell dark.

You did not burn a records room to destroy it. Any fool could destroy a records room; a match through a window would serve. You burned a records room *the night before you needed its absence*. And the absence you needed was every page that contradicted the story you intended the historians to write.

Kellin Bridge existed now in exactly two places in the world: in official dispatches praising a heroic stand, and in a green-covered notebook sewn into the lining of Sebastian Halloran's coat.

After tomorrow, after the fire was written up and explained, it would exist in only one.

"They're not hiding anymore," Sebastian said quietly. Amity was beside them; she was always beside them lately, a fixed point. "Corvane spent weeks being subtle. Courteous. Offers, hints, sermons. Now this. Now they burn the building down in the middle of the night and call it a lamp."

"Subtlety is for men who still believe they might be forgiven," said Amity. She was looking at the ash with an expression Sebastian couldn't read. "Or for men who believe someone is watching. When a creature stops being careful, Miss Halloran, it is because it has decided the watching is done."

"Or because the deadline moved." Sebastian's breath fogged. "He told me the victors' historians arrive within weeks. Perhaps it's not weeks anymore. Perhaps it's days."

"Then you should not be standing in this doorway where anyone with eyes can see you doing arithmetic." Amity took their elbow, firm as a tourniquet. "Come. Bray is simple, but the chaplain is clever, and clever men walk slowly past fires looking thoughtful."

* * *

The chaplain did indeed walk slowly past the ruin the next morning, hands clasped behind his back, head bowed, and if Sebastian didn't know better they would have said the man was praying. He paused at the threshold, regarding the ash with tender regret.

"Such a loss," he said, to the room in general and to Sebastian in particular, since Sebastian was the only one there. "Four years of faithful service, recorded and preserved. Gone in an hour." He sighed. "Providence is instructive, don't you find? It teaches us what paper is worth."

"What is paper worth, Chaplain?"

"Why—all kinds of things." He smiled. His smile was the exact temperature of the ash. "Some paper is worth a great deal to the right buyer. Other paper is worth more as smoke. It depends entirely, Sister Halloran, on what is written on it." He tapped the doorframe twice, a small cheerful gesture, and walked on.

Sebastian stood in the cold and understood, with the clarity that only ever arrived after it was useful, two things at once.

First: the chaplain knew the ledger existed. Not suspected—knew. The sermon had been a warning; the fire was a demonstration; and this conversation was an invoice. Somewhere in the last fortnight the walls had grown ears, and Sebastian had been too busy teaching letters and falling in love with a child to hear them.

Second: if the ledger was known, then the search would come. Not the fumbling midnight ransack of the records room—that had been theater, an alibi in flame. The real search would be quiet, patient, and aimed at one coat, one cot, one person. And it would come soon, because men who burn buildings do not then wait politely.

* * *

Sebastian spent the day performing normalcy the way other people performed surgery: with clean instruments and steady hands and a heart rate they declined to discuss.

They changed dressings. They argued with the quartermaster about gauze—a small argument, conducted for the pleasure of arguing about something honest. They ate the midday stew, which tasted like the midday stew, which was its own kind of consolation. And all the while they made their arrangements inside their head, quietly, item by item, the way one packs a bag for a journey one refuses to name aloud.

The ledger stayed where it was, sewn against Sebastian's ribs, warm from their body, heavier every day in a way that had nothing to do with paper. Odile Ferran lay in the third ward, mending by inches, her testimony the living twin of the ink. The duplicate pages—the copy Odile was to carry to Verenne—waited folded in the false bottom of Amity's sewing basket, which no one on God's earth would search twice. And Wren—

Wren was the problem. Wren was always the problem, in the way that anything precious is a problem. A child cannot be sewn into a coat lining.

In the evening Sebastian found Tomas Reyes in the linen store, folding sheets with the exaggerated precision of a man avoiding a conversation.

"You've heard about the fire," Sebastian said.

"Everyone's heard about the fire."

"And?"

Tomas set down the sheet he was holding and smoothed it flat, though it was already flat. "And I think," he said carefully, "that you should tell me what happens next. Because you have that face, Seb. The one you get before you do something enormous and don't tell anybody until afterward."

Sebastian looked at their oldest friend—at the sheet-folding, the careful hands, the brother in the Fourth Regiment whose name had come up once too often in Corvane's mouth—and felt the terrible weight of trusting someone, the full cost of it, due and payable.

"Soon," Sebastian said. "I'll need you soon. Two nights, perhaps three. Can you be ready to be surprised?"

Tomas laughed, but it came out wrong, high at the edges. "I'm always ready to be surprised. That's my whole life with you." He picked up another sheet. He would not quite meet Sebastian's eyes, and Sebastian, filing it away in the ledger of small wrongnesses they had begun keeping behind their breastbone, chose—for the last time in their life, though they did not know it—to believe the best of a friend.

* * *

That night, Wren would not sleep.

She sat up in her nest behind the supply crates, blankets around her shoulders, watching the door with the patience of a sentry.

"The fire," she said finally, when Sebastian came to check on her. "Whose fault was it? Really. Not the lamp."

Sebastian sat down on the crate beside her. Outside, frost was forming on the single window; the hospital creaked and settled around them like an old ship.

"Do you remember Adjutant Corvane?" Sebastian asked. "The tall man with the kind voice?"

"Yes. He looked at me like I was sums."

"He did," said Sebastian, "and I have never heard it put better. Yes. I think it was his fault. I think the men he works for wanted to burn up all the papers that remembered the wrong things. So that when the war ends and everyone writes the story down, the story will say whatever they want it to say."

Wren absorbed this. Then: "But they missed our book."

"They missed our book."

"Because you're sneakier than a general."

"I am *vastly* sneakier than a general. It's my one accomplishment."

Wren lay down then, satisfied, and was asleep within minutes, because seven-year-olds can conduct the fall of empires and then require a drink of water and twelve hours of unconsciousness. Sebastian tucked the blanket to her chin and stayed a while in the dark, listening to the child breathe and feeling the ledger press against their ribs with each of their own breaths, patient as a second heartbeat.

The enemy had stopped hiding. Very well. There was a certain relief in it; even fear gets tired of tiptoeing. Tomorrow Sebastian would speak to Odile. Tomorrow the plan would stop being a shape in the dark and become a road with a name at the end of it: Verenne, Verenne, the archive, the tribunal, the record that could not burn because it would live in too many places at once.

They were almost to the door when they heard it—footsteps in the east corridor, past midnight, soft and unhurried, stopping just outside the storeroom wall. A pause. Then the footsteps moved on toward the ward where Odile Ferran slept, and stopped again there, and did not move on for a long time.

Sebastian stood in the dark with their hand on the door latch, counting the silence, and understood that the search had already begun.
