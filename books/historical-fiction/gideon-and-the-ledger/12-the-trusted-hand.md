## 12. The Trusted Hand

Tomas Reyes had three talents in this life, and he counted them honestly, the way a man counts coins he knows won't last. The first was folding bandages so that they lay flat and came open easy, which sounded like nothing until you were the one whose hands shook too hard to do it yourself. The second was making Sister Amity laugh on the days the ward smelled of rot and there was nothing else to be made of anything. The third was knowing people — really knowing them, the way an orderly knows them, from the feet up: what a man's stride says before his mouth opens, what a woman's silence weighs.

It was the third talent that was going to kill him.

He'd known Sebastian Halloran for six years, which in hospital years was a marriage and a half. He knew that Sebastian took their tea bitter enough to strip paint because sweetness was "a luxury of people with time." He knew that when Sebastian wrote in the ledger at night — and Tomas had always known about the ledger, had pretended not to for six years, because some friendships are built on pretending not to see things — they pressed so hard on the pen that the names bled through to the next page, as if ink alone couldn't be trusted to hold them. He knew that Sebastian said "I'm fine" in exactly one tone, and every other tone meant something else entirely.

And he knew, now, where the child slept.

That was the trouble with knowing people. You couldn't unknow them when it became convenient.

* * *

Corvane had come to him at the laundry line, of all places, three days after the fire in the records room. Tomas was pinning sheets, and the adjutant appeared beside him holding one end of a pillowcase as if he'd been helping all along, which was somehow worse than if he'd arrived with soldiers.

"You're Reyes," Corvane said. "Tomas Reyes. Orderly, eleven years' service. Brother stationed at the eastern garrison."

"Emilio," Tomas said, before he could stop himself. "You've done your reading."

"I do everyone's reading." Corvane folded the pillowcase with unsettling neatness and handed it back. "Your brother has an interesting file, Señor Reyes. Interesting is a word I use carefully. It means a file that could go two ways depending on who's doing the reading."

The wind took a sheet and snapped it between them like a small thunderclap. Neither of them flinched, which Tomas would think about later, how neither of them flinched.

"Emilio drives supply wagons," Tomas said. "He's driven them badly and late for three years. His most serious crime is a card debt to a lieutenant who cheats."

"And yet there's a letter in his file from a sergeant who says Emilio was seen, twice, letting conscripted men slip away from his column at night. Twice is a kindness. Kindness reads as treason these days, depending on who's reading." Corvane smiled, and it was the smile of a man who found the weather disappointing but expected nothing better. "I'm told you're Halloran's closest friend. I'm told Halloran trusts almost no one. That must make you precious."

"I don't know what you want."

"Yes, you do," Corvane said gently. "You've known since I walked up. That's why your hands stopped moving." And indeed they had, hanging in the air with a pin between the fingers, useless as a stopped clock. "The child, Señor Reyes. Where Halloran keeps her. That's all. One name of a place, one direction of a corridor, even a floor. Nothing happens to the child — she'll be better cared for than any orphan in this country, I promise you that personally. Nothing happens to Halloran; they'll be transferred, which is a promotion wearing different boots. And your brother's file becomes very dull reading indeed. Boring files save lives, Señor Reyes. I've built a career on boring files."

Tomas pinned the sheet. His hands did it without him, which he supposed was the mercy of hands: they kept working when the rest of a man had already surrendered.

"I need to think," he said.

"Of course," said Corvane. "Take until Thursday. Though I should mention — and only because I dislike surprises — that we intend to search the east wing regardless, Wednesday night. If you tell me first, no one will ever know you told me. If you don't, everyone will wonder why you didn't warn her." He patted the laundry line once, companionably, and left the way good adjutants leave everything: slightly worse than he found it.

* * *

He thought about it, all right. He thought about it lying awake in the orderlies' dormitory listening to a man three cots over talk in his sleep about his mother's kitchen, thought about it while scrubbing floors, thought about it so thoroughly that by Tuesday evening he had worn two grooves in his own mind, walking the same circle.

Because here was the thing nobody warned you about betrayal: it doesn't feel like betrayal when you're doing it. It feels like arithmetic. A brother, warm and stupid and alive, driving wagons badly somewhere in the east, against one child he'd never met and one friend who — well. Who would understand? Wasn't that the obscene joke of it? Sebastian, of all people, might even understand. Six years of watching Sebastian weigh things in that ledger, name by name, cost by cost. Surely the woman or man or whatever saintly category Sebastian belonged to who wrote down dead soldiers' names so history couldn't cheat them would look at the scales here and say: *one living brother, Tomas. Do it.*

Except.

Except that Tuesday night he passed the linen room and heard Wren laughing.

She was learning her letters. Sebastian had spread the ledger open on the folding table among the clean sheets, and Wren sat cross-legged on a stack of pillows with a pencil gripped wrong in her fist, copying names in a wobbling hand. Tomas stood in the doorway where the lamplight wouldn't reach him and listened.

"That's an F," Sebastian was saying. "Ferran. Odile Ferran. She came through the ward in autumn with a fever, and she swore at me in three languages, and I wrote her name down anyway, because everyone gets their name written down. Even the ones who swear."

"Why?"

"Because a name is proof you were here. That's the whole trick of it, Wren. Wars can take everything except the name, if somebody's stubborn enough to keep it."

"F," said Wren, with tremendous concentration, drawing something that resembled a fence blown over in a storm.

"That's beautiful. That's the best F in the history of letters."

"It's terrible."

"It's terrible," Sebastian agreed, "and it's beautiful, both at once. Most true things are."

Wren laughed again — a child's laugh, unguarded, the kind of sound this hospital hadn't produced since before Tomas arrived — and Sebastian laughed too, quietly, and Tomas stood in the dark doorway and understood, with the full weight of his third talent, that he was not looking at a nurse hiding evidence.

He was looking at a person becoming someone new, at the exact moment it happened, and it was happening because of that terrible F and that laugh, and there was no arithmetic in the world that covered it.

He went to bed and did not sleep, and in the morning Emilio's face floated behind his eyes the way it did every year on their mother's birthday — nineteen years old in the memory forever, gap-toothed, holding up a fish he'd caught with his bare hands and refusing to believe it wasn't a miracle. Their mother had cried. She cried at everything; it was how she loved. *Mírate,* she'd say to whichever son was nearest, grabbing a face in both hands. *Mírame. Don't forget me.*

Don't forget me.

Well. There was a family business, forgetting and remembering, and apparently every Reyes was enrolled in it whether they liked it or not.

* * *

Wednesday he tried to warn Sebastian. That was the plan, the whole of his clever plan: find Sebastian, say something oblique and orderly-like — "the east wing gets inspected soon, might be wise to air out unusual linens" — and trust to six years of friendship and Sebastian's quick mind to do the rest.

He got as far as the ward door at noon and found it full of light and moaning and Sebastian moving down the rows with that unhurried step, and a boy of maybe seventeen caught Tomas's sleeve and asked him, voice cracking, whether dying hurt much, and Tomas held the boy's hand for forty minutes and forgot every word he'd prepared. By the time he got free, Sebastian was gone into surgery, and the afternoon collapsed the way afternoons did, and then it was evening, and then it was Wednesday night, and Corvane's men would come at midnight, and there was no more time for clever plans or oblique warnings.

There was only the choice, standing in front of him at last with its hat in its hands, patient as death.

He went looking for Corvane and found him in the chaplain's office, drinking tea the chaplain had sweetened for him, which told Tomas everything about which side the institution had chosen.

"The east wing," Tomas said. His voice came out steady, God help him. Steady as Sunday. "Third floor. There's a disused linen store behind the old isolation ward, the room with the painted-over window. She sleeps there. Halloran moves her before dawn every day, so if you want her, you want her tonight, before four."

Corvane set down his cup with a click, and for just a moment his face did something Tomas hadn't expected — softened, almost, the way a man's face softens when a debt he dreaded collecting turns out to be payable.

"Thank you," Corvane said, and meant it, which was the worst part. "Your brother's file is now extremely dull. Duller than it has ever been. I give you my word."

"My brother catches fish with his bare hands," Tomas said, for no reason he could name. "When he was nineteen. He thought it was a miracle."

"Then he sounds worth saving," said Corvane, courteously, and signed something, and Tomas walked out of the office on legs that belonged to somebody else.

* * *

Here is what Tomas Reyes did with the rest of Wednesday night, and let the record show it, since he had spent his life around ledgers and knew a thing or two about what records are for:

He did not sleep. He sat up in the dormitory with his one talent — knowing people — turned on himself like a lamp, and examined what he saw there without flinching, mostly.

He thought: I have saved perhaps four hundred lives in eleven years, folding bandages flat and lifting men gently and knowing from their feet when they were going to quit, and tomorrow morning I may undo all of it in exchange for one wagon-driving fool in the east, and the mathematics of that will never balance, not if I live to be a hundred.

He thought: But Sebastian would do the same. Wouldn't they? For anyone in that ward? They break protocol daily, hourly, they've broken the largest protocol there is, and they'd call my sin a smaller version of theirs, loyalty misfiled rather than absent —

He thought, and this was the thought that finally bent him double in the dark with his forehead on his knees: No. Sebastian would not do the same. That's the entire point of Sebastian. Sebastian writes down the names other people decide aren't worth keeping. Sebastian keeps the account when keeping it costs everything. That is the whole of who they are, and I have just sold the ledger's keeper, and there is no version of this where I am forgiven, least of all by myself.

And then, because he was still Tomas Reyes, still the man who made Sister Amity laugh on the days nothing else could be made of anything, he got up.

He got up at half past eleven, in the dark, and he moved through the hospital he knew better than his own heartbeat — past the wards, past the chapel where the chaplain's lamp already burned, down the service stair — to the linen store behind the old isolation ward, the room with the painted-over window, and he lifted the latch that he alone had oiled for Sebastian months ago, back when friendship was simple and latches were just latches.

Wren was asleep on her nest of blankets, one fist curled under her chin, breathing the deep slow breaths of a child who has been told she is safe by someone she believes.

Sebastian was not there. Of course. Sebastian would come before dawn to move her, as always.

So Tomas did the only thing left in his power, the small crooked thing, the thing that was not enough and was also everything: he took the stub of pencil from his pocket and, on the back of a folded linen list, in handwriting that shook worse than any wounded man's he'd ever steadied, he wrote what he could not say.

*I'm sorry. I had a brother. Go now, before midnight. Any way. Anywhere. — T.R.*

He put the note under the child's shoe where she'd be sure to find it, kissed his own fingers and touched them to the doorframe the way his mother used to touch their faces — *mírame, don't forget me* — and left the latch unlocked behind him, which would buy minutes, maybe.

Maybe enough. Maybe not.

He was halfway down the service stairs when he heard, below him, the front doors opening and boots coming in — many boots, unhurried, the boots of men who had been told they had all the time in the world because the prize was already theirs — and above him, faint through three floors of stone, a child's voice beginning to wake.

* * *

By Thursday noon, the whole hospital knew that Halloran and the girl had vanished into the night like smoke, that Adjutant Corvane's men had found an empty linen store and a note, and that Sister Amity had been taken to headquarters for questioning, walking out the gate with her spine straight as a lance and her hands folded, for all the world like a woman going to market.

They knew none of it was Tomas's doing. Not one soul guessed. Corvane kept his word about that, kept it perfectly, the way he kept all his words: technically.

Only Tomas knew. Only Tomas stood in the linen store that afternoon, restocking shelves that didn't need restocking, staring at the place where the nest of blankets had been, and understood that he had gotten exactly what he'd asked for — a dull file in the east, a brother alive, a friendship intact on the surface where surfaces were all anyone saw.

And only Tomas heard, that same afternoon, the rumble of wheels at the gate, and looked out the window to see a supply wagon arriving under escort, and a familiar figure climbing down from the driver's bench with a canvas bundle hugged to his chest like a fish he'd caught with his bare hands — Emilio, grinning, gap-toothed, waving — while at the far end of the yard, unnoticed, a second wagon was being loaded, and the man directing the loading wore an adjutant's grey coat, and was pointing west, toward the collapsing front, toward the road to Verenne.

Tomas's stomach dropped through eleven floors of the earth.

Because he knew people. It was his one gift, his curse, his whole inheritance. And he knew, watching Corvane point west, that emptying a hospital of its witnesses was not the same as letting them escape — and that whatever came next would begin on that road.
