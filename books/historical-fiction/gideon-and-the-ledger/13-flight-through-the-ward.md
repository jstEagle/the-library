## 13. Flight Through the Ward

*Sebastian*

The lamps in Ward C guttered at half past four in the morning, and Sebastian Halloran counted soldiers the way they counted everything now: by what remained.

Thirty-one men who could walk if they had to. Nine who couldn't. Four orderlies, one of whom was a traitor and didn't yet know that Sebastian knew. And beyond the windows, in the grey not-yet-dawn, the shapes of Corvane's men moving between the ambulances like crows deciding which field to land in first.

"They've been there since midnight," Tomas Reyes said from the doorway. His voice was carefully light, the voice of a man carrying a bucket of water across ice. "I counted twelve. Maybe fourteen. They keep shifting."

"Maybe you should go back to bed, Tomas."

"I couldn't sleep." He said it too quickly. Then, softer: "Seb. Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm planning to change some dressings."

He looked at Sebastian for a long moment, and there was something almost unbearable in his face—the old Tomas, the one who had shared contraband oranges and gossip through six years of war, still in there somewhere behind the fear that had sold them all. "You were never a good liar," he said. "It's one of the things I liked about you."

Then he was gone, and Sebastian stood among the sleeping wounded with their heart going like a bird against a window, and thought: *not yet a good liar, Tomas. But learning.*

* * *

*Amity*

Sister Amity had been folding linens for thirty-one years, and she had long ago concluded that laundry was the true spine of any army. Ammunition won battles; clean bandages kept men alive to fight them; and no officer, however decorated, had ever once noticed either. There was a sermon in that. She'd been meaning to write it down.

This morning the linen cart was loaded with unusual care. Sheets on the bottom—heavy ones, hospital-grey. In the middle, curled around her one surviving son's knitted scarf because she refused on principle to send the child into the cold without it, Wren, who had insisted on bringing the slate and who had, after considerable negotiation, agreed to keep very quiet by pretending to be a parcel.

"Parcels don't giggle," Amity had told him.

"Parcels can't help it," Wren said, and giggled.

Now she pinned one last sheet over the top, tucked the corners with the crisp efficiency of decades, and addressed the boy through the cloth. "When I cough twice, you stay still. When I say the weather's turning, you stay still. If anyone looks under the sheets—"

"Stay still."

"You are a very bright parcel."

She took up the handles. The cart weighed more than linens ought to, but she was a broad woman built of sixty years of farm work before the nursing, and grief had never once made her weak—it had only made her particular.

At the ward doors, Sebastian was waiting with the look of someone about to do something theatrical and hating themselves for it.

"The courtyard gate's watched," Amity murmured. "But the east path past the mortuary is only watched by Private Dunmore, and Dunmore owes me for his hemorrhoids."

"Heaven preserve us from military secrets."

"Go on," Amity said. "Sing something. You sing like a cat in a drainpipe, but it'll serve."

* * *

*Sebastian*

The diversion was, by necessity, improvised from the materials at hand, and the materials at hand were thirty-one convalescent soldiers, four orderlies, and Sebastian Halloran's complete lack of singing ability.

They began at the far end of Ward C, dropping a metal kidney dish. It rang against the flagstones like a churchbell, and nine rows of men woke with the speed of veterans, which is to say instantly and with profanity.

"Sorr-ry," Sebastian called. "Clumsy. Everyone back to sleep."

Nobody went back to sleep. That was rather the point. Ward C at dawn, woken deliberately for the first time in living memory, generated noise the way a dropped beehive generates opinion. Private Calloway wanted his leg looked at immediately. Someone accused someone else of stealing boots. A man in the third bed began, inexplicably and tunelessly, to sing a song about a miller's daughter that contained words Sebastian sincerely hoped the chaplain couldn't hear from the chapel.

And then, as planned—or as much as anything was ever planned in this war—a stretcher case arrived at the admitting doors, carried by two of Corvane's own soldiers who had evidently been ordered to intercept new arrivals, and the ward's attention swung to it like a compass finding north.

"Fresh wounded!" Sebastian shouted, loud enough to carry through stone walls, out into the yard, and hopefully into the ears of every sentry wondering why the hospital sounded like a fairground. "Bring him here! All of you, clear a bed!"

The two enemy soldiers hesitated in the doorway, torn between their orders and their training. Training won; it usually did. They came in, and Sebastian met them in the aisle, all brisk authority, taking the stretcher poles. "Gunshot, left shoulder?"

"Fever, more like," one said, uneasy. "Picked him up at the crossroads. We were told—" He glanced at the shouting, swearing, singing ward full of men. "We were told to ask questions. About a child."

"Wonderful," Sebastian said, steering them deeper into the din, away from the east door, away from the courtyard, toward the one place in this building guaranteed to hold any soldier longer than duty would: "You can ask me all your questions while you help me lift him. Orderly! Hot water! And if either of you faints at the sight of a wound, do try to fall where Sister Amity can mop you up."

* * *

*Amity*

The east path was exactly as promised: one sentry, young, red-nosed with cold, standing where Dunmore should have been and clearly regretting his entire military career.

"Good morning," Amity said, sailing past with the cart.

"Halt. Where are you going?"

"Mortuary," she said, without breaking stride. "There's a gentleman in Ward B died at two o'clock and nobody's moved him yet, and I'll tell you what, son, if I have to smell him by lunchtime, so will you." She let the cart's wheel catch the rut just so, producing a slosh and a muffled thump from within, and clicked her tongue. "Buckets of water for the slabs. It's a grim business. You don't want to come."

He did not want to come. He was perhaps nineteen and had the eyes of a boy who'd signed up to be heroic and instead been assigned to watch a middle-aged woman push laundry past a corpse house. He stepped aside with visible gratitude.

"Weather's turning," Amity said quietly, in passing.

From beneath the sheets, silence. Good parcel.

She got twenty paces. Twenty glorious paces, past the mortuary wall, to where the hedge opened onto the lane and the lane ran—according to Odile Ferran's careful map drawn in charcoal on the back of a dressing paper—all the way to the river road and the refugee column heading west.

Then boots on gravel behind her, several pairs, moving quickly.

"Sister." Corvane's adjutant's voice, mild as milk. Not Corvane himself—one of his lieutenants, a narrow man with ink stains on his fingers. "That cart's due for the boiler room, isn't it? The linen rotation. I checked the schedules last night."

"How industrious of you," Amity said, and set the cart down, and turned to face him, and put her whole life into deciding what came next.

She could weep. She had seen women weep their way past harder men than this. She could bluster; she had the build for bluster. But the lieutenant had already put one hand on the cart's rail, and the boy inside it—her bright, brave parcel—was breathing in the small, controlled sips of a child trying very hard not to exist, and Amity found that she was simply done, done entirely, with letting armed men look under sheets at children.

So she hit him.

Not elegantly. Thirty-one years of folding had done nothing for her right hook, and the lieutenant was expecting a nun, not a fist. But he went down into the gravel with his nose blooming red, and Amity stood over him breathing hard and said, "That's for Kellin Bridge, you vulture, whatever you had to do with it or not."

By then three soldiers had reached her, and it occurred to her distantly, as hands took her arms, that she had just given the finest performance of her career: because every eye in the yard was now on a broad old woman fighting like a fishmonger's wife, and nobody—nobody—was looking at the cart.

"Run, you little fool," she said, not softly, not at all softly, because a confession needed witnesses and this was hers. "Whatever you're doing, whoever you are—run."

* * *

*Sebastian*

Sebastian heard the shout from inside the ward—a woman's voice, furious, cut short—and knew with a sick drop of the stomach exactly what it meant and exactly what it cost.

For one whole breath they stood frozen between two stretchers, listening to the ward roar around them, wanting with everything in them to run to the courtyard and do—what? Nothing. Nothing Amity hadn't already chosen. The plan had accounted for capture; it had accounted, God forgive them both, for Amity insisting she be the one taken, overruled all objections with a single sentence: *"I've buried sons. You've still got raising to do."*

"Orderly!" Sebastian barked instead, voice steady by main force. "This man needs lancing and I need hot water yesterday!"

The enemy soldier grabbed their sleeve. "Wait—you're Halloran. The adjutant wants—"

"The adjutant wants a nurse doing her job," Sebastian snapped, shaking free. "Or does headquarters treat fever by interrogation these days? Hold his legs down, he's burning up, and if you've got a prayer in you, now's the hour for it."

And the soldier—twenty, terrified, holding down a stranger's flailing legs—did exactly what soldiers do. He followed the nearest order, because the alternative was to think.

Through the window, Sebastian saw the laundry cart. Not the east path—the cart had stopped, abandoned, sheets thrown back and empty, in the shadow of the mortuary wall. Empty. *Empty.*

Beyond it, past the hedge, the lane lay open and grey and utterly unguarded, because Amity had bought every eye in that yard with her fists and her freedom.

"Get out," Sebastian whispered to the morning, to the small fleeing shape already lost among the hedgerows, to no one. "Run well, my darling. Run all the way."

Then they turned back to the fevered man on the stretcher, because that was the job, and the job was the shield, and the shield had to hold until nightfall.

* * *

*Amity*

They put her in the records room, which she considered a compliment. Of all the rooms in that hospital, they chose the one lined floor to ceiling with the names of the dead, as if the room itself might interrogate her.

A young guard sat outside the door, the same red-nosed boy from the east path, looking at her through the gap with an expression that had progressed from gratitude to confusion.

"You hit Lieutenant Masse," he said.

"He needed hitting. Most people do, once in their lives. It's a service."

"He was asking about the cart."

"Was he?" Amity folded her hands. Somewhere under her ribs, where fear should have been, there was instead a sensation she recognized from the day she'd watched her sons' train pull out: the terrible, clean lightness of having done the one thing that mattered. "Well. I expect he'll want to ask me about it when his nose stops bleeding. Do fetch me some water when you can, dear. And—" she paused, and decided, and spent the words like the last coins in her purse, "—if a woman comes through here tonight asking after me, dark coat, tired eyes, tell her which way I went and which way I didn't. It's the Christian thing."

The boy frowned. "Which way did you go?"

"That," said Sister Amity, "is between me and the linen."

* * *

*Sebastian*

Nightfall came, and with it Corvane himself.

Sebastian heard him before seeing him—those measured footsteps in the corridor, unhurried, the walk of a man who had already won and was collecting signatures. The ward went quiet by degrees, the way water goes still when a large fish passes.

He stopped at the foot of Sebastian's last occupied bed and regarded them over his gloved hands.

"Sister Amity has been detained," he said pleasantly. "Assaulting an officer. Serious business. Though I note the linen cart was empty when they found it, and the east sentry swears he saw nothing, which is odd, because he saw everything, and knows it." He smiled without warmth. "You've had a busy day, Nurse Halloran. So have I. I've sealed the roads to the river. I've doubled the checkpoint at Marle. And tomorrow morning, my historians arrive early—the war is ending faster than anyone scheduled."

Sebastian kept their hands flat on the counterpane and their face arranged into polite blankness, and thought of a small shape running west through hedgerows, hours ahead of the sealing, hours ahead of Marle.

"So the question," Corvane went on, gently, terribly, "is whether you'd like to know how much time you have left to be clever. I'd suggest—" he consulted a pocket watch he did not need, "—until midnight. After midnight, the record closes, and everyone in it becomes official."

He left. Sebastian stood in the darkening ward a long moment, among the sleeping men whose names they had written down one by one in a book that was, at this hour, sewn into a coat walking the river road beside a child.

Midnight. The record closes at midnight.

Sebastian reached for their own coat. There was a tribunal forming at Verenne, and a race to run, and no time left in this war for invisibility.
