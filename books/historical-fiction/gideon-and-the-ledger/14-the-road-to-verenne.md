## 14. The Road to Verenne

The refugee column moved the way water moves through a cracked field—slowly, finding every path of least resistance, pooling at each obstacle before spilling over it. Sebastian walked in its middle, where the cart wheels and the old men and the women with children on their hips made a kind of camouflage. To walk at the edge of a column was to be noticed. Sebastian Halloran had spent eleven years learning how not to be noticed, and the skill had followed them out of the hospital gates like a loyal dog.

The ledger rode against their ribs.

It had taken Sister Amity's sewing needle and most of one night to do it properly—a channel cut between the coat's outer cloth and its lining, the pages folded flat and slid in, the seam closed with stitches so small they looked like flaws in the thread. Amity had worked by candle stub, tongue between her teeth, and when she finished she'd pressed her palm flat against Sebastian's chest as though checking for a heartbeat.

"There," she'd said. "Now you're carrying it the way it should be carried. Close."

She had not said goodbye. She had said, "Mind the mud on the Marle road," as if that were the whole of what she meant, and then she had turned back to her ward, and Sebastian had understood that this was how Amity said goodbye: with practical instructions, delivered briskly, while her hands shook where Wren couldn't see them.

That had been four days ago. Four days of mud on the Marle road.

* * *

Wren walked well, better than a seven-year-old should have, which was itself an indictment of something. The child had learned early that stillness and quiet were currencies, and spent them carefully. But even careful children wore down, and by the third afternoon Wren had begun asking questions in the particular rhythm of a child rationing curiosity against exhaustion—one question per mile, saved up like sweets.

"Is Verenne big?"

"Big enough."

"Bigger than Kellin?"

Sebastian hesitated a half-step too long, and Wren caught it, because children always catch it. But Wren only nodded and filed the name away, and Sebastian's chest did the thing it had been doing since the farmhouse—the tightening, the cold slide of it—every time the bridge surfaced in the child's mouth.

They slept in ditches and hay barns and once, gloriously, under a bridge that was not Kellin Bridge, which Sebastian chose deliberately and would not have explained to anyone, least of all themself. They ate bread gone hard as roof slate and apples with more bruise than fruit, and Wren chewed everything with the grim diligence of a soldier, which made Sebastian laugh the first time and ache the second.

"You chew like Private Odile," Sebastian said, before thinking.

Wren brightened. "The loud one? At the hospital? She gave me half her pudding once and told Sister Amity I needed building up." A pause, then, quieter, the question rationed and precious: "Will we see her again?"

"At Verenne, if she made it. Which she will have. People who've decided to be brave don't usually stop halfway."

"Did you decide to be brave?" Wren asked. One question per mile, but some miles were longer than others.

"I decided to be stubborn," Sebastian said. "Brave is mostly stubborn wearing nicer clothes."

Wren considered this with enormous seriousness, then nodded once, satisfied, and asked no more until the next mile.

* * *

On the fifth day the column swelled. Word had come down from somewhere—rumor traveled faster than armies in this war—that the front was collapsing entirely, that the regiments were pulling back, that the roads east would be open perhaps a week, perhaps two days, nobody knew. And so the road filled with everyone who had waited too long to leave and everyone who had left too late: farm families with their geese tied to carts, old men pushing wheelbarrows of things no one would ever want, a woman carrying a birdcage with no bird in it, who, when Sebastian asked, said only, "It sang," and kept walking.

Sebastian found they could not stop cataloguing names. It was the habit of years; the hand wanted a pencil, the mind wanted a page. The woman with the cage—no name given, but the bird had sung. The wheelbarrow man—called himself Perrin, pushed his barrow like it held his mother's bones, and perhaps it did. Sebastian said none of it aloud. There was no ledger to write it in. There was only the ledger there was, stitched against the heart, full, closed, and meant for other hands.

Still, the names accumulated somewhere behind the eyes, pressing. Sebastian had begun to understand that the ledger had never really been paper. Paper burned. Amity's records room had proven that. The ledger was whatever part of a person refused, year after year, to let a name go unspoken—and you could stitch that into a coat, but you could also stitch it into nothing at all, into memory alone, and carry it across any border ever drawn.

Wren tugged Sebastian's sleeve. "There's a boy with a hurt eye."

"Where?"

"Behind us. He's trying not to cry about it."

Sebastian turned. Some twenty paces back, a boy of perhaps ten walked with one eye swollen shut and his jaw set like a fortification. Sebastian fell back beside him, produced from their coat the last clean rag and the last of the water, and did what nurses do—which is to say, made small competent noises while doing very little, and let competence itself be the medicine.

"Fence post?" Sebastian asked.

"Cow," the boy said, with dignity.

"Ah. Cows are worse."

"They don't mean it," the boy said. "That's what my mam says. But it doesn't help when you're the one hit."

"No," Sebastian agreed. "It generally doesn't."

The boy's mother appeared shortly, wary as a deer, and Sebastian retreated, having accomplished nothing except the acknowledgment of a swollen eye in a world that preferred such things unwitnessed. That was the trade, Sebastian thought. You could not fix anything anymore—you were a fugitive with a needle and a rag—but you could witness. Witnessing was nearly nothing. Witnessing was everything.

Wren watched the exchange with the expression of someone taking notes.

* * *

They stopped that evening in the lee of a collapsed barn, and Sebastian opened the coat—not the lining, never the lining—and pulled out the heel of bread and divided it with the fairness of long practice. Wren ate their share and then sat looking at the darkening road with the terrible patience of a child assembling a hard question.

Sebastian braced. Hard questions from Wren tended to arrive at dusk, when the column settled and the day had no distractions left to offer.

"Wren," Sebastian said, preempting, "eat your crust."

"I ate my crust."

"Then ask what you're going to ask."

Wren's feet swung, not quite reaching the ground from the low wall they sat on. "If I'd forgotten the face," the child said, "like the note said. If I'd done what it said. Would we be safe?"

The question landed the way the cow had landed on the boy: without meaning to, and worse for it.

Sebastian looked at the child—this small person wrapped once in a general's blanket, whose face a powerful man had ordered erased, whose existence a whole apparatus of adjutants and chaplains and fires had bent itself against—and understood that Wren had been carrying the note in their head all this time the way Sebastian carried the ledger. Folded flat. Close. Rationing the question until dusk.

"No," Sebastian said. Because lying to Corvane had turned out to be possible, but lying to this face was not.

Wren blinked. "But the note said—"

"The note was wrong. Or rather—" Sebastian sat down on the wall too, so their shoulders were nearly level, which seemed important. "Listen. If you'd forgotten, do you know what would have happened? Nothing good. You'd have been some child with no name and no face, and children like that get absorbed. Sent to institutions. Given new names that aren't theirs. Or lost entirely, which happens more than anyone writes down." A breath. "And I'd have handed you over at the gate like a parcel, and there'd be one fewer witness in the world, and I'd have slept fine, probably, because people always sleep fine after doing the convenient thing. That's the horror of it. It wouldn't even have cost me sleep."

"But we're not safe now."

"No," Sebastian said. "We are extremely not safe now."

"Then forgetting would have been safer."

"For whom?" Sebastian asked, and heard, with a wince, the exact tone of a lecturer, and softened. "Here. Let me try again, because you deserve a straighter answer than that."

Wren waited, patient as the road.

"When I was younger—I'm talking before the war, before nursing—I believed something very comfortable. I believed that if you stayed small and quiet and useful, the world would leave you alone. History happened to other people. Big things happened elsewhere. My job was just to keep my head down and my hands clean and my name off every list." Sebastian turned the bread heel over in their fingers. "I was wrong, but here's the thing: I wasn't stupid to believe it. Lots of people believe it. It's even true, for a while. For years, sometimes. And then one morning there's a child at the back door with a note, and the comfortable belief dies, and you find out what you actually think."

"What do I actually think?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Sebastian smiled despite everything. "You asked whether forgetting would have kept us safe. Here's my honest answer: maybe. For a while. Maybe a long while. Maybe forever, if forever means dying quietly in some village where nobody knows what you were. Safe isn't nothing. I'd be lying if I said I didn't want it. Every step of this road I've wanted it, wanted to put you on a farm cart heading south and take my ledger to Verenne myself and testify and never once say your name."

Wren was very still. "Why didn't you?"

"Because of the faces at Kellin Bridge." Sebastian said it plainly, because plainness was all the truth had left. "Because the ledger has forty-one names in it from that day, and not one of them is yours, but you saw what I only wrote about secondhand. You're not evidence, Wren—I need you to hear me—you're a person. But you're also the only living pair of eyes that saw what those forty-one saw, and if I let them erase your face, I'd be helping them erase the bridge all over again. And I did that once already, for eleven years, in my own way. Writing names down at midnight and telling myself it was enough. Telling myself history happened elsewhere."

"It doesn't happen elsewhere?"

"It happens wherever somebody decides to remember out loud," Sebastian said. "That's the whole secret of it. It's never elsewhere. It's wherever you're standing."

Wren absorbed this in silence. Somewhere down the column a baby cried and stopped; a goose complained and didn't. The sky went the color of a bruise healing.

"So the note lied," Wren said finally.

"The note was afraid," Sebastian said. "Whoever wrote it—they were frightened, and they thought fear was wisdom. It usually dresses itself up that way." And does that not describe me perfectly, Sebastian added silently, to nobody.

Wren slid off the wall and stood close, not touching, in the way of a child who had learned touch was rationed too. Then, deliberately, the small cold hand found Sebastian's and held it.

"I'm glad you didn't forget," Wren said. "Even though it's dangerous."

"So am I," Sebastian said, and discovered, examining the sentence afterward, that it was entirely true, and that being entirely true made it heavier and lighter at once. "Though ask me again at the Marle checkpoint and I may give you a different answer."

"What happens at Marle?"

"A checkpoint," Sebastian said. "Soldiers, papers, questions. We'll be fine. I've spent eleven years being invisible. I'm very good at it."

"You said you're done with that."

"I said I testified to it," Sebastian corrected. "Being done is a process."

Wren laughed—the first real laugh since the hospital, high and startling like a bird returning to a cage it had escaped—and Sebastian held the sound the way they once held names, refusing to let it go unrecorded.

* * *

They walked the next day with the boy and his cow-wounded family, and the day after that the column passed a burned supply wagon and everyone pretended not to look and everyone looked. On the eighth morning the land changed—the hills flattening, hedgerows giving way to open country—and refugees ahead began to murmur that Verenne lay past the river, two days, maybe three, that the archive city had stayed neutral, that neutral cities had tall pale towers and gates that never closed.

Sebastian allowed themself, for exactly one hour, to imagine it: the ledger surrendered into proper hands, testimony given, a small room with a lockable door, Wren fed and schooled and safe. The imagination fit poorly, like a coat tailored for a smaller person, but it was warm, and Sebastian wore it anyway.

Then, near midday, the mood of the column changed.

Sebastian felt it before seeing it—the way a ward feels fever before the thermometer confirms it, a ripple running backward through the crowd, heads turning, voices dropping. People began peeling off toward the ditches, driving carts into the grass, gathering children.

At the far bend of the road, where it crossed a low rise, riders had appeared. Not many. Four, five. Dark uniforms, unhurried, sitting their horses with the particular stillness of men who are not fleeing anywhere.

One of them raised a hand, and the column ahead stopped moving, the whole river of it going suddenly pond-still, and in the silence Sebastian heard a voice carry down the wind—courteous, unhurried, horribly familiar—asking, in the tone of a man requesting directions, whether anyone had seen a nurse from the rear hospital.

Beside Sebastian, Wren's hand found theirs again and gripped.

"Don't move," Sebastian murmured, eyes on the riders, mind already running the road ahead faster than any of the horses could. "Don't run. Running tells them everything."

The rider on the white horse began, slowly, to come down the hill.
