## 15. Checkpoint at Marle

Odile Ferran had learned to count in hospitals. Count the steps from bed to window. Count the bells between one dressing change and the next. Count the names she could not say aloud without her throat closing. So now, three days on the road ahead of two people she had sworn to protect, she counted again: eleven wagons ahead of hers in the queue at Marle, four soldiers at the barricade, one sergeant with a list, and — she made herself look twice to be sure — no sign yet of Sebastian and the child.

That was the arrangement. That was the plan she had agreed to with her whole chest aching and her hands steady, which was more than she'd managed for anything since Kellin Bridge. She traveled ahead. She drew the eyes. She gave them something else to look at while a nurse and a small quiet child passed through the checkpoint like smoke.

*You don't owe them anything,* Tomas had told her once, back when he was still a friend and not whatever he was now. *You survived. That's your whole duty.*

But survival, Odile had discovered, was not a place you arrived. It was a debt accruing interest, and every night on this road she lay in whatever barn or ditch the refugee column offered and felt the sum growing.

"Name?" The sergeant at the barrier did not look up. He was a heavy man with a clerk's boredom and a soldier's hands, and the list under his elbow was longer than any checkpoint list needed to be.

"Ferran. Odile Ferran." She kept her voice flat, the way Sister Amity had taught them all — pain presented plainly gets you a cot; pain performed gets you a screen drawn around you and forgotten.

"Occupation."

"Widow. Seamstress." Both true, in their ways. She had sewn through half a war's worth of uniforms, and she had buried a husband before the war took an interest in her personally.

The sergeant ran his thumb down the list. Odile watched his thumb and thought, absurdly, of the ledger. Of the night in the hospital when Sebastian had shown it to her — pages of names in a small careful hand, the dead of Kellin Bridge restored to existence one line at a time. Private Odile Ferran, it did not say, because she was not dead, though some mornings the distinction felt administrative.

She had refused, at first. When Sebastian asked her to carry the duplicate to Verenne, she had said no with every fiber of a woman who had spent a year learning that hope was just grief wearing its good coat. Then she had gone to check on Wren, found the child asleep with one fist curled around a scrap of paper bearing the letter *W*, practiced over and over until the page wore soft — and changed her mind. Courage, she understood now, was not a decision. It was two decisions, and the second one always cost more than the first.

"No Ferran," the sergeant said. He looked up. His eyes were the color of dishwater and about as warm. "Where from?"

"Saint-Aubin way. Before it burned."

A flicker. Not sympathy — recognition. Half the column was from places that were now words instead of places. He waved her forward, then, as her cart creaked past the barrier, put out a hand. "Hold."

Her heart performed a maneuver she hadn't authorized.

"You've got soldier's boots," he said.

"They were my husband's." Also true. Bless Amity, who had packed Odile's bag herself and chosen every lie inside it like instruments.

The sergeant grunted, unconvinced but uninterested, and waved her through into Marle proper.

* * *

Marle had been a market town once. Now it was a funnel — the road narrowed there where the river bent, and everything the collapsing front displaced poured through: carts, goats, prams, grandmothers, an entire family carrying a grandfather in a chair with poles lashed to the arms. And at the neck of the funnel, the checkpoint, and beyond it the garrison yard where a second, quieter inspection happened for anyone whose papers invited curiosity.

Odile found a spot at the edge of the square where she could watch the queue without appearing to watch anything. She bought roasted chestnuts from a woman with a voice like gravel and made them last an hour. She had told Sebastian: *I'll go through first, mid-morning. If I see trouble, I'll make trouble. Give me until noon, then come through no matter what.* Noon was a long way off, and the queue was long, and somewhere in it — she would not let herself search, searching was how you found — were the two people she was here to spend.

She spent the waiting time doing what she did best, which was remembering things she'd rather not.

Kellin Bridge, in the hour before dawn. The fog coming off the water so thick the far bank was a rumor. The civilians driven ahead of the troops — Vane's order, though history would call it heroism — farmers and shopwives and children pushed onto the bridge as the enemy's artillery opened up, human screens in nightclothes. Odile had been behind them in the ranks, close enough to hear a woman singing to a child to keep it calm, some little looping tune, and then close enough to hear the singing stop. She had survived by falling into the river with the dead, which was fitting, since part of her had died there anyway and simply declined to inform the rest.

She had told Sebastian all of it, in the hospital, in a whisper, and Sebastian had written it down — not paraphrased, *written*, her exact words, as if her voice were evidence and not just noise. Nobody had ever done that. The officers had taken her report and given it back to her corrected, tidied, heroic. Sebastian had taken her testimony and given it back to her whole.

That was why she was sitting in a cold square eating the world's most stubborn chestnuts, waiting to throw herself on a grenade made of paperwork.

At midday the queue shortened. At ten past noon it happened.

* * *

She saw the cart before she saw them — a vegetable cart, borrowed, driven by a woman in a nurse's shawl with a boy asleep under sacking in the bed of it. Even disguised, even at fifty paces, Sebastian Halloran sat a horse and cart like a person apologizing for occupying space, and Odile nearly laughed aloud at how badly they wore anonymity. And the sacking shifted, just slightly, in the rhythm of a child breathing, and Odile's heart went out ahead of her like a scout and came back reporting danger.

Because the sergeant with the list had stopped being bored. A lieutenant had joined him. Behind the lieutenant stood a man in a headquarters coat, immaculate amid the mud of Marle, and Odile knew that posture from descriptions whispered in hospital corridors: Adjutant Corvane, or one of his hounds wearing his manners. He was examining each traveler the way a man examines coins for clipping.

They were checking faces against a paper. Odile didn't need to see the paper. There was one face in the province worth a description and a reward, and it was seven years old and currently pretending to be laundry.

*Give me until noon, then come through no matter what* — those had been Sebastian's instructions, and Odile was now revising them, alone, in a square full of witnesses, with perhaps ninety seconds of queue left before a vegetable cart reached the barrier.

So she stood up, brushed crumbs from her skirt, and did the bravest stupid thing of her life with all the naturalness she could manufacture.

She began to scream.

* * *

"The miller!" she shouted, pointing back down the refugee column, toward a gray-bearded man three carts back who had the profound misfortune to resemble nobody in particular. "That's him! That's the man from Saint-Aubin — he was at Kellin Bridge — he drove the carts for them, he—"

Every head in the square turned. The lieutenant turned. Most beautifully, Corvane's hound turned, and — this was the miracle, this was the thing Odile would feed on for years if she lived to have years — he turned *away from the queue.*

"What man? What about Kellin Bridge?" The hound crossed the square toward her at speed, and the sergeant abandoned his list to help him, and the barrier was suddenly held by one bored private and the general gravitational confusion of a crowd watching an accusation unfold.

"He wore the gray coat," Odile pressed on, tears arriving now — genuine ones, which was convenient, grief being a well she never lacked for. "I'd know him anywhere. He was there when—" Her voice broke, artfully, truthfully. "He was there when my husband died."

The miller, poor soul, had gone white as flour and started denying things no one had yet said. The crowd, which had walked a hundred miles and eaten nothing worth the walking, closed around the spectacle with the gratitude of people offered free theater. And through the middle of it — Odile saw it only in glimpses, snatched between heads and shouts — the vegetable cart rolled up to the barrier, the private glanced at a nurse's travel chit, glanced at the sleeping lump of sacking, and waved it through with the weary indifference of a man who wanted his lunch.

Sebastian did not look back. That was the discipline of them; Odile loved them for it and cursed them in the same breath. The cart passed out of Marle on the north road and became a speck and then a memory, and Wren slept on under the sacking, carried toward Verenne by a plan that was working, which never happened, which meant—

"—and you, madam." The hound was in front of her. Close. He smelled of ink and boot polish. "You'll come to the garrison and make this statement properly. You too," he added to the miller, almost as an afterthought, which was the moment Odile understood the shape of the trap she had built around herself. An accusation of Kellin Bridge involvement was not noise. It was a thread Corvane's people had been pulling across three provinces, because Kellin Bridge was the one story the victors could not afford to have told, and anyone who volunteered knowledge of it — accuser or accused — got collected.

"I'm only passing through," she said. "My statement—"

"Will be taken down properly, madam." He smiled. It was a courteous smile. Everything about him was courteous, and everything about him was a wall. "You clearly have information relevant to an ongoing inquiry. We're very grateful to citizens like you."

Behind his shoulder, the sergeant had picked his list back up, and Odile saw — with the terrible clarity that visits at the worst moments — that the list was not a checkpoint list at all. It was a ledger. Someone else's ledger. Names being gathered even as names were being erased, and hers might already be on it, because Private Odile Ferran, survivor of Kellin Bridge, existed in exactly one document in the world, and she had sewn that document into another woman's coat and sent it north.

*Well,* she thought, letting the hound guide her by the elbow toward the garrison yard, *at least it isn't in mine anymore.*

* * *

They gave her tea, which was the courtesy part, and a locked room, which was the other part. The miller sat beside her, weeping gently into his beard, having grasped faster than she had that innocence was no armor here. Through the window she could see the yard, the barrier, the queue thinning as afternoon wore on. Somewhere on the north road, a small cart was making slow honest time toward an archive and a tribunal and a midnight deadline, carrying a ledger and a child and the entire unexpurgated truth of the war.

All Odile had to do was keep this room's attention on herself.

It should have been hard. It turned out to be the easiest work she'd ever done, because the hound — his name, he said, was Lieutenant Casque, and he said it the way other men drew pistols — wanted what she wanted: talk of Kellin Bridge. He wanted dates, units, officers' names. Every question he asked to trap her, she answered with the truth, which is the one answer interrogators are least prepared for. Yes, she had been there. Yes, the civilians were driven onto the span. No, she would not say who ordered it, not to him, not into whatever record he was keeping — because she had given her testimony already, to the right person, into the right book, and it was beyond his reach now, and the knowing of that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal, burning and warming at once.

"There is no such book," Casque said, when she told him so, smiling his courteous smile. "There is the official record, and there is rumor, and rumor is a fire we put out."

"Then you'd know," Odile said, "being in the business."

He studied her for a long moment. Outside, the light was going amber. The queue at the barrier had dwindled to stragglers, and none of them, she noted with a satisfaction she allowed herself exactly once, were nurses in vegetable carts.

"You'll stay the night," Casque decided, rising. "In comfort. We may have more questions."

"And if I decline?"

"Madam," he said, with real gentleness, which was the chilling part, "the war is ending. Soon there will be no declining anywhere. Consider yourself preserved."

*Preserved.* Like a specimen. Like a name in someone's ledger.

They locked the door, and Odile sat in the fading light across from the still-weeping miller — whom she would apologize to, she promised herself, if either of them lived, and buy a drink besides — and took stock. She had spent herself completely: her cover, her freedom, quite possibly her future, traded for ninety seconds of a crowd's attention and one cart waved through a barrier. By any arithmetic she'd been raised on, a bad bargain. A fool's bargain.

But then, her arithmetic had changed the night she'd watched a child practice the letter *W* until the page wore soft.

She went to the window. Below, a lantern was lit at the barrier. Beyond the town, the north road ran black and certain toward Verenne, and somewhere along it — slower now, no doubt, the horse tired, the child asking questions in that grave small voice, Sebastian probably apologizing to the horse — the truth was traveling.

Let them preserve her, Odile thought. Let them write her name in whatever lists they liked. Names were not the erasure they seemed to think. Names were seeds. She knew that now. You could lock a woman in a room, but you could not lock up what she'd set walking down a road.

She heard boots in the corridor — two pairs, unhurried — and then the rattle of the key, earlier than any interrogation had a right to begin.

The door opened, and standing behind Lieutenant Casque's courteous shoulder, immaculate despite the dust of three provinces, was a man Odile had never seen but knew instantly from a hundred descriptions: gray-eyed, patient, kind-faced as a portrait of mercy.

"Private Ferran," said Adjutant Corvane, closing the door softly behind him. "Or should I say — witness for the defense. I understand you've been telling the truth to my lieutenant. How refreshing. Now tell it to me, and we shall see which of us the historians believe."
