## 16. The General's Letter

The farmhouse had been a handsome thing once. You could tell by the bones of it—the wide stone hearth, the staircase balustrade still standing like a row of broken teeth, the pattern of faded roses on wallpaper that clung to the walls in patches the way ivy does, out of habit rather than hope. Now its roof was mostly sky, and snow came in through the gaps and lay on the kitchen floor in thin white sheets, as if the house were making its bed for guests who would never come.

Sebastian chose it anyway. Refugee columns do not stop where it is comfortable; they stop where it is possible, and this was possible. The walls still stood three-quarters high. There was a hearth to burn what could be pried loose, a back door that opened onto a birch wood—escape, if escape became necessary—and, most importantly from Wren's point of view, there was a hayloft above the stable that had not yet burned.

"A castle," Wren pronounced, standing in the doorway with their chin lifted, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat two sizes too big.

"It's a ruin," Sebastian said.

"It's a *ruined* castle," Wren said, with the patience of a tutor correcting a slow pupil. "That's better. Ruined castles have secrets."

They did not know how right they were. Not yet.

* * *

The column passed through at dusk without stopping—a river of carts and donkeys and people carrying their whole lives balanced on their heads—and Sebastian decided they would stay one night only, leave before dawn, put Marle well behind them before Corvane's men thought to look this far down the road. The checkpoint at Marle was four days gone now, and Odile four days gone with it, taken because she had shouted at the wrong sergeant about the wrong wagon, and Sebastian had walked out of that checkpoint carrying her sacrifice like a stone sewn into their chest, right beside the ledger sewn into their coat.

So: one fire, small. One meal of barley and salt pork, stretched thin. One night of sleep in a house with secrets.

Wren, fed and warm, explored the way children explore—thoroughly, loudly, and with no respect whatsoever for ruins. Sebastian let them. It was good to hear footsteps in a dead house. It made the house remember it had been alive.

Sebastian themselves went looking for firewood, which meant going through the rooms methodically, which meant—and they knew this about themselves, had known since the first name written in the first ledger—that they would end up reading whatever paper the war had left behind. Nurses read everything. It was not curiosity; it was triage of information, the same instinct that sorted the dying from the wounded from the merely frightened. A piece of paper might be nothing. It might be orders. It might be names.

The front parlor had lost half its roof and gained half a birch tree, which had come down in some storm or shellfall and now lay across the floor with its roots in the fireplace like a guest who'd tried to move in. Snow filtered through the broken rafters. Along one wall stood a bureau, cherrywood, its mirror cracked into a map of rivers.

In the drawer of that bureau, under a child's stocking and a broken thimble and a handkerchief embroidered with forget-me-nots, Sebastian found a packet of letters tied with ribbon.

They nearly left them. They truly did. Some other night, some other year, they would have—there is only so much grief a person can carry, and refugee roads are already heavy with it. But the topmost envelope bore a seal Sebastian knew. They had seen it pressed into wax on hospital dispatches, on requisition forms, on the death lists that never matched the ward counts.

An eagle standing on a broken wheel.

General Aldous Vane's seal.

* * *

Sebastian sat down on the parlor floor with their back against the bureau, coat pulled tight around them, and broke the seal the way you'd open a wound you already knew was there.

The letter inside was written in a fine, upright hand—the hand of a man who had been taught penmanship by tutors and never once used writing to say anything true. It was dated eleven months ago. It was addressed simply: *To the Lady Elowen Marsh, at the house by the mill, Kellin.*

And it began, *Madam,* which told Sebastian before anything else did that there had been no tenderness here at all. Men who love write names. Men who are managing a problem write *Madam.*

*It has come to my attention that you persist in certain claims regarding the parentage of your daughter. I will be plain with you, as I trust you will be wise enough to be plain with me.*

*The war enters its final year. What is written in this year will be written forever. I have spent my life in service to this country, and I will not see that service reduced to rumor by persons of no account.*

Then came the sentence that Sebastian read three times, because the mind refuses such things on the first pass and argues on the second:

*I am informed you intend to present the child publicly. You will not. Arrangements have been made. By month's end she will be placed where no inquiry can reach her, and you will be compensated for your silence. This is not a threat, madam. It is an act of mercy, which you may interpret as you choose. A man must be permitted to finish his work unencumbered. History will thank us both.*

*Forget the face. It is kinder to her than you understand.*

*A.V.*

Below the signature, in a different hand—a clerk's hand, smaller, dutiful—was a line that turned Sebastian's blood to winter: *Disposition authorized per Gen. Order 44. Escort: Capt. R. Corvane. Destination withheld from record at the General's instruction.*

Corvane. Who had stood in Sebastian's hospital ward with his hat in his hands and offered promotion for silence. Who had offered passage home and immunity, and called it mercy. Who had signed himself, eleven months before he ever set foot in that ward, as the escort assigned to make a seven-year-old girl disappear.

*History will thank us both.* Sebastian sat very still in the cold parlor and felt something in themselves go quiet—not fear, though fear was there too, humming along underneath like a wire—but clarity. Cold, clean, terrible clarity. They had spent weeks imagining why. What threat could a child possibly pose? What intelligence, what witness, what weapon? And here it was, in the General's own hand: none at all. No threat but a face. A face with Vane's own jaw, perhaps. Vane's own eyes. Living proof, walking around in the world, that the hero of Kellin Bridge had a daughter he could not claim and could not permit.

Not duty. Vanity. Vanity wearing duty's uniform, saluting in mirrors, decorating itself with other men's graves.

And the note at the hospital door—*forget the face*—had not been a kidnapper's caution. It had been the general's own phrase, sent out like a coin, worn smooth from use. He had said it to Elowen Marsh first. He had probably said it to himself every morning for eleven months, a kind of prayer. *Forget the face, and history will thank us both.*

Somewhere below, Wren was singing. A small tuneless song about barley, invented on the spot, verses added as inspiration struck. Sebastian folded the letter with hands that did not shake—they were a nurse; their hands never shook when it mattered—and untied the ribbon on the rest of the packet.

There were six letters in all. Five were from Elowen Marsh, unsent replies, kept and then kept again, the way people keep letters they cannot answer and cannot burn. Sebastian read them all. The lady of the mill-house had begun firm and ended fierce, and somewhere in the middle had written a paragraph that Sebastian knew they would carry to the tribunal and beyond:

*You speak of history as though it were a house you own, sir, and we the furniture in it. But I was at Kellin. I saw what crossed that bridge and what was driven across it. My daughter was born of that day as surely as of any bed, and she will not be made a secret so that your statue may stand clean. You ask me to forget her face. Sir, I looked at it every morning for seven years. It was the best thing I ever saw. Whatever you take from me, you will not take that.*

The last letter was dated nine months ago, and stopped mid-sentence.

*If you send your men to the house by the mill I will tell them the truth of the bridge, every—

Nothing after. No closing, no signature. Just the nib dragged hard enough to tear the paper, and then, in the margin later, in a shaking hand, two words: *they came.*

The house by the mill at Kellin. Sebastian looked at the torn sentence and understood, with the flat certainty of a diagnosis, that they were holding the last thing Elowen Marsh had ever written. And that the child singing about barley in the room above had watched the men come, and been carried away by Captain R. Corvane per General Order 44, destination withheld from record—and had somehow, by some mercy or fluke or the care of someone whose name was now also lost, ended up wrapped in a general's blanket on the back step of a rear-echelon hospital with a note instructing the world to forget her.

Placed where no inquiry can reach her. Except she had reached a hospital instead. And the one person in that hospital who forgot nothing had found her.

Sebastian did not believe in fate. Nurses cannot afford it; fate is what people say when they want the chaos excused. But they did believe in arithmetic, and the arithmetic here said: this letter must survive. This letter plus the ledger plus Odile Ferran's testimony plus whatever a seven-year-old carried in her head—that was the case. That was the record. That was the difference between Vane's statue standing clean and the truth standing at all.

The fire had burned low. Upstairs, the singing had stopped, replaced by the particular silence of a child asleep. Sebastian sewed the packet into the lining of their coat alongside the ledger—six stitches, seven, careful even work, the work of hands that had closed a thousand wounds—and sat for a moment longer in the ruined parlor while the snow came down through the broken roof and covered the birch tree, the cracked mirror, the dust of a family that had fled or died or both.

*You will not take that,* Elowen Marsh had written about her daughter's face.

"No," Sebastian said aloud, to the empty room, to the woman wherever she was, to the general in his headquarters polishing his reputation like brass. "We won't."

* * *

"Seb. *Seb.* Wake up. There's a ghost."

Sebastian surfaced from sleep in the loft's straw to find dawn grey through the gaps in the boards and Wren's face an inch from theirs, urgent with importance.

"There's no such thing as ghosts," Sebastian murmured, "and if there were, they wouldn't wake people up to announce themselves."

"This one would. He's downstairs and he's *rude*. He knocked over the chair."

Sebastian was fully awake now, hand already finding the coat, the seams, the paper-weight of the letter against their ribs. They crept to the loft ladder and looked down into the kitchen.

The chair by the hearth had indeed fallen over. The cause of this was not a ghost but a goat—a scrawny, ash-grey goat with one crooked horn, standing in the wreckage of their small fire's ashes and regarding the stairs with the flat insolence of its kind.

"That," Sebastian said, "is a goat."

Wren considered this from the top rung. "He looks like a ghost."

"He looks like breakfast, if we're lucky and he's stupid."

"You can't eat a ghost-goat," Wren said gravely. "He'll haunt our insides."

Despite everything—the road, Marle, the letter sewn against their heart—Sebastian laughed. It came out rusty, like a hinge in a house nobody lived in, and it seemed to surprise them both. Wren grinned, delighted at having produced this effect, and scrambled down to negotiate with the goat, informing him sternly that this was their castle and he could stay only if he shared secrets.

Sebastian stood by the window while the negotiation proceeded below, watching the birch wood for movement, listening for hooves that were not goats'. The column would be moving by now, miles east. Corvane's men would find Marle checkpoint's logs, find the false lead Odile had spun, and begin casting further down the road. A day, maybe two. Then Verenne, and the archive, and whatever cautious sanctuary neutrality offered.

But something else was stirring under the practical reckoning, something Sebastian had been circling for days without naming. The letter named Elowen Marsh. It named Kellin, the house by the mill. It dated the taking of the child to eleven months ago—which meant Wren had been at Kellin before that. Before the bridge? Near it? Seven years old, born of that day, Elowen had written, as surely as of any bed.

Sebastian descended the ladder slowly, watching the child attempt to feed a goat a crust it had no intention of accepting, and asked, as lightly as they could, "Wren. Before the hospital. Where did you live?"

Wren didn't look up. "With Mama. At the mill." The crust vanished; the goat sneezed. "By the water. The wheel went round and round and Mama said—" The small face tilted, and something moved behind the eyes, some door opening onto a long corridor. "Mama said the bridge sang. When the wind came up the valley. Did you know bridges sing?"

"I didn't," Sebastian said quietly.

"People walked on it," Wren went on, and the playfulness had gone out of their voice entirely, leaving something older, something that did not belong to a seven-year-old. "Lots and lots of people. And then it didn't sing anymore." The child looked up, and their eyes—Vane's eyes, God help them all—were wide and puzzled, a scholar confronted with a page that wouldn't add up. "Seb, why do I dream about the bridge? I wasn't supposed to see it. Mama always said close your eyes. She held my hands over my ears and sang louder. But I *did* see it."

The goat, sensing the change in the weather, wandered off to inspect the ashes.

Sebastian knelt down on the frozen kitchen floor of a ruined farmhouse and took both small cold hands in theirs, the way Elowen Marsh must have done, the way mothers do, covering ears that no longer needed covering.

"Tell me," Sebastian said. "Only if you want to. Only when you're ready. But Wren—if you remember—tell me everything."

Outside, far down the valley road, too faint for Wren's ears but not for a nurse's trained ones, came the sound Sebastian had been dreading for four days: engines. More than one. Coming not from the direction of Marle.

Coming from Verenne.
