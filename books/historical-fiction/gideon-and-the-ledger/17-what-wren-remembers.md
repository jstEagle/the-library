## 17. What Wren Remembers

Wren had a system for the nights when the dreams came.

It was not a very good system, as systems went. It involved counting the cracks in whatever ceiling hung overhead — this farmhouse had eleven, or possibly twelve, because one of them might have been two cracks being friendly — and naming everyone Sebastian had ever written in the ledger that Wren could remember seeing. Which was cheating, maybe, because Wren wasn't supposed to know about the ledger's insides. But nobody had said anything about knowing them on the *inside of your own head*, and heads were where nobody could search.

The trouble with the system was that the ledger names were all soldier names, and the dream names were not.

In the dream there was a bridge. That was the part Wren never told anyone, not even in the half-asleep mutterings that made Sebastian sit up fast in the dark and say *Wren, Wren, you're here, you're at Marle, we crossed already.* In the dream there was a bridge, and it was made of wood, and it sang. Not music-sang. Groaned, the way floorboards do when someone heavy walks on them, except the whole bridge did it at once, so many someones that the someones were the bridge itself.

Wren knew that was a strange thing for a seven-year-old to carry around. Wren didn't know the word *atrocity*, which was a word Sebastian said sometimes in a low voice to the coat, to the seam where the ledger lived sewn flat against ribs. Wren only knew that other children — the ones from before, the ones whose faces were supposed to be forgotten — would have dreamed about supper or dogs or a birthday. Nobody else Wren's age walked a singing bridge every night and counted the people on it instead of sheep.

Tonight the eleventh crack was definitely two cracks, making twelve, and Wren gave up on the system and looked across the ruined room instead.

Sebastian slept sitting up against the wall, the way soldiers did, though Sebastian was not a soldier. Nurse, refugee, smuggler of books and children — Wren was fuzzy on the exact list, but the sleeping-upright thing had come along somewhere between the hospital and the road, like a coat borrowed and never given back. The fire was down to its last orange opinions. Outside, the wind worked at the farmhouse roof the way Wren worked at knots: patiently, expecting eventually to win.

There was a question Wren had been carrying for days now. It sat in the chest like a swallowed stone, smooth on the outside and heavy all the way through. It was about the bridge. It was about whether the things in Wren's head were worth anything, or whether they were just broken pieces of a broken time that ought to be swept out so a person could grow up normal.

Wren decided — in the way that seven-year-olds decide things, suddenly and completely, with no committee involved — that tomorrow was the day to ask.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of wet ash. Sebastian was already up, doing the mysterious adult ritual of making tea out of almost nothing: a pinch of this leaf, a dried apple slice, hot water, faith. Wren watched from the doorway, hair full of straw, and thought about how to begin. Beginning was hard. Adults always started with weather or food, little flat stones skipped across the surface of a thing, and Wren hadn't learned yet how deep you had to go before the real question would float.

"Your tea has a hair in it," Wren said, because that was an opening move even a beginner could play.

"That's my hair."

"It's still in your tea."

"Then I'm drinking my own hair, and no one can stop me." Sebastian blew on the cup. "Sit. We eat, we walk, we're in Verenne by nightfall if the road is kind."

Wren sat. The bread was yesterday's bread, which is the worst kind unless you toast it, and there was nothing to toast it on, so it was the worst kind. Wren chewed and chewed and finally put down the stone.

"Sebastian. If somebody remembered something, but they were little when it happened, would it count?"

Sebastian went very still. Wren knew all of Sebastian's stillnesses by now — the careful one for Corvane-shaped dangers, the tired one, and this one, this held-breath one, which meant *you have said something important and I am afraid to spook it.*

"Count toward what?" Sebastian asked, gentle as anything.

"Toward the record." Wren had heard that word enough times on the road to know it was the biggest word there was. Bigger than *war*. Bigger than *home*. "Toward what really happened. Or does it only count if you're grown?"

Sebastian set the cup down. For a moment Wren thought the answer would be a deflection, one of those kind lies adults kept in their pockets like coins — *eat your bread first*, or *some things are too heavy for small shoulders* — but Sebastian Halloran, keeper of names, hater of omissions, apparently couldn't lie twice in one lifetime and had used the allotment on Adjutant Corvane months ago.

"Why?" Sebastian asked. "What do you remember?"

And there it was. The door open, no turning back. Wren looked at the fire pit, at the straw, at the twelve-friendly cracks, anywhere but at Sebastian's face, and began.

* * *

"The bridge sings," Wren said. "That's the first thing. Everyone thinks bridges just stand there but this one sang when people walked on it, a groaning sound, like — like a giant with a sore throat. I was little-little. Four, maybe. Mama carried me the first time and I rode her hip and put my hand on the rope and I could feel the song in my teeth."

Sebastian had gone to the coat. Not touching it, just near it, the way you stand near a sleeping animal you don't want to wake. "Go on."

"There were carts. Carts aren't allowed on it, that's why it sang so bad, because there were so many carts, and horses, and the horses hated it. And people. So many people, and they weren't soldiers." Wren frowned, digging. Memory at four was not a picture; it was a smell, a sound, a pair of hands. "Soldiers stood at both ends. I remember thinking they were there to help us across, like bridge-guardians, because that's what I thought soldiers were then." A small, bitter laugh escaped, far too old for the face it came from. "Mama knew different. She went quiet in a way she never went quiet. Quiet like held breath."

"What happened on the bridge, Wren?"

"I don't know all of it." Honest to the bone; the ledger would have approved. "I know the middle went down. Or the middle was made to go down, I heard a man behind us say *they've cut it, God preserve us, they've cut the spans* — and then the song changed. It got higher. Like it was screaming instead of groaning. And Mama —"

Wren stopped. This was the part that lived deepest, and pulling it up hurt the way pulling a splinter hurts, worse before better.

"Take your time," Sebastian said quietly.

"Mama put me through the rail. There was a gap, a place where a plank was missing, and she put me through it onto the bank side, into the reeds, and she said —" Wren's voice went thin. "*Forget the face. Forget my face, forget yours, be nobody, be fog.* Those words. She said them like a spell, over and over, while she pushed me through the gap. And then she turned around and went back onto the singing part, back to the people, because that's where her sisters were. Her hands —" Wren's own hands came up, remembering without permission. "Her hands pushed me so hard. I fell in the mud and the mud got in my mouth and I didn't cry because she'd said forget, and crying is remembering with your whole body. So I lay in the reeds and I watched through the gap under the rail."

"Wren —"

"I saw the ends," Wren said, faster now, the words tumbling like things escaping a tipped cart. "I saw the soldiers light something. Fire on both ends at once, orange, in the mist, and the smoke went sideways because the river makes its own wind. And I heard a whistle. Three notes, high-high-low. Somebody whistled three notes and everything got loud all at once. I hear it sometimes. In the dreams. High-high-low, and then the screaming-song."

The farmhouse was very silent. Even the wind seemed to be leaning in at the window.

Sebastian knelt. Slowly, the way you kneel to a frightened horse, and took Wren's hands — the small cold ones — and held them.

"Wren. The woman's hands. Your mother's hands. Tell me about the hands."

"They were rough. From washing, she washed for people, laundry, she had soap-cracks in her knuckles, here and here." Wren traced the lines on their own fingers. "She smelled of lye and lavender because she hid the lavender in the soap to make it bearable. And she had a ring. Not a fancy ring. Wire, wrapped wire, silver-colored, on her right middle finger, and it left a mark, a pale stripe, because she never took it off. When she pushed me, I saw the stripe. Right here." Wren touched the inside of their own wrist. "The wire-ring lady. That's what I called her in my head after, when I was trying not to forget on purpose and also trying to forget on purpose at the same time. It's very confusing, doing both."

"It is," Sebastian said, and something in Sebastian's voice cracked slightly, like ice over moving water. "Wren, there is a page in the ledger. I have read it a hundred times. A laundress of Kellin village, name unrecorded in any dispatch — until mine. She drove refugees to the bridge the morning of the massacre because the soldiers told her the far side was safe. She wore a wrapped-wire ring. Her description stops there, because the survivor who told me broke off before he learned her name."

"Odile knows her?"

"Odile *is* the survivor who told me." Sebastian's thumb pressed gently against Wren's wrist, right on the pale place that existed only in story. "He said the woman with the wire ring carried a child on her hip. He said she was the last one he saw standing before the spans burned through."

Wren considered this. Somewhere inside, the swallowed stone shifted, and became — not smaller, but differently shaped. Rounder. More like something that could be carried rather than something that would sit and poison.

"So I'm the child on the hip," Wren said.

"You're the child on the hip."

"And the ledger says she did a brave thing but doesn't say her name."

"The ledger says what I knew. It never lies, but it has gaps, and gaps are how they win." Sebastian's eyes were bright and fierce. "But you, Wren. You have what I don't. You have the song of the bridge. You have the whistle — high-high-low, is it? — you have the fire at both ends in mist, you have the plank-gap and the reeds and the ring. Do you understand what that means? Every detail you hold matches the account I wrote from another mouth entirely. Two witnesses who never met, saying the same true thing. That isn't memory anymore, little bird. That's corroboration."

"It sounds like a disease."

"It sounds like proof." Sebastian laughed — a wet, astonished laugh, half sob — and pulled Wren in, and Wren let himself be pulled, cheek against the coat, feeling through the cloth the flat hard presence of the book that had been sewn there since before Wren knew Sebastian's name.

"I saw faces too," Wren said into the wool. "On the bridge. I don't remember most of them. But there was a man who played a flute. He played while everyone waited to cross, a walking-tune, so the horses wouldn't spook, and I liked him. And there was a girl with red ribbons, older than me, maybe ten, and she waved at me from the crowd like we knew each other, like waving was something people still did." Wren's throat closed. "Did they go in the ledger? The flute man and the ribbon girl? Because nobody official ever wrote them, I asked Mama once if the king knew about the bridge and she said the king knew exactly what he wanted to know."

Sebastian was quiet a long moment.

"The ledger has a section I never show anyone," Sebastian said at last. "Not Amity, not Odile. Unnamed entries. Descriptions without names, because a name refused to come, but the person refused to leave. There's a line that says *a musician, flute, played to calm the horses, middle span, second hour.* And beneath it: *a girl, red ribbons, waved at a stranger.*" A breath. "I wrote those two years ago from Odile's telling, and I have wondered every day since who they were. And here you are, having waved back."

"The ribbon girl waved at *me*. I'm the stranger."

"Yes," Sebastian said softly. "You are the stranger. And now you're also the witness."

* * *

They walked after that, because roads didn't care about revelations and Verenne didn't move closer for grief. The gray morning turned to a paler gray, and the refugee column thickened around them — carts and donkeys and a man carrying a rooster like a baby, which Wren watched with deep respect.

Wren turned things over. That was the new occupation: turning things over. The bridge. The whistle. The fact that a four-year-old on a hip could hold a piece of history in their teeth the way the rope of the bridge had hummed. Grown-ups thought children forgot. Maybe children mostly tried to. But trying to forget, Wren was learning, was just remembering with the doors shut.

"Sebastian."

"Mm."

"When we get to the tribunal. Will they ask me? Or will they do the thing where they talk over my head like I'm a parcel?"

Sebastian glanced down, and there was a whole argument happening behind those eyes — Wren could see it, the wanting-to-shield wrestling the needing-to-ask — and Wren realized, with a strange adult-sized clarity, that this was exactly the choice Mama had made on the bridge. Push the child through the gap, or let the child carry something. Love wearing two different coats.

"They'll try to talk over your head," Sebastian said finally. "Historians are very tall, intellectually speaking. But you and I are going to walk in together, and you are going to tell them about the song of the bridge, and the ring, and the girl with the ribbons, and they are going to write it down. Every word. Because that's what archives do — they keep what's true — and what you have, little bird, is true all the way down."

"Even the mud in my mouth?"

"Especially the mud. Details are how truth proves it's been somewhere."

Wren walked a while on that. Then, because fairness mattered: "If I testify, they'll know my face. The note said forget the face. Whoever wrote it —" Wren had done the arithmetic weeks ago, in the quiet ruthless way children do; Sebastian's face at the mention of General Vane had finished the sum — "whoever wrote it wanted me to be fog. But fog doesn't have a mother with a wire ring. Fog doesn't wave at ribbon girls."

"No," Sebastian said. "It doesn't."

"Then forgetting the face would have kept me safe and made everything else stay lost forever. That's a bad trade." Wren kicked a stone off the road with great authority. "I choose the face."

Beside Wren, Sebastian made a small sound — half laugh, half something breaking open — and said nothing, because some things a person doesn't need to answer out loud.

Behind them, down the white line of the road, a rider appeared. Then two more. They moved with the easy, terrible confidence of men who had never once needed to hurry, and the refugee column ahead began, instinctively, like birds before weather, to edge toward the ditches.

Sebastian's hand found Wren's shoulder.

"Don't run," Sebastian murmured. "Walk with me. And Wren — start memorizing the road behind us. Everything you see. If today goes wrong, you're not just the witness to Kellin Bridge anymore."

The riders crested the hill. Gray cloaks. Headquarters colors.

"You're a witness to whatever happens next."
