## 18. The Archive at Verenne

The road into Verenne was lined with plane trees that had, in some earlier and more sensible century, been planted by someone who believed cities should welcome people rather than search them. Sebastian had decided, somewhere past the last checkpoint, that they loved those trees. It seemed important to love something on purpose before walking into a city that might not let them leave.

Wren walked with one hand in Sebastian's and the other holding a heel of bread that had gone stale two days ago but which the child refused to surrender on principle. "It's still bread," Wren had said, with the moral seriousness of a magistrate. "Bread doesn't stop being bread."

"No," Sebastian had agreed. "It doesn't."

The ledger rode against Sebastian's ribs, sewn into the lining of a coat that smelled of woodsmoke, river water, and the particular fear of three weeks on the road. Some nights Sebastian had slept sitting up with a hand pressed flat against it, the way one might check on a sleeping child — except there was an actual sleeping child beside them, so the arrangement required a certain economy of hands.

Verenne announced itself first by bells. Not the thin, panicked bells of towns near the front, rung to warn of shelling, but fat bronze bells ringing the hours as though hours were things worth celebrating. Then came the walls — old, pale, patched with brick of six different centuries — and then the gates, open, unhurried, guarded by men in grey coats who belonged to no army Sebastian could name. Neutral coats. Coats that had never once been fired at on principle.

"Is this it?" Wren asked. "Is this where the names go?"

"This is where we ask very nicely if the names may come in."

"What if they say no?"

Sebastian considered the question honestly, because Wren had earned honest answers even when honesty was heavy. "Then we ask again tomorrow. That's most of how anything gets done."

* * *

The Archive of Verenne sat behind the old law courts, a building of pale stone and tall windows that gave it the look of a face trying to appear thoughtful. Inside, the air smelled of dust and glue and something faintly sweet — the paste they used for binding, Sebastian would learn later, made from a recipe unchanged since before the war, since before several wars.

A clerk with spectacles and the posture of a drawn curtain looked up from his desk.

"We're closed to military personnel," he said.

"I'm not military."

"Everyone is military," the clerk said, without malice. "It's a condition of the age. Like lice."

"We've come a long way," Sebastian said, keeping their voice level, warm, reasonable — all the tools of the hospital repurposed. "I have documents. Original documents. Testimony concerning events at Kellin Bridge."

Something happened in the clerk's face. It was small — a tightening at the corner of the mouth, a flicker behind the spectacles — but Sebastian had spent years reading faces over bedsheets, learning which pains were spoken and which were managed. The clerk knew the name Kellin Bridge. And the clerk was afraid of it.

"The archive is neutral," the clerk said. "That word has obligations attached to it. We do not accept material that would compromise—"

"Compromise whom?" asked a voice from the stairwell.

The man descending was seventy at least, with white hair swept back like weather and a cardigan worn over what might once have been an academic's gown. He moved slowly, but Sebastian noticed his eyes arrived everywhere first. He looked at Sebastian. Then at Wren. Then, and here Sebastian felt it like a hand on the back of the neck, at the lump in Sebastian's coat where the ledger sat.

"Director Halden," the clerk said, straightening.

"You were saying something about neutrality, Pell." Halden reached the bottom of the stairs. "Finish it. I find unfinished sentences educational."

Pell finished nothing. Halden turned to Sebastian.

"Kellin Bridge," he said. "You understand that the official account of Kellin Bridge has already been drafted? The victors' historians passed through in the spring. Very thorough people. Very fine pens. There will be a monument. There may be a poem." His voice was dry as old vellum. "You are proposing to bring me documents that disagree with a poem."

"Yes," Sebastian said.

"And you understand why I cannot simply—"

"He has names," Wren said.

Everyone looked at the child. Wren, who had been standing very still in the way children do when they have decided the adults are taking too long, lifted their chin.

"In his book. He keeps everybody's names. Even the ones the officers threw away." A pause, and then, with terrible seven-year-old precision: "Even my mother's."

The silence that followed had texture. Pell took off his spectacles and cleaned them, which is what frightened men do instead of speaking.

Halden crouched, joints cracking like green firewood, until he was at eye level with Wren. Sebastian watched him take in the face — the face with its note folded away in Sebastian's memory now these many weeks: *forget the face.*

"What is your name, child?" Halden asked gently.

"Wren. For now." Wren glanced up at Sebastian, checking, and Sebastian nodded. "We haven't finished choosing yet."

Halden rose, slowly, and looked at Sebastian for a long moment. Whatever he saw there — a nurse, a smuggler, a liar by their own account, a person who had crossed a collapsing front with a stolen ledger and someone else's child — he did not seem reassured. That was the trouble with careful men; they were careful precisely because they understood exactly how much there was to lose.

"Bring me nothing today," he said. "Understand me. If I accept your documents, I accept a fight — with the historians' tribunal, with whatever government survives the peace, possibly with history itself, which fights dirty. I will not make that decision in a hallway with a tired stranger and—" he glanced down at Wren, and something in his face softened perhaps a millimeter, "—a witness too small to reach my shelf. Come back tomorrow. Let me read what you have. Privately. Then we will see whether neutrality is a wall or a window."

"And until tomorrow?" Sebastian asked.

Halden was already climbing the stairs. "Until tomorrow," he said, "you are nobody in particular. In Verenne, that is a renewable status. Guard it."

* * *

They found a room above a bakery that charged by the night and asked no questions, partly out of kindness and partly because the baker's wife had taken one look at Wren and produced a bun with the reflexive generosity of women who fed armies through worse wars than this one. Wren ate the bun and half of Sebastian's, having established earlier that bread does not stop being bread and apparently neither do buns.

"Doesn't the director like us?" Wren asked around the last of it.

"He likes caution. They aren't the same thing, but they live on the same street."

"Will he keep the names?"

"He'll decide," Sebastian said. "That's what careful men do instead of being brave. Sometimes, if you wait them out, the decisions turn into bravery. It happens. I've seen it."

"Have you?"

"Once or twice." Sebastian tucked the blanket under Wren's chin. "Mostly I've seen the other thing. But once or twice is enough to bet on, when the alternative is nothing."

Wren was quiet a moment. The bakery below had gone dark; the smell of tomorrow's loaves was already rising through the floorboards like a promise.

"When they write it down," Wren said, sleepily, "will it say what she looked like? My mother?"

"It will say everything," Sebastian said. "That's the whole point of the book."

Wren nodded, satisfied, and was asleep between one breath and the next, the way children sleep — falling as though trust were a floor.

Sebastian sat by the window a while, watching the lamps of Verenne gutter out one street at a time, and allowed themselves the dangerous luxury of believing, for almost four full minutes, that it might actually work.

* * *

Morning came with bells and bad news in equal measure.

Sebastian woke to shouting in the street — not panic-shouting, which has a loose quality, but organized shouting, shouting with paperwork behind it. They went to the window with the cold certainty of someone who has worked hospitals long enough to know the sound of authority arriving where it isn't wanted.

At the far end of the street, near the fountain, stood six men in the grey-green of the general's headquarters. Not soldiers — staff. One of them held a document and was reading it aloud to a cluster of unhappy Verennians. Even at this distance Sebastian could see the wax seal catch the light, red as a wound.

They didn't need to hear the words. But the words reached them anyway, carried up by the acoustics of narrow streets and the peculiar cruelty of mornings:

"—by warrant of the tribunal provost, the person styling themselves Sebastian Halloran, nurse, late of the Third Field Hospital, is sought for the abduction of a minor, wanted for questioning in the matter of stolen state records, and is to be detained—"

Abduction. Stolen records. Sebastian leaned against the window frame and laughed, once, briefly, the way one laughs at a joke made by an enemy who knows them too well. Of course. Of course this was the shape of it. Not *murderer*, which would require proof. Not *spy*, which would invite scrutiny. Kidnapping — a crime against a child, the one accusation that would turn every mother in Verenne against them before breakfast, the one charge that needed no evidence because suspicion did all the lifting.

Corvane hadn't sent soldiers. He'd sent a story.

"Halloran?" The baker's wife stood in the doorway, flour to her elbows, her face doing arithmetic. "They're saying downstairs there's a nurse took a general's child. That true?"

"The child's father wants the child erased," Sebastian said quietly. "There's a note. *Forget the face.* I can show it to you. I can show you a book with four hundred names in it that the same general paid to have forgotten."

The baker's wife looked at Wren, still asleep, one hand curled around nothing. She looked a long time. Then she pulled off her apron.

"Cellar," she said. "There's a door to the alley behind the ovens. My husband will argue with them about flour deliveries. He argues beautifully; it's his gift." She paused at the threshold. "I had a brother at Kellin Bridge. Official account says he died a hero. Nobody ever told me how." Her jaw set. "Go. Argue later. You argue beautifully too, I expect. Now's not the hour for it."

* * *

The alley behind the ovens was narrow and smelled gloriously of yesterday's crusts. Sebastian woke Wren with a hand over the child's mouth out of pure old habit, then felt sick at it and replaced the hand with a kiss on the hair.

"We have to go," Sebastian whispered. "Right now, quickly, no questions till we're moving."

Wren, to their eternal credit, asked zero questions and was fully dressed in forty seconds, which Sebastian filed away as either excellent parenting or damning evidence of how much practice the child had lately had at running.

They went out through the cellar, along a drainage channel that stank but ran unobserved, and came up two streets from the Archive through the grate of a cistern, emerging into a fishmonger's yard to the visible distress of several cats. By the time the warrant-men had worked their way down the bakers' street, demanding entry, asking after nurses, describing a woman — they always described a woman, Sebastian noted, Corvane's imagination had limits — Sebastian and Wren were standing in the great reading room of the Archive of Verenne, dripping ditch water onto a floor polished by three centuries of scholars.

Pell saw them first. His spectacles fogged with dismay.

"Director," he called, weakly. "The nobody-in-particular is back. Ahead of schedule."

Halden came down the stairs with a paper in his hand and an expression on his face that Sebastian recognized the way one recognizes weather. He had heard. Of course he had heard; Verenne's gates had warrant-men at them since dawn.

"There are men outside my door," Halden said, without greeting, "carrying a legal document that describes you as a criminal. They have not entered, because entering requires my consent and the consent of the city magistrate, and because neutral ground is the last superstition this war has left standing. But they are *patient* men, Halloran, and patience is a weapon like any other." He held up the paper. "This is your ledger's index page. Your friend Odile Ferran's duplicate arrived by courier two nights ago. Half-burned. She was taken at Marle getting it through — you should know that, I think, before anything else happens today."

The floor tilted slightly. Sebastian gripped the edge of a reading desk. Odile. Taken. For them.

"She's alive," Halden went on, watching Sebastian's face with clinical interest. "Detained, not disappeared. There is a difference, at present. Whether the difference holds depends considerably on what happens in this building in the next—" he consulted a pocket watch, "—thirty-six hours. The historians' tribunal convenes tomorrow evening to close the final record of the war. At midnight the account is sealed. Sealed means sealed, Halloran. After midnight, even I cannot amend it, and believe me, I have amended things."

"Then read the ledger," Sebastian said. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Tonight. All of it. Every name. And then decide whether neutrality is a wall or a window — your phrase, Director, and I've had three weeks on the road to think about it, and I'll tell you what I concluded. Walls don't get to choose whom they keep out. Someone else chooses for them. Those men at your gate with their warrant — they've already chosen for you. The only question left is whether you sign it."

Halden looked at Sebastian for a long moment. Then at Wren, who had planted herself in front of the great catalogue cabinets with her arms crossed, guarding the card drawers as though the entire history of the world were filed among them — which, in a sense, it was.

"Pell," Halden said finally. "Lock the doors. Fetch the good lamp. And send word to the magistrate that the Archive will receive a formal deposition tonight, under seal, one witness, one child, and one book."

Pell hesitated. "And the men outside?"

Halden smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile, and Sebastian thought: *there he is. There's the decision turning into bravery, right on schedule.*

"Tell them," Halden said, "that the Archive is closed to military personnel. It's a condition of the age."

But even as Pell hurried to the doors, even as the good lamp was fetched and the lock turned with a sound like a promise, Sebastian stood at the tall windows and watched the six patient men across the street, and saw that two of them had begun, unhurriedly, to unpack writing cases — as though preparing to wait, or to report, or to draft something new.

Somewhere in the building a clock began striking noon. Twenty-four hours until midnight tomorrow. One book. One child whose face was about to stop being forgotten, whichever way the scale fell.

Sebastian turned from the window and went to help Pell find the good lamp wick. Whatever was coming through those doors — historians, warrants, the whole armed weight of a rewritten war — it would find them ready.

Or it would find them reading.

With Sebastian Halloran, those had always amounted to nearly the same thing.
