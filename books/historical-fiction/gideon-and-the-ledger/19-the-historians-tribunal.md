## 19. The Historians' Tribunal

The archive at Verenne smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of the river that ran beneath it — a cool stone breath rising through the floors, as though the building itself were trying to stay calm. Sebastian had been awake since before dawn, sitting in the reading room with Wren asleep against their shoulder, watching the light come up gray over the catalog drawers and thinking: *this is where history lives when no one is fighting over it.* It had seemed, for one hour, like a safe thought.

By nine o'clock the historians arrived, and the building stopped being an archive and became a battlefield with better furniture.

They came in twos and threes, in gray coats and good boots, carrying dispatch boxes stamped with the victors' seal — a laurel around a sword, which Sebastian had always thought was a strange order to put things in. There were eleven of them. Sebastian counted twice. Counting was a habit; the ledger had taught them that everything true began with counting.

Wren woke, rubbed their eyes, and whispered, "Are those the ones who write it down?"

"Yes."

"Are they the good ones or the other ones?"

Sebastian considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "We're about to find out," they said, "and I suspect it will be some of each, which is how most things are."

* * *

The tribunal convened in the map room on the second floor, a long chamber with a table built for generals and now occupied by men — nine men, two women — arranging themselves according to seniority with the unconscious precision of birds on a wire. At the head sat Chancellor Edouard Marchetti, a broad, pink-faced man with the voice of someone accustomed to being heard across courtyards. Beside him, taking notes in a hand so small it looked like embroidery, sat Madam Ilse Brandt of the Verenne school, the only historian present whose name Sebastian recognized from actual scholarship rather than official commemoration.

Sebastian watched from the doorway, Wren's hand in theirs, until a clerk informed them — politely, in the tone reserved for weather and other unavoidable nuisances — that the proceedings were closed.

"Closed," Sebastian repeated.

"The record is being finalized," the clerk said. "Submissions closed at the last session. Yesterday."

"Then it's fortunate," said a dry voice behind them, "that I never told you when submissions actually close."

Director Anselm Voss — no relation to anyone, he liked to say, and certainly not to Sebastian, whom he had met three days ago under circumstances he described as "an intrusion" — came down the corridor with his keys jangling at his belt like a small hostile orchestra. He was seventy if he was a day, thin as a bookmark, with eyebrows that seemed to be conducting arguments of their own.

"Director," Sebastian began.

"Don't." He held up one finger. "I have spent forty years keeping this archive neutral. Neutral means I do not take sides. It does *not* mean I take naps. Come to my office. Both of you. And stop standing in doorways looking like a painting called *Persecution*."

* * *

His office was narrow and crammed to the ceiling with boxes, and there was exactly one chair, which he did not offer to either of them. He lowered himself onto a stool instead, wincing, and fixed Sebastian with a look of profound administrative grievance.

"Let us review your position," he said. "You arrive in my city with a child wanted for kidnapping — by warrant, signed, sealed, very tidy work — and a ledger you claim contains the names of four hundred and six soldiers omitted from the official record, including the entire second company lost at Kellin Bridge, which the final draft commemorates as, let me see—" he pulled a paper toward himself and read with relish, "*'a heroic stand, all souls accounted for.'* All souls accounted for. Except the ones who weren't."

"That's the sum of it," Sebastian said.

"And you want me to help you force it into the record before midnight tonight, when the historians sign the final volume, after which any correction requires a new war, a new government, or both."

"Ideally neither."

"Mm." Voss took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, which Sebastian understood was not about the spectacles at all. "Do you know what happens to me if I help you?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's the interesting part. Neutrality has always been a kind of cowardice with better posture, and I've worn it comfortably for forty years." He put the spectacles back on. "But here is my difficulty, Halloran, and I want you to hear it plainly. The tribunal is not corrupt. That would almost be easier. Most of them are honest men and women doing honest work with dishonest materials. They have the dispatches. They have the commendations. They have nothing else, because everyone who could contradict the dispatches is dead, or silent, or — in your case — hiding in my archive eating bread I paid for."

"We can reimburse—"

"*Sit down.*" There was only the stool, so Sebastian stayed standing. "Your problem is procedure. You cannot walk into that room and wave a book. They will call it unverified. A private document, anonymous entries, no provenance. They'd be right to. It would die in committee and be buried properly by spring." He tapped the table. "What you need is standing. A right to submit evidence. And that—" his eyebrows performed something complicated, "—requires a sponsor inside the tribunal."

"Madam Brandt," Sebastian said.

Voss looked at them for a long moment. "You've done your reading."

"Wren did, actually." Wren, seated on a box of parish records, looked up with the modest pride of a seven-year-old who had recently learned that letters could be interrogated. "Her name was in three books. She writes the smallest handwriting. Small handwriting means she says more."

"She says more," Voss agreed gravely, "and is listened to least, which is the usual arrangement. But yes. Brandt. She fought the Kellin Bridge language in the third session and was outvoted nine to two. She has been writing her dissent in margins ever since." He rose, joints crackling like a fire being lit. "I will speak to her. Not because I believe you — belief is for chaplains — but because I checked your ledger against our own casualty indices last night, and four hundred and six names is not a number one invents. Numbers that precise are *counted*. Someone counted."

"I counted," Sebastian said quietly.

"Then we understand each other." He paused at the door. "One more thing. Corvane's men filed their warrant with the city magistrates this morning. By noon, every gate clerk in Verenne will have your description. Whatever happens in that room tonight — and I make no promises about what happens in that room tonight — you should understand that the law outside these walls has already chosen its side. Archives are the last places in the world where a fact can still outrank a general. Let us see if it remains true past midnight."

* * *

Madam Ilse Brandt received them at four o'clock in a corner of the reading room she had evidently annexed by sheer accumulation — books stacked in ramparts around a single lamp, a teapot balanced on a folio of treaties. She was smaller than Sebastian had imagined, with ink-stained fingers and the alert, unimpressed eyes of someone who had spent thirty years finding errors in things other people loved.

"You're the nurse," she said. Not a question.

"I'm a nurse."

"With a ledger." She poured tea without asking whether they wanted any, which Sebastian respected enormously. "Voss showed me six pages. I read them twice. Do you know what struck me first? Not the names. The *handwriting*. It changes over three years — steadier, faster, more abbreviations — but the format never varies. Name, unit, date, manner of death, officer reporting the omission. Every entry. You kept discipline with yourself even when no one was watching. That's not how forgers work. Forgers get bored." She set down the cup. "It's how archivists work. Are you certain you aren't one?"

"I'm certain I wasn't allowed to be one."

A flicker at the corner of her mouth — the ghost of a smile, quickly disciplined. "Here is our situation. The tribunal reconvenes at eight this evening to ratify the final text. The signing is at midnight. Between eight and midnight, the floor is technically open to 'supplementary evidence of verified provenance,' a clause written in 1809 and used successfully perhaps four times since. To invoke it, I must move that the record remain open, and I need a second, and I need the evidence itself to survive five minutes of scrutiny from eleven tired people who want to go home." She looked at Sebastian over the rim of her cup. "Can it?"

"It survived three years of a war," Sebastian said. "It can survive five minutes of a committee."

That earned a real smile, brief as a match strike. "Voss tells me there's a witness. A survivor of the bridge."

"Private Odile Ferran. She's coming. She was ahead of us on the road." Sebastian did not say the rest — *she was taken at Marle buying us our passage* — but something in their face must have shown, because Brandt set down her cup.

"And the child?"

Sebastian hesitated. This was the part they had turned over all night, Wren asleep beside them, the note's four words worn smooth as river stones in their mind. *Forget the face.*

"The child saw the bridge," Sebastian said finally. "From the far bank. With their mother. The mother didn't come back across." They heard their own voice go careful and flat, the voice they used for wounds. "There was a note left with the child. Instructions to forget the face. Someone decided the smallest witness was the most dangerous one, and they were nearly right."

Brandt was quiet a moment. Then: "Will the child testify?"

"They're seven."

"So was I when my village burned, and I have testified about it twice in my life, both times badly, both times truthfully." She folded her hands. "I am not asking you to put the child forward, Halloran. I am asking whether the child *wants* to be in the room. There is a difference between evidence and a person, and tribunals forget it constantly. If the child sits in the gallery, that alone changes the temperature. Eleven historians can argue with a book. It is much harder to argue with a child who is watching you decide."

* * *

At half past six, Sebastian found Wren on the archive steps, feeding crumbs to a pigeon with the focused diplomacy of a seasoned negotiator.

"There you are," Wren said, not looking up. "The lady with the little writing says I can sit in the big room tonight. She asked if I *wanted* to. Nobody asks that."

"What did you tell her?"

"I said yes." The pigeon, sensing a conclusion, departed. Wren brushed off their hands and looked up, and in the last of the daylight their face was very serious. "Sebastian. If I say the things I remember — the song, and the woman's hands, and the man with the loud voice on the bridge — will it bring her back?"

The question went through Sebastian like a splinter finding its depth. They sat down on the step, joints protesting, and took Wren's hand.

"No," they said, because lying to this child had become impossible somewhere on the road, somewhere between Marle and here. "Nothing brings anyone back. That isn't what remembering is for."

"Then what is it for?"

Sebastian thought of the ledger, four hundred and six names in a changing hand. Thought of Amity in a cell somewhere, and Odile on a road somewhere, and Tomas — no, they would not think of Tomas tonight. Thought of Sister Amity's oldest lesson, delivered over bandages: *we write it down because we cannot carry it all, and we cannot carry it all because we are meant to share the weight.*

"It's for the ones still here," Sebastian said. "So that when they ask what happened — and they will ask, Wren, people always ask eventually — somebody can answer honestly. Tonight, the somebodies are you and me and Odile Ferran. That's the whole army."

Wren considered this. "Eleven historians," they said, "against a nurse, a soldier, and a seven-year-old."

"When you put it that way it sounds bad."

"It sounds like Kellin Bridge," Wren said simply, and Sebastian had no answer to that at all, and didn't try to find one.

* * *

At ten minutes to eight, Director Voss appeared at the foot of the stairs holding two candles and wearing the expression of a man attending his own funeral out of politeness to the deceased.

"They're seated," he said. "Marchetti opened with a speech about closure. He used the word *healing* four times. Brandt has already passed me a note asking whether I've taken leave of my senses, which I've chosen to read as encouragement." He handed Sebastian one of the candles. "The clause is invoked. She moved to keep the record open; a young historian from the eastern delegation — drunk on principle, they usually are — seconded it. You have until midnight. After that, the wax seals, and facts go back to ranking below generals." He paused, and for just a moment the dryness fell away from his voice like a coat set down. "Halloran. Forty years I've told myself neutrality was a virtue. Tonight I find out whether it was. Try not to make a liar of me in either direction."

Sebastian took the candle. In the pocket of their coat, sewn flat against their ribs, the ledger made its small familiar weight — four hundred and six names, three years of nights, the whole quiet country of the uncounted.

Wren slipped a hand into theirs.

Together they climbed the stairs toward the map room, where the lamps were lit and the historians waited, and where — God willing, or failing that, the record open until midnight — the forgotten were about to be remembered out loud.
