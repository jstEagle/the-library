## 22. The Bound Volume

The book arrived on a Tuesday, in a crate, with a customs stamp and a smell like a library that had survived something.

Sebastian had been teaching arithmetic to a room of eleven children—Verenne's archive school took orphans, refugees' leftovers, one archivist's nephew who could not be made to care about numbers—and the knock at the door interrupted a lesson in long division that was going badly for everyone involved. Odile the younger was out of her seat before Sebastian could say anything, which was usual; she had a talent for doors.

"It's heavy," she reported from the hallway, straining against it with the theatrical effort of a child who has recently learned that effort can be admired. "It's *very* heavy."

"Then we'll open it together," Sebastian said, and dismissed the class early, to a cheer they did not bother disguising as gratitude.

Together—which is to say, Sebastian lifting while Odile supervised—they got the crate onto the schoolroom table and pried up the lid. Inside, packed in wood shavings like something precious being smuggled, lay four copies of *The Official History of the Border War, Volume III: The Western Theatre, with Appendices*. And sewn into the back of each one, bound in grey cloth exactly like the rest of the volume so that you would never know it was different unless you knew—the ledger. Every name. Every omitted man, every misfiled death, every soldier the dispatches had decided not to be.

Sebastian put a hand flat on the cover and stood very still for a moment.

"You're doing your face," Odile said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're somewhere else." She peered into the crate with frank curiosity. "Which one is it?"

Sebastian lifted the nearest copy down and opened it to the appendix. The title page read: *A Ledger of the Omitted, Restored by Testimony Before the Historians' Tribunal at Verenne.* Below that, in small type, the tribunal's seal, and below *that*, in smaller type still, the sentence Sebastian had read perhaps two hundred times since the proofs arrived and had not yet gotten used to: *Entered into the permanent record over the objection of no party.*

Over the objection of no party. A year ago there had been an entire faction whose whole business was objection.

"That one," Sebastian said, tapping the page. "This is the book I told you about. This is my handwriting, before they printed it."

Odile leaned in. She was eight now—or nearly; her birthday was a matter of scholarly dispute, and she had elected to celebrate it monthly—and she read slowly and stubbornly, letter by letter, the way Sebastian had taught her at a farmhouse table while the war finished collapsing behind them. Her finger moved under the first line.

"*Private... Ansel... Coote,*" she said, and looked up, triumphant. "I know that name."

"You do."

"He's the one who liked the wrong hymns. You told me. He sang them anyway."

"He did," Sebastian said. "Loudly. Out of time. Private Ansel Coote, Second Rifles, died at Kellin Bridge, eleventh of March, and no dispatch ever said so until this one." Sebastian turned pages. Names ran down them like water finding its level. "There are nine hundred and forty-one of them in here. Every one a person somebody's mother asked after and was told nothing."

Odile considered this with the seriousness she brought to anything involving other people's mothers.

"And now the mothers can look?"

"In the archive reading room, any day it's open. Anybody can. That was the whole fight, more or less."

"More or less," Odile repeated skeptically, because she had heard enough of Sebastian's stories by now to know that *more or less* meant *there was a checkpoint at Marle and Odile Ferran went to prison for six weeks for you*, though she had been carefully taught not to say that part aloud at dinner parties. She turned another page instead and stopped. "This is the funny one."

It was. *Sergeant-Major Bertrand Hollis, Fourth Sappers, died of a bee.* Officially, Sergeant-Major Hollis had fallen heroically in an artillery barrage; unofficially, per three witnesses recorded in the ledger in Sebastian's careful hand, he had stepped on a hive and expired with more dignity than the situation deserved, and the barrage had been invented afterward to spare him the embarrassment. It had taken the tribunal eleven minutes to rule that the truth was funnier and therefore admissible.

"Do you think he minded?" Odile asked. "Being a bee instead of a cannon?"

"I think," Sebastian said, "that somewhere he is delighted. There are worse things than being remembered accurately. Most people go their whole lives without anyone writing down what actually happened to them."

* * *

In the afternoon the archive's director came by, as she did most days now under the pretense of inspecting the school and in fact to sit with her tea and complain about her knees. Madame Iris Quennel was seventy, built like a filing cabinet, and had spent the war perfecting the art of being neutral, which she described as "the discipline of surviving everyone." She had hesitated at her own door the night Sebastian and Wren had come begging sanctuary; she had then forged a catalog entry, hidden a child in the map room, and lied to a tribunal officer with a fluency that had frankly alarmed Sebastian. Neutrality, it turned out, was a skill set, not a conviction.

"You got your crates," she said, settling into the good chair uninvited.

"We got our crates."

"Well." She looked at the bound volume on the table without touching it, the way she touched nothing she respected. "I'll tell you something I haven't said plainly, Halloran, because plainness is a habit of the young. When you came to my door with that ledger sewn into your coat and a child pretending to be asleep on your shoulder—badly, incidentally, the child is a terrible pretender—I nearly turned you away. Not from fear. From caution. They are different animals, and only one of them has teeth."

"I know," Sebastian said. "You've mentioned."

"I have. I mention things repeatedly; it's how I stayed neutral." She drank her tea. "But I want it in the record between us, since you're a great one for records: keeping that book off my shelves would have been easier, safer, and entirely defensible by every rule I have lived by. And it would have made my life's work a lie. So. There. Now it's in the record."

Sebastian found, somewhat to their own surprise, that they could not answer for a moment. A year ago this would not have happened—could not have. A year ago Sebastian Halloran had been a person to whom things did not happen, a nurse at the edge of other people's history, invisible by design, keeping names in a notebook the way other people kept prayers. The plan had always been simple: record everything, belong to no one, survive. It had been a good plan. It had lasted precisely as long as a blanket-wrapped child on a back step.

"There's a boy in my third-form class," Madame Quennel went on briskly, rescuing them both, "whose father was at Kellin Bridge. The family came north two years ago thinking themselves alone in it. He found his grandfather's brother in your appendix last month. Sat in the reading room for four hours. When he left he thanked me, and I didn't know what for, and it took me a day to work out that it wasn't me he was thanking." She stood, joints announcing themselves. "That's all. Tell the girl the map room misses her and that I've hidden nothing new in it, which she will correctly take as a challenge. Good day."

* * *

The letters lesson resumed at four, as it did every weekday, and Odile the younger sat in her usual seat by the window with the bound volume propped before her on a cushion, because she was the only student permitted to touch it and she treated the permission with the gravity of a knighthood.

Today's task was copying. She had progressed from letters to words to sentences, and Sebastian had given her the opening lines of the ledger's preface—the testimony, as accepted by the tribunal, in Sebastian's own printed words—to copy out fair.

*"I, Sebastian Halloran, nurse, second-class, did keep a private record..."*

Sebastian watched her work. Her tongue appeared at the corner of her mouth when she reached a difficult word, an inheritance from no one that anyone could name, entirely self-made. Her hair was growing out crooked from the year's first proper haircut. She wore one of Sister Amity's old scarves, sent north with the last parcel from the hospital, wrapped twice around her neck because Amity had decreed in her accompanying letter that northern springs were a fraud and the girl was thin-blooded.

Amity was coming to visit at midsummer. She had been released within a week of the tribunal—Corvane's confession had unraveled half the charges in the province like a pulled thread—and had gone back to nursing, because, as she wrote, someone must mind the living while the historians mind the dead, and she had never trusted historians to lift properly. Tomas Reyes wrote too, awkwardly, from the border provinces where he had gone to be near his brother; his letters always began with an apology that neither Sebastian nor Amity had ever asked for and ended with news of his garden. Sebastian answered both, every month, on time. It was strange, learning to be someone who answered letters. It had turned out to suit them.

Odile looked up. "What does *did keep* mean? Why not *kept*?"

"Because it was true then and it's true now, and the tribunal wanted it said in a way that couldn't stop being true later. *Did keep* means it happened forever."

She frowned at this the way she frowned at long division, then copied it out anyway, trusting it to make sense eventually, which was more faith than Sebastian had ever managed at any age.

They worked through the afternoon light. Outside the window Verenne went about its neutral, cautious, ordinary business—carts, pigeons, a bell tower repairing its clock, the market shouting down prices. Somewhere south of here General Aldous Vane lived in comfortable disgrace, giving interviews to nobody, his faction dissolved, his name in the history books attached forever to Kellin Bridge in the exact posture in which he had earned it. Sebastian did not think about him often. That was its own kind of verdict: not vengeance, just proportion. A man remembered precisely, no more than that.

"Finished," Odile announced, and pushed the page across.

It was laborious and beautiful, the letters leaning various directions like a crowd listening hard, but every word correct, including *forever*. At the bottom she had added, unprompted, in her smallest and proudest hand:

*Copied by Odile Halloran.*

They had argued about the surname for a week the previous autumn. Sebastian had proposed nothing—had assumed, out of old habit, that names were things one declined rather than accepted—and it was Odile, aged seven and three-quarters, who had settled the matter by simply informing the school registrar that her name was Odile Halloran, thank you, next question. Sebastian had been standing right there and had not corrected it. Had not wanted to. Had walked home afterward feeling like a house whose owner had finally moved in.

"It's not quite right," Odile said now, studying her own signature with a critic's eye. "The H leans too much. But it's mine."

"It's yours," Sebastian agreed. "Though for the record, you chose it yourself. I want that entered somewhere official."

"You can enter it in my copybook. That's official enough."

Sebastian laughed—not the careful indoor laugh of the hospital years, but the loud kind, the kind that made the third-formers look up—and took the pen to add a witness line beneath the signature, date and all, because some habits were worth keeping. The record would note that on the seventeenth of April, in the schoolroom above the archive at Verenne, Odile Halloran learned that *did keep* means forever, and signed her own name to the proof.

Below the window, in the reading room, someone had already opened Volume III to the appendix. Through the floorboards Sebastian could hear the small sounds of a stranger turning pages—stopping, going back, stopping again—the sound Sebastian had once made alone at a hospital desk at midnight, and would never again need to make alone.

Nine hundred and forty-one names, restored. One face, entered over the objection of no party. One notebook, once a shield, now a home with the door left open.

"Read me the next line," Odile said, finger waiting on the page. "I want to get the hard ones while it's still light."
