## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The frost came up the river that morning like a rumor, and by the time Zephyrine Iversen reached the Sable Bridge, the whole city of Vessany had gone quiet in the way that meant soldiers.

He had learned to read quiet the way other men read print. There was the quiet of a market before dawn, generous and half-asleep. There was the quiet of a house where someone had died. And there was this: the quiet of a checkpoint at first light, when the brazier smoke hung flat and blue over the barricade and six men with carbines watched the ice-fog for movement, because orders had come down in the night that movement itself was now a question to be answered.

Zephyrine answered it by not asking it. He walked at the pace of a man going nowhere important — not hurrying, which invited pursuit, not dawdling, which invited suspicion — and he kept his breath shallow so the steam of it would not rise above his collar and mark him as nervous. Nervous men got searched. Searched men got shot, eventually, somewhere out of sight, and their names went into a ledger no one read twice.

He was twenty-six years old and he had been a courier of the Third Regiment for three years, and in those three years he had assembled, stitch by stitch, the rules that kept couriers alive. He thought of them as his liturgy. He did not pray, but he recited them all the same, silently, on every crossing, the way a drowning man counts.

*First rule: be forgettable.* Wear the coat everyone wears. Walk like everyone walks. Have a face that is a door left slightly ajar — nothing behind it worth opening. He had practiced his face in a cracked mirror until even he found it dull.

*Second rule: carry nothing you cannot lose.* The dispatch case held copies, innocuous ones, trade manifests and grain requisitions, papers designed to be read. The true dispatches were sewn into the lining of his coat, flat against his left ribs, where a searching hand would have to want very specifically to find them. He had stitched them himself, by lamplight, with a sailmaker's needle and thread the color of the lining, and he had sewn them the way his mother had once sewn coins into her hem before crossing out of Kesselra province — badly, then better, then invisibly.

*Third rule: never lie well.* A perfect lie is a performance, and performances draw eyes. Lie small, lie bored, lie like a man who has told this truth a hundred times and resents being asked again. *Courier, Third Regiment, routine run to the Chancery annex, yes sir, cold enough for you.* Boredom was armor. He wore it under the coat, against the ribs, next to the sealed things.

*Fourth rule:* — and this one he had learned from Colonel Aurel Vance himself, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, two winters ago — *never carry what you are not willing to die inside of.*

Vance had said it without drama, turning a compass over in his long fingers, not looking at him. That was Vance's way: the most important things delivered sideways, like a card slipped under a door. Zephyrine had understood, even then, that the sentence was not about dispatches. But he had been twenty-three and newly sworn, and he had taken it literally, because taking it literally was safer than taking it any other way.

"Name and business." The sergeant at the barricade had a voice like gravel poured slowly. His breath plumed white. Behind him, the brazier cracked, and beyond the brazier the river lay frozen to its far bank, gray-white, holding its breath under the ice the way the whole republic seemed to be holding its breath.

"Iversen. Courier, Third Regiment." He produced the pass from his inner pocket with two fingers, unhurried, already half-bored. "Run to the Chancery annex. Manifests."

The sergeant looked at the pass. Then, as they always did, at him — that flicking assessment, boots to shoulders, the arithmetic of threat performed in a second and a half. Zephyrine let his shoulders slump a fraction. Let his eyes go to the middle distance, to nothing. *A door left ajar.*

"Manifests," the sergeant repeated, without interest. "Cold enough for you."

"Cold enough," Zephyrine agreed, and hated how much of his life fit into those two words.

They waved him through. He crossed the bridge at the pace of a man going nowhere important, and only when he had put the fog and the brazier between himself and the checkpoint did he allow his heart its single loud beat, one, like a knock on a door, and then silence it again.

The city on the far side was worse. Posters had gone up overnight on the Chancery wall — he saw them pasted over last week's posters, which were themselves pasted over the week's before — new decrees in heavy black type: *CURFEW AT DUSK. ASSEMBLAGE PROHIBITED. THE REGIMENT PROTECTS.* The ink was still sharp-edged; the paste still glistened. Someone had written beneath the last line, in charcoal, in a hand that shook either with rage or cold: *protects whom?* By noon it would be scrubbed away. By dusk the man who wrote it might be scrubbed away too. Zephyrine looked at it for exactly as long as a bored courier would look at anything, and memorized it anyway, because that was the fifth rule, the unwritten one, the one that lived below the others like bedrock:

*Fifth rule: see everything. Say nothing. Keep the ledger in your head.*

His ledger was thick these days. Three years thick. It had entries in it he did not fully understand yet — requisitions that didn't add up, trains that carried grain outward and came back empty, villages in the eastern provinces that stopped appearing on the manifests altogether, as if paper could unmake them. He kept the entries anyway. A courier's job, Vance had said once, is to be the memory the army wishes it didn't have. Zephyrine had laughed at that, then. He wasn't sure anymore what the joke had been.

* * *

His lodging was a third-floor room above a shuttered chandlery on Rue des Ombres, reached by a stair that smelled of tallow and old rope. He paid his rent in cash, on time, to a landlady who asked no questions because he paid her, in courtesy, the small coin of being unremarkable. He had chosen the room for its window: it overlooked the street, and the stair, and the door, and a courier who could not see his own door was a courier already halfway to dead.

He turned onto Rue des Ombres at half past four, with the light failing and the frost coming down again, fine as sifted flour, and he saw the shape on his doorstep from thirty paces and made himself keep walking at the pace of a man going nowhere important.

It was a bundle. No — a child. Small, five years old perhaps, maybe six, curled on the stone step with his knees drawn up and his head pillowed on his own arm, asleep or pretending to sleep, which in this city amounted to the same survival skill. He was dressed in a coat that had been cut down from an adult's, sleeves rolled and rolled again, and there was ash worked into the fabric of him, into the creases of his knuckles, the whorls of his ears, the seam of his collar — ash that had been washed at but not defeated, ash that had become part of the boy the way grain becomes part of bread.

Zephyrine stood over him and felt his carefully ordered world tilt, gently, like a table with one short leg.

There was a note pinned to the coat. Heavy paper, good paper, the kind used for official correspondence. He took it with two fingers, angling his body so the street saw only a man reading a letter, and read:

*Forget the face.*

Nothing else. No name, no explanation. Just those three words in a firm, slanted hand he almost — almost — recognized, though recognition slid away when he reached for it, like fish through cold water.

He looked down the street. Empty. Frost falling. A cart rattling somewhere streets away, and under it, fainter, the sound the city had been making all week at dusk, the low massed murmur of crowds that curfews do not disperse but only press underground, where murmurs become plans.

The boy opened his eyes.

They were dark and enormous and utterly awake — he had not been asleep at all, Zephyrine realized; he had been doing exactly what Zephyrine did at checkpoints, performing stillness, waiting to see who would stop and who would walk on. For a long moment the child simply looked at him, cataloguing him the way Zephyrine catalogued sergeants, and Zephyrine understood that he was being assessed by a five-year-old and possibly losing.

Then the boy's gaze dropped to the note in Zephyrine's hand, and something in his small face closed like a shopfront at curfew.

"You're supposed to forget me," the boy said. His voice was hoarse, rusted, the voice of someone who had not spoken in days. "That's what the paper says."

"And yet here I am," Zephyrine heard himself say, "reading it aloud."

The boy considered this with grave suspicion. "You're not supposed to talk to me either."

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "I'm not." He crouched, slowly, keeping his hands visible, the way one does with frightened animals and frightened children and, if he was honest, frightened men. Up close the ash was worse; up close there was a burn scar along the boy's jaw, healed crooked, weeks old. "Are you hurt?"

The boy didn't answer. Instead he did something that stopped Zephyrine's breath in his chest: he reached into his coat and drew out a scrap of paper, folded small, soft at the creases from much handling, and held it out — not offering it, exactly. Showing it. The way a card is shown, half-hidden, at a gambling table.

Zephyrine took it. Unfolded it. And the cold that went through him then had nothing to do with the weather, because there on the scrap, drawn in a child's careful, laborious strokes — copied, clearly, from something the child should never have seen — was a cipher. Columns of figures, grouped in fives, bracketed at the head by a symbol he knew intimately, had known for three years, had sewn into his own coat lining not twelve hours ago at lamplight with a sailmaker's needle.

It matched. Not approximately. Exactly. Group for group, symbol for symbol, down to the little hook the regimental clerks put on the final figure — a habit, a flaw, a fingerprint.

Someone had given this burned child a key to the regiment's most sealed language. Or the child had stolen it. Or the child had *been* the thing the cipher described, and had escaped carrying the description of himself.

The frost fell. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, muffled, apologetic. The boy watched him with those enormous dark eyes, waiting to see what kind of man had found him, and Zephyrine stood on his own doorstep holding two halves of a truth that had not been introduced to each other until tonight, and felt the first rule of his liturgy — *be forgettable* — crumble quietly, like ice going out from under a boot.

"Come inside," he said finally, because the light was failing, and the curfew was coming, and there was no version of this in which he walked up the stairs alone. "Before someone sees us both."

The boy gathered himself up without a word, without thanks, with the economy of someone who has learned that shelter is temporary and gratitude is a debt that can be collected. At the threshold he paused, looking back down the empty street — toward the east, Zephyrine noted, toward the river, toward whatever was left of wherever he had come from — and then he stepped inside, out of the falling frost, into the narrow hall that smelled of tallow and old rope.

Zephyrine locked the door behind them, and stood a moment with his hand on the bolt, listening to the silence of the building, the silence of the street, the deepening silence of a republic holding its breath.

In his coat lining, flat against his ribs, the sealed dispatches waited. On the table upstairs, a child's scrap of paper waited. Between them, unstitched, unspoken, something was already beginning to open.

He climbed the stairs.
