## 2. Instructions to Forget

The child slept through the whole of the second day, and Zephyrine learned nothing about him except the sound of his breathing.

He had tried, in the methodical way he tried everything. He had gone to the widow Kessl, who kept the register of lodgers on the street and knew every face between the canal and the powder works; she had looked at Milo—though the boy had not yet given up his name—and shaken her head with the flat certainty of a woman who had buried two sons and no longer pretended the city held surprises. He had walked to the foundling house on Vane Street and stood in the queue of the desperate for an hour before the door opened and a clerk told him, without looking up, that the house had been requisitioned for billeting in the autumn and its records burned for warmth in November. He had even, in a moment of weakness he was not proud of, asked the lamplighter who worked the corner by the bridge whether anyone had been seen leaving a child at his door. The lamplighter had considered the question with professional gravity and reported that the night in question had been foggy, and that fog was the city's oldest accomplice.

So the boy remained a fact without a history, which in Zephyrine's experience was the most dangerous kind of fact. Documents could be forged, but they could also be read. A child with no origin was a document someone had gone to great trouble to erase.

The note itself he examined by lamplight, holding the paper at angles the way he had been taught to hold anything that might be more than it appeared. *Forget the face.* Four words in a hand that was careful without being elegant, the letters upright, the ink a common iron-gall that had dried without bloom. Nothing there. But paper, paper was honest in ways that people were not. He tilted it against the flame and saw it: a ghost of a watermark, pressed into the fiber, visible only when the light came through rather than onto it.

A tower. A star above it. The crest of the Ninth Regiment of the Line.

Zephyrine set the note down and did not touch it again for a long while. His hands were steady—his hands were always steady, that had been the first thing anyone ever praised in him—but something behind his sternum had begun, very quietly, to swing like a lamp in wind.

The Ninth. Vance's regiment. Vance's crest, on paper left with a child who had been carried out of something, though Zephyrine did not yet know what, and delivered to the one door in the city where a regimental courier slept.

*Forget the face.* As if the face were a thing that could be ordered away, like a dispatch, like a memory. As if whoever wrote it had known exactly what kind of man they were writing to—a man who had spent three years learning that some instructions were survival and some were shackles, and that the difference was only ever clear afterward.

Milo stirred on the cot—the boy had given his name on the first evening, one syllable offered like a coin to a toll-keeper, and nothing since—and Zephyrine banked the fire and lay down in his chair and did not sleep. He listened to the child breathe and thought about watermarks and about the colonel, and the two thoughts kept trying to become one thought, and he kept pulling them apart, because he was not ready for them to be one thought.

The summons came at dawn, as the good ones always did.

* * *

The barracks of the Ninth occupied a block of the old artillery school, and the colonel's quarters sat at its top like a thought at the top of a mind. Zephyrine had climbed those stairs perhaps two hundred times in three years, and he knew the exact number of steps, and he knew that on the ninety-first step—there were a hundred and six—you could see, through the tall window on the landing, the whole gray sweep of the river and the bridges upon it, and that on winter mornings the ice on the water held the light the way a held breath holds a secret.

He stopped on the ninety-first step. He always stopped on the ninety-first step. It was the one indulgence he permitted himself in that building, three seconds of river, and then he climbed the rest.

Vance was at his desk with the window open despite the cold, which he did always, claiming that sealed rooms made men think sealed thoughts. He was not in uniform—not quite. He wore the plain gray coat he reviewed dispatches in, no braid, no insignia, and without them he might have been any tired man of forty-odd years at a desk, except for the hands. Zephyrine had never been able to make those hands ordinary. They were long and scarred across the knuckles and they moved when he talked the way a conductor's hands move, as if even in stillness they were keeping time with something.

"Courier Iversen," Vance said, without looking up. "You look as though you haven't slept."

"I slept, Colonel."

"You're lying."

"Yes, Colonel."

That, at least, made the gray eyes come up, and there it was—the thing that was never named, arriving on schedule, punctual as the dawn summons itself. Vance looked at him the way he looked at difficult terrain: wholly, assessing, and with something underneath the assessment that had been there for three years and had been fed, carefully, on nothing. On ninety-first steps. On wine poured and not drunk. On the particular silence two men could share that was louder than speech.

"Sit," Vance said.

Zephyrine sat. The room smelled of woodsmoke and ink and, faintly, of the cedar oil the colonel rubbed into his boots, and Zephyrine hated how familiar it all was, how the familiarity itself was a kind of evidence against him.

Vance took a folded packet from the drawer—not the dispatch case, a separate packet, heavier—and laid it on the desk between them but did not release it. His hand stayed on it.

"In four days," he said, "there is a thing that must reach the Veyran embassy. Not the ministry. Not the general staff. The embassy, and a specific person inside it, and no one else. Three checkpoints between here and there. The bridge, the custom house, the gate. You know them."

"I know them."

"You will not know them by Thursday. Martial law comes down tomorrow at noon. After that, the checkpoints stop being ceremonies." Vance's thumb moved once across the packet's wax seal, a small unconscious gesture, the way a man touches a wound. "The city is going to be a closed fist, Zephyrine. You are going to be the thing moving inside it."

"Alone?"

The question was out before he could weigh it, and Vance heard what was underneath it, because he always heard what was underneath it. Something crossed his face—there and gone, like a bird past a window.

"Alone," he said. "That's the point of you."

*The point of you.* Three years ago, on the training ground at Marrow Fen, a younger Vance had said almost the same words to a raw recruit who couldn't lie yet: *You're no one's son, no one's lover, no one's fool. That's the point of you.* And Zephyrine had believed it had been a compliment, and had spent three years discovering it was a sentence.

"The contents?" Zephyrine asked.

"Yours to carry, not to read."

"Colonel." He kept his voice level. "The last courier who carried unread to the embassy—"

"—was caught at the custom house, and what was in his case killed him faster than a firing squad would have. Yes. I wrote the report." Vance finally let go of the packet and stood, and crossed to the window, and stood looking down at the frozen parade ground with his back to Zephyrine, which was either trust or the opposite of trust, and Zephyrine could not tell which, and the not-telling was beginning to feel like the whole shape of his life. "Which is why you will not carry it in your case. You will sew it into your coat, left breast, over the heart, where a search for papers never goes because no one is sentimental enough to look there. Sergeant Brandt will do the sewing. He has done it before."

"Brandt is a talker."

"Brandt is a widower with three daughters and a debt, and he is loyal to me, which in the coming week is the only loyalty in this building worth the name." Vance turned around then, and the light from the window was behind him, and Zephyrine could read nothing in his face, which was itself a kind of message. "There is a second thing."

"Colonel?"

"The boy."

The room did not change. The fire did not crackle differently. But Zephyrine felt the floor tilt, very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

"What boy?" he said.

"Zephyrine." Vance said his name the way he said it only in this room, the whole of it, all three syllables, unhurried. "I have known you for three years. I have watched you lie to provosts, to ministers, to a duchess, once, magnificently. You are the best liar I ever made. And you just asked me *what boy* in the voice of a man asking what rain is."

Zephyrine said nothing. The silence stretched. He had been taught this silence too—taught it by the man now standing in front of him—taught that the courier who fills a silence is the courier who dies in it.

Vance crossed back to the desk and stood over him, close, closer than the conversation required, and for a moment neither of them was a colonel or a courier. The woodsmoke smell. The scarred hands resting on the desk's edge on either side of the packet. The gray eyes that had never once, in three years, said the thing, and had never once stopped saying it.

"Whatever he is," Vance said quietly, "he is not your concern. Whatever was left with him is not your concern. You have one task, and four days, and a city that is about to close around you like a hand. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you, Colonel."

"Do you." It was not quite a question. Vance straightened, and the moment closed like water over a stone. He slid the packet across the desk. "Report to Brandt at the sixth hour. Rest. Eat. Thursday, at moonset, you move."

Zephyrine took the packet and rose and saluted, because the form was the armor and the armor was all that was holding.

At the door, Vance spoke again, and his voice had something in it Zephyrine had never heard there before, and would spend the rest of his life failing to name.

"Courier. Whatever you find in the next four days—" a pause, precise as a blade laid flat, "—remember that orders are not the same thing as the man who carries them. Or the man who signs them."

Zephyrine went down the hundred and six steps without stopping at the ninety-first. Outside, the snow had begun again, fine and dry, the kind that erased footprints in an hour. He walked home through it with the packet against his ribs and the watermark burning in his memory like a brand, and by the time he reached his door he had stopped pretending the two thoughts were separate.

The boy was asleep on the cot. The note lay on the table where he had left it. Zephyrine held it up to the lamp one more time, and the tower and star floated up out of the paper, and he thought: *Vance sent the summons before I ever brought the child inside. Vance knew.*

And beneath that thought, arriving without permission, quiet as the snow: *Or Vance is the one who left him at my door—and is now telling me, in the only language we have, to forget the face.*

He did not sleep that night either. He sat by the banked fire with the boy's cipher—the strange stitched symbols Milo clutched even in sleep—and his own dispatch case open beside him, and he told himself he was only organizing his papers.

He was lying. He was very good at it. He had been taught by the best.
