## 4. Loyalty Is a Kind of Blindness

The wine was a Vessian red from the Karst vineyards, which meant it came from a country that no longer existed except in bottles. Colonel Aurel Vance poured two glasses in his quarters above the parade ground and listened to the frost ticking against the shutters like a telegraph operator sending the same word over and over: *soon, soon, soon.*

He had chosen the vintage deliberately. Zephyrine would recognize it. That was the point of everything Vance did now — every gesture had become a message he could not send honestly, so he sent them sideways, wrapped in ordinary things. A bottle. An invitation at nine instead of seven, late enough that no clerk would note it. A fire built high so the room's shadows would be kind.

He heard the courier's step on the stair before the knock. Three years of training a man teaches you his feet. Zephyrine climbed stairs the way he crossed checkpoints: heel first, weight committed slowly, as if the ground itself might demand papers.

"Colonel."

"Sit down, Iversen. You look half-frozen."

"I was half-frozen, sir."

"Then drink."

Zephyrine sat across the small table and took the glass, and their fingers did not touch, which was its own kind of language. Vance watched the boy — man, he corrected himself, though the correction never held for long — watch him back. Amber eyes, patient as a lamp flame. The eyes of someone who had learned that looking was safer than speaking and more dangerous than either.

So. He knew something. Vance had been almost certain since the summons this morning, when Zephyrine had arrived at the office two minutes early and stood too still while receiving orders he should have questioned. Stillness was what Zephyrine did with fear. Most men fidgeted; couriers trained by Aurel Vance went quiet as sealed rooms.

And tonight he would find out how much the boy knew, gently, over wine, the way you opened any lock worth having.

* * *

The first time Vance saw him, the boy had been seventeen and caught stealing.

Not money. Papers — or rather, a glance at papers, three seconds too long, in the requisition office where he'd been hired to sweep floors after his mother's death left him hungry in a city that had stopped pretending to feed anyone. The duty sergeant had him by the collar, ready to break the hand, and Vance had happened past on an evening he could never afterward account for.

"What did you see?" Vance had asked him, privately, later.

"Nothing," the boy said, and his pulse was jumping visibly in his throat, and he said it again, calmer: "Nothing, sir."

It was beautifully done. It was done well enough that Vance understood instantly he wasn't looking at a thief. He was looking at raw material. Some people lied out of panic; a rare few lied out of architecture, building their falsehoods with load-bearing walls.

"You saw the troop rotations," Vance said. "You saw that the Third is moving south a week before anyone will admit it. Say it back to me."

A long pause. Snow coming down outside the guardhouse window, the lamp guttering.

"The Third is moving south," Zephyrine said at last, "and I saw nothing."

Vance had laughed — actually laughed, alone, for the first time in months — and thought: *I will make something of this one.* He had bought him dinner. He had taught him, over the following years, the whole catechism of silence: how to carry a face like currency, how to let questioners find exactly the answer they expected, how to be forgettable on command. How to sew dispatches into a coat lining so they rode against the heartbeat, because a lie told close to your own heart sounds truer.

He had taught him all of it believing the Republic deserved such instruments. Believing the regiment was the spine that kept the body upright.

He poured more wine now and watched the instrument sit across the table, grown into his own hands, and wondered when precisely he had begun teaching him to lie to *himself*, because that was the lesson Vance had never intended and could not unteach: loyalty is a kind of blindness, and the blind are led.

* * *

"To the Regiment," Vance said, raising his glass.

"To the Regiment," Zephyrine answered.

They drank. Outside, somewhere east, a single gunshot cracked the night and rolled away, and neither man remarked on it. Gunshots in Vessany that winter had become weather; you noted them the way farmers noted wind.

"The route is ready?" Vance asked.

"It's ready."

"Three checkpoints. Bridge of Sighs, the Custom House, then the Gate. After tomorrow the corridors tighten further — martial law wants paperwork for breath, soon." He smiled, and made sure the smile reached his eyes, because Zephyrine read eyes the way other men read documents. "You'll carry the sealed case. Nothing else. No side papers, no private correspondence, nothing I haven't seen."

"Understood."

"You understand," Vance repeated, softly, tasting the words. "You've always understood me unusually well, Iversen."

"I try, sir."

"Do you." He turned the glass on the table, one slow rotation, watching the candlelight swim in the red. "There was a report this morning. A child found on a doorstep in the Weavers' Quarter. Nobody's child. Nobody's doorstep. The patrol log lists the address." He looked up. "Odd, the things that end up in logs."

The stillness came down over Zephyrine like snowfall — total, soft, deliberate. Vance felt it land between them and knew he had drawn blood, or very nearly.

"Lots of children in this city," Zephyrine said. "Lots of doorsteps."

"Yes." Vance leaned back. "There are. And most of them stay where they're put."

* * *

Here was the arithmetic that kept Vance awake, that had him pouring wine at nine o'clock at night for a man he was interrogating with tenderness:

Kesselra. He had signed the order. His hand, his countersignature, beneath General Malvreau's seal — under duress, yes, with a pistol-shaped argument in the room, yes, but ink does not record the temperature of the hand that makes it. Four hundred souls in a valley village, evacuated, and *evacuated* written in a grammar everyone on the staff had learned to read correctly. He had signed. And six weeks later a child had appeared on the doorstep of the one courier who would carry the proof of it, wrapped in a cipher cut from the same cloth as the dispatches in Vance's own vault.

Someone had done that. And the list of someones was short enough to fit inside this room.

Either Zephyrine had engineered it — impossible; the boy was brilliant but had no reach into Kesselra, no hands there — or someone had used Zephyrine's doorstep, which meant someone knew what Zephyrine was, which meant someone might know what Zephyrine *carried*, which meant the whole delicate machine Vance had spent months quietly gumming with sand — misfiled manifests, reassigned companies, a supply column deliberately lost for three days — was about to be crushed between Malvreau's fingers.

Or. The third possibility, the one he turned over at 3 a.m. like a coin worn smooth: someone had put the evidence into Zephyrine's hands *on purpose*. Had chosen the courier the way you choose a vessel. Had trusted him with the truth of Kesselra because he was exactly the man Vance had made him.

In which case the boy was already carrying the fire, and this dinner was not an interrogation at all. It was an act of mercy — Vance warning his own creation, without saying a word, that the wolves had his scent.

* * *

"You're quiet," Vance said. "Quieter than usual."

"I'm drinking good wine, sir. It deserves attention."

"It does." Vance refilled both glasses. His hand did not shake; he had trained it not to, decades ago, in another winter, another country that believed itself permanent. "I want to tell you something, Iversen, and I want you to attend, because I'll only say it once."

Zephyrine's eyes came up. Amber, steady, guarded as a strongbox with the key thrown away. God, he was beautiful when he was frightened. That was the obscenity at the bottom of all of this — that Vance could stand here, architect of silences, signer of orders, and still feel the old pull like gravity toward this one person, and that the pull had never once been spoken aloud in three years because speaking it would have made them both into evidence.

"When I found you," Vance said, "in the guardhouse, with snow in your hair and a sergeant's fist in your collar — do you remember what I asked?"

"You asked what I saw."

"And you said?"

"Nothing, sir."

"And that," Vance said quietly, "was the correct answer. It has always been the correct answer. Whatever you see, whatever is put into your hands, whatever stands on your doorstep in the dark — *nothing*. Do you follow me? There are men in this building who would take you apart joint by joint to learn what a courier knows, and the only armor you will ever be issued is the silence I taught you. Use it now. Not for me. For yourself."

A pause long enough to fall through.

"That sounds like a warning, Colonel."

"It is the only kind I can give."

* * *

They were lying, both of them, and lying gorgeously, and each of them knew the other knew, and neither would break first. It was, Vance thought wildly, the purest conversation they had ever had — two masters of concealment meeting on equal ground, fencing with candlelight. Every sentence double-loaded. *The route is ready. / It's ready.* Meaning: *do you know what you carry?* Meaning: *do you know what I've done?* Meaning, beneath all of it, in the register neither dared write in: *I would burn all of it for you if you asked, and I am afraid you are about to ask.*

Zephyrine set down his glass with a click like a bolt closing.

"Sir," he said, "if a man were carrying something — a letter, say, or a stone — and didn't know its weight until he'd already walked halfway, what would you tell him to do?"

Vance held his gaze and did not blink.

"I'd tell him," he said, "that a courier delivers what he's given. That's the oath. But—" and here he let one true thing slip through the wire, small and fatal as a moth through a gap in a wall, "—oaths are made to men, Iversen, not to monsters wearing men's signatures. If he ever learns what the stone truly weighs, he should ask himself who benefits from the delivery. And who bleeds."

Something moved behind Zephyrine's eyes — grief, or decision, or both wearing each other's coats.

"Yes, sir," he said. "I'll remember."

* * *

Later, at the door, with the frost thickening on the window glass and the city holding its breath below, Vance stopped him with a hand on his shoulder — the first touch in a year, allowed because tomorrow the routes closed and caution would cost nothing further.

"One more thing," Vance said, low. "Your route has been flagged. For protection." He watched the boy absorb it — hope and dread arriving together, twins. "Whatever happens at those checkpoints, walk through them like a man with nothing in his pockets. Don't run unless running is faster than thinking."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then run," Vance said, "toward the river. Never away from it."

Zephyrine went down the stairs, heel first, weight committed slowly, and Vance stood listening until even the trained ears of a soldier could no longer distinguish the footsteps from the wind. Then he poured the last of the dead country's wine and drank it alone, and took from his desk drawer a single sheet of paper bearing Malvreau's seal, and read again the order that would post armed patrols to every bridge and custom house by dawn — including, circled twice in the general's own hand, the courier routes.

By morning, Zephyrine would be walking into the first checkpoint with the proof of Kesselra sewn against his heart, and Vance had perhaps six hours to decide whether the flag on his file was a shield or a leash — and whether he would spend what remained of his honor cutting the wire, or standing on it.
