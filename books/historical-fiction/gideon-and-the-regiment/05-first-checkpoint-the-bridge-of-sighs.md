## 5. First Checkpoint: The Bridge of Sighs

The dead made the best couriers in Vessany. No one searched them twice.

Zephyrine had learned that in his second year, watching a widow's coffin pass the bridge guards at Dornmark Gate while every living soul in the queue was turned out, shaken, unbuttoned, humiliated in the snow. The soldiers lifted the lid, glanced at the wreaths, and waved the funeral through with the bored courtesy men reserve for what frightens them. Grief was a uniform they did not stop. It was the one disguise the regime had never thought to outlaw, because to forbid mourning would be to admit there was something worth mourning.

So tonight there was a coffin in the back of a hired hearse, and Zephyrine sat beside the driver with Milo asleep against his ribs, and the requisition papers—regimental channels, Vance's own stamp, though Zephyrine could not let himself think too long about whose hand had pressed the seal—said *Marek Duval, joiner, aged fifty-one, died of the lung fever*, which was true of no one and everyone, because Marek Duval had never existed at all.

The child had not asked where they were going. He had asked only once, three nights ago, waking from a dream of smoke: *"Is it far, the place where we put it down?"* And Zephyrine had said it was far, and Milo had nodded with the terrible competence of children who have already survived the worst thing, and gone back to sleep with his thumb hooked into the collar of Zephyrine's coat like an anchor.

The coat was the problem now. The dispatches were out of it.

He had spent four hours with a surgeon's needle and the lining of a coffin pillow, sewing the oilcloth packet flat beneath the stuffing, working by one candle shielded inside a washbasin, while Milo kept watch at the shuttered window as Zephyrine had taught him—*count the boots, tell me if they stop*. The boy counted boots like other children counted stars. When it was done, the packet made no ridge a hand could find unless the hand already knew. The cipher matched the orders. The orders named Kesselra. Eleven hundred souls marched out of a valley in the first snows and none of them come down again—that was what slept under the wreaths tonight, wrapped in regimental linen, riding toward the neutral embassy under a forged name and the honest grief of the whole fallen republic.

"Uncle." Milo tugged his sleeve. Not asleep after all. His voice came out small in the dark of the moving hearse. "There are lights on the water."

Zephyrine looked. The Bridge of Sighs arched ahead over the frozen canal, and it was lit like a feast day, which meant it was hunting. Lanterns strung along both parapets, braziers at either end, and the queue of vehicles backed up along the embankment for a quarter mile, breath and torch-smoke rising together into the black. Martial law had reached the river. Somewhere behind them, across the sleeping districts, the curfew bell would already have struck; they were moving on borrowed minutes.

"The name," Zephyrine said quietly.

"Milo Iversen." The boy recited it like scripture. "Your sister's son, come from Harrowmere for your mother's burying. I am sad. I look at my feet."

"You are sad. You look at your feet. If anyone speaks to you, you say nothing, because grief has closed your throat. Do you remember your cough?"

Milo produced the cough. It was very good—a dry, hollow little sound, practiced nightly until Zephyrine had almost begun to worry about it in earnest.

"And if they take me away from you," Milo said, in the flat way he had of asking dreadful things, "I run to the woman selling chestnuts and I cry, and she will hold my hand, because women hold crying children's hands even here."

"Yes."

"And you will come."

"I will come." He said it the way you say a thing you intend to make true by force of saying it. "Whatever happens on this bridge, Milo—I will come."

The boy considered this with those grave dark eyes, then nodded once, satisfied, and burrowed back against Zephyrine's side as the hearse joined the queue.

They moved forward by inches. Frost feathered the glass of the carriage lamps. Along the parapet Zephyrine could see the search teams working the line of waiting traffic: two soldiers to a vehicle, one opening doors, one watching faces. Faces were the dangerous part. Coffins they respected; coffins they opened with gloved ceremony and a muttered prayer or none. But faces they studied, because the regime had learned—too late, thank God—to read faces, and Zephyrine's face was known at eleven checkpoints in this city, drawn in charcoal on a file somewhere with the notation *courier, trusted, Vance's man*, a notation he was betting his life they had not yet amended.

A lantern swung level with their window. A soldier's palm struck the driver's panel twice—the signal—and their driver, a gray silent carter who took coin without questions, hauled the brake.

"Funeral detail," the soldier said, bending to the window gap, breath steaming. "Papers."

Zephyrine passed the folder through. Requisition, death certificate, transit permit, all bearing the crossed-sabers stamp of the Fourth Regiment. The soldier thumbed through them with the particular slowness of a man who cannot read well and is ashamed of it, holding each page closer to the lantern.

"Fourth Regiment," he said. "Whose seal is this?"

"Colonel Vance's," Zephyrine said. "The deceased was a regiment pensioner. His family asked the Fourth to see him home." Every word of it was true except the man in the box, and lies built on true bones held best; Vance had taught him that in the first month, over the training table, tapping the dispatch case. *Never invent, Zephyrine. Rearrange. The truth carries its own weight; invention has none.*

The soldier looked up. Past him, at the far end of the bridge, a second checkpoint stood with its brazier burning, and beyond that, on the embankment steps, the neutral embassy's flag hung furled and dark. Four hundred yards. It might as well have been four hundred miles.

"Open it," the soldier said.

* * *

There is a discipline to fear, and Zephyrine had been drilled in it by the best officer he ever loved. Fear wants to run; discipline makes it stand still. Fear wants the hands to shake; discipline folds them neatly in the lap. So Zephyrine climbed down from the hearse with Milo's cold fingers tucked in his, and walked beside the carters as they drew the coffin onto the trestles the soldiers set up on the bridge stones, between the parapet and the black ice of the canal, and he arranged his face into the mask he had worn at his actual mother's actual funeral six years ago—dazed, courteous, elsewhere.

The boy coughed. Small, dry, perfect. A sergeant near the brazier glanced over, and something in his expression shifted—the softening men still permitted themselves when a child looked ill in winter.

"Name of the deceased?" This from the search officer, a lieutenant with a ledger and a wet red nose.

"Marek Duval. Joiner. Pensioned out of the sappers after Corvel Bridge." Zephyrine let the pause stretch a fraction. "It took his breathing in the end. The fever. He went quietly, they tell me, which is more than the war gave him."

The lieutenant wrote it down. Around them the queue idled; somewhere a horse stamped; the canal ticked and groaned beneath the ice. Two soldiers took the coffin lid's handles. Zephyrine felt Milo's grip tighten on his hand to the edge of pain, and answered it—one squeeze, steady as a heartbeat—while his own pulse hammered so hard in his ears he feared it carried across the frosty air like a drum.

The lid came up.

Wreaths. Linen. A pillow of white stitching, and beneath it—if any hand pressed, if any eye measured—the faintest wrongness of bulk, the thickness of folded oilcloth and sealed paper where only flowers should sleep. Zephyrine watched the lieutenant lean over the box, watched the lantern-light slide across his face, and understood with sudden terrible clarity that he had gotten everything right and it would not matter; that regimes do not fall to cleverness alone; that some bored, tired, ordinary man was about to lift a pillow—

"Sergeant." The lieutenant straightened, sneezing violently, and gestured vaguely at the coffin. "Regimental paperwork's in order. Close it up. Next vehicle, get the queue moving, we're backing up to the Custom House turn."

Sergeant Hollis—Zephyrine would learn the name later, would carry it longer than the lieutenant's—did not move at once. He stood looking into the box with the pillow under his gaze, and then his eyes came up and met Zephyrine's across the coffin, one professional to another, one silent man to another, and held there for a moment that contained an entire conversation conducted in the language Zephyrine had served for three years.

Then Sergeant Hollis reached in, adjusted the wreaths slightly, laid his palm flat on the pillow—pressed once, briefly, the way a man tests a mattress in a shop window—and closed the lid.

"Lung fever," Hollis said loudly, to the soldiers at the handles, to the night, to anyone counting. "Best keep it shut, sir. Family'll want him seen to quick." He spat over the parapet into the canal. "Bridge is clear. Let the mourners through."

The lieutenant was already blowing his nose, already turning to the wagon behind them. Zephyrine gathered up Milo, murmured the carter onward, and did not look back at the sergeant, because men who look back are remembered, and remembering was the one currency the regime never stopped trading in.

The wheels found the far arch. The far lanterns. The second checkpoint waved them through on the strength of the first man's chalk mark, chalk trusting chalk, the machine consuming its own paperwork—and then the bridge was behind them and the embassy wall rose ahead, iron railings furred with frost, and Milo let out a breath he had been holding since the lid came off.

"You didn't lie," the boy said, wonderingly. "Everything you told them was true."

"Rearranged," Zephyrine said. His voice shook only a little. "Remember that, Milo. It's the first rule."

Ahead, at the embassy gate, a single lamp burned above the brass bell-pull, and beneath it, leaning against the railing with his collar up and his saber at his hip as though he had merely stepped out for air, stood Colonel Aurel Vance.

Waiting. As if the route had always ended here. As if he had known exactly which door his courier would knock upon—and had chosen to be standing at it, alone, in the last hour of the old republic, when any man in Vessany with a warrant and two witnesses could have taken them both.
