## 6. The Inheritance of Ash

Milo knew the fire first by its smell.

He was asleep in the loft above the goats, where the hay smelled sweet and the roof leaked one drop for every ten of rain, and the smell came into his dream as bread burning, which was a kind thing to dream, because it meant morning and his mother's hands. Then the dream let go of him and the smell was still there and it was not bread.

He remembers it in pieces, the way you remember anything that is too big to hold: not whole, but in the parts your hands can carry.

The door of the house open and no house behind it, only orange.

His mother's voice saying his name wrong — shorter than his name, cut off in the middle like cloth torn.

The goats screaming. Goats scream like children. He had never known that before. He knew it forever after.

Running along the ditch because his father had shown him the ditch once and said, *if ever anything comes, you go low and you go quiet*, though he had laughed when he said it, because what could ever come to Kesselra? Kesselra was nothing. Forty houses, a well with a bucket that squeaked at the top and bottom of every pull, a shrine to some saint whose name the old people said but nobody believed in anymore. Nothing comes for nothing.

But something had come for nothing, and it burned nothing very thoroughly.

He lay in the ditch water with his coat over his head — his good coat, the one with brass buttons he was saving to grow into — and he watched boots go by on the road above him. Boots make different sounds. He learned them all that night. Soldiers' boots march even when they are only walking, because they have been taught to keep time with something inside themselves. The boots went by in time. Left, right, left, right, going toward the church, and then they stopped, and then the church made a sound like the well bucket breaking, over and over, and then it made a sound like nothing, because it was falling down.

He did not come out until the cold found the water and started drinking the warmth out of him through his knees.

* * *

Morning in Kesselra was gray and soft, ash sifting down like the snow that would come later, and Milo walked through his life while it was still warm.

The house was mostly gone but the chimney stood, and against the chimney lay a thing he looked at for a long time without understanding, because understanding has to arrive from somewhere far away and it walks slowly when the road is bad. His mother's ring hand. Just that. The rest of her was smoke by then or would be soon, but rings are patient things; they stay.

He sat down beside the chimney and held the hand. It was heavy the way wet wood is heavy. He talked to it. He told it about the goats, which were quiet now, quieter than goats should be, and about the ditch, and how he had done what his father said, gone low, gone quiet, and how it hadn't helped, and wasn't that funny, that doing the right thing hadn't helped at all.

A crow landed on the well and squeaked the way the bucket used to squeak, at the top and bottom of every pull.

That was when he stopped talking.

* * *

The man came in the afternoon, or what passed for afternoon under the ash sky.

Milo heard the horse first and flattened himself against the chimney the way he had flattened himself against the ditch, because now he knew what boots meant and he assumed horses meant the same thing. The horse stopped. Boots dismounted — these were walking boots, not marching ones, they broke their own rhythm, lazy, tired — and then a shape stood over him, blocking the gray light, wearing gray and darker gray, and Milo shut his eyes because shutting your eyes is the last wall a small person has.

Nothing happened. That was the thing. He waited for the wall to fall and nothing happened, and when he finally opened his eyes the man was crouching, which soldiers do not do, crouching puts your face level with a child's face, and this man had put his face level with Milo's on purpose.

He wore the insignia of the Ninth Regiment. A silver gate on a field of black, stitched at the collar. Milo knew it the way every child in Vessany knew it, from posters, from parades, from the coin with the gate on one side and an eagle on the other. The gate of the Fallen. The regiment that guarded everything.

The man looked at the hand against the chimney. Then he took off his own glove — slowly, showing Milo his empty palm first, the way you show a dog — and he covered the hand gently, both of his around it, and he said something in a low voice that Milo could not hear properly, or perhaps heard and refused, because what he remembered hearing was *I'm sorry*, and sorry is a word that does more damage than most, when it comes from the right mouth at the wrong time.

Then the man picked him up.

Milo fought. He was seven and light as a loaf and he fought the way ash falls, uselessly, everywhere, and the man took it, wrapped him in the long gray coat, and carried him out of Kesselra past the well, past the shrine, past the church that was no longer anything. Milo watched it all upside down over the man's shoulder, his village sliding away like a plate pushed off a table, and he bit the man's neck through his collar, hard enough to taste wool and salt, and the man never once tightened his grip or made a sound.

At the crossroads the man set him down, gave him water from a flask that tasted of tin, and looked at him for a long moment with an expression Milo was too young to read and has spent every night since trying to read. Then he lifted Milo onto the horse's back, tied him there softly with a scarf, and slapped the horse's flank.

The last thing Milo saw of him was the silver gate at the collar, catching the ash-light, getting smaller.

He never saw the face again clearly. Faces leave. Insignias stay.

* * *

Here is what Milo knows now, in the safehouse with the heavy curtains, in the city that smells of ice and iron: names.

The man who carries him food calls himself Zephyrine, which is a name like a bird flying into a window. He talks too much when he is frightened and too little when he is calm, and Milo has decided this means he is honest, because liars are the same amount either way.

Zephyrine asks him questions at night, sitting on the floor beside the cot so he won't loom, and Milo gives him fragments, because fragments are all that is left and also because Zephyrine flinches at each one and Milo wants to know if he will keep coming back anyway. He keeps coming back. That is data. Milo collects it the way other children collect bottle caps.

Tonight Zephyrine asked about the soldier. The one at the chimney.

"The gate," Milo said. He drew it on the frost of the window with one finger: the arch, the two pillars. "Silver. Here." He touched his own collar.

And Zephyrine went still.

Milo watches people the way he used to watch the kettle — waiting for the tremor that says it's about to go over. And Zephyrine trembled. Not outside. Outside he became very smooth, smooth like frozen water, which Milo has learned is worse. His hand went to his coat, to the inner pocket where the papers live, the papers sewn into everything, the papers Milo has understood since the doorstep are the reason for the running and the hiding and the food that arrives on schedules.

"Say it again," Zephyrine said. Quietly. "The insignia."

"The gate," Milo said. "Black field. Silver gate." And then, because he had hoarded this a long time and hoards get heavy: "He put his hands over my mother's hand. On the chimney. Like it was cold."

Zephyrine closed his eyes.

Milo has seen men lie. The soldiers in Kesselra, afterward, when neighbors came asking — lying is a loosening, a shrug in the voice, nothing to see here, move along. Zephyrine did not loosen. Zephyrine tightened. Every line of him pulled taut around something, the way a rope goes hard when it takes a weight, and Milo understood, in the wordless arithmetic of children, that the weight was enormous and that Zephyrine was deciding, right there, in front of him, whether to drop it or hold on.

"He carried me out," Milo said. "He didn't have to. Everyone else—" He stopped. Fragments. He gave Zephyrine the last one he had: "He smelled like horses and gun oil. And he was sad. Sad like it was his."

Zephyrine opened his eyes and they were wet and furious at once, which Milo had not known eyes could be.

"Milo." Zephyrine's voice did the thing where it steadies itself by force. "The man who carried you. Was he tall? Was there grey at his temples? Did he have—" and his own hand rose, without permission, and touched the side of his own jaw, tracing something on his own face that belonged to another one.

Milo considered. Faces leave. But hands stay, and voices stay, and the pressure of a glove covering yours stays.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "I know the gate."

Zephyrine sat back against the wall and looked at the ceiling for a long time, and Milo looked at him, and between them the frost-gate melted slowly, drop by drop, running down the window like the ditch water that had once tried to save him.

Later, when the lamp was out, Milo heard Zephyrine moving in the dark — packing, quietly, the way he did before checkpoints — and heard him stop, and stand very still over the cot, and say one word into the darkness. Milo pretended to sleep. It was a skill he had perfected in ditches and under coats.

The word was *"Aurel."*

It sounded like a question. It sounded like a wound being reopened on purpose, to check whether it still bled.

In the morning there was a new paper in Zephyrine's coat, a route marked in a hand Milo wasn't allowed to see, and a second checkpoint waiting somewhere across the frozen city with soldiers at it wearing the silver gate — and Zephyrine, feeding him bread with steady hands and unsteady eyes, said: "We leave at dusk."

Milo chewed and thought about gates. Some gates keep things out. Some keep things in. And one, somewhere in the ash of Kesselra or somewhere behind the eyes of the man called Aurel, had been left open — just wide enough for one small boy and a horse.

He wondered, chewing, whether it would be open wide enough for two.
