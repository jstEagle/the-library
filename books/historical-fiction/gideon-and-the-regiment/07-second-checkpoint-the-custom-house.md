## 7. Second Checkpoint: The Custom House

By the third day of martial law, the city had learned a new grammar. Curfew was its comma; the drumbeat at dusk, its full stop. Men spoke in half-sentences and finished them with their eyes. In the fish market, where the ice had gone grey and the gulls had grown bold enough to walk among the stalls like creditors, Zephyrine heard a woman sell her opinions for the price of two mackerel and understood that everyone was now, in some small way, a courier.

He was simply better at it than most.

Milo walked beside him with his collar up and his hair stuffed beneath a cap three sizes too large, and Zephyrine kept his hand on the boy's shoulder not out of tenderness but because a hand on a shoulder is the oldest lie in any city: *this child is mine, this errand is ordinary, nothing here is worth your notice.* The boy had learned the game faster than Zephyrine liked. Three years old past whatever age he truly was, and already he could make his face go empty as a shuttered window.

The custom house rose at the end of the Salt Quay like something that had been built to be hated — a squat block of granite the colour of old bruises, its windows barred, its doors flanked by two soldiers who stamped their feet against the cold in perfect unison, as if even their discomfort had been drilled. Above the lintel the republic's arms still hung: the sheaf, the scale, the star. Someone had thrown ink at the star. It had dried black and looked less like vandalism than like an amendment.

"Papers," said the soldier at the steps, before they were near enough to smell the brazier.

Zephyrine produced his courier's warrant with the flat calm of long practice. The soldier read it upside down, which told Zephyrine he could not read it right side up, which told him this was not the man to fear. The man to fear stood inside, behind the ledger desk, where an officer with a face like a closed account was turning pages one by one.

"Two," the officer said without looking up. He had heard the boy's boots on the marble. "Warrant covers one."

"He's my sister's son. Travelling under family papers."

"Family papers." The officer's pen scratched. "Show them."

*There it is,* thought Zephyrine, in the place behind his sternum where fear kept its cold little fire. *There is the wall.*

* * *

The forger worked from the back room of a print shop that had once produced almanacs and now, by unspoken civic agreement, produced nothing at all. His name — the name Zephyrine was permitted to know — was Hessel. He had hands stained silver from nitrate and a way of looking at people as though estimating their weight in paper.

"Two sets," Zephyrine said. "An adult travelling on regimental business, a child listed as kin. Family name Marchetti." It was the first time he had ever invented a surname for himself, and it tasted strange — his mother's maiden name, spent now like coin.

Hessel turned a page of specimen blanks with one finger. "For a child you'll want a registry stamp. Registry stamps come from the census office, and the census office has been requisitioned by the provost since Tuesday." He named a price.

It was more money than Zephyrine carried. They both knew it. Hessel knew it the way craftsmen know things, without being told, and so Zephyrine did the thing he had been refusing to do since he'd left his lodging: he drew the ring from beneath his shirt, where it hung on a cord against his heart, warm from his skin.

His mother's ring. Silver, worn thin on the inner band, set with a garnet the colour of wine held up to lamplight. She had worn it through two sieges and a famine and had pressed it into his eighteen-year-old hand with instructions he had never followed: *Keep something that is only yours. Not the regiment's. Not anyone's.*

Hessel took it and held it to the window light, and for a moment his professional detachment slipped and showed something almost like regret. "Garnets are out of season," he said quietly. "Everything honest is."

"Will it cover the work?"

"It will cover the work twice over." He weighed the ring in his palm. "I'll return the difference."

"Keep it," Zephyrine said. "If I'm stopped, the papers will be worth nothing and neither will the change."

Hessel looked at him then, fully, for the first time. "You're going through the custom house."

"Yes."

"Then hear me." He lowered his voice, though there was no one else in the room but Milo, who sat on a stool by the cold stove drawing invisible things on the floor with his finger. "Your route has been flagged. Not flagged for search." A pause, precise as a knife laid down between them. "Flagged for protection. There's a standing instruction in the provost's circulation — courier Z. Iversen, south approach, priority passage, no detention. Signed under Colonel Vance's own seal."

The room went very still. The stove ticked as its iron cooled. Outside, a patrol passed singing something that used to be a hymn.

"Protection," Zephyrine repeated.

"That's what the paper says." Hessel spread his stained hands. "Paper says what it says. Whether it means what it says—" he shrugged, and the gesture contained the entire republic: every order ever softened, every kindness ever doubled as a leash. "A flag can be shelter. A flag can be a mark on a beast the hunters have agreed to save for last. I've seen both. I cannot tell you which yours is."

"When was it filed?"

"The morning after the bridge. Before dawn."

The morning after the Bridge of Sighs, where a sympathetic sergeant had looked away from a coffin that did not hold a dead man. Someone had known about the coffin. Someone had known about the route. And instead of sealing the bridge, they had opened the road ahead of him — and Zephyrine had walked down it grateful, like a fool walking into a corridor built around him brick by patient brick.

*Is it surveillance or shelter?* The question would not sit still. It turned over and over like a coin spun on a table, showing first a face he loved, then a face he feared, and never landing.

"Finish the papers," he said.

* * *

They came for the documents at dusk, when the light through the shop's front windows had gone the colour of weak tea. Hessel handed them across with a craftsman's contempt for his own skill: two passports, a travel writ, a registry extract certifying one Milo Marchetti, aged seven, born Salt Quarter, father deceased in the Kesselra levies.

That last line, Zephyrine had asked for himself. It was a lie shaped exactly like the truth, which is the only kind of lie that holds weight.

"Father deceased in the Kesselra levies," Hessel murmured, watching him read it. "Sentimental."

"Useful."

"Cruel, too. Whoever reads it will nod. Everyone nods at Kesselra now. It's become a word people use to mean *nothing we can prove*." Hessel wiped his hands on his apron. "You carry something heavier than papers, courier. I don't ask what. But if it's what I think it is — and the whole city thinks it's something, the whole city smells smoke that isn't there — then understand what a flag for protection means. It means someone powerful has decided you matter alive. Those decisions get revisited." He glanced toward Milo. "And children complicate decisions. Children are the thing men forget to include in their plans, and then the plan fails because of them, and then they blame the child."

"Is that advice or warning?"

"It's arithmetic." Hessel held out his hand, palm up, and Zephyrine realized after a confused moment that the ring lay in it. "Take it back."

"You said—"

"I said it covered the work twice over. The work is done. I don't need twice over." He closed Zephyrine's fingers around it with his silver-stained hand. "My mother died owing money, courier. Nobody ever gave me anything I didn't forge. Let me give one true thing away while I can still choose to." He stepped back, embarrassed by his own sentence, gruff again at once. "South gate closes at nine bells. After that, the customs officers hand everything to the provost, and the provost's men are not the reading-upside-down kind."

* * *

They slept that night in the loft above a tannery, where the smell kept patrols away better than any lock, and Zephyrine lay awake with the ring's cord back around his neck and Milo's breath against his ribs, small and even as a metronome counting down to something.

He held the boy's forged passport up to the slice of moon coming through the slats and studied Hessel's work. Excellent work. Registry stamp correct. Seal correct. Everything correct except—

Except the date on the filing line. One day early. The passport was dated yesterday, which was normal practice for forgers covering their traces, and would pass any inspection, and yet.

Yet the flag had been filed the morning after the bridge. Yet Vance had signed it. Yet Vance had summoned him three nights ago and poured wine with hands that were steady and eyes that were not, and asked him, gently, terribly, whether he had found anything *interesting* in his travels lately — and Zephyrine had smiled and lied beautifully, and watched Vance smile and know he was lying, and neither of them had failed openly, because failing openly would have required naming the thing between them, and neither of them could afford to name anything anymore except duty.

*A flag can be shelter. A flag can be a mark on a beast saved for last.*

Vance had left the child at his door. He was almost certain of it now. The regimental watermark on the note; the timing; the way the colonel's questions always bent, however subtly, toward whether the boy was safe, fed, warm. A man does not ask those questions about evidence. He asks them about a child he put somewhere on purpose.

But Vance had also countersigned the Kesselra order. Zephyrine had seen the hand. He knew that hand the way he knew the colonel's voice — the slight forward lean of every letter, the discipline of it, the restraint. The same hand that had written, in the margin of Zephyrine's cadet examinations six years ago, *This one understands silence. Cultivate him.*

*Cultivate him.* Like a thing in a garden. Like something planted for later harvest.

Milo stirred and murmured in his sleep, a fragment of a word from a village that no longer existed, and Zephyrine smoothed the cap back from the boy's forehead and felt his heart do the thing it had been doing for days: splitting along a seam he could not find, one half pulling him toward the embassy and the truth, the other half pulling backward through every frozen checkpoint and every learned silence toward a door in the Colonel's quarters where a lamp always burned and a voice always said, *Sit down, Iversen. You look half-frozen. You never take care of yourself unless someone makes you.*

Tomorrow: the Gate of the Fallen. The third checkpoint. The last one.

And at dusk, as he folded the forged papers back into the dispatch case where they lay alongside the cipher and the massacre order and the whole unbearable weight of sealed things, he heard boots on the tannery stair — unhurried, familiar boots, taking the steps with the measured tread of a man who had never once needed to hurry anywhere in his life.

Zephyrine blew out the candle and put his body between the door and the child.

The latch lifted without a knock. Only one man in all of Vessany entered rooms that way.
