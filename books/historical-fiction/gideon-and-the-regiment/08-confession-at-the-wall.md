## 8. Confession at the Wall

The wall behind the arsenal had been built to keep enemies out, and like most things the Republic built, it had come to face inward instead. Aurel Vance stood beneath its frozen shadow and watched the sentries pass with lanterns, their light sliding across the stones like water over a closed door.

He had chosen this place because it was empty after the ninth bell, because the wind funneled along it and made eavesdropping impossible, and because he had once kissed a man against these very stones on a night neither of them had ever mentioned again. Sentiment was a tactical liability. He had chosen it anyway.

Zephyrine came at the tenth bell, as Vance knew he would. The courier moved the way he always had — quiet as falling snow, shoulders loose, hands empty, everything about him arranged to be overlooked. Three years of training, of shaping, and Vance could still pick him out of a crowd of thousands by the way he held his left hand slightly open, ready to be useful.

"Colonel." Zephyrine stopped six paces away. Correct distance. Respectful. A lie told with the body.

"Mister Iversen." Vance let the silence stretch. "You walk well at night."

"You taught me."

"I taught you not to be seen." He stepped out of the shadow of the wall. "Tonight I need to know if you learned anything else."

Above them the stars were hard and cold, scratched into a sky the color of gun metal. Somewhere beyond the river a bell rang wrong — two strokes too many — and did not correct itself. The city was coming apart in small ways first.

"You flagged my route," Zephyrine said. "Through the custom house. For protection, they told me."

"They told you that?"

"A clerk did. Smiling."

"Clerks smile when they're frightened." Vance clasped his hands behind his back, the old parade-ground posture, the one that meant nothing could be read from him. But tonight there was nothing left to protect by concealment except the last few minutes in which concealment was still possible. "How is the boy?"

The flinch was small. Beautiful. Terrible. Vance watched it travel through Zephyrine like current through wire.

"He eats," Zephyrine said carefully. "He sleeps. He speaks in pieces."

"Fragments are how survivors talk. I've heard it before." He paused. "Kesselra."

The name dropped between them like a stone down a well. Neither man reached for it. The wind came off the wall and cut sideways, carrying the smell of ice and horse and distant smoke — the city burning in places already, small fires that would grow.

"You know what I carried," Zephyrine said quietly.

"I have known since the bridge."

"Then you know I have to finish it."

"I know you'll try. That's different." Vance turned to face him fully. It cost more than he expected; he had faced firing squads with less effort than it took to hold this man's eyes. "Walk with me. Ten minutes. Then you can hate me properly for the rest of your life."

They walked. The wall stretched away toward the river gate, black stone furred with frost, older than the Republic that claimed it.

"I signed the order," Vance said.

Zephyrine said nothing. His breathing did not change. Good training. Bad sign.

"The Kesselra evacuation order came down from General Marovet's office in October. It arrived with a signature line and an aide who waited in my quarters while I read it — not in the office, mind, in my quarters, where my letters were, where the photographs were. They waited while I signed because they wanted my hand on it and my eyes seen signing it. Marovet needed a decorated colonel attached to the atrocity so that if it ever surfaced, the scandal would drown a man the government trusted too much anyway." He laughed once, without warmth. "I was given a choice between my signature and my arrest, and I chose like a soldier. Quickly, and believing someone above me had already weighed the consequences I hadn't."

"And the countersignature—"

"Is mine. In full. Unmistakable." He stopped walking. So did Zephyrine, though he kept half a pace of distance, the way one keeps distance from a fire that has already burned you once. "I won't insult you by explaining duress. Duress explains nothing to the dead. Four hundred people, Zephyrine. A village that no longer appears on maps printed after November. I put my name on that, whatever they held over me, and there is no wine enough in the world to wash it into something else."

"Then why am I standing here?" Zephyrine's voice was low and even, which was worse than shouting. "Why aren't you arresting me? You've known since the bridge. You've had men watching my route. You corner me at midnight against a wall with no witnesses, and you confess." His jaw tightened. "Either you're here to kill me, Colonel, or you're here to be forgiven. And I don't think you came for forgiveness."

"No," Vance said. "I came because in about thirty-six hours this city closes like a fist, and after that nothing gets out — not documents, not children, not truth. And because you deserve to hear it from me rather than decode it from paper." He drew a breath, and the air hurt going in. "Marovet's operation has been failing for months. Convoy routes misfiled. Ammunition requisitions delayed until the windows closed. A list of names that reached the press office of a foreign newspaper with three columns missing. Reports that contradicted each other so thoroughly that two of Marovet's own majors stopped trusting each other. Someone inside the machine has been feeding it sand."

He watched Zephyrine's face change — the slow reordering of a man recalculating everything.

"That was you."

"For eleven months. Small things. Patient things. Sabotage is mostly bookkeeping, it turns out. It's not glorious. Nobody writes songs about altered ledgers." He looked up at the wall, at the frost silvering the stone. "It also saved almost no one. Kesselra happened anyway. I slowed the machine. I did not stop it. I want that understood — I am not offering you my sabotage as absolution. I'm telling you so you understand what I left at your door."

The wind stopped for a moment, or seemed to. The whole city held its breath around them.

"The note said *forget the face*," Zephyrine whispered.

"Yes. I wrote it. I carried Milo out of Kesselra myself, four days after the order was executed, when I went back to survey the site for the report that was supposed to call it a quarantine action. He was under a floor. He bit me." Vance turned his wrist reflexively, an old ghost of a scar under the glove. "I couldn't leave him and I couldn't keep him and I couldn't explain him, so I did the only cowardly clever thing available: I gave him to the one person I trusted to keep a child alive, and I didn't sign that decision either. First thing in three years I've done unsigned."

Zephyrine's breath fogged and hung between them. When he spoke, his voice had gone somewhere Vance couldn't follow. "You used me."

"I trust you. There's a difference, however unforgivable the methods." Vance stepped closer, close enough to see the frost melting on Zephyrine's collar where his own breath had touched it. "And now I'm going to use you once more, and then never again. Listen to me. Tomorrow night, a grain wagon crosses the second checkpoint at the fourth bell. The driver owes me his brother's life. Inside the grain, there will be room for two: you and the boy. No papers, no dispatch case, no coffin, no evidence. It goes to the coast. From the coast, any ship, any flag. You disappear, both of you, alive, and the Republic can do what dying things do."

"And the documents?"

"Stay with me. I'll bury them so deep in the regimental archive that no one will find them for fifty years, and by then everyone they incriminate will be comfortably dead of old age."

"No."

The word landed between them, small and absolute as a nail driven flush.

Vance had prepared for arguments. He had prepared for tears, for bargaining, for the cold arithmetic he himself would have used — *one courier's life against a city of checkpoints*. He had not prepared for the flat calm in Zephyrine's voice, the calm Vance himself had installed there, lesson by lesson, year by year.

"Zephyrine—"

"No." Louder now, and the wall took it and threw it back. "You slow the machine for eleven months and save almost no one — those are your words. You sign an order and four hundred people burn, and your remedy is a grain wagon and a locked archive. You're offering me survival, Colonel. You've offered it to yourself for a year, in installments, and look what it bought." He stepped forward into the space between them, and this close, Vance could see he was trembling, and could see the will holding the trembling still, and it was the bravest thing Vance had ever witnessed, including anything done under fire. "The documents go to the embassy. Tomorrow night. Through all three checkpoints. With the boy, because the boy is proof that can't be forged — a living witness with a cipher sewn into his coat that matches mine."

"They'll kill you at the third checkpoint. Martial law means shoot first and file later. You know this."

"I know it. I learned it from you."

For a moment neither spoke. Above them the damaged bell tolled again, still wrong, still uncorrected, and far off to the east a red glow sat on the horizon like a coal refusing to die.

"Come with me, then," Zephyrine said, softer. "Both of us. Three seats in a grain wagon. The embassy doesn't care whose signature is on whose sins — they take defectors every day. You know things, Aurel. Names, routes, numbers. You could burn Marovet down from the outside for the rest of your life."

It was the first time in three years either of them had said the other's given name aloud in the open air, and Vance felt it land somewhere beneath the armor, exactly where it had always landed, exactly where he had spent a career pretending he had no exposed ground.

"You know I can't."

"I know you *won't*. There's a difference, however unforgivable the distinction."

Vance laughed — really laughed, briefly, helplessly — because the boy had thrown his own words back at him with interest, and because there was no other response left in him that wasn't grief. "If I vanish, Marovet knows within a day, and the first thing he does is lock the gates and the second thing he does is tear the city apart looking for what I took. Your route dies at the first checkpoint. As long as I'm here in uniform, the machine trusts me, and while it trusts me—" he met Zephyrine's eyes— "the third gate stays soft. I can pull the garrison duty rotation. I can make sure the men on that gate tomorrow night are tired, cold, and inclined to believe paperwork. That's worth more to your mission than my name in a foreign newspaper."

"And after?"

"After, there is no after. There's tomorrow night, and there's doing the job." He reached up, slowly, telegraphing every inch of the movement, and took Zephyrine's gloved hand. Through two layers of leather he pressed something small and cold into it: the brass compass he had carried since the academy, its lid worn smooth, its needle faithful to a pole neither of them would ever see. "North is that way. It always has been. I'm not certain I've ever once followed it, but you should start."

"Aurel—"

"Don't." His voice cracked on it, just barely, just enough. "Don't say it here. If you say it here I'll stay, and staying kills you and the boy and everything you're carrying. Say it tomorrow, past the wall, past the gate, on the other side of the river where it can't be used against either of us." He released the hand. He stepped back into the posture of a colonel again, spine straight, face shut, the whole performance assembled one last time from the wreckage. "Fourth bell. Grain wagon. One last offer of a seat — and I mean it. After that, you're on your own, courier, and I never saw you, and you never saw me."

Zephyrine stood holding the compass like it weighed more than the dispatches, more than the child, more than the Republic entire. He looked at Vance for a long moment with an expression that was not hatred and could not yet be called hope.

"If you're lying about the gate," he said quietly, "I'll haunt you."

"If I'm lying about the gate," Vance answered, "you won't get the chance."

Zephyrine turned and walked toward the dark end of the wall, and the shadows took him the way Vance had taught him — silently, completely, like snow falling into snow. Vance stayed beneath the frozen stones a while longer, listening to the wrong bell toll and the far fires breathe, and then he went to write the orders that would strip his own garrison from the Gate of the Fallen.

By morning, the city would be burning in earnest.

By fourth bell, he intended to be standing at the gate himself — to hold it open with his own hands, if it came to that, for as long as hands were any use at all.
