## 9. Third Checkpoint: The Gate of the Fallen

The city began to burn an hour before dawn, and the light of it reached them before the sound did—a red bloom on the underside of the clouds, like a lamp lit behind flesh.

Zephyrine stood at the mouth of Tanner's Row with Milo's hand in his and watched the sky change color. Three years of carrying other men's secrets had taught him to read a city the way sailors read water: the slackness in the market stalls, the soldiers moving in pairs instead of fours, the way no dogs barked. All winter he had watched the republic tighten like a fist. Now he watched it open its fingers, and everything it had been holding fell out at once.

"It's early," Milo said. Not a question. The boy had learned to measure the world in hours of safety remaining, the way other children measured it in sweets and saints' days.

"It's early," Zephyrine agreed. "That changes nothing. We go now."

They went through the burning quarters the way water goes through cracks—never straight, never hurrying where haste would draw the eye. The coffin had been left for them behind the chandler's shop as arranged, though the arrangement was three days old and half the men who'd made it were probably dead or changed sides by now. That was the nature of tonight: allegiances shedding like snow off a warm roof. The coffin rode on a handcart, and inside it, wrapped in sailcloth beneath a respectable shroud, lay the truth of Kesselra.

Milo walked beside the cart without being asked to. Somewhere in these weeks the boy had stopped needing to be told things; he simply did them, quiet as a held breath. He wore the coat Zephyrine had bought too large so he could grow into it, and under it, against his chest, the cipher that had started all of this, folded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

"The gate," Milo said, when they paused in the shadow of the tannery to let a patrol pass. "Will he be there?"

Zephyrine did not pretend to misunderstand. There was no one left to misunderstand for. "He'll be there. It's his post."

"Will he let us through?"

Zephyrine thought of wine going cold between them on the table at the wall, of Vance's face in the lamplight when he'd said *I can get you out—just you, just the boy, leave the papers, and none of this ever has to have happened.* Thought of his own answer, which had been to pick up the dispatch case and walk toward the door.

"I don't know," he said, because lying to Milo had become a thing he could no longer do, even kindly. "But we're going to find out."

* * *

Vance heard the city break before his runner arrived—the deep concussive cough of the arsenal going up across the river, and then, rolling after it, a sound he had never heard in thirty years of service: the whole capital screaming at once, ten thousand throats opened together, grief and fury indistinguishable.

He was at the Gate of the Fallen with two hundred men and orders written in an hour that would be laughed at by morning. *Hold the gate. Permit no passage to the embassy quarter. Fire on crowds as necessary.* The signature above his own was already ash somewhere, or evidence, depending on who won the night.

"Colonel." Captain Dreez came down the stair from the wall, pale under his helmet. "The crowd—they've got the customs house. Sergeant Ovel's detachment went over to them. Just—walked off their posts, sir. Walked off."

"And the third checkpoint?"

"Is this gate, sir."

Yes, Vance thought. This gate. He looked out through the wicket at what was coming up the Processional Road: not an army, nothing so clean. A flood of torches and lanterns and burning chairs lashed to poles, and in among it faces he knew—clerks from the ministry, women with their sleeves rolled, a printer's apprentice he'd once caught stealing apples from the garrison carts and let go because he'd been that age himself once. They carried the dead with them on doors and tables. The fallen, coming to their gate. Someone had named it better than they'd known.

"Sir?" Dreez said. "The order—"

"Is to fire on civilians." Vance kept his voice level, the parade-ground flat that Zephyrine had once told him sounded like a door closing. "I know what the order is, Captain. I can read. What I'm asking myself is whether I can obey."

Dreez stared at him. Behind Dreez, along the wall, forty faces had turned very slightly, the way a field of wheat turns—not toward him exactly, but toward whatever he was about to be.

There had been a time, Vance thought, when the oath had meant something simple. Protect the republic. And there had been a night in a border village called Kesselra when the word *republic* had been used to him like a crowbar, to pry loose the thing he actually believed, and he had signed the paper they put in front of him because the alternative was a cell and worse, and he had told himself that a man alive could still work against the machine from inside its gears. He had spent months learning whether that was true. It was true only in this way: a man inside the machine could, if he was very careful and very lucky, place one child on one doorstep. Could lose one set of countersigned orders into another man's hands. Could stand at one gate, on one night, and make of his whole corrupt, compromised career a single honest act.

It was not enough. Nothing would be enough. But it was what he had.

"Captain Dreez," he said. "I am relieving you of your orders. All of them. Every man here—I relieve you. Whatever you do in the next hour, do it as free men, and live with it afterward as I intend to live with mine."

* * *

They heard the gate before they saw it: a sound like a struck bell, huge and wrong, and then the crowd noise changing pitch, and Zephyrine pressed the handcart into an alcove and pulled Milo close and waited for the volley that would come, the one that always came, the arithmetic every courier learned.

No volley came.

Instead, over the roar, a single voice, amplified by the gate's stone throat, pitched to carry—and Zephyrine's whole body recognized it before his mind caught up, the way his body still turned toward certain footsteps in empty corridors.

*"This gate is open!"* Colonel Aurel Vance was saying to a crowd that had come, most of them, to die at his barricade. *"By order of the garrison of the Gate of the Fallen, this gate is OPEN! Stand down! STAND DOWN—all companies! Weapons DOWN!"*

And then a silence with two hundred rifles in it, and then the silence broke, not into gunfire but into the clatter and clash of arms being grounded, slung, dropped—and the crowd's roar turning inside out, becoming something else entirely, and Zephyrine stood in the alcove shaking.

"He's doing it," Milo whispered. "He's letting them through."

"He's letting everyone through." Zephyrine's voice came out strange. Through the crack of the shuttered window across the lane he could see the gate square now: torchlight, the mass of people flowing toward the arch like a river finding the sea, and above them, on the gatehouse steps, a figure in a colonel's greatcoat with his hands raised, turning slowly so all could see them empty.

*Everyone,* Zephyrine thought, *except the two men whose names are on the paper in this coffin.*

* * *

Vance saw them at the edge of the square and knew, in the way he had always known where Zephyrine was in any room, without looking.

They came with the flow of the crowd, pushing the handcart, the boy walking close, the man scanning rooftops with those courier's eyes that missed nothing—and Vance felt something in his chest come apart and reassemble itself as resolve. Because he was not the only one who had seen them. Lieutenant Harn of the provost detail was on the wall with twenty men who had not grounded their arms, men whose loyalty ran to the Ministry and not to any colonel's conscience, and Harn was watching the cart with the particular attention of a man who has been told what to look for.

*Flagged for protection,* the route order had said, and Vance had written it himself, meaning it both ways, surveillance and shelter, and tonight the ambiguity came due.

He crossed the square fast, shouldering through the crowd, and took the cart's handle out of Zephyrine's grip as though he had every right to it, as though this were an inspection.

"Colonel—" Zephyrine started.

"Don't talk." Vance steered them toward the arch, matching the crowd's pace, his greatcoat swinging wide to hide the cart's outline from the wall. Up close Zephyrine smelled of smoke and cold iron, and there was a cut on his cheekbone, and Vance wanted with a physical ache to stop and touch it and say everything, and instead said, quietly, under the din: "Harn's on the north parapet. Provost detail. They didn't stand down. Twenty rifles, and he's under orders to stop the documents specifically—he doesn't care about you, but he will fire on the cart."

"Then we go around—"

"There is no around. There is only through, and quickly." They passed under the arch itself, into the tunnel of stone where the torchlight couldn't follow, and in that brief darkness Vance stopped pretending to steer the cart and gripped Zephyrine's wrist instead, hard, once, a whole letter written in five seconds of pressure. "The embassy is four streets. The green door with the brass knocker. Ask for Madam Sorel by name. Zeph—" His voice failed at the name, caught on three years of not saying it. "You were the best thing I ever trained and the only good thing I ever loved, and both of those are true and neither one was ever a lie."

"Aurel—"

"Go." He pushed them out the far side into the torchlight of the embassies' quarter road, and turned back toward the gate.

* * *

Milo looked back. Children look back; that is their privilege and their curse.

From the middle of the open road, the gate square showed whole and small, like a picture seen through the wrong end of a spyglass. He saw the soldier who had carried him out of the fire climb the gatehouse steps, above the crowd, above his own men, arms spread wide—not surrendering, Milo understood somehow, though he was small and much had been taken from him. Standing between. The way the big soldier had stood between him and the burning barn door, that last night in the village that no longer existed, except that it did exist, it lived in Milo's chest and in the cipher folded against his heart.

"Keep moving," Zephyrine said hoarsely, pulling the cart. But he was looking back too.

The first shots came from the north parapet. Milo saw the sparks of them, little orange flowers, absurdly small for what they did. He saw the crowd surge. He saw the colonel on the steps stagger, and turn—deliberately, Milo would think later, deliberately, so that his body blocked the sight-line from the parapet down the road, so that the rifles tracking the coffin-shaped bundle on the cart would have to shoot through him—and he saw him reach up and seize the great iron counterweight chain of the portcullis housing and haul the lever, jamming the raising mechanism open with his own hands on the bar, holding the gate up the way you hold a door.

The way you hold a door.

"Zeph." Milo's voice cracked. "Zeph, he's—"

"I see him." Zephyrine was running now, hauling the cart, tears cutting tracks through the soot on his face, running toward the green door with the brass knocker while behind them the volleys cracked and cracked again, steady as a clock, and the gate stayed up.

They reached the door. Zephyrine hammered on it with the flat of his fist, once, twice, and it opened onto lamplight and a tall woman with grey hair and a face that had already heard tonight's news in her bones, and behind Zephyrine, far up the road, the firing stopped all at once, the way a heart stops.

Milo turned.

Down the long street, in the mouth of the gate, the torches of the crowd had closed over the place where the colonel had been standing, and the great gate was coming down at last, slow and grinding, and the last thing visible before it met the stones was nothing at all—just the ordinary dark of an ordinary doorway, and beyond it a city full of people who would tell this story tomorrow, and the day after, forever, in a thousand versions, only one of which was true.

Zephyrine's hand found Milo's shoulder and gripped it, and together, at the threshold of the embassy, they watched the dust of the fallen gate rise into the burning sky.

Then the door opened wider, and Madam Sorel said, "You'd better come in. Both of you. And you'd better show me what he died to carry."
