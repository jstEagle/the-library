## 2. Letters I Was Never Meant to Read

There is a particular hour in Manchester when the fog stops being weather and becomes architecture. It builds walls between the lamps, closes streets into corridors, turns every approaching figure into a rumour. I learned to drive in that hour, two years ago, with my hands frozen on the wheel of Mrs. Marchmont's Hupmobile and my heart going like a loom. Now the fog and I are old colleagues. It hides me; I hide things for it.

On the fourth of February, 1913, I drove Mrs. Edith Marchmont through that grey architecture from Cheetham to Ardwick and back again, and if you had asked any of the men slouching past the motor what the driver looked like, they would have told you: a cap, a muffler, a woman probably, young certainly — and nothing else. That was my whole biography, as far as Manchester knew it. A cap, a muffler, a guess.

Mrs. Marchmont dictated letters in the back seat, her voice pitched low over the engine's clatter as though even inside her own motor she might be overheard.

"Tell Miss Garrud the Sheffield date must move," she said. "The eleventh, not the ninth. And tell her to burn this."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Ines—" The pause. I watched her in the small mirror mounted by the windscreen, a face like a portrait come to judgement, handsome still at fifty-one, grey hair pinned with the severity of a woman who had argued with God and got a draw. "You remember nothing you hear in this motor. You understand me?"

"Every word forgotten before it's finished, ma'am." I turned us onto Great Ancoats Street. "That's why you keep me."

"That's one reason." She smiled then, quick and private, and I felt it land somewhere under my ribs where I kept the things I didn't look at directly. She did not say *because you're clever* or *because you're loyal*. She said *that's one reason*, and left the rest hanging in the fog like washing left out overnight.

I took the long way back to Cheetham Hill. Not because the short way was dangerous — it wasn't, not yet, not in February — but because the long way gave her time to finish her correspondence and gave me time to think, which is a commodity I rationed carefully in those days. Thinking too much was how girls got found out. My brother Joe had thought too much about a till drawer full of somebody else's money, and now he thought instead about eucalyptus trees in a country he'd never see, if he thought at all.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. That morning I only thought about dinner, because Thursday was the cook's night for oxtail, and Mrs. Peel's oxtail could make a Methodist speak in tongues.

* * *

The Marchmont house on Wellington Street announced itself to the neighbourhood in three ways: the brass plate with *E. MARCHMONT, ORGANISING SECRETARY* engraved in lettering modest enough to be deniable; the flagpole on the roof that flew purple, white and green when Parliament was sitting and nothing when the constables grew restive; and the smell, on Thursdays, of Mrs. Peel's oxtail drifting down the area steps like an ambassador from a better country.

I brought the motor round the mews behind, chocked the wheels, covered the bonnet, and came in through the kitchen as I always did, because drivers come in through kitchens. Front doors are for ladies with gloves.

"Well, the wanderer returns." Mrs. Peel didn't turn from her range. She was a wide woman built along the lines of a ship's boiler, with forearms that could have opened oysters at forty paces, and she governed that kitchen with the calm tyranny of the truly competent. "There's stew. There's bread. Don't stand there dripping on my floor."

"You missed me," I said.

"I missed quiet." But her mouth did the thing it did — the almost-smile she kept in reserve for people she'd decided were worth feeding twice.

The kitchen table was laid for four, because it always was. Mrs. Peel at her end nearest the range. Ada the parlourmaid — seventeen, freckled, with a laugh like a bucket of spoons — on the left. Me on the right. And the fourth chair, at the head, unoccupied, set with a cup and saucer nobody used.

"That chair's getting ideas above itself," I said. "It's been empty so long it'll start charging rent."

"It's Mr. Quill's chair." Ada said it the way she said everything, instantly and at volume, and then went pink, because we weren't meant to talk about Mr. Quill.

Tom Quill. Typist to the organising secretary. Quiet man, ink-stained at the second knuckle of his right hand, precise in speech, who had eaten at this table eleven months and then gone and drowned himself in the Irwell in September, or so the inquest said — fallen from Victoria Bridge, taken by the current, poor soul, the water that year running high and cold. Mrs. Marchmont had wept once, briefly, elegantly, and then had all his papers boxed and sent to storage. Mrs. Peel still set his place. Nobody had ever asked her to stop. Nobody ever would.

"He was kind," Ada said, quieter now, pushing potatoes at me. "He taught me my letters' capitals. Proper ones. He said I had a good eye."

"He said that about everyone," Mrs. Peel rumbled. "Man never met a person he couldn't find something good in. Except himself, seemingly." She ladled stew with the finality of a judge passing sentence. "Eat your dinner, the pair of you. Grief keeps. Food doesn't."

So we ate. Ada told us about a gentleman caller that afternoon who'd tried to hand her a petition and gotten her mixed up with the new girl next door, and Mrs. Peel told us about the fishmonger's prices as though they were a personal betrayal, and I sat in the warm yellow light with stew in me and steam on the windows, and I thought — not for the first time, but with more fear than usual — *this could nearly be home.*

Nearly. That word again. I'd learned to keep it in a locked drawer. Kitchens like this one belonged to women whose names went on census forms, whose references could be traced, whose faces existed somewhere besides the memory of policemen. Mine lived best unwritten. Belonging was a thing other women could afford, like fur stoles and seaside holidays. I wanted it anyway, wanted it the way you want a fire in winter — unreasonably, constantly, knowing better — and so I pretended I didn't, and passed my plate for more gravy, and laughed at Ada's gentleman-caller story until my ribs hurt.

Above us, through the ceiling, I could hear Mrs. Marchmont's footsteps crossing and recrossing the study floor, patient as a clock.

* * *

After dinner came my other work, the work that had no title and no wage line and no witnesses.

Every evening Mrs. Marchmont wrote or received letters that needed delivering quietly — no postmark, no clerk's eyes — and every evening I carried them across the city in the motor's hidden panel, and every evening after the last delivery I sat somewhere alone with copies. Because here is a thing about me that Mrs. Marchmont suspected and never confirmed, and that I will tell you plainly now: I read them. All of them. I had taught myself letters off delivery manifests and newspaper hoardings and a borrowed Shakespeare with pages missing, and I read everything that passed through my hands, and I remembered it, and I told no one.

It wasn't treachery. Or rather, it was the opposite — I read the letters the way you count the exits in a strange building. Knowledge was my coat. If they ever came for me, whoever *they* might be, I intended to know precisely why, and in what order the doors would be locked.

That February night the last delivery took me to a lodging-house near Strangeways, and the return took me past the gasworks, and I stopped the motor in the black stretch between lamps — third lamp dark since November, the council never mended it, another thing history would do to some other generation — and unfolded the copy I'd made of a letter I wasn't meant to see.

It was written on cheap paper in a crabbed, crowded hand. No signature. Addressed to *the friend of friends, Cheetham Hill.* It began the way half the movement's mail began, with grievances: arrests in Newcastle, a magistrate in Leeds, the Prisoners (Temporary Discharge for Ill Health) Act doing its ugly mechanical work, women fed by tube in cells while the newspapers called it mercy.

Then, halfway down, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Angrier.

*I will say plain what others only whisper. There is one among you selling names. Since September three of our sisters have been taken within days of meetings whose lists passed through inner hands. Three, madam, and each time the arrest came from a direction only the centre could have informed. I do not accuse. I ask you to look at your own household, your own office, your own trusted circle, and ask what the Government pays for a name these days. Ask who profits. Ask who survived.*

*Burn this.*

I read it twice by the matchlight, cupping the flame against a wind that hadn't yet arrived. Then I struck a third match and burned the copy over the kerbstone, watching the paper curl and go orange and die, and I stood there in the dark between lamps with my breath coming out in little ghosts, and I told myself what I had told myself about every such letter since I'd started reading them:

Paranoia. Movements breed it. Half the membership suspects the other half on principle; informants are the movement's ghost stories, told in lodgings after midnight. Tom Quill dies and suddenly everyone sees traitors in the tealeaves. Three arrests prove nothing — Special Branch doesn't need letters, it needs coppers on corners, and coppers on corners are free.

Besides. *Your own household. Your own trusted circle.* Who would that even be? Mrs. Peel, who set a dead man's place out of love? Ada, who couldn't keep a secret longer than a bus ride? Me? The thought was so absurd it warmed me like brandy — me, Ines Thackeray, invisible as fog, informing on anyone. I knew exactly what a government did to people like my family. You don't sell names to the machine that ate your brother.

I cranked the motor, drove home through the closing fog, and put the letter away in the vault behind my eyes alongside two years of other people's secrets, and slept soundly, and would have gone on sleeping soundly still, I think, if the week had ended there.

* * *

Friday I drove Mrs. Marchmont to a committee meeting in a warehouse off Whitworth Street, and waited two hours on the cobbles with my collar up, and heard through the loading-bay doors nothing useful except applause. Saturday there was a deputation to see off at London Road. Sunday, rain, the motor under canvas, me under Mrs. Peel's roof reading last week's *Clarion* by the kitchen range while Ada practiced her capitals on the tabletop with a pencil stub, tongue out, printing *Q* over and over until the page went soft.

"That's Quill's letter," she said suddenly, tapping the page. "Look. He made his Qs like that — like a cat's paw. He said most people's Qs are just Os with pretensions."

"Ada," Mrs. Peel said mildly, from the sink.

"Well, he did." Ada's chin came up, defiant and grieving at once, seventeen years old and keeping a dead man's handwriting alive in pencil on a kitchen table. "Nobody talks about him. Nobody says his name. It's like we've all agreed he was never here, and I won't do it. He ate at this table. He taught me my letters. Somebody ought to remember him properly."

"We remember him," Mrs. Peel said. She nodded at the fourth chair, set as always with its unused cup. "We remember him every day. Some remembrance doesn't want words around it."

I said nothing. I looked at the chair, and at Ada's careful cat-paw Qs, and something moved in me that I chose not to examine — the same something the unsigned letter had poked at, the unease under the contentment, a splinter working deeper whenever you forgot it was there. Since September, three sisters arrested within days of meetings. Since September, one typist dead in the Irwell. I laid the facts side by side on the table of my mind the way Mrs. Peel laid knives, and I looked at them, and then — deliberately, the way you close a drawer on something you're not ready to count — I closed them again.

Monday changed everything, though Monday didn't know it yet. Monday began with frost on the windscreen and Mrs. Marchmont in the back seat dictating invitations to a fundraising dinner — the inner circle, the twelve who mattered, dining Friday at the house — and ended with me scraping ice off the steps at ten at night while a figure detached itself from the coal gate at the bottom of the area stairs and stood there swaying like something barely anchored to the earth.

I straightened up with the scraper in my fist, heart slamming, already composing the scream I'd need to bring Brack and the stablemen running. Fog stood thick in the street beyond him. He was gaunt as famine, coatless under a sack of some kind, lips grey, and he looked at me — actually *looked*, the way nobody had looked at me in two years, seeing past the cap and the muffler straight through to the reader underneath — and he said, in a voice scraped down to the wire:

"You're the driver who reads."

And in his frozen hand, held out to me like a summons, he carried a fold of papers — and even at that distance, even by one bad lamp, I knew the writing on the topmost page. I'd seen it a hundred times in the study, filed and boxed and carted away to storage. The Qs like a cat's paw.

A dead man's hand, held out to me in the fog, wanting to be read.
