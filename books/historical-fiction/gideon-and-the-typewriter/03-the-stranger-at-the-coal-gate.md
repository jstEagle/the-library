## 3. The Stranger at the Coal Gate

The coal gate was the one place in the Marchmont household where I could be certain of being alone, and I had learned to treasure it the way other women treasured jewelry.

It sat at the end of the kitchen yard, past the scullery door and the line of washing that Mrs. Pell hung out even in February because she said a house without white sheets on the line looked like it was hiding something. The gate itself was iron, older than the house behind it, its bars thick with paint layers the color of old bruises. Through it came the coal men every Tuesday, and through it, that grey Thursday morning, came something else entirely.

I had gone out to check the tires. That was my story and I would have sworn to it under oath: I went out to check the tires of the Hupmobile because the near-side rear had been soft all week and I didn't trust the patch young Alfie had put on it. It was even true. But truth is a funny thing about stories—the good ones always have some in them, like sand in bread.

The morning was the kind Manchester does best, which is to say no morning at all, just a slow grey seep from night into day without the sun bothering to involve itself. Smoke lay along the rooftops like something sleeping. The gas lamps down Tavistock Street were still lit at half past eight, burning pale orange holes in the fog, and I remember thinking—later I would think of it often—that the world looked exactly as it always looked, exactly the way an invisible woman likes it. Ordinary. Unwatched. Safe.

I crouched at the rear wheel with my gauge and my cap pulled low and my driving coat smelling of engine oil and the lavender sachets Mrs. Pell insisted on tucking into the pockets despite everything, and I was listening to the hiss of the tire when I heard the gate.

Not the rattle of the coal men, who arrive banging and shouting their orders like generals. Not the scrape of a neighbor's bin lid. This was quieter—a hand laid flat against the iron, and then the small wet sound of a man breathing through cloth.

I stayed crouched. That's instinct with me; I've had it since I was small enough to hide under tables during rent week. You don't stand up for sounds you can't name. You listen, and you let the world decide what it's going to show you before you agree to be part of it.

The breathing came closer along the bars. Then a voice, low and cracked, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled since Victoria's day.

"You're her. The driver."

I stood up slowly, keeping the bulk of the motorcar between us and my hand closed around the tire gauge, which would have made a poor weapon but a better one than my fist.

Through the fog I made him out in pieces. A man of forty-odd, or fifty, or thirty-five worn down to look like sixty—it was impossible to say, because hunger and cold flatten men into the same shape eventually. Tall, or had been. A coat that had once belonged to a better man, greasy at the collar, buttoned wrong. His boots were the worst of him, split at the seams and wrapped in what looked like sacking, and he stood with his weight thrown forward onto the gate as if the iron were the only thing in Manchester holding him upright.

His face was gaunt the way winter trees are gaunt—all structure, no flesh—and his eyes moved constantly, ticking from the windows of the house to the washing line to the empty street, never resting anywhere longer than a heartbeat.

"I'm the driver," I said. "Who's asking?"

"The driver who reads."

Everything in me went still. I mean truly still—not the stillness of politeness or caution but the stillness of a rabbit that has heard the hedge move. Because there were perhaps four people alive who knew I read Edith Marchmont's letters after hours, and two of them were dead, one was in Holloway, and the fourth was me.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, "and if you don't clear off, I'll fetch the police."

That was a lie, and he knew it, and I knew he knew it, and something in his ruined face almost smiled.

"No you won't," he said. "A woman who reads other people's letters by lamplight doesn't call the police. She wants to know what I've got."

* * *

I should have walked away. I want that written plainly, wherever this account ends up: I knew, within four sentences of that man's voice, that whatever he carried was going to burn down the small warm world I'd built for myself brick by careful brick, and I did not walk away. I told myself it was curiosity. I told myself it was duty. Neither was true, or both were, and the truest reason was the ugliest one—I had spent eleven years making myself invisible, and here was a man calling me out of the dark by name. By *name*. Not Ines Thackeray, either. Something worse. *The driver who reads.*

He came through the gate when I opened it, moving sideways like a crab, flinching at the clang of the latch. Up close he smelled of river. Not fish, not weed—river water in January, cold stone and mud and something faintly chemical underneath, the smell of the Irwell at the weir where the mills drain into it. My stomach understood that smell before my head did.

"Inside," I said. "Not the house. The coach house."

The Marchmont coach house had been a coach house once and was now mostly a shrine to the motorcar, with a stove in the corner I kept fed because my hands were no good to anyone frozen stiff on the gear lever. I got the stove up hot, put him on the upturned crate I used for scrubbing the running boards, and made him tea in the chipped enamel mug that lived on the shelf above the workbench. He held the mug in both hands the way children hold things, and drank nothing, just let the heat into his fingers.

"Name," I said.

"Silas Crane." He said it like a fact he'd checked against a list. "I was a friend of Tom Quill's."

"Tom Quill's dead."

"Yes," said Silas Crane. "I know. I watched them do it."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the house, muffled by two walls and a yard, Mrs. Pell was beating a rug with the steady violence she brought to everything she loved.

"Faked drowning," Silas said. "September. You'll have read the reports—they fished a hat out by Mode Wheel, called it done. He wasn't drowned. He'd been typing for the inner circle eighteen months, letters, minutes, lists. He copied everything. Every word that passed through that machine, he kept a second copy of, and he hid the copies because a man who knows too much sleeps better with insurance." He looked up at me then, and his eyes stopped moving for the first time, fixed on mine, and they were terribly tired. "Then somebody decided the insurance was worth more than the man, and they pushed him in the canal anyway. For real. Only they didn't find the copies, and neither did I, not until November, and by then I was the only one left who knew what they were."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because you're the one who reads." He set the mug down on the workbench with exaggerated care, the way drunk men do, though he hadn't touched a drop of the tea. "Because Quill said so, before he went into the water. He said if anything happened, find the Marchmont household, find the driver, the quiet one, the little dark one who reads everything and says nothing. He said—" and here Silas Crane's voice failed him, cracked clean across, and he had to start again, "he said *she sees more than any of them know, and she owes nobody.*"

The coach house was very warm suddenly, and very small.

"He wrote to you?" I said, stupidly, because the alternative understanding was arriving in my chest like ice water and I would have paid money to delay it.

Silas reached inside his coat.

What he brought out was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string, and it was flat, and thin, and heavier than any package has a right to be. He unwrapped it on the workbench under the swinging lamp, and there were papers—six, eight, ten of them, folded, creased, handled soft at the edges like prayer cards.

And I knew the handwriting on the top page before I'd read a single word.

You have to understand what that means. Tom Quill typed for the movement—typed the minutes, typed Edith's correspondence when her hands were bad, typed the circulars that went out under three different organizational names depending on which constable was watching which street. But everyone types, you'll say; a typewriter makes one handwriting out of many. Except it doesn't, not wholly. There's a slant to a man's typing, a pressure, a way he hits the shift and a way he strikes through his errors and a way his lowercase e comes out bruised when he's hurried. I had read hundreds of pages off Quill's machine. I knew his e's like I knew Mrs. Pell's footsteps on the stairs.

And these pages were full of his bruised e's.

"This one," Silas said, and turned the top sheet toward me with one finger, gently, the way you'd turn a wounded bird, "is the last thing he ever typed. Three days before they killed him. Read it. I can't anymore."

I read it.

It was addressed to no one. It began mid-thought, the way a man writes when he isn't sure he'll be allowed to finish:

*They have asked me for the third list. I gave them the first two and told myself it was strategy, that names given up were names already burned. This third list is not strategy. It is Miss V——, and Mr. H——, and the girl at the Bradford office whose name I will not write even here. If I type it, they die or they vanish, and I will have typed it. E. says the movement survives its members, not the reverse. I am beginning to understand what she means, and I wish to God I did not.*

Below that, in the same bruised type, a column of names. Fourteen of them. And beside each name, in smaller type, an address, an occupation, a note—*watched*, *detained*, *safe (for now)*—in a shorthand I recognized because I had seen it before, in a letter I'd read by lamplight in October and dismissed as paranoia, a letter I now understood I had held in my own two invisible hands.

"E.," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "E. says."

"Yes," said Silas Crane.

"Edith." Saying it aloud felt like breaking something in a church. "Edith Marchmont gives names to Special Branch."

"She calls it protecting the core," Silas said. "She gives them the fringe—the clerks, the fund-holders, the ones who won't be missed—so the leadership stays free to lead. That's how Brack frames it, anyway. Brack does the handing over; he's the broom. But the lists come off Tom's machine, and Tom's machine sits in Edith's morning room, and the hand on the keys answers to her." He gathered the pages toward himself, then stopped, and pushed them across the bench to me instead, all of them, the whole soft-worn stack. "Quill wanted them somewhere no one would think to look. Not a safe house, not a bank, not another comrade. Everyone thinks in terms of comrades. He thought in terms of servants. He said the safest place in any revolutionary household is the person nobody considers a person."

The stove roared softly. Out in the yard, Mrs. Pell's rug-beating had stopped, which meant she was probably standing at the scullery door looking for me, and in five minutes she'd come looking properly, and in ten the whole warm machinery of the household would close around this coach house like water around a dropped stone, and Silas Crane with his river-smell and his dead man's papers would have to be gone, hidden, explained away.

I looked at the pages and did not touch them.

"Tom's machine," I said. "Where is it?"

"That," said Silas, and for the first time since he'd come through the gate he looked afraid in a new way—not the fear of a hunted man, which wears smooth from practice, but the fear of a man about to ask for something he knows is too big to ask—"that's why I've come to you. It's in a left-luggage office at London Road station, under a false name, and the ticket runs out Monday. After Monday it goes to the lost property sale, and some collector buys a typewriter, and sooner or later he changes the ribbon, and everything Tom died holding onto goes out with the week's rubbish." He swallowed. "There's more. The ribbon itself matters. Every letter it ever typed is pressed into that ribbon—you can read the whole conspiracy off it, backwards, like footprints in snow. The lists. The dates. Everything."

"Monday," I said.

"Today is Thursday," said Silas Crane. "And there are three checkpoints between here and the press that will print it, and one of them is Brack's own gate."

Outside, beyond the coach-house door, the fog was lifting off Tavistock Street, and I could hear the ordinary world going about its ordinary morning—a milk cart, a train shunting somewhere toward the goods yard, Mrs. Pell's voice raised now, calling *Ines? Ines Thackeray, where have you got to?*—the voice of a household I belonged to, or had believed I belonged to, a household whose morning room held a typewriter whose ribbon remembered fourteen names.

I had driven Edith Marchmont for two years. I had read her enemies and her friends and her fears by lamplight, and I had loved her the way you love a lighthouse: from outside, usefully, at a distance.

Now the light swung round, and showed me what it had been shining on all along.

"Ines!" Mrs. Pell called again, nearer.

I folded the dead man's pages, wrapped them back in their oilcloth, and put them inside my coat, against my heart, where nobody looks.

"Come in through the coal gate after dark," I told Silas. "And God help us both."
