## 5. What the Ribbon Remembers

The typewriter sat on my bed like a coffin for a small man.

Silas had carried it up the back stairs of the lodging house in Salford with his coat thrown over it, as if a machine could blush, and now it stood there under the gaslight's yellow eye—black enamel, round keys worn to the shine of old teeth, a brass maker's plate reading *Imperial*, London. Ordinary. That was the horror of it. It looked like the typewriter in every office from here to Whitehall, the kind that had typed my wages, Edith's speeches, the minutes of meetings where I poured tea and was not asked to sit.

"You're certain this is his?" I said.

"I watched him buy it." Silas sat on the one chair with his long legs folded up like a heron's, his hands wrapped around a chipped cup of tea he hadn't touched. "September, year before last. He paid cash and haggled over the ribbon, and the shopman thought him mean, and Tom laughed about it all the way home. *Every halfpenny is a soldier,* he says." His voice caught on the last of it. He drank then, finally, because his mouth needed something to do that wasn't speaking.

I had seen Tom Quill twice in my life and heard him a hundred times—the clatter through the study door at Marchmont House, always after dark, when the inner circle's letters went out. A small quick man with ink on his cuff and a joke for the maids. In June they'd pulled a body out of the Ship Canal wearing his coat, and there had been an inquest, and Edith had wept at the memorial meeting with two hundred women watching her do it, and I had believed her because grief like that cannot be counterfeited. I had believed it the way I believed in the milk arriving and the trams running. A fact of the world.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

Silas unwound the scarf from his throat as though it were a bandage from a wound, and I understood he was deciding how much truth he could afford to show me. He'd been deciding since the coal gate, probably. Men like Silas spent their lives in ledgers of debt.

"Tom kept copies," he said. "Of everything."

"Everyone keeps copies."

"Not like this." He lifted the typewriter's lid. Underneath, where the mechanism lived, he pointed to the ribbon—a length of silk, black inked, spooled between two brass reels like a bandage for the machine's eyes. "You know what happens when a key strikes?"

"It prints."

"After it prints. The type bar comes down, punches through the ribbon into the paper. Ink goes forward onto the page. But the ribbon—" he drew a few inches of it out with two fingers, delicate as a surgeon lifting bowel, "—the ribbon takes the blow too. Every letter leaves its shape pressed into the silk. The impression fades with use, but it doesn't vanish. Not for thousands of strikes. The ribbon remembers everything it has ever been made to say."

The gas flame hissed. Somewhere below, the landlady's dog turned over in its sleep and sighed.

"So you read the ribbon," I said slowly, feeling my way across the idea the way you cross ice you don't trust. "Hold it up to a light—"

"—and every document Tom ever typed on this machine comes back. In order. Backwards, mirror-wise, ghost-grey, but legible. He worked it out himself, first year. Called it his confession factory. Said any machine that writes can also be made to remember, and no one in government or journalism or—" he paused, and I knew he was editing Edith's name out of the sentence, "—or anywhere else had ever thought to ask the ribbon what it knew."

"He was copying the inner circle's correspondence."

"He was *archiving* it. Every letter Edith sent him to type clean. Every minute. Every list." Silas set the cup down and his hands were very steady, which was worse than shaking. "Tom was a coward about some things and the opposite about others. He couldn't lie in company but he could betray anyone for a principle. He used to say the movement would eat itself someday and someone ought to keep the receipts."

"And Special Branch found out."

"Special Branch didn't need to find out. That's the thing you have to understand, Mrs. Thackeray." He had learned my married name off me somewhere along the road and deployed it now like a small courtesy, which from a man like him meant something. "Nobody discovered Tom. Tom walked into the water himself—or appeared to—and came out the other side alive, and kept working, because he thought disappearing was safety. He thought being nobody was armour." His eyes rested on me a moment too long, and I felt the words land somewhere they weren't aimed, or maybe they were exactly aimed. "He was wrong. They killed him anyway. Because it doesn't matter what a man knows if he might speak, and dead men are the only ones who never might."

* * *

We read the ribbon by lamplight with the curtains double-drawn, holding lengths of silk against the flame's glow, and I will tell you what it was like: it was like standing in a graveyard at dusk and hearing the headstones begin to talk.

Backwards letters, grey ghosts, but readable once your eye learned the trick of them. We started at the outer end of the spool, months old, impressions faded almost to rumour. A circular letter to Bradford organizers, urging calm after a window-smashing incident. A receipt for printing. A warm note to a woman named Ada in Leeds whose son had been jailed—Edith's own phrasing, unmistakable even mirrored, that cadence she used, half duchess and half scripture: *We do not abandon our wounded; we carry them, and carrying them we grow strong.*

I knew Edith's voice on paper better than most, better than her sisters perhaps, because I read her post by lamplight three nights a week and told no one. Reading her ghost-letters now felt like eavesdropping on a woman already dead.

Then we reached October.

The impressions were fresher there—deeper, darker, closer to the living end of the spool. And the documents changed character. No more warmth. No more Ada in Leeds.

A list. Fourteen names. Each with an address, an occupation, a notation beside it in Edith's abbreviated hand that Tom had faithfully reproduced in type: *watched*, *turned*, *expelled*, and—one I had to hold close to the light twice to be sure—*delivered*.

"What does delivered mean?" I asked, though I already knew. You always already know. Knowing arrives in the body before the mind consents to it, a coldness behind the breastbone, and the mind spends days catching up.

"It means the name went to Special Branch," Silas said. "It means the woman named is now known to the police as a militant, whether she ever threw a stone or not, and her life is about to become very difficult, and the movement gets a payment or a favour in exchange, and Edith calls it triage."

"Triage."

"Pruning," he said. "She'd say pruning. A garden must be pruned or it chokes itself. She believes it, Mrs. Thackeray. That's the devil of it. She doesn't sell these women the way a fence sells stolen goods. She convinces herself each sacrifice buys ten others safety, and the ledger balances, and God or History—she uses them interchangeably—will absolve her." He wound a fresh length of ribbon past the lamp, and another list swam up out of the silk. November. Twelve names. Two marked *arrested*. One marked *transported? pending*.

Transported. Pending.

My brother Joe had gone to Australia in the hold of a ship eleven years ago for stealing four shillings' worth of lead off a roof, and I had learned every trick of being unseen in the years after, and I do not apologize that my hands shook so badly Silas had to take the ribbon from me before I tore it.

* * *

We worked until nearly three. By the end I knew the ribbon the way you come to know a bad room—with your body, without wanting to.

There were lists for September, October, November, December, and February. There were memoranda to a man identified only as "R." at a Whitehall address, written in language so careful it took me three readings to understand that R. was not a correspondent but a handler. There was a bill of sale—actual money, itemized—for "information re: Lancashire federation, complete." There was a letter in which Edith, with her beautiful scriptural cadence, instructed Tom to type nothing further regarding "the February group," and to burn his notes, and thanked him for his discretion in terms so warm that I had to put the ribbon down and go stand at the window with the frost coming through the gap in the frame onto my knuckles.

And there was one more thing. Near the living end, struck just days before Tom died.

A single line, typed twice, the second time shakier:

*I have copied everything and hidden it where only I can find it. If anything happens to me it will all come out. T.Q.*

"He sent that to Edith?" I said.

"To R., copy to Edith. His insurance policy. His last bluff." Silas laid the ribbon down flat on the table, both hands bracketing it like a man framing a picture of the dead. "Three weeks later he was in the canal."

The vertigo came then, the real kind, the kind I'd only had once before, on a high gallery at the Free Trade Hall looking straight down into the crowd. The room seemed to tilt. Because I understood the size of it all at once, the true scale, and scale is the thing that breaks people—not cruelty, cruelty you can hate, but *scale*. This wasn't one treacherous letter. It was a system. Months of it. Dozens of women fed to the police a few at a time, each small enough a betrayal that no single one would ever look like a pattern unless you stood where I was standing now, holding a dead man's ribbon up to a lamplight in a Salford lodging house at three in the morning.

And it wasn't only the names already given. It was the machinery still turning. The lists would continue. There would be a March list. There would be women in Manchester this week, laughing at meetings, sewing banners, trusting Edith Marchmont with their liberty and their bodies and the names of their friends, who were already written down somewhere in a fine hand, waiting for their notation. *Watched.* *Turned.* *Delivered.*

One of those names could be mine. I had driven Edith to eleven federations in three counties. I knew the routes, the safe houses, the couriers. If Brack or whoever ran this kept records of drivers—

"Stop," I said aloud.

"Mrs. Thackeray?"

"You're doing it. Thinking like the lists think." I turned from the window. My breath was visible. "Who else has seen this ribbon?"

"No one living."

"And who else knows Tom kept copies?"

"Edith suspects. R. certainly suspects—that's why Tom's dead. They didn't kill him for what he knew, they killed him for what he might have *kept*." Silas rose, and up close he smelled of coal smoke and river and fear worn down to a fine grit. "Which means somewhere in that machine or on that ribbon or in whatever hiding place Tom boasted of, they believe, there exists enough evidence to hang the whole arrangement out in daylight. They'll be looking for it. Quietly, but they'll be looking. Brack has already been through Tom's lodgings twice."

Brack. Even the name had a texture, like wet rope. Edith's man, broad and soft-spoken, who checked the gates at congress and stood too near the stairs. Who had once, at a garden party in Chorlton, watched me for a full minute while I polished the windscreen and said, *You're careful, missus. Careful drivers live longest,* and smiled, and I had laughed and agreed because invisibility is the only safety a working-class woman can afford, and I had gone home that night and lain awake for two hours.

"The ribbon isn't proof," I said, because someone had to say the hard thing and it was evidently going to be me. "Impressions fade. A clever barrister would call it hearsay from a piece of silk. What turns this from suspicion into evidence?"

Silas looked at me with something that was almost respect, or almost pity, hard to say which at that hour.

"Nothing," he said. "Not yet. A ribbon alone proves a typewriter typed things. To prove *who* typed them and *where they went*—you'd need the machine and the ribbon together, matched, plus the originals if they exist. Plus testimony." He hesitated, and I watched the decision move through him like weather. "Plus someone willing to stand in a courtroom and be a person. A named person. Names are the whole currency of this business, Mrs. Thackeray. Lists of names bought these women's ruin, and only a name given openly, in public, under oath, buys them back."

He let that sit. Outside, far off toward the river, the ice boomed—a great deep cracking groan, the sound the Irwell makes in a warm February when the thaw starts early, like the earth clearing its throat before it speaks.

Neither of us mentioned it. Neither of us had to.

I looked at the typewriter on my bed, at the ribbon lying in its loose coils among the shadows like something shed, and I understood that I was already decided. Had been decided since the coal gate, probably, since the moment a frozen stranger said *the driver who reads* and my secret, my safety, my eleven years of careful practiced nothing, cracked open like the river was cracking outside. There was no route back to unseen. There was only forward, carrying this weight, and the question was not whether but how, and past whom, and alive.

"There are checkpoints," I said quietly. "Between here and anywhere this can be printed and protected. Brack watches the northern roads himself when he's nervous, and he'll be nervous now."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I've driven every road out of this city for two years," I said, "and nobody notices a driver, which means I've had time to notice everything else."

Silas began, very slowly, to smile—the first time I'd seen his face do anything but grieve—and I turned down the lamp, because whatever came next was going to need the dark.
