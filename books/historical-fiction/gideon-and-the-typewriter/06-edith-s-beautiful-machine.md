## 6. Edith's Beautiful Machine

Edith Marchmont had learned long ago that a room could be read the way Ines read a road—by feel, by pressure, by the small tremors that came before something gave way. Tonight the drawing room on Cavendish Row was telling her that the movement was tired. She could see it in the shoulders of the women arranged before her fire: Mrs. Pellowe with her chapped hands folded like an apology; the Misses Hargreave, twin and identical in their exhaustion; young Winifred Cotter, who still burned, God bless her, but whose burning had begun to gutter at the edges. Even the fire itself seemed to be listening rather than speaking, popping low and apologetic in the grate.

She stood before them and did not sit, because sitting was surrender, and she said, "They have taken Mrs. Pankhurst again," and let the word do its work. It always did. *Taken.* The word had weight the way a coffin has weight—not elegant, but honest.

"We are told to be patient," she went on, moving slowly along the hearth rug so that every woman had to turn her head to follow, which was a trick she'd learned from watching preachers and pickpockets alike. "We are told that the Liberal government is our friend, that Mr. Asquith has our interests at heart, that if we would only be quiet and reasonable and feminine—" here she smiled, and the smile was a blade wrapped in velvet, "—then in some golden by-and-by, the vote will be handed to us like change from a shopkeeper's palm."

Someone laughed. Good. Laughter was fuel.

"I have been quiet," Edith said. "I was quiet for eleven years. I wrote letters. I signed petitions. I wore out my eyes copying addresses by lamplight while my husband—God rest his tedious bones—told me that politics was bad for my digestion." She paused at the mantel, where Frederick's photograph kept its silver stare, and permitted herself the small private pleasure of having outlived him into usefulness. "Eleven years of quiet bought us nothing but the privilege of watching men decide, decade upon decade, whether we were persons. And we are meant to be grateful for the deciding."

The room had come alive now. She felt it happen, felt the current pass through the assembled women the way it always did, and she thought—as she thought every time, with a satisfaction that was nearly physical—*this is the machine, this is my beautiful machine, and I am the hand upon the crank.*

"Next month there will be a deputation. A lawful one, a polite one, all gloves and hatpins and good manners, because we must always first be polite—it makes what follows unanswerable. And when they refuse us, as they will refuse us, then we escalate. I will not tell you tonight what escalation looks like. I will only tell you that it will require courage, and money, and silence, and that any one of you who cannot give all three should stand up now and go home to your tea, and no one in this room will think the less of you."

No one stood. No one ever stood. That was the terrible, intoxicating arithmetic of it: say the word *coward* without saying it, and watch them stay.

She dismissed them gently, warmly, pressing hands and murmuring names, because a leader who forgot names was a general who forgot maps. Mrs. Pellowe, whose son drove a tram. The Hargreaves, who ran the printing in Salford. Winifred, whom she held an extra moment. "You're thinning, dear," she said. "Eat. We need you strong for spring."

When the last of them had gone out into the Manchester drizzle, Edith remained standing at the window, watching their umbrellas bloom and scatter down the wet street. Behind her the fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs the clock in the hall struck nine, and beneath the strike she heard another sound—the motor car turning in at the gates, headlights sweeping the laurel, Ines bringing the motor round from the garage after the evening run to the stationer's in Deansgate.

Ah. Ines.

* * *

Edith found her in the motor court, standing beside the Humber with a rag in one hand, wiping mud from the running board though it hardly needed it. That was Ines exactly—never idle, never ostentatious, finding small work to do with her hands the way other people found it with their words. Twenty-two years old, maybe twenty-three; she'd never given an age and Edith had never asked. Cap pulled low, collar up, grey eyes that took everything in and reported nothing.

"Ines. Come in out of the wet."

"Yes, ma'am." She fell into step, half a pace behind as always, and Edith suppressed the flicker of irritation that half-pace always gave her. She had told the girl a dozen times to walk beside her, and the girl obeyed in everything else—in routes, in schedules, in the lettering of the delivery ledger—but never in that. Some people's deference was a wall built brick by brick, and you did not breach it by ordering it down.

"The roads?"

"Bad past Strangeways. Water standing in the dips. I took Great Ducie instead."

"You always know the alternatives."

"It's the job, ma'am."

In the hall, Edith stopped beneath the gas bracket and looked at her properly, and had the odd sensation—one she could not remember feeling before—of wanting to look at her properly. The girl had been with them fourteen months. She had driven Edith through police lines and hecklers' crowds and one memorable night in Bury when a brick had come through the windscreen and Ines had not so much as lifted her foot from the accelerator. She read letters flawlessly and never asked about them. She was invisible, and Edith had hired her for precisely that quality, and had somehow, over fourteen months, ceased to notice how strange a thing it was to employ a person whose whole talent was not being seen.

"Do you know," Edith said, "that I cannot recall the last time I thanked you?"

"There's nothing to thank, ma'am."

"There is everything to thank. There is the fact that I am never late. There is the fact that my papers arrive where they must, when they must, and no constable between here and Rochdale has ever opened a satchel of ours. There is the fact that when Mrs. Pankhurst's sister needed driving to the station at four in the morning, in the rain, with two plainclothesmen watching the house—you did it, and I slept through it, and I have never even asked whether you were frightened."

Ines's face did something very small. Most people would have missed it. Edith had spent thirty years reading rooms, and so she caught it: not pleasure, exactly. Something more guarded than pleasure. Surprise, perhaps, or the wariness of a creature that had learned to distrust sugar.

"I wasn't frightened," Ines said.

"You are either lying or you are made of iron, and I have watched you too closely to believe the second."

That won her the ghost of a smile—the most Ines ever surrendered—and Edith felt the warmth of it move through her like a swallow of brandy. She thought: *I have a hundred women like her. I have hundreds.* Girls who typed and copied and carried and drove and were never photographed and never quoted and never thanked, and it was on their backs, on the Ineses of the world, that she and Mrs. Pankhurst and the famous names rode toward history. She knew this. She was honest enough, alone, at night, to know it completely.

And she was honest enough to know something else, which was that knowing changed nothing. That was the bargain. That was the price of the machine. You could not pull a nation's chain with soft hands, and every cog that ground was ground by another cog, and somewhere at the bottom of the gearing there was always oil, and the oil was always people.

"Come to the study a moment," she heard herself say. "There's a letter going to Liverpool tonight. I want it driven, not posted."

* * *

The study smelled of ink and coal smoke and the lavender sachets her maid hid against the damp. Edith sat at the desk and wrote quickly—a name, an address, a time, instructions phrased in the oblique shorthand the inner circle used—while Ines waited by the door with her cap in her hands, hair dark and flattened by rain.

Three names in the middle of the page. Edith wrote them without hesitation, because she had stopped hesitating over them months ago, and because hesitation, she had decided, was a luxury purchased by women who had never watched a friend force-fed through a tube.

That was the reasoning she returned to, always, in the small hours when the reasoning needed returning to. She had seen what the government did. She had stood in the visitors' gallery at Holloway and listened through the door. She had held Charlotte Marsh's mother in this very room while the woman wept. Against that, what was a list? What were three names, passed quietly to a man at the Home Office who had promised—had sworn—that they would be used only for surveillance, only to prevent the reckless from getting the careful killed? Because that was the argument that had persuaded her, in Frederick's study, two winters ago, with Brack sitting solid and reasonable across the desk: that the movement bled at its edges, that agents provocateurs were already inside it, that every careless new recruit was a fuse waiting for a match, and that a controlled channel—a discreet pruning—was mercy dressed in hard clothing.

They had promised discretion. They had promised no arrests. Two of the three names on the page in front of her had already been arrested.

But not charged, she reminded herself. Not transported. Released within the week, warned, terrified, alive. Alive, Edith thought, pressing her signature down harder than the pen required. *Alive.* The alternative—the reckless alternative, the romantic alternative—was Winifred Cotter in a cell being fed through a tube, and Edith had chosen, and choosing was the whole of leadership, and she would carry it, she alone, because she had made herself the kind of woman who could carry it.

She sealed the envelope. Wax, because wax could not be steamed open by a curious postmistress.

"Liverpool," she said. "The Adelphi, ask for Mr. Rennick at the porter's lodge, give him this, wait for nothing, drive back tonight or take a room and return at dawn, whichever is safer. You'll decide better than I can from here."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Ines—" She rose, came around the desk, and did the thing she had been circling toward since the motor court. She took the girl's cold hands in both of hers. "I don't say this lightly, and I don't say it often, because flattery corrupts and I've seen what it does to young women in this movement. But you should know that I trust you beyond anyone in my household. Beyond Brack. Beyond my own secretary. You have never once given me cause to doubt you, and in these times"—she squeezed the cold fingers, willing warmth into them—"in these times, loyalty like yours is worth more than a hundred speeches. When this is won—I believe it will be won, whatever they do to me first—I intend that women like you will be remembered. Not the ones who make the newspapers. The ones who make the newspapers possible."

For a moment Ines looked at her, and Edith had the vertiginous sense of looking down a well she had never noticed in her own hallway—something deep and still and utterly unreadable at the bottom of the girl's grey eyes.

"Thank you, ma'am," Ines said. "I'll take the Liverpool road now, before the fog settles."

She went out. Edith heard the front door, the motor court gravel, the engine turning over and gathering itself, the tires hissing away into the dark toward Liverpool, toward Rennick, toward the envelope in the satchel with three names in it.

She stood a long while by the window with her hands clasped at her waist.

*It's necessary,* she told herself, in the voice she used in the small hours, the voice that had answered every objection for two years now and never once lost the argument, chiefly because it was the only voice allowed in the room. *Brack's arrangement is ugly, but ugliness is not the measure. Outcome is the measure. History will not ask whether our hands were clean. History will ask whether we won.*

And underneath the voice, fainter, in a register she did not often permit: *the girl's hands were cold.*

She sat down at the desk again and took up the next letter, and the pen scratched, and the machine ran on.

* * *

It was past midnight when the sound came back up the drive—earlier than a Liverpool run could possibly allow, even at speed—and Edith, still at her desk with cold feet and a third cup of tea gone lukewarm, felt the small alarm of it climb her spine before her mind supplied any reason. Drivers did not come back early unless something had happened. Tyres, weather, police—

She was at the window when the car rolled to a stop. Headlights off. Engine off. And then the motor sat there, silent, unmoving, and nobody got out of it.

Edith watched for a full minute, teacup forgotten in her hand.

Then the driver's door opened, and Ines stepped down into the lamplight—but she was not alone. There was a figure on the far side, tall and gaunt, moving like a man whose joints had been broken and badly reset, and Ines glanced up at the window—directly, deliberately, at the window where Edith stood—before leaning in to speak to him, low and urgent, her hand resting on the satchel strap as though the satchel mattered more than anything else in the street.

Edith set down the cup.

Fourteen months of perfect invisibility, of perfect loyalty, praised to her face not six hours ago—and here was Ines Thackeray, at midnight, smuggling a stranger through Edith's own gates and looking up at the study window while she did it.

From the drawer of her desk, without letting herself think about why, Edith took the little bell that rang for Brack.
