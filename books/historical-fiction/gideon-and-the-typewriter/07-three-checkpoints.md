## 7. Three Checkpoints

Silas drew the map on the back of a laundry list, because a man carrying a map of his own escape route is a man asking to be searched. He flattened it against the floorboards of my attic room with two fingers, and I knelt beside him with the lamp turned so low that the flame was more suggestion than light. The paper was cheap and grey, ruled for someone else's purposes—collars, cuffs, shirts by the dozen—and across it he inked our future in pencil strokes so faint I had to lean close enough to smell him: coal smoke, wet wool, and under all of it the sourness of a man who had been sleeping rough since the river gave up a body that wasn't Tom Quill.

"Manchester to Preston," he said, tapping the first line of road. "That's forty miles before anything happens."

"Forty miles before *anything* happens," I repeated. "Not nothing. You've never driven north in February."

"I've never done this at all." He said it without shame, which I was beginning to understand was his one honest virtue. Silas could lie about everything except his own fear. It sat on him like weather. "I'm told you're the best driver in the household."

"Told by whom?"

"Told by everyone who watches you leave and come back and asks no questions about either." He looked at me then, gaunt face half-lit, eyes with that particular shine of a man who has decided to trust someone out of desperation rather than judgment. "You drive for Edith Marchmont four days out of seven. You've carried documents she'd deny under oath. And you have never once been stopped."

"Never once been *searched*," I corrected. "Stopped is different. A woman alone in an automobile gets stopped everywhere. They stop me to look at me. To wonder what sort of woman has learned to work an engine. Then they get bored of wondering and wave me on." I took the pencil from him. My hand was steadier than I expected it to be, and that steadiness frightened me more than the plan itself did. "Being waved through is not the same as being invisible, Mr. Crane. It's being *dismissed*. It works until the day they decide not to be bored."

He flinched at his own false name. We had given it to him two nights ago over cold mutton in Mrs. Pellowe's kitchen, after midnight, when he'd finished telling us what was in the ribbon and Mrs. Pellowe had gone white as her apron and said, in the voice of a woman rearranging her entire understanding of the house she'd kept for eleven years, *Then we are already dead men, and best start acting like ghosts.* Silas Crane. It suited him the way an ill-fitting coat suits a scarecrow. But names were currency now and we were all counterfeiting.

"Show me again," I said. "From the top."

* * *

The route north ran like this.

Out of Manchester before dawn, taking the Bury road rather than the Bolton because the Bolton had been widened for the tram lines and widened roads invited constables. Through Bury while the mill hands were still streaming to their shifts—a hundred thousand grey figures who would remember nothing, because nobody remembers a motorcar passing at six in the morning. Then the long climb east of Rawtenstall, where the moors opened out and a lone car could be seen for two miles in any direction, which meant speed there and no stopping, and if the engine failed on those heights we would freeze before anyone found us.

The first checkpoint stood at the bridge over the Calder, below Hebden Bridge. Not a permanent post—a temporary thing, sawhorses and a hut and two constables, thrown up three weeks ago along with its twin farther north, when Special Branch finally admitted to itself that Tom Quill's drowning had been too convenient and started looking for whatever he might have left behind.

"They're not looking for you yet," Silas said. "They're fishing. Every vehicle stopped, every driver questioned, nothing written down unless something smells wrong. Sergeant named Hollis runs it. I met him."

"You *met* him."

"Before." His jaw worked. "Before the river. They brought me in on suspicion of forgery—this was two years ago, when Quill first started keeping his copies. Hollis asked the questions. He has a way of—" Silas stopped, pressed his lips together, started again. "He makes you want to confess things you haven't done. Just to have it over. Eight hours in a room with Sergeant Hollis and you'd sign your mother's name to a confession if he asked it plainly enough."

I wrote HOLLIS — CALDER BRIDGE on the laundry list and underlined it twice.

Past Hebden Bridge the road climbed again into the Dales, and here Silas drew the second mark, near a village whose name he wrote small: Grassington way, where the road narrowed between stone walls and a second temporary post sat at the junction. This one, he said, was manned from Leeds, and Leeds men were different—they were younger, keener, hungry for promotion, the kind who patted down sacks of flour to be seen doing diligence.

"And between them?" I asked. "Nothing? Forty miles of moors with two fishing nets strung across the roads and open water between?"

"Not nothing." He drew a third mark, and his pencil hesitated over it so long I thought the lead had broken. When he lifted it, the mark looked no different from the others, but he had pressed hard enough to score the table beneath. "Skipton. The wool merchant's yard off the Keighley road. That's the third one."

"That's not a police post."

"No," Silas said. "It isn't."

And we both looked at the mark, because we both knew whose yard it was. There is a geography to fear, and every woman in Edith Marchmont's orbit carried the same map in her chest, and on that map Skipton was marked with one name.

Mr. Brack.

* * *

I had seen Brack perhaps thirty times in fourteen months and spoken to him four times, and each conversation had felt like being weighed on a set of scales I couldn't read the numbers of. He was not large. That was the first thing strangers got wrong about him—the papers liked their villains bull-necked, and Brack was instead a dry, narrow man, grey at the temples, neat in a dark suit that must have had a dozen identical brothers hanging in his wardrobe. He did the movement's arithmetic. That was how Edith described him, once, to a room of donors who laughed appreciatively: *Mr. Brack does our arithmetic.* What she meant, and what they heard, and what I knew, were three different sentences.

Brack found things out. When a shop steward in Salford turned informer, Brack had known within a day. When a printer in Bradford got cold feet about a pamphlet, Brack arrived at his shop before the man had finished composing his letter of withdrawal. He knew which members owed money and to whom. He knew that Mrs. Pellowe's sister drank. He had once told me, apropos of nothing, standing beside my motorcar while I checked the water level, that my brother had been transported on a bad witness and bad law both, and that Edith's movement intended to build courts worthy of the name, and that he hoped—I remembered the exact phrasing, because the phrasing was the whole man—that I hoped, Miss Thackeray, that you will bring the same discretion to your work for us that such painful experience teaches.

It had sounded like kindness. It had taken me a full day to hear it correctly: *I know about your brother. I know everything about everyone. Bring me your discretion.*

"He keeps the ledgers," Silas said now. "The membership rolls, the courier schedules, the safe houses. If Quill typed it for the inner circle, Brack filed a copy of it somewhere we'll never find, and if Brack learns pages are missing—if he learns a dead man's typewriter has left the city—he will not wait for the police. He'll handle it himself. Quietly. For the good of the cause." Silas said the last words flatly, without mockery, and somehow that was worse. "Two police checkpoints, Miss Thackray, are a problem. The third checkpoint is a catastrophe."

* * *

We worked until the candle guttered. That is the honest shape of that night—not drama, but arithmetic of our own. Silas talked; I converted talk into miles and minutes and fuel. The motorcar was Edith's Darracq, the strong one, and she had ordered it made ready for a journey to Harrogate on the twenty-sixth—a real journey, a real errand, a meeting with a Liberal MP's wife who fancied herself sympathetic, which meant the Darracq could travel north with legitimate papers in my name and no lie required at the first two stops beyond the ones the police invented themselves.

"The ribbon travels in the tire repair kit," I said. "Under the patches and the rubber solution. No sergeant alive searches a tire kit unless he's already searching everything, and if he's searching everything we're finished anyway, so the hiding place hardly matters." I said it coolly and was proud of how coolly I said it, and only afterward understood that this was what planning does to you—it teaches you to speak your own death as a contingency and call it logistics.

"And the typewriter?"

"The typewriter stays here."

Silas's head came up sharply. "The ribbon alone proves—"

"The ribbon alone proves everything, and the typewriter alone proves nothing they can hang quickly. Listen." I took the pencil and drew the machine itself, crude and boxy, then crossed it out. "A ribbon is cloth. A ribbon can be found in a hedge, dropped from a passing motorcar, lost by a careless laundress, and no one is hanged for possessing one. But a typewriter with Quill's serial number, in our possession, on the Harrogate road?" I shook my head. "If we're stopped and truly searched, I am a driver running an errand for her employer and you are my mechanic's man, and there is nothing in that motorcar connecting either of us to a drowned man. The moment the typewriter enters it, we are conspirators carrying relics. The ribbon is evidence, Mr. Crane. The typewriter is a confession with keys."

He stared at the crossed-out box a long time. Then, quietly: "Quill loved that machine. Carried it through two drownings' worth of trouble."

"Quill is past caring where it sleeps."

That was cruel, and I heard it land, and I did not take it back. Something was happening to me that night which I did not yet have words for, though I have since found them: the softness was going out of me and purpose was going in, and purpose is harder stuff. It fills the spaces where fear lived and it does not apologize for the displacement.

* * *

Near three in the morning we came at last to the part neither of us wanted to say aloud, and so I said it.

"If Brack stops us at Skipton, arguing won't save us. Running won't save us. The yard has one road in and the same road out, and his men will be at both ends of it." I laid the pencil down. "There's only one thing that saves us at the third checkpoint, and it's that Brack doesn't know what we carry. So the question—the only question—is whether he finds out before the ferry or after."

"And if before?"

"Then I give him a reason to let us cross anyway."

Silas laughed, one dry syllable with no humor in it. "You'll out-argue Brack."

"I don't intend to argue at all. Arguing is for people with something to prove." I folded the laundry list along its creases, collars and cuffs and shirts by the dozen, and beneath them a road north and three gates and my name, unrecorded, on none of them. "I intend to tell him the truth. All of it. What Edith sold, and who paid, and where Quill's body is actually buried, and what happens to her beautiful machine—to all of them—when the newspapers print it in letters an inch high. Because Brack believes he's protecting the movement, and the truth is the one weapon he has no defense against, because he's never once had to use it himself."

The candle died. In the dark, Silas was quiet for so long I thought he'd fallen asleep sitting up.

"Hollis at the Calder," he said finally, to the blackness. "Leeds men at the junction. And Brack at the ferry."

"Yes."

"You've never been searched."

"No."

"Do you truly believe you can pass all three?"

I lay back on my pallet and listened to the frost creaking in the rafters, and thought about the river north of Skipton, and how Mrs. Pellowe had mentioned—only that evening, only in passing, setting down a dish of potatoes—that the thaw had come early this year, dreadfully early, that the ice on the Wharfe was already black at the edges and the ferryman grumbling about the season ending before it should.

Three checkpoints, then. And behind the third of them, a river preparing to close its single road forever.

"We'll find out Thursday," I said.
