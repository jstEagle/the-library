## 8. The Ice on the River

The thaw came early that year, and it came like a thief.

I noticed it first in the sound of the gutters. For weeks Manchester had rung with frost—the cobbles crackling under the wheels, the water troughs lidded with grey glass, my breath hanging behind me as I drove like a scarf I couldn't take off. Then, on a Tuesday in late February, I woke to dripping. Not the polite drip of a tap but a whole chorus of it, eaves and drainpipes and window-ledges all talking at once, and when I went down to fetch the car from the mews the yard was soft underfoot, mud where the ice had been, and the sky had the colour of wet slate rather than wet iron.

Mrs. Pilling, the cook, stood at the back door with her arms folded, watching the snow slide off the coal shed roof in slabs.

"Early," she said, in the tone other women used for deaths in the family. "My mother always said an early thaw means a hard year. River'll go all at once, you mark me. No manners about it."

I didn't mark her, not then. I was thinking about the carburettor and whether the cold snaps were finished, because a car that wouldn't start was a worse enemy than any sergeant. But her words followed me into the day like a dog that had decided I belonged to it.

*The river'll go all at once.*

* * *

Silas came to the mews at dusk, three days after the coal-gate night, moving along the wall the way he did now, as though his body had learned that walls were safer than open ground. He'd been sleeping—I still don't know where—a room above a chandler's on the Salford side, he let slip once, paying by the week and keeping his boots on. He looked like a man who slept in his boots. He looked like a man who might have to run in them.

He carried the typewriter wrapped in sacking, strapped across his back like a soldier's pack, and he never set it down if he could help it. When he did—lowering it to the straw between us while we talked, both of us crouched in the shadow of the Daimler as though the machine were a sick animal we were nursing—he kept one hand on it the whole time.

"The ferry," he said. "Tell me about the ferry."

It wasn't really a ferry. That was the first thing I told him. It was a flat-bottomed barge on a chain, worked by a crank, that hauled itself across the Irwell below Broughton where the road gave out, carrying carts and cattle and the occasional motorcar when the driver could be persuaded to risk it. In summer it ran from six in the morning till dark. In winter it ran when the ice allowed, which most winters meant always, because the Irwell this far inland only froze hard enough to stop it once or twice in a decade, and even then the ferryman—old Hargreaves, who had held the crank through two coronations and one divorce scandal—would break a channel with an iron bar and keep working, swearing, until the ice beat him.

"But this year," Silas said.

"This year the thaw's early." I heard Mrs. Pilling's voice come out of my mouth. "If the river goes—if the ice breaks up properly—Hargreaves can't work the crossing. The floes would stove the barge in. The route closes."

"For how long?"

"A fortnight. Three weeks. However long the river takes to settle."

Silas was quiet for a moment. In the half-dark of the mews his face was all hollows, cheekbones and eye-sockets, a face like something carved out of weather. "And the northern route," he said slowly, "the one past Brack's checkpoint—it crosses there."

"It crosses there."

"Then if the ice breaks before we move—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. We both sat looking at the sacking-wrapped shape between us, at the dead man's typewriter with its dead man's ribbon coiled inside it like a black tongue, and understood the same thing: that the evidence had a season. That truth, like everything else in this country, was subject to weather.

"They'd wait it out," I said. "Brack would wait. Edith would wait. They'd let the river close the road and then they'd have all the time in the world to find us, and by the time the ferry ran again the ribbon would be so much ash in some grate, and Quill would be a drowned man twice over."

"Yes," Silas said. "That's exactly what they'd do."

* * *

I want to be honest about what those days were like, because later—in the papers, in the pamphlets, in the version of events that got printed and bound and put on shelves—everything acquired a kind of inevitability, as though I had marched from the coal gate to the press room in a straight line with my chin up. I didn't. I lay awake at night doing arithmetic with fear.

Because here is what nobody tells you about a deadline: it doesn't feel like urgency. It feels like subtraction. Every morning I woke and counted what was left. Four days of ice, maybe five. Minus the time to get the ribbon out of the house without Brack's ledgers catching up with me—and Brack had begun checking drivers' ledgers, I knew that much, had been seen in the coach-house turning pages with his thick deliberate fingers, hunting for a journey that shouldn't exist. Minus the distance north. Minus the checkpoints. Minus the ordinary catastrophic accidents of any journey: a lame horse ahead of you, a burst tyre, a policeman in a bad mood.

What remained after all that subtraction was a number so small it made my hands go cold on the steering wheel, and I drove Miss Marchmont to her meetings with that coldness in my fingers and smiled at her through the windscreen gap and said yes, miss, no, miss, the roads are dreadful, miss, and all the while part of my mind was standing on the bank of the Irwell watching the ice rot.

Because that is what it does. It doesn't break dramatically, not at first. It rots. It goes grey and honeycombed, it drinks the warmer water underneath until it's more water than ice, and then one night the river simply shrugs and the whole surface lifts and groans and starts to travel downstream in plates the size of doors. Old Hargreaves told me about it once, years ago, when I'd driven a party of Edith's ladies out to look at the crossing because someone had decided the ferry was picturesque. He'd stood with his hands on the crank and described the breakup the way other men describe battles.

"You'll hear it a mile off," he said. "Like furniture being moved in a very large house. And then it comes down, all of it together, and anything on the water that day wishes it weren't."

I had laughed then. I thought of it now every time I crossed a bridge.

* * *

On the Thursday I drove Edith to a committee meeting in Cheetham Hill and waited outside in the falling damp, and while I waited I did something I had never done before: I read the river.

Not literally—I couldn't see it from Cheetham Hill—but I read its signs everywhere else. The snow retreating up the hillsides like a tide going out. The canal at the bottom of the hill, usually scummed with ice, running black and restless. A cart horse drinking greedily from a trough that hadn't needed breaking that morning. The whole city thawing around me, loosening, and I sat in the Daimler with my hands around a flask of tea that had gone lukewarm and felt the deadline pressing against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

Five days. Perhaps four. Perhaps tomorrow, if the rain came.

And the rain wanted to come. You could smell it in the air, that green wet smell of February deciding to become March.

When Edith came out, flushed with the meeting, glowing the way she glowed when she'd been praised or fought—she made very little distinction between the two—she paused at the car door and looked at me with those clear grey eyes that had made a hundred women believe impossible things.

"You're quiet today, Ines."

"I'm always quiet, miss."

"No." She got in, arranging her skirts, and said it lightly, almost fondly, which was somehow worse: "You're watchful today. There's a difference. A good chauffeur is watchful." She settled herself. "Home, please. And take the long way round by the park—I want to see whether the crocuses are up."

So I took the long way round, past the park where the crocuses were indeed coming up, purple and yellow spears through the black mud, the first flowers pushing through exactly as the last of the winter died, and Edith said, "Look at that. Look at that, Ines. Everything moves whether we're ready or not," and I agreed with her, and neither of us knew how precisely she had spoken.

* * *

That evening I went to find Silas, and I found him in a state I hadn't seen before, and it frightened me more than the ice did.

He was at our meeting place—an archway by the old goods station, dry-ish, dark, smelling of creosote and horses—and he had the typewriter unpacked, the sacking thrown back, and he was sitting looking at it in the light of a candle-end stuck to a brick, and his hands were shaking.

"We're going too slow," he said, before I'd even spoken. "You keep saying soon. Soon. Every time I ask, it's soon, it's nearly arranged, it's a few more days. There are no few more days. Do you understand what's in this thing?" He put his palm flat on the machine's lid, and I saw that his palm was sweating. "Every name. Every name they sold. I watched him type them, Ines. I watched Tom copy out the lists in his neat little hand, and I thought he was mad, keeping copies, making carbon after carbon, hiding the ribbon when it wore out instead of binning it. I thought, what a strange careful man. And then they drowned him for it, and I understood: he wasn't being careful for himself. He was building a case. He was going to be the witness at his own murder trial, only he never got the chance."

"Silas—"

"And now I'm the witness, and I'm hiding in an archway eating bread a laundress gives me, and the river is going to close, and you keep saying *soon*." His voice cracked on the word. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I haven't slept properly since the coal gate. Every noise, every footstep—I keep hearing them coming for me the way they came for him. I keep thinking about the water. He went into the canal, you know. Not the river. The canal. Slow water. They say drowning in still water is—"

"Stop." I said it more sharply than I meant to, and he stopped, and in the silence I knelt down beside the typewriter and put my own hand next to his on the lid. "Listen to me. Tomorrow I'll know the route for certain. Every mile of it, every checkpoint, every place a wheel might fail. The day after, we go. Not soon. The day after tomorrow, whatever the weather's doing, whatever Brack's doing. If the ferry's still running, we cross it. If it isn't—" I swallowed. "If it isn't, then we've lost, and we may as well know it honestly."

He looked at me for a long moment. Candlelight moved in his eyes.

"Why do you care?" he asked suddenly. "Truly. You had a good place. Warm kitchen, steady wage, a lady who trusts you. Why throw it into the river for a dead man's ribbon?"

I thought about telling him it was justice. I thought about telling him it was for Tom Quill, whom I'd never met, or for the women whose names were on those lists, or for my brother somewhere on the far side of the world picking oakum with his transported hands. All true. None of it the whole truth.

"Because I've spent my whole life being invisible," I said, "and I told myself it was safety. And now I know it's just a slower way of drowning."

* * *

I walked home the long way, over the bridge, because I wanted to see it.

The Irwell ran high and brown beneath me, swollen with melted snow off the moors, carrying sticks and straw and one entire hurdle fence that some farm upstream had lost. Along the edges, where the current slackened, the old ice still clung—grey, rotten, riddled with holes like stale bread—and the river was eating it bite by bite. As I watched, a slab the size of a table tore loose, turned over once, showing its glassy white belly, and slid away south under the bridge arches, gone.

A man beside me at the parapet, a labourer with his collar up, spat philosophically over the edge.

"She'll go proper tonight or tomorrow," he said, to me or to the river. "Hear her grumbling? Like an old woman being woken. Hargreaves'll be done. No more crossing till she settles."

"How long?" I asked. "If she goes tonight. How long till the ferry runs again?"

He shrugged. "Fortnight, if the rains hold off. Three if they don't." He tipped his cap and trudged away into the dark, leaving me alone with the sound of the river working at its chains.

I stood there until my feet went numb, and I made myself look at the water, really look at it, because I had decided something on that bridge and I wanted to look at the thing I'd decided against. Below me the last of the winter was travelling south in pieces, unstoppable, indifferent, taking the ferry route with it, and beyond the ferry route the road north, and beyond the road north the printing press that was our only destination and our only hope, and somewhere behind me in the warm lit streets of the city, Brack was turning ledger pages with his thick fingers, patient as winter, certain that the river would do his work for him.

Three days, I thought. Give me three days and I'll have it past him.

The river answered with a long grinding groan, deep under the arches, like furniture being moved in a very large house.

I turned for home, and I began to count.
