## 9. A Dinner Among Traitors

The invitation came down the servants' stairs the way all Edith Marchmont's invitations did: as an assumption.

"Mrs. Pym says you're to serve tonight," Cook told me, not looking up from her dough. "Seven covers. The inner circle." She said it the way other people might have said *the plague ship docks at noon*, and then, because Cook was Cook and could not help herself: "Mind your hands. Mind your mouth. Mind everything."

"I always do," I said.

It wasn't entirely true anymore. Three days had passed since Silas Crenge stood shivering at the coal gate with a dead man's pages inside his coat, and in those three days I had done things no invisible woman ought to do. I had hidden a typewriter ribbon inside the spare wheel of the motorcar, wrapped in oilcloth and packed in grease like something butchered. I had memorized Silas's route north — the two police checkpoints on the turnpike, and the third one that kept me awake at night, the one nobody wrote about because it existed nowhere official: Mr. Brack, standing sentry at the ferry crossing, in the name of the movement itself.

And I had learned that Mrs. Fairweather, who ran the accounts, and Mr. Delahay, who wrote the pamphlets, and the rest of the names on Quill's terrible lists were to be gathered around one table tonight, under one roof, passing the salt.

*Serve tonight,* Mrs. Pym had said, meaning it as an order to a chauffeuse pressed temporarily into parlour work, because the regular footman had a cough and good help was impossible to find. She meant it as nothing. It was everything. A whole evening inside the room where the lies were made.

I should have refused. I understood that even then. An invisible woman survives by never standing where the light falls, and there is no brighter light in Manchester than a dinner table full of important people wanting things.

But you cannot smuggle the truth past three checkpoints if you don't know what shape the lie has grown into. So I put on the black dress and white apron that Mrs. Pym dug out of some forgotten trunk, pinned my hair under a cap, and practiced, in the glass of the scullery window, the art of looking through people instead of at them.

I'd had years of practice. I only hoped it would hold at close range.

* * *

The dining room at Marchmont House was built for merchants' grandeur and furnished for revolution, which is to say the chandelier stayed but the tablecloth had been darned in three places, and Edith called it *honest cloth*. Seven candles burned down the center because Edith believed electric light made conversation lazy. Everything about her was like that — a small old-fashioned austerity deployed so precisely it looked like principle rather than theatre.

I knew the guests by their files before I knew them by their faces.

Mrs. Fairweather: accounts, sixty, steel spectacles, a laugh like a drawer slamming. Quill had typed the transfer of four hundred pounds from the widows' fund into an account whose purpose was written as *contingencies — E.M.'s discretion.*

Mr. Delahay: pamphlets and press, forty, soft-handed, charming. Quill had typed his own resignation letter once, at Brack's instruction, back when Quill was still alive and still obedient — a letter Delahay never sent, kept instead as leverage, or insurance, or both.

Dr. Kessler: lectures on political economy, a guest from London, innocent as far as the pages showed. Innocence at this table was going to be its own kind of performance.

And at the head, Edith, grey silk, silver at her throat, holding her wine glass by the bowl the way she'd been taught by some dead husband or dead father, radiating the particular warmth that made three thousand women march when she whistled.

I poured the soup. Nobody looked at me. That was the first lesson of service, and the second, and the ninth: pour, withdraw, become furniture. I let myself be furniture and I listened.

"—the Home Office inquiry will come to nothing," Delahay was saying. "Whittaker's too cautious to push, and half his informants are ours now anyway."

*Ours.* I set the tureen down without a sound. Quill's ribbon sat twenty miles away in a wheel of a car parked in a mews off the Rochdale Road, and every hour it held the impressions of exactly this kind of sentence, typed by a man who was officially at the bottom of the Irwell.

"Half?" said Dr. Kessler, delighted. "You flatter yourselves."

"We don't flatter ourselves," Edith said mildly. "We count accurately. It's the first discipline of any movement that intends to survive."

Mrs. Fairweather laughed her drawer-slamming laugh. "Edith counts everything. She counts the spoons."

"And finds them short," said Delahay.

"Often," said Edith, and smiled, and the table laughed, and I stood against the wall with my hands folded and felt the truth of it settle over me like cold water: they weren't hiding what they did from each other. They were hiding *how much* from each other. Each of them knew one corner of the machine. Only Edith — and Brack, and whoever had put Tom Quill in the river — knew the whole of it.

A traitor inside the movement, the routine letter had said, weeks ago, and I had dismissed it as paranoia. Paranoia. It was worse than paranoia. It was bookkeeping.

* * *

The fish course came and went. I learned that the police raid planned for the print works in Salford had been *delayed indefinitely, thanks to friends* — Delahay's phrase, delivered with a little bow toward Edith, who accepted it the way a queen accepts tribute. I learned that two organizers from the Leeds branch, women whose letters I had carried and read by lamplight until I knew their handwriting better than my brother's, were to be *retired from the platform* — eased out, their committees dissolved — because they had begun asking where the money went.

Mrs. Fairweather defended that one. "They were troublemakers."

"They were asking questions," said Dr. Kessler.

"The same thing," said Mrs. Fairweather, "in this business."

I refilled glasses. My hands did not shake. I noted that later with a strange professional pride, the way a soldier might note that he stood steady under fire the first time: *hands did not shake; poured the claret cleanly; nobody died.* Because somebody had died. Tom Quill had sat at a desk like a good clerk, copying lists of names into a government ledger, and when he tried to stop — when he copied the copies for himself and faked his own drowning to buy room to breathe — someone had decided that a typist was cheaper lost than heard.

Which of you? I wanted to ask, moving down the table with the decanter. Which of you signed it, which of you merely looked away, which of you paid the man who did it? Delahay's soft hands? Fairweather's ledgers? Or was it all Brack's arms and legs while Edith slept beautifully in her grey silk and dreamed of the franchise?

That was the question I couldn't answer, and it frightened me more than the rest, because part of me — a large part, a shameful part — still wanted it not to be her. Three years I had driven that woman through the rain and the heckling and the stone-throwing seasons. Three years she had said *thank you, Ines* at the end of long days, my actual name, when to everyone else in that house I was *the driver*, singular, furniture with a licence.

Don't, I told myself. You're here to count, not to mourn. Count.

* * *

The near-disaster came with the game course.

It began harmlessly, the way these things always do. Dr. Kessler, warming on the claret, asked about correspondence — how a movement of thousands conducted its heart through the post, how many letters Edith answered herself.

"More than is wise," Edith said. "Though I confess I have help. I read the important ones aloud to my household staff sometimes — the driver particularly. Ines has a memory like a courthouse clerk. Half my strategy comes out of that motorcar."

The wall held me up. That is nearly literal; I felt my shoulder blades find the wainscoting behind me and press against it as though the wood were the only honest thing in the room.

Because I remembered the letter. Of course I remembered the letter — remembering was the whole crime of me. A fortnight past, a Tuesday, lamplight in the mews after a delivery run, a letter in a round schoolroom hand from a woman in Burnley, a weaver named Hartley, warning that her branch had been approached by a man offering money for membership rolls, and asking whether the offer came from the executive. And Edith, next morning in the car, dictating her reply while I drove, and then — I can see it still, her glove resting on the door of the Daimler — saying, *Mark that name, Ines. Hartley of Burnley. If she writes again, bring it to me before anyone else sees it. Before Brack especially.*

Before Brack especially. I had thought it loyalty then. A leader protecting her people from her own enforcer. Now the sentence turned over in my chest and showed me its underside, and the underside had teeth.

"She'll remember," Edith was telling Kessler, gesturing vaguely in my direction with her fork, not looking — never looking, that was the mercy and the horror of invisibility, that she could speak of me to the whole table without once turning her head. "Ask her. Ines — the Burnley letter. The weaver. What did she warn of?"

The room turned. Not all of it — Fairweather went on eating, Delahay studied his wine — but Kessler looked, and young Miss Ainsworth, who was pouring at the far end, looked, and for one full second the light fell on me the way it never did, seven faces' worth of attention arriving all at once like weather.

And underneath the terror, something cold and quick and clear woke up and went to work.

Because here was the thing: she hadn't quoted the letter. She'd quoted my *memory of the letter*, and there was a difference wide enough to drive a Daimler through. If I gave back the words exactly, every clause in order, I confirmed I was the safe repository everyone believed — and I became, permanently, the woman whose testimony about that night would matter. If I faltered, I was merely a servant being asked to perform a parlor trick, and servants who performed badly were pitied, not suspected.

Pity was survivable. Precision, tonight, was not.

So I hesitated — just enough, a beat, a servant's apologetic pause — and said:

"The weaver, ma'am. Warned that her branch was offered money for the membership rolls." A small frown, honestly manufactured, because I had spent three years making my face do what the moment required and I made it do that now. "There was a bit about... whether the executive knew of the approach. I couldn't say it proper, ma'am. It was in her words, not mine."

"*Whether the offer came from the executive,*" Edith supplied, pleased, and turned back to Kessler. "You see? Verbatim, near enough. I keep telling the executive committee we should hire her away from the steering wheel."

Laughter, brief, warm, gone. Kessler raised his glass to me across the table — *to the archives,* he said, meaning it as wit — and the light moved off me and back to the candles and the meat, and I withdrew along the wall to the sideboard with the second course of vegetables and stood there with my heart going like a loom.

*It was in her words, not mine.* Fourteen syllables. The smallest lie I had ever told, and the largest. I had taken a true memory and blunted it, given back less than I held, and only Edith — only a woman who had also read that letter and remembered it perfectly — could have known the difference. And Edith was smiling at Dr. Kessler and quoting Gladstone.

She didn't know. She truly didn't know. That was the discovery of the evening, arriving late and enormous: I had spent three days assuming that somewhere behind her grey silk Edith knew exactly what had happened to Tom Quill, had ordered it or blessed it, and the dinner was dismantling that assumption plate by plate. She was managing liars, not being one. She suspected leaks and disloyalty everywhere except directly beneath her own roof, because beneath her own roof she had built a machine she trusted — drivers, clerks, cooks — and the machine was invisible to her precisely because it served her well.

Brack, then. Brack and whoever Brack answered to. Edith was not the spider. Edith was the fly who had learned to love the web because it kept the wind off.

I don't know which was worse. I only know that hating her would have been simpler, and the dinner denied me even that.

* * *

Coffee was served in the drawing room, and I was released — Mrs. Pym's nod, the universal sign that the furniture could revert to being people — but I lingered in the corridor with the coffee tray on the pretext of waiting for the bell, because the door stood six inches open and Edith had drawn Fairweather and Delahay aside from Kessler, into the window embrasure, and voices carry differently when the speakers believe themselves unobserved by anyone worth observing.

"—the Quill matter is closed," Fairweather said. Low. The word *Quill* crossed the corridor and struck me between the shoulders.

"It is not closed," Delahay said, "until the paper stops printing rumours of him. There was a piece in the Salford weeklies again. A man answering his description, seen alive."

"A man answering any description—" 

"Gentlemen." Edith's voice, quiet, and the quietness was worse than shouting; I had heard that voice twice in three years, once before a magistrate and once in a hospital corridor. "If Tom Quill lives, he is a frightened clerk with copies of nothing that matters, and if he is dead, he is a tragedy we must bear with dignity. Either way, Brack is handling it, and either way this table does not discuss it again. Am I understood?"

Silence. Then Delahay, softly: "Handling it. Yes. That's one word for it."

"Mr. Delahay." A pause you could have poured into glasses. "*Count your spoons.*"

Fairweather laughed — one short bark, quickly smothered. Chairs shifted. The conference was over.

I was ten feet down the corridor, tray level, face empty, when the drawing-room door opened and Edith came out alone, moving toward the stairs, and passed within arm's reach of me. She did not look at me — she never looked at me, that was the whole architecture of our three years — but halfway past she stopped, one hand on the banister, and said to the wallpaper, or the gas bracket, or to me:

"Ines. You're up tomorrow at six. We're going north."

North. The word dropped through me like a coin down a well. North — toward the turnpike checkpoints, toward the ferry crossing, toward the ice on the river that Silas swore had days left in it, toward the very road my evidence had to travel.

"Yes, ma'am," I said. "What time shall I bring the car round?"

"Six sharp." She climbed two steps, paused, and added, without turning, in the mild voice she used for arrangements rather than orders: "We'll take the Rochdale Road. Mr. Brack will want to inspect the vehicle before we cross. He's thorough about such things since the break-ins."

Her heels went up the stairs, unhurried, measured, the sound of a woman entirely at peace with her own house.

I stood in the corridor with an empty tray in my hands and the whole map of the next three days rearranging itself behind my eyes, and at the centre of the new map, where before there had been only a ferry and a river and a road, there now sat a single immovable fact:

Edith Marchmont was going to hand me, my car, and everything hidden in its spare wheel directly to Mr. Brack — and I had six hours to decide whether the driver she trusted most in the world was going to be at the wheel when she did.
