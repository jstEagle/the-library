## 10. Brack

Mr. Brack rose before the gulls did.

It was a habit from the mills, where the foremen who lasted were the ones already standing when the whistle blew, so that the men coming through the gates understood, without a word being said, that the day had begun on someone else's terms. He kept the habit now that the mill was twenty years behind him and his work was quieter. Quieter, but no less a matter of gates and of who passed through them.

He shaved by candlelight in the room above the stable mews off Cheetham Hill, studying in the small mirror a face that had never once surprised him. Heavy jaw. Eyes the grey of wet slate. A scar through one eyebrow he'd stopped explaining decades ago because the explanations bored even him. He did not look like a man who believed in anything, which was useful, because people mistook unbelief for cruelty and cruelty for simplicity, and while they were busy making those mistakes he could do his work.

His work, as he thought of it — and he thought of it every morning, deliberately, the way other men said prayers — was this: the movement would survive. Whatever it cost, whatever it required of him or anyone else, the thing Edith Marchmont had built would not be allowed to die of its own contradictions. Empires fell from within. So did causes. He had watched good unions strangle themselves on their own committees, good strikes collapse under the weight of the strikers' private grievances, and he had drawn from all of it a single conclusion that he carried like a coin in his pocket: somebody must guard the door, and the person guarding the door cannot also afford to be loved.

He put on his coat, checked that the ledger was in the inside pocket, and went down to the mews where the cars slept under canvas in the dark.

* * *

The Marchmont household kept four motorcars, two of them genuinely hers, two belonging to sympathisers who preferred that their generosity remain anonymous — aldermen's wives, a brewer's widow, at least one man whose name Brack had seen on a magistrate's list and never mentioned to anyone. Brack maintained all four himself. He had taught himself engines from manuals stolen out of a mechanic's shop in Salford, and he trusted machines more than people for the plain reason that a machine, if it failed, failed for a reason you could find with your hands.

People failed for reasons they hid even from themselves.

By six he had the first car running, the engine settling into its low idle like a large animal calming. By seven the kitchen maid brought him tea in a chipped enamel cup — they were afraid of him, the staff, all of them, and he permitted it, cultivated it even, because fear kept a household honest in ways affection never would — and by half past seven he was sitting in the cold office above the coach house with the drivers' ledgers open before him and the candle burning low against the greying window.

There were three ledgers. One for fuel and mileage, one for repairs, and one — the third — that Edith knew nothing about. In it Brack recorded what he actually cared about: who had driven whom, where, at what hour, and how often the same route repeated itself until repetition became pattern and pattern became something you could read the way a policeman reads footprints in soft ground.

He turned pages with fingers gone thick and careful from thirty years of machinery.

Miss Thackeray. The name came up again and again, in her neat, slightly schoolroom hand, each entry precise: *Delivered Mrs. M., Deansgate, 2:40. Waited 50 min. Returned via Oxford Rd.* She was thorough, that one. He had noticed it months ago and noted it without alarm, the way you note weather. A driver who wrote everything down was a driver who could be trusted to have nothing to hide — that had been his assumption, an assumption he had built on the same foundation as everything else he believed: that invisibility was its own kind of loyalty.

But assumptions, he knew, were the things that killed men. He had buried friends on assumptions.

Because here was the problem, laid out on his desk in three days' correspondence: Tom Quill was dead, officially drowned in February, and yet last week a letter had arrived at the Deansgate office — postmarked Manchester, hand-delivered, unsigned — containing a paragraph of typing that Edith herself had gone white reading and then burned over the office grate while Brack stood watch at the door. He had not seen the whole of it. He had seen enough. The typing was Quill's. He knew Quill's typing the way he knew engines; he had handled hundreds of the man's documents, minutes, lists, memoranda. There was a slight lift in the man's lowercase *e*, a hesitation in the strike that left the letters a hair deeper on the right side of each line. Machines remembered their operators. That was the whole trouble with typewriters, and the reason Brack had never learned to love them: they testified.

A dead man's typing, arriving through the letter slot of a movement that was supposed to have no secrets from itself.

And beneath the shock of it, colder and more patient, another thought had been turning in him all week like a stone in a mill race. Quill had been dead since February. If pages in his hand were circulating now, then either Quill had not died in February — which meant everything Brack had been told about the drowning was a lie, and there were only three men alive who could have arranged such a lie, and Brack knew all their faces in the glass — or someone had been holding Quill's papers for months and had chosen now, in April, to let them loose into the world.

Either way, there was a leak. And leaks in movements did not spring in the walls. They sprang in people.

* * *

At nine, Edith's bell rang, and Brack went up through the back stair as he always did, arriving in the morning room while she was still at her letters, her grey hair pinned with the severe elegance of a woman who had decided decades ago exactly who she was and saw no cause to revise.

"Mr. Brack." She didn't look up. "You've heard about the letter."

"I heard about a letter."

She did look up then, and for a moment he saw her tired, truly tired, the way she only ever allowed herself to be tired in front of him — not because he was kind, but because his kindness was irrelevant to her safety and therefore cost her nothing. "You know what was in it."

"I know whose machine typed it."

"Then you understand my position."

"I understand it better than you do, ma'am, if you'll forgive me saying."

That earned the ghost of a smile. "You never forgive yourself for saying, so I suppose I must forgive you. What do you need?"

"A fortnight," Brack said. "No new routes. No unscheduled calls. Nothing goes in or out of this house that hasn't passed across this desk first — including the post, including the laundry, including the girls who take the petitions round the terraces. And I want the ledgers."

"The ledgers?" Her pen paused above the page.

"Every driver. Every route book. Mileage doesn't lie, ma'am. Fuel doesn't lie. If someone has been carrying things they shouldn't — or carrying them somewhere they shouldn't — the miles will say so before any witness will."

"You suspect the drivers." It wasn't quite a question, and there was something in her voice he couldn't immediately name — not anger. Something nearer to hurt, which surprised him. He filed it away.

"I suspect arithmetic," Brack said. "Arithmetic has never once lied to me. People lie to me daily, and most of them believe themselves while doing it."

She waved him permission and returned to her letters, and he withdrew, and only on the stairs did the thing in her voice resolve itself: it had been guilt. Faint, buried, but real. Which meant Edith Marchmont suspected the leak too, and suspected it lay somewhere close to her own arrangements, and did not want the drivers examined too closely for reasons that had nothing to do with drivers.

Brack stood a moment in the dim stairwell, listening to the house wake around him — doors, water in pipes, the cook's voice rising and falling below.

Everyone lies, he thought. Even the honest ones. Especially the honest ones, because honesty is the best disguise ever devised, and he had known men walk into police stations wearing nothing but their sincerity and walk out again untouched.

He went back down to the ledgers.

* * *

He started with the oldest books, February forward, and worked the way he had once worked a loom that kept throwing faults: systematically, without hurry, trusting the process more than his eyes.

Fuel consumption first. Miss Thackeray's entries were immaculate — almost suspiciously so. He set them aside and pulled the repair log instead, and there, on the ninth of March, found the first thread worth pulling.

*Tyre punctured, nearside rear. Repaired roadside by driver. Kit replenished 10th.*

Roadside repairs were common enough. But the kit replenishment was logged the following day, and Brack remembered the tenth of March because it was the day after the inner circle had met at the house to discuss — among other things — the question of what to do about Tom Quill, should Tom Quill prove not to be in the Irwell at the bottom of the matter as everyone had been encouraged to believe. Brack had sat at the edge of that meeting like a piece of furniture, and had listened, and had said nothing, and had watched Edith choose, in her beautiful voice, the thing that Brack had spent his life choosing and she had spent hers pretending she was above.

Only now the choice had come home through the letter slot, typed in a dead man's hand.

He read the entry again. *Repaired roadside by driver.* Where? The location given was a lane off the Oldham Road, nowhere near any route she'd logged that day. A tyre failing on a road she hadn't travelled, repaired with a kit she restocked the next morning, alone, at dusk, in March.

Brack sat very still. Outside, a dray rumbled past the mews, and the horses in the stalls shifted at the sound, and the light through the office window moved slowly across the desk and illuminated, for a moment, the whole architecture of his mistake.

He had assumed that the quiet ones were safe. That the girl who drove well, spoke little, wrote neatly, and asked for nothing was the least dangerous creature in the household. He had built his entire method upon that assumption, and now the method itself — his own ledgers, his own meticulous arithmetic — was showing him that the quietest person in the house had done something on the ninth of March that made no sense in fuel or miles or time, and had recorded it anyway, because recording it was precisely what made it invisible. Who suspects the woman with the perfect books?

Someone had used that tyre stop for something. To meet someone. To move something. To bury something. And the something, if it connected to Tom Quill's ghost-typing, was not a small thing at all.

* * *

In the afternoon he walked the mews while the second driver, old Hargreaves, washed down the touring car, and Brack stood beside him in an easy silence, watching the water sheet off the mudguards, and asked his questions the way he always asked them — sideways, unhurried, disguised as talk about the weather of engines.

"Hargreaves. The ninth of March. You were on the Rochdale run?"

"Was that the Thursday? Aye, Rochdale."

"And Miss Thackeray?"

Hargreaves's sponge paused, just barely — a pause no one would notice who hadn't spent thirty years watching men's hands for a living. "Don't know, sir. Not my business to know."

"No," Brack agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

But it had been a pause, and pauses were testimony. He thanked the old man, walked once around the courtyard, and let the picture assemble itself the way he liked it assembled — slowly, from parts, each part tested before it was trusted.

Miss Thackeray. Twenty-four, maybe twenty-five. Brother transported — he remembered the file, he read every file, petty theft, seven years, gone when she was sixteen. No sweetheart. No debts. No vices he could find. Came to the movement through the WSPU offices two years back and had risen exactly as far as invisibility could carry her: driver, courier, shadow. The sort of girl, he'd thought, who had learned early that the world eats visible women, and had arranged herself accordingly.

And that, he now understood, was precisely the profile of the most dangerous kind of loyalist: the kind whose loyalty is not to the movement but to the one place inside it where she is safe. Such women do not betray for money. They betray when the safe place turns out to be built over a grave.

Had it turned? Had she seen something, read something — she was clever enough, he'd marked that long ago — something in the letters she carried? He had warned Edith about the letters a year ago, in his flat northern way: *the girl reads, ma'am, whether you permit it or no.* Edith had laughed — actually laughed, that warm magnificent laugh that could fill a Albert Hall and disarm a magistrate — and said Ines Thackeray was the soul of discretion, and Brack had let it go, because you can only guard a door against the dangers you're allowed to name.

Now the danger had a shape, and the shape wore driving gloves, and the evidence of it sat in his own handwriting in his own secret ledger: *9 March. Roadside repair, unexplained location. Kit replenished following morning.*

He closed the book.

* * *

That evening Edith held one of her small dinners — the inner circle, eight covers, the talk low and brilliant, the candles doing their ancient flattering work. Brack did not attend dinners. He attended doors. He stood in the hall while laughter moved like weather through the dining room, and he reviewed in his head the sequence he had settled on, the patient sequence, the foreman's sequence.

Tomorrow he would call the drivers in one at a time, ostensibly about the spring servicing schedule, and he would ask each of them, casually, to account for their March mileage, and he would watch their hands. Hargreaves first, to establish the rhythm. Then the boy who took the night runs. Miss Thackeray last — not because she was least important, but because by the time she sat down opposite him he would know exactly which questions mattered, and a man who knows which questions matter does not need many.

If she explained the ninth of March cleanly, he would apologize inwardly, tighten the routes regardless, and return to guarding his door.

And if she could not explain it —

Brack looked down the length of the hall, at the coats and hats and umbrellas of eight people who believed themselves to be soldiers in a righteous war, and felt the old familiar weight settle onto his shoulders, heavy as machinery and just as necessary. He did not relish it. He wanted that understood, by God, if not by anyone living then at least by whatever ledger was kept in heaven: he did not relish it. Someone had to protect this thing from itself, and protection was ugly work done by ugly men, and he would rather be the ugliest man in England than stand at Edith Marchmont's funeral knowing he had kept his hands clean while they buried her.

From the dining room, warm with candlelight, he heard her voice lift into some anecdote, and the table break into laughter, and he thought — not for the first time, but tonight with a strange tenderness he would never have permitted aloud — *you beautiful, reckless woman, you have no idea what I do so that you may go on being magnificent.*

Upstairs, in his room, he took out his private ledger, opened it to the March pages, and ruled a fresh column with a straightedge. At its head he printed, in block capitals small and hard as rivets:

**THACKERAY — 9 MARCH — WHO DID SHE MEET?**

Then he capped his pen, snuffed the candle, and sat a while longer in the dark, listening to the house below him go quiet room by room — and somewhere in that quiet, faint through the floorboards, he heard the side door of the mews open and close, and footsteps cross the yard, soft and quick, heading for the coal gate.

He did not move. He only began, silently and with great care, to count.
