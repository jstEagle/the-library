## 11. What Ines Kept

The ledger was where Brack would look first. Ines knew this the way she knew the weight of her own hands—not as a thought but as a fact older than thinking. She had spent the evening after dinner in the motor house, ostensibly checking the mileage entries against the petrol receipts, and all the while she had been reading her own handwriting as if it belonged to a stranger, hunting for the place where she had gone careless, where she had let wanting something show.

She found nothing. That was the trouble with being careful for eleven years: when you finally needed to find your own mistake, you couldn't, because you had never practiced making one.

She closed the ledger. Set it square on the shelf where Brack's eye would find it tidy and unremarkable. Then she sat down on the running board of the Hupmobile in the cold and let herself remember what she had spent eleven years not remembering, because tomorrow she would drive north with a dead man's ribbon sewn into her life, and a woman who went to that kind of trouble ought to know exactly why.

It was the smell that came first, the way it always did. Tar and river weed and the particular sweetness of rotting rope, the smell of Salford Quays in the last week of August, 1902.

* * *

Her brother's name was Nathaniel but everyone called him Natty, even their mother, who believed names were given by God and shortened by the devil. He was seventeen that summer, three years older than Ines, with the same dark hair and the same habit of standing just inside doorways as though he'd been invited nowhere and meant to make the best of it.

He had stolen a coat.

That was all. That was the whole of it, the crime entire: a coat off a rail outside a pawnbroker's on Chapel Street, a good heavy coat with an astrakhan collar, taken at dusk on a Tuesday while the shopkeeper argued with a man about the value of a wedding ring. Natty wore it home through the back streets with his chin up like a lord, because he was going to sell it—of course he was going to sell it, they owed six weeks' rent and their mother's cough had turned wet in the mornings—and somewhere between the pawnshop rail and the cellar on Grimes Lane, wearing it had changed him. For forty minutes he had walked through Salford as a man who owned a coat with an astrakhan collar, and Ines, running to keep up, had seen what it did to his shoulders. They went back. Not with pride, exactly. With memory. As if his body remembered a shape it had never actually had and mourned it.

He was caught the next morning. The shopkeeper knew him—everybody knew everybody on that stretch, which was the whole problem with being known—and there was a witness about the ring argument who remembered seeing a boy at the rail. Natty didn't deny it. That was the part Ines could never stop turning over, all these years: he didn't deny it, didn't run, didn't lie. He stood in the magistrates' court in his one shirt and said, yes, sir, it was me, sir, as though honesty were a currency that could be exchanged for something. It could not. The magistrate gave him seven years and transportation to Western Australia, and he pronounced it in the flat tone of a man reading out the price of bread, and Natty looked over the heads of the court until he found Ines and their mother in the public gallery, and he smiled.

He smiled, and Ines understood, at fourteen, the single most important thing she would ever learn: that being seen was not safety. Being seen was how they got you. Every person in that courtroom who recognized Natty Thackeray—the shopkeeper, the witness, the neighbor come to gawk—had helped to hang his name around his neck like a placard. If nobody had known him, nobody could have sworn to him. If he had been no one, he would have gone home free.

Their mother took to her bed for nine days and then got up and went back to the laundry, because grief is a luxury priced beyond a washerwoman's wages. But Ines began, that autumn, the long apprenticeship of disappearing.

* * *

It started small. She learned to walk on the balls of her feet so the stairs wouldn't announce her. She learned the servant's trick of the downward glance—not servility, but a kind of optical camouflage; people cannot describe a face they have never properly seen. She learned that if you moved slowly in a crowded room, eyes slid off you, and if you kept your hands busy with some humble task, you became furniture. She learned to listen without appearing to listen, to hold her face in an expression of mild, pleasant vacancy, a lamp with the shutter drawn.

By nineteen she could stand in a kitchen doorway while a mistress wept over a letter and leave carrying every word of it, and the mistress would say afterward, "Is the girl new? I don't recall her."

She told herself this was power. Everyone told themselves that, the invisible ones—the maids and drivers and charwomen and errand boys, the great grey workforce of England, unseen and therefore supposedly safe. And there was truth in it. Ines had watched visible women suffer for their visibility: the parlormaid dismissed for a pretty face that drew the wrong kind of notice, the organizer at a suffrage meeting pointed out by a neighbor and hauled before a magistrate, the girls force-fed in prison while newspapers printed their names for anyone to read. Names were dangerous. Faces were dangerous. A photograph was practically a confession.

But there are two ways to be unseen, and Ines knew both by heart, the way you know two doors into the same room. There is the invisibility you choose—a curtain you draw, a tool you pick up and put down. And there is the invisibility that chooses you, that is done to you, the way blindness is done, the way poverty is done. The world does not distinguish between them. The world simply sees nothing, and what it sees nothing of, it does not love.

Natty had written once from Fremantle. One letter, in a round clerkly hand that wasn't his—he'd learned to write proper at the reformatory school, the warders' hand, the empire's hand—and the letter said the climate was fine, the work was out of doors, and he hoped Mother's cough was better. Four lines. It cost sixpence to send and arrived four months late, and their mother read it aloud twice and then put it in the biscuit tin with the birth certificates, and Ines understood that the letter was a fiction, a courtesy, a dead man whistling across the ocean so the living wouldn't have to hear what silence sounded like. He wrote the way you speak to a child about death. Everything true had been left out because everything true would have broken them, and there was no point breaking people six thousand miles away whom you could never again touch.

There was no second letter. Either he died, or he stopped believing letters crossed the water, or the man who wrote those four polite lines simply dissolved into the colony the way he had once tried to dissolve into a coat. Ines never found out. There was no mechanism in the world for finding out, not for people like them. You could vanish entirely by committing a crime worth seven years, or you could vanish entirely by doing nothing at all—by being poor in the wrong decade, by coughing in the wrong winter. Visibility and its opposite were both punishments. The only variable was who administered them.

* * *

So when people later said—in the papers, in the trial coverage, in the pamphlets, all of it afterward—that Ines Thackeray must have been a woman of extraordinary courage, she would want to laugh, or worse, to explain. Courage had nothing to do with it, or rather, courage was only the last coat in a very long line of coats.

What she had was practice.

She had been rehearsing for the ribbon for eleven years without knowing it. Every servant's hall where she made herself agreeable and forgettable. Every employer's study she dusted without reading, then graduated to reading without appearing to. Every route she memorized driving for Mrs. Marchmont—the tram timings, the police stations, the streets where a constable always stood, the alleys where a car could turn if a car needed to turn—all of it had been Natty's coat, worn inside out, turned from a thing of shame into a thing of use. Take the visibility that ruined him and reverse it. Learn everyone's name so they never need learn yours. Know the city better than the men who think they own it, and own, in return, the single advantage they cannot confiscate: you are beneath their notice, and beneath notice is beneath suspicion.

That was the story she told herself, sitting in kitchens and on running boards, and it was nearly true, and nearly true is the most dangerous kind of true, because it will bear a person's weight right up until the moment it doesn't.

Because here was the rest of it, the part she kept even from herself until nights like this one, when the ledger was squared on the shelf and Brack was coming and there was nothing left between her and the memory but cold iron and the smell of the motor's oil: invisibility had not saved Natty either.

He had been seen, arrested, named, sentenced, shipped, erased—the full catastrophe—and none of her careful learning had touched it. She had stood in the gallery with her downward glance and her furniture-stillness and her gift for vanishing, fourteen years old and already fluent in disappearing, and it had bought him precisely nothing. The magistrate had never seen her at all. She could have been made of smoke. The whole machinery of law and property and empire had ground her brother up while she stood invisible beside it, and invisibility had not slowed the gears by one tooth.

That was the wound under the skill. Not that she had chosen to disappear, but that she had told herself disappearing was a virtue. Safety. Wisdom. The clever woman's answer. When it was only a scar dressed up as a strategy. She had built a life inside the shape of her brother's destruction and called the shape freedom.

And now—

Now there was a typewriter in a crate under the coal sacks behind her, and a ribbon coiled in oilcloth like a black snake sleeping, and a gaunt man named Silas sleeping badly upstairs at a lodging house under a false name, and a deadline measured in ice. Now the strategy itself was failing, because Mr. Brack had begun pulling threads, and the thread he would pull first was the driver's ledger, and the driver's ledger was clean, and clean would not save her, because Brack was not a magistrate reading out the price of bread. Brack was worse than that: Brack had loved the movement the way she had once loved belonging to Edith Marchmont's household, and men who love a thing hunt its traitors with tenderness, which is the most thorough kind of hunting there is.

She saw it suddenly, sitting on the running board with her breath smoking in the dark of the motor house—saw the whole shape of the next three days the way she sometimes saw a route complete before she'd driven it. She was going to be visible. Not by choice, not in the way she'd rehearsed, but dragged into the light by the collar, named, described, posted on a wall like Quill's likeness was surely posted by now. Silas would break—she had watched his hands shake over the tea he couldn't drink, had heard the years in his voice when he spoke about the men who had questioned him, and broken men break again, that was arithmetic, not prophecy. When Silas broke, he would give them her name, and then every constable and every one of Brack's quiet men would know exactly who Ines Thackeray was, and the eleven-year apprenticeship would end not in triumph but in exposure, the one thing she had built her entire self to avoid.

Her chest went tight, and for a moment she was fourteen again in the gallery, watching her brother smile at her across a courtroom because smiling was all he had left to give.

Then she did what she had done since 1902. She breathed. She catalogued. What do I control? The crate under the coal sacks. The route north. The ice, which answers to no one. What don't I? Silas's nerve. Brack's patience. Edith's heart, whatever was left in it.

And underneath the catalogue, quieter, a thought she did not summon but that arrived anyway, the way truths do, uninvited and punctual:

If they were going to see her—if the world was going to insist on it—then let them see her doing something. Let the visibility mean what Natty's had meant, but reversed. His name had carried him to the hulks because he'd been seen taking a coat. Hers could carry her somewhere else because she'd been seen carrying the truth. Same coin, other side. The empire had taught her family exactly what being seen cost; she intended to present it the bill.

She stood up. Her knees cracked in the cold. From the house came the muffled sound of the staff having supper—Cook laughing at something, the girl Ada shrieking happily and being shushed—and Ines felt the old ache, the wanting-to-belong ache, and set it aside with the discipline of long practice, though it weighed more each time.

Tomorrow she would check the ledgers one final time, then load the car as if for a routine run, and pray the ice held longer than her luck.

Upstairs, faint through two floors, a door opened and did not close. Silas, walking his lodging room like a cage. She listened to the footsteps go back and forth, back and forth, and counted the hours until dawn, and wondered—not for the first time, but for the first time admitting it—how many days it would take before those footsteps stopped pacing and started running.
