## 12. The First Checkpoint

We left before the milk carts.

Ines had learned long ago that the hour before dawn belonged to no one—to no constable, no clerk, no committee—and therefore to whoever was bold enough to claim it. She claimed it now, cranking the motor in the blue dark of the mews while Silas stood with his collar up and his hands jammed in his pockets, looking like a man waiting for a doctor to pronounce something. The engine caught on the third pull. Above them, the gables of Manchester were still black cutouts against a sky not yet decided about the day.

"North," she said, more to herself than to him. "A6 as far as Prestwich, then the Bury road, then whatever God and the signposts give us."

Silas lowered himself into the passenger seat the way a man sits in a dentist's chair. He had slept—or lain sleepless—in his coat on her sister's parlour floor, and it showed: the hollows under his eyes had gone from grey to violet. "How far to the ferry?"

"If the roads are kind, two days. If they're not—" She let the gears take hold and did not finish. Some sentences were better left as roads: untravelled until necessary.

They rolled out through Ancoats while the mills were coughing their first smoke, past women trudging to the gates with shawls over their heads, past a boy sweeping steps by candlelight who looked up at the motor with the flat appraisal of someone who would never ride in one. Ines kept her eyes forward and her speed modest. A woman driving alone with a male passenger at that hour invited questions, and questions were a currency she could not afford to spend.

The typewriter rode behind her seat, wrapped in an old motor rug and strapped down with the leather ties she used for luggage. The ribbon—*the ribbon*, which had sat all night in her imagination like a coal burning through every layer of cloth she imagined around it—was elsewhere entirely.

That had been her own idea, arrived at somewhere around three in the morning when sleep refused her and the ceiling offered nothing but shadows. A typewriter case said *evidence*. A tire repair kit said *competence*. No sergeant in England searched a woman's puncture outfit unless he wanted a lecture on vulcanisation, and no sergeant alive wanted that.

So now, under her seat, wedged where a jack might live, lay the tin kit with its patches, its rubber solution, its little roll of chalk—and, folded flat between two sheets of sandpaper like the most boring document ever committed to paper, every name Tom Quill had typed for men whose names he'd also typed. She had handled it once, with fingers she told herself were steady, and put it away, and made herself think about carburettors.

"You're very calm," Silas said, somewhere past Middleton. It was not quite a compliment.

"I'm not calm. I'm occupied." She changed gear with a double-declutch she felt rather than heard. "There's a difference. Calm is what people feel when nothing's at stake. Occupied is what you do instead of screaming."

He almost smiled. It aged him backwards, that almost-smile—took ten years off the ruin and showed her, briefly, whoever he had been before Quill drowned and Silas began. "Quill used to say things like that. About fear being a job like any other. You clock in, you do the work, you clock out."

"And did he? Clock out?"

The smile died where it stood. Silas turned to the window and the black hedges flying past, and Ines understood that she had asked the one question there was no answer to, or that the answer was the whole reason for this journey, and she drove on in silence and let him keep it.

* * *

The sun came up watery and low as they passed through Bury, gilding the tops of the chimneys and doing nothing for the mud. Ines stopped at a livery yard she half-remembered from a delivery two summers past, bought petrol and a bacon barm cake from a man who looked at her license—forged, but good; Edith's network was thorough about documents if nothing else—and asked whether the road north was quiet.

"Quiet enough." He handed back the license without really reading it. "There's a checkpoint past Ramsbottom. New since Christmas. They're stopping everything, asking where you're bound, what your business is." He shrugged, a shrug that contained the whole philosophy of Lancashire regarding authority. "Motorcars they like well enough. Motorcars mean money, and money means nobody troubles you."

"And if money doesn't mean that?"

"Then God help you, missus." He went back to his barrels.

She relayed this to Silas in the car, keeping her voice level, watching the road. His knee had started bouncing—a quick, arrhythmic tremor he seemed unaware of, and which she found herself cataloguing the way she catalogued routes: data to be managed.

"How do you want to play it?" she asked. "You can be my mechanic. Bad teeth, bad temper, doesn't talk to strangers. That covers most of what you'll need to do."

"And if they ask for names?"

"Names get written in the ledger and forgotten. That's how it works at these things. They stop a hundred people a day and remember none of them, because remembering is work and sergeants on cold roads don't do work." She glanced at him. "Can you look bored?"

"I can look terrified."

"Terrified works too. Nobody suspects a terrified man of anything except having a nagging wife. Just don't look *guilty*. There's a difference. Bored says I do this every week. Guilty says search me. Give me bored, or give me terrified, but keep guilty off your face."

"And which are you?" he asked.

She thought about it honestly, because he had earned honesty. "I'm going to be slightly irritated," she said. "Irritation is invisible. Everyone believes an irritated woman has somewhere better to be."

* * *

They heard the checkpoint before they saw it—a whistle, the clatter of a barrier dropping, a mounted constable wheeling his horse aside to let a cart through. Around the last bend before the bridge over the Irwell, the road narrowed to a single lane between sandbags, and a canvas tent sagged beside a brazier, and three men in the uniform of the county constabulary did what men in uniform mostly do, which is wait for something to happen to somebody else.

Ines ran her drill one last time, the way she ran it before every difficult junction. Ribbon under the seat. Papers in order. Story short. Stories got long when people got nervous, and long stories were confessions wearing hats.

The barrier arm came down in front of the bonnet with ceremonial slowness.

A sergeant approached her window—not young, not old, with the reddened, unshaven face of a man who had been outdoors since before dawn and resented every minute of it. Behind him, a younger constable circled the car with the aimless diligence of a dog checking fences. Silas's breathing had gone shallow beside her; she registered it the way she registered engine noise, as something to be tuned out.

"Morning." The sergeant's breath steamed. "Where are you bound?"

"Prestwich, then over toward the ferry at Radcliffe if the roads allow." She had chosen the destination carefully: plausible, dull, and true enough that any lie embedded in it would sit comfortably.

"Business?"

"Our firm has a motor van laid up with a cracked block near Bury." She produced the forged papers with the weary fluency of a woman who did this four times a week and found it tedious. "I'm fetching spares from our northern depot. My mechanic's come along to identify them, because God knows I can't tell a con-rod from a clothesline, and if I bring back the wrong part it'll be my fault twice."

It was a good story because it insulted her own competence. Self-deprecation disarmed men faster than flattery; it gave them nothing to push against.

The sergeant glanced at the papers. He was, she saw, cold, damp, hungry, and profoundly bored—the four horsemen of small officialdom. The youngest constable had drifted to the rear of the car and was prodding at the spare tyre with one boot.

"Bit early for motoring," the sergeant said.

"Bit early for standing in a road," Ines agreed, and let the smallest edge show, the edge of a woman with a schedule and a foreman who took names. "Is there trouble ahead, Sergeant? Should I know about a detour? Only I've a tide to consider, or a ferry, or whatever it is they run when the river's in a mood."

The word *tide* did quiet work. Rivers, ferries, schedules—these were the language of practical women, and practical women were not smugglers of state secrets. Practical women were the camouflage of the age, and Ines wore hers the way other women wore furs.

"No detour." He handed the papers back, already looking past her at a farm cart coming up behind. "Roads are open. Mind the frost heaves past the mill, they've been breaking axles."

"Frost heaves. Wonderful. Thank you, Sergeant."

The constable at the rear straightened up. "Sarge. Want me to have a look in the—"

"For God's sake, Dobson, it's a lady's motorcar, not an arms shipment." The sergeant was already turning toward the cart, whose driver held out a sack of something for inspection with the resignation of a man who underwent this daily. "On you go, missus. Mind those heaves."

Ines let out the clutch so smoothly the car didn't so much as nod, and eased past the barrier with a raised hand, and did not accelerate—not fast, not slow, the speed of innocence—until the bend took the checkpoint out of her mirror.

Then, only then, she discovered that she had been holding her breath since the sandbags, and let it go in a long plume that fogged the windscreen, and laughed—one short, startled bark of a laugh that had been sitting in her chest since Bury.

"We're through," Silas said, as if reporting someone else's miracle.

"We're through the *first* one." She wiped the windscreen with her sleeve where her breath had fogged it. "Say it again when we're on the ferry."

But something had shifted in her, and she was honest enough with herself to name it, even privately, even now. It wasn't relief. Relief was passive—it happened to you. This was the other thing, the hot thing, the thing that lived just under her sternum and hummed like the engine: she had looked a representative of the law full in the face, lied to him with perfect composure, and *won*. Not slipped past. Won. Out-thought him, out-performed him, walked through the front door of his attention and out the back while he waved her through.

It was intoxicating, and she knew it, and knowing made it worse, because the dangerous part wasn't the sergeant. The dangerous part was how much she liked it. Invisibility had been a discipline; this was a pleasure, and pleasures made people bold, and bold people made mistakes, and the ribbon riding under her seat could not afford a single mistake for two more days and one more checkpoint.

"You're smiling," Silas observed.

"I'm concentrating."

"That's a smile."

"It's a concentrated smile." She changed gear. The road ran on between black hedgerows with frost smoking off the fields beyond, and somewhere north of them a river waited with ice going rotten at its edges, and somewhere behind them a sergeant named nothing at all was waving through sacks of turnips, never knowing what had passed him in a lady's motorcar at seven in the morning.

For the first time since the coal gate, since the dead man's handwriting, since her whole careful invisible life had cracked open like the river ice it was racing—she felt something adjacent to hope.

It lasted eleven miles.

They stopped at a lock-keeper's cottage outside Radcliffe to ask about the ferry, and the lock-keeper's wife, wiping her hands on her apron in the doorway, looked past Ines's shoulder to where Silas stood by the car, and her face changed.

"You," she said. "I know your face. There's men came round the public houses with it on a sheet. Asking after a fellow answering that description." Her eyes came back to Ines, narrow now, recalculating everything—the motorcar, the forged papers, the concentrated smile. "Five pounds, it was. For information leading to—"
