## 13. Silas Breaks

The room had a smell. That was the first thing that came back to him, before the fear, before the faces—just the smell of it. Cold tea gone sour in a cup nobody drank from. Boot polish. Wet wool drying on a rail by the stove. And underneath it all, faint as a watermark, the iodine tang of a room where men had bled into the floorboards and someone had done their best with carbolic.

Silas Wren sat in the back parlour of a temperance hotel in Huddersfield with his hands flat on the table, because if he let them move they would go to his pockets and then to his mouth, and a man chewing his own knuckles was a man announcing himself to every constable between here and Leeds.

*They never asked you twice,* he thought. *That's how you knew them. Ordinary men ask twice. These ask once, and wait.*

Six years ago it had been a cellar off Charterhouse Lane. He'd been twenty-four and clever and certain that a man who memorized everything could be threatened by nothing, because what did they want with a typist? He was the pen, not the hand. They had asked him once about a meeting in Salford, and he'd said he knew nothing, and the tall one—Rooke, though he hadn't known the name then—had smiled like a schoolmaster hearing an interesting error, and said, *Let's begin again, Mr. Quill's friend, and this time let's not waste each other's morning.*

That was how it started. Not with fists. With time.

Time was the instrument. They had a clock on the wall of that cellar with a second hand that ticked slightly off the beat, so that the sound never settled into rhythm, and after four hours Silas had understood that the clock was the whole interrogation. The questions were incidental. They could leave him in the chair for two days and come in fresh, and their freshness was the violence—their clean collars, their cups of tea, Rooke saying *now, where were we* as though they were friends resuming a conversation across a pub table.

He had broken on the third day. Not dramatically. He had simply begun answering, and once a man begins answering he cannot stop; the answers come out of him like water from a cracked jug, and he hates every one and pours them anyway.

Tom Quill had never known that. Tom Quill—the real one, drowned in the papers eleven months ago, alive in a rented room above a cobbler's in Ancoats until October—had believed Silas when Silas swore he'd told them nothing that mattered. And perhaps he hadn't. Perhaps the names he'd given were names already spent, already watched, already burned. He had told himself that for six years. He was telling himself that now, in the temperance hotel, watching the door.

Except now there was more to lose, and the more had a name and drove a motorcar and looked at him with those level grey eyes that measured everything and forgave nothing, and she was downstairs paying the bill, and he was sitting here with the ribbon in a leather case under his coat, and every minute she was gone the room got smaller and the smell got stronger.

*It isn't the same room,* he told himself. *Different town. Different year. Different man.*

But his hands were flat on the table like a man awaiting sentence, and his heart was going at the pace of the clock he couldn't hear anymore but could feel, tick, tick, off the beat, never settling, and when Ines knocked—two quick, one soft, their signal—he came out of his skin.

* * *

She knew the moment she saw him.

She didn't say so. She counted out coppers for the landlady, thanked her for the hot water bottle Ines had never requested, and walked out into the grey Huddersfield drizzle with Silas trailing her like a man following his own coffin. But she knew. She had grown up reading people the way other girls read novels—it was the only literature available to a chauffeur's daughter—and she read him now: the too-straight back, the hands jammed in his pockets, the way his eyes went to every man on the street and stayed a half-second too long, cataloguing, comparing, matching faces against a cellar six years dead.

Two days on the road together had taught her his rhythms. When Silas was steady he talked—low, fast, a running commentary on escape routes and sightlines, the anxious industry of a frightened man keeping his fear employed. When Silas went quiet, the fear had gotten loose inside him and was doing its work unsupervised.

He had been quiet since Bradford. Quiet through Leeds, quiet through the change of trains she'd made him take on the spur of the moment, quiet now, walking beside her toward the yard where the little Bean car waited under tarpaulin with its false plates and its engine still warm from the boy she'd paid to run it.

"Say it," she said, without turning her head.

"Say what?"

"Whatever it is you've been not saying since Bradford."

They turned into the yard. Rain ticking on the tarpaulin. A cat beneath the far wall, watching them with the flat patience of cats. Ines pulled the tarpaulin back, checked the wheels by habit, felt the cold of the mud coming up through her boot soles.

"I've been thinking," Silas said, "about the route north."

"There is one route north. You drew it yourself."

"There's a route that isn't north." His voice had the bright, reasonable tone of a man presenting an argument he'd rehearsed all night. "Newcastle. There's a printer in Newcastle—Mackenzie's cousin, he'd take the ribbon, publish it same week. We go east instead of west, we avoid Brack's territory entirely, we—"

"You told me the ferry road was the only road the ribbon can travel before Thursday." Ines straightened, wiped her palms on her coat. "You told me once the ice breaks, the crossing shuts, and whatever's left on the wrong side of that river stays there. Those were your words, Silas, standing in Edith Marchmont's coal yard with frost on your collar."

"That was before I knew they'd posted Quill's description at Bradford." His voice cracked upward. "Before I stood in a police station doorway reading my own—reading *his* face on a notice while two sergeants looked me over like meat. You don't understand what those men are. You've been invisible your whole life, Miss Thackeray, it's served you beautifully—but I have been *seen*. I have sat in a chair while a man with a cup of tea asked me questions for three days, and I told him things, things that got a woman named Ada Cresswell arrested in '09, and she got eighteen months hard labour and I have carried that—"

He stopped. The rain filled the silence.

Ines looked at him. Really looked. And what she saw frightened her less than it should have, because it was familiar: a man standing at the exact spot where the ground gives way, deciding whether to run back or jump. She had stood there herself, the day her brother's ship went down the Mersey with him on it and she understood that disappearing would keep her alive and nothing else.

"Ada Cresswell," Ines said quietly. "You never told me that part."

"No."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because—" His jaw worked. "Because if I'm going to ask you to trust me with your life on Thursday, you ought to know what my nerve is worth. It isn't worth much. It broke once. Men like Rooke know it broke. That's why they'll use the clock again, and I will break again, and everything you're carrying in that repair kit will come out of my mouth along with my own name and yours."

The cat moved. The rain kept on.

"So run," Ines said.

Silas blinked. "What?"

"You heard me. Newcastle, Mackenzie's cousin, the eastern road. Take the morning train. Nobody follows a man who isn't carrying anything. Leave the ribbon with me."

"And you'll cross alone? Through three checkpoints? Through *Brack*?"

"One of us has to be worth trusting," Ines said. "It might as well be the one whose nerve hasn't broken yet."

She hadn't meant it cruelly. But cruelty is a quality of the ear, not the mouth, and she watched the words land on him like weather, and something happened in his face—not anger. Something older and sadder. Shame finding its floor.

* * *

He sat on the running board of the Bean car in the rain, not caring, and Ines sat beside him because she discovered she could not stand over a breaking man and feel righteous about it.

"I answered them," Silas said, to the mud. "Third day. Rooke poured tea and asked about the Salford meeting again, polite as church, and I answered. And the terrible thing—the thing I need you to understand, because you're deciding right now whether I'm a coward or only damaged—is that it *relieved* me. Breaking was rest. Six years I've been waiting to break again. Every knock on a door, every sergeant's eye sliding over me—I'm not afraid of dying, Miss Thackeray. Dying is finished business. I'm afraid of the chair and the clock and the cup of tea."

Ines said nothing for a while. Down the street a milk cart rattled past, the horse's hooves throwing up spray.

"My brother Joe," she said at last. "Transported—no. Transportation had ended by then. Prison, then. Seven years, for eleven shillings' worth of lead piping he took off a building site because our mother was coughing her lungs out and the doctor cost money. He was fifteen." She watched the rain gather on the tarpaulin edge and fall. "I learned something visiting him in Strangeways. The warders didn't break the men with brutality. Brutality makes heroes of some men. They broke them with routine. Same porridge, same bell, same walk around the yard, until a man forgot there had ever been any other shape to a day. Joe came out obedient as a dog. He wasn't weak, Silas. He was *worn*. There's a difference, and everyone who's ever been worn knows it, and everyone who's never been tested doesn't."

Silas turned his head. His eyes were red-rimmed and very tired.

"You're saying I'm worn."

"I'm saying worn steel still holds an edge if someone grinds it back true." She stood, brushed off her coat. "And I'm saying I've decided something, so listen. You don't get the train to Newcastle. You cross with me Thursday, on the ferry, because you're the only living soul besides me who knows what's on that ribbon and can swear to it under oath. Your testimony is half the evidence. The ribbon without your voice is paper against paper."

"That's why you need me. Not because you trust me."

"It's why I *need* you," Ines agreed. "Trust is separate. Trust is what I'm giving you free, against all evidence, because I watched you read Quill's letters last night and cry over the spelling mistakes, and cowards don't grieve like that. Cowards grieve efficiently."

A sound came out of him that was half laugh, half sob, quickly strangled.

"Now," she said, "the practical matter. If Rooke's men pick you up—and they may—you will break again. I accept that going in. So we arrange matters so that your breaking costs nothing." She held up one finger. "First: you memorize nothing further. Everything written goes in my head or on paper sewn into my lining. Second—" a second finger— "you carry the decoy case. Leather, same weight, full of old laundry lists. Let them find it. Let them watch you give it up. A man who surrenders a case convincingly gets searched lightly. Third—"

"Third," Silas said slowly, catching fire despite himself, "you don't tell me where we're sleeping Wednesday night."

"You're learning arithmetic at last."

He laughed properly then—a short, cracked, astonishing sound—and pressed both hands over his face, and stayed like that a moment, and when he lowered them he looked ten years lighter and ten years older at once.

"They'll use the clock," he said. "On Thursday, if it comes to it. I want you to know what I'll do. I'll hold for a while. An hour, maybe two. Then I'll start talking, and I won't be able to stop, so whatever you do—whatever you hear—don't come back for me. Don't let my mouth be the thing that kills you."

Ines looked at him a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and brought out the ribbon's true case, and held it up between them, rain darkening the leather.

"Then here's the last piece of arithmetic," she said. "This doesn't leave my body until it's in Mackenzie's hands. Not the car. Not a pocket. Not a hotel safe. My lining, my person. So when you break—and you may—they'll be breaking you for a case of laundry lists, and you can tell them anything at all, and it will all be true enough."

Silas stared at the case. Then at her. Something moved across his face that she would remember years afterward: the expression of a man being handed back his own soul and told to mind it better this time.

"You're a harder woman than Edith Marchmont," he said.

"Harder," Ines said. "But she's the one they write ballads about. Get in the car."

He got in. She cranked the engine, swung up behind the wheel, and pulled the goggles down over her eyes, and as the Bean lurched out of the yard into the wet northern morning, Silas looked back once at the temperance hotel and its sour-smelling parlours, and found the smell had lost its power. Rooms were only rooms. Clocks were only clocks.

It lasted, that feeling, all the way to the outskirts of Huddersfield. It lasted until they topped the rise above the valley road and Ines stood on the brake, and below them, spread across the narrow bridge at the bottom of the hill with a lantern already raised against the dusk, stood a row of police motorcycles—and a black saloon car that Silas recognized in one sick, freezing instant, because he had last seen it parked outside a cellar off Charterhouse Lane six years ago.

"Rooke," he whispered.

Beside him, calm as arithmetic, Ines began turning the wheel.
