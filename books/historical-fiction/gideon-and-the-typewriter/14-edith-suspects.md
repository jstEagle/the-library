## 14. Edith Suspects

Edith Marchmont had built her life on the principle that a woman who wanted to move the world must first learn to read it — every face, every silence, every small wrongness in a room. She had been reading rooms since she was nine years old, standing in her father's mill office while men twice her age explained to him why his daughter's arithmetic was charming but not useful. She had learned to see the gap between what people said and what they meant, and she had made that gap her country. She governed it now as skillfully as any minister governed his.

Which was why, on the third morning after the dinner where everything had gone so nearly wrong, she sat at her desk with the household ledger open before her and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from confirmation.

The page was missing.

Not torn out — torn pages announce themselves, leave their ragged stubs like confession letters. No, this page had been removed cleanly, with a razor or a careful thumb, and replaced with a duplicate from the back of the book, aged with tea water in a way that was almost convincing. Almost. The ink of the duplicate sat on top of the paper rather than sinking into it. Edith had run a boarding house for suffragists for eleven years; she knew how ink behaved the way other women knew how bread rose.

She had noticed it at the dinner, actually, before she had noticed anything else. Ines Thackeray's face, for half a second, when Edith had quoted the Liverpool letter — that flicker, there and gone, like a bird crossing a window. Most people would have called it nothing. A servant listening, servants always listened, that was the bargain of service and everyone pretended otherwise. But the flicker hadn't been the blank look of a girl catching fragments of a world above her station. It had been the look of recognition. Of *placement*. Ines had known that letter before Edith spoke it, and she had known it in its entirety, and she had spent the rest of the evening being very carefully, very competently invisible.

Invisible people were only invisible until they weren't. Edith had staked her life on that truth more than once, from both directions.

She closed the ledger and sat for a moment with her hands flat on the desk, looking out the window at the motorcar in the yard, where Ines was checking the tyres with her sleeves rolled, her breath steaming in the grey morning. Small woman. Quick hands. A way of holding her chin when she thought no one was watching that suggested an entire interior country no one had ever been invited into.

Edith had chosen her precisely for that. Two years ago, from among forty applicants, she had picked the one whose references were thinnest and whose eyes were sharpest, because a driver must be discreet and a driver must be clever and above all a driver must be loyal to the person who pays her, and loyalty, Edith had found, grew best in soil that had never been given anything else to grow toward. It was not sentiment. It was husbandry. She had fed Ines wages, warmth, a place at the staff table where her opinion was asked, a coat at Christmas with real fur at the collar. She had watched the girl soften by degrees, like something frozen beginning to thaw, and she had counted it among her quieter successes.

And now someone had taken a page out of her ledger.

* * *

She did not summon Ines. That was the first decision, and it cost her something, because summoning was her habit and habits were power. Instead she waited until noon, until the car was due back from the errand to Prestwich, and then she walked out into the yard herself, hatless, which she never was in front of the staff, and stood beside the motorcar while Ines wiped her hands on a rag.

"Mrs. Marchmont." The surprise was well controlled. Only the pause before the name gave anything away, and it was a very small pause. "Shall I bring the car round? You've the two o'clock at the Free Trade Hall."

"In a moment." Edith looked up at the sky, grey as dishwater, and let the silence do its work. She had learned long ago that most confessions could be harvested simply by standing near a guilty person and refusing to speak. But Ines Thackeray was not most people, and besides — this was not to be a harvest. This was to be a rescue.

"Walk with me," Edith said. "The yard is fine. The gravel keeps the maids from overhearing, and I find I want to say something without an audience."

They walked. Ines matched her pace exactly, which Edith noted and admired even now — the girl adjusted her stride to her employer's the way a good driver adjusts to a road.

"You've been with me two years in May," Edith said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"I remember the day you came. You drove Mr. Halloway's Darracq around the block three times without touching the brake, and when he got out he said you'd either be the best driver in Manchester or the death of him, and I said I'd take the gamble." She smiled at the memory, genuinely. "I have never regretted it."

"Thank you, ma'am."

"But I am going to speak plainly now, Ines, because plainness is a courtesy between women who trust each other, and I mean to find out this morning whether we are those women."

Beside her, she felt rather than saw the change: the shoulders settling a fraction lower, the hands going still. Not guilt, exactly. Readiness. It was, Edith thought with a strange pang, the readiness of a soldier rather than a servant, and she wondered how she had failed to see the uniform under the apron all this time.

"The household ledger," Edith said. "Page forty-one. The week of the twelfth. It has been replaced with a copy, and not a skilled one. Now — before you say anything, I want you to understand that I am not accusing you. I am asking. Because there are only four people with access to that book, and of the four, you are the only one I have never once had cause to doubt, and if that has changed I need to know it today, not next month, not after whatever is set in motion has crushed us all."

The gravel crunched. Somewhere beyond the wall a cart rattled past, and a man shouted at his horse in that cheerful abuse of the Manchester poor.

"If it wasn't me," Ines said quietly, "you'd still be asking whether I took it. You'd just be kinder about it."

Edith stopped walking. She turned and looked at her driver properly, and Ines looked back, and for a moment neither of them performed their roles.

"That," Edith said, "is the first honest thing anyone has said to me in this house in a year, and I include myself in that count."

* * *

She had rehearsed the offer the night before, lying awake in the dark while her husband slept — or breathed in the manner of sleeping, which after thirty years amounted to much the same — and now she laid it out with the care she brought to everything, because she believed in it, truly believed, that this was the last door through which the girl could walk back into safety.

"There is a position opening," she said. "Organizing secretary. It means a salary of ninety pounds a year, your own room in this house, a seat at the inner table. It means that when you speak, I listen, and when I go to London, you sit in the front of the hall, not behind the wheel of a car outside it. It means—" she paused, choosing the true bait, the deep one— "it means a name. Your name, Ines, spoken in rooms where things are decided. Not Thackeray the driver. Thackeray the organizer. Thackeray, whom the movement knows."

She watched the words land. Watched them land the way she watched all her words land, cataloguing the effect, adjusting. And she saw what she had seen at the dinner — the wanting. Real wanting. The girl's whole face leaned toward the offer like a plant toward a window, and Edith understood suddenly and completely how much this child had starved, how many years she had gone unspoken-to and unnamed, and she hated whoever had done that to her, and she used it, because that was what she did, that was the machine working as designed: love as leverage, belonging as payment.

"And in exchange," Ines said. Her voice was steady. Too steady. "You'd want to know what happened to page forty-one."

"In exchange, I would want what I have always wanted. To protect this movement, and the women in it, and you among them." Edith softened her voice deliberately. "Ines. Listen to me. There is a storm coming. I have known it for months — since before Quill died, since before the inquiries began. There are men whose entire profession is the destruction of what we have built, and they do not destroy it by argument. They destroy it by finding the weakest thread and pulling. If someone in this house has taken something — a page, a ribbon, a name — then that someone is standing in the path of a machine that will not stop, that cannot be reasoned with, that has already killed once as far as I know." She let that sentence stand. Let *killed* do its work. "I am offering you a place inside the walls. Inside my protection. I protect my people, Ines. Ask anyone who has worked for me. I have never yet lost one."

It was almost entirely true. There had been Tom Quill, but Tom Quill had drowned, and drowning was nobody's fault, and Edith pushed the memory of the inquest report down into the cellar of herself where she kept such things, locked, dry, unlabeled.

"I don't doubt you'd try, ma'am," Ines said.

*But you believe I'd fail.* The girl didn't say it. She didn't need to. Edith heard it in the space where her answer should have been, and something in her chest turned over — not anger. Recognition. It was the sound of a lie meeting a truth across a kitchen table and both of them knowing.

* * *

Later — much later, after the two o'clock, after the speech, after she had stood on the platform at the Free Trade Hall and thundered at fifteen hundred women about courage and sacrifice and the dawn that was coming whether the government liked it or not — Edith sat alone in the green room behind the stage and allowed herself, for exactly four minutes by her watch, to be honest.

She had offered the girl everything because she suspected her of everything.

That was the shape of it, stripped of velvet. Page forty-one. The flicker at dinner. The way Ines had looked at Brack lately, sidelong, the way one watches a dog that has bitten before. And underneath it all, older and colder: the letter from Liverpool that Ines had supposedly delivered sealed and unread, quoting itself back at Edith from the girl's silence like a coin turning in poor light. Edith had built her machine out of invisible people — the drivers, the typists, the couriers, the quiet ones who moved through the world unseen so that the visible ones could stand on platforms. She had told herself invisibility was a gift she conferred. Safe. Necessary. Honored in its way.

But invisibility, she remembered now, remembering too late, was also the perfect condition for reading. For seeing everything. For keeping copies.

*Like Quill,* whispered the cellar voice. *The invisible boy who kept copies.*

She closed her eyes. On the other side of the wall, the crowd was still singing, the hymn of the movement, hundreds of voices braided together, and it sounded, from back here, from inside the machine, less like a hymn and more like weather. Like ice groaning somewhere far off, under weight.

When the four minutes were up, Edith wound her watch, fixed her hat, and became again the woman the movement needed. But she rang for Brack on her way out, and she wrote three words on a card and sealed it, and gave it into his hand with instructions to follow it precisely and report to no one but her:

*Watch the driver.*
