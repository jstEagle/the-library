## 15. The Second Checkpoint

The road out of Huddersfield narrowed where the moors began, and Ines felt the change before she saw it—the way the hedgerows thinned, the way the light went flat and silver over the tops, the way Silas stopped talking mid-sentence about his sister in Leeds and did not start again.

"What is it," she said. Not a question. She had learned that from Edith: phrase a thing as a statement and people answer faster, eager to correct you or confirm you, either way they talk.

"Smoke," Silas said. "Ahead. See the pitch of it—too straight for a chimney. That's a brazier."

She saw it then. Half a mile on, where the road bent down toward the canal bridge, two police vans stood nose to tail with a tarpaulin strung between them, and men moved behind it with the unhurried purpose of men who had all morning. A brazier burned at the roadside, its smoke rising in a hard grey column against the pale sky. A queue of carts and lorries waited, steam curling from the horses' backs.

Ines pulled the car into the shade of a sycamore and set the brake, and her hands were steady on the wheel, which surprised her, because everything behind her sternum was moving like water finding cracks.

"They've a description," Silas said. His voice had gone thin. "Of Quill. Of me. They'll have my height, my colouring, the scar—" He touched his chin as if the scar might have healed overnight. "You said the first checkpoint would be the worst."

"The first checkpoint was a bored sergeant who wanted his tea." She watched the queue creep forward. "This is something else. This is paper." Even from here she could see it: a board propped against the van's running board, papers pinned to it, a constable holding one up to each driver as they passed. "They're not looking in boots today. They're looking at faces."

Silas had gone the colour of porridge. "I should go. Over the fields. You take the—"

"You can't carry the case across three miles of moorland with your hands shaking like that, and I'm not leaving it in an unattended motorcar at a police checkpoint." She heard Edith's crispness come into her own voice and hated it, and used it anyway, because it worked. "Look at me. Look. They don't know your face, Silas. They know a description written by someone who saw you once, frightened, in bad light. What they have is a man of medium height, dark hair going grey at the temples, a scar on the left side of the jaw, travelling with a woman." She recited it flatly, the way she'd memorised it off the noticeboard outside the magistrate's court in Manchester, where she had gone at six in the morning, before the clerks arrived, and read what was posted there with her collar up.

"How do you know all that?"

"Because I went and read it. While you were sleeping off the gin." She let that sit. "Now listen. There's a village back along the canal, quarter mile. There'll be women walking to the mills, and there will be washing on every line because it's Monday and God never gave us a Tuesday for it. Get yourself a cap and keep your chin down and walk behind me like a man carrying his wife's bag, and if anyone asks, you're deaf in the left ear from the mill and too simple to be worth questioning. Can you be simple?"

Something flickered across his face that was almost a smile. "I've been simple before. It comes back."

* * *

The laundress found them rather than the other way around, which is how Ines preferred these things to happen.

She was coming up the towpath with a great woven basket balanced on her hip, red-knuckled and flushed, singing something tuneless under her breath, and when Ines stepped off the path to let her by she stopped and looked at the motorcar standing by the bridge with its bonnet up and its two useless occupants fussing around it, and she laughed.

"That's a fine machine for breaking down in the middle of nowhere," she said. "What's wrong with it?"

"Magneto," Ines said promptly, because it was the first mechanical word she knew and because drivers always believed their own lies best. "Water in the works. We were hoping to find a garage in the village."

"There's no garage. There's Mrs. Pickles and there's the mill and there's forty families who'd tell you forty different things wrong with a motorcar." The woman shifted the basket to her other hip. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow and her forearms were the colour and grain of old saddlery. "You'll not get it seen to before the checkpoint clears, anyway. They've had the road since seven. Looking for somebody." Her eyes went to Silas, incurious and then, briefly, curious, and Ines watched the arithmetic happen behind them.

"We're not involved in anything," Ines said.

"Nobody ever is." The woman began to walk on, then paused. Ines would remember this pause for the rest of her life, would turn it over in the small hours like a coin she could not spend. "You want my basket?"

Ines stared.

"For a shilling and whatever's under that cape of yours that fits nobody in this county," the laundress said. "There's a nurse comes through Tuesdays and Thursdays for the mill children—sister from the cottage hospital at Colne Valley—and she leaves her spare apron and shawl washed and folded at Mrs. Pickles's, hanging on the line at the back, and nobody touches it because nobody touches a nurse's things, not even the police, especially not the police." She said all this in the tone of a woman discussing weather. "Or you can stand here being innocent at me until the constables get bored of reading their notices."

Ines paid her. The cape—her good driving cape, wool, tailored, the one thing she owned that had ever made her feel like more than a pair of hands on a wheel—went into the basket under a sheet, and the laundress walked off up the towpath wearing it like she'd been born to it, and did not look back, and Ines understood that she had just been robbed by an honest woman and would never see either garment again, and found, absurdly, that she almost didn't mind.

The nurse's things were exactly where promised: a grey dress, plain and immaculate; a white apron starched to the stiffness of paper; a shawl the blue of faded ink; a small round cap. Ines changed behind Mrs. Pickles's wash-house with Silas standing lookout, and when she came out he stared at her for a moment too long.

"Well?" she said.

"You look—" He seemed to search for the word and settle on the truth. "Kind. You look kind. Nobody looks twice at kindness."

It was, she thought, the truest thing anyone had said to her in weeks, and it stung worse than an insult would have. She bundled her hair under the cap, took the empty laundry basket the laundress had left, and put the leather document case inside it, covered with a folded sheet that still smelled of coal soap and other people's lives.

"The nurse carries her own bag," she said. "Nurses are expected to have bags. It's the one thing in this whole business that no one will question."

"And me?"

"You're my porter. My brother. Deaf in the left ear." She handed him the cap. "Walk three paces behind and look at nothing but the ground ahead of you, and if we're separated—if anything happens—you do not look for me. You go north on foot, the way we planned, and you wait at the ferry landing until Thursday's boat, and if I'm not there Thursday you give the case to no one and you take the ribbon out and you burn it rather than lose it whole. Do you hear me? Burn it. Better destroyed than displayed."

"It's the only copy."

"It's the only copy," she agreed, "and dead men's evidence hanged no one yet. Say yes, Silas."

He said yes. Neither of them believed him.

* * *

They joined the stream of mill workers half a mile before the bridge, and Ines learned immediately that the disguise worked better than she deserved, because nobody looked at her at all.

That was the trick of it, she realised, walking with the basket on her hip among women whose hands were ruined and whose faces were already done with the day before it started. The cape had been a costume; the nurse's uniform was a key. A cape invited the eye—a fine cape, an unusual cape, whose-is-that-and-what's-she-doing-here. The uniform dissolved her into a category. Nurses, like laundresses, like drivers, were furniture of other people's emergencies. A constable's gaze slid over her the way water slides over stone, and she felt the old pride rise in her—the pride of the unseen, the necessary, the safe—and then she remembered the laundress walking away with her cape and the pride turned over and showed its underside, and it was not pride at all. It was just loneliness wearing its work clothes.

The queue at the tarpaulin moved slowly. She counted the constables: four, plus a sergeant with a ledger, plus a man in civilian clothes with a bowler hat and a policeman's stance who did nothing but watch faces, and it was the watching man who frightened her. Braziers and vans were theatre. Bowler hats were policy.

Ahead of her a cartload of turnips was waved through. Behind her a brewer's dray argued about its manifest. The constable with the notice board held it up to a bicycle clerk, turned it sideways as though the information might change, and let him pass.

Then it was her turn.

The constable was young, with a moustache that was trying very hard. He looked at the basket, at the apron, at the cap, and his shoulders dropped half an inch with relief, because nurses meant paperwork he didn't want and questions he couldn't answer.

"Sister." He actually touched his helmet.

"Constable." She kept walking.

"There's a—there's a notice, ma'am, we're obliged to show all travellers—" He held the board up, and there it was, in smudged letterpress: *Thomas Quill, typist, believed drowned June last, now sought in connection with inquiries. Medium height, dark hair greying, scar left jaw. May be travelling in company of a woman, age uncertain, possibly employed as a driver.* And beneath it, smaller: *Any person harbouring or assisting—*

"Can you read?" the young constable asked, and then went crimson, because she was looking at the notice with the weary patience of a woman who read things all day, and he had just asked a nurse if she could read.

"I can read, Constable." She lowered the basket to her hip and studied the notice with professional attention, and let a small frown gather, and said, slowly, "Is this the man they think was at the Hollin Lane house last week? Matron mentioned it. Fever case, they said, wouldn't give a name." She shook her head. "If he comes to a cottage hospital he'll have fever or he'll have nothing, and either way we'll have him registered before your sergeant's finished his breakfast. But it isn't the gentleman I attended on the Leeds road, if that helps you. That one was tall. Red whiskers. And drunk," she added, with the exact contempt nurses are permitted to display about drunk men, and the young constable nodded along, grateful to be told what the world contained.

"Red whiskers," he repeated. "That won't be him then."

"No, Constable. That won't be him." She smiled—the smile nurses keep for patients who are going to be fine—and walked through the gap in the tarpaulin, and the bowler-hatted man glanced at her, glanced away, and glanced back, and it was the glance back that put ice down her spine, but he was already turning to the dray behind her, because a nurse with a laundry basket is a solved problem and a brewer's dray with a vague manifest is not.

Three paces behind, head down, cap pulled low, Silas passed the constable like a shadow crossing a floor. No one spoke to him. No one ever speaks to the porter.

She did not stop walking until the bridge was behind her and the road forked toward the moors, and only then did she let herself breathe, and the breath came out ragged, and she stood a moment by the roadside pretending to adjust the shawl so that no one would see a nurse laughing and crying at the same time, which would have raised questions even in a county that had seen everything.

They had done it. Two checkpoints down. One left, and the third was not the government's.

* * *

The trouble came at Colne Bridge, an hour later, and it came the way trouble always came: dressed as ordinary life.

The market square was full—market day, and a wake besides, a funeral party spilling out of the church with black armbands and hard-boiled eggs, and the crowd thickened around the square like cream going off. Ines wanted only to cross it and reach the lane on the far side that led down to the mill road and the long climb north. She should have crossed quickly. Instead she paused—thirty seconds, no more—to buy bread from a barrow, because they had eaten nothing since dawn and because Silas's hands were shaking again and hunger makes shaking worse, and in those thirty seconds the crowd closed between them like water closing over a stone.

She felt it happen before she saw it. One moment his sleeve brushed the basket's rim; the next, a knot of funeral mourners surged between them, jostled her against the barrow, apologised in unison, and moved on, and the space where Silas had been contained only strangers.

She stood on her toes. Grey caps everywhere. Black coats everywhere. Every man of medium height with his collar up became him for one lurching heartbeat and then became somebody's uncle, somebody's son home from the pit.

"Silas," she said, not loudly. A nurse does not shout in a market square. "Silas."

Nothing.

She circled the square once, fast, the basket banging her hip, telling herself he had simply stepped aside—that he had seen a constable and done the sensible thing and gone to ground, and that he would make his own way north, that this was even part of the plan, the plan she herself had made him swear to: *if we are separated, you do not look for me.*

But he had sworn it looking at her, not agreeing with her, and there is a difference, and she had known the difference when she heard it and filed it away with all the other things she chose not to know.

She found his cap. It was lying by the horse trough, crushed, ordinary, empty. Beside it, trodden into the mud, a scrap of paper—not a note, nothing so useful, just the corner of a printed handbill torn in haste, advertising a political meeting at a mill off Bradford Road. *All welcome,* it said. *Come and hear the truth for yourselves.*

Silas couldn't have dropped it. He'd been carrying it folded inside his glove since Manchester, she'd seen him touch it a dozen times like a talisman—he was the sort of man who needed to hold proof that somewhere people still gathered. Which meant he'd been taken quickly enough to lose it. Which meant he hadn't run.

Which meant Brack's men were closer than either of them had guessed, and had not followed her, and had taken him instead, and she stood in the churned mud of a strange market square holding a loaf of bread and a laundry basket full of a dead man's words, and understood with perfect clarity that she was alone now in the way she had spent ten years learning to be, and that she had mistaken that loneliness for safety, and that it had never once kept anything safe, least of all her.

Across the square, above the heads of the crowd, she saw the bowler hat from the checkpoint. He was standing at the mouth of Bradford Road, not searching, not hurrying—waiting, the way a man waits at a gate he knows the sheep must pass through.

Between him and the moor road lay the mill.

Ines pulled the shawl tighter, shifted the basket to her hip, and started toward it.
