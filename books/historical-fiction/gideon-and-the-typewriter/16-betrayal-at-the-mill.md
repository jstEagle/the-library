## 16. Betrayal at the Mill

The mill had been dead for six years. Silas knew it the way a man knows his own bad teeth—by the ache of it. Hollin Mill, they'd called it, before the looms went silent and the windows went dark and the whole red-brick bulk of it settled into the earth like something that had given up on standing. He had walked past it a hundred times in another life, when he was a boy with coal dust in his lungs and no notion that he would one day be running toward such a place instead of away from it.

He was running toward it now.

Behind him, somewhere in the tangle of streets below the canal, he could hear nothing at all, and that silence frightened him worse than footsteps would have. Brack's men did not make noise. That was the thing about Brack that no one at the Marchmont house ever seemed to grasp, the thing Ines—sharp, brave, impossible Ines—had argued against with her chin set at that stubborn angle: *he's just Mrs. Marchmont's man, he does what he's told.* As if doing what you were told made you harmless. Silas had spent two years typing the names of people who did what they were told, and he knew better.

The lane to the mill was rutted with frozen mud. His breath came out in rags. He had not slept properly since Manchester—since the second checkpoint, since the crowd by the market hall swallowed him whole and he let it, since he turned around in that press of bodies and coats and baskets and understood that Ines was gone. Not lost. Gone. Deliberately, sensibly gone, because he had been shaking like a wet dog and she had looked at him with something worse than pity in her eyes and made her choice.

He couldn't blame her. That was the devil of it. He had stood in a doorway with her, the ribbon wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, and told her about the room in Scotland Yard with the green-shaded lamp and the man who never raised his voice, and he had watched her face change—not with fear for herself but with fear *of* him, fear of what he might do or fail to do—and then the crowd came between them like a door swinging shut.

So now: the mill. It was the only place he knew where the roof held and the doors were rusted shut enough to discourage casual visitors. It was also, though he did not fully admit this to himself, the kind of place a man goes when he has stopped believing he will get anywhere at all.

He found the gap in the north wall where the brickwork had slumped, the same gap boys used for smuggled cigarettes and stolen apples a generation ago, and he squeezed through into the dark. Inside, the mill was a cathedral of nothing. Shafts of grey light fell from broken skylights onto floors furred with dust and plaster. A pigeon startled somewhere above him and its wings cracked through the silence like whips. He stood still until his heart slowed, then picked his way toward the old stairwell, thinking: fire, if he could find wood; rest, if his body would permit it; morning, and then north again, alone, following the route Ines had memorized because he had been too broken to carry even a map without trembling.

He was halfway across the weaving floor when the lamps came on.

Three of them, hurricane lanterns, lifted at once from three different shadows, and the dark rearranged itself around men who had been standing in it patiently for hours. Silas stopped. The dust hung in the lamplight like snow falling upward. There were four of them—he counted four, plus a fifth by the stairwell door, heavyset, hands folded in front of him like a servant awaiting instructions—and Silas's legs simply quit, the way they had quit in that room with the green lamp, and he sat down on the floor of Hollin Mill without anyone touching him at all.

"Mr. Crake," said the heavyset man. "Or do you prefer Quill? We've kept both names warm for you."

Silas said nothing. His mouth had gone to paper.

"You gave us a long chase," the man continued, coming closer, setting his lantern on a broken beam as if he were settling in for tea. "Manchester. Then Stockport. My lads nearly had you at the market hall this afternoon—shame about the crowd. Though I'll confess we didn't need to catch you today. We only needed to see who you'd meet." He smiled, thin and professional. "We saw."

*Ines.* The name arrived in Silas's mind before the thought did, whole and terrible. They had seen her. They had seen the oilcloth.

"I don't know what you mean," he said, and heard how it sounded—a dry leaf of a sentence, already crumbling.

"No," the man agreed pleasantly. "I expect you don't. Not yet."

* * *

They did not beat him first. That was the cleverness of Brack's operation—everyone expected violence, braced for it, promised themselves they would hold out against it, and so when it didn't come they had prepared for the wrong war entirely. Instead they tied him to a chair that had once belonged to some overlooker of the weaving shed, and they built a small fire, and they made tea, and the fifth man—who was Brack himself, Silas realized with a lurch, here in person, which meant Edith had let him off his leash, which meant things had moved further than Silas had dreamed—took out a notebook and began, of all things, to chat.

"Do you know what I admire about you, Mr. Quill?" Brack said, pouring. "You kept copies. Every document that crossed your machine, copied in your own hand and hidden away. That's discipline. Most men in your position would have drunk themselves stupid or bolted for America. You stayed. You watched. You waited years." He handed over a chipped cup, and after a moment's paralysis Silas took it, because his hands were shaking so badly he feared he'd drop it otherwise, and because the warmth was obscene and human and he hated how much he wanted it. "A man like that doesn't want to die in a mill. A man like that wants it to have *meant* something."

"What do you want," Silas said. Not a question. His voice sounded far away to him, someone else's voice, a voice from the bottom of a well.

"The ribbon. And the name of whoever's carrying it."

"There is no ribbon."

Brack nodded slowly, the way one nods at a child's explanation of where the jam went. He opened his notebook. "Let's begin with what I know, so we don't waste each other's evening. I know Tom Quill did not drown in the Irwell in September. I know he surfaced twice since—once in Salford, once in Bury—to contact persons associated with certain documents he typed for persons he should not have typed them for. I know those documents contain lists. Names of members. Names of sympathizers. Names passed along to gentlemen in London whose interest in the suffrage movement is not, shall we say, philosophical." He looked up. His eyes were pale and unhurried and utterly without cruelty, which was somehow the worst part. "And I know that whatever Quill hid before he died—the real prize, the ribbon—it is currently moving north in the possession of someone who was seen with you this afternoon outside the market hall in Stockport."

"She's nobody," Silas said. "A laundress. I paid her a shilling to walk with me."

"A laundress." Brack wrote it down, mockingly. "With an oilcloth parcel under her arm and driver's calluses on her palms. I've spent months reading the ledgers at the Marchmont house, Mr. Quill. I know every hand that comes and goes. A laundress with a grip like a coachman is not a laundress."

The tea trembled in Silas's cup. He set it down on the floor beside him with exaggerated care, both hands, like a man defusing something.

"I don't know any drivers," he said.

* * *

Later—he would never know how much later; the mill had eaten time the way the river had almost eaten him—they took the chair away and left him on the floor by the fire, untied, which was its own kind of message: *we are not worried about you.* One of the men sat by the doorway rolling cigarettes he didn't smoke. Another slept, genuinely slept, sprawled against a fallen beam with his cap over his eyes. Brack had withdrawn to the stairwell with his notebook and his patience, visible only as a silhouette against the faint glow of the sky-lights, and the waiting stretched out like taffy.

This was the part they had done before, in the other room, the one with the green lamp. Silas recognized the choreography. First the courtesy, then the facts laid out like cards, then the withdrawal—because a man alone with his own thoughts in a cold mill will torture himself more thoroughly than any professional can, given time. Given time, he will inventory everything he loves. Given time, he will hold each item up to the light and ask himself what it weighs.

Silas had very little inventory. That was the plain truth of it, and he faced it now as he lay watching the fire breathe. No wife. No children. A sister in Canada who believed him dead and was probably better served by the belief. Parents in the churchyard at Prestwich. Two years of work that had ended with him floating face-down in a canal he hadn't drowned in, betrayed by the men who'd promised to protect him, running on fear and copies ever since. Copies. Everything he had, everything he was, came down to copies—copies of documents, copies of names, a copy of a life. Even his survival was a forgery. Tom Quill, typist, informant, drowned. The man lying on the floor of Hollin Mill was the copy, and copies wear out.

But there was Ines.

There was the image he kept returning to, the one his mind played when the shaking got bad: the doorway in Stockport, her face half-turned, the oilcloth bundle clamped under her arm like a mother carrying a child through fire. She had looked at him and he had seen her decide—not to abandon him, exactly. To save the work instead of the man. She'd had the clarity to know the difference. He had loved her for it in that instant, fiercely and hopelessly, the way you love someone standing on a platform as the train pulls out.

And now Brack knew she existed. Knew the shape of her, the calluses on her hands, the direction she'd gone. Which meant every hour Silas stayed silent was an hour Brack spent finding her anyway—with dogs, with informers, with the patient machinery of a man who reads ledgers for pleasure. Silence wasn't protection. Silence was just a slower, crueler arithmetic.

Unless she was already through. Unless she was past the checkpoints, past Brack's net, on the ferry road, moving fast in some borrowed motor-car with the pedal down and her jaw set. He prayed for it. He prayed the way he hadn't prayed since he was eleven, formlessly, desperately, bargaining: *let her be through, let her be through, and I'll tell them anything.*

He heard himself making the bargain and understood, dimly, that he had already lost.

* * *

Brack came back at what must have been near dawn, though the sky beyond the skylights stayed the color of dishwater. He sat down on the beam across from Silas and folded his hands.

"I'm going to tell you something," he said quietly, so as not to wake the sleeping man. "Not as a tactic. Well—" a small shrug, an honest man's shrug— "partly as a tactic. But mostly because I think you deserve to hear it from someone. Everyone else in this business is too busy being righteous."

Silas said nothing.

"I've read every letter Tom Quill typed for that circle. Every name he copied for the gentlemen in London. Do you know what I did with that knowledge? I spent four months quietly getting families out. The ones on the lists—the shopkeepers, the schoolmistresses, the girls who trusted the wrong committee—I warned them. Moved them. Broke three of my own networks doing it." His pale eyes didn't blink. "I am not the villain of this story, Mr. Quill. Neither is Mrs. Marchmont. She made bargains I would not have made, and I clean up after bargains I did not approve, and somewhere in the middle of all that, women get to keep meeting in halls and printing pamphlets without the Yard kicking the doors in. That's the whole ugly machine. It runs on men like me and men like you."

"And Quill," Silas said. His voice was gravel. "What did the machine need him for?"

Brack was silent a moment. "Quill was the price," he said finally. "Not mine to pay. Not mine to prevent, once it was decided. I'll say this much: he was supposed to be in Ireland by now, and the men responsible for him going in the river instead are no longer employed by anyone I answer to." It was the closest thing to an apology Silas would ever get, and they both knew it, and it was worthless, and it landed somewhere in Silas's chest anyway, because he was that empty.

"The woman," Brack said. "Give me the woman and the ribbon, and you walk out of here. Not free—there are conditions, there's always conditions—but alive. Warm. Fed. Alive matters, Mr. Quill. I've seen what happens to martyrs' causes when the martyr dies badly. The cause becomes the death, and the death swallows everything. Your lists, your evidence, your grand exposure—all of it becomes a footnote to a funeral." He leaned forward. "Whereas if she's stopped quietly, if the ribbon comes back quietly, then in ten years none of it ever happened, and the movement goes on, and girls vote someday because men like me did ugly things in mills at dawn. You can be the man who saved the future, or you can be a corpse in chapter sixteen of somebody's account. I know which I'd choose."

*It's not a tactic,* Silas thought, *except entirely.*

And yet.

He lay there and felt the last of himself go soft, like ice underfoot in March, and he thought of Ines's face in the doorway, and he thought—this was the unforgivable thought, the one he would carry longer than anything else—he thought: *she left me in the crowd.* She had. She had looked at him and chosen the work, chosen the ribbon, chosen history over a shaking man in a doorway, and she had been right to, and it had still felt like drowning, and here in the dark with the fire dying and Brack's pale eyes waiting, the feeling and the fact came apart, and the feeling reached for the nearest weapon, which was resentment, and picked it up.

She'd be through by now, he told himself. She'd be past the checkpoints. Giving her name wouldn't matter—wouldn't help Brack, wouldn't hurt her. It was only a name. It was only insurance. It was only so that he would be warm again, so that he would live, so that one day he could stand in a courtroom or a kitchen or a churchyard and explain, and be understood, and be forgiven—

"Her name," Silas said, and the words came out of him like blood from a reopened wound, quiet and unstoppable, "is Thackeray. Ines Thackeray."

Brack wrote it down without hurry. "Thackeray," he repeated. "The driver."

"The driver," Silas said. "Yes."

He had expected to feel the relief Brack had promised. What he felt instead was a great stillness, as if he had finally gone under the water for true, down where it was cold and quiet and the light came through green, and everything above—the fire, the mill, the dawn arriving over the broken skylights—belonged to other people now.

Brack stood, pocketed the notebook, and signaled to the sleeping man, who woke instantly, cap off his eyes, awake the way soldiers wake. Brack paused at the stairwell door and looked back.

"For what it's worth," he said, "she'll likely be at the ferry crossing by mid-morning. The thaw's early. Once the ice breaks, that road closes." He settled his hat. "Which means I'll want to leave now."

The lanterns went out one by one. Silas stayed where he was, on the cold floor of Hollin Mill, listening to the engines start outside, and he understood that he had not bought his life so much as sold hers, and that the difference between the two transactions was a distinction only he would ever trouble to draw.

Somewhere to the north, a river was groaning under its ice.
