## 18. The Ice Breaks

The river spoke first.

I heard it before I saw it—a long groan rolling up from under the ice like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and every hair on my arms stood up beneath my coat. The ferry landing at Marple Cut was a wooden tongue sticking out into a white plain, and the white plain was moving. Not visibly, not yet. But I could feel it in the soles of my boots, the way you feel a train coming through the rails before you hear the whistle.

Brack stood between me and the water.

He had come out of the toll house with his collar up and his hat low, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because we both knew what this was. The third checkpoint. The one no map had warned me about, because Silas hadn't known it existed—because Edith's people didn't put their gates on maps. They put them in men.

"You've come a long way to stand on ice," Brack said.

"So have you."

He almost smiled. That was the worst of him—he wasn't cruel in the way the sergeants at the first two checkpoints were cruel, bored men enjoying small power. Brack believed. He believed in Edith Marchmont the way priests believe in God, and belief doesn't negotiate; it only waits.

"The typewriter," he said. "And the ribbon. You'll hand them over, Ines, and then you'll walk back to your room above the mews, and tomorrow morning you'll drive Mrs. Marchmont to her meeting in Stockport as though nothing has happened. That is the offer. It does not come twice."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you're a thief and a traitor to women who have given everything for you." He said it without heat, which made it worse. "Do you know what she sacrificed for this movement? Do you know what she gave up? A woman like you reads about sacrifice in newspapers. She *made* it."

"I know what she gave up," I said. "I typed the lists of what she gave up. I mean—I read them. Every name Tom Quill ever fed to Special Branch went through that machine, and the ribbon remembers, Mr. Brack. Every letter. Every name. Annie Kessler. The Pankhurst organizers in Leeds. The girl who died in Strangeways. They're all still in that ribbon, pressed into the cloth like footprints in snow, and I'm going to make sure the whole country reads them."

Something moved behind his eyes—not doubt, exactly. Calculation. He glanced past me, down the road, looking for Silas, and I let him look, because Silas wasn't there. Silas was an hour behind me with a busted axle and a face like wet paper, and by now he might be anything: free, caught, or talking. I had decided somewhere outside Glossop that I could not wait to find out. The ice had decided for me. All night the thaw had been working at the river, and the ferrymen were saying last crossing at noon, and noon was twenty minutes gone.

"You're alone," Brack said.

"I'm enough."

He stepped toward me and I stepped back, and my heel came down on the edge of the ramp where wood met ice, and the whole landing shuddered—and the river answered.

* * *

I want to set down what it sounded like, because nobody will believe me otherwise. It did not crack, the way ice cracks in a glass or under a skate. It *groaned*—a sound with a body to it, deep and grinding, rising from the middle of the channel where the current ran fastest, and the groan climbed and climbed until it stopped being a sound and became a feeling, in the teeth, in the chest, and then the white plain between the two banks split open like a seam tearing down a coat.

A black line appeared thirty yards out. Then forty. Then it was running toward us along the channel, faster than a man could run, and the ice on either side of it buckled and heaved up into ridges, plates the size of doors tilting against each other with a shriek of grinding crystal, and the ferrymen on the far bank began shouting and hauling on their rope, and the two or three passengers waiting in the toll house came pouring out onto the boards, and somebody screamed, and somebody laughed—the mad laugh of a person who has just watched the world rearrange itself and finds it funny—and through all of it Brack stood perfectly still.

That was the moment I understood him, I think. He was a man who would stand still while the world broke, because moving meant admitting the world could break. Edith had built him that way, or found him that way; it amounted to the same thing.

"Ines." His voice was quiet under the roar. "Give me the case."

"There is no case," I said, which was true—the ribbon rode against my ribs in a oilcloth wrap inside my shirt, warm from my own skin, and the typewriter was in pieces in a fish crate on the ferry already, paid for, loaded, invisible among baskets and bicycles. "There never was a case. You've been watching the roads for luggage, and the evidence has been walking beside you the whole time wearing my coat."

He lunged.

I don't think he meant to lunge; I think the ice breaking did something to his certainty and his body went before his mind agreed. His hand closed on my sleeve and I twisted away the way you twist from a horse that bites, and my button tore off and rang on the boards, and then the ferryman was shouting LAST CROSSING, LAST CROSSING, GOD'S SAKE, and the crowd on the ramp became a crowd, elbows and breath and panic, and a woman with a market basket drove her hip into Brack hard enough to stagger him, entirely by accident, entirely without knowing she'd saved me.

I ran down the ramp onto the ferry deck as they hauled in the gangplank.

* * *

The Merwell ferry was nothing—a flat barge with a rail and a rope running hand-over-hand through an iron eye, worked by two brothers who had crossed that channel forty years and trusted the ice the way other men trust God. The younger one, red-faced, took my coin without looking at me. The older one looked. He looked at the river, rather, and his face was the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"She'll hold," he said, mostly to himself. "She held in 'ninety-one."

The rope tightened. The barge swung out.

For the first hundred feet it was almost ordinary—the groaning under us steady now, constant, like the sound of a mill, and the broken channel behind us closing and reopening as the wind pushed the floes together and apart. I stood at the rail with my hands on the cold iron and watched the south bank fall away, and Brack on it, small now, motionless at the end of the ramp, watching me go. He didn't shout. He didn't send anyone after me. There was nowhere to send anyone; the road ended at the water, and the water was ending too.

Halfway across, the river changed its mind.

It came up through the hull first—a blow from below, once, hard, as though something had struck the keel with a hammer the size of a chapel. The older brother swore. The younger brother dropped the rope handle and grabbed the rail. And then the ice field ahead of us, the unbroken white we were aiming for, lifted—all of it, a quarter mile of frozen river lifting at once, slow as a whale surfacing—and tilted, and broke along three lines at the same time, and the sound was not a sound anymore but a weather, a roaring avalanche running sideways across the valley, and the ferry was suddenly not riding on a river but adrift in a sea of moving mountains.

"Back water!" the younger brother screamed. "Back water, we can't—"

"We CAN'T," the older brother roared back, and he was right. There was no back. The bank we'd left was a wall of grinding slabs; the bank we sought was worse. The current, freed from its lid, had woken angry, and it took the ferry sideways like a dog taking a rag, and we spun, and I went down on my knees on the wet deck with my arms around the fish crate, around the dead man's typewriter, and I remember thinking with perfect clarity: *if the river takes it, the river takes it, and Tom Quill drowned for nothing, and I drowned with him.*

Then the rope caught.

Somehow—one of the brothers, I never learned which, had kept his grip, had wrapped the rope twice round the iron post despite everything—the rope caught, and the ferry swung its nose upstream, and the current, having thrown us, relented enough to let us fight. The brothers fought. They fought the way men fight when they've done one thing all their lives and will not be bad at it on the day it matters, hauling hand over hand, boots braced, cursing the river in the intimate language of long acquaintance, and inch by inch, yard by yard, the north bank came at us.

An ice floe the size of a hay wagon ground past our stern close enough to touch. Another rose on its edge ahead, hung there balanced like a door on a hinge, and crashed down flat ten feet off our bow, throwing spray across the deck that froze where it landed. I knelt in it, soaked, shaking, holding a crate of typebars and ink ribbon against my heart, and I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me like the river's groan had come out of the ice—something vast that had been held too long under a thin surface.

Because I understood, kneeling there, what the river was telling Brack. What it was telling Edith. *You do not get to hold.* All winter the movement had lain under its smooth white surface, respectable, unified, letters flowing quietly from the inner circle to the men in Whitehall, and everyone had agreed not to notice the current underneath. But currents don't stop for agreement. Currents only wait for the thaw. And the thaw had come early this year, the way truth always comes early, before anyone is ready, before the arrangements are made, and there is no checkpoint you can build that stops a river deciding to break.

The bow struck the north landing.

Hands pulled me up—ferrymen, strangers, a boy of maybe fourteen who took the fish crate from my arms and carried it up the ramp as though it were his own grandmother's coffin. Behind us the channel was open water now, black and churning, full of tumbling plates, and the south bank was a country away. Brack was a speck on it, or perhaps not even there anymore. It didn't matter. No ferry would cross again today. Perhaps not for days. The ice had closed the gate behind me more thoroughly than any enforcer could have done, and the evidence was on the north side, and so was I, dripping, bruised, one button short, alive.

The boy set the crate down at my feet. "You right, miss?"

"No," I said. "But I'm here."

* * *

I walked.

There was a carrier's cart going toward Hebden Bridge and I rode on the tail of it with the crate between my knees, wringing out my skirt, and the driver asked no questions because carriers never do, and the fields went by brown and streaming with meltwater, every ditch running, every hedge dripping, the whole world in flood and movement. Twice I put my hand inside my coat to feel the oilcloth wrap, and both times it was there, dry, warm, patient. Forty names. Forty women and men who had trusted Edith Marchmont's beautiful machine and been fed to Special Branch through it. By nightfall they would be in a printer's hands, and by Thursday they would be in ten thousand hands, and there was nothing left in England that could press them back into silence.

At the top of the hill above Hebden I looked back south. The valley smoked with mist off the flooded river. Somewhere down there was Brack, and beyond him Manchester, and beyond Manchester Edith in her drawing room with her tea and her martyrs and her arithmetic of acceptable losses, and I felt—this shames me a little, but I set it down anyway—I felt no triumph. Only a terrible tenderness, the kind you feel at a funeral for someone you loved who wronged you. Because she had taught me to read the routes, to watch the mirrors, to be invisible and necessary, and everything I had used to escape her, she had given me. The river breaks, but the river was made by rain that fell on hills a hundred miles away.

The cart turned off. I walked the last mile down into the town with the crate in my arms, past lit windows, past a newsboy already shouting about the floods, and stopped before a narrow doorway with a green-painted fascia and gold letters half worn away: *W. FERRIS & SON, PRINTERS.* Light in the window. Smell of ink and hot metal reaching the street.

Through the glass I could see a man at a composing stick, sleeves rolled, setting type line by line the way you build a wall—one truth at a time, laid end to end until it holds.

I shifted the crate in my arms, wiped my face with the back of my wrist, and pushed open the door.
