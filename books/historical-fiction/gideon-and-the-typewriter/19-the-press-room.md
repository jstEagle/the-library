## 19. The Press Room

The print shop announced itself before I could see it: a smell of ink and hot metal rolling out through a propped door like breath from a furnace mouth, and beneath it the hammering of the presses, a rhythm I felt in my teeth before I heard it properly. The street was narrow and mean, somewhere off Deansgate where the warehouses leaned together like conspirators, and the sign above the door said only *Hollis & Co., Jobbing Printers* in letters gone grey with soot.

I had walked the last half-mile on foot. The motorcar was left three streets back behind a livery stable, under a tarpaulin that made it look like something abandoned rather than something hidden, because a woman alone in a fine car draws eyes the way a dropped coin draws hands, and I had spent four days learning what it was to be followed by my own heartbeat. The typewriter rode against my chest inside a carpet bag, wrapped in a shawl, heavy as a sleeping child. The ribbon was sewn into the lining of my coat, flat against my ribs, where it rose and fell with every breath as though it were alive. In a way it was. It remembered everything.

I stood at the threshold a moment longer than I needed to. Through the doorway I could see the shop: a long room lit by gas jets turned low against the afternoon, cases of type ranged along the walls like books in a language of small metal teeth, two men bent over a stone hand-setting a page, and a third at the far end feeding paper into a flatbed press that clattered and spat like something impatient to speak. Ink hung in the air so thickly I could taste it. Everything was black-sleeved and black-fingered and utterly indifferent to me, and for one strange moment I felt the old comfort of invisibility settle over my shoulders like a coat — and then I understood that I had not come here to be invisible. I had come here to end it.

The man at the composing stone looked up first. He was perhaps fifty, bald as an egg except for two tufts of white hair over his ears, with spectacles pushed up onto his forehead and forearms stained to the elbow. He set down his composing stick with the deliberateness of a man who has learned never to move quickly at anything.

"Shop's closed," he said. "Orders."

"Mr. Hollis?" I asked.

"Who's asking?"

"A woman with a dead man's handwriting," I said, "and the machine that wrote it."

Something moved behind his spectacles when he put them down onto his nose — not surprise exactly; more the look of a man watching weather he had been predicting for years finally arrive. He wiped his hands on a rag that was itself mostly ink, came around the stone, and looked me over without hurry: my travel-crushed skirt, the carpet bag, the bruise-yellow shadows under my eyes that no amount of cold water had fully lifted.

"You'd better come through," he said. "Mind the stone. Don't touch nothing."

* * *

The room behind the shop was smaller than the room in front of it, which seemed wrong until I understood that this was deliberate — the front room for the world, the back room for the truth. There was a desk buried under galley proofs, a stove with a kettle just off the boil, and a single window so grimed with city grime that the light came through the colour of weak tea. On the wall hung framed copies of things Hollis had printed over the years, and I read them while he fetched a second chair: a strike bulletin from the mills, a pamphlet on the dockers' lockout, a funeral notice for a man killed at Tonypandy with the word *MURDERED* set in letters an inch high. The government had tried him twice for criminal libel. You could see it in the way the frames were hung — slightly crooked, defiantly unstraightened, as if straightening them would be a kind of surrender.

"Silas Crane sent you," he said. It wasn't a question.

"He told me you'd printed for Tom Quill. Before."

"Quill." Hollis said the name the way a priest says a name at graveside. "Sat where you're sitting, more than once. Had fingers like a pianist and a face like a Sunday school. You'd never have picked him for it." He lowered himself into his chair and studied me across the ruined desk. "Crane wired me Tuesday. One line. Said a woman would come north with the ribbon and that I was to listen before I argued. So." He folded his hands. "Listen first. Then argue. That's the order of business."

So I told him.

I told it plainly, because I had discovered over the past days that plainness was all I had left — the flourishes had been burned out of me somewhere between the second checkpoint and the ferry landing. I told him about Edith Marchmont's household, the letters I read by lamplight after deliveries, the way a driver becomes furniture if she does her work well. I told him about the stranger at the coal gate with pages in a drowned man's hand. I told him how Tom Quill had typed every document that passed through the inner circle's private correspondence — memoranda, minutes, lists — and kept carbon copies of everything, and how when he tried to buy his silence back they gave him the river instead. And then I told him about the ribbon.

"The ribbon remembers," I said. "Every letter typed on that machine cuts into the cloth. The impressions are still there — you can read them under a glass, or take a cast, or photograph them under raking light. Every name Quill ever fed to Special Branch is pressed into that strip of silk. Dates. Payments. The initials of the men who received them. And the initials of the women who approved it." I reached into my coat, and my hands were steady, which surprised me; I had expected them to shake. "Edith Marchmont's initials among them."

Hollis did not reach for the ribbon when I laid it on the desk between us. He looked at it the way a surgeon looks at an X-ray — reading, measuring, weighing what the cutting would cost.

"How many names?" he asked.

"On the lists? Forty-one. Some of them girls younger than I am. Girls who trusted her because she was magnificent, because she stood on platforms and said *we will not be governed without our consent*, and meant it, God help me, I believe she meant it — and then sold their names to the men she swore to fight, because some of them were informers, or might become informers, or knew someone who might, and it was tidier to give them up than to protect them." My voice cracked on the last of it and I let it. "She thinks it's arithmetic. She thinks the movement is a body and a few fingers can be cut away to save the hand. She told me once that history forgives whoever feeds it. I believed her for two years."

"And now?"

"Now I've driven past three checkpoints with a dead man's typewriter in my lap, and I find I'm done believing anyone who asks me to be quiet."

* * *

Hollis took up the ribbon at last. He carried it to the window, tilted it against the tea-coloured light, and held a jeweller's loupe to his eye, and for a long time the only sound was the presses hammering in the next room like a heart that wouldn't slow down. I watched him read another woman's crimes out of a strip of silk and thought: *this is what evidence looks like when it has been carried in a living body for four days.* It was warm still, faintly, from lying against my ribs. Or perhaps I only imagined that. Perhaps warmth was the one thing I couldn't stop lending to everything I touched.

He set it down very carefully, aligned its edge with the edge of the desk, and took off his spectacles.

"I'll tell you what happens if I print this," he said. "Not what might happen. What *will*."

"All right."

"Criminal libel. Seditious libel if the Crown is feeling ambitious. They'll seize the run, seize my type, seize this building if they can find a magistrate with the stomach for it. I've been jailed twice already; a third stretch at my age is likely to finish me, and my wife knows it, and she'll say yes anyway, which is worse for her than for me." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "And that's the cheap part. The expensive part is what happens to your movement. Because make no mistake, miss — it's yours too, whatever you think you've lost. When this breaks, the Marchmont organisation doesn't stumble. It dies. Branches will fold. Money will evaporate overnight — half their subscriptions come from people who'll burn their membership cards rather than be photographed standing near it. The government won't have to lift a finger. We'll do their work for them, in public, with headlines." He put the spectacles back on and looked at me with terrible kindness. "Forty years of building, pulled down in a week. Are you prepared to be the woman who pulled it down?"

I had rehearsed an answer to this on the ferry, and on the train, and in the livery stable at three in the morning when sleep wouldn't come and the typewriter sat on the next bench like a witness. All my rehearsals had been arguments. Standing in the ink-smelling room, I found I didn't need any of them.

"Do you know what my brother got?" I said. "Fourteen years' transportation for lifting a bolt of calico he couldn't sell and didn't need. He was seventeen. Nobody pulled down anything to save him. Nobody even wrote about it, because writing about it wasn't worth the risk to anybody important." I nodded at the ribbon. "There are forty-one names on those lists. Most of them are servants, mill girls, seamstresses — the same class of woman as me, the class that does the work and reads the letters and stays invisible so the visible ones can be brave. Edith Marchmont spent them the way she'd spend postage stamps. And if we say nothing, she'll spend forty-one more, and the movement will go on being magnificent, and the magnificent ones will keep deciding whose names are affordable." I heard my own voice drop, and I let it. "You asked if I'm prepared. Sir, I've been prepared since I was eleven years old. I just didn't know what for."

Hollis was quiet for a while. From the front room came the clatter of the press, patient and enormous, like a thing waiting to be fed.

"There's a girl on the lists," he said at last. "Annie Seddon. Stalybridge. She set type for me the summer before last, between factory shifts. Sixteen years old. Sharp as broken glass." His jaw worked. "If I'd known she'd drifted toward Marchmont's crowd—"

"She's on page three of the lists," I said quietly. "Dated last March. Payment of two pounds ten through an intermediary in Manchester. She was either informing or she was suspected of it. With Brack doing the suspecting, there's no telling which."

"Two pound ten." Hollis repeated it the way other men swear. Then he stood, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at nothing for the length of ten heartbeats, and I understood that I was watching a man bury something — not the ribbon, not the risk, but the version of the world where he'd grown old believing the movement was clean. When he turned around his face had settled into something older and harder and, strangely, lighter.

"Here's how it will be done," he said. "Not a broadsheet — they'd suppress it in a day. A pamphlet. Eight pages, cheap paper, ten thousand copies, set tonight, printed tomorrow, distributed by morning through every channel we have — newsagents, union halls, chapel doors, stuffed in bread deliveries if it comes to that. No imprint, no address, but signed —" he tapped the ribbon — "*signed with this.* Photographs of the impressions, reproduced by process block, alongside the typescript transcription, so nobody can say we invented it. And your testimony, miss, written in your own hand, under your own name."

The room went very still around me. Even the presses seemed to hold their breath.

"My name," I said.

"Ines Thackeray. Driver to Mrs. Marchmont, two years' service. Yes." He watched me the way a man watches a fuse he has just lit. "Anonymous pamphlets get dismissed. A named witness gets investigated — which means she also gets *believed*. But understand what you're agreeing to. Once your name is on that page, it's on it forever. There's no going back to being furniture. Every door in Manchester will know your face. Marchmont's people will know it. Brack certainly will. You'll never drive a motorcar for a respectable household again, never pass a police checkpoint without your heart in your mouth, never—" he paused, choosing the words with a compositor's care — "never again have the luxury of not mattering. Some women would call that a fair price. Some would call it ruin. I've known both."

I thought of my brother on the dock at Portsmouth, a number pinned to his jacket, disappearing into a ship's belly while the crowd on the quay looked anywhere but at him — because looking was dangerous, because seeing made you responsible, because invisibility was the only safety a working-class woman could afford. I had built my whole life inside that sentence. I had been proud of it. I had called it skill, and caution, and sense.

"I want to write it myself," I said. "Tonight. While it's all still true in my mouth."

Hollis almost smiled. "Compositors' rule," he said. "Set it while it's hot, or don't set it at all." He pulled a fresh sheet of paper from the drawer, laid a pen beside it, and turned the inkwell toward me with two ink-stained fingers. "There's a table by the stove. Take your time. I'll be setting the front page."

* * *

I sat down at the table by the stove and the pen felt enormous in my hand, heavier than the steering wheel, heavier than the typewriter, heavier than anything I had ever lifted. Outside, the February dark was coming down over the rooftops, and somewhere beyond it the river ran, carrying its broken ice north toward the sea, and I thought of Silas — testifying now, or fled, or vanished into whatever country takes men who have given their names away twice — and of Edith, who did not yet know that by tomorrow noon her beautiful machine would be speaking in every street in England.

I wrote: *My name is Ines Thackeray, and for two years I drove Mrs. Edith Marchmont's motorcar, and read her letters, and saw nothing, because seeing nothing was my employment.*

The pen moved. The stove ticked. Behind me, Hollis began to set the type, each letter chosen by hand and placed in order, the way a man builds a wall he intends to stand behind — and as the first lines of the pamphlet took shape under my hand, I felt something loosen in my chest that I recognized, with a kind of grief, as the last of my hiding places giving way.

It was nearly midnight when the boy came pounding up the stairs from the street — Hollis's apprentice, cap askew, breath smoking — with a newspaper crushed in his fist and terror all over his face.

"Mr. Hollis," he panted. "Special Branch raided the Marchmont offices an hour ago. It's in the late edition. And there's coppers walking Deansgate with a description of a woman — they're saying she stole state papers, Mr. Hollis. They're saying murder."

Hollis took the paper. He read it once, slowly, then looked at me across the lamplit table, at the pages of my own handwriting spread out like an open wound, and said:

"Well, miss. It seems we'd best print fast."
