## 21. A Letter, Signed

The letter came on a Tuesday, in an envelope of good cream stock, addressed in a hand I knew better than my own. *Miss Ines Thackeray* — no *Mrs.*, no *Missus*, and no title beyond that. Edith Marchmont had always written my name the way she wanted the world to be: plainly, boldly, as if daring anyone to object.

I stood at the door of my lodgings with the envelope in my hands for a long while. Mrs. Prewitt's lodger clattered down the stairs behind me with a coal scuttle and said, "Bad news, love?" and I said, "No," because I did not know yet what it was.

*Dear Miss Thackeray,* it ran. *I should like to see you. Not to reproach you — you will believe that or you won't, but I ask you to come and test it against my face. Thursday, four o'clock, at the house in Chorlton. The house is empty now; you know how well it keeps secrets. — E.M.*

She had signed it with her initials only, which was somehow worse than a full signature. A full signature would have been an appeal. Initials were a summons.

I had not seen her since the story broke in April — since the *Clarion* ran the ribbon's testimony across four columns and the world learned that the names of forty-one women and men had been passed from the inner circle of the movement to Special Branch, typed neatly by a dead man's hand on a dead man's machine. I had not seen her since the newspapers printed her photograph under the headline they gave her, and since Brack vanished into whatever country takes in men like Brack.

Three months. Long enough for the ice to go out of the river entirely. Long enough for me to learn what it cost to be visible: I could no longer walk into a room and count on being forgotten before I reached the far wall. People knew my name. Some spat it. Some shook my hand as though I'd won something.

I went on Thursday.

* * *

The house in Chorlton had been sold, or soon would be; the furniture stood under dust sheets like a congregation waiting for a service that would never be held. The hall clock still worked. It ticked through the emptiness with the patience of things that outlast us.

Edith was in the morning room, where she had always taken her letters. She sat by the window in grey wool, no black band on her arm — she had never worn mourning, not even for Tom Quill, though God knew she had more to mourn than most. Her hair was whiter than I remembered. Everything else was exactly as it had been: the spine straight, the chin level, the eyes that made you feel either rescued or caught, depending on which you needed.

"Ines," she said. "You came."

"You asked."

"I've asked many people for many things. Most of them have stopped coming." She gestured to a chair across from her. "Sit. You look tired."

"So do you."

That startled a laugh out of her — a short, real one, the kind I'd heard perhaps a dozen times in five years of driving her through Manchester rain. "Yes. Well." She folded her hands. "Tea? The kettle still works. Most of me doesn't, but the kettle does."

I made the tea myself, because there was nobody left in that house to make it, and because my hands needed something to do. When I set her cup down she looked at it and said, "Two sugars. You remembered."

"I remember everything," I said. "It's the whole trouble with me."

* * *

We talked about small things first, the way people do when the large thing sits between them like a loaded gun on a table. The weather, which had been unseasonably fine. The state of the roads out toward Buxton. Whether Mrs. Danvers had found another situation (she had; she was keeping house for a widowed vicar in Altrincham and reportedly happy). Edith asked after each of them by name — the cook, the maids, the gardener's boy who'd once been caught stealing apples from the wall and whom Edith had refused to dismiss. She kept a ledger in her head of everyone she'd ever been responsible for. That was the thing about Edith that made her so hard to hate: she genuinely believed she had carried us all.

Finally she set down her cup.

"Read me something," she said.

"What?"

"Anything. From the papers, from one of those dreadful pamphlets you carry about. I find I can't read any longer. Since April. The words swim." Her voice stayed perfectly level. "The doctor says it's nerves. I say it's justice, arriving late."

"Edith—"

"There's a newspaper on the sideboard. Read me the front page. I want to hear it in your voice. Yours was always the best voice in the house for reading aloud — you did the voices of the correspondents, all those pompous MPs, you made us laugh at them." She smiled, and the smile had cracks running all the way through it. "Humour me."

So I read her the front page. A debate in the Commons about franchise reform, reported with the usual condescension. A shipping notice. A small item about the reopening of inquiries into the death of one Thomas Quill, typist, formerly of Salford, whose body — recovered last winter and buried as a drowning — was now understood to have died of other causes. The coroner had been persuaded to reopen. Witnesses were being sought.

"Witnesses," Edith repeated softly, when I finished. "They mean you."

"They mean whoever will speak."

"You'll speak."

"It wasn't a question when I decided it. Yes. I'll speak."

She nodded slowly, looking out the window at the garden, where the roses had gone over and the hips were forming, red and small as drops of blood. Then she turned back to me and asked the question she had summoned me here to ask.

"Was it worth it?"

Not *how could you*. Not *don't you understand what you've destroyed*. Just that, put down on the table between us like a card she'd been holding for three months.

And I understood, sitting there with the dust sheets and the ticking clock, that this was the closest Edith Marchmont would ever come to asking forgiveness — that she would frame it as a question of accounting, because Edith thought in ledgers too, just like Brack, just like the government men. Everything weighed. Everything costed. Even her own ruin.

"No," I said.

Her eyebrows rose. Of everything I might have answered, I think that was the one she hadn't rehearsed for.

"No?" she repeated.

"No. It wasn't worth it. Nothing is worth it — that's the whole point, Edith. Worth is what people like Brack and your gentlemen in Whitehall deal in. Forty-one names traded for the movement's survival. Tom Quill traded for security. Me traded for — what was I traded for? Silence. Tidiness." My hands had gone cold around my teacup and I set it down before I cracked the saucer. "You don't get to ask if it was worth it, because *worth* is the currency that bought all of it. The question isn't whether the truth was worth the cost. The question is whether we're people who pay in other people's blood, and we were, and I couldn't stay."

The clock ticked. Somewhere outside a cart went by, and a dog barked at its wheels.

"That's very fine," Edith said at last. "Very fine. You've been rehearsing it."

"Every day for three months."

"And if I told you that if I had it to do again, I would do it again — the names, the bargains, all of it — because the alternative was watching the movement die in its cradle with the whole world watching and laughing? Would you despise me?"

"Yes," I said. "But less than I despise myself some nights, for having loved you enough not to see it sooner."

That hit her. I saw it land — a small flinching around the eyes, quickly mastered, but I had spent five years learning to read that face and I read it now.

"Loved me," she said quietly. "Past tense."

"I don't know." I looked at my hands. "That's the honest answer. I don't know what I feel. There's the woman who taught me to drive in traffic, who paid for my brother's solicitor when nobody else would lift a finger, who told a hall full of two thousand people that a washerwoman's vote counted the same as a professor's. And there's the woman who let Mr. Brack keep his lists. They wear the same coat. I haven't worked out how both of them fit in it."

"They don't," Edith said. "One of them wins, eventually, every time. That's the discovery of my old age, Ines. I thought I was the strong one and the compromises were temporary scaffolding around the building. Turns out the scaffolding is the building. Take it away and there's nothing standing." She said it without self-pity, almost with scientific interest, and that was somehow the saddest part. "You took it away. Thank you, I think. Though I may curse you again on another day."

"You'd be welcome to. I've had practice."

* * *

She rose, eventually, and walked me to the door herself, refusing my arm on the stairs but not refusing my presence, which was its own concession. In the hall, under the dusty light of the fanlight, she stopped.

"The Quill business," she said. "The coroner. You know they'll try to hang it on Brack alone. One bad actor, case closed, everyone else walks free."

"I know."

"And you'll let them?"

"I'll say what I know. What they do with it—" I shrugged. "I'm done carrying other people's conclusions, Edith. I carried enough."

"Brack will be found someday," she said. "In Buenos Aires or Marseilles, fat and respectable. Men like him always are."

"Then he'll be found. And somewhere a girl like me will read about it in the paper and know that the world notices sometimes. That's not nothing."

She studied me then — a long, level look, the old assessing look, but stripped of command now. Just one woman looking at another in an empty house.

"Do you know what I admired most about you?" she said. "Five years, and you never once asked me for anything. Not a favour, not a reference, not a seat at the table. I told myself it was loyalty. It never occurred to me it was survival." She paused. "It never occurred to me to ask why a woman so young had learned invisibility so thoroughly. That's my indictment, isn't it? Not the lists. That I surrounded myself with people whose silences I mistook for consent."

"My brother was transported for eleven pounds of lead pipe," I said. "He was fourteen. Nobody asked him anything either. After that I decided the safest place in England was inside someone else's errand." I straightened my gloves. "Turns out it's the most dangerous place of all. You taught me to drive, Edith. You never taught me where the road goes."

"No," she said. "That part you have to find yourself. All of us did."

At the door she took my hand — hers dry and cool and lighter than I remembered, as if the last three months had burned fuel off her like a lamp running low.

"I won't apologize," she said. "You know that. If I apologized it would be a lie, and I've told enough of them."

"I know."

"But I'll testify. At the inquest. Publicly, on the record, in my own name." Something moved behind her eyes — not quite a smile. "If they're going to bury me, they'll do it with my signature on the stone. I've earned at least that much honesty, and you taught me the taste for it. So. Consider that my letter, signed."

I stood on the front step in the late sun, and I found that my eyes were wet and that I was not ashamed of it. Reconciliation, I thought, was a word for church halls. What we had was smaller and harder: two women who knew exactly what each other was, standing in the wreckage, neither pretending anymore. It wasn't forgiveness. It might have been more rare.

"Goodbye, Edith."

"Not goodbye," she said. "The fight isn't over, and I've noticed you never could stay out of it. We'll meet at the next bad meeting, Thackeray, you and I — I'll be in the back row with a veil on, and you'll be at the front with your name in the programme." The cracked smile came back, briefly, and this time it held. "Save me a pamphlet."

I walked down the avenue toward the tram, and behind me the door closed on the empty house, and ahead the road went on — the same roads I had memorized years ago for escape, and here I was walking them in daylight, in plain sight, with nowhere to hide and no wish to.

The tram conductor touched his cap. "Where to, miss?"

And I opened my mouth to answer, and found — for the first time in my life — that I already knew.
