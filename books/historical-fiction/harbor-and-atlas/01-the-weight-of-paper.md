## 1. The Weight of Paper

The ferry that brought Cyrus Dunmore to Verrigan was the last one of the season that would bother crossing at all, and its engine had coughed and knocked for two hours across the gray water like an animal that knew it was being worked past its usefulness. He stood at the rail with the other passengers — three fishermen, a woman with a caged goose, a boy selling nothing — and watched the town assemble itself out of fog: first the mole, a long dark arm; then the masts, a forest of bare poles; then, last and lowest, the rooftops, red-brown and salt-stained, hunched against the hill like people leaning into wind.

He counted the masts. It was a habit he had never trained out of himself. Seventeen, eighteen — there, behind the fish sheds, a nineteenth, larger than the rest, twin funnels, blue hull. The *Atlas*. She sat low in the water with her boilers cold, which meant she wasn't sailing soon, which meant she existed, which meant the plan still had a shape.

That was all Cyrus ever asked of a plan. Not that it succeed. Only that it keep a shape.

The ferry kissed the pier with a groan of old rubber tires lashed to the pilings, and a dockhand in an oilskin the color of wet slate caught the line and made it fast without looking up. Cyrus let the others go down the gangway ahead of him. The woman with the goose went first, arguing with it. Then the boy. Then the fishermen, who moved like men stepping into their own kitchens.

Last, unremarkable, a carpenter named — he checked inside himself the way another man might check his watch — *Karel Voss*, stepped onto the pier at Verrigan and became nobody in particular.

The papers said Karel Voss. A soft-faced man of forty-one, born in some inland province no customs officer could point to on a map, trade of joinery and carpentry, seeking winter work. The photograph was of a man with heavy shoulders and light eyes and no expression worth remembering, and Cyrus had spent four days in a boarding house outside Tarnow practicing the face until the mirror stopped flinching. The papers were good papers. They had cost him almost everything he had left, which was the correct price for papers. Cheap papers were the expensive kind.

The leather case rode against his left ribs, sewn into the lining of his coat between two layers of canvas, flat as a hymnal and twice as dangerous. Eleven inches by seven. It weighed, as far as Cyrus was concerned, approximately what a man weighs. He had carried it through three checkpoints, one border, and a night train compartment shared with a sergeant of gendarmes who had snored like a landslide, and every step of the way he had been aware of it the way you are aware of a bad tooth — not constantly, but always returning, always there when the mind went quiet.

Nobody looks at a carpenter's coat. That was the whole theology of it.

* * *

The harbor master's office was a wooden hut at the landward end of the pier, and the harbor master was a wide man reading a newspaper he had clearly already finished, holding it upside down out of what appeared to be pure stubbornness rather than error. Behind him, through a clouded window, the fog was doing its slow work on the town.

"Purpose of visit," the man said, without lowering the paper.

"Winter work." Cyrus set the papers on the desk. "Carpenter."

The harbor master read at the speed of continental drift. Cyrus stood with his hands at his sides and let himself be looked at, which is a skill, and harder than it sounds. The trick is not to be innocent. Innocence invites inspection. The trick is to be dull — to be a thing so categorically uninteresting that the eye slides off before curiosity can get a purchase. He thought about wood grain. About the proper angle for planing a door edge. About whether the chandlery near the fish sheds would need crates repaired before the freeze. His face did nothing at all.

"Voss," the harbor master said. "Karel."

"Yes."

"Bridge is out, you know." He tapped the newspaper, which had been right side up after all. "Frost took the drawbridge two nights ago. Engineer says six weeks, eight weeks, maybe spring. Road south is closed till then. You're stuck here same as everyone."

"That suits me," Cyrus said. "I'm looking for work, not a road."

Something in that answer pleased the man — honesty of a narrow and practical kind, the only kind that doesn't arouse suspicion. He stamped the papers with a stamp that required two hands and a kind of moral commitment, and slid them back.

"Chandlery on the east quay takes on men sometimes. Halloran's. Tell them the harbor master sent you, if it helps. It won't." He returned to his newspaper. Then, as Cyrus reached the door: "Steamer sails in five weeks. Maybe less, if the ice comes early. If you're thinking of leaving."

"I'm not thinking of anything," Cyrus said, which was true in ways the man couldn't imagine, and walked out into the fog.

* * *

Verrigan in fog was a town made entirely of sound. Boots on cobbles. Gulls arguing over something unseen. The slap of water under the piers, patient as a clock. Chain rattling somewhere, rising and falling, someone raising or lowering or securing something — the whole language of harbors, spoken slowly. Cyrus walked the east quay from end to end and let the town tell him about itself.

He had been told, once, by a man whose name was now burned along with everything else, that the first day in a new place decides whether you live there or merely stay. You learn the rhythms. When the work comes in. When the money moves. Who watches whom. Where the exits are, and — more important — where the exits appear to be, because apparent exits are how towns catch people.

There were two ways out of Verrigan, and now one of them was broken. The drawbridge, south, closed until spring. And the sea — the *Atlas*, sailing in five weeks, or sooner if the ice came early. Everyone in this town was doing the same arithmetic Cyrus was doing. That was the danger of small places in bad seasons: scarcity makes every stranger arithmetic too.

Halloran's chandlery was a long building of black timber at the foot of the east quay, smelling of tar and rope and hemp from thirty paces off. Inside, a broad-shouldered man with a beard like a rusted brush was arguing prices with a fisherman over a coil of net. Cyrus waited until the argument exhausted itself — it ended with both men satisfied and slightly angry, which seemed to be the local custom — and then asked about work.

"Crates," Halloran said. "Sheds are full of split cases and the *Atlas* loads in five weeks and everything going aboard has to sit in sound wood. Can you handle a saw without handling your thumbs?"

"I've done it before."

"There's a loft above the shop. Room's cold and the stove's temperamental. Twelve a week and the room free if you fix the stove, which I'll believe when I feel it." He looked Cyrus over with the frank inventory of a man who bought and sold objects for a living. "You've got a joiner's shoulders. Hands are wrong, though. Too careful. Fishermen ruin their hands. Carpenters just wear them. Yours look like they've been kept in a drawer."

"They've been idle," Cyrus said. "Work will fix that."

It was a good answer. It was the answer a man would give. Halloran grunted and pointed at a staircase behind the rope racks, and just like that — with twelve crowns a week and a temperamental stove — Karel Voss acquired a life.

Cyrus climbed to the loft and stood in the middle of it while the fog-light came grayly through the single seaward window. Wooden floor, wooden walls, a straw mattress, a stove that had opinions. From below, the muffled sounds of commerce. Through the window, above the fish sheds, the masts of the *Atlas*, her blue hull visible between roofs like a piece of sky fallen into the street.

Five weeks. Thirty-five days. He unpacked nothing that didn't need unpacking. He hung the coat on the back of the chair, and then — because habit is stronger than sense and in his case habit *was* sense — he took the coat down again, turned it inside out along the hidden seam, and checked the stitches.

The case lay where it had lain since Tarnow: flat, dark, patient. He did not open it. There was nothing in it for him. That was the bitter joke of his situation, the joke he allowed himself to consider exactly once a day and never longer: the case contained a republic. Constitutions, ciphers, the signatures of men who were dead or scattered, the entire portable skeleton of a government that had lasted eleven months and been eaten by generals. To anyone else it was worth passage, pardon, a fortune, a bullet. To Cyrus it was worth exactly one thing: the promise he'd made in a room that smelled of carbolic and candle wax, to a woman whose hands had gone light as paper in his.

*Get them across the sea, Cy. Both of them. Whatever it costs.*

He had paid so far: his commission, his name, his country, and four hundred crowns to a forger with a limp and a conscience. He considered it money well spent, and the consideration changed nothing, because promises don't take payment in installments. They want everything. He had known that going in.

He sewed the seam shut with waxed thread, bit the end clean, and put the coat back on the chair, ordinary as any coat in Verrigan.

* * *

The first week he worked.

There is a mercy in work that Cyrus trusted the way other men trust prayer. Splitting and rebuilding shipping crates is honest labor with an honest geometry — measure, cut, fit, nail — and it asks nothing of a man except that he show up with his hands and leave his soul at home. He rose at five, worked the stove until it surrendered heat, ate bread and cheese at the bench, and nailed crates until his carefully-kept hands blistered and then callused, which improved them considerably. By the fourth day they looked like working hands. By the sixth they looked like nobody's hands in particular, which was better.

He learned the harbor the way he learned everything: quietly, sideways, without asking questions that had edges. He learned that the fishing fleet went out at slack water and came back whenever God felt generous. He learned that the customs officers drank at the Anchor & Chain on Tuesdays and payday, and were therefore blind on Wednesdays. He learned that the mole's outer face had a ladder at its far end that nobody watched after dark, and that the night watchman at the coal yard was a boy of perhaps sixteen who watched the fog instead of the gate, because the fog was more interesting.

And he learned — because everyone in Verrigan spoke of it eventually, the way drowning men speak of the shore — about the *Atlas*.

"She's the last boat south till thaw," Halloran said one evening, counting nails into a paper twist. "Half the town's got tickets. Other half wishes they had. Captain's a Swede, runs a tight ship — confirmed tickets only, no favors, no deck passengers, no exceptions. You want to be on her, you have a paper that says so, and it says so in ink."

"I'm staying for the work," Cyrus said.

"Everyone's staying for the work," Halloran said, with the mild contempt of a man who had never wanted to leave anywhere, "until the ice comes."

Cyrus carried crates. The *Atlas*'s berth schedule was posted on the harbor office wall, and he passed it twice a day without stopping, and had memorized it the first time: coaling on the twenty-second, provisioning the twenty-ninth, departure the fifth of December, weather permitting. Weather permitting. In Verrigan those two words were doing very heavy lifting.

On the seventh evening, coming back from the chandler's with lamp oil, he saw the smugglers for the first time — or rather saw the shape they made, which was how smugglers preferred to be seen. Three men at the corner of the fish sheds, not talking, just present, the way stones are present in a shoe. One of them watched Cyrus come down the quay, and his eyes went over the crates under Cyrus's arm and the paint on his hands and dismissed him in half a second, completely and correctly.

Cyrus nodded the nod one workingman gives another and passed by, and felt the eyes go between his shoulder blades and slide off. Unremarkable. Invisible. Still true. Still holding.

In his room he allowed himself, as he did each night, sixty seconds of accounting. Papers: good. Money: eleven weeks at current burn, less if the children — but he did not think about the children yet, because thinking about them before they arrived was a way of being robbed twice. Exit: the *Atlas*, twenty-eight days. Danger: moderate, diffuse, unnamed. Nobody in Verrigan knew what he carried. Nobody in Verrigan knew who he was. The republic he served no longer existed to send anyone looking, and the men who had eaten it were busy eating each other.

For the first time since Tarnow, since the border, since the night train and the sergeant's snoring, he slept nearly through the night.

* * *

On the ninth morning the frost came down the hills like an army and wrote its signature on every puddle in Verrigan, and Cyrus woke to a town gone white and silent, and stood at his window watching the harbor steam faintly where the sea met the colder air, and thought, with a calm he did not entirely believe: *early.*

The ice was early. Everything else would be too.

He was carrying planks along the east quay at midday when the news reached him the way news reached everyone in Verrigan — by shouting. A boy ran the length of the waterfront yelling it before anyone could stop him: *"Bridge is out! Drawbridge snapped in the frost! Engineer says spring!"*

The quay reacted as one animal. Heads came out of doorways. Men looked south toward the river gap where the bridge should have shown its raised spine above the rooftops, and found only fog. A woman began to cry, quietly and without drama, the way people cry at funerals they expected. Somewhere a man started laughing, which was worse.

Cyrus set down his planks and did his own arithmetic, fast and cold, while around him a whole town recalculated its life in a single morning.

The bridge closed meant the road south closed. It meant no couriers in, no pursuers out — which cut both ways, and he was experienced enough to know it cut toward him more than away. It meant the *Atlas* was now the only door in Verrigan, and every soul in the town was going to spend the next five weeks trying to squeeze through it. Ticket prices would climb. Tempers would shorten. Eyes that had slid off a carpenter's coat would start sliding back, because in a town where everyone needs the same scarce thing, strangers get measured twice.

He picked up his planks and carried them to the shed, and worked, and watched.

At the end of the day Halloran stood in the doorway looking south, and said, mostly to himself: "Ice early, bridge broken, one steamer. Going to be a long winter, Voss."

"Yes," Cyrus said. "Looks like it."

But it was that evening, climbing his stairs with bread and lamp oil, that he heard the knock. Three knocks, small, at the shop door below — hesitant, then firmer, the way you knock when you have nowhere else in the world to knock. And through the floorboards, muffled by timber and fog and the whole width of the sea behind them, a voice he knew better than his own dead name asked the shopkeeper whether a Mr. Dunmore lodged here — a voice that had crossed three borders and half a country to find him, a child's voice asking for a man who did not exist anymore.

Cyrus stood on the stairs with the bread going cold in his hand, and every careful, invisible, unremarkable wall he had built in nine days came down at once.
