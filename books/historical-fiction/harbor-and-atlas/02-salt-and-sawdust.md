## 2. Salt and Sawdust

The chandlery smelled of tar, hemp, and candle wax — the smell of a town that kept itself running by hand. Cyrus Dunmore stood in its doorway on his first morning in Verrigan with a carpenter's paper in his pocket and a leather case full of a dead republic's secrets sewn flat against his ribs, and he let the smell teach him what the fog had hidden overnight.

The shop was narrow and deep, shelved floor to ceiling with the inventory of small maritime desperation: coils of rope graded by wear, bundles of sail needles, tins of pitch, lantern glass wrapped in straw, blocks and pulleys hung from hooks like so many wooden fists. Behind the counter a woman with flour-white forearms was rendering tallow into molds, and she looked up at him without alarm, which he took as a good sign. Alarm would come later, if it came at all.

"Room?" she asked, before he'd said a word.

"If it's quiet," Cyrus said. "I work early."

"Dockhands all say that." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Most of them mean they drink late and wake sore. Loft's above. Two kroner the week, paid Mondays, and I'll thank you not to bring the sea indoors with you." Her eyes went down his coat, cataloguing. "You're not built like a dockhand."

"Carpenter," Cyrus said. "Crates, mostly. The docks eat boxes faster than they make them."

"Frau Hessel," she said, as though that settled something. "Stairs are behind the counter. Don't smoke up there unless you want to meet the insurance man before you meet God."

He climbed to the loft and did not unpack, because there was nothing to unpack that could be seen. His spare shirt and a whetstone went into the communal chest by the window like any workingman's goods; everything else he carried stayed against his body, where it had lived for eleven weeks and four borders. He tested the floorboards out of habit — one near the north wall sounded hollow, a note he filed away the way another man might remember where he'd left his keys. A room with a loose board was a room with a future.

From the loft's single window he could see the harbor laid out below like a thing someone had spilled and then tried to scoop back up: the fishing fleet moored three abreast along the inner quay, the coal barge sulking at its pier, the breakwater arm curving around the mole where the deep-draft vessels tied. And there, third berth past the customs shed, sat the *Atlas*.

She was a mail steamer, middle-aged and honest about it — black hull going grey at the seams, buff funnel, derricks folded like sleeping birds along her mast. Not beautiful. But she ran a line south twice monthly through the strait to neutral water, and she was scheduled to run it twice more before the season closed, and for eleven weeks Cyrus had thought about her schedule the way other men thought about a woman: constantly, specifically, with a mounting inability to concentrate on anything else.

*Twice more,* he told himself. *One trip to learn her ways. One to be on her.*

He went down and out into the morning before Frau Hessel could ask anything else, because questions were how cover wore thin — not torn, just worn, like a strap rubbed across the same shoulder too long.

* * *

The harbor had a grammar, and he set about learning it the only way that worked: by showing up every day until the sentences made sense.

The work found him the way work finds men who look like they can do it. By the second morning he was at the crate yard behind the fish-packing house, where a foreman named Grolla sized him up, handed him a hammer and a bundle of slats, and said, "Boxes that'll hold ice and herring without weeping. If a box fails and the catch spoils, I take it out of everyone's pay, and everyone will know whose box failed. Understood?"

"Understood," Cyrus said, and meant it in a way Grolla couldn't have guessed. There was no better disguise in a port town than competence. A man who fixed things well was assumed to be exactly what he appeared, because no one wasted a good carpenter on an interesting lie.

So he built crates. He squared them true, nailed them clean, braced the corners where herring ice shifted, and within four days Grolla had stopped watching him, which was worth more than wages. The other men talked over and around him the way dockworkers talk around anyone new — weather, wages, the price of coal, a fight at the tavern — and he listened the way he'd been trained to listen, back when listening had been his trade: not to words but to structure. Who deferred to whom. Which names came up without laughter. What was said quietly instead of loudly, because quiet was where power kept its voice.

By the end of the first week, the harbor's grammar had begun resolving into paragraphs.

The fishing fleet worked the tide, nothing hidden there. The customs men worked the customs house hours and inspected according to a pattern Cyrus mapped in his head within days — heavy at the mail landings, thin mid-morning, absent entirely during the noon meal, which told him the *Atlas*'s arrival windows were also the town's windows of official blindness. The coal barge crew drank at a tavern called the Anchorline and fought among themselves and no one else. And then there was a third rhythm underneath the other two, harder to hear: certain crates moved at night that hadn't moved by day. Certain boats went out with nets and came back lighter than nets alone explained. Certain men on the quay nodded to each other with the economy of people who never needed to finish sentences.

Smuggling, probably. In a port this size, certainly. It wasn't his business, and he intended to keep it that way. Every enterprise has its immune system, and a stranger poking at the wrong crates would find himself treated like a splinter.

What mattered was the *Atlas*. He watched her the way he watched everything — sideways, through the corner of routine. He learned that she berthed at the third slip past the customs shed, starboard side to, gangway forward. That her mail was worked first and passengers boarded after, tickets checked at the head of the gangway by a mate with red side-whiskers who checked them again on deck, which meant papers would need to survive two examinations, not one. That she coaled two days before sailing, which meant the two days prior would be the harbor at its busiest and its most distracted. That her captain kept to his own deck and her purser kept to his office above the ticket agency on the front quay, a small dark window with brass letters that read, with a confidence Cyrus envied: *BOOKINGS.*

He passed that window once, unhurried, reading the posted schedule the way any traveler might. Twice monthly through the season. Final sailing: weather permitting.

*Weather permitting.* Four words that could mean fog, or ice, or nothing, or everything. He wrote them off as anxiety and wrote them back in as arithmetic: if he meant to leave, he bought his passage early, confirmed in ink, and stood ready when the date came. A plan with a date was a plan. A plan waiting on weather was a prayer.

On the ninth day he walked into the ticket agency, put his carpenter's money on the counter — earned honestly, in herring-brine and sawdust — and asked for one berth, second class, final sailing, south.

The clerk, a young man with immaculate cuffs and the eyes of someone who counted other people's futures for a salary, wrote the name without looking up. "Dunmore. Occupation?"

"Carpenter."

"Destination?"

"The next port that isn't here," Cyrus said mildly, and the clerk almost smiled, stamped the ticket, and slid it across with the bored courtesy of a man who processed departures the way bakers processed bread.

Cyrus held the ticket on the street outside and felt something dangerous loosen in his chest. Paper. Stamped. Legitimate. One name, one berth, one date — the whole architecture of his exit reduced to a rectangle of card he could burn or swallow or sew into a hem. He had carried codes that could move armies; this small ticket weighed nothing and was worth infinitely more, because it was a door that opened outward, and for eleven weeks he had lived in rooms where every door opened inward onto more room.

He walked back along the quay with the ticket in his breast pocket, on the far side from the case, and permitted himself — carefully, briefly, like a man testing thin ice with one boot — the feeling of hope.

It fit badly. He wore it anyway.

* * *

Routine is a kind of armor, if you can afford the weight of it. By the second week Cyrus had assembled enough routine to outfit a small garrison.

Up at half five. Down the chandlery stairs before Frau Hessel lit the lamps, a nod exchanged over the tallow smell. Work at the crate yard through the morning. Midday meal at the harbor wall among the dockhands, bread and cheese eaten fast, talk absorbed through the skin. Afternoons whatever Grolla needed — and after the first week, Grolla needed him increasingly, because a man who squared a crate true was soon asked to square a doorframe, mend a handcart, replace a rotted stair tread at the packing house. Competence, Cyrus had long ago learned, was both camouflage and currency; it bought him the right to be everywhere on the waterfront without seeming to go anywhere at all.

And everywhere on the waterfront, he noticed, was the same distance from everything. From the customs shed: forty paces. From the *Atlas*'s berth: a hundred and fifty. From the ticket agency: two minutes' walk. From the Anchorline tavern, where the loud men drank and therefore where nobody important listened: across the road. He had spent years learning to think of geography as safety expressed in paces, and Verrigan was arranging itself in his mind like a diagram he might one day need to draw very quickly, in the dark, from memory.

He was mending a stack of export crates behind the packing house one gray afternoon when Sef found him.

He'd noticed the old net-mender before, of course — it was impossible not to notice Sef, who occupied a patch of the quay the way rocks occupy coastlines, cross-legged amid a snowfall of netting, fingers moving with a patience that seemed geological. Half the harbor's gear passed through those hands. Men brought him beer. Nobody hurried him. And now, apparently, the old man had decided to hurry over to Cyrus instead.

"You're the carpenter," Sef said. It wasn't a question, and something in how it wasn't a question put a fine wire of attention down Cyrus's spine.

"I'm the carpenter," Cyrus agreed.

"Squares his nails before he drives them." Sef lowered himself onto an upturned crate with the sound of an old ship settling at anchor. "Saw that. Most men drive them crooked and call it speed."

"Crooked nails hold badly in ice."

"Do they." The old man's face was a topography of squints and creases, and none of it gave anything away except a kind of amused patience, as though Cyrus were a knot he was considering whether to pick at. "You've worked in cold country, then."

"Every country is cold if you stand still long enough."

That got a short exhale that might have been a laugh. "True enough." He watched Cyrus set a slat, tap it home, square it, pin it. "Good hands, though. Too good. Dockhands are ruined hands. Yours have been ruined somewhere else and repaired." He said it the way a man remarks on weather. Then he gathered his netting and his needles and rose. "No offense meant, carpenter. Hands are like boats. You can always tell where they've been."

Cyrus drove the next nail slowly, and after the old man had shuffled away he sat back on his heels and considered the exchange the way he'd consider a creak in a staircase at night: probably nothing, possibly load-bearing.

Sef knew things. Old men who mended nets knew everything that happened on a waterfront, filtered through decades and indifference — but indifference, Cyrus knew, was often a costume worn by excellent observers. He filed the encounter under *watch*, not *threat*. A threat would come with demands. This had come with observations, and observations were only dangerous if they accumulated.

Still. That night in the loft, lying under Frau Hessel's coarse blankets with the harbor breathing below the window — the slap of water, the clang of a buoy bell, the groan of the drawbridge over the river mouth as the night traffic crossed — Cyrus conducted his nightly audit, the ritual he'd performed every evening since the republic fell.

Ticket: secured, sewn now into the false hem of his kit bag, confirmed for the final sailing. Papers: forged, adequate, consistent — carpenter, no history anyone in Verrigan could check in less than a month, by which time the month would be over. Funds: sufficient, modest, unremarkable. Cover: holding. The case against his ribs: present, silent, and — he pressed a palm flat against the coat hanging on the chair, felt the hard leather edge of it through the wool — patient.

Eleven weeks he had carried it. Through checkpoints where soldiers' fingers had come within a coin's width of finding it. Across a border lake at night in a leaking boat. Through three names that weren't his and one interrogation he did not let himself remember in daylight. The case held cipher tables, route keys, the sealed correspondence of a government that existed now only in filing cabinets and exile — and every one of those pages was a death sentence printed twice, once for whoever held it and once for whoever had carried it last.

He had tried, on the crossing, to feel nothing about it. To make it cargo. It refused. It sat against his sternum like a second heart, one that beat only when he did, and reminded him nightly that he was not a carpenter, had never been a carpenter, and would be a corpse the day anyone competent looked too closely at his hands.

But tonight the audit came out different. Tonight the numbers balanced.

For the first time in eleven weeks, the plan was complete. Lodging. Wages. Ticket. Date. Every link in the chain forged and tested — and beyond the last link, open water, and past the water, a shore where no one had ever heard of Cyrus Dunmore, where even the name could be traded in for a newer one, cheaper, lighter, local.

Routine had done this. Routine had made a shape of his days that the days could be trusted to repeat, and repetition, he thought drowsily as the buoy bell clanged its slow count, was nearly indistinguishable from safety. Nearly. He was asleep before he could examine the *nearly* too closely.

* * *

The dream, when it came, was the lake one, and he woke from it the way he always did — silently, completely, hands already fisted — and lay in the dark listening to the harbor until his body believed the sounds: water, bell, bridge, wind. Not oars. Not the engine cutting. Not Livia's voice saying what she had said, which he did not permit himself to recall while awake and which visited him anyway, faithfully, whenever he slept.

Afterward he got up, dressed, and went down the back stairs into the blue hour before dawn, because sleep was finished with him and the harbor was never finished with anyone.

The quay at that hour belonged to gulls and to the skeleton crew of the fishing fleet preparing for the tide. Fog lay on the water in low shelves, and the lamps along the mole burned in halos, each one a small defended territory of light. Cyrus walked the length of the inner quay with his collar up and his breath ghosting, doing what he did perhaps every fifth dawn when the dreams shook him: walking the escape.

Gangway position: forward, port side tonight — she'd swung with the tide. Distance from the chandlery to the *Atlas*'s berth at a walking pace, carrying nothing: seven minutes. Carrying a case and hurrying children he didn't yet have a reason to imagine: eleven. Customs posture at dawn: negligible. Sight lines from the customs house windows: blocked by the coaling shed. Alternative embarkation, should the gangway be denied: the ladder at the mole's root, used by pilots, exposed but short.

It was madness, probably — rehearsing an evacuation for dependents who did not exist, in a town where no one was hunting him, on behalf of a life he had supposedly left on the other side of three borders. But the rehearsal steadied him the way counting steadies other men. And the harbor at dawn, empty and silver and utterly indifferent, forgave him the habit. The harbor, he thought, doesn't care why you memorize it. It only cares that you're respectful.

He reached the mole's end as the sky went from slate to pewter, and there he stopped, because the *Atlas* lay ahead of him in the lifting light, coaling smoke already rising from her funnel in a thin gray feather, and the sight of her did what the ticket alone could not: it made leaving real. Three hundred tons of steel and schedule, indifferent to secrets, bound south on a timetable printed in brass letters. All he had to do was stay small, stay dull, stay a carpenter with crooked-nail ambitions for six more weeks, and she would carry him out of the story of his own life and into a sequel with no title page.

He stood there a moment longer than caution strictly allowed, letting the almost-peace of it fill him like ballast. The plan holds. The exit exists. Say it like a rosary. The plan holds; the exit exists.

Behind him, boots on stone — unhurried, deliberate. He turned.

Frau Hessel stood at the quay's edge wrapped in a shawl, a lantern swinging from one fist, regarding him with the expression of a woman who has rented rooms to forty years of strangers and can sort them by their insomnia alone.

"Can't sleep either?" she said. It wasn't a question either, and like Sef's non-question it contained more than it showed.

"Carpenter's shoulders," Cyrus said. "The bed's too soft. I sink."

"Hm." She looked at him a moment longer, then out at the steamer, her lantern throwing their two shadows long across the stones. "She sails twice more, they say. After that the ice decides, and the ice doesn't negotiate. My husband used to say — the harbor freezes early some years, and when it does, it freezes all at once, between one tide and the next, like a door shutting." She turned back toward the chandlery. "There's porridge on in an hour, if you're minded. Workingmen shouldn't start on an empty stomach."

She shuffled away, and Cyrus remained at the mole's end, the cold coming up through his boot soles, watching the *Atlas*'s smoke feather and shred against a sky going pale at the edges.

Between one tide and the next, he thought. Like a door shutting.

He had six weeks, then. Perhaps less. He turned back along the quay, and he was halfway to the chandlery, composing his morning around bread, work, and invisibility, when he heard it — faint, at first, easy to mistake for a gull — a child's voice, high and thin, coming from the direction of the harbor gate. And then a second voice answering it, and the sound of small feet running on wet stone, running toward him through the fog.
