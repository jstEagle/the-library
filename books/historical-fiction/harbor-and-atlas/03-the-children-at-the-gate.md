## 3. The Children at the Gate

Marta had learned to measure the world by doors.

There was the door of their house in Brezany, which had been blue once and then gray, which had closed softly behind her mother when her mother went out and came back. There was the door of the schoolhouse, heavy as a coffin lid, that the schoolmaster let swing on Tomas's fingers because Tomas had laughed at the wrong moment in arithmetic. There was the door of the house where Aunt Renata had sent them after the funeral — a door with a brass knocker shaped like a fish, polished by hands that were not glad to see them. That door had opened four inches and no further. *She's not here,* said the woman through the gap, though Marta could see the shadow of someone standing in the hall the whole time they walked away.

And now this door: a green chandlery door on a street that smelled of tar and cold salt, in a town called Verrigan that Marta had never heard of until eleven days ago, when the name had been pressed into her palm on a folded paper along with three coins and instructions whispered too fast to hold all at once.

*Ask for the carpenter. Dunmore. Say nothing else.*

The paper had been carried against her skin under three shirts across two borders, folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth, its ink bleeding into a bruise-blue smudge where her sweat had soaked it. She had read it forty times by lamplight in rooms where reading was forbidden. She knew every crease of it. It said very little — a name, a town, a line about guardianship written in her uncle's cramped left-slanting hand — but it was the only document in the world that said she belonged to somebody, and she had guarded it the way other children guarded a saint's medal.

Tomas stood behind her on the step, breathing through his mouth the way he did when his stomach hurt, which was always now. He was nine and had stopped growing somewhere in the autumn; his coat had been his father's and hung off him like a curtain. His eyes went to the knocker — iron, plain, honest — and then to Marta, waiting, as he always waited, for her to decide what the world was going to do to them next.

Marta knocked.

Nothing. She counted to thirty, watching her breath hang white above the iron ring, and knocked again, harder, and somewhere inside a bell moved, a dull clank like a bucket dropped down a well.

Footsteps. Not hurrying. The particular unhurried tread of a man deciding whether to answer at all.

The door opened the width of a hand, then stopped — and Marta's heart dropped through the boards of the step, because she knew that pause, she knew the geometry of a door opening exactly far enough to refuse someone.

Then the man on the other side saw them properly. Saw her, and then looked past her shoulder and saw Tomas, and something happened in his face that she would remember for the rest of her life: it went from suspicion to recognition to grief in the space of one breath, like weather crossing water, and grief won. Grief, and underneath it, fear — but the grief was real. She had spent a year learning to tell real things from performed things. This was real.

"You're Marta," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

He looked at the street over their heads, both directions, quick and practiced, the way a man checks a road before crossing with something breakable. Then he opened the door all the way.

"Inside," he said. "Quickly, please."

Marta took Tomas's sleeve and stepped over the threshold, and the door closed behind them with a sound she catalogued immediately, hungrily: soft, deliberate, final in the good way. The sound of a door being shut *for* you instead of against you. She did not let herself believe it yet. Doors lied. But she put the sound away somewhere, the way she'd put away the paper, against future need.

* * *

Inside smelled of rope and lamp oil and candle wax, and under it all the sea, which was everywhere in this town the way dust was everywhere in Brezany. A shop front, dim now as evening came on: coils of hemp, stacks of tarred canvas, barrels with stenciled letters, a counter scarred white by decades of knives. Behind the counter a stair went up into darkness.

The man lit a lantern and turned up its wick and looked at them in its light, and Marta looked back, taking inventory, because that was what she did now. Uncle Cyrus — though he hadn't said so, and she wasn't supposed to say so either. Tall, but stooped slightly, as if trying to take up less room even in an empty shop. Hands that didn't match the rest of him: too fine, too still, a scholar's hands wearing a laborer's calluses like borrowed gloves. Dark hair going early gray at the temples. Eyes the color of wet slate, moving constantly, never resting anywhere longer than a heartbeat except on Tomas's face, where they rested too long, and flinched.

"You have her chin," he said quietly. "Lina's chin."

"Are you our uncle?"

"Yes." Then, as if the word cost something or gave something: "Yes, I am."

Marta reached under her coat, into the inner pocket she had sewn shut and re-sewn twice when the stitches tore, and brought out the paper, softened now to felt, and held it out. He took it with those careful scholar's hands and unfolded it as though it might crumble, and read it standing there in the lantern light, and she watched his throat move.

"It doesn't say much," Marta said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "It just says you're to have us. It says *guardian.*"

"It says enough." He folded it and handed it back — handed it back, she noticed, not keeping it, and something in her chest unlocked half a turn. "Keep this. Don't give it to anyone, don't show it to anyone, not here, not ever in this town. Do you understand? If anyone asks how we're related, I'm a cousin. Distant. We're not uncle and niece and nephew, we're nothing at all. Can you do that?"

"Why?" Tomas said. First words. His voice was rusted from disuse.

Cyrus crouched down so he was smaller than either of them, which was a thing adults almost never did, and Marta added it to the ledger. "Because there are people," he said, "who would be very interested in a man who has family. Family is a handle. They can pick a man up by his family and carry him wherever they want. So we tell everyone you're strangers lodging here. Strangers who work for their keep. All right?"

Tomas looked at Marta. Marta looked at the man crouching on the floorboards of a strange shop in a town at the end of the map, and thought: *he is afraid, and he is telling us the truth about being afraid, and nobody since Mama has told us the truth about anything.*

"All right," she said.

He showed them the stairs.

* * *

The loft above the chandlery was low-ceilinged and smelled of hemp and salt, one long room under the rafters with a ship-shaped shadow of a window looking out over rooftops toward the harbor, where masts stood in the fog like a forest of bare trees. There was a cot, a straw pallet, a stove small as a hatbox, a table, two chairs. To Marta, who had slept in a hay barn, in a church vestry, in the luggage rack of a goods wagon while soldiers walked the length of it, it looked like a palace pretending modestly to be a shed.

"You'll sleep here," Cyrus said. "I'm across the landing in a smaller room, when I'm here. Some nights I won't be. You won't ask where I am. You'll bolt this door behind me and unbolt it for no one but my knock." He rapped a pattern on the rail of the cot — two, pause, three. "That one only. Anyone else, any pattern, any voice, you stay quiet and you stay hidden. There's a crawl space behind the chimney stack. Blankets are already there. You've done this before, I think."

It wasn't a question, so Marta didn't answer it. "You've done it before too," she said.

"A little." Something crossed his face. "Your mother wrote to me in September. Did you know that?"

"No."

"She said if anything ever happened, I was to come. I was—" He stopped. Started again. Marta watched a man lose an argument with himself and win a different one. "I came too late. For her. I intend not to be late for you. That's the whole of it. That's everything I'll promise tonight, and I keep few promises, so the ones I make will hold."

Tomas was standing by the window with his arms wrapped around himself, looking down at the harbor. "Is it true the big ship goes across?" he asked. "The Atlas. Across to the other side. Where it's safe."

Cyrus was quiet a moment. "It's true she sails south when the season ends. Whether it's safe on the other side—" he shook his head. "Safer. Nothing is safe. But safer, yes, and warmer, and there's work. That's the plan. We go when she goes. Until then we're quiet, we're small, we're strangers. Can you be strangers?"

"I'm good at it," Marta said.

Tomas said nothing, which was his way of saying yes.

Cyrus went down and came back with bread, dark bread and hard cheese and a pot of honey with a spoon standing in it, and set it on the table without ceremony, the way you'd set out tools, and Marta understood that too — understood he was feeding them fast and plain on purpose, no feast to frighten them with questions, no fussing to make them feel like charity. She ate and watched Tomas eat and made herself chew slowly because Tomas copied her pace, always, and if she wolfed it he would make himself sick.

Halfway through the honey, Tomas stopped. Put the spoon down. His face had gone the color of the tallow candle.

"Tomas—"

"I'm fine," he said, in the flat voice that meant he was not fine, and then he was sick in the corner by the stove, violently, everything he'd eaten, and cried without sound while he did it, because somewhere in the last year he had learned to cry silently and Marta hated whoever taught him that more than she had ever hated anyone, including the men with rifles, including the woman at the fish-knocker door.

Cyrus didn't rush at him. That was the first correct thing. He got a rag and water and cleaned the floor himself, on his knees, while Tomas sat shaking on the pallet, and he said nothing about it at all except, when he'd finished, quietly, to the stove rather than to Tomas: "My sister — your mother — she couldn't keep anything down on the boat that brought her out of Vistula when she was seventeen. Six weeks crossing. Bread and river water and most of it back up. She used to say the sea keeps what it takes a liking to." He wrung the rag. "She got her sea legs eventually. You will too, when we sail. And till then, we eat slow, and small, and often. That's the trick of it. Small and often."

Tomas looked at him from under his hair. "Was she really seasick?"

"Terribly. Once threw a whole officer's hat over the rail because he laughed at her."

A sound came out of Tomas that took Marta a full second to recognize as a laugh — small, cracked, rusty, but a laugh, the first in weeks — and she saw Cyrus hear it too, and saw him go very still, guarding it, the way you guard a flame in wind.

Later, when the stove was fed and the loft was warm for the first time in Marta's memory of warm, Cyrus spread blankets on the cot for Tomas and pointed Marta to the pallet by the window — the better spot near the heat given to the boy, the watch-spot by the window given to her, which was another correct thing, because Marta would not have slept anyway unless she could see out. He banked the fire. At the door he stopped.

"Marta."

"Yes."

"The paper. And the knocking pattern. And the crawl space. I know it feels like a great many rules for people who've just arrived." His slate eyes were steady in the dying light. "I want you to know the rules aren't walls. They're rigging. They exist so the thing holds together in bad weather. That's all they are."

"Do you say that to everyone you hide?"

"I've never hidden anyone before," he said, and left, and she heard his boots on the stair, and the bolt sliding home under her own hand, and lay in the dark listening to the harbor bells counting the hour in fog-muffled strokes.

Three doors in one year, she thought. Blue-gray, brass fish, green. She fell asleep doing sums she had no names for: whether shelter could be weighed against strangeness, whether a rule could be measured in cubits of wall or fathoms of rigging, and where on the scale a man who cleaned up your brother's sickness on his knees ought to stand.

* * *

Morning came gray and early, and with it the sounds of the shop waking below — a bar scraped back, crates dragged, a customer's voice negotiating the price of oakum — and Marta woke with her heart hammering before she remembered where she was, and then lay still, memorizing the rafters, putting the place into herself plank by plank the way you swallow medicine in sips.

They worked. That was the arrangement Cyrus laid out over the last of the bread: the chandler downstairs, a widower named Herrgott who asked no questions because Cyrus paid his rent in silence and cash, believed the children were a cousin's family fleeing the northern disorders, which was true enough to survive inspection. In exchange for the loft, hands. Marta could sweep, count stock, run deliveries along the waterfront where a child attracted no eyes. Tomas could sort and coil small lines, which was mostly sitting still, which Tomas was excellent at.

It was on the second morning, carrying a parcel of sail needles down to the pier-head, that Marta first saw the harbor properly. Fog had lifted just enough, and there between the mole heads lay the fleet of the stranded world — fishing boats, coastal traders, and there, biggest of all, black-sided and patient, the mail steamer herself, the Atlas, funnels cold, riding at her berth like a promise with a date on it. People crowded the quay railing looking at her the way starving people look through bakery glass. A woman beside Marta whispered to her neighbor, "*She leaves when the ice says leave, and not a day after*" — and the neighbor crossed herself.

On the third day, the drawbridge broke.

Marta was delivering rope to the net sheds when the great chains froze mid-lift with a shriek of tortured metal that carried across the whole waterfront, and the engineer's men swarmed the mechanism, and by evening the news had traveled street to street faster than the fog could follow it: the bridge was done, the road south was cut, repairs would take past the season. The town talked of nothing else. In the shop below, Herrgott sold three extra lamps and doubled his price for candles and hummed while he did it, and Marta, coiling line in the corner, understood with a cold slide in her stomach that the humming was the sound of a man whose merchandise had just become precious.

That night she stood at the loft window a long time, Tomas asleep behind her, looking down at the black water and the steamer's riding lights, twin orange eyes in the dark. Below in the street a patrol went by, boots ringing, and she counted them out of habit — four, carbines, unhurried — and when they'd passed she realized Cyrus had come up the stairs without a sound and was standing at the landing rail watching her watch the harbor.

"You should sleep," he said.

"When does the ice come?" she asked. "Here. When does it usually come?"

He was quiet a moment. "Late. Usually late. January, sometimes February."

"But not always."

"No," he said. "Not always."

She turned back to the window. Down at the quay a lantern was moving where lanterns shouldn't be, out along the mole past the point where the lamplighter's rounds ended, pausing, moving on, pausing. Watching it, Marta felt again the old familiar tightening, the door-sense, the animal arithmetic of thresholds — and then she heard behind her the small sounds of Cyrus banking the stove for the children's night, laying tomorrow's bread where they'd find it, moving through the loft with those too-careful hands making the space safe around them, and she made herself add it to the ledger anyway.

Two columns. Shelter on one side. Danger on the other. Every door she'd ever known had been one or the other, and none had ever been both.

Out on the mole, the strange lantern went dark.
