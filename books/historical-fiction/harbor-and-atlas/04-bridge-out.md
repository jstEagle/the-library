## 4. Bridge Out

Ilsa Brandt woke before the frost did.

She had kept her husband's hours for eleven months now — risen in the dark, boiled the kettle, climbed the iron stair to the bridge house with a lamp in one hand and her gloves in the other, as if Henrik had only gone out for a walk and might come back wanting his tea warm. The bridge house was a small glass room perched at the summit of the drawbridge's great frame, looking south down the causeway and north across the harbor of Verrigan, and from it she could see everything the town was: the masts in the fog, the smoke rising crooked from the chimneys, the mole curving its stone arm around the water like a man shielding a candle flame.

This morning the fog was thicker than usual, and colder. She knew what that meant before she touched the rail. The frost had come down from the hills overnight and settled on the ironwork, and when she laid her bare palm against the bridge house door it came away wet and stinging. First frost. Henrik had always marked the day on the calendar with a red cross, because first frost meant the season was turning, meant the harbor would thicken at its edges, meant the last ships of the year were already deciding whether to risk the northern route or winter where they lay.

She lit the stove in the bridge house and looked down through the gridded floor at the machinery beneath — Henrik's kingdom, all greased gears and counterweights and chains thick as her wrist, the great bascule leaves that lifted twice a day to let the masted traffic through the channel between the inner harbor and the sea road. He had maintained that machinery for nineteen years. He had died maintaining it, in fact, four hundred meters below her feet, a winch drum taking his sleeve while he worked alone on a Sunday because he trusted no apprentice to touch the tension settings. They had told her it was instant. She had chosen to believe them.

The bridge had run without complaint since then. Ilsa had learned its moods by listening — she was no engineer, but she had spent thirty years married to one, and she knew the difference between the groan of cold metal waking up and the groan of metal that was failing. This morning, when she threw the lever at half past six to lower the span for the milk cart and the mail wagon, the sound that came up through the floor was neither.

It was a shriek, high and thin, like something alive being hurt. Then a crack — not a creak, not a pop, but a *crack*, deep and structural, the sound of a tooth breaking at the root — and the whole bridge house shuddered under her boots. The near leaf froze halfway down, hanging at an angle over the channel, its far tip two meters short of the lock it was meant to seat in. Below it, the fog swallowed the water whole.

Ilsa stood very still with her hand still on the lever.

"Well," she said aloud, to nobody, in the flat voice she used for the worst things. "There it is."

* * *

By eight o'clock there were forty people on the southern approach, and by nine there were eighty, and the fog had burned off into a sky so clean and pale and blue that the whole scene might have been painted for a postcard: the broken bridge suspended in mid-fall like a dancer caught kneeling, the white frost silvering every rope and railing, the harbor behind it all glittering like hammered tin. It was, Ilsa thought grimly, exactly the sort of view that made people move here. Nobody ever put the machinery in the picture.

The engineer came up from the mainland side at ten — young Voss, who had apprenticed under Henrik for two years and had left for a better-paying post in Kellstadt the following spring. He knelt at the fracture point for a long time with a lamp and a mirror on a stick, and then he climbed down into the chain gallery, and Ilsa watched him disappear into the machinery pit and waited, because she already knew. You could tell everything by how long a man took to come back up.

Voss took forty minutes.

"It's the main trunnion," he said, wiping his hands on a rag that was already black. "The bearing seized in the night — cold contraction, and there'll have been water in the housing since autumn, I said it needed repacking in September—" He stopped himself, because he remembered whose bridge this was and who had said *it needs repacking in September* first. "The shaft's scored. The leaf dropped on a seized pin and cracked the saddle plate besides. Mrs. Brandt, you can't patch a thing like this. You can't even properly assess it until we jack the whole leaf clear."

"How long?" someone called from the crowd. Ilsa didn't turn to see who. She kept her eyes on Voss.

He looked at the fractured saddle plate instead of at anyone, the way men do when they are about to say a number they've already checked three times. "I need a new trunnion shaft. Forged, not cast. There's nothing within a hundred kilometers that size — I'd have to order it from Kellstadt foundry, maybe further. Six weeks for manufacture, weather permitting. Then transport." He paused. "The road's open, the transport's fine, but—"

"But the bridge is the road," Ilsa said.

"The bridge is the road," Voss agreed. "Southbound wagons can ford at the shallows nine kilometers east if they're willing to ruin their axles, but nothing heavy crosses until this span seats again. And Mrs. Brandt—" now he did look at her, and there was something in his face that wasn't professional at all, something almost apologetic, as though he were sorry to be the instrument that delivered it — "the sailing season closes when the harbor freezes. That's usually late January. If this winter comes early, like they're saying in the taverns—"

"Then the repairs finish after the Atlas sails," Ilsa finished for him.

Nobody answered. The crowd along the approach had gone quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet when everyone has done the same arithmetic at the same moment. Ilsa heard it anyway — the sum being computed in forty heads at once. The mail steamer *Atlas* made one departure a month from Verrigan's quay, and the December sailing would be the last before ice closed the roads of the water just as surely as the broken bridge had closed the roads of the land. Anyone who needed to leave — and there were more people in Verrigan who needed to leave than anyone liked to say aloud — had one ship, one berth market, and now no way to walk away.

A trap with a beautiful view. Henrik used to call Verrigan that affectionately, back when it was a joke. *We live in a jewel box, Ilse, and the lid shuts every winter.* He had never imagined the lid shutting on him. She hadn't imagined it shutting on the town.

"Can you work in the cold?" she asked Voss.

"I can work in anything. My hands can't." He flexed them; she saw the stiffness already, and it was barely past freezing. "Fine fitting in January cold is slow work, Mrs. Brandt. And if the inner harbor freezes early, I can't even float a barge alongside to lift the leaf. Everything gets harder by the week."

Ilsa nodded once, the way Henrik used to nod when the news was bad and complete. Then she turned to the crowd, because somebody had to, and because the widow of the bridge keeper was the closest thing to an authority standing on the approach.

"Go home," she said. "The bridge is closed to all traffic. If you have business south, do it today, because today you can still row across. Tomorrow the ice starts and even the boats will argue with you." A few people moved; most didn't. She saw their faces — the traveling draper with his sample case, the family who'd been saving for two years to take the *Atlas* to the cousin in the west, old Halvard the customs clerk who crossed twice a month to file at the regional office. They weren't looking at her. They were looking at the gap in the world where the road used to be.

"We're trapped," said the draper, not loudly, but in the carrying voice of a man who has just discovered drama. "That's what this means, doesn't it? We're all trapped here until spring."

"No," Ilsa said. "Not quite." She pointed north, across the water, to where the *Atlas* rode at her berth, black-hulled and patient, her white superstructure catching the morning light. "The sea's still open. The steamer still sails. But you'd best stop pretending the tickets will be easy to get, because starting this morning, every soul in this town who wants out is bidding for the same berths — and some of them," she added, before she could stop herself, thinking of Rhea Vance's schooner riding at anchor beyond the mole with her cargo manifest nobody ever inspected, "will sell you a place on a boat that isn't going anywhere at all."

The crowd murmured. Ilsa gathered her coat around her and went down the iron stair, and behind her the broken bridge hung over the channel in the bright cold air, kneeling, waiting, useless as an unfinished prayer.

* * *

The town hall meeting that evening was standing-room only, which in Verrigan meant seventy people and a dog, and Magistrate Corven presided from the front bench with the weary patience of a man who had clearly hoped never to see this particular assembly in his term of office.

Ilsa stood at the back. She had put on her good black dress and Henrik's wool scarf, and she had come with a purpose, which was to say nothing until the right moment. She listened to the magistrate outline the situation in careful, bloodless sentences — repair timeline, ford conditions at the eastern shallows, postal arrangements by boat until further notice — and she watched the room absorb it the way sand absorbs water: unevenly, some places pooling fast.

"The tickets," said Frau Behrens, the ferry master's wife, getting straight to it as she always did. "The *Atlas*. What does the company say about demand?"

"The company says nothing yet," Corven replied. "The December sailing has berths allocated as always. First class is largely booked. Second class has availability. Steerage—"

"Steerage is sold out," said a voice near the front, and Ilsa recognized the harbormaster's assistant, a nervous young man named Pell who handled the manifests. "Sold out as of yesterday afternoon. Every berth. And there's a list."

"A list," repeated Frau Behrens.

"A paid list. People are buying berths off other passengers. Reselling. At double, some of them. There's a man on the quay — I won't say who — offering third-party passage 'with guaranteed documentation,' and I don't think his documentation is worth the paper, and I don't think his boat exists." Pell wiped his forehead, though the hall was cold enough to see breath. "Magistrate, people are frightened, and frightened people with money are going to buy whatever's offered, and—"

"And that is fraud, and it will be prosecuted," Corven said, in the voice that had once emptied taverns. But Ilsa, watching from the back, noticed that the voice arrived a half-second after the words, like thunder after lightning, as though the man operating it had to remember to pull the cord. Something was off with the magistrate tonight. His eyes kept going to the door.

The meeting unraveled the way such meetings do — into petitions, accusations, the draper demanding compensation from somebody for something, the question of who owned the bridge (the province) and who paid for it (nobody, apparently, quickly). Ilsa waited through all of it. Then, when Corven called for adjournment, she stepped forward into the lantern light, and the room quieted, because everyone in Verrigan knew whose widow stood before them.

"Two things," she said. "First: my husband rebuilt that mechanism in 1911, and he drew every part himself. His drawings are in my house, complete, including the trunnion shaft. Mr. Voss doesn't need to wait for Kellstadt to design a replacement. He needs only to have one forged to spec. That shaves weeks off the schedule, if anyone cares to ask me for the drawings instead of talking over my head."

Corven inclined his head. "The province thanks you, Mrs. Brandt. Your husband's service continues through you."

"My husband's service stopped eleven months ago," Ilsa said evenly. "What continues is mine. Which brings me to the second thing." She let her gaze travel the room, all seventy faces and the dog. "You're all doing sums about tickets and berths and who gets out. Do them, but hear me first. When the road closed today, this town stopped being a place people pass through and became a place people are *in*. Every debt, every grudge, every secret anybody carried south for the winter is staying here now, with us, in the same box." She pulled Henrik's scarf tighter. "I kept this bridge for a year because it was his. From tomorrow I keep it because it's yours. And I'm telling you, as the woman who knows better than anyone in this room what happens when iron fails in the cold: things break quietly first. The loud crack comes later. Watch your neighbors. Watch the quay. And whoever is selling passages off the *Atlas*'s manifest — Verrigan remembers, come spring, exactly who grew rich in the winter we couldn't leave."

She walked out into the dark before anyone could applaud or jeer, and the cold took her breath at the door, sharp and clean.

* * *

She stopped at the harbor edge on the way home, as she often did, at the bench Henrik had bought for the town with money he couldn't really spare, dedicated with a small brass plate that said *FOR THOSE WHO WAIT.* Tonight the bench was frosted white and nobody sat on it, and beyond it the harbor lay flat and black between its lights, breathing fog.

The *Atlas* rode at her berth amidships of the quay, deck lamps burning yellow, a thin thread of coal smoke standing straight up from her funnel in the windless air. Loading cranes stood ready along the waterfront like patient herons. Somewhere aboard her, Ilsa knew, there were perhaps forty empty berths left, and somewhere in the sleeping town there were four hundred people who wanted one, and the number in the second group was growing daily — refugees drifting in from the south with the road cut behind them, stranded travelers, the desperate and the hunted, all washing up against the same closed harbor mouth.

And out beyond the mole, riding at anchor where the customs launches never seemed to visit, the smuggler captain Rhea Vance's schooner swung slowly on her chain, dark except for one lantern at her stern, like a cat watching a mousehole.

Ilsa sat down on the frozen bench despite the cold, because Henrik had sat here, and pulled the scarf over her mouth, and thought about the arithmetic of winters. The bridge would take eight weeks, if the drawings helped, if the foundry didn't fail, if the cold relented. The harbor would freeze in five, if the early signs meant anything — and they did; she had lived beside this water long enough to read its mood, and tonight it lay under the frost like something holding its breath.

Which left a window. Three weeks, maybe less. One ship. One departure. And a town full of people — herself among them, she admitted, though she'd never once said it aloud — who had reasons to be elsewhere, and a growing number of others who had decided that what belonged to the departing also belonged to them.

The lantern on Vance's schooner went out.

In the sudden darkness Ilsa felt the dread settle into her properly at last, not as a thought but as a weight, the way frost forms: silently, evenly, on everything at once. She rose, stamped feeling back into her feet, and turned toward home — and stopped, because halfway down the quay a lamplighter was hurrying the wrong direction, toward the chandlery at the harbor's end, where a knot of figures had gathered under the eaves and voices were rising. Someone was pointing at the water. Someone else was running for a boat hook.

Something had come in on the tide.

Ilsa stood a moment longer in the middle of the empty quay, the broken bridge kneeling at her back and the frozen harbor lying before her, and then she followed the lamplighter down toward the light, because in a town that had just become a trap, whatever washed ashore next was everyone's business now.
