## 5. A Ledger of Debts

The letter arrived on the morning coach, which meant it had crossed the drawbridge for the last time that season, and Magistrate Corven did not yet know it.

He was at his desk before seven, as he had been every working morning for nineteen years, with the harbor fog pressed against the window like a face trying to read over his shoulder. The stove ticked. His clerk, Pell, had laid out the post in two stacks — official and personal — though the personal stack had grown so thin over the years it was less a stack than an apology. Corven poured his coffee, cracked the wax on the first official envelope, and read the name of the sender twice before the words arranged themselves into meaning.

*Ministry of the Interior. Provisional Affairs Section.*

The republic's ministry, he thought, in a republic that no longer answered its mail.

He read on. A courier bearing seals of the old republican legation had been traced as far as the northern coast. Given the disruption of the armistice and the dispersal of legation assets, regional magistrates were instructed to be alert to documents of state significance entering their jurisdictions through unofficial channels. Any such materials were to be secured for inspection. Discretion was emphasized. Twice.

Corven set the letter down and looked at it for a long moment, the way a man looks at a snake that has come into his house through a door he believed locked.

It should have been nothing. It *was* nothing — a routine circular sent to every magistrate on the coast, generated by some ministry clerk who had never seen Verrigan and could not have found it on a map unless the map was printed sideways. In nineteen years he had received four such circulars, and three had concerned smugglers who were already dead.

But the fourth line of the letter said something the others had not. *Intelligence suggests the courier may have entered your jurisdiction within the past fortnight.*

Someone knew. Someone in some ministry office, working from manifests and informers' reports, had drawn a pencil line to Verrigan and written his name beside it.

Corven drank his coffee. It had gone cold without his noticing, which was happening more often lately — whole minutes passing beneath him like bridges he'd slept through crossing.

*Discretion,* the letter said. He folded it, put it in the drawer where he kept things that would destroy him if they were found, and noted with a dull, familiar horror that the drawer now required two hands to close.

* * *

The trouble with guilt, Corven had learned, was not that it weighed on you. Anyone could carry weight. The trouble was that it *interpreted*. Every document, every conversation, every knock at his door arrived pre-loaded with menace, and he could no longer read any text innocently. The circular was a circular until it wasn't. A question about shipping schedules was a question about shipping schedules until the man asking it smiled too slowly. For three years now, since the night in Rhea Vance's cabin when he had signed what he told himself was a courtesy and understood only later was a confession, every honest act had become a loaded gun pointed at his own head.

Do his duty, investigate the courier, and he might find the man — and whatever the man carried — and hand it all upward, cleanly, and be praised. And Rhea Vance would learn within a day that her magistrate had gone honest, and she would open the ledger she kept, and the first entry in that ledger was a receipt for eleven crates that had never contained brandy, witnessed by his own signature, dated eight months before the last smuggler he had ever proudly prosecuted went to the gallows believing, as everyone believed, that Aldous Corven could not be bought.

Refuse to investigate, bury the circular under other papers, and he was no longer a magistrate at all but a tollgate with a man's face painted on it.

He spent the morning hearing cases and did his job well, because that was the cruelest part — he was still good at it. A fishwife accused of shorting weights, cleared when he noticed her scale had been salted by a competitor. Two brothers disputing a mooring rights inheritance, settled with a compromise neither wanted and both accepted, because Corven had a way of making men feel heard even when he was ruling against them. Old women came to him with grievances they wouldn't bring to a priest. *An honest man,* they called him, to his face, in the market, and each time the word landed like a coin dropped into a collection plate he hadn't earned the right to pass.

At noon, Pell brought the docket for the afternoon and lingered.

"The harbor master's lists," Pell said. "You asked for the landings this month."

Had he asked? He had. Three days ago, before the letter, when it had been mere diligence. Now the request sat in his memory like evidence someone else had planted.

"Anything irregular?" Corven asked, keeping his voice at the flat register he used for verdicts.

"One thing." Pell laid the list down and tapped a line with a dry finger. "The *Meridian*, coasting packet out of Halvern. She put in nine days ago with twelve souls aboard, logged six. Harbor master assumed clerical error — those Halvern masters can't count their own fingers. But then she cleared out same tide, no explanation filed, and the pilot says she rode high leaving. Lighter than she came, or lighter than she claimed coming in."

Six souls logged, twelve carried. Or twelve logged, six carried, and something else entirely riding in between. Corven felt the familiar sensation, which he had once called instinct and now recognized as fear wearing instinct's coat, that the world was arranging itself around a center he was forbidden to look at directly.

"A passenger put ashore," he said.

"Possibly. Nothing in the alien register. No lodging house on the waterfront reported taking anyone new — except—" Pell hesitated, and Corven watched him hesitate, and hated how quickly his mind ran ahead to betrayal, to the thought *Pell knows, Pell has always known*, before reason caught up. Pell was sixty years old and loyal the way furniture is loyal. "Except the chandlery took a lodger early in the month. Quiet fellow. Carpenter, supposedly, though Ostrand at the chandlery says he's never once seen him saw anything. Mends crates at the docks. Pays cash."

"Carpenters are scarce in winter," Corven said. "Cash is scarcer."

"That's what I thought, sir."

That's what I thought, sir. The sentence hung in the cold office air. Nine days ago, a ship carrying double her manifest had put a quiet man with too much money ashore in his jurisdiction, at nearly the same season the ministry wrote to say a courier bearing republican seals might have entered it. Any competent magistrate would already be drafting a warrant for a discreet search of waterfront lodgings. Corven picked up his pen. Put it down. Picked it up again.

"Nothing further," he said. "Leave the lists."

Pell left. Corven sat with the pen in his hand and understood, with total clarity, exactly what he was doing: he was deciding whether a man he had never met deserved the protection of the law, and the answer depended entirely on whether protecting him would cost Corven himself. That was what corruption was, finally. Not the taking of money. He'd taken no money, not ever, not one coin — Rhea had been subtler than that, and crueler. Corruption was this: sitting with a pen over a warrant, weighing a stranger's life against your own reputation, and finding that the scales moved too easily.

He drafted the warrant anyway. Halfway through, his hand stopped at the phrase *any premises, vessels, or persons within the waterfront district*, and he heard Rhea Vance's voice from that night in her cabin, amused and low: *Aldous. You write beautiful warrants. I've read them all.*

She had read them all. Which meant she would read this one, because there was no longer any meaningful difference between his desk and her table, and a sweep of the waterfront would turn up her cargo as readily as his courier, and she had made him understand — without ever saying it in so many words — what happened to magistrates who searched where they shouldn't. There had been a magistrate in Corvane, two ports south. Honest man, by all accounts. Fell down a wet stairway. The stairway had been dry for a week, witnesses said afterward, but no one had said so at the time.

Corven fed the half-drafted warrant to the stove and watched the edges of the paper go bright and curl inward, and thought: *this is the second thing I have burned instead of doing.*

The first had been larger and could not be burned, only signed.

* * *

He walked the harbor in the afternoon because walking the harbor was what he did, had always done — the people's magistrate, boots on the stones, hat brim dripping, visible. Nineteen years of it had made the habit a kind of armor. Today the same walk was reconnaissance, and he was appalled at how easily one became the other, how thin the membrane was between service and surveillance.

The inner harbor lay gray and still under the first hard cold. Dockhands worked in huddles, breath steaming. At the crate yard he saw, without seeming to, the carpenter — a lean man mending packing cases with a neatness that no dockside laborer possessed, hands moving with the economy of someone trained to fine work and hiding it. Corven slowed. Made himself slow further, made himself stop and exchange words with the yard foreman about ice on the apron, ordinary words, while behind the foreman's shoulder the stranger worked on, unremarkable, deliberately unremarkable, and Corven thought with something close to grief: *I know that craft. That is the craft of a man whose life depends on being nobody.* He had seen it before, in fugitives and informants and once, long ago, in a mirror.

The man looked up. Their eyes met for perhaps half a second — gray eyes, watchful, already moving on — and Corven walked away with his heart going harder than any smuggler's stare had ever made it go.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not escape, the sum that ate at him all the way up the hill to the courthouse as the light failed: if the courier was this quiet carpenter, then everything flowed from what Corven did next, and every branch of the decision tree ended in ruin for someone. Report upward honestly, and the machinery of two ministries would descend on Verrigan, and among the papers dredged up in the sweep would be eleven crates and one signature. Protect the man by doing nothing, and if the codes passed through Rhea's hands — and everything in Verrigan passed through Rhea's hands eventually — they would buy her the kind of leverage that didn't expire, and she owned him already, but owning a magistrate's silence and owning a nation's ciphers were different animals, and he did not want to learn what she would become with the second animal in her hold.

And underneath both calculations, in the flooded basement where he kept the things he admitted only at night, was the truest one: somewhere in this town was a man carrying papers for a country that had died, still faithful to a corpse, still risking his life for a government that could no longer reward or punish or even thank him. And Aldous Corven, who had once wept at the reading of the abdication decree, who had prosecuted smugglers in the republic's name because he believed the republic was the best thing men had built on this cold coast — that man looked at the living ghost of his own dead faith and could not afford to salute it.

Every honest act now threatened to expose him. So the dishonest ones must multiply to hide in. It was that simple, and he had run out of ways to pretend it was complicated.

* * *

Evening brought frost ferns on the courthouse windows and one visitor Corven had not invited and could not refuse, because refusing Rhea Vance required a version of himself that had died in her cabin three winters ago.

She came to his chambers ostensibly about mooring disputes — her ships generated a steady drip of them, manufactured the way a mill generates flour — and dismissed Pell with a smile that Pell obeyed and would not remember obeying. Then she stood at the window looking down at the black water and said, without preamble:

"You received the ministry letter."

His stomach dropped through the floor joists and into the harbor. "You open my post."

"I don't have to." She turned. Small woman, sea-colored eyes, the kind of pleasant face that made her ruthlessness a constant surprise even to people who knew better. "Your clerk mentions it to the harbor master, the harbor master mentions it to my purser over cards, my purser mentions it to me. Your town talks to me, Aldous. It has for years. That's rather the point of me." She let that sit, then, gently, almost kindly: "So. A courier with republican seals. Right here in your little parish. You must be delighted. All those instincts, all that integrity — finally something worthy of them."

"What do you want, Madame Vance?"

"Nothing tonight." She straightened his inkstand, which was already straight, a small proprietary violence. "Only to remind you, as a friend, that the season is closing. Bridge is broken — you knew that? Of course you did. Whole town sealed up like a jar until spring. Everyone in the jar together. Whatever that man is carrying, it isn't going anywhere." At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back with an expression he had learned to dread more than any threat, because it was genuine. "Whatever you decide, Aldous, decide it knowing I'll read the decision either way. I always do. Sleep well."

The door closed. Corven stood alone in the lamplight among nineteen years of lawful judgments, and after a while he opened the drawer that needed two hands, took out the ministry letter, and read it a third time.

Then he pulled fresh paper toward him and began, in a hand he had not used since law school, to write — not a warrant, not a report, but a private memorandum, unsigned, undated, addressed to no one. *If a courier exists,* he wrote, *then proof of what he carries exists. Proof is power. Power, in Verrigan, has exactly two purchasers.* He stopped there, sickened by the direction of his own reasoning, and struck it through.

Below the strike-through, in smaller letters, he wrote the only honest sentence he had produced in three years:

*I need to reach him before she does. God help me, I need to be a magistrate again, quietly, just once, just enough.*

He folded it small and hid it inside the hollow spine of his old law dictionary, where his wife's letters used to live, and blew out the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the foghorn counting the seconds between itself and the rocks.

Down on the waterfront, in a loft above a chandlery, a man with careful hands was sleeping with a leather case sewn into his coat, and neither of them knew yet that the same woman already held the thread that bound them, and that she was patient, and that the jar was sealed.

In the morning, Corven told Pell to prepare a list of all recent arrivals to waterfront lodgings — quietly, he said, purely administrative — and watched his own hand sign the instruction, and wondered which of them Rhea would read first: the order, or the man who had ordered it.
