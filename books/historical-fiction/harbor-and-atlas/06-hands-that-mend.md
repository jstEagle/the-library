## 6. Hands That Mend

Sef Ostrand had been mending nets on the Verrigan waterfront for forty-one years, and in that time he had learned that a man's hands told the truth long before his mouth got around to it. Hands didn't know how to lie. They only knew what they had done.

So when the new one came — Dunmore, the carpenter, or so his papers said — Sef watched the hands and not the face.

The face was good. Sef would grant him that. Plain, weathered just enough to be believable, with the kind of expression that slid off memory like grease off wet slate. You could look straight at him and an hour later you couldn't have picked him out of a line of five. That was a skill. Most men didn't have it. Most men wanted to be seen.

The hands were another matter.

They came down the gangway of the *Pelican's Daughter* at half-past six with the fog still lying on the water like something drowned, and they took up the net Sef left for them — deliberately, the way Sef always left one, the way a farmer leaves a gate open to see what walks through — and they began to mend. And they mended *well*. Too well. Sheet bend, then a double sheet bend where the strain would run, then a proper flake of the repaired section instead of the sloppy coil any dockside laborer would have given it. The fingers moved the way fingers move when they've been taught by someone patient and formal and paid to teach them.

Carpenters didn't learn knots like that. Carpenters learned nails.

"Huh," Sef said, to nobody.

The new man glanced up — a flicker, no more, quickly back to the work — and Sef saw him do the arithmetic every careful man does when he realizes he has shown too much: how much was seen, whether it can be unseen, whether the watcher matters. Sef let his own face go blank and stupid, which after forty-one years it did very convincingly, and spat tobacco into the harbor, and said nothing about anything.

But he thought about it all morning.

* * *

The fog burned off by nine and the town came out from under its blanket looking rumpled and half-ashamed. Verrigan in November was a town of two hundred souls who pretended, most of the year, to be a port, and became one properly only during the steamer season, when the mail boats came up from the south and the harbor remembered it had a purpose. The season was shortening now. Everyone could feel it in the water. The gulls had gone quiet and greedy, the fish had gone deep, and the great iron drawbridge at the harbor's mouth — the town's pride, its single connection to the coast road — groaned through its openings with a sound like an old man climbing stairs.

"Bridge sounded bad last night," said Piet, the young one, who worked the winches and talked the way water runs, constantly and downhill. "My uncle says the gears is worn through. Says come a hard frost she'll seize solid."

"Your uncle says the herring are coming back every year since we were boys," Sef said. "Your uncle says a lot."

"This year though." Piet leaned on the capstan, lowering his voice with the pleasure of a boy manufacturing dread. "This year they say it might freeze early. Larsen at the lighthouse says the birds are coming down from the north already, whole rafts of them, and birds know before men do."

"Birds don't pay for passage," Sef said. "Men do. Ask the Atlas's clerk which he listens to."

The *Atlas* lay at the outer berth where she'd been since the week's start, taking on cargo slow and quarrelsome, her black hull streaked with rust, her name painted fresh over old paint so that in certain lights you could see the ghost of a previous name underneath — some other ship she had been before she was the Atlas, in some other trade. Sef had watched four seasons' worth of ships come and go and he thought there was something honest in a ship that didn't hide what it used to be.

Unlike some.

The new man — Dunmore, carpenter, lately of nowhere anyone had heard of — worked through the morning without wasting a motion. That was the other thing. Sef had watched ten thousand men work and he could count on one hand the ones who never wasted anything: not a step, not a glance, not a word. It looked like industry but it wasn't, quite. It was economy. It was the movement of a man crossing ice.

At noon the crew knocked off and went up to Halvard's for bread and beer, the way crews did, and Dunmore took out a wrapped heel of bread and ate it sitting alone on the bollards, facing the water, where he could see anyone coming from three directions.

*Alone on purpose,* Sef thought. *Facing out on purpose.*

He went and sat down next to him anyway, because curiosity in an old man is heavier than caution, and because forty-one years on this stone had taught him that the interesting ones always came to Verrigan eventually. The town sat at the end of nothing in particular, a knuckle of harbor between marsh and sea, half-forgotten by the capital, half-used by everyone else. Legitimate ships came because it was cheap. Other ships came because it was cheap and quiet and the magistrate asked comfortable questions. And sometimes — twice in Sef's lifetime that he knew of, and more often than that if you counted the war years, and he did, nightly — people came through who needed exactly those two things at once: cheap, and quiet.

"Good splicing," Sef said. "For a carpenter."

Dunmore chewed. Swallowed. "My father was a rigger," he said. "Before the yards closed."

It was a good lie. It came fast and flat and unadorned, the way lies do when they've been rehearsed until they're tired. Sef nodded slowly and looked at the water.

"Rigger," he said. "Where?"

"South. It doesn't matter now."

"No," Sef agreed. "Most places don't, anymore." He worked a wad of tobacco around his jaw. "I'll tell you something about nets, since you mend them so nice. A net doesn't hide anything. People think it does — all that mesh, all those holes. But a net is nothing *but* telling. Every knot tells you where the strain came from. Every repair tells you what hit it and when. You want to know a net's history, you read the knots. Only thing a net can't tell you—" he pointed the stub of his pipe at the harbor, at the gray water and the black hull and the white thread of foam where the tide turned, "—is what it's fishing *for*."

Dunmore looked at him then, fully, for the first time. His eyes were steady and very tired and much older than his face, and behind them something moved quickly away, like a fish under a hull.

"I'll remember that," he said.

"See you do," Sef said, and got up, joints cracking, and went back to his bench, and that was the whole conversation, and both of them understood it had not been the whole conversation at all.

* * *

Three days later the acceptance came, grudging as promised.

It happened the way these things happen on a working waterfront: somebody needed help and didn't have time to be suspicious. The *Merrow*, a coaster out of the north with a hold full of salted cod and a crew mostly hungover, lost a net over the side in a squall and dragged it up fouled past saving, and her mate stood on the quay swearing with real artistry while the tide took the mess toward the pilings. There was maybe an hour of light left. It was cut loose or it was saved.

Dunmore went down the ladder without being asked, boots first, coat off, into the cold slap of the harbor, and spent forty minutes chest-deep in the black water untangling a net from a piling while the wind tried to take his breath and the rest of the crew stood around being useless in the universal manner of men watching someone else work. When he came up the ladder, blue-lipped, hair plastered down, the net saved and flaked neatly over his shoulder, nobody cheered — that wasn't the custom — but the mate pressed a coin on him and Piet clapped him on the back hard enough to knock fog out of him, and that evening at Halvard's there was a chair left open at the table where he sat, and nobody moved it away.

Grudging. But acceptance.

Sef watched it from the end of the room with his one beer and his pipe, and he noticed what nobody else noticed, what the crew was too pleased with itself to see: Dunmore accepted the welcome exactly as much as he had to and not a drop more. He drank half a beer. He laughed twice, both times correctly, at the right moments, like a man following a score. He learned three names and used them once each. And he left before nine, alone, walking the long way along the dark quay, checking the reflections in the shop windows behind him.

"He's all right," Piet said, dropping onto the bench beside Sef. "Quiet, but all right."

"Mm."

"You don't think so?"

"I think," Sef said, knocking his pipe out against the bench leg, "that quiet is a big country, boy, and most of the people who live there didn't choose it."

Piet frowned at that, decided it was old-man talk, and went back to arguing about football. But Sef stayed a while longer, watching the lamp flame flutter in the draft under the door, thinking about countries.

He had known a man once — before the war, in the years when Sef ran things other than fish across the strait at night, small cargoes, desperate passengers, the work you did because someone had to and because it paid in the only coin that ever mattered to him — a man who had been quiet the same way. A clerk from the capital, fleeing whatever clerks flee. Quiet as held breath. He'd stayed three weeks in Verrigan waiting for a boat, and for three weeks he'd been the friendliest, most forgettable man in town, and the night the boat came, so did two men in civilian coats with government shoes, and the clerk went into the harbor between the mole heads and never came up, and the two men walked back down the coast road and were never seen again, and the town talked about it for a month and then very carefully never mentioned it again.

Sef had ferried people after that too, for another year, until the night he lost his son doing it — a story he kept where he kept all his important possessions, somewhere he never went — and then he had stopped, and grown old, and become the man who mended nets and watched hands.

But he had never stopped knowing what quiet looks like when it's running.

* * *

On the fifth day he tested him.

Not cruelly. Sef had no cruelty in him anymore; the sea had taken it out of him along with everything else. But there was a question he needed answered, and there was only one way to ask it.

He brought out the old net.

Every net-mender kept one — the impossible piece, the knot that shouldn't hold, the splice inside a splice that some dead genius had worked out fifty years ago and nobody living could reproduce. Sef's had come from a Breton skipper in exchange for passage nobody official ever learned about, and in thirty years Sef had shown it to perhaps a dozen men, and two of them had solved it, and one of those had later been hanged for reasons unrelated to knots.

He laid it on the bench next to Dunmore's work and said nothing, and went away, and mended something else, and watched sideways out of forty years of practice.

Dunmore looked at the old net for a long time. Ten minutes, maybe more. He turned it over. He traced the splice with one fingertip, reading it, and Sef saw the exact moment the reading became understanding — a small stillness in the shoulders, the stillness of recognition — and then Dunmore picked up his own twine and needle, and began, carefully, without hurry, to reproduce it.

And here was the thing. Here was the thing that turned suspicion in Sef's chest over and laid it gently down on its other side:

Halfway through, Dunmore stopped. Looked at what he was doing. Looked at the original. Then he deliberately made the next knot wrong — subtly wrong, wrong in a way that would pass casual inspection but not close inspection — wrong the way a man makes something wrong when he understands it perfectly and does not wish to be understood.

Then he tied off, set it down beside the original, and returned to his ordinary work as if nothing had happened.

Sef came over slowly. He picked up both pieces. He compared them for a long moment.

"Why'd you spoil it?" he asked quietly. "Last knot's wrong. Deliberately wrong."

Dunmore didn't deny it. He looked out at the harbor, at the *Atlas* taking on crates under the pale noon sky, and when he answered his voice was low and even.

"Because whoever ties the perfect knot is the man they're looking for," he said. "And I'm just a carpenter."

The wind came off the water and pushed the fog inland, and somewhere across the harbor the drawbridge groaned through another opening, that grinding note in the gears that everybody had started pretending not to hear. Down the quay, two men Sef didn't recognize stood talking outside the customs house — too well-dressed for fishermen, too bored for merchants, standing the way men stand when they're waiting for something rather than someone.

Sef folded the old net carefully and put it away in its canvas bag.

"There was a man came through here, years back," he said. "Quiet type. Kept to himself. Very good at being nobody." He paused. "They found him in the harbor."

Dunmore went very still. Not afraid, Sef noted. Calculating. Weighing how much this old man knew, what it cost, what it bought.

"That's a sad story," Dunmore said.

"It is. What made it sad is he almost made it. Boat was leaving the next morning." Sef settled onto his bench, took up his needle, and spoke to the twine in his hands rather than to the man, because some things are easier said to twine. "I'm not asking what you're carrying, carpenter. I'm too old to carry anything heavy, even other people's secrets, and I've buried enough of my own. I'm only going to say one thing, and you can hear it or not."

He drew the needle through, snug, true.

"The *Atlas* sails when she sails, ice or no ice. But a harbor is a trap that lets you watch yourself being caught. If the day comes when you need a door that isn't a gangway—" he jerked his chin, once, toward the row of net sheds at the far end of the quay, the third one, the one with the green door and the broken latch he'd never bothered fixing, "—old men sleep light. That's all."

Dunmore looked at him for a long moment. Whatever lived behind those careful eyes turned over, considered, and — not surrendered, but extended something. A hand across a table, in the currency of a single nod.

"That's very kind," he said.

"It isn't," Sef said. "Kindness costs. This is just interest." He went back to his mending. "Now get back to work. Piet's been watching us for ten minutes and by supper the whole waterfront will have a story about it, and it may as well be a boring one."

Dunmore almost smiled — the first true thing Sef had seen his face do — and bent to the net.

They worked through the afternoon in silence, two menders among many, the least remarkable men on the whole gray quay, and Sef found himself thinking, not unhappily, that invisibility was indeed a story, the oldest one, and he knew how it usually ended — but he had mended enough broken things in his life to know that occasionally, rarely, the story ended differently, and the quiet man walked out on the morning tide with everything he loved, and the harbor let him go.

It was that hope — thin as twine, holding more than it should — that kept him watching the water.

Which is why, three evenings later, when a small figure in a torn coat came along the tideline below the mole, bending and picking at the wrack like the scavenger children did, and stopped, and stood frozen over something half-buried in the weed, and then ran — ran home, Sef would learn, toward the chandlery, carrying something dark and dripping in both arms — the old man's hands went still on the netting needle, and he watched her go, and felt the whole quiet season begin, very slowly, to tighten.
