## 7. What the Tide Brought

Marta had learned the harbor's moods the way other children learned their catechism: by repetition, in cold, with consequences for error.

Low tide was generous. High tide kept its secrets. And a wind out of the north — the kind that came down off the ice fields and made the fishermen swear and spit into their collars — pushed things ashore that the sea would otherwise have kept. Uncle Cyrus didn't know she went to the tideline. He thought she went for bread, because she carried the bread basket, and the bread basket made her respectable, and respectable girls walking an errand did not draw the eye of dock police or of anyone else whose attention might travel up the chain from Marta to the chandlery loft where four people lived on three lies.

She went for bread first. That part was true. But then she went along the mole, past the last bollard, down onto the shingle below the sea wall where the north wind's leavings collected — and this morning it had brought a harvest.

A hatch cover, tarred and sound. A coil of rope, salt-stiff but salvageable. Two bottles, one whole. A crate end stamped with letters she couldn't read, which would burn nicely. Marta gathered these the way she gathered everything, with quick hands and quicker eyes, sorting value from rubbish in a glance. It was not greed. Tomas needed boots; the soles of his old ones had gone soft as porridge, and Noor said you could patch them with tarred canvas if they had any, and they didn't. Everything on the tideline was boots, if you looked at it right.

The fog had come in low over the water, and the gulls were loud about something.

Marta noticed the gulls before she noticed anything else. They were gathered at the tide line a hundred yards down, where the shingle gave way to a shelf of dark rock, and they were not diving and they were not fighting and they were not doing any of the loud greedy things gulls did. They were standing back. Watching. The way the dogs in the fish market stood back from the eel cart — knowing there was something there, waiting to see what it would cost them.

She should have gone home. She knew that even then, some part of her knew it, the part that had learned on three borders that a crowd meant danger and a silence meant worse. But Tomas needed boots, and the crate end was heavy and good, and she was already moving along the tideline with her arms full of salvage when she saw what the gulls were watching.

A man lay on his back among the rocks, one arm flung above his head like he'd been reaching for something just out of grasp.

Marta stood very still. Her breath went out of her in a small white cloud and hung there.

Dead men came ashore sometimes; Sef had told them that the first week, matter-of-fact as a weather report. The winter sea took sailors and the spring gave some of them back, and a body on the beach was sad but it wasn't strange. What you did was tell a grown-up, and the grown-up told the harbormaster, and the harbormaster's men came with a handcart, and everyone pretended not to look, and that was all. That was the whole of it. She turned to go and do exactly that.

Then she saw the case.

It was strapped across the dead man's chest, or it had been — the strap had half torn away, and the case itself lay against him at an angle, black leather, water-swollen, with a brass clasp and a seal pressed into a wax disc on its face. The man's hand had been locked around the strap. Even now, even with whatever the sea had done to him, his fingers were curled through the leather like a child holding a mother's skirt.

Marta knew the seal.

She wanted to pretend she didn't. She wanted to be a child who saw only a pretty stamp, some shipping company's mark, a crest of no meaning. But she had watched Uncle Cyrus sew a leather case into the lining of his coat with a sailmaker's needle, working by candlelight after the children slept, his face the color of tallow, and she had seen the wax disc he melted and pressed with a signet ring he kept wrapped in a sock at the bottom of his bag. She had never been supposed to see either thing. Children were not supposed to see most of what they saw.

The seals matched. Not nearly. Not approximately. Matched — the same crossed anchor and broken star, the same odd double border, the same everything, down to the little chip in the lower left corner of the die, because dies wore the way everything wore, and whoever had cut the dead man's seal had cut it from the same worn stamp that had sealed her uncle's case.

The wind came off the water and moved her hair across her face and she did not brush it back. She could hear her own heart in her ears, faster than the waves.

*Take it,* said the part of her mind that had fed Tomas all autumn. *Take it home. Whatever's in it is worth something, and worth something means boots, and bread, and coal, and a ticket on the Atlas for everyone, and no more arithmetic at night when she thought no one could hear her counting coins against mouths.*

*Leave it,* said the other part, the older part, the one that had crossed borders with her eyes down. *Things that men hold onto while they are drowning are the things that got them drowned.*

The gulls shifted and resettled. Somewhere behind her, up on the mole road, a cart rattled past, and a man's voice called out something about the ice coming early this year, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary twenty feet above her head.

Marta set down her salvage. She walked to the rocks. She knelt beside the dead man the way you kneel beside a sleeping stranger, ready to run.

He was young. Younger than Uncle Cyrus. His coat was good once — a courier's coat, she realized, or close enough, cut for riding and travel, with reinforced shoulders — and his boots were better than anyone's in Verrigan, which seemed like a terrible joke. His face had gone the way faces went in cold water, and she made herself look at it anyway, because Noor had taught her that: look at the face, remember the face, a person you have looked at is harder to forget than a person you've glanced away from. Someone somewhere had known this face. Someone might come looking.

His fingers were still threaded through the strap.

Marta worked them loose one at a time, and each finger gave with a stiffness that made her stomach turn over, and she whispered *sorry, sorry, sorry* under her breath without deciding to, the words coming out small and automatic, an apology to the man and maybe to God and maybe mostly to herself. Then the strap was free and the case was hers and she was standing again, backing away across the shingle with the black leather held tight against her chest, cold seeping through the wet wool of her coat from the case itself, as if the sea had gotten into it and intended to stay.

She took one step toward the stairs up to the mole road and stopped.

The harbormaster's men would come for the body. They always did, eventually — a fisherman would report it, or the gulls' behavior would draw someone else down the shingle, and then there would be questions. *Did you see anything? Did you touch anything? Who else has been here?* And if the case was gone — if the one thing a dead man had died holding was missing — then the questions would stop being routine. Questions about a drowned stranger stayed routine forever, because nobody cared. Questions about a missing dispatch case traveled. They climbed stairs. They knocked on doors.

But if she left the case here, someone else would find it. And whoever found it would open it, or sell it, or bring it to the magistrate, or bring it to Rhea Vance's people, who paid best and asked worst, and the seal on its face would lead every one of those roads back to the same place. To the loft above the chandlery. To the coat lining. To Uncle Cyrus, who had done nothing wrong that Marta knew of except carry something somebody wanted badly enough to drown for.

The two cases were the same case. She understood that the way she understood tides and bread prices, completely and without wanting to. Whatever was chasing this dead man had been following them since before Verrigan. The sea had just delivered the proof to her feet, gift-wrapped in fog, and left it to an eleven-year-old to decide what to do about it.

Down on the shingle, alone with the gulls and the drowned courier, Marta Dunmore made the choice that children make and adults spend years paying for: she picked the dangerous thing up and carried it home, because home was where you carried things, and she was eleven, and the boots were real, and the fear was far away, and the fog made her feel invisible, which was the closest thing to safe that she knew.

* * *

Getting the case up the cliff path unseen was easier than it should have been. The north wind had emptied the mole road; sensible people were indoors, and the fish cellars vented steam near the bottom of the stairs, and Marta simply became a girl carrying a heavy basket of salvage, hunched and unremarkable, the case wedged beneath the tarred hatch cover where nobody looks twice. Invisibility, she had learned from Uncle Cyrus without him ever teaching it, was mostly a matter of posture.

Noor was the one who saw her come in.

That was the problem with Noor, and also the reason Marta loved her: she missed nothing. Marta came up the loft ladder with her cheeks burning red from the wind and her arms aching, and Noor glanced up from the net she was mending — everyone mended nets now, it was how you earned your keep without questions — and her hands stopped moving.

"What's under the wood?"

"Wood," Marta said.

"Marta."

Tomas was out with Sef, learning to splice, which meant the loft was empty except for the two of them and the smell of lamp oil and dried kelp. Uncle Cyrus was at the docks, working, counting Atlas berths in his head the way he counted everything. There was perhaps an hour. Maybe less.

Marta lifted the hatch cover off and set the case on the floor between them, and Noor went very still, the way she'd gone still the first time a border guard had walked the length of the wagon she was hiding in. Then she crouched, slowly, and read the seal with her eyes, and Marta watched her understand it — watched the recognition move across her face like weather, because Noor had crossed three borders and knew documents the way farmers knew clouds.

"Where." Noor's voice was flat. Flat meant scared; Marta knew that too.

"The tideline. Under the point." She swallowed. "There was a man. He was — he'd been in the water."

"Was he wearing a courier's coat? Good boots?"

"Yes."

Noor closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, she was older than seventeen. "Then somewhere in this town, someone is already asking who he was, and where he came from, and whether he was traveling alone." She touched the seal with one fingertip, barely, the way you touch a stove you've been burned by before. "Does your uncle know?"

"Not yet."

"We can't hide this from him."

"I know." Marta's chin came up. "But we can't give it away either. If I put it back, Rhea Vance gets it, or the magistrate does, or —"

"You don't know what's in it."

"No. But I know what the seal means. Don't you?"

Noor didn't answer, which was an answer. She sat back on her heels and pulled her knees up and looked at the case the way you look at a trap you're already standing in. Outside, the fog was thickening, pressing against the loft's one window, turning the lamplight yellow and small. Somewhere down at the docks a bell rang twice — the signal that a ship had signaled the harbor, and both of them jumped at the sound, and that jumping told Marta exactly how much trouble they were already in, before anyone knew, before anyone had done anything at all.

"He was still holding it," Marta said quietly. "When I found him. His fingers were through the strap. He held onto it all the way down, and all the way back." Her throat tightened. "Why would you do that? Why wouldn't you let go, if it was dragging you?"

Noor was quiet a long moment. "Because letting go wouldn't have helped him," she said finally. "Not really. Men like that — the thing follows them anyway. It follows whoever they gave it to, or whoever they took it from." She looked at Marta. "Or whoever finds them."

The lamp flickered. Below them, faintly, through the floorboards, came the sound of the chandlery door, and boots on stairs, and a familiar careful tread that Marta knew better than her own heartbeat.

Uncle Cyrus, home early.

Noor rose in one motion and put herself between the case and the ladder, which made no sense and perfect sense at once — as if a girl with nothing but speed and stubbornness could stand between Cyrus Dunmore and the sea's worst news. Marta scrambled up beside her, and they stood shoulder to shoulder in the yellow light like soldiers of some army of two, listening to the tread climb toward them.

The ladder creaked. A hand appeared at the top rail, then the face above it — tired, watchful, softened for one unguarded second at the sight of them waiting for him — and then Marta opened her mouth to say something, anything, some ordinary word that could hold the world shut a moment longer, and failed, and Cyrus Dunmore looked from her face to Noor's to the black leather case sitting in the middle of the floor, and Marta watched the color leave him like water leaving a lock.

He climbed the rest of the ladder without a word. He knelt. He turned the case until the seal faced him, and his thumb came to rest on the chip in the lower-left corner of the wax, the way a man touches a scar he thought he'd left behind in another country.

"Where," he said.

"The tideline," Marta whispered. "There was a man."

Cyrus Dunmore closed his eyes. And in the small yellow circle of lamplight, with the fog thick against the glass and the harbor bell ringing again below, he began, very quietly, to pray.
