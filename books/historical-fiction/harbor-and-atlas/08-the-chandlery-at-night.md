## 8. The Chandlery at Night

The chandlery below went quiet an hour after dark, the way harbors do — not all at once but in layers, first the carts, then the voices, then last of all the water itself slapping at the pilings like something patient waiting to be let in. Cyrus sat on the edge of his cot in the loft with a stub of candle between his knees and listened until he was certain the building was only breathing.

Below him, Marta and Tomas slept in the nest of sacking behind the coil of hemp line, Tomas with his fist closed around the length of rope Cyrus had given him that evening, as if even in sleep he meant to hold on to something. The boy had cried out twice already tonight. Both times Marta had soothed him before Cyrus could move, murmuring in the low, practiced voice of a girl who had stopped being a girl somewhere on the road south of the border. Nine years old and she had her sister's trick of making fear sound like a lullaby.

Cyrus waited another quarter hour anyway. He had learned patience in rooms like this one — attics in Brast, a cellar in Ostenfeld, a hayloft whose owner had died before the hay was sold — and patience was the only currency he had never spent down to nothing.

Then he reached beneath the loose board at the back of the chimney breast and drew out the drowned man's case.

It was smaller than his own, this one, though made of the same gray leather, the same brass hasp gone green at the edges from its long bath in the tideline. Water stains bloomed across it like continents on a map no one intended to draw. Marta had carried it home cradled against her chest the way children carry anything — treasure, dead bird, bread — without the least idea what it weighed. *Look what the tide brought, Uncle.* She had been proud of her find. She had wanted to open it at once, right there on the shingle with the gulls screaming at her for her prize.

He had told her it was ship's property, that he would see it returned to the harbormaster. A lie delivered smoothly enough that she had believed it, and believing it, surrendered it. That was the worst part, he thought now, turning the case over in his hands. Not the lying. He had lied so long and so well that lying had become a kind of native language. The worst part was how easily they trusted him — these two half-starved children who had crossed three hundred miles on the strength of a note in his sister's hand — and how little of himself there was underneath the lie to deserve it.

*You're worth killing twice now,* said the arithmetic in his head, in the flat voice he used for such sums. *Once for your own case. Once for hers.*

His sister's seal was stamped into the wax still holding the hasp — or rather, the Republic's seal, which had been her seal, which had been the seal of every clerk and courier and minister of a government that existed now only in dispatch cases and in the memories of men like Cyrus who had nowhere else to put their loyalty. The eagle-and-anchor, struck deep, the wax cracked but not broken. The sea had gotten to the leather but not through it. Whatever was inside was dry.

He worked the point of his knife under the wax and paused.

Outside, far off across the frozen mud of the harbor flats, a dog began to bark, considered its opinion, and gave it up. A shutter banged twice somewhere up the hill where the magistrate's court kept its lamps burning later than any honest house needed to. Cyrus had noticed those lamps his first week in Verrigan. He had told himself it was nothing — clerks catching up on ledgers, a magistrate fond of late work. But he had spent four days now mending crates within sight of the customs house, and he had counted the men who came and went at strange hours, and he did not like the proportions of what he'd tallied.

*Open it,* said one voice, the old courier's voice, the voice that had carried codes across borders for six years because someone had to and he had been good at it.

*Burn it unopened,* said the newer voice, the dockhand's voice, the voice that had learned to want nothing except a berth on the Atlas and a head start on spring.

He opened it.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its work perfectly, lay two things: a packet of papers bound with black thread, and a letter sealed separately, addressed in a small precise hand to *The Legation, upon arrival.* No name. The legation it named belonged to a country that had surrendered in the spring, which meant the letter was addressed the way prayers are addressed — to somewhere that might or might not be listening.

Cyrus set the letter aside and unwrapped the packet.

He knew what he would find before he saw it. He knew the format the way a priest knows liturgy: tables of paired groups, five figures to a group, keyed to a book both sender and receiver kept under lock. Book codes, renewed monthly, valid — if the schedule held — through February. Beneath the tables, a single sheet in cipher listing the renewal dates, which was itself enciphered, which meant whoever broke this case would need either a cryptographer or the key book, and the key book had gone down with the ministry.

Except.

He spread the pages across his knees and read them with the cold professional part of his mind, the part that had never once trembled, and the cold part found what he had been afraid it would find. These were not duplicates of what he carried. They were *complementary*. His own case held the diplomatic traffic — instructions, credentials, the republic's last official words to its allies. This one held something better. Or worse. These were the financial tables. Account numbers. Routing instructions for funds that had left the capital in the final weeks, dispersed across banks in four neutral countries, awaiting claimants who could prove themselves with exactly this cipher.

A fortune, waiting. Enough to buy passage for a family of four on a private yacht, with change enough to buy the yacht. Enough to buy a pardon, in a country where pardons were for sale. Enough, held by the wrong hands, to fund whatever Rhea Vance and her kind imagined the lawless sea might become.

And — he turned to the second-to-last page and felt his stomach drop through the floorboards into the salt water below — enough, decoded by the new government that now sat in the republic's ministries, to identify every account, seize every fund, and trace every claimant. Every courier. Every loyal servant who had run carrying paper instead of running carrying nothing.

His name would be in the index. It was certainly in his own case, under his working name; it might be in this one under another. Two cases, and together they were a complete map of everything the old republic had been in its last month of life: its words and its money, its promises and its accounts. Separately, each was dangerous. Together, they were worth more than the town of Verrigan.

He sat very still for a long time, the candle flame standing straight as a nail in the dead air, and let the calculation finish itself, because unfinished calculations were how men died — they hesitated at the moment when hesitation looked like caution and was really just delay.

If he kept both cases, he became the richest target in the harbor. Smugglers, magistrates, agents of the new government — all of them would be looking, sooner or later, for a drowned courier's missing partner, and the drowned man had been fished out of *their* tideline. Sooner or later someone would ask: a courier comes ashore dead, but couriers don't swim alone; where is the other one? And the answer would walk up the hill wearing a carpenter's coat, and two children would be left in a loft above a chandlery, waiting for an uncle who had promised.

If he destroyed them — both cases, both packets, the whole archive of a dead nation's last secrets — then he became worthless. Unfindable. A dockhand with a forged carpenter's ticket and nothing in his pockets but lint. The hunting would pass over him like weather. He and Marta and Tomas and Noor—

Noor. He had not thought about the girl since the commotion at the Atlas's hold two days past, since she'd been put ashore with her bundle and her stubborn chin. He had given her a copper and directions to nobody in particular. He should have known better than to imagine anyone in Verrigan stayed lost unless they chose to.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight's problem was in his hands.

He rose, took the candle to the stove — the little iron potbelly in the corner that heated the loft badly and ate coal steadily — and opened its door. The coals banked overnight glowed a dull orange, enough light to burn paper by. He held the financial tables above the firebox.

His hands did not move.

That was the thing he would remember afterward, the thing that cracked the cold calculation clean down the middle and let the fear in: not that he couldn't do it, but that some portion of him — the courier, the believer, the younger brother of a woman who had died for this paper — refused. Six years of his life were encoded in those tables. His sister had pressed his own case into his hands with her last strength and said *someone must carry it across, someone must keep faith.* Burn the financials and he burned the last proof that the republic had trusted anyone with anything at the end. Burn them and he was not a courier anymore. He was just a man running.

And yet keeping them made him prey, and being prey made the children prey, and the promise he had made at a deathbed had never once mentioned codes. It had mentioned names. *Marta. Tomas. Get them across, Cyrus. Promise me the children.*

Not the republic. The children.

The candle guttered in a draft from the stairwell and steadied. Down in the loft's far corner, Tomas whimpered once and subsided.

Cyrus closed the stove door without feeding it anything.

He stood in the middle of the room feeling the fear arrive properly at last — not the sharp fear of checkpoints and drawn pistols, which he knew like an old coat, but a slower dread, domestic, that moved in and unpacked. He was worth killing twice over. He had been worth killing since the day he arrived; he simply hadn't known the full price on his head. Knowledge changed nothing about the danger and everything about the weight of it. Before tonight he had protected a secret. Now he protected a treasury, and treasuries drew a different class of wolf.

He repacked the tables, threaded the binding, and fitted the packet back into the drowned man's case. Then he took his own case from beneath the floorboard — the one sewn into the lining story was wrong, actually, it was loose, hidden under the boards with his spare papers — and laid the two side by side on the cot. Gray leather twins. The republic's last will and testament, in duplicate parts.

He could not burn them. He could not carry them. He could not give them to the sea again, because the tide had proven it returned what it took.

There was one option left, and it was the coward's option, and he chose it with his eyes open: he would hide them so thoroughly that even he forgot where. Not under a loose board — loose boards were the first place any searcher looked, and he had searched houses himself, he knew the trade. He would bury them in the one place no warrant would reach and no memory would betray: inside the structure itself, among the old chandlery's bones, where a man would have to take the building apart plank by plank to find them.

He knelt and began taking up floorboards.

The loft floor was laid over the joists of the shop below, old pine gone silver, nailed fast in places and sprung in others. He worked quietly, prying with the flat of his knife, and chose his spot with a tradesman's care: not near the chimney, where heat and inspection concentrated; not near the walls, where mice ran and boards loosened; but mid-span, beneath the great roll of anchor cable the chandler stored up here in winter, a mass of tarred hemp too heavy for two men to shift without purpose. Anyone moving it would wake the whole building. He pried up a short offcut section between two joists, a board barely two feet long that had clearly been replaced sometime in the last decade by a carpenter less particular than himself, and dug.

The cavity between the joists was filled with ship's rubble — oakum, a broken pulley block, drifts of salt dust. He wrapped both cases in the oilcloth, then in the sacking he'd brought his tools in, and lowered them into the dark, and covered them with the rubble, and tamped it down, and replaced the board, and pegged it flush, and swept the dust smooth, and rolled the anchor cable back over the spot until it lay exactly as it had lain, a ton of indifference sitting atop his whole past.

Then he sat back on his heels in the dark and discovered he was shaking.

Not from cold, though the loft was cold enough. From the specific vertigo of a man who has just buried the only thing standing between himself and irrelevance — and knows it was the right choice and cannot stop mourning it. He had been somebody once. Second secretary attaché, courier first class, a name in a ministry registry, a desk with his initials brass-tacked to the corner. Now his name was under a floorboard with everything else, and what remained walking around the loft was a shape called Mr. Carlsen, carpenter, who mended crates and taught a traumatized child to tie a bowline and would, God willing, board the Atlas in three weeks as nobody at all.

Nobody at all, he told himself, is the safest thing a man can be.

He banked the stove, pinched the candle, and lay down without undressing, boots by the cot, coat over his legs. From below came the sound of Tomas turning over, and Marta's voice, barely awake, murmuring the same three words she always used — *it's all right, I'm here* — and then quiet.

He was nearly asleep when he heard the footsteps.

They came along the waterfront from the direction of the taverns, unhurried, deliberate, two sets of boots on frost-hardened planking, and they stopped directly outside the chandlery. Cyrus was upright in the dark without remembering moving, knife off the cot board, listening so hard his pulse roared louder than the harbor. The boots did not approach the door. They did not leave, either. They simply stayed — the way men stay when they are posted somewhere, when staying is the job.

After a long while, one of them laughed softly, a woman's laugh, low and amused, and then the boots moved on, fading toward the mole.

Cyrus did not sleep again. He lay watching the seam of grey light form around the shutter and understood, with the terrible clarity that follows a night of arithmetic, that his calculations had been overtaken by events. Someone had begun counting the chandlery's lodgers before he had finished counting his own dangers. Whatever came next would not wait for him to feel ready.

At dawn, when he went down to draw water for the children's porridge, he found a slip of paper folded under the chandlery's door, written in a bold, looping hand that smelled faintly of tobacco:

*A word in your ear, Mr. Carlsen, whenever you've finished your breakfast. The Smoke Room, tonight. I'm told you're a careful man. Careful men make the best conversation. — R.V.*

* * *

He read it three times, then fed it to the stove, and stood watching it curl. Behind him, on the stairs, Marta's bare feet paused.

"Uncle?" she said. "Who's Mr. Carlsen?"
