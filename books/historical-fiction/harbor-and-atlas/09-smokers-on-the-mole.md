## 9. Smokers on the Mole

Rhea Vance had a rule about information: never buy what you could wait for, and never wait for what you could take. She stood at the seaward end of the mole with the wind pulling gray fingers through her hair, smoking her third cigarette of the evening and watching the carpenter who was not a carpenter carry a crate of ships' biscuit up from the *Gannet* like a man who had never once in his life dropped anything.

"He counts his steps," said Piet, her boatswain, hunched against the wind beside her. "Did you see? Coming off the gangway he looked back twice — once for the crate, once for the water."

"Once for whoever might be behind him," Rhea said. "The water he doesn't care about. Men who are afraid of drowning make poor couriers."

"You're sure that's what he is."

She let the cigarette burn down between two fingers and did not answer immediately, because the answer was worth more unspoken than spoken, and Piet knew it. That was half of what she paid him in: her silences. He had learned to find them generous.

The man calling himself Herr Voss — carpenter, late of somewhere flat and northern, papers signed by a port official whose signature she had seen on four other men's documents that season alone — set the crate down inside the chandlery door, spoke three words to the chandler's boy, and walked back out onto the quay without hurrying. That was the thing about him. Everything about him was calibrated not to hurry. Fugitives hurried. Smugglers hurried when they were losing. This one moved like a clock with its case closed.

"Get me everything," she said. "Where he sleeps, where he eats, which window of that loft goes dark first. Don't let him see you doing it — he notices eyes. I want to know if he writes letters and where he burns them."

"And the seal?"

"Ah." She smiled, and Piet had the good sense to look away from it. "The seal is mine already. He just doesn't know it yet."

* * *

What Rhea knew, she had bought in pieces, the way one buys a ship that has run aground — cheaply, skeptically, and always prepared to discover the hull is rotten.

The first piece had come in October, before the frost, from a customs clerk in Halmstad who owed her money and repaid in gossip: a courier had gone south carrying republican dispatch codes, the whole cipher book of a government that had ceased to exist in June, and had vanished somewhere along the coast. The second piece came from a boarding-house landlady in a town whose name Rhea had already forgotten, describing a quiet lodger who paid in banknotes too crisp for a workingman and asked questions about steamer schedules the way other men asked about weather. The third piece was the drowned man.

That one she kept to herself entirely, even now, even from Piet. In September her crew had lifted a body off the shallows below Kessel Point, face eaten to suggestion by fish, coat pockets sewn shut with thread that no sailor owned. In the inner lining, wrapped in oilcloth: a dispatch case, brass-cornered, stamped with a seal she recognized from the newspapers of a country that no longer printed any. Her quartermaster had wanted to throw it back — bad luck, dead men's mail — but Rhea had read the sea differently. The sea did not deliver things by accident. The sea delivered things to whoever was positioned to receive them, and Rhea Vance made a career of being positioned.

So when a soft-handed stranger with a carpenter's name arrived in Verrigan in November and took a loft over a chandlery within sight of the *Atlas*'s berth, she did not need to open the man to know what was sewn inside him. She only needed him to understand it himself.

That was the art of it. Anyone could rob a courier. Robbery created corpses, inquiries, magistrates with nothing better to do than pull threads. But a man who *sold* you what you wanted — a man who came to believe the sale was his own idea, his own necessity, perhaps even his own mercy — that man stayed alive, stayed quiet, and stayed grateful. Leverage was cargo that did not spoil. She had a hold full of it: a customs captain's gambling debts, a shipwright's forged age, a widow's second marriage certificate with the dates wrong. People thought smuggling was about brandy and tobacco. Rhea thought it was about owning the version of every man in the harbor that he could not afford to have told.

And the carpenter, she suspected, was a whole shipment in himself.

* * *

She found him on the mole three nights later, as she intended him to. This required some theater. She sent Piet ahead loudly on one errand and followed quietly on another; she timed her walk so that they would arrive at the seaward rail at the same moment, two people converging on the best view of a frozen harbor beginning to think seriously about winter.

"Herr Voss." She offered the name like a hand of cards laid face-up. "You'll forgive me. Out here everyone is someone else, and it saves time to skip the part where we pretend."

He turned. Up close, in the gaslight that reached this far and no farther, he had the kind of face built to be forgotten — ordinary brow, ordinary jaw, the sort of features a portraitist would render and then lose. Only the eyes worked overtime. They went to her hands first, then the rail behind her, then the dark water past her shoulder, cataloguing exits. A carpenter would have looked at her face.

"Madam," he said. "I'm afraid you have the advantage."

"Rhea Vance. *Gannet*, out of nowhere in particular." She drew a cigarette case from her coat — silver, dented, older than her fortunes — and held it out. "Smoke?"

"I don't."

"More for me." She lit one, cupping the match, and let a long breath of it go seaward. "Cold coming early this year. You've heard what they're saying about the harbor?"

"That it freezes."

"That it freezes *early*. There's a difference worth money." She leaned against the rail and studied him with the frankness of a buyer at auction. "The last steamer south is the *Atlas*, and the *Atlas* sails when the ice says or before it, whichever comes sooner. Half this town believes they're leaving. Most of them aren't. Berths are like conscience, Herr Voss — everyone claims to have one, and there's never enough to go around."

Something moved behind his forgettable face, quick and quickly buried. There it was, then. He had children hidden somewhere — she'd had that confirmed two days ago, the chandler's daughter telling a schoolmate that the lodger bought bread for three — and a man with children counted berths the way starving men count spoons.

"You know something about sailing schedules," he said carefully, "or you sell them?"

"I know about everything on this coast that has a price. Schedules included." She tapped ash into the wind. "But I didn't climb out here on a freezing night to talk freight. I climbed out here because I've taken an interest in you, and I find that when I take an interest in a man, it's kinder to tell him before winter does it for me."

"Kindness," he said. "From a smuggler."

"From a businesswoman. Smuggling is just trade that hasn't been bribed into respectability yet." She let the smile sit a moment, then folded it away, because the next part deserved a level voice. "Here is my interest. A month ago a body washed up near Kessel Point. Drowned courier. No papers on him — none anyone found." She watched the word *anyone* land. It landed well; his stillness afterward was the stillness of a man recalculating everything. "The seal on his coat buttons was from a republic that died in June. Curious thing, a dead government still sending mail. Curiouser still, a living man arriving in Verrigan the same season, with hands too clean for sawdust and a way of walking like the whole town owes him silence."

The wind came off the water and shoved between them. He didn't step back from it. She gave him credit for that.

"If you're threatening me," he said quietly, "you should know I'm not worth the rope."

"Oh, I don't threaten. Threats are for people who need scaring, and you're clearly terrified already — you'd only enjoy the attention." She flicked the cigarette into the black water and watched the spark die. "What I'm offering is simpler. Winter protection. My word over this harbor that no one touches you, yours, or your little household above the chandlery."

His face did not change at all, and that was how she knew she'd drawn blood. A man with nothing hidden would have frowned and asked *what household?*

"In exchange for," he said.

"Conversation. Eventually. When you're ready." She pushed off the rail and straightened her collar, unhurried, letting him watch her leave before she actually left — the oldest trick in negotiation, making the exit the expensive part. "Verrigan is a small town, Herr Voss, and it's about to get much smaller when the bridge stays broken and the water turns to glass. Small towns do two things to strangers: they forget them, or they eat them. Which happens depends entirely on who's standing nearby with a fork." She began to turn, then paused, as if remembering something trivial. "Oh — and the mending you do at the nets. Left-handed menders splice under, not over. Sef noticed. Sef notices everything; it's why he's still alive. Be kind to the old man. Good friends are rare at your price."

She walked back along the mole with the wind at her spine and did not look around. Behind her, the not-carpenter stayed at the rail a long time, a dark shape against darker water, and she carried his silence home the way she carried everything — carefully, and intending to sell it.

* * *

In her cabin aboard the *Gannet* — a room she had furnished deliberately like the parlor of a woman she had once pretended to be, all lace curtains and a tea service nobody drank from — Rhea opened the drawer beneath the chart table and reviewed her collection.

Not gold. Gold was common and heavy and lied about its own value. Papers. Here, a customs manifest showing three hundred cases of "machine parts" that were rifles, useful against the harbormaster. Here, a letter in a magistrate's own hand, dated four years back, instructing a seizure to be delayed one tide — one tide, one small tide — and a fortune of contraband slipping out on it, and Magistrate Corven's signature at the bottom like a wound that never quite closed. She had owned Corven for two years and had never once needed to use him, which was the most elegant form of ownership: a tool so frightened of being used that it sharpened itself nightly.

She turned Corven's letter under the lamp and thought about the courier's codes.

Because that was the true prize, wasn't it. Not the paper itself — paper rots, paper burns, paper makes you a target of every desperate government remnant between here and the capital. What mattered was what the codes could *do*. A dead republic's cipher book meant access to whatever loyalists still transmitted, wherever exile committees still plotted, wherever money moved in the names of men who no longer officially existed. It meant, held correctly, a relationship with powers across the water. It meant legitimacy someday, if the winds turned, and Rhea Vance had survived ten years of smuggling by betting early on which way winds would turn.

Or it meant simple money. A buyer in the south had already hinted at figures with too many zeros. Either road paid. That was the beauty of rare cargo: it appreciated while you decided.

The knock came at the hatch. Piet, stamping frost off his boots, bringing cold air and a report.

"The chandlery loft. Four windows lit tonight. Two small."

Children. She had known, but knowing confirmed by lamplight was different — heavier, more usable, more dangerous. Children changed the arithmetic of a man like the carpenter. A man alone negotiated with his wits. A man with children negotiated with his throat.

"And the old net-mender?" she asked. "Sef."

"Patched his shed roof today. Bought lamp oil. Bought more lamp oil than one lamp needs, if you follow me."

Rhea smiled slowly. So the carpenter had heard her, had understood her, and was already moving his household sideways — toward the one man in Verrigan with quiet hands and a history. Clever. Clever was fine. Clever men were predictable, because clever men always believed they had more time.

"Anything on the *Atlas*?"

"First mate's been drinking at the anchor-watch. Says the captain's talking days now, not weeks. Ice reports from the point."

Days. She sat back and let the numbers assemble themselves, the way she liked them: one steamer, shrinking schedule, a harbor going to glass, a magistrate on a hair-trigger of fear, a courier worth killing twice, and two children asleep behind four small windows. Somewhere in that arithmetic was her fleet, her future, her way off this coast before the law remembered her name. All of it waited on one conversation with a man pretending to mend crates.

"Piet," she said, "when the time comes, we may need the chandlery watched day and night. And I want a word arranged with the magistrate — the polite kind. Tell Corven I have new paperwork for his inspection. Tell him it concerns seals."

Piet hesitated in the hatchway. "And if he asks what seals?"

"Then tell him the truth, or the shape of it. Tell him the past has washed ashore in Verrigan, and I'm offering to help him bury it." She closed the drawer on her collection, on Corven's signature, on four years of patient appetite. "Magistrates will do anything for a quiet burial, Piet. It's the one funeral they never get to refuse attending."

He went. The lamp guttered and steadied. Outside, the harbor ticked and groaned as the first skin of ice thickened along the pilings, and somewhere beyond it, in a loft above a chandlery, a man who called himself Voss was teaching his hands to be stupid again, and failing, and knew it.

Rhea pinched out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the ice grow, thinking, with deep contentment, that everything in this town was now either hers or about to be — except one small stubborn thing that believed it still had choices left.

She would correct that before the tide.
