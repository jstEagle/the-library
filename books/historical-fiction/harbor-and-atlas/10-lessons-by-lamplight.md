## 10. Lessons by Lamplight

The dream always started with the water.

Not the gray chop of Verrigan harbor, which Tomas could see through the loft's round window when he dared to look — but the other water, the black kind, the kind that had been between him and the far shore for eleven days in the hold of a boat whose name he made himself forget on purpose. In the dream the water came up instead of staying down. It rose through the boards of the loft, patient and certain, and it had his mother's voice.

*Tomas. Tomas. Come down here where it's warm.*

He woke the way he always woke: all at once, hands clawing at the blanket, a sound caught in his throat like a fishhook. Above him the beams of the chandlery loft were silvered by moonlight through frost-furred glass. To his left, Marta slept the way she did everything — efficiently, curled tight as a coil of rope, one arm over her face. On the pallet by the stair door, Uncle Cyrus lay without moving, and for a bad half-second Tomas thought *he's dead* the way he thought it every night, and then Cyrus breathed, a slow deliberate breath, and Tomas counted them until his own heart slowed to match. One. Two. Three. His sister's note had said *guardian*, a word that meant nothing Tomas understood, but breathing, apparently, was part of the job. He did it on purpose, loud enough to be counted. Marta said he snored. He didn't care if he snored.

Tonight, though, the counting didn't work. The water was still too close. He sat up, hugged his knees, and looked out the little window at the harbor lying flat and dark below, and he thought about how everything you could see from up here — the mole, the masts, the fat black shape of the steamer Atlas — was just the top of something that went all the way down, and that under all of it there was more water than anyone could count, forever.

"You're doing the drowning arithmetic again."

Tomas nearly fell off the mattress. Cyrus was sitting up in the dark, hair flattened on one side, eyes already fully awake. He never seemed to wake *into* things; he arrived, complete, as if he'd only ever been resting with his boots on.

"I wasn't—"

"Your face does it." Cyrus crossed the loft in three silent steps and folded himself down onto the floor beside Tomas's pallet, back against the wall, long legs drawn up. From this angle, in the moonlight, his face looked less like an uncle's and more like one of those carvings on old church doors — all planes and shadow, weathered by something. "It's a bad habit. I do it myself."

"Nobody can drown in their sleep," Tomas said, because Marta would have wanted him to say something tough.

"No," Cyrus agreed. "But you can drown in the remembering of it. That's worse, because no one can see it happening, so they can't throw you a line."

Tomas scowled at his knees. Down in the harbor, a night bird cried once over the water and quit. "I wasn't remembering anything."

"Mm," said Cyrus, who was a liar about some things but not, Tomas had learned, about others, and who let the lie sit there without stepping on it. Then he reached into the pocket of the coat he slept near — he kept the coat near him always, Tomas had noticed, even now, especially now — and drew out a length of cord, soft with age, about as long as his forearm. He held it out across the dark like an offering.

"Do you know what this is?"

"It's string."

"It's a bowline," said Cyrus. "Or it will be, in about a minute, if you pay attention. A bowline is the most important knot in the world. It makes a loop that will not slip, will not jam, and will come apart the moment you ask it to, even after it has borne your whole weight. Sailors call it the king of knots. If a man falls overboard, someone throws him a line with a bowline in the end, and if the knot is good, the man lives." He turned the cord over in his fingers. "I knew a man who owed his life to a bowline tied badly by a stranger. So you see, it matters who knows how to tie one."

Tomas took the cord. It was warmer than he expected, body-warm, as if Cyrus had been holding it a while, waiting.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

Cyrus taught the way he did everything else: quietly, precisely, without any wasted praise. He didn't put his hands over Tomas's hands the way adults liked to, which mostly taught you that your hands were wrong. Instead he laid his own cord alongside Tomas's and worked one step ahead, so that Tomas was always chasing him, and the chasing made him forget to think about water.

"The rabbit comes out of the hole—" The working end dove through the loop. "—around the big tree—" it wrapped the standing part "—and back down the hole." And there it was, ugly but real, a loop that held when Cyrus hauled on it and opened at a touch.

"Again," Cyrus said, and untied both.

Tomas fumbled it twice, then got one that held, then lost it, then got it again. His fingers were clumsy from cold and from sleep, but the cord didn't mind. Cord was patient. Cord didn't care if you'd spent eleven days in a black hold listening to grown men pray in four languages. By the fourth try his hands had gone somewhere on their own, someplace quiet where there was only the rabbit and the tree and the hole, and when he looked up, the window had gone gray with the first light of morning and Marta was sitting up in her blankets watching them with an expression he couldn't read.

"What are you two doing?" she whispered.

"Learning the king of knots," Tomas said, with as much dignity as a boy in a nightshirt could manage.

Marta looked at Cyrus. Something passed between the two of them, some adult arithmetic done in half a second, and then she said only, "There's porridge downstairs if Mr. Pell doesn't mind us borrowing his stove again," and went to dress, and the ordinary-ness of it — porridge, stove, morning — settled over the loft like snowfall, soft and covering everything.

That was the first night. There were many more.

* * *

It became a thing without anyone deciding it should be, the way real things happen. Some nights Tomas woke gasping; some nights he woke before the dream could get its hooks in, waking himself on purpose to claim the lamp first; and twice or three times a week, whether he'd dreamed or not, Cyrus would sit up against the wall with the lamp turned low and the coat across his knees, and they would work.

Knots first, then more knots. Bowline, cleat hitch, sheet bend ("for joining two ropes, the way two stories join — the bend is weaker than either rope, remember that"), rolling hitch, fisherman's knot. Cyrus named each one's use in the world, never its use in a game: *this one holds a dinghy in a swell; this one will save a net; this one, see, this one a smuggler taught me in another life, and if anyone asks, you never learned it from me.* Marta pretended not to listen from her corner, and then one night Tomas found her practicing a monkey's fist in the dark with her eyes shut, and they didn't say anything about it, but after that she stopped pretending.

When the knots ran thin, Cyrus brought out maps.

He had them folded inside the coat — Tomas understood by then that there were papers in that coat that were none of his business, and that some of the maps were innocent and some might not be, and Cyrus never explained which was which, and Tomas never asked, because asking felt like the sort of question that could break something. But the sea charts were spread open without fear: the coast running north in its ragged pencil-line, Verrigan a small inked curl around its harbor, the shallows marked in stippled dots like a skin condition, the deep channel a winding blue throat that the great ships followed in.

"This is us," Cyrus said, tapping the curl. "Now. Where are we going?"

"The Atlas goes south around the cape and then east," Tomas recited. "Across to the neutral ports. Eleven days if the weather holds. Sixteen if it doesn't."

"And what do we steer clear of?" Cyrus slid his finger along the coast. "Read the chart to me."

Tomas leaned in. The lamplight made small gold islands on the paper. "The Kettle Shoals, here, where the dots go all the way across. The wreck off Saint Alder's point — there's a little drawing of a ship breaking on it, right on the chart, like a warning somebody drew in." He paused at a stretch of empty-looking water east of the harbor mouth. "And this, but it doesn't say anything."

"Ah." Cyrus's voice changed, very slightly — the way a rope changes sound when its load shifts. "That's where nothing is written down, and therefore where everything happens. Unmarked water belongs to whoever knows it best." He tapped a spot. "There's a channel through there, at half-tide, that maybe forty people alive can steer. Rhea Vance is one of them."

"The smuggler captain," Tomas said. "With the red boat."

"She'd be hurt to hear you call it red. Oxblood." Almost, almost, a smile crossed Cyrus's face and decided against it. "Point is, a chart tells you what everyone knows. The rest you learn from people. Charts are the map; people are the territory."

Tomas considered this. "Marta says you're a carpenter."

"I am a carpenter," said Cyrus, evenly, spreading the next chart.

"But you know smugglers' channels and tide tables and—" Tomas gestured vaguely at the whole paper continent, "—all this. Carpenters don't know this."

For a moment the lamp hissed, and outside, far off, the ice on the harbor creaked — a long groaning sound like a door in an enormous house, which had started two weeks ago when the first hard freeze came and which everyone in town pretended not to hear. Cyrus smoothed the chart flat with both hands, taking his time about it.

"When I was younger than you," he said at last, "I wanted to make maps. Not use them — make them. Survey coastlines. Put names on nameless water so other people wouldn't be afraid of it." He said it in the flat voice he used for facts, but Tomas was learning to hear underneath the flatness, where the true things lived. "Then other things happened, and I became the kind of person who needs maps more than he loves them. Which is a different trade entirely." He folded the chart along its creases, each fold exact. "Someday I'll tell you what happened. Not tonight. Tonight you should sleep, because tomorrow I'm teaching you to read the tide table, and it has defeated better men than either of us."

"Tomas," Tomas said.

Cyrus looked up.

"You never say my name. Or Marta's. You talk *about* us to other people, but not *to* us." He hadn't planned to say it; it came out the way the dream-sound came out, hook-first. "Pell calls us the lodger's kin. Sef calls us the little ones. Even you write letters and put our names in them but you don't say them out loud."

The lamp hissed. The ice groaned. Cyrus sat very still, and Tomas watched something move behind his face like a fish under harbor ice — there, gone, there.

"Cyrus," he said finally, and his voice came out lower than before, sanded down. "That's why. Names are — I've spent a long time being careful with my own name, keeping it still, like a coin you don't dare spend. I suppose I got in the habit of not spending yours either." He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the folded chart, once, twice. "Tomas. Marta. There. Spent." And the smile that had declined earlier now arrived, small and crooked and real, and transformed his whole face into someone's brother. "Goodnight, Tomas. Tide tables at breakfast."

"Goodnight," said Tomas, and discovered, climbing back under the blanket, that he wasn't afraid to close his eyes.

* * *

December thickened. The harbor froze outward from the inner basin in plates and pans of gray-white ice that knocked together with a sound like crockery, and the town talked of nothing else, and the Atlas sat at her berth like a promise with a date on it that kept moving. But up in the loft, other things grew.

Marta began setting the little table on its side against the wall as a shelf, and putting three cups on it instead of leaving them in the crate, which was a declaration of some kind. Mr. Pell from downstairs complained weekly about the porridge and the noise and the way children stared at his stock, and every complaint was delivered while handing up something extra — ends of candle, a heel of bread, a strip of tarred canvas — so that the complaining became a language of its own, and everyone understood it. Sef the net-mender, when Cyrus brought Tomas down to the sheds on milder days, watched Tomas's hands go through a bowline blindfolded and grunted in a tone that everyone within earshot recognized as a medal.

And one night — a bad night, the worst since the crossing, because that day they'd walked past the customs house and seen men loading a third-class passenger list, and the lists had names in columns, and columns were exactly the shape of the hold — Tomas woke not with the fishhook in his throat but actually crying, silently, furiously, hating himself for it, and before he could get his breathing under control, Marta's blanket was being dragged over to his pallet and Cyrus was sitting down on the floor between them without a word, back against the wall, present the way furniture is present, the way a wall is present.

They stayed like that. Nobody spoke. After a while Cyrus picked up the cord he kept in the coat pocket — he always had one, Tomas realized; the man traveled armored in string — and began, very slowly, in the dark, without looking at either of them, to tie a monkey's fist one-handed, feeling his way through it, letting the small clicks of the cord's passage fill the room instead of words.

"That's the one for throwing," Tomas managed, wetly.

"Mm. Weighted end of a heaving line. You throw the fist, the line follows it, and someone on shore or aboard catches it and hauls." The knot clicked closed in his palm. "It only works if someone is willing to catch. That's the whole design. Half the knot is in the thrower and half is in whoever's ashore with their hands ready." He set the finished fist down on the blanket between the three of them, a small hard ball in the darkness. "You're ashore now, Tomas. Both of you. Whatever the crossing was — and I know it was eleven days of the worst water there is — it ended. You're ashore, and there are people here with their hands ready. Starting with me. I'm not good at much else, but I'm reliable at catching."

Marta snorted in the dark, which was her way of crying too, sometimes.

"In the morning," Cyrus went on, settling back against the wall as if he might actually stay there till dawn, which he did, "we'll do something new. I'm going to teach you both to box the compass. All thirty-two points. By the time the Atlas sails, you'll know her heading better than half her crew. How's that?"

"Thirty-two points is too many," said Marta.

"North, north by north, north-northwest—" Tomas began counting on his fingers.

"Go to sleep, both of you," said Marta.

But nobody slept, not really, and it didn't matter, because the lamp stayed low and the cord kept clicking and the ice groaned out on the harbor like some vast animal turning over in its den, and the loft — smelling of tar and tallow and the ghost of porridge — held the three of them the way a hull holds water out: not by being grand, only by being whole.

Tomas drifted last. On the very edge of sleep he heard Marta whisper, "Is he staying? When we sail? There's only berths for — I heard Pell saying tickets—"

And he heard Cyrus answer, after a pause a shade too long, in the flat voice with true things underneath it:

"Sleep, Marta."

And Tomas, half-dreaming already, thought: *he didn't spend that answer.*

Down in the harbor, the ice pressed in against the Atlas's hull, and the winter tightened one more turn, like a knot no one had asked to be tied.
