## 11. Corven's Dilemma

The summons came at dusk, folded inside a summons that was not a summons.

Corven found it on his desk when he returned from the customs house — a single sheet of good paper, unsigned, bearing only an address and a time: *The Salt Glass. Nine o'clock. Come alone, or don't come at all; the paper will find its way to the capital either way.*

He read it twice, standing in his own study with his coat still on, the lamp unlit, the room going blue around him with the last of the winter light. He knew what the paper was before he touched it. He had known for eleven years, in the way a man knows the location of a tumor he has never allowed the physician to confirm: always there, always growing, never named.

It was a page from a ledger. His ledger. The one he had burned in the spring of 1908, or believed he had burned, on the night he had stood in this same room as a younger man with a righteous jaw and fed the record of four smugglers' bribes into his own stove — all but one page, which he had kept, God help him, which he had kept as insurance against the men who might otherwise have decided a magistrate who knew too much was a magistrate who knew too much. And then the war had come, and the men had died or scattered, and the page had stayed in its oilcloth sleeve beneath the false bottom of the deed box like a splinter worked so deep into the flesh that the body grew around it and called the shape of it normal.

The page in front of him now was not his page. It was a copy — a careful, hand-inked copy, in a clerk's neat script he did not recognize — but it was accurate to the coin. Dated the ninth of March, 1908. *Received of Captain E. Vance, for consideration of harbor passage, four hundred crowns.* And beneath it, in the handwriting the whole waterfront would have known anywhere, because it had sentenced half of them: *A. Corven.*

He sat down slowly in his chair. Outside, the fog was coming up off the water the way it did every evening now, thickening the town into shapes and rumors. Somewhere down the hill a ship's bell struck eight, muffled, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

*The Salt Glass,* he thought. Rhea Vance's tavern. Her father's before her, and her grandfather's before that, and in all those years no Corven had crossed its threshold except in irons or in disguise.

He lit the lamp. He put on his better coat, the one without the ink stain on the cuff, and he went out into the night to be blackmailed.

* * *

The Salt Glass was warmer than it had any right to be, and fuller than he'd have liked. Not full of people — full of *watchers*, which was worse. Three men playing cards who did not look at their cards. A woman behind the bar polishing a glass that was already dry. The fire built high enough to read by, which someone had done deliberately, because when Rhea Vance rose from the corner booth and gestured him toward it, the light fell across the table like a stage lamp, and what lay on the table was meant to be read.

"Magistrate." She poured him a drink without asking. "You look like a man who walked here the long way. Round by the customs shed, was it? Checking who saw you?"

"I walk where I please, Captain Vance."

"You do," she agreed pleasantly. "That's the tragedy of you."

She was perhaps thirty-five, built like a topmast — everything about her angled into the wind — and she had her father's eyes, which Corven remembered from the dockside of his first year on the bench: gray-green, amused, patient as gulls. He had sent Elias Vance to prison for eighteen months on evidence that had been thin and true. He remembered the sentencing. He had prided himself on the sentence. He had said something from the bench about the corrosive effect of corruption upon a port's honest commerce, and the young woman in the gallery — the captain's daughter, nineteen then — had not wept or shouted. She had watched him the entire time with those gull's eyes, taking his measure, the way you'd study a lock you intended to open someday.

Someday had arrived. It was wearing a red wool scarf and pouring brandy.

"Sit," she said.

He sat.

On the table between them lay three items arranged with a curator's care. First, the copy of the ledger page — though he had left his at home, so this must be hers, one of however many existed. Second, a letter in a faded envelope, addressed to Elias Vance in a hand Corven recognized with a lurch as belonging to Harl Muntz, the harbormaster, dead these six years: a letter that could only be the letter confirming the second bribe, the winter payment, the one Corven had always told himself had never quite happened. Third — and this was the thing that made his hands go cold on the glass — a photograph. Grainy, amateur, taken from a doorway. It showed a younger Anton Corven, robes and chain of office, standing at the mouth of the alley behind Muntz's office, shaking hands with a man whose face was turned away but whose coat and build Corven knew as well as his own.

"There's a negative," Rhea said, watching him look. "And there are copies of the copies. I'm told the postal service is slow in winter, but spring comes eventually everywhere."

Corven lifted the brandy and drank. It was good brandy, which offended him obscurely. "This is extortion," he said. "In writing, effectively. You've just confessed it to me across a table."

"Yes," she said. "I have."

The word landed on the table and lay there. He looked up.

"That's the difference between us, Magistrate, and I want you to understand it early, because I think you're a man who understands things late but thoroughly." She folded her hands. "Everything I do is in the open. I run cargo the customs house hasn't taxed and I pay the men this town forgot how to feed, and everyone knows both facts, including you, and you've known for years, and done nothing, because doing something costs more than you can pay anymore. That's the arrangement. It has been since 1908. You didn't create it, but you signed it, and I hold the signature." She tapped the ledger page once, lightly, almost fondly. "So no, I haven't extorted you. Extortion is demanding something a man hasn't already given. I'm only asking you to keep giving what you've given for eleven years. Continuity, Magistrate. It's the soul of good business."

"And what you want continued?"

"A sweep." She said it the way a person names a dish they're ordering. "Paper, legal, properly stamped. A search of the waterfront lodgings — chandleries, boarding houses, net sheds, anywhere a man might sleep who doesn't want to be found. Looking for documents. Courier's papers, dispatch cases, anything bearing republican seals or foreign ciphers."

The room tilted very slightly, and righted. Corven found he had set the glass down without deciding to.

"There's been talk," Rhea went on, conversational now, settling back. "Talk of a courier in town. A man with carpenter's hands that have never swung a hammer — you'd know the type, you've sentenced enough of them. Talk of a leather case sewn into a coat. And my sources tell me the republic he carried those papers for doesn't exist anymore, which means whatever's in that case isn't diplomacy anymore. It's salvage." Her eyes were perfectly level. "Salvage belongs to whoever raises it. That's maritime law, and you're fond of maritime law. But there are other parties circling — parties from over the border, parties who'd pay a magistrate a great deal for the courtesy of *legal* seizure. Papers taken under warrant. Papers no questions asked about, held 'for inspection' — your phrase, your stamp, your authority. Clean. Untouched by any smuggler's hand. Worth more than the papers themselves, that kind of cover. Worth more than you make in ten years."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the copies travel. The capital still remembers the Republic of Verrigan's clean-handed magistrate; there are men there building careers out of prosecuting exactly the sort of rot you represented yourself as fighting. They'd love you. Trial, disgrace, the works. Your clerk would testify. Your own records would convict you — you wrote beautiful judgments, Anton. So precise. Every lie you ever told on the bench is indexed."

The fire popped. Behind him, the card game had stopped pretending.

Corven looked at his hands. They were steady, which surprised him; he had expected them to shake, and their steadiness felt like the deepest betrayal yet, his own body declining even the dignity of visible terror.

"I want you to hear something," he said quietly. "Not as argument. As fact. In 1908 I took four hundred crowns because my wife was dying in a sanatorium that cost more than a magistrate's salary, and I told myself it was once, and it was twice, and then it was an arrangement, and then she died anyway, and the arrangement lived on. Every year since, I have told myself that next year I would end it, and every year ending it became harder than keeping it, until keeping it was simply what I did, the way a lame man walks." He looked up. "You've bought nothing tonight, Captain Vance. You cannot buy a man with a debt he already owes. Whatever I do next, I do because the alternative destroys me, not because you've persuaded me of anything. I hope that comforts you less than it should."

For the first time, something moved behind the gull's eyes — not warmth, exactly. Recognition. The look of a chess player finding an unexpected piece still standing.

"It doesn't comfort me at all," she said. "But I'll tell you what does. You'll issue the warrant. Tomorrow, day after — soon. Waterfront lodgings, general inspection, public health and security grounds, all very dull. And when my people bring me what the warrant turns up, you'll hold it 'pending inquiry,' and the inquiry will take as long as I need it to take." She stood, smoothing her scarf, signaling with two fingers to the bar that the interview was over. "One more thing, Magistrate. There are children involved, I'm told, at some of these lodgings. Refugees. Be a shame if a lawful sweep frightened anyone into running. Running people get caught. Caught people get questions. Keep your men gentle."

At the door she paused, holding it open for him, courteous as a hostess.

"Oh — and Anton." She used his Christian name the way you'd step on someone's foot. "If you're thinking of the confession route — writing it all down, falling on your sword, taking me with you — remember the letter goes to three addresses the hour you do. Muntz is dead, but his widow isn't, and neither is my proof. You'd burn. I'd sail. Weigh it."

He stepped past her into the fog. It closed around him like water, and for a dozen steps he simply walked, blind, hearing the tavern door shut behind him and the town's sounds arrive one by one — a dog, a hawser creaking, ice clicking somewhere out in the black harbor where the season's first skin of frost was forming, early, weeks early, the watermen muttering about it.

He stopped under the broken lamp at the head of Quay Street and was sick, quietly, into the gutter, and then stood wiping his mouth with a handkerchief that monogrammed hands had embroidered for a better man.

*Justice outside the law now wears my robes.* The thought arrived complete, cold, and final, and he knew it was the truest thing he had thought in eleven years. Rhea Vance did not need to corrupt him anymore. Corruption was finished work, cured like mortar. What she needed was his living hand, moving daily, stamping her appetite with the appearance of law — and the horror of it, the suffocating heart of it, was that the hand would obey. Not from cowardice alone. From arithmetic. From the ledger of lives that any refusal would spend: his clerk, who was innocent; Muntz's widow, who was old; the refugees in the lodging houses, whom a panicked flight through a frozen country would kill more surely than any interrogation.

He could refuse and die clean. He could obey and live filthy. Or—

Or there was a third thing, and he did not let himself look at it directly, the way you don't look at the one candle in a dark room lest looking put it out. A third thing that required a courier to exist, and papers to be found, and a moment when the machine he was being forced to turn might be turned instead — once, precisely, against its makers. It required him to become a better liar than he had ever been a judge.

*Tomorrow,* he thought. *First the warrant. Give her the warrant. A drowning man grabs the rope she offers, and maybe, maybe, the rope runs all the way back up to the deck.*

He walked home through the fog with his collar up, and behind him the harbor ticked and groaned as the ice grew, sealing the town inch by inch into a trap with a beautiful view, and in his study, long past midnight, Anton Corven drew a fresh sheet of paper toward himself, dipped his pen, and began, in the beautiful precise hand that had convicted half the coast, to write the order that would loose the smugglers onto every door in Verrigan — while somewhere below, in the loft of a chandlery on Rope Walk, a man who did not yet know his name was being hunted slept with his arm around a child, and the tide brought the ice in, and the tide took the sound of the pen away.

By morning the warrant would bear the seal of the law.

By evening, so would the hunt.
