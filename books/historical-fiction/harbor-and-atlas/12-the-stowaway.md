## 12. The Stowaway

Noor had learned to count in borders, and she had survived three of them.

The first was a river crossing at night, when she was twelve, her mother's hand cold around hers and the water trying its best to take both of them. The second was a checkpoint where a soldier with bread crumbs in his mustache looked at her forged papers for so long that she forgot how breathing worked, and then waved them through because a cart behind them was honking. The third was the mountains — no soldier, no river, just cold and hunger and her own legs refusing, over and over, to stop.

So when the hatch above her head opened at dawn and light poured down into the Atlas's hold like something spilled, and a bosun's voice bellowed *stowaway* in a language she half spoke, Noor did not panic. Panic was for people who still believed things could be taken from them only once.

She stood up slowly from between the coils of tarred rope where she'd spent eleven days, and she made herself tall. That was the trick. She'd discovered it somewhere around the second border: if you made yourself small, the world agreed you were small. If you stood like you had somewhere to be and a right to be there, the world hesitated, and hesitation was all a body ever needed.

"Name," said the bosun. He was a slab of a man with a beard like rusted wire and a voice that had been used on sailors for thirty years.

"Noor," she said. Just that. Names invited questions, and questions invited answers, and answers were the things that got you sent back across fourth borders.

"Noor *what*?"

"Just Noor."

He looked at her the way men look at weather they can't decide whether to respect. She was sixteen, or seventeen — the difference had stopped mattering two winters ago — with a face that hunger had carved down to essentials and black hair cut raggedly at her jaw with a fish knife. Her coat had been good once. Her boots had been resoled twice by herself, badly. Everything else about her was willpower.

"Stowaways go ashore," the bosun said. "That's the law of it. Captain won't carry what he didn't sell a ticket for."

"I'm not cargo," Noor said. "I'm a passenger without money. There's a difference."

"There is," he admitted, and something almost like amusement moved under the wire beard. "It's that passengers without money also go ashore."

She had known this would happen. She had known it since the third night in the hold, when she'd counted the engine's rhythm and understood they were making for Verrigan, and that Verrigan was not far enough. It was a port town; port towns meant harbors; harbors meant ships; and ships meant that going ashore was not an ending but a change of vehicle. She had survived three borders by never letting any of them be the last one.

But knowing did not stop her hands from shaking as she climbed the ladder into the gray morning, or stop her chest from doing something complicated when she saw land — actual land, roofs and smoke and gulls wheeling over a harbor fogged like breath on glass. Eleven days in the dark of a hold, eating nothing but the biscuits she'd stolen in increments small enough that no one counted them gone, drinking condensation she wrung from a tarpaulin into a tin cup. Now the world again, vast and indifferent and smelling of salt and coal smoke and fish.

They put her off at the customs quay like a parcel nobody would sign for.

A harbor official, gray-coated and gray-faced, asked her name and she gave him *Noor* and watched his patience fail. He asked where she came from and she said *south*, which was true of nearly everywhere. He asked for papers and she produced the folded scrap she carried against her heart — the third set, bought from a man in a mountain village whose ink had been suspiciously fresh — and watched him hold it up to the light as though honesty were visible in the fibers.

"This says you're a seamstress," he said.

"I can sew," Noor said. This was true.

"It doesn't say anything about entry rights."

"Neither do I," she said, "but here I am."

He did not laugh. Behind her, the Atlas's crew coiled lines and pretended very hard not to listen; one young sailor, whose biscuit ration had been mysteriously short for eleven days, found something urgent to study in the deck planks. The official stamped nothing, wrote nothing, and finally waved her off the quay with the weary gesture of a man whose jurisdiction ended at the water's edge and whose compassion had been frozen out of him sometime before November.

"There's work on the fish quays if you've hands," he said, not unkindly. "There's nothing else. And when the Atlas sails, girl, don't be standing on my quay. I'll have to notice you then, and neither of us wants that."

*When the Atlas sails.* Noor turned the words over like coins. So the ship wasn't leaving today. The ice on the inner harbor, she noticed even through fog-thick exhaustion, had come early this year — sheets of it grinding against the pilings with a sound like slow applause. A ship delayed was a clock ticking, yes. But it was also time. Time was the only currency she'd ever had much of.

She walked up from the quay into Verrigan.

* * *

The town assembled itself in her mind the way towns always did, in layers of usefulness. First the practical layer: the chandler's shop on the low street with barrels of pitch and coils of rope stacked outside, a loft window above with lamplight in it. Then the layer of exits: the frozen bridge to the south, closed for winter and crawling with officials; the mole where fishing boats tied up; the dark mouths of alleys between buildings, each one a road that went somewhere if you were desperate enough to find out where. Finally the layer of eyes: who watched, who looked away, who counted strangers. In every town there were three kinds of people and only the proportions changed.

Verrigan, she decided within an hour, was mostly the second kind — lookers-away. A town living off the edge of some war it wanted to forget, full of people who had arrived recently and stayed because leaving required more energy than remaining. Refugees recognized refugees the way dogs recognize dogs. Half the faces on the fish quay had that particular quality she knew from her own mirror: watchfulness worn smooth by repetition.

She begged work at six places.

At the fish-quay sheds they told her they had all the hands they needed, which was a lie delivered with varying degrees of apology. At a bakery a woman looked at her hands — cracked, rope-scarred, nothing like a seamstress's — and closed the door while Noor was still mid-sentence. At a tavern the answer came with a look that made her leave quickly and keep her knife loose in her sleeve for three streets afterward.

At the sixth place — the chandlery, the one with the loft lamp — the door was answered by a man who looked like furniture someone had tried to make invisible: medium height, medium build, brown hair going gray at the temples, hands that were wrong in a way she couldn't place until later. Too careful. Hands that had been trained to do something other than what they now did, and performed their new work like a pianist mending fences.

Noor had learned long ago that the best lies were the ones closest to true, so she gave him the true parts first.

"I need work," she said. "I can mend, haul, scrub, count, carry. I don't eat much." She paused. "That last part is verifiable."

Something crossed his face that was almost a smile and got lost on the way. He studied her with eyes that were doing the same calculating she was — exits, eyes, proportions. A man used to assessing doors and windows.

"You came off the Atlas," he said.

"Yes."

"They put you ashore."

"Yes."

"Why come to me? There are fish sheds closer to the quay."

"Because the fish sheds said no already," Noor said, "and because your loft has a lamp burning in daylight, which means someone lives up there who isn't you, and a man hiding extra people under his roof understands why a girl needs work that comes with a place to sleep."

The silence that followed lasted four seconds, and Noor counted every one of them, because in four seconds a man either decides you're clever or decides you're dangerous, and clever was survivable, and dangerous was sometimes survivable too.

"That's a lot of noticing," he said quietly, "for a stowaway."

"That's a lot of hiding," she said, "for a carpenter."

He looked at his own hands as if they'd betrayed him. Then he stepped back and opened the door wider.

"I can pay in meals and floor space, not coin. Not yet. There's crate-work and net-mending and hauling pitch, and if anyone asks, you're my sister's girl, come for the winter, helping out."

"Do you have a sister?"

"No."

"Then neither do I," Noor said, and stepped inside.

* * *

The chandlery smelled of tar, hemp, linseed oil, and beneath everything the faint sweetness of old wood. The loft above it held, as she had deduced, a household in hiding: two children — a boy of perhaps eight with shadowed eyes that flinched at loud sounds, and a girl of eleven or twelve with a scavenger's sharp face and flour on her sleeves — and a landlady downstairs named Mrs. Havel who charged rent in gossip and pretended not to count footsteps overhead.

The children watched her the way she remembered watching strange adults at the second border: from behind cover, cataloguing threat. The girl, Marta, catalogued openly, chin up, arms crossed. The boy, Tomas, watched from behind his sister's shoulder and looked away whenever Noor glanced back.

"You're the stowaway," Marta said. Not a question. Word traveled fast along the waterfront, faster than ice formed, apparently.

"I'm the stowaway," Noor agreed. She set down everything she owned in the world — the coat, the tin cup, the fish knife, the false papers, a stub of pencil, and a small flat stone worn smooth that she kept in her pocket and touched, once, the way other people touch charms — and sat down on the offered pallet as if her legs had merely been waiting for permission.

"What's the stone?" Marta asked.

"Marta," the boy whispered, scandalized.

"It's a stone," Noor said. "From a river." She touched it again and put it away. "You cross a border with somebody, you end up carrying a piece of the place. That's just how it works."

"Which border?"

"The one with the river."

Marta's eyes sharpened. She was clearly a child who understood that information was currency — Noor recognized the exchange rate in her face and filed that away for future reference, because children who traded in information were useful and dangerous in roughly equal measure, and it was better to know which you were dealing with early.

"We came overland," Marta said, in the tone of someone making a deposit. "Before the bridge broke. Our uncle brought us."

"Your uncle's the carpenter?"

"Our uncle's whatever he says he is today," Marta said, with the precise bitterness of a child who loved someone and knew too much about them. "Today he's a carpenter."

Tomas tugged his sister's sleeve and whispered something. She relayed it with the weariness of long translation duty: "He wants to know if you ran from soldiers."

"No," Noor said, and watched him begin to relax, and decided — not consciously, it was more reflex than decision — that she would not lie to this boy. "Soldiers stopped looking for me years ago. After that I was running from geography. Geography is worse. Soldiers get bored. Rivers and mountains never do."

Tomas considered this with enormous seriousness, nodded once, and retreated behind his sister, which Noor understood to be approval.

* * *

Work, it turned out, was real. The carpenter — Dunmore, he said, though the name sat on him like a coat belonging to a taller man — paid as promised: breakfast, supper, a corner of the loft, and small tasks without end. Crates to repair for the shipping agents. Rope to coil. Pitch kettles to tend. And nets — God, the nets — endless gray webs of them brought up from the quay for mending, because the freeze had driven the boats in and left the fishermen nothing to do but repair gear and drink and gossip.

Noor threw herself at the work the way she threw herself at everything: completely, silently, and with an economy of motion that came from years of never wasting a calorie. By the third day she could splice. By the fifth she could read a net's damage at a glance — where the tear was simple wear and where it was a snagged anchor, where a knot was honest and where it had been retied by someone lazy. The dockworkers began, grudgingly, to nod at her. One of them, an ancient man called Sef with fingers like driftwood, watched her work for most of an afternoon and then corrected her sheet-bend grip with two words instead of twenty, which among that tribe constituted a decoration for valor.

At night she lay in the loft listening to the house settle and the harbor ice grind, and she took inventory.

Three borders. That was the past, and the past was a country she did not intend to visit again.

Present assets: roof, food, work, a name (borrowed, functional), a knife, a stone, and — this was new, this was the strange item in the ledger — a household. The children had begun leaving things for her: Marta saved her the crust ends she pretended not to want; Tomas, wordlessly, had shown her how the loft lamp wick was trimmed so she wouldn't burn it wrong and give them away. Small trusts, laid down like kindling. She handled them carefully. She had carried fragile things before.

Present liabilities: she had no papers worth the name, no coin, and a face that the harbor official had already noticed once. When the Atlas sailed — and the ice said soon, days now, maybe less — every eye on that waterfront would be counting who boarded and who didn't. A girl with no ticket and no country would be exactly the kind of arithmetic officials liked to show their work on.

And beneath the liabilities, unspoken but heavy as ballast, the thing she was beginning to sense about the house itself: that the carpenter and his careful hands were not hiding from nothing. That Marta traded gossip like a dockside trader and didn't know yet what information cost. That men had begun appearing at odd hours near the chandlery — broad men with sea-coal eyes who looked at the windows too long, and once a woman in a captain's coat who stood across the street in the fog for a full minute, motionless, like a lighthouse that had decided to stop working and start hunting.

Noor had survived three borders by learning to read the weather in people. The weather over this little house was changing.

On the eighth night, she came up to the loft late, after hauling pitch kettles until her arms burned, and found Tomas sitting upright on his pallet, awake, in the grip of whatever nightmare visited him most nights — she heard it through the wall, the small trapped sounds, the name he sometimes said that wasn't his sister's and wasn't his uncle's and must belong to someone the sea or the soldiers had taken.

She didn't wake him. She sat on the floor beside his pallet, within reach but not touching, and she waited, the way you wait out weather. After a while his breathing slowed. After a longer while he blinked at her in the lamplight, embarrassed.

"You were dreaming about the crossing," Noor said. It wasn't a guess.

Tomas nodded.

"I was on a ship for eleven days," she said. "In the dark. Under the ropes. Nobody knew I was aboard except me." She held up her hand, fingers spread. "Eleven. You know what I thought about? Not monsters. Not drowning." She leaned close, conspiratorial. "I thought about bread. Every night, bread. Specific bread. The round loaves with the cross cut in the top that a woman in a mountain town sold me once, right out of the oven, so hot I had to hold it by the paper. That's what I fought the whole ocean with. One loaf of bread."

Tomas's mouth twitched. "That's silly."

"It's tactics," Noor said. "The dark can't hurt you if you're somewhere else. Bread is somewhere else. So are round loaves with crosses in them. So are rivers with stones in them, if you had somebody with you when you crossed." She tapped her pocket where the stone lived, and saw his eyes follow the gesture, and saw him understand it, because children who had crossed things always understood.

"Did you cross with somebody?" he asked.

"No," Noor said. "Not the last one."

"Then who gave you the stone?"

"I took it," she said. "Somebody has to bring the pieces back."

He lay down then, and this time he slept. Noor banked the lamp and sat a while longer in the dark, listening to the harbor ice applaud its slow, terrible applause, and let herself do the thing she almost never allowed: she planned past survival.

When the Atlas sailed, she would not be on it. Fine. She had been left by better ships than that. But she was done being cargo that washed ashore wherever the tide got bored. Whatever happened next — the sailing, the ice, whatever storm was gathering around the carpenter and his locked cases and his borrowed names — she intended to have a hand on the tiller this time. Fourth border or no fourth border. She had crossed worse with less.

Below her, in the frost-bright street, boots rang on cobbles — several pairs, unhurried, passing the chandlery door, pausing, moving on.

Noor blew out the lamp and went to the window, and through the fog she watched the shapes resolve: men in seamen's coats, and at their head, unmistakable even at distance by the way the torchlight bent around her, the woman in the captain's coat, looking up at the chandlery's darkened windows the way a lender looks at a debtor.

Counting the lamps, Noor thought. Counting what burned, and what had just gone out.

She turned from the glass, quiet as a held breath, and went to wake the others.
