## 13. Bargains in Smoke

The Anchor & Iron smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular despair of men who drank at two in the afternoon because the ice had taken their work. Cyrus chose his seat the way he chose all his seats now: back to the wall, sightline to the door, close enough to the stove to look like a man warming himself and far enough from it not to be noticed lingering. He had a glass of the house's thin dark beer in front of him, half-finished, and a length of rope he was splicing into a repair strop — dockwork he'd carried inside so that even his drinking looked like labor.

He should not have come. That was the first honest thought he allowed himself all day. Marta had said there would be flour at the chandlery by evening; Tomas needed new boots before the real cold arrived; Noor — though she'd never asked directly — had been eating less than her share for a week. The errands could have waited. But Ilsa Brandt had stopped him on the quay that morning with word that made his stomach drop like a stone in still water: the harbor had frozen overnight in the inner basin, earlier than any winter in living memory, and the *Atlas*'s captain had gone aboard without a word to anyone. When a captain went silent, either he was negotiating or he was leaving. Both possibilities meant the same thing: the clock Cyrus had been living against had just started running faster.

So he had come to the tavern, where rumor collected like condensation on glass, to learn what the town knew about the ship's departure.

What he learned, in the first twenty minutes, was nothing about the *Atlas* and everything about himself.

"—swears he saw her crew taking soundings off the north channel," a fish-hauler was saying to his neighbor, low but not low enough. "At night. With no lights."

"Nobody sounds the north channel in winter unless they mean to sail it."

"You don't say."

"I do say. And I say when Rhea Vance starts sounding channels, somebody's paying her to go somewhere, and it isn't anywhere honest."

The talk moved on — bridge gossip, ticket prices, a fight over forged papers at the fairground — but Cyrus had stopped hearing it. His hands kept working the splice, needle through strand, strand through needle, while behind his face something cold settled in and made itself at home.

Rhea Vance was sounding channels. Rhea Vance, who had found him on the mole three weeks ago in the fog, who had looked at him the way an assayer looks at ore and named his weight almost exactly. *You mend nets like a man who's mended worse things,* she'd told him then, smiling. *I collect interesting people.* He had laughed it off. He had walked away feeling the way a deer feels crossing open ground — exposed, unshot, lucky.

She was not sounding channels for someone else's cargo.

*Don't drink the last of the beer,* he told himself. *Leave enough in the glass that you look unhurried.* He rose, wound the finished strop around his fist, dropped coins beside the empty half, and was halfway to the door when the room's noise changed.

It didn't go quiet. Nothing so dramatic as that. It simply reorganized itself around a new center of gravity, the way iron filings find a magnet, and Cyrus understood before he turned that she had entered and that every conversation had adjusted its volume to make room for hers.

"Carpenter." Her voice reached him across the room, warm as brandy, pitched so that only a man already listening for it would catch the edge beneath the warmth. "You're a hard man to find. And I'm a woman who finds things."

He turned. She stood near the hearth in a coat the color of wet slate, fur at the collar too fine for this town and worn carelessly enough to pretend otherwise. Two of her men lounged by the door they'd come through — not blocking it, never quite blocking it, but making the geometry of leaving complicated. Behind her back, her first mate, a slab of a man called Doss, examined the ceiling beams with the studied boredom of a guard dog told to sit.

"Mistress Vance." He inclined his head the precise degree owed to a shipping owner by a dockhand. Not more. "I wasn't aware I was lost."

"Everything in Verrigan is lost. That's what makes it profitable." She crossed to his table without hurrying, took the chair opposite as if it had been held for her, and nodded at the bench beside her until he sat — because refusing would be a statement, and statements were what he could not afford. "Sit. Finish your beer. You'll want your hands steady for what I'm about to show you."

"I'm a carpenter, Mistress Vance. My hands are always steady."

"So they are." She smiled. From her coat she drew a folded square of paper, opened it on the table between them, and turned it so it faced him.

It was a manifest. Cargo list, vessel name redacted by ink, port of origin redacted, destination left blank — but the column headings were in the civil service hand he knew better than his own these days, and the seal at the foot, though smudged, had been pressed by a matrix he could have drawn from memory in the dark. A republic seal. The republic's seal. On paper that had no business existing anymore, in a harbor town at the end of the world, in the hands of a smuggler.

He looked at it for exactly as long as a curious carpenter would look at another man's paperwork — two seconds, mild interest, nothing more — and then looked up at her face.

"Nice handwriting," he said.

"Isn't it?" She folded it away again, slowly, letting him watch her watch him. "Found it in the effects of a drowned man. Terrible thing, drowning. Terrier thing, all the questions that follow a body ashore. Who was he? What was he carrying? Why did his pockets hold papers sealed by a government that the newspapers tell us doesn't exist?" She leaned back, spreading her hands, the picture of a reasonable woman burdened with unreasonable mysteries. "And here's the thing that keeps me up at night, carpenter. That seal. I've seen it once before. Three weeks ago. On a case sewn into a man's coat, in a fog, on a mole, belonging to a stranger with careful hands who came to my town claiming to fix crates."

The tavern noise washed around them. Someone fed the stove; the fire popped. Cyrus became aware of his own heartbeat and made it slow down by force of will, the way he'd taught himself to during interrogations in another life, in another country, wearing another man's name.

"I think you've mistaken me for a courier," he said. "Couriers dress better."

"I think," Rhea said, "that you're the most interesting thing to blow into Verrigan since the war blew everyone out of everywhere. And I think you know exactly what I'm holding, because you flinched — right there, at the corner of your eye, don't bother denying it, I've watched faces across card tables for fifteen years and yours is a good one but not that good — you flinched when I opened the paper." She let that sit. Then, softer, almost kind: "I don't want to be your enemy, carpenter. Enemies are expensive. I want to be your ferryman."

"Ferryman."

"The *Atlas* sails soon. Days, not weeks — you know it, I know it, the whole frozen town knows it. One berth left by the booking ledger, maybe two if the captain's mood improves. And a private vessel" — her eyes glinted — "a fast one, recently surveyed, with a captain who knows the north channel like her own kitchen, might be persuaded to carry passengers. Four passengers. A family, say." She let the number land and watched it land. "A family of four, with papers, warm cabins, no ledgers, no manifests, no magistrate reading names aloud at the gangway. For a price."

The splice-strop was cutting into Cyrus's palm. He hadn't noticed he was gripping it. He set it on the table, flat and deliberate, and made himself meet her eyes.

"And what price does a ferryman charge?"

"A book." Rhea's voice dropped to nearly nothing, so that he had to lean across the table toward her, which he understood was the point — the posture of conspiracy, performed for any watching eyes. "Not the drowned man's copy. Yours. The original. The key along with it. I know there's a key, because a code without a key is just expensive paper, and nobody sews expensive paper into their coat for sentimental reasons."

"And if I were this courier you imagine," Cyrus said quietly, "why would he trade a thing like that for passage? He could buy passage."

"He could try." She shrugged, elegant, terrible. "But passage bought honestly can be revoked. Tickets get challenged. Names get read at gangways by magistrates who've lately developed sudden appetites for inspection — you may have heard Corven's men have been busy." Something flickered behind her eyes at her own mention of the magistrate, satisfaction banked like coals. "Passage bought from me stays bought. That's the difference between me and the law, carpenter. The law sells you hope. I sell you arrival."

For a moment — one long, dangerous moment — Cyrus let himself actually consider it. Not the trade; the arithmetic underneath it. Four berths. Warm cabins. No gangway, no ledger, no magistrate. Tomas asleep without screaming. Marta eating until she was full. Noor standing on a deck as a passenger instead of hiding under one as cargo. It would cost him the codes — the codes that were already worthless, that served a republic that existed now only in dispatch cases and graveyards, that had cost him his commission and his name and his sister and would, if he carried them much further, cost the children too. Burn them or trade them, either way they had to stop being his problem. Wasn't that already clear?

But then he looked properly at Rhea Vance — at the patience in her, the collector's calm — and he understood the trap inside the offer, and it was not the price.

It was that she was asking at all.

"You're wondering," she said, reading him again, damn her, "whether to believe me."

"I'm wondering," Cyrus said, "what happens to the last man who sold you something he couldn't afford to lose."

"He's rich," Rhea said. "He lives in Marburg and writes me letters at Christmas. You're thinking of other people's smugglers." She stood, smoothing her coat, and the two men by the door straightened and then thought better of it. "I'll leave the offer where you can find it. No deadline — deadlines are for amateurs, and besides—" she smiled down at him, and for one unguarded instant there was something almost like respect in it, which frightened him more than the threat would have— "you'll answer sooner than you think. Winter has a way of making everyone's position clear. Ice takes options away one at a time. All a sensible person has to do is wait, and count what's left."

She dropped a coin on the table — more than the beer cost, a tip for a dockhand, an insult dressed as generosity — and walked out. Doss followed. The room's noise closed over the space she'd occupied like water over a stone, and within two minutes the fish-haulers were arguing about bridge repairs as if she had never existed, and Cyrus sat alone with his half-empty glass and the coin, understanding exactly what had just happened to him.

* * *

He did not go straight back to the chandlery. He walked the long way, along the frozen inner harbor where the ice had grown gray and ridged overnight, past the moored dinghies locked in it like flies in amber, and he made himself think instead of feel, because feeling was a luxury the next few days would not include.

Here was what he knew. First: she had the drowned courier's duplicate case — or its contents — which meant the danger he'd buried under the chandlery floorboards was no longer a secret he kept; it was a secret she shared, and shared secrets have a way of becoming public property. Second: she had not gone to Corven with it yet. A woman like Rhea Vance did not sit on leverage out of sentiment; she sat on it because spending it prematurely would bring the magistrate's men crashing through the waterfront, and whatever the raid turned up would become the magistrate's, not hers. She wanted a quiet trade before the loud one. Which meant the raid was coming anyway — later rather than sooner, but coming — and his window for moving was measured in days at best.

Third, and worst: she had said *four*. He had told no one the size of his household. Not Sef, not Ilsa, certainly not the chandlery keeper. Four meant she had watchers on the loft, or on the children, or on Noor, and he had been so proud of his invisibility that he had never once checked whether someone else could see him.

He stopped at the harbor rail. Below him, a dockworker was chipping ice off a barge's gunwale with a maul, each blow ringing across the flat white silence like a bell in an empty church. Cyrus watched him and thought about cover — the word meant one thing to an infantryman and another to a hunted man, and he had been both, and both kinds ran out the same way: gradually, then all at once. Every refusal cost him. Refuse Rhea, and she tightened the screw a quarter-turn — a watcher on the children today, a whisper in Corven's ear tomorrow, the drowned man's seal spread on a magistrate's desk the day after. Each time he said no, he paid in safety, and she paid nothing, because asking was free and she could ask forever. That was the vise. It didn't crush you with force. It crushed you with patience.

There was a third path, of course — the one he'd been avoiding since the mole. Give her something true but incomplete. Sell her a page, a fragment, enough to keep her fed and looking elsewhere while he moved the children. Every instinct he had honed over ten years of carrying secrets said that was how professionals survived: never give everything, never refuse entirely, keep the buyer believing the next payment buys the rest.

But professionals survived alone. Professionals traveled light, unloved, unaccompanied, and died in comfortable rooms with their secrets intact. He had four people now. He had a boy who associated knots with bedtime and a girl who traded gossip for bread without knowing what bread cost, and a stowaway with a split lip who had already taken one beating on his family's behalf. The professional playbook was written for men with no one to lose, and he had burned that man's identity on a dock somewhere south of here and had never once, in his honest hours, missed him.

So: no fragments. No partial payments. Rhea Vance would ask again — tomorrow, next week, through an intermediary, through Corven — and every time she asked he would have to pay something just to keep saying no, until the account ran dry. Unless the *Atlas* sailed first. Unless the ice relented. Unless Corven's men searched some other street. Unless, unless, unless — a fugitive's life was a ladder of unlesses, and he had just felt the bottom rung crack.

He pushed off the rail and turned toward the chandlery, and it was then that he saw the boy.

A child's figure at the far end of the quay, small against the frozen harbor, hunched in a coat too big for him — watching the barge worker, watching the tavern door, watching Cyrus with the fixed attention of someone who had been sent to watch and wanted very much to do it well. Ten years old, maybe eleven. Cyrus did not recognize the face, which meant nothing; Verrigan was full of dock children. But the boy looked away too quickly when their eyes met, and there was a coin-bright eagerness in the fumbling way he pretended to study the ice, and Cyrus thought: *Rhea's watcher. Or Corven's. Or the gossip-monger's. Or all three, and none of them know whose boy they've hired.*

He did not change his pace. He walked home the way an ordinary tired carpenter walks home, past the fish stalls and the shuttered customs house, and only when he reached the chandlery door did he allow himself one glance back down the quay.

The boy was gone. In his place, farther off, a man in a stevedore's cap stood studying the same stretch of ice, and he was not studying the ice at all.

Cyrus went inside, barred the door, and climbed to the loft, where the lamplight was yellow and small and the whole world consisted of four souls and a floorboard. Marta looked up from mending a stocking. Noor was teaching Tomas a card game with a deck missing its court cards. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind came off the frozen harbor with a sound like slow tearing.

"We'll need to move soon," Cyrus said quietly. "Tomorrow, likely. There's a friend with a net shed by the east stairs. We'll go in ones and twos, like shoppers."

Marta's needle stopped. "Did something happen?"

He looked at her — at the sharp, guilty, brave face of her — and decided the truth, this once, was cheaper than the lie.

"Yes," he said. "Someone asked me a question today that I couldn't afford to answer. And now we wait to see whether she believes me."

Below them, out on the black water beyond the ice, a ship's bell rang once — the *Atlas*, taking soundings of her own, counting her days in a harbor that was closing around her like a fist — and Cyrus sat down among his borrowed family and began, calmly, to plan the moves of a man who had just discovered he was being hunted by someone with all the time in the world.
