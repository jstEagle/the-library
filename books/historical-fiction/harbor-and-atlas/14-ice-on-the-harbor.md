## 14. Ice on the Harbor

Ilsa Brandt woke before dawn to a silence she had never heard in Verrigan.

She had lived beside the harbor her whole life — first in her father's house above the sail loft, then as a bride in the bridge keeper's cottage with its windows facing the water — and she knew every sound the sea made the way other women knew their children's voices: the slap of hulls against pilings, the groan of mooring lines, the gulls quarreling over the fish offal at first light, the far-off cough of the Atlas's engines taking on steam for practice runs. The harbor never slept. It murmured all night like a sick man who would not quite die.

This morning there was nothing.

She lay still in the cold bed, listening to the absence, and understood before she looked what had happened. She dressed in the dark, wrapped her husband's old coat over her nightgown because the fire had gone out hours ago and coal cost more than pride could pay, and opened the door onto a world gone white and still.

The inner harbor had frozen.

Not at the edges, not in the thin lacework of pans that formed most Decembers and broke apart by noon. It had frozen whole — a single unbroken sheet from the mole to the shipyard stairs, gray-white under a sky the color of dishwater, so flat and vast that for one disorienting moment she thought the sea had simply been removed, lifted away in the night like a tablecloth pulled from under the dishes. The boats sat in it to their waterlines as if set into stone. Gulls stood on the ice where water should have been, looking affronted, refusing to believe in it.

And there, wedged fast at its berth, lay the Atlas.

Ilsa walked down to the seawall, her boots crunching on frost-stiffened gravel, and stood at the rail that had been Henrik's post for thirty years. She put her bare hand flat against the iron and felt the cold come up through her palm like a question.

"Henrik," she said quietly, "you would have known what this means."

She knew what it meant herself, of course. Everyone in Verrigan would know by noon. The harbor was closed. And the Atlas — the last steamer of the season, the last door out of the town before winter shut it entirely — was caught in the very ice that made leaving necessary.

* * *

By eight o'clock the quay was crowded, and by nine it was a kind of madness wearing the face of an orderly crowd.

Ilsa stood near the customs shed where she could see everything, the way she had learned to stand during Henrik's illness — slightly apart, hands folded, missing nothing. The dockworkers were out in force with poles and pikes, testing the ice at its edges, breaking pans loose from the pilings around the Atlas in the futile hope that a channel could be kept open. Old Sef was among them, mending a net with his back against a bollard as if it were any other morning, though his eyes moved constantly over the crowd. The chandlery's loft window was shuttered. Ilsa noticed that. She noticed most things.

Captain Ostrand of the Atlas appeared on his quarterdeck at half past nine, and the crowd's murmur rose to meet him like wind filling a sail. He was a heavy man, gray-bearded, honest by the standards of men whose honesty had been tested by forty years of harbors, and he did not make them wait for the news or dress it up.

"The ice master says three days," he called down. "Three days of this cold and no ship moves. If the wind comes southerly before then, we go. If it doesn't—" He spread his hands. "Then we sit until the ice breaks, and none of us knows when that will be."

"When do you sail if the wind comes?" someone shouted — Vosk, the wool factor, whose voice always sounded like a man arguing with God.

"Within the tide. I will not risk being frozen in solid. A ship that cannot move is a dead ship."

"And the passengers?" That was Frau Kessler, who ran the boarding house, whose voice carried the way thin ice carries — with cracks running through it. "My sister's family has waited six weeks—"

Ostrand raised a hand, and the quay went quiet in the way crowds go quiet when they already sense the answer will be bad.

"I'll say it plain," he said. "There are four hundred names on my manifest and berths and deck passage for perhaps two hundred. Until now I have taken people in the order they applied, and turned no one away who had money for a ticket even without a berth — deck space, standing room, whatever the weather allowed." He looked out over their heads toward the bridge, toward the road south that no longer went anywhere. "That's finished. When the ice clears, I board confirmed-ticket holders only. Cabin and steerage, booked and paid, name matched to manifest. No deck passengers. No standees. No exceptions."

"Why?" The word came from several throats at once.

"Because a ship overloaded in ice is a coffin," Ostrand said flatly. "Because my owners will not insure her otherwise. And because—" he paused, and Ilsa saw something flicker across his heavy face that was almost shame — "because I am told, and I believe it, that when I open that gangway there will be more people on this quay than I can carry, and some of them will not ask politely. Order is the only kindness left to me."

He turned and went below, and the quay erupted.

It was not screaming, not yet. It was worse than screaming — it was arithmetic. Ilsa watched it happen in a hundred faces at once: the rapid, terrible calculation of people discovering that a thing they had counted certain was now a lottery. The wool factor grabbing his clerk by the sleeve and hurrying toward the shipping office, already composing arguments. Three fishermen from the outer row pushing through the crowd together, moving with the sudden unity of men who had just realized they shared a problem. A woman Ilsa didn't know — new to town since the bridge failed, one of the stranded travelers who slept four to a room at the Anchor — standing perfectly still amid the motion with her hands pressed flat to her sides, as if holding herself together, and then turning and walking very fast toward the ticket office as if walking fast could hold her place in a line that no longer existed.

By noon the shipping office had a queue four streets long, and by two o'clock it had a riot.

* * *

Ilsa had gone home to mend — she mended everything; it was how she prayed — and was at the window stitching a tear in a net float when the sound came up the hill from the waterfront: not shouting now but a deep animal roar, the sound a crowd makes when it stops being a crowd and becomes a flood.

She reached the ticket office as the window shattered.

They had not broken in for money. That came later, and only a little. They had broken in for the ledger — the great bound book in which the Atlas's agent recorded every booking, every payment, every name — because a name in the ledger was a berth, and a berth was life, and the book was behind a barred window with a frightened clerk inside it and four hundred desperate people outside it. Ilsa arrived in time to see two men wrenching the office door off its hinges while others surged in behind them, and the clerk — a pale boy of maybe nineteen — climbing onto his desk with the ledger clutched to his chest like an infant.

"Back!" the harbor sergeant was shouting, wading into the press with two constables, his baton doing little but making noise. "Back, all of you! Names will be honored! No one loses a booked place!"

"That's a lie!" a woman screamed, and the terrible thing, Ilsa thought, watching from the edge, was that no one could prove it wasn't. That was what scarcity did. It made truth impossible, because everyone had a reason to lie, and so every statement became just another move in the fight.

The boy with the ledger was dragged down off the desk. For one horrible moment the book itself was in the air between twenty grasping hands — Ilsa saw it spin, saw pages tear free and flutter like white birds among the dark coats — and then the sergeant and his constables reached it, and somehow, whether by courage or luck or the simple weight of uniformed bodies, they got the ledger back and the crowd shoved out and the door held shut against them with furniture.

The torn pages drifted down onto the quay. People fell on them. Ilsa watched a gray-haired man pick up a scrap with half a name written on it, read it, and close his eyes as if struck. Somewhere in that paper snow was either his future or a stranger's.

She walked home slowly through streets that felt changed. Shopfronts were closing early. At the Anchor, travelers' trunks were stacked in the doorway like sandbags. Two boys were selling places in the ticket queue — actual places, numbers chalked on slate — and grown men were paying. Outside the chandlery, Rhea Vance's people were loading crates onto a lighter at the one gap the dock crews had kept open in the ice, working with the unhurried competence of people for whom chaos was not weather but climate, and Ilsa caught the captain herself standing on the mole, watching the crowd around the ticket office with an expression Ilsa could only call satisfaction.

Their eyes met. Rhea Vance tipped her head, almost courteously, and smiled, and Ilsa walked on with her spine straight and her heart beating hard. Henrik had warned her once, years ago, about the smugglers: *They don't break laws, Ilsabe. They wait for the law to rot, and then they harvest.* The harbor was rotting in front of her. She could smell it.

* * *

That evening she went to the bridge.

She went most evenings now, though there was no duty left to perform. The drawbridge had died with the first hard frost — its gearing seized, its counterweights dropped, its iron jaw hanging open over the channel like a broken promise — and the engineer, a decent, harried man named Poltz, had delivered his verdict three weeks ago: parts fabricated in the south, spring at the earliest, the season lost. Since then the bridge had become Verrigan's monument to its own captivity. Travelers came to look at it the way pilgrims look at relics. Some wept.

Ilsa climbed the maintenance stair to the operator's platform out of habit, took out Henrik's wrench — she carried it everywhere now, a foolish talisman, three pounds of cold steel — and laid her hand on the seized mechanism as she did every evening, the way you touch the gravestone of someone you're angry with.

"It should not take a spring," she told the machinery. "Henrik replaced that gear train himself in 'eleven, with parts from the yard here. Poltz is wrong, or Poltz is lying, or someone has made sure the parts don't exist."

She had begun to suspect the third thing. She had watched the yard foreman's face when Poltz mentioned the gear castings — a small flicker, quickly killed, the look of a man who has seen something in an inventory that shouldn't be there. And she had heard, secondhand through the harbor gossip that flowed through Verrigan like bilgewater, that Rhea Vance's lighters had been unusually busy this autumn, carrying south-bound cargo that no one seemed to inspect.

Tonight she stayed later than usual, in the dark, thinking. Below her the frozen harbor lay like a held breath, and the Atlas rode at its berth with lamps burning along its deck, a lit promise that might be kept and might not. Somewhere beyond the breakwater, out on the black open sea, was the rest of the world — her sister in Corvel, letters twice a year, grandchildren she had never held. Sixty miles as the gull flew. An ocean away by road, now that the road was severed at its only crossing.

Two hundred berths, four hundred names. Half this town was going to be left behind, and everyone on the quay this morning knew which half they intended to be.

* * *

She found Poltz the next day at the bridge foot, supervising two men who were chipping ice off the counterweight chains with more energy than hope.

"You told the magistrate spring," she said, without preamble. She had learned from Henrik that the best way to talk to engineers was the way you talked to weather — directly, and without apology.

Poltz wiped his hands and looked at her with tired wariness. "Mrs. Brandt."

"You told him the gear castings must come from Hallberg's works in the south, and Hallberg's works are closed for winter, and therefore the bridge waits for spring. I want to know why they must come from Hallberg's."

"Because that's the specification—"

"Your own predecessor recast a sector gear in this yard in nineteen-eleven. I have the receipt in my husband's papers. This yard. With this yard's molds." She let that sit. "You're a good engineer, Herr Poltz. So answer me as an engineer. Is the delay technical?"

A long silence. One of his workmen had stopped chipping and was listening openly. Poltz glanced at him, then took Ilsa's elbow and walked her a few steps along the railing, out of earshot, which told her more than anything he might say.

"The delay is logistical," he said quietly. "The gears could be cast here, yes. The bronze, however, cannot. There is no bronze in this town to cast. I put in a requisition for stock in November. It was answered last week." He drew a folded paper from his coat — an inventory sheet, stamped with the harbor trust's seal — and held it so only she could see. "Four hundredweight of gunmetal ingots entered this port in November under a salvage declaration. They never reached the trust warehouse. They were landed at the east slip and carted away by night. I have asked where. I have stopped asking."

Ilsa looked at the paper, then at the frozen harbor, at the Atlas waiting on its ice like a fly in amber.

"Vance," she said.

"I said no such name."

"You didn't have to." She handed the paper back. "How much bronze do you need, and how fast can your men work once you have it?"

"Six hundredweight. Ten days to cast and fit, working round the clock. Why?" And then she watched the understanding arrive in his face, slow as sunrise. "Ten days," he repeated. "The ice master says the harbor could clear in three. But if the bridge opens in ten—"

"—then nobody needs the Atlas at all," Ilsa said. "The road south reopens. Coaches, carts, anyone who can walk. Every soul in this town gets out who wants out, and Vance's monopoly on escape dies on the same day."

Poltz stared at the ice a long moment. Then he began, very carefully, to fold the inventory sheet, and his hands were not quite steady. "If I requisition that bronze officially, the requisition goes through the magistrate's office, and the magistrate's office—" He stopped.

"Is compromised. Yes."

"If I requisition it unofficially, I'm stealing harbor property."

"You'd be recovering it," Ilsa said. "From thieves." And then, because she had been a bridge keeper's wife for thirty years and knew exactly how much weight a structure could bear before it failed: "Herr Poltz. You don't have to decide today. But decide soon. Because in three days the ice breaks, and after that the only way out of Verrigan is a boat with room for half of us, and I have watched what half versus whole does to people. It does this." She nodded back toward the town, toward the boarded ticket office, the queue-sellers, the paper snow. "I am not prepared to live in that."

* * *

She did not sleep that night.

She sat at her kitchen table with Henrik's papers spread around her and the lamp turned low, reading receipts and requisitions by the light of a candle stub, and somewhere past midnight she heard the ice speak.

Everyone in Verrigan heard it, that night. The freeze had thickened past three inches in the inner harbor, and the tide beneath it kept moving, and the two facts could not coexist forever. Around two in the morning the whole sheet groaned — a vast, whale-deep sound, rising through the ground and the pilings and the bones of every house on the waterfront — and then cracked, somewhere out by the mole, with a report like a cannon. Dogs barked in a dozen yards. Windows rattled. And in the silence after, Ilsa heard doors opening up and down her street, and footsteps in the frost, and voices calling questions that no one could answer.

She went out onto her step. Neighbors stood along the street in nightclothes and coats thrown over them, breath steaming, faces turned toward the harbor. Down at the water the ice sheet had split in a long jagged seam that caught the moonlight like a scar. Farther out, the Atlas's riding lights burned steady and indifferent.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Frau Kessler, wrapped in two shawls, said what they were all counting:

"Three days. Maybe less. Maybe tomorrow."

An old man near the wall — Ilsa couldn't see his face, only his shape against the moonlit ice — said, in a voice like something worn smooth: "My son is in the north country. If I miss this boat I will not see him again until thaw. If the thaw comes early, spring. If it comes late..." He didn't finish. Nobody needed him to.

"We should organize," someone else said — Vosk the wool factor again, appearing out of the dark like guilt itself. "A committee. Those with tickets should coordinate, ensure the boarding goes properly—"

"Those with tickets," said Frau Kessler, very evenly, "are going to find out exactly how many neighbors they have."

And the street laughed — a short, ugly, frightened laugh — and dispersed, and Ilsa stood alone on her step listening to doors close one by one against the cold and against each other.

She thought of Poltz's folded paper. Six hundredweight of bronze, carted away by night. Ten days of labor. A bridge that could turn half a town's desperation into a road.

She thought about what it would cost her to go looking for that bronze — where it had gone, whose roof it slept under, what kind of men guarded it. She thought about Henrik, who had operated that bridge through two storms and one war and had never once refused a traveler, because a bridge keeper's whole creed was that people should be able to cross.

The ice groaned again out in the darkness, settling, testing its own strength.

"Well then," Ilsa Brandt said to the frozen harbor, and to the dead, and to no one. She went inside, put Henrik's wrench on the table where she could see it, and began, by candlelight, to make a list of every warehouse and cellar on the east slip — and of every person in Verrigan who owed her a favor, or her husband a debt, or both.

Tomorrow the town would wake and start clawing for two hundred berths. But Ilsa had seen, in an engineer's frightened face and a stolen inventory sheet, another number entirely.

Ten days. One bridge. Room for everyone.

All she had to do was steal it back.
