## 15. Marta's Bargain

Marta counted the coins twice, and both times there were fewer than the day before.

She did this in the corner of the loft where the lamplight didn't reach, crouched over the tin that had once held boot polish, and she counted the way her mother had taught her to count before a market run: quickly, without hope. Eleven coppers. Two of them were green at the edges from salt air. Yesterday there had been fourteen.

The arithmetic was simple enough for a girl of eleven to do in her head. Tomas ate like a growing boy because he was one. Noor ate like someone who had learned never to leave food on a plate, because a plate might be her last. Uncle Cyrus — she still couldn't say the name comfortably in her mind; he was *the lodger* half the time and *Uncle* only when he smiled — gave them everything on his own plate and claimed he'd never been hungry in his life, which was a lie so enormous it had a kind of grandeur to it. And Sef brought fish when he remembered, but Sef forgot more things now than he used to, or so Noor said, and Noor noticed everything.

Eleven coppers would buy bread for two days if she haggled. Three if she didn't eat.

She put the lid back on the tin and slid it under the loose board where Cyrus kept his secrets, though not the same secret, never that one — she'd seen him kneel at the far board near the window one night when he thought they slept, lifting it, looking down into the dark beneath the floor as if it were a well he could drown in. She had never asked. In Marta's experience, the questions you asked were the ones that got answered, and some answers changed the shape of the room you were standing in forever.

Down in the chandlery below, old Piet was arguing with a delivery man about the price of oakum, their voices rising through the floorboards like weather. Above the argument she could hear Tomas laughing at something Noor said, and the laugh did what it always did to her chest, which was squeeze it and warm it at the same time, like a fist around a coal.

She was the one who went out for bread. That had been decided early, in the first week, because Marta had a face people forgot. Tomas had their father's red hair, visible across any harbor. Noor moved like a stray cat, all edges and watching. But Marta could walk through the fish market with a basket and become part of the general grayness of girls running errands, and no eye followed her home.

That morning the fog had come in thick off the water, the kind that made the masts look like drowned trees, and Marta walked down Rope Lane with her hood up and her hand closed around the coins, doing sums.

Bread, four coppers if Grelda was in a mood, three if Marta caught her before the morning rush. Cooking fat, two. There was nothing left after that for anything warm, and tonight the wind had swung north, and Noor's cough had come back.

That was the whole of the ledger. That was what eleven coppers bought: almost enough, minus warmth, minus medicine, minus the small margin that kept a household from sliding off the edge of itself.

And then, at the mouth of Fishgut Alley, she saw the sign in the window of Greta Vosch's parlor, and everything afterward began right there, though Marta wouldn't understand the shape of it until much later, when understanding arrived like a bill long past due.

GOSSIP EXCHANGED FOR GOODS, the card read, in careful copperplate. NEWS IS THE ONLY CROP THAT GROWS IN WINTER.

* * *

Everyone in Verrigan knew about Greta Vosch. She was a broad, soft-spoken woman of sixty-odd who had turned her front parlor into something between a shop and a confessional. You came in, you sat by the stove, you told her something true, and depending on how true it was and how much she wanted it, she gave you tea, bread ends, sugar, an egg, occasionally money. Half the dockside called it the Liar's Pantry behind her back, and the other half called nothing at all, because the other half owed her silence.

Marta had passed the window a hundred times. Today, with the fog pressing against the glass and her stomach remembering yesterday's short ration, she stopped.

It wasn't stealing, she told herself. It wasn't lying, even. Grown-ups traded in this town every day — Rhea Vance's men traded in silence, the magistrate traded in signatures, the harbor master traded in berths. Nobody starved from telling a woman that the *Sea Lark* had come in short-handed, or that the harbormaster's clerk drank.

And what did she actually know? She knew there was a lodger above the chandlery. A man who kept to himself, who mended nets with hands too careful for a laborer, who paid Piet's sister rent in coin that didn't match his story. That wasn't secret. Half the quay had already noticed him; Sef had made a joke about his hands within a week of meeting him. Marta wouldn't be telling Greta anything new. She'd just be — packaging it. The way fishmongers wrapped herring. The goods inside were the same whether she carried them or somebody else did.

She stood in the fog for a long moment, hearing Tomas laugh again in memory.

Then she pushed open the door, and a little bell rang, and the smell of the parlor — strong tea, lamp oil, dried lavender — folded around her like a curtain.

"Well." Greta Vosch looked up from her armchair, where she sat surrounded by knitting that never seemed to progress. Her eyes were pale and quick and missed nothing, which Marta registered too late as a problem, because a face people forget is only invisible to people who aren't looking for it. "A new face. Sit down by the stove, child. You're blue round the ears."

Marta sat. The stove heat hit her legs and nearly undid her resolve right there, because warmth was a currency too, and she was suddenly rich in it.

"Tea?"

"I can't pay for tea."

"Tea's free. Everything else costs." Greta poured anyway, unhurried, and set the cup in Marta's hands. "So. What brings a girl out on a morning like this? Bread money short, I'd guess, from the way you're holding your basket like a shield."

Marta looked into the tea. Steam rose off it in slow ropes.

"You trade news," she said. "What kind of news?"

"The useful kind." Greta picked up her needles, put them down again. "Ship movements. Prices before they're posted. Who's quarreling with whom. What the magistrate eats for breakfast and who paid for it. What's under whose floorboards — " she smiled, mild as milk, " — metaphorically speaking."

The word *floorboards* went through Marta like a fishhook, and she had to remind herself, firmly, that the woman couldn't know. Couldn't possibly know. It was a general question. Grown women talked in generals all day.

"There's a lodger," Marta said, "above Piet's sister's chandlery. On Rope Lane."

"Mm." Greta's needles clicked once, twice. "There are lodgers all over Verrigan this winter. The bridge being out and all. Stranded folk everywhere."

"This one's different." And here Marta paused, because she was at the edge of something, and some sensible voice far back in her head was saying *turn around*, and another voice — the one that had done the counting last night, the one that had heard Noor coughing through the thin wall — was saying *you're already wet, you may as well swim.* "He says he's a carpenter. He doesn't mend like one. He mends nets the way — " she searched for it — "the way a person writes letters. Careful. Like it matters. And he pays rent in southern coin, the old republic kind, and he goes down to the mole every day at low tide to watch the *Atlas*'s berth, and he stands there counting something on his fingers, and he's got children hidden up there that nobody's supposed to see, and — "

She stopped. The sentence had run further downhill than she'd meant it to.

Greta Vosch had gone very still. The needles stopped clicking entirely, and somehow that silence was louder than the bell had been.

"Children," Greta repeated, softly, tasting the word.

"A boy and a girl. They're — " Marta's mouth was dry. "Family. Mine. That part isn't news, that part's mine, you can't have that part."

"Of course, of course." Greta resumed knitting, but her hands had a different quality now, brisk and precise, like a clerk entering figures. "And the man himself. Big, small? Scarred? Accent?"

"He talks like a book sometimes. When he forgets himself. He — " Marta thought of the knots, of Cyrus teaching Tomas the bowline by lamplight, of the map he'd drawn in charcoal on a plank, coastlines from memory, a whole sea recalled line by line. "He knows the whole southern coast by heart. Every port. He drew it once, from nowhere, just out of his head."

Silence again. Outside, the fog horn sounded, once, distant, like something clearing its throat.

Greta reached under the side table and produced a cloth bundle, unwrapped it: four thick slices of dark bread, a twist of paper she opened to reveal sugar, and, deliberately placed on top, a single silver coin worth twenty coppers.

"That's for what you've told me," Greta said. "And there'll be more each week, if you keep your ears open. Nothing that puts anyone in danger, you understand." Her smile returned, warm as the stove. "I'm just an old woman who likes to know her neighbors. Verrigan's a small town. Small towns survive winter by knowing things. That's all this is."

Marta took the coin. It was heavier than it should have been, or her hand was lighter; she honestly couldn't tell.

At the door, Greta's voice caught her, gentle as a hand on the shoulder:

"One thing, child. The lodger. Does he have a name?"

And Marta, who had been raised better, who had been drilled in caution since the day her uncle first appeared in her life wearing another man's coat, heard herself answer with the truth, because it seemed so harmless next to everything else she'd already given away:

"Dunmore. He calls himself Dunmore."

* * *

She bought bread, and fat, and — because the silver coin burned in her pocket like a coal of guilt — a twist of honey candy, three pieces, and a paper of throat lozenges for Noor that cost more than the candy.

The walk home took ten minutes. She spent eight of them constructing the story she'd tell: she'd helped a woman carry parcels, that was all, and been paid for it, because rich ladies paid for carrying, everyone knew that. By the time she climbed the chandlery stair she had it smooth and worn like a river stone, and it came out of her easily when Noor asked, eyebrows up, *where'd all this come from?*

"Carried parcels for a lady on Rope Lane," Marta said, setting the loaf on the table. "She was generous."

Noor looked at her a moment longer than comfortable. Noor had survived three borders, Tomas said, and it showed in moments exactly like this one, in the way her eyes did arithmetic. But she was also seventeen and starving and the loaf smelled like heaven, so she only said, "Well. Thank the lady for us," and started cutting bread.

Cyrus came up the stair an hour later, smelling of tar and cold, and the household assembled around the table the way it had begun to do, unremarked, four chairs and a crate, and for a while Marta watched them the way you watch a fire you've built — proud of the warmth, aware of the danger, unable to remember exactly which spark had started it.

Cyrus broke the loaf and gave Tomas the biggest piece without appearing to, which was a trick Marta had catalogued early. He asked Noor about her cough; she lied about it; he pretended to believe her. Tomas recounted a dream from the night, a nightmare really, but told now in the daylight-safe way, ships with teeth, water rising, and Cyrus listened with total attention, the way he listened to everything, as though nothing anyone in this loft said could ever be unimportant.

*This is fine,* Marta told herself, chewing the sweetest bread she'd eaten in a month. *He said nothing dangerous. She said herself, nothing that puts anyone in danger. It's just talk. Talk is like fog — it goes everywhere and touches nothing.*

But the coin sat in her pocket through the whole meal, and once — only once — Cyrus glanced at her across the table, a glance that lasted perhaps half a second longer than his glances usually did, and she could not for her life tell what he saw there, and that frightened her more than anything Greta Vosch had asked.

* * *

Three days later, Marta went back.

She hadn't planned to. But the coin was spent — bread, fat, a second candle, wool socks for Tomas from the rag stall — and the tin held four coppers again, and winter had settled onto Verrigan like a lid. Besides, Greta had said there'd be more. Weekly. Just for keeping her ears open. And keeping your ears open wasn't work, exactly. It was practically the same as living.

This time the parlor felt different, though Marta couldn't have said why. The stove was just as warm, the tea just as bitter, Greta just as soft. But there was a second teacup on the tray, unused, still faintly steaming, and a chair drawn up slightly from the table at an angle, as though someone had risen from it shortly before Marta knocked. A man's glove lay on the sideboard. Good leather, salt-stained.

"Sit, sit," Greta said, and poured. "How's the household? Everyone well?"

"Well enough."

"No coughs? Fevers?" A pause, delicate as a pin going into cloth. "Small rooms, winter, children — sickness travels fast in lofts. Be a shame if anyone took ill right before a sailing."

"We're all well," Marta said, and heard how thin her voice came out.

"Good, good." Knitting needles, click click. "Now. My neighbor asked me a question the other day, and I confess I couldn't answer it, and he does hate to be disappointed. He wanted to know — purely idle curiosity, you understand, the kind of thing sailors wonder about over brandy — when the lodger means to board the *Atlas*. Has he a ticket yet? Has he been to see the mate? Or is he waiting for something?" The needles paused. "Waiting, perhaps, for the ice to make up his mind for him."

Something cold slid down the back of Marta's neck, colder than the fog outside, and for the first time she understood — fully, physically, the way you understand a wave only when it breaks over you — that the second teacup had not been there by accident, that *my neighbor* had a face she'd seen on posters nailed to the harbor office wall, a face above the words REWARD and QUESTIONS and APPLY TO THE OFFICE OF MAGISTRATE CORVEN, and that the glove on the sideboard belonged to a hand that had shaken others in dim rooms over things like *Rope Lane* and *children* and *Dunmore*.

"He hasn't said," Marta whispered.

"But he will board. When it sails. He'll want the children aboard." Not a question. Greta said it the way you read off a list. "Which berth do they sleep in, child? Bow end or stern? Near the hatch, or away from it?"

"I don't — I don't know anything about berths. Please. I don't know anything about — "

"There, there." Greta set down her knitting altogether and leaned forward, and her face rearranged itself into kindness so practiced it was almost a craft, like lacquer over wood. "No one's in any trouble. Least of all you. You've been a great help, and help deserves paying." The silver coin appeared between her fingers. Then a second one beside it. "You've a good heart, Marta. I can always tell. You're doing all this for your brother and your cousin, aren't you? For the coughing girl? That's love, child. There's no shame in love."

*Then why,* Marta thought, taking the coins because her hand did it without asking her, *does it feel like drowning?*

She walked home the long way, along the seawall, where the wind came raw off the black water. She tried to rebuild the story — *parcels, generous lady* — but the story had cracks in it now, and through the cracks she could see the thing she had actually done, laid out in sequence like washing on a line: the lodger, the children, the accent, the maps, the name. *Dunmore. He calls himself Dunmore.* Given freely, for bread and sugar and a lie about kindness.

She thought about going to Cyrus. Standing in front of him and letting the whole thing spill out of her, the parlor, the stove, the gloves, the questions about berths, and watching his careful face do whatever it did next. She got as far as the foot of the chandlery stairs. She stood there in the dark with her hand on the rail, and through the floorboards above she could hear Tomas laughing again — he laughed so easily now, that was the miracle of the loft, he laughed more every week — and she thought: *if I tell, the laughing stops. If I don't tell, maybe it was nothing. Maybe the neighbor was only curious. Maybe fog touches nothing.*

She climbed the stairs, put forty coppers' worth of lies in the tin, and said nothing.

* * *

That same evening, in a parlor on Rope Lane lit by a single lamp, Greta Vosch wrote three lines on a slip of paper in the same careful copperplate as her window sign, folded it once, and sealed it. She gave it to a boy of fifteen who earned his keep running messages through the fog, pressed a coin into his palm, and said:

"Captain Vance's boat. The one tied at the north mole. Don't let the watch see you go aboard."

The boy pocketed the note and vanished into the white.

And in the loft above the chandlery, Marta lay awake between Tomas's steady breathing and Noor's suppressed cough, staring at the rafters, listening to the tide turn in the harbor below — out, always out, toward the open sea, carrying whatever it carried — and somewhere across the sleeping town, in a cabin that smelled of rope and rum, Rhea Vance broke the seal, read three lines by lamplight, and began very quietly to smile.
