## 16. The Search Warrant

The warning came by way of a boy selling hot chestnuts, which was how most things came in Verrigan when the weather turned.

Cyrus bought a twist of paper full of them because that was what one did — dockhands bought chestnuts, carpenters bought chestnuts, men with nothing to hide stood at braziers and warmed their hands like everyone else — and the boy, a gap-toothed child of perhaps nine who had never once looked him in the eye in three months of trade, said to the coins rather than to Cyrus: "Magistrate's clerks came down the quay an hour before light. Taking names at the chandlery first."

"Is that so," Cyrus said.

"Sweeping every lodging from the mole to the fish market. Warrant posted on the customs house door." The boy folded Cyrus's coins into his palm and closed his fingers over them, a strange little ceremony, as if sealing a pact. "Said there's a courier in town with republican seals on him."

"Terrible business," Cyrus agreed, and walked away unhurriedly, eating chestnuts, while inside his chest something old and cold unfolded itself and began, methodically, to count.

* * *

He had always known this morning would come. He had simply persuaded himself, over the long gray weeks of nets and crates and lamplight in the loft, that it would come later — after the Atlas sailed, after the children were across the water and safe, after he had finished pretending to be a man who mended things instead of a man who carried them. Hope had made him sloppy. That was the truth of it, and he made himself say it plainly while he walked, because plainness was all he had left before the day ended: hope had made him slow.

Three months of slowness. Three months of saying good morning to Sef without scanning the quay behind him. Of letting Marta hum while she scrubbed the loft floor, letting Tomas fall asleep against his shoulder during lessons, letting Noor laugh at his knots. He had begun to trust the fog, the tide-bells, the particular creak of the chandlery stairs. He had let a town grow around him like scar tissue, and now the knife was coming to find out how deep it had healed.

The warrant changed everything, but it changed it in order, and Cyrus had learned long ago — in another country, under another name, from men who died of disorder — that panic is only arithmetic done badly. So he did the arithmetic walking, chestnut by chestnut, and by the time he reached the harbor steps he had the shape of the whole day.

First: the children were not at the chandlery. They were at the fish market running Noor's errands, which meant they would walk back along the quay directly through the sweep. Second: the cases. One sewn into his coat lining, one buried beneath the third floorboard from the wall, under six inches of salt-pork barrel and a year of other men's secrets. Third: Sef. Fourth: the tide, which would turn at half past two and give him cover either way, depending on which way he needed to be invisible.

He ate the last chestnut, brushed the paper into the harbor, and went to collect his family.

* * *

He found them where he expected — Marta bargaining fiercely over herring heads for soup, Tomas holding the string bag with the gravity of a quartermaster, Noor standing slightly apart with her arms crossed, watching the crowd the way she always watched it: exits first, faces second. She saw him coming before the others did, and something in his face must have shown, because she crossed to meet him halfway and asked, low, "What is it?"

"Warrant sweep. Waterfront lodgings. Starting at the chandlery." He kept his voice at the pitch of ordinary conversation, a trick he'd learned from a dead man whose name no longer existed. "They're looking for me. Or someone shaped like me."

Noor didn't waste breath on questions about innocence or proof; she had survived three borders and understood instinctively that neither mattered. "The kids?"

"Go get them. Herring woman, then home for nothing — you pass the chandlery, don't stop, don't look at the clerks, come out the alley behind Kessler's and down to the water. I'll meet you at the break in the sea-wall."

"And if we're stopped?"

"You're three refugees buying fish," Cyrus said. "Nobody sweeps for refugees. They sweep for couriers." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and swallowed it. "Ten minutes. If I'm not at the wall by the quarter bell, take them to Rhea Vance."

Noor stared at him. "You'd trust her?"

"I'd trust anyone with a boat more than I trust any clerk with a list." It wasn't entirely true and Noor knew it wasn't, but there was no time for better truths, and she went, quick and silent, threading the market like water finding its level.

Marta lingered a moment, herring heads secured, studying him with those too-shrewd eyes of hers — twelve years old and already fluent in the language of adults lying calmly. "Uncle Cyrus," she said carefully, using the name they'd agreed on, "is it a bad day?"

"It's a moving day," he said. "Sometimes they're the same."

* * *

The chandlery was easy to leave because it had never quite been his. That was the cruelty of it — he had spent three months making the loft a household and could strip it back to nothing in forty minutes, because everything that mattered fit into two canvas bags and a floorboard. He packed the children's clothes first, then the small hoard of domesticity that hurt most to fold away: Marta's tideline collection of blue glass, Tomas's knotted practice rope, the stub of candle stubs they'd melted together into one lopsided tower. These went in last, wrapped in the good blanket, because a household that leaves no trace has still, somehow, been robbed.

Then the floor.

He worked fast and quiet, prying the board with the blade of his trowel, and lifted out the second case — the drowned man's case, salt-stained, seal unbroken — from its grave of pork barrels and dust. Holding it again after weeks of deliberate not-thinking was like touching a stove he'd promised himself he'd forgotten. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Everything in Verrigan wanted what was inside it; the smugglers, the magistrate, whoever waited across whatever border for whatever republic these codes had outlived. He was worth killing twice over, and both times over a child's head, and he stood in the emptying loft feeling the weight settle into his hands like it belonged there.

He wrapped the case in sacking, nested it in the bottom of the larger bag beneath the children's clothes, and nailed the floorboard back down. Let them tear the chandlery apart. There was nothing under it but a story about pork.

At the window he allowed himself ten seconds — he counted them, because grief expands to fill whatever time you grant it — to look at the room. The table they'd eaten at. The map of the northern coast Tomas had drawn wrong on purpose, so that their town sat inland, protected by fictional mountains, and Cyrus had pretended not to notice. Ten seconds. Then he put on his coat, settled the sewn case flat against his ribs where a hundred dockworkers' shoulders had pressed without ever noticing, and went down the back stair into the alley smell of brine and tar, closing the chandlery door behind him as quietly as if it were a coffin lid.

He did not look back. Looking back was a habit for men who planned to return.

* * *

The town was already changing shape around him. That was the thing about warrants — they didn't just search houses, they searched people, and a town being searched moved differently. Dockworkers who had nodded to him for months now found reasons to examine cargo. The gossip women clustered at corners like gulls over a gutting table. Two clerks in magistrate's gray stood outside the customs house with a ledger open, and a queue of lodgers had formed beside them, each man stating his name, his trade, his length of stay, each answer going down in ink that might someday be read aloud in a room he wouldn't survive.

Cyrus became a carpenter again, thoroughly, the way an actor becomes a role. He limped slightly — a splinter injury, days old, plausible — and carried his bags openly, because a workman moving his gear between jobs attracted less attention than a man hiding anything. He walked the length of the quay without hurrying, nodded to the foreman at the timber yard, and cut through the net-drying yard where the frames stood like the skeletons of ships, strings of cork floats swaying in the cold.

Sef was there, of course. Sef was always there; the old man treated the net yard as other men treated their parlors. He was splicing a trawl line, fingers moving with the blind certainty of sixty years, and when Cyrus came to a stop in front of him he didn't look up.

"Heard they're taking names down the waterfront," Sef said.

"They are."

"Mm." A knot cinched tight. "And here you are, carrying your whole life in two bags at eleven in the morning."

"Sef—"

"I've got a shed," the old man said, still not looking up. "Smells like fish and dead crabs, leaks at the seam, and the roof comes down if a gull sneezes on it. Nobody's swept it since the last war, on account of nobody wants to go in it." He bit the twine off with his teeth and finally raised his eyes — pale, shrewd, entirely unsurprised. "There's a key under the third stone. Same as your floorboards, boy. Threes are unlucky, which is why nobody checks them."

Cyrus stood very still. "How do you know about my floorboards?"

"I mend nets by the chandlery window twice a week. You nail loud." Sef went back to his splice. "Take the shed. Take the little ones. Don't tell me why, because then I'm an honest man who doesn't know anything, and that's worth more than a lie that knows too much." His hands paused, just once, over the mesh. "I've hidden people before. It doesn't always end well. I'm choosing it anyway, and I'd thank you not to make it sentimental."

For a moment Cyrus couldn't speak. This was the worst part, worse than the clerks and the ledger — that the town he'd been prepared to flee from had, without being asked, decided to stand in front of him. "Sef. If they trace it—"

"They won't trace an old man's crab-smelling shed until spring, if ever, and by spring you'll have done something clever or something stupid, and either way it'll be over." He jerked his chin toward the sea-wall. "Quarter bell rang. Your lot's waiting."

* * *

The net shed was exactly as advertised — low, dark, reeking gloriously of fish, with a dirt floor and a single salt-fogged window that looked out on nothing but rope. It was perfect. It was the safest place Cyrus had slept since leaving the republic, and standing in its doorway watching Noor bank the little stove and Marta claim the dry corner with the authority of a general and Tomas unpack his knotted rope as though hanging pictures, Cyrus felt the adrenaline drain out of him and leave grief standing in its boots.

Because this was the shape of it, laid bare at last: he had come to Verrigan to be no one, and for three months the town had kindly permitted it, had fed him anonymity like bread, and now the permit was revoked. Every hour from here forward he would be hunted through streets that knew his walk. The chandlery loft was gone. The routine was gone. The small, hard-won almost-safety was gone, and what remained was a fugitive's arithmetic: hours, routes, luck. He had lived this way before. He had sworn, over his sister's grave, that the children would never learn to live this way, and here they were learning it in a fish shed, cheerfully, as if it were camping.

That was the part that broke something in him — not the danger, but how quickly they adapted. Marta had already inventoried the shed and posted Tomas to watch the window. Children learned fugitive grammar the way they learned language, by immersion, and every day they got better at it was a day he had failed them by a different measure.

"You're doing the face," Marta observed, not looking up from sorting cork floats.

"What face?"

"The one where you're sorry about something you haven't told us yet."

Cyrus sat down on a coil of rope that smelled of twenty years of herring. Outside, faintly, he could hear the sweep working its way down the quay — doors, questions, the scratch of the clerk's pen. Somewhere in that sound was the record of a man named Dunmore who had lodged above a chandlery since September, written down in ink, waiting to be noticed.

"Noor," he said quietly, when the children were occupied. "If it goes wrong — if they come here — you take the sea-wall route to Vance's ship and you don't come back for us. Swear it."

Noor fed a stick into the stove. "I watched you carry those bags like they were full of rocks and you didn't want anyone to see you strain," she said. "One of them's papers, isn't it? The kind people kill for."

"Don't."

"I'm not asking what's in them." Her jaw was set, that stubborn border-crosser's set he'd learned to fear and rely on. "But you should know I've seen men run from what they're carrying, and I've seen men hand it over, and the ones who gave it up were the ones who thought they could buy their way out one piece at a time. Whatever you're keeping, keep it whole, and decide now what it costs." She looked at him steadily through the stove-light. "You've already decided, haven't you. You just haven't said it out loud yet."

Through the salt-fogged window, down the lane, Cyrus could see the clerks' gray coats moving door to door, and behind them, unmistakable even at this distance, the tall silhouette of Rhea Vance watching her property values with her hands in her pockets, patient as ice.

Somewhere across the frozen harbor, the Atlas lay at her berth with three tickets in her purser's book and four names that needed to fill them, and Cyrus understood, sitting in the dark of a borrowed shed with everything he loved stacked around him in two bags, that the invisible life was over for good. From tonight forward every step was witnessed, every silence was evidence, and the town he had trusted was the same town hunting him.

The half past two bell rang across the water. Under the floorboards of the chandlery they would be pulling up planks by now, finding nothing but pork and dust — and finding nothing, they would widen the search, and widen it, until the whole harbor was a net drawing slowly closed.

"We stay quiet till dark," he told them. "Then we decide what comes next."

He said it like a plan. Only Noor, watching his face in the stove-light, heard it for what it was: a man discovering that the next move was no longer his to make.
