## 17. Among the Nets

Sef Brandt had mended nets for forty-one years, and in that time he had learned that every net tells you what it has caught. A herring net comes in light and silvered, smelling of the deep water. A cod net comes up heavy, dragging its sorrow. And a net that has been cut — slashed by a desperate knife in the dark of some hold somewhere — comes in with edges that curl like lips pulled back from teeth.

The four who came into his shed on the night the waterfront burned with lantern-light came to him like a cut net. Curling at the edges. Telling him everything by the way they frayed.

He had seen Cyrus Dunmore coming for weeks, of course. That was the thing about invisible men: only another man trying to be invisible could see them. Sef had spent thirty years watching men pretend not to exist — first as a ferryman in the years before the war, then as an old fool with cold hands and rope calluses — and he knew the posture. Shoulders drawn in to shrink the silhouette. Eyes that counted exits without appearing to move. Hands too careful with ordinary things, because they had once handled extraordinary ones.

The carpenter who mended crates and nets with a sailmaker's stitch. Sef had noticed that the first week. Nobody taught themselves a sailmaker's stitch unless they had stood on a deck where it mattered whether the work held.

So when Dunmore had come to him three nights ago, hat in hand, voice pitched low as chanteys sung into a wind, and said *I need a place, old man, just for a few days, I can pay* — Sef had looked at him a long moment across the smell of tar and herring brine, and had said:

"You'll bring children."

Dunmore had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man realizing the water is deeper than he thought.

"How—"

"Because men don't run," Sef said. "Men with money and no family, they walk out the front gate and tip their hats. Only men running *from* something small and breathing come to old net-menders at midnight." He had put down the shuttle he was working, deliberate, so the man could watch him choose. "How many?"

"Two. Mine. My sister's." The correction had come fast and raw, and Sef had understood it completely. *Mine* because he meant it. *My sister's* because he would not steal even the claim of it from the dead.

"And the girl? The stowaway off the Atlas?" Sef had asked, and watched the second stillness land. "Town's small, friend. Dockside talk travels faster than the mail boat. She works at the chandlery now. She's got a bruise on her jaw shaped like a fist and she holds her head like a girl deciding not to flinch anymore. She'll be part of this whether you say so or not."

"Then yes," Dunmore said. "All of them."

Sef had nodded once, picked his shuttle back up, and gone back to his net.

That had been the whole negotiation. Some things you did not haggle over. Haggling meant you were weighing, and there were weights a man should not put on a scale, ever, not once, or he'd find the scale remembered.

* * *

Now they were here, all four of them plus the old dog that belonged to no one and everyone, crowded into the net shed among the hanging coils and the smell of pitch, while outside the windows the torches moved along the quay like slow fireflies with bad intentions.

Sef made them tea on his single-ring burner, strong enough to stand a spoon in, because that was what you did. Ritual before refuge. You fed people; it told their bodies the emergency had paused, if nothing else.

The loft above his actual rooms was too obvious — the magistrate's men had already walked past it twice, Sef had watched them do it, taking their time, reading doorways. But the net shed sat on pilings over the water at the end of the rotting north pier, half-collapsed, full of gear nobody had claimed since the war took the boats it belonged to. There was a crawl space behind the stacked crab pots where the floorboards had rotted through to a dry pocket between the joists, big enough for children. Big enough, in a squeeze, for a grown man who had learned to fold himself small.

"The space behind the pots," Sef told them. "When anyone comes, you go there, quiet as fish. The floor sounds hollow but it isn't — I put a board over the gap two winters ago for exactly this reason." He let that sit. "Not for you. For the last ones."

Marta, the girl — nine, maybe ten, with her uncle's same careful eyes — asked the question children ask because adults are too cowardly to. "What last ones?"

"Marta," Dunmore said, low, a warning.

But Sef raised a hand. He found he didn't mind the question. He found, in fact, that he had been waiting twenty years for someone to ask it, and that it had turned out to be a child, which was either God's joke or God's honesty, and at his age he no longer distinguished.

"No," he said. "She should hear it. They should all hear it, since they're sleeping under my roof-line tonight." He lowered himself onto the upturned crate he used for a chair, and his knees announced their opinions as always, and he waited until the four of them had arranged themselves — the man standing, the girl curled against her brother, the stowaway Noor sitting cross-legged with her back against the wall where she could see both doors, which told Sef more about her three borders than any story would have.

"I wasn't always a net-mender," he began. "You probably guessed. Sailmaker's stitch gives me away." He glanced at Dunmore, who had the grace not to look away. "Before the war I ran a ferry out of the south quay. Small boat, honest work, tourists in summer, cargo in autumn. Then the republic started coming apart, and the work changed. People started paying not to go fishing, if you take my meaning. Paying to *leave*. And I discovered something about myself that surprised me, which at forty is a rare gift." He sipped his tea. "I discovered I didn't much care whose papers a man carried, or whose blood ran in a child, if the alternative was leaving them on the dock for whoever came next."

Tomas, the boy — seven or eight, with the hollow-cheeked look of a child who had learned hunger recently and thoroughly — had stopped fidgeting. Children always listened hardest to stories about other children.

"There was a route," Sef went on. "South along the coast, out past the light, to the neutral ports. I ran it eleven times. Eleven crossings, forty-some souls, and I never lost one. Never. Not to weather, not to patrols." He set the cup down. His hands, which shook slightly these days unless he was working rope, were steady now. He noticed that. He always noticed that — how the tremor left him when it mattered. As if his body knew better than his mind what his hands were for.

"On the twelfth crossing I took my son Pieter as crew. He was nineteen. Wanted to see the work, wanted to be part of it, wanted—" Sef smiled briefly, and the smile was an old coat, worn thin at the elbows. "Wanted to be like his father. Which is the most dangerous thing a boy can want, because fathers are rarely what sons imagine. We were boarded off the point by a patrol cutter — not smugglers, mind, not the corrupt kind, the *honest* kind, which is worse, because honest men follow orders and orders don't listen. Pieter panicked. Nineteen-year-olds panic beautifully; there's nothing half-done about it. He went over the side to swim for it, draw them off the passengers, give the families time to get below and play at being cargo." Sef looked at the dark window, where the torchlight slid past like a slow thought. "The passengers lived. Every one of them made landfall, and I know because I kept track for years afterward, wrote to them, got letters back from six countries. Forty-one souls, saved by a boy who couldn't swim well."

The shed was very quiet. Outside, somewhere down the pier, boots rang on frost-hardened planking, passed, faded.

"They never found him," Sef said. "The sea keeps what it takes without giving receipts. And I quit the route after that — everyone expected it, everyone said *of course, Sef, of course you did, who wouldn't.* And I let them expect it, because it was easier than telling them the truth."

"What truth?" Noor asked. Her voice was rougher than the girl's, sea-glass where Marta was river stone.

"That quitting was the coward's version," Sef said. "Twenty years I've mended nets and told myself I'd done my part, paid my price, retired from the war of it. Twenty years telling myself the risk had been spent, like a man who pays once and thinks the debt is settled." He looked around at them — at the fugitive with his sewn-in secrets, at the girl who scavenged tidelines, at the boy with nightmare shadows under his eyes, at the stowaway with her fist-shaped bruise and her unbroken stare. "And then a man with a sailmaker's stitch in his hands walks onto my dock, and I watch the torches come down the pier looking for him, and I find that the debt was never settled at all. It was just waiting for collection."

Dunmore spoke for the first time since the story began. "I can take them elsewhere. If this puts you at risk—"

"Sit down, son."

To Sef's mild surprise, the man sat.

"You misunderstand what I'm telling you," Sef said. "I'm not telling you a sad story so you'll appreciate your lodging. I'm telling you so you understand the terms. Twenty years ago I ferried strangers because it was right and I hadn't yet learned what it cost. Tonight I'm sheltering you *knowing* what it costs. That's not the same act. The first was instinct. This one—" he tapped his own chest, twice, "—this one is a decision. I've thought about it since midnight when you knocked, and again tonight when I saw the magistrate's men counting doors, and I keep arriving at the same answer. Deliberately. That's what makes it worth something. Pieter chose in three seconds of panic and it was the finest choice anyone ever made in front of me. I intend to make mine slowly, with my eyes open, the way an old man should."

* * *

They settled in after that, the way people do when a hard thing has been said and survived. Sef showed them the crawl space himself, made Tomas and Marta practice going into it silent — twice, three times, until the girl could slide in like an eel and the boy only knocked his elbow once. Noor insisted on learning the shed's sightlines, and Sef walked her through them: the gap in the north wall boards, the broken pane high in the gable that looked out over the pier approach, the two routes off the end of the pier onto the ice if it came to that — though he said *God keep us from that*, because the harbor ice was barely a week old and it lied about its strength the way young men lie about their courage.

"You've done this before," Noor said to him, not quite a question, as she memorized the wall gaps.

"Once upon a time."

"It shows." She hesitated, and for a moment the armor of her dropped and he saw the fifteen-year-old underneath, exhausted, jaw aching, running on will and chandlery scraps. "Why didn't you stop? After your son. Why start again now?"

Sef considered lying to her, decided she'd earned better, and said: "Because I dreamed about him every year on the anniversary, and in the dream he never asked me why I stopped. But lately I've started dreaming he asks anyway. Old men should get ahead of their dreams while they still can."

She nodded slowly, accepting this with the terrible economy of the young who have crossed three borders — everything weighed, everything used, waste forbidden. Then she went back to studying the pier approach, and the armor came back down, and she was a weapon again, and Sef thought: *that one will hold the line. Whatever line she's given.*

* * *

Past midnight, when the children had finally gone down — Marta curled like a comma, Tomas clutching the length of rope Dunmore had given him months ago, his talisman, his blanket of knots — Sef and the fugitive sat up together over the banked burner, sharing the last of the tea and the kind of silence that gets built rather than merely endured.

"She traded information about you," Sef said quietly. "The girl. Marta. Before all this."

Dunmore's head came up.

"Don't look like that. Dockside gossip runs through my shed like water through a torn net — I hear everything eventually. A fruit-seller bought something from her. Weeks ago. Whether any of it traveled anywhere useful, who can say. Rhea Vance buys gossip by the basket and uses maybe a tenth of it. But I thought you should know from a friend and not find out from a fist." He paused. "You knew?"

"Suspected. She's been buying bread and dried fish we haven't caught her earning." Dunmore rubbed his face. "What was I going to do, old man? Turn her out? She was feeding her brother. She's ten. Children in a siege do arithmetic differently than we do."

"Aye." Sef looked into his cup. "My Pieter did sums like that too. Always giving his portion away and calling it not being hungry." He set the tea aside. "Listen to me, Dunmore. Whatever name you're carrying this week. The magistrate's men were at my loft today asking about extra mouths. That means the circle is closing, and it's closing on this pier next — a shed on stilts at the end of a dead pier is where anyone would stash what you're stashing. So we need to talk about what happens when they come. Because they will come. Maybe tomorrow, maybe in three days, but they'll come."

Dunmore looked at him across the low flame, and Sef watched the man do his cold arithmetic — the case sewn somewhere, the duplicate case buried somewhere else, the children, the girl, the one berth on the Atlas that Sef had also heard about through his net-work of dockside water, because everything flowed through him eventually. Watched him weigh it all with that terrible bookkeeper's calm that Sef recognized because it was grief wearing an accountant's apron.

"When they come," Dunmore said at last, slowly, "you tell them the truth. You tell them a stranger begged shelter and you gave it, because that's the law of the pier and you're an old man and they'll believe an old man's sentimentality. You give me up. Do you understand me? The children won't be here by then — I'll move them. But whatever they take me for, it stops with me."

"No."

"It stops with me, Sef."

"It doesn't work like that," Sef said, mild as milk. "You think Rhea Vance's men arrest one man and go home satisfied? They arrest one man and shake his lodgings, his associates, his laundress, and the old net-mender who harbored him gets a visit from the *other* kind of questioners — hers — asking what he knew and when and why he didn't sell it to them first. Giving you up doesn't save me, son. It just saves them the trouble of finding me later." He smiled, and this time the smile was not worn at all. "Besides. You're assuming I'll be here when they come."

Dunmore went still in that way of his. "What have you done?"

"Nothing yet." Sef reached over to the corner where he kept his working coat, heavy with tar and years, and lifted from beneath it a folded chart, soft as cloth from handling, marked in pencil in a hand twenty years out of practice. "But I've been remembering a route south along the coast, out past the light, that I used to know like my own kitchen. And I've been noticing that the patrol cutter that used to work these waters got reassigned after the freeze, and that Rhea's boats are laid up on the far side of the mole tonight, and that the ice along the western channel is thick enough to walk a sled across if a man knows where the springs feed in thin." He spread the chart on the floorboards between them, and his hands did not shake at all. "I ferried forty-one souls out of a burning country once. I don't propose to sit in a shed counting torches while the number stays at forty-one forever."

For a long moment Cyrus Dunmore — carpenter, courier, ghost, uncle — stared at the pencil lines of a dead man's route, drawn by a living man's stubborn hand.

"You'd take the children out by sled," he said. "Over the marsh ice. Before the raid."

"I'd take the children out over the marsh ice, aye. And the stowaway, if she'll come, and I suspect she will — girls like her can smell a working plan the way gulls smell gutted fish." Sef began rolling the chart, precise, unhurried. "Which leaves you, and whatever it is you carry, and the berth on the Atlas, and whatever devil's arithmetic you're doing about who deserves it. I'm not touching that sum. A man's soul is his own ledger." He stood, knees protesting, and clapped the younger man on the shoulder with a rope-callused hand. "But you should sleep, son. Because tomorrow I take your family across the marshes to the fisherman's chapel at Kessel Point, where a widow keeps a stove and asks no questions — and you, my friend, will need to decide, before the tide turns, exactly what it is you're staying behind to carry."

Outside, far down the dark harbor, the ice boomed once — long and deep, the sound of the freeze settling its weight into the world — and in the west, faint through the gap in the wall boards, a lantern was moving along the pier road toward them.
