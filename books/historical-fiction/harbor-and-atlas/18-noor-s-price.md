## 18. Noor's Price

Noor had learned to count exits before she learned to count money.

It was the first thing you did in any new place — the border shed at Kelvaren, the freight yard at Ostrow, the fish market at Verrigan where she'd slept three nights among the barrels before the *Atlas* found her in its hold. You found the doors. You found the windows. You found the gap under the fence that no one watched because no one thought anyone would be fool enough to use it. Then, and only then, did you let yourself breathe.

The chandlery had two exits: the stair to the street and the loft window over the coal yard, which dropped you onto a roof slope soft with old moss and from there into the alley behind the cooper's. She had measured the drop with her eyes a dozen times. Four feet, maybe five. Marta could make it. Tomas would need a boost.

She was thinking about that drop now, standing at the counter of the chandlery while Mr. Pell argued with a delivery man about the price of oakum, because three men in oilskins had just come through the door behind her, and the smallest one had said her name.

Not a name. A description. "Girl, fourteen, maybe fifteen, dark hair, came off the *Atlas*, been seen around the waterfront asking after work." He wasn't talking to her yet. He was talking to Pell, who had gone the color of old tallow.

But she was the only girl in the room.

*Don't look at the loft stairs.* It was the oldest rule and the hardest one. The children were up there — Marta mending a shirt with clumsy stitches, Tomas supposed to be sorting rope ends but probably asleep against the flour sacks, both of them worn thin from a week of sleeping on Sef's shed floor and moving again every time a boot scraped outside. Cyrus had taken the second case to the shed before dawn. He should have been back by now.

Noor picked up a coil of twine from the counter and began, very slowly, to count its strands.

"Shopkeep," said the big one — the one in front, with a face like a slammed door and knuckles that had been broken more times than they'd been washed. "Question for you."

"I'm closed to questions," Pell said. "Open to sales."

The big one smiled without warmth. He set a coin on the counter, a silver piece, and spun it. It wobbled and fell flat with a sound like a small bell. "That's a question-asker's fee."

Pell looked at the coin the way a man looks at a snake he suspects is venomous. Behind him, the delivery man gathered his hat and left without finishing his argument, which told Noor everything about how this was going to go. People who stayed to watch were people who hadn't understood yet. People who left had understood.

"The girl," the big one said. "Dark hair. Came off the *Atlas*. Seen at the fish market, seen at the net sheds, seen going into buildings where she's got no business. Where does she sleep?"

"Why?" said Noor.

She hadn't planned to speak. The word came out of her the way words did sometimes — flat, quick, with an accent still clinging to it like burrs from another country's grass. All three men turned. She kept her eyes on the twine. Eleven, twelve, thirteen strands.

"You're her?" the big one said.

"I'm a girl," Noor said. "I came off the *Atlas*. They took me ashore and dumped me on your pier with nothing. I ask after work because I'm hungry. That's my whole crime."

"It's not hers we're asking about," the second man said. He was thinner, with a clerk's stoop and a smuggler's eyes, and it was him Noor decided to be afraid of. The big one was a hammer; hammers you could dodge. The thin one was a hook. "We're asking about who she sleeps *near*. Two children. Boy and girl. Arrived with a man called Dunmore. You know them?"

"No."

"You're sure?"

"I know nobody," Noor said. "Nobody knows me. That's how it works when you come off a boat with nothing." She let a little bitterness into it, and it wasn't even acting. "You want to interrogate somebody about being poor, start with half this town."

The big one laughed. It was not a kind laugh but it was a real one, and something in Noor's chest unclenched half an inch. Laughing men stopped counting exits. Laughing men looked at faces instead of staircases.

"She's got a mouth," the big one said to the thin one.

"She's got an accent," the thin one said. "North of the marshes. Or east." He drifted along the counter as he spoke, casual as a customer, and Noor saw his eyes travel — past the barrels, past the coiled line, up, just for a flicker, toward the loft stairs — and come back down empty and satisfied, and she understood with cold clarity that he had already looked, that he'd looked when he walked in, that this whole conversation might be theater performed for an audience of one shopkeeper, and that what happened next depended entirely on whether there had been anything upstairs worth hearing.

There had been. There was a cough. Small. Muffled. A child's cough, badly smothered, the kind that lived in cold lofts and thin chests.

Tomas.

Nobody moved. The sound hung in the chandlery like smoke.

"That," said the thin one softly.

"That's a rat," said Noor.

"A big rat."

"This whole harbor is rats." She turned to face him fully for the first time, and she made herself stand the way Rhea Vance stood, weight even, chin level, a person to whom things belonged and who belonged to nothing. "Lofts are full of them. Grain lofts especially. You want to check the loft, check the loft. But you'll want a warrant or a reason written down somewhere, because Mr. Pell here sells to half the crews on this waterfront and they'll hear whose men tore up a chandlery looking for vermin."

The thin one looked at her differently then. Not warmer. Recalculating.

"You know a lot about warrants."

"Two borders taught me about papers," Noor said. "Papers are all anybody wants. Papers and somebody to blame. I can't help you with either."

"We're not the law, girl."

"No," she agreed. "You're worse. The law writes things down."

The big one laughed again, shorter this time, and glanced at his partner as if to say *this one's wasted on a chandlery*. And that — that flicker of shared amusement between professionals — was the crack in the wall, and Noor went through it.

"Besides," she said, "if you're Vance's men, you're wasting her time. The children she wants aren't here. They're registered."

It was a lie so clean it surprised her. She heard herself say it the way she'd once heard her mother sing — a voice she didn't recognize coming out of her own body.

"Registered where?"

"With the magistrate. Corven. When the sweep orders went out, Pell made sure everyone lodging above shops had their names down. It's why I'm standing here and not hiding in a net shed like some criminal." She shrugged. "Go ask the courthouse. Ask Corven himself. Tell him Rhea Vance sent you to check his registry. See how he likes that."

Silence. The coin still lay on the counter. Outside, gulls fought over something in the gutter, and the tide sucked at the pilings beneath the floor, patient as everything in Verrigan.

The thin one looked at the big one. The big one looked at the coin. And Noor understood that the lie would work — that it would work *because* it was insolent, because it invoked Corven's name, because no frightened child tells armed men to bother a magistrate — and that it would also work against her later, when someone checked the registry and found no such names. Every lie was a debt. Her mother had told her that too, in a different language, hanging washing in a different country. *Every lie is a debt, Noor. Pay them fast.*

The men paid themselves with the coin. The big one swept it back off the counter, touched his cap in something between mockery and respect, and said, "Keep your ears open, girl. Information pays better than oakum. Captain Vance remembers people who help."

"I'll remember you refused to search a lawful shop," Noor said.

They left. The door banged. The bell above it shivered and went still.

For ten full counts Noor did not move. Neither did Pell. Then, from the loft, came a creak — the third stair from the top, the loose one, the one she'd told Tomas twice to skip — and Marta's face appeared over the banister, white as the flour sacks.

"Are they gone?"

"Get down," Noor said. "Both of you. Now."

* * *

They sat on the floor of the storeroom among the smells of tar and hemp, and Noor told them what to do next, because next was all anyone ever really had.

"When I knock twice and then once, you go out the loft window. Down to the coal-yard roof, along to the end, drop to the alley. Marta first, then you catch Tomas. You run to Sef's shed and you don't stop for anything. Not for me. Not for dogs. Nothing."

"What about you?" Marta asked.

"I'll be right behind you."

"You're lying," Marta said. Not accusingly. Nine years old, and she already knew the architecture of lies, having lived inside other people's.

"I'm lying slowly," Noor said. "It gives you time."

Tomas had started to cry, quietly, the way he did everything now — quietly, as though loudness had been confiscated along with his home. Noor crouched in front of him. Up close she could see how the weeks had carved him: hollows under his eyes, chapped lips, hands too big for his wrists. Three borders ago that had been her in a mirror.

"Listen," she said. "Those men are dogs on a chain. Dogs bark at anything that runs. So you don't run until I tell you, and when I tell you, you run *well*. Can you run well?"

He nodded, hiccupping.

"Show me your knots."

A ghost of a smile. He held up his hands and mimed the bowline Cyrus had taught him, fingers moving through the shape of a rope that wasn't there. *The rabbit comes out of the hole, around the tree, back down under.* Somewhere across the harbor Cyrus was presumably doing exactly what he always did, which was turning problems into plans, and Noor felt a fierce, unreasonable anger at him for not being here, and knew it was really fear wearing anger's coat.

"He'll come back," Marta said, watching her face. "Uncle Cyrus always comes back."

"I know."

"Then why are you scared?"

Because he's late, she didn't say. Because the second case is missing and so is he, and those are either the same fact or opposite ones, and either way men like the thin one will be checking registries by nightfall. Because I've been caught twice before and I remember exactly what it costs. Because you two are the first people since my mother died that I would rather bleed for than run from, and bleeding for people is a habit that doesn't quit gently.

"Because I care," she said, which was true enough to stand on.

* * *

They came back at dusk, and they did not ring the bell.

Noor was banking the stove when the door opened without knocking and the big one filled it, and this time there were four of them, and this time the thin one held a paper, and this time nobody laughed.

"Registry says," the thin one read, with relish, "no children. No Dunmore. No record of any lodger but—" he squinted at Pell's cramped hand— "a N. Saleh, laborer, age given as nineteen." His eyes came up. "You look young for nineteen."

"I eat well," Noor said.

"Where are the children?"

"There are no children."

"Marta," the big one said, stepping inside and closing the door with terrible gentleness. "And Tomas. We have a witness at the fish market. A gossip woman who sells better stories than fish. Says the chandlery lodger keeps two kids upstairs like contraband." He cracked the knuckles of his right hand, one by one, an accountant settling accounts. "So. One more time, and then we stop asking nicely. Where. Are. They."

*At Sef's,* Noor did not think. *Three streets north, behind the net racks, under the smell of tar where no dog's nose is worth bringing.* She put the location in a box and shut the box and stood in front of it, the way she had stood in front of her little brother at the second border, arms spread, telling the guard there was no one else in the wagon, only her and her mother and their grief.

"They were here," she said. "Weeks ago. Cyrus Dunmore hired me to watch them while he arranged passage. Then he took them south overland before the bridge broke. I stayed on because Pell pays honest." Every word cost her; she could feel each one leaving like blood leaving a body. "Check the road ledgers. Check the bridge toll book. There'll be a Dunmore in it."

"There'll be a Dunmore in a lot of books," the thin one said. "That's rather our problem." He folded his paper away and nodded at the big one, and Noor saw the nod for what it was — permission, procedure, the boring machinery of violence getting underway — and she had perhaps two seconds to choose.

Running was the sensible choice. Running was what had kept her alive across three borders and one drowned winter. Run out the back, over the coal roof, into the dark, and let whatever happened in this room happen to whoever was left in it.

But the loft stairs creaked — no, they didn't, she imagined it — and Marta's face floated up in her mind, and Tomas's hands making a bowline out of air, and Cyrus's voice saying, that first week, *you saved my life and I don't even know your name,* and her own voice answering, surprising them both: *Noor. My name is Noor.*

People who knew your name could hold you to things.

"I told you," she said. "South. Overland. Before the frost."

The big one sighed, genuinely regretful, a craftsman sorry to damage good material. Then he hit her.

* * *

Pain, Noor had discovered, was mostly information. The first blow told her the man pulled his punches when unprompted, which meant he wanted a confession, not a corpse. The second told her she'd guessed wrong about which cheek to turn. By the fourth she had stopped analyzing and started doing what she'd done at Kelvaren, at Ostrow, in the hold of the *Atlas* when the crew found her: she went somewhere small and interior, a room with no windows, and she counted.

She did not give them Sef's shed. She did not give them the coal-yard roof route. She gave them the overland story, over and over, varying it slightly each time — south road, then coast road, then a farmer's cart, then her cousin's cousin — because inconsistency reads as stupidity, and stupid people get dismissed, and dismissed people get to keep breathing.

"Where did Dunmore take the children?"

"South. Overland."

"Where is the courier's case?"

"I've never seen a case."

"You were seen with a man matching a courier's description at the fish market."

"I was seen with forty men at the fish market."

The blows came and she counted them and somewhere past ten she lost arithmetic and just held the number of her name. *Noor. Noor. Noor.* Her lip had split early; blood filled her mouth, iron-tasting, familiar. Her mother's voice surfaced unbidden: *Never let them see where it hurts, little bird. Show them a wound and they'll farm it.* So she showed them nothing. She lay on the chandlery floor with her arms over her head, and she looked up through the cage of her own elbows at the rafters where a family slept, and she thought, with a lucidity that felt almost like joy: *they never came upstairs. Whatever happens, they never came downstairs, either.*

The thin one crouched beside her at the end. That was the worst part, the crouching, the sudden courtesy of it.

"Last chance to matter," he said.

"South," Noor said, through the blood. "Overland."

He straightened. "Nothing here," he reported. "Gutter rat. Wrong accent, wrong age, wrong everything. Vance wants couriers, not orphans." To Noor: "Stay out of our sight, girl. Next time the questions get longer."

The door opened. Cold air came in like a blessing. And then — footsteps on the boards, fast, and a voice Noor knew, roughened by running:

"Noor—"

Cyrus. On his knees beside her before she could focus, his hands hovering over her without landing, a man suddenly afraid of everything he touched. Behind him, impossibly, Marta and Tomas, who were supposed to be at Sef's, who had clearly not stayed at Sef's, whose faces were the exact expression she'd spent eleven blows refusing to make.

"Don't," she managed. "Don't lift me yet. Count to a hundred first."

He counted. Of course he counted. His lips moved with hers, and when they reached a hundred together, in the dark of the ruined chandlery, with the tide going out and the ice creeping further every hour across the harbor where one berth remained, Cyrus lifted her as carefully as a full cup and said, "Why?"

Noor smiled through the split in her lip, and tasted salt, and told the truth for free.

"Because you're mine," she said. "All of you. I decided."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the loft stair creaked — and out on the quay, unseen, the thin man with the clerk's stoop paused in the shadows, watching the lit windows of the chandlery where a beaten girl was being cradled by the very man four searches had failed to find, and took out his pencil, and began to write.
