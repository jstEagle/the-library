## 20. The Frozen Fair

The harbor froze on a Tuesday, and by Thursday the market had moved onto the ice.

Ilsa Brandt heard it before she saw it — the particular sound of a town walking on its own reflection, boots and cartwheels and iron-shod poles chattering against the frozen surface like a thousand small bells rung by nervous hands. She came down from the bridge keeper's house with her husband's old coat buttoned to the throat and her good shawl over it, and she stopped at the top of the mole stairs and looked at what Verrigan had become.

The inner harbor lay white and still as a laid table. Out past the point, where the channel opened toward the sea, the water was still black and moving, but here — in the sheltered bowl between the breakwater and the quay — the ice had taken everything. The fishing fleet sat locked in place, masts bare, tarps frozen stiff as boards. And across the middle of it, where herring boats had worked all autumn and children had skated last winter, someone had strung lanterns on rope lines and set up stalls: the fishmongers first, then the wool merchants, then everyone else, until the whole frozen basin had become a fair ground with the sky for a roof.

They were calling it the Ice Market already. The name had traveled through the town faster than any news Ilsa could remember, faster than the armistice, faster than the fire at the cannery four years back. *Ice Market, Thursday, come early.* As if it were a festival.

It was not a festival. Ilsa knew that before she took the second stair down. You could hear it in the quality of the crowd's noise — not the bright layered chatter of a market day but a lower, flatter hum, the sound of people conducting transactions they did not want overheard. A festival was loud because everyone was happy. This was quiet in patches because everyone was selling something that shouldn't be sold.

She went down anyway. There were things a person needed, and there were things a person needed to see.

* * *

The stairs from the mole were salted with ash from somebody's stove, and Ilsa took them carefully, one hand on the rail. At the bottom, the ice began.

It was good ice, she'd give it that — a hand thick at least, gray-white and grained like polished bone, frozen down from the edges inward over three black nights. The town had tested it the way towns test things: two boys had walked out first, then a man with a horse, then a cart, and when none of them went through, the whole population followed. Now there were burlap runners under the stall legs and straw scattered in front of the fishmongers' slabs, and the whole surface rang faintly underfoot, a hollow note like a struck cask, so that Ilsa felt every step twice — once in her boot and once somewhere behind her ribs.

The stalls nearest the quay sold ordinary things at extraordinary prices. Salt pork. Lamp oil. Turnips gone soft at the crown. Ilsa walked the row slowly, listening more than buying, and the listening told her everything the sight of the thing already had.

"—heard it myself, from the mate's own cousin. Confirmed tickets only now. No ticket, no gangway."

"—three berths left, maybe four, and forty souls who want them—"

"—the *Atlas* sails when the captain says, and the captain says soon. Days, not weeks."

"—my brother-in-law paid eleven crowns for a third-class ticket in October. Eleven. Someone offered him sixty for it yesterday and he wouldn't take it. Sixty!"

That was the new arithmetic, Ilsa thought. Every conversation in Verrigan now contained a number underneath it, and the numbers were all climbing. Tickets had become currency — better than currency, because crowns would still be crowns in spring, but a ticket would be a life. She had watched prices behave badly before; she had never watched *salvation* develop an exchange rate.

At the fourth stall she bought turnips she didn't need, because the woman running it was Hesta Kroll and Hesta Kroll knew everything on the waterfront worth knowing and told it for the price of a customer.

"You've heard," Hesta said, wrapping the soft turnips in brown paper as though they were precious, which by next week they might be.

"I've heard days, not weeks."

"Days." Hesta leaned across the counter. "And only ticket holders past the barrier. The captain posted it last night. Which means—" she straightened, and her eyes went along the ice to where a knot of men stood near the center of the market, apart from the stalls, talking among themselves with the studied looseness of people pretending not to be together, "—which means the rest is coming."

"What rest?"

Hesta gave her a look that said *don't make me say it out loud on open ice*. "Forged papers," she said, barely above breath. "Somebody's been selling them. Off the end stall, past the wool. Go look. But don't buy, and don't ask questions."

Isla bought a twist of salt as well, for cover, and walked on.

* * *

The end stall past the wool merchant's line was nothing much — a plank table on barrels, a canvas awning stiff with frost, a young man behind it with a clerk's neat hands and a traveler's bad boots. He sold pens, ink, blotting paper, sealing wax. All perfectly legal. All arranged, if you understood arrangement, so that a person could stand at his counter and acquire, in five innocent purchases, everything required to make a passport.

He wasn't even pretending very hard. That was what frightened Ilsa most, standing at a distance with her basket and watching. A month ago such a man would have operated from a back room with shutters drawn. Now he traded in the open, under lantern light, in view of the whole town, because the whole town wanted what he sold and the whole town knew it, and shame, which had survived the war and the blockade and two changes of government, could not survive scarcity.

Around him the market arranged itself into its true shape.

Ilsa had lived beside this harbor her whole married life and she had believed she knew its factions — fishermen against cannery men, merchants against dockworkers, the magistrate's office against everyone. Those lines were still there, but they'd gone translucent, and behind them another map showed through, drawn in wants instead of loyalties. Near the pen-seller stood the people who had money: a cannery owner's widow with her fur collar up, two brothers from the timber office, a customs man off duty and drinking from a flask. Farther out, at the edges, stood the people who had nothing but their hands, watching the way hungry men watch through windows. And moving between them, touching elbows, trading low words, the brokers — the ones who'd realized that in a town where everyone wanted to leave, the man who could connect a seller to a buyer owned the only road out.

She saw Rhea Vance's people working the crowd. Of course she did. Two of them, a big man with a scarred jaw and a quick younger one, drifting stall to stall, not shopping, just being seen — and everywhere they passed, conversations died and restarted quieter. The smugglers weren't selling tickets. They were doing something worse: *appraising*. The big man stopped at the wool stall, spoke four words to the merchant, glanced once at the pen-seller's customers, and moved on, and Ilsa understood that she had just watched an inventory being taken. Not of goods. Of desperation, itemized by name.

A hand touched her sleeve. Hesta Kroll, materializing at her shoulder with her own basket held like a shield.

"Don't linger," Hesta murmured. "Vance's boys clocked you already. You're the bridge keeper's widow; they know you watch things." Then, before Ilsa could answer, pulling her a few steps aside, behind the frozen herring slabs: "You want to hear something? The bridge parts. The manifest. It's true, what some are saying — crates marked for the cannery, machine stock, sitting in Vance's warehouse since November, and every crate sized for our bridge gears. I know a stevedore who carried two of them. He didn't know what he was carrying till after."

Ilsa's heart gave one hard knock against her ribs. She'd suspected it for weeks — the bridge hadn't broken honestly; broken mechanisms failed messily, snapped teeth, bent shafts. Hers had failed *cleanly*, like a machine that had been helped. But suspicion was smoke. A manifest was a document. A stevedore was a witness.

"Where's the warehouse manifest kept?" she asked.

Hesta looked at her the way countrywomen look at women about to do something stupid or magnificent, without yet knowing which. "Ilsa Brandt," she said, "you're seventy paces from your own front door and you're asking me about smugglers' paperwork."

"The bridge is my husband's grave, near enough," Ilsa said. "He kept those gears turning twenty-two years. If they were pulled on purpose so this town couldn't leave—" She stopped, because the rest of the sentence was too large to finish standing on ice in public.

Hesta said quietly: "Harbor office. Second floor, the strong room behind the customs clerks' room. But there'll be a lock on it, and there'll be Vance's man within fifty feet of it, day or night." She squeezed Ilsa's arm once, hard. "Now walk away from me, dear, before we're both interesting."

Ilsa walked. But her mind was no longer on the market at all, and it took her half the length of the ice to notice what she was hearing behind her — the noise changing.

* * *

It started, as these things do, over almost nothing.

A voice raised near the wool stalls — "That's my name! Look at it, that's my *name*, where did you get it?" — and then a second voice, smooth, denying, and then the first voice again, cracking, and the crowd around the pen-seller's stall began to move the way water moves before it finds its level: away from the sound, then back toward it, everyone wanting to see and no one wanting to be close.

Isla turned and pushed forward with the rest, because she was the rest; there was no one in Verrigan that morning whose fear didn't outrun their dignity.

Two men at the stall. One was a fisherman she knew slightly, Anselm, young, a herring man whose boat was frozen in and whose wife had a sister across the sea. The other was a stranger — traveling man, city clothes worn wrong, the kind of face you forgot while looking at it. Between them on the plank table lay a passport, open, blue-gray, official-looking, real enough to stop your breath.

"That's the Verrigan issue stamp," Anselm was saying, jabbing a finger at the page. "That's my *name* in it. I bought a passport Tuesday, full price, clean paper, and now this man walks up holding the twin of it — same stamp, same clerk hand — how many did you sell him? How many of us bought paper that's worthless the moment the mate at the gangway sees the second copy?"

"It's a misunderstanding," the stranger said. His voice stayed pleasant. His hand had drifted inside his coat. "Different serials, friend. Different serials entirely."

"Show me the serials, then. Show—" 

The stranger's hand came out of the coat and it was not a gun, it was worse in a way, it was *fast* — it closed on the passport and tore it, once, twice, and the pieces went up in the wind off the point like small dark birds, and Anselm made a sound Ilsa had never heard a grown man make. It was the sound of a person watching money burn, except it wasn't money, it was the crossing, it was his wife's sister, it was the whole other side of the sea going up in confetti.

He hit the stranger with his whole body. Both of them went down on the ice, and the crowd roared and surged, and Ilsa was pressed backward by shoulders and baskets, and for a moment she saw nothing but coats and flying wool caps and the lantern ropes swinging overhead, throwing wheeling shadows across the white harbor like gulls.

Then the crowd opened the way crowds do around violence — a ring, breathing hard — and she saw the fight plainly. Anselm was stronger but the stranger had done this before; he rolled with the blows, got a knee up, got free, stood, backed away with both palms showing, blood on his lip, still pleasant, *still pleasant*, saying something to the ring of watchers Ilsa couldn't hear over the wind. And the ring — this was the part she would remember, the part she would carry — the ring didn't help the fisherman. The ring looked at the stranger with something that was nearly respect. Because he had torn the evidence, and the evidence was what everyone secretly feared: that the papers were forged, that the market was rotten, that the price of leaving had been paid by people who would never reach the gangway.

Anselm knelt on the ice picking up scraps of his passport out of the trampled snow, and nobody helped him either. Helping meant kneeling too. Kneeling meant admitting you might need to.

Rhea Vance's scarred-jaw man waded in at last, not for justice but for order — you could tell the difference; order protected commerce. He put a hand on each man's collar, separated them, said words Ilsa didn't catch, and the crowd absorbed the stranger, who melted backward between the stalls and was gone as though the ice had taken him. Anselm stood alone in the ring with a fistful of torn blue paper, and the market resumed around him at exactly the volume it had used before, everyone pretending, everyone recalculating, every buyer and seller in Verrigan now silently asking the same question: *is mine real?*

Ilsa found her hands were shaking. Not from cold.

* * *

She climbed the mole stairs slowly, stopping halfway to look back at what the town had made of itself.

From up here the Ice Market was almost beautiful — the lanterns in their long strings, the smoke rising thin and blue from the braziers, the crowd moving across the white harbor like a dark tide. From up here you couldn't hear the numbers being traded. You couldn't see the pen-seller's stall or the torn passport blowing out across the frozen basin, little fragments of blue settling onto the ice where the children would skate them flat tonight and read names that belonged to no one.

Scarcity, she thought, doesn't strip a town to its needs. Needs are simple — bread, fuel, passage, warmth. Scarcity strips a town to its appetites, and appetites have faces. She had seen them all this morning: the widow with the fur collar hoarding berths, the customs man with his flask, the brokers selling connections, Vance's people taking inventory of human want, the stranger tearing proof to pieces and being admired for it. And underneath all of it, patient as the ice itself, the biggest appetite of all — Rhea Vance's — which wanted not a berth but the whole ship.

Ilsa stood on the stairs with her basket of soft turnips and made herself say it plainly, the way her husband had taught her to say plain things about machines: the bridge was sabotaged, the parts are in Vance's warehouse, the manifest proves it, and the manifest is in the harbor office strong room behind a lock and a hired man with a scarred jaw.

A widow with a wrench against a smuggling empire. It would have made her laugh, a month ago. Nothing laughed now.

But she had spent twenty-two years keeping a drawbridge alive on spare parts, stubbornness, and other people's contempt, and she knew one law better than any magistrate in Verrigan: machinery can be stolen, paperwork can be burned, but a witness who has decided cannot be un-decided. The stranger had torn the passport. He had not torn Anselm. Anselm was standing on the ice holding the pieces, and Anselm had a wife, and a sister-in-law across the sea, and a memory of a stranger's face, and so did half the crowd.

Evidence burns. People don't.

Ilsa climbed the rest of the stairs, set her basket on the kitchen table, took her husband's oilskin coat from its peg, and turned toward the harbor office.
