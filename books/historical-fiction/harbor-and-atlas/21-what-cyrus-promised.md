## 21. What Cyrus Promised

The net shed smelled of tar and salt-rot, and in the dark before dawn it smelled like the hospital ward in Vellichory where Liana had died — that particular compound of carbolic and damp stone that Cyrus had never been able to scrub from his memory no matter how many harbors he passed through. He had not meant to think of her. He had come to the shed at four in the morning to check on the buried cases a final time before the day's negotiations, and instead he had sat down on Sef's overturned dory, and the smell had reached up out of the planks like a hand closing around his throat, and now he could not stand up.

So he let it take him back.

* * *

Vellichory, fourteen months ago. Rain on the hospital roof like applause for something nobody had done well.

The ward had been a converted ballroom in what had once been a merchant's house — you could still see the ghost of the ceiling frescoes above the rows of cots, cherubs and garlands gone gray under decades of pipe smoke, and now wounded men lay beneath them staring upward as if the faded angels owed them an explanation. Cyrus had walked past two checkpoints with papers that said he was an inspector of medical supplies, which was true in the sense that his satchel did contain bandages, wound around the thing that actually mattered.

Liana was in the third cot from the tall window. She had insisted on a cot near the window since her first week, when she could still walk to it, because the window looked east across the rooftops toward the river and the river was the color of the sky at dusk and, she said, if you squinted, it might have been the sea.

"Cy." Her voice was a whisper with gravel in it. The fever had been working through her lungs for six weeks, and every breath cost her something she could not afford. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other inspectors of medical supplies."

She laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and he held her hand through it and hated himself for the joke. When she could breathe again she studied his face the way she had always studied things — patiently, without mercy. She had been the older sister by four years, but she had read him since before he could remember being readable.

"You came through the north checkpoints," she said. "Your coat's wet on the left side only. And you're wearing a clerk's boots."

"Occupational hazard."

"Cyrus." Just his name, and the room went quiet around it. A man in the next cot groaned in his sleep. Somewhere below, a tram bell rang in the empty street.

He sat down on the edge of the cot, careful of her legs, and told her. Not all of it — never all of it, even then, even to her — but enough. The republic was dying by inches; the ministries were burning their own archives; his commission would not survive the winter, and neither, the doctors said, would she. And Marta and Tomas were in the care of a neighbor who had already asked him, twice, with elaborate politeness, how long he intended this arrangement to continue.

"The children," Liana said. It was not a question. It had never been going to be a question.

"I'll take them."

"Across the sea." Her fingers tightened on his. Her hands had been a pianist's hands once; the fever had stripped them to birds' bones. "Not across the river. Not to some farm in the south where they change your name slowly until you forget there was another one. Across the sea, Cy. Where they can be children. Where they won't grow up learning which door to be afraid of."

"Across the sea," he said.

"Both of them." Her eyes were very clear. Fever burned the nonsense out of people sometimes; he had seen it in dying soldiers, who spent their last clarity on truths they'd been dodging for years. "I know how you think. You're already doing arithmetic. You're thinking one is easier to move than two, one berth is cheaper than two, one child is harder to trace than two, and if the worst happens you can come back for the other. Don't. I can see it behind your face. Both, Cyrus. Swear it on something you can't take back."

"There isn't anything I can't take back. That's rather my specialty."

"Then swear it on me."

The rain applauded on the roof. Below the gray cherubs, the wounded men breathed in ragged unison, a congregation at a service none of them had chosen to attend. Cyrus looked at his sister's face — thinner than the face he carried in his memory, but underneath the wasting still unmistakably her, the girl who had taught him to swim by pushing him off the pier and waiting to see what he would do — and he understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only once or twice in a life, that she was asking him to become someone, not merely to do something. That the promise was a door, and once he walked through it he would not be the man who had made excuses on the far side.

"Both of them," he said. "Across the sea. On my name."

"Your name's about to be worth nothing."

"Then it costs nothing to promise it."

She smiled — the old crooked smile, the one that had gotten her free bread from three separate bakers during the famine year — and closed her eyes, and for a moment he thought that was the end of the conversation, and was grateful.

"Cy." Her eyes stayed closed. "You'll want to survive. Everyone wants to survive; it's the strongest instinct there is, stronger than love, most days. But survival isn't the assignment. I'm not asking you to keep breathing. I'm asking you to make sure they get to have lives. Do you understand the difference?"

He did not answer, because he did not yet understand the difference, and she knew it, and he knew she knew it, and that was the last dishonest moment they ever shared. She died nine days later, on a morning so bright the whole city seemed to have been washed and hung out to dry, and Cyrus signed the death register with a false name because his true name was already worth a prison sentence in three jurisdictions, and he walked out of the ballroom with its fading angels and did not cry, because crying felt like spending money he would need later.

* * *

The cost had begun before the funeral ended.

His commission — and here, sitting on Sef's cold dory in Verrigan with the frost creeping up the windows, Cyrus permitted himself the word he had avoided for over a year: it had been a good commission. Directorate Seven, cipher section, second chair. He had been young for it and quietly proud of it, proud in the way of a man who owns a fine instrument and keeps it oiled and tells no one. The work suited the shape of his mind: puzzles with consequences, loyalty expressed as precision. He had believed in the republic the way you believe in a bridge — not passionately, but structurally. You crossed it daily. You assumed it would hold.

But bridges, he had learned since, snap. And when they snap, everyone standing on them must immediately choose: jump to one bank or the other, or go into the water.

Directorate Seven dissolved in the third week of the bombardment, officially reorganized, actually looted — senior men selling codebooks to whichever future government seemed likeliest to pay pensions. Cyrus had watched his direct superior, a decent, dull man named Halvic who had never once raised his voice in eleven years of service, seal a leather case of current ciphers and carry it out of the building under his arm like a lunch pail, and Cyrus had understood that the case was now currency and that everyone who knew what it contained was either a creditor or a target.

He had done the unforgivable thing, the thing that cost him everything: he had reported it. Written it up, dated it, signed his true name, filed it through channels that by then existed mostly as tradition. Halvic was arrested — briefly; he was released in nine days when the arresting officers themselves defected, taking the file with them, and the file surfaced months later in a smuggler's archive, and Cyrus Dunmore, signatory, became a name on lists he had never consented to join. To the old loyalists he was the informer who had broken a colleague. To the new powers he was a witness who knew where bodies were buried. To the smugglers he was inventory.

And there was a worse entry in the ledger, one that did not appear on any list but lived in him like a splinter worked too deep to draw: the codes themselves. When Directorate Seven burned its archives, Cyrus had been the officer tasked with verifying destruction — and at the end of that long night, standing over a stove in a basement while the city shook, he had kept one set back. Not sold. Not traded. Kept, in a leather case sewn into the lining of a coat, for reasons he had told himself were strategic and knew, even then, were sentimental. They were the last living proof that the republic had existed as more than a rumor and a flag. They were also, he understood perfectly well, the single most dangerous object he could possess, a thing worth killing for in both directions, and he had carried them out of a collapsing country like a man carrying his own corpse on his back.

Survival, Liana had said. Survival isn't the assignment.

She had known him too well. Because in the fourteen months since her death he had done exactly what she predicted: he had survived, brilliantly, invisibly, at the price of everything except the children. He had surrendered his name — Dunmore was a death warrant in the coastal provinces now; he traveled under three others, rotating them the way other men rotated shirts. He had surrendered his profession, his country, his future, his right to grieve in public, his habit of looking people in the eye. He had become a carpenter, a dockhand, a clerk, whatever the harbor required, a man shaped like whoever was not looking at him. And he had told himself each morning that this was temporary, that the steamer would come, that across the sea he would set it all down and pick his sister's children up out of history like picking up two suitcases, and deliver them intact to a life where none of it had happened.

Intact. That was the lie at the center of it, and lying in the dark of the chandlery loft listening to Marta's careful, silent breathing — the child slept like a soldier, ready to run — he had begun to understand what Liana had tried to tell him from the third cot by the window. You cannot deliver children intact through a country on fire. You cannot preserve them from the crossing; the crossing goes through them. Marta already bargained like a dockside trader because hunger had taught her. Tomas already woke screaming at the sound of steam whistles. They were not luggage to be kept dry. They were people being forged, right now, in the same fire that was forging him, and his promise was not a delivery guarantee. It was a commission of a different kind: build them a life, out of whatever survives.

* * *

Frost had fully claimed the window by the time he surfaced. The shed was graying toward dawn. Out on the frozen harbor he could hear the ice talking to itself — that deep, structural groaning, like a giant turning over in sleep — and somewhere beyond it, faintly, the Atlas's crew washing down her decks, getting ready to take three names across the sea instead of four.

Cyrus sat with his hands flat on his knees and took honest stock of himself, which was a discipline he practiced perhaps twice a year and always hated.

He had promised a dying woman two children delivered alive into lives. He had not promised himself safe. Somewhere between the hospital ballroom and this frost-furred net shed, the promise had quietly rotted into a smaller thing: get yourself and whatever you can carry onto a boat. And the arithmetic he had been doing all week — one berth left, three children who needed it and only two names to cover, and himself, the adult, the carrier of secrets, the man worth killing — that arithmetic was just the rot showing through at last. He had been preparing to stay behind and call it nobility when in truth it was only the oldest cowardice dressed in a better coat: the cowardice of a man who believes he is what he carries, and cannot imagine being valued empty-handed.

Noor had taken a beating from Rhea's men for children she had known eleven days. Sef had opened his shed to fugitives knowing what it had cost him the first time. Corven was, at this hour, presumably deciding whether to burn his career for strangers. Even Ilsa Brandt, with her widow's wrench, was fighting for a bridge she might never cross.

And he had been planning to give away the one seat and call it love.

"I understand the difference now," Cyrus said aloud, to the empty shed, to the dead, to whoever kept the ledgers of such promises. His breath hung white in the air and slowly faded. "You don't get to have lives unless somebody stays to hold the door. Fine. But nobody holds it alone. That's the amendment. Take it or leave it."

The ice groaned out on the harbor, long and low, like assent or like warning — with that family of sounds you could never be sure which.

He stood, joints cracking, and began to work through the plan one more time in the new light, and it was different now, he could feel it turning in his hands like a knot finally loosening: the mate, the magistrate, the codes, the berths. Two berths minimum, clean papers for four, and if the world refused to sell him that, he would build it out of parts, the way you built anything worth having. He would walk into Corven's parlor tonight carrying the only leverage in Verrigan that mattered, and he would ask — not for safety, never again merely for safety — but for a family.

Outside, the first light found the harbor, and the Atlas stood black against the pink, and Cyrus Dunmore pulled his collar up and started back along the frozen tideline toward the chandlery, moving like a man who had finally decided which direction his name faced.

He did not yet know that Marta was awake in the loft, sitting up in the dark, working up the courage to tell him what she had done.
