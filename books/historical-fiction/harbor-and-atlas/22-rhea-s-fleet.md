## 22. Rhea's Fleet

Rhea Vance kept her ledgers the way other captains kept their trophies: locked, hidden, and consulted nightly. The cabin of the *Kestrel's Mercy* was small enough that a tall man had to duck under the deck beams, and she liked it that way. Small rooms told the truth faster. There was nowhere in them to perform.

She sat with the lamp turned low and three lists spread before her on the chart table, weighted at the corners with a compass, a pistol, and a tin of tea gone cold hours ago. The first list was crew. The second was cargo. The third — written in her own hand, in a cipher of her own devising, because she trusted no clerk alive — was names. Names of men aboard the *Atlas* whose wages had been paid late. Names of officers who drank. Names of the ones who would look the other way for coin, and the ones who would need something heavier than coin.

The harbor ice creaked outside her hull, a long groaning sound like a door opening in an empty house. She had grown fond of that sound over the past week. It was the sound of the town's options closing one by one.

A knock. Two short, one long — her own signal, answered by no more than three people in Verrigan.

"Enter."

Brann came down the companionway stooped, bringing the cold with him. Her bosun had a face like a shovel and the discretion of a gravestone, which was why he was bosun.

"The mate took the money," Brann said. "Second officer's still chewing on it. He'll chew till the last night, then swallow."

"And the engineers?"

"Both. The chief wants passage for his sister as well. I told him we'd see."

Rhea smiled. "Tell him yes. Sisters are cheap. Loyalty isn't." She turned the crew list toward the lamp and drew a line through two names. "These two won't do it. The older one prays and the younger one loves him. Take them off the watch rotation starting tomorrow. I want them ashore when it happens."

"Shore leave's not hard to arrange."

"Nothing is hard to arrange, Brann. That's the whole point of arrangement." She tapped the third list without looking at it. "And the magistrate?"

"Corven signed the sweep order. Waterfront lodging houses, the net sheds, the chandlery quarter. Your man watched from the fish market. They turned out a dozen refugees and found nothing but lice."

"They'll find nothing anywhere," Rhea said. "Because what they're looking for is already somewhere else." She thought of Cyrus Dunmore — or whatever his true name was; she had stopped believing in *Dunmore* weeks ago — sitting in a loft above a chandlery with two children who hadn't existed in his story until suddenly they did. A carpenter with hands that had never swung a hammer. A man who mended nets like he was defusing something. She collected leverage the way other captains collected cargo, and she had never once held a piece this heavy or this fragile.

"He didn't come to the tavern tonight," Brann said.

"No. He won't come again. I've asked my last polite question." Rhea stood, took up the cold tea anyway, and drank it. It tasted like tannin and patience. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow night I'm going to show him the future."

* * *

She chose the venue herself, because venue was argument. Not the tavern, where smoke made everyone equal. She sent a boy — paid in bread, trusted for exactly one errand — with a note in a fair copybook hand: *The customs house, top floor, nine o'clock. Come alone or don't come.*

The customs house had been built by men who believed the sea could be governed, which was why she loved it. Grand windows facing the water. A balcony of black iron where officials once stood to survey everything that entered their jurisdiction and imagine they controlled it. Now the building stood half-empty, its records sold or burned, its brass fittings stolen room by room. She climbed the dark stair with the ease of ownership.

Cyrus came at nine minutes past nine. Of course he did. A punctual man is a man who has calculated the cost of every minute, and she had known from the first week that Dunmore calculated everything.

He came out onto the balcony cautiously, coat buttoned to the throat, eyes going first to the exits — stair, window, roofline — before settling on her. Good instincts. Wasted on land.

"Captain Vance." His breath fogged. Below them the harbor lay sheeted in grey-white ice, the *Atlas* moored at her berth like a sleeping animal, lamps lit along her decks because her captain refused to believe winter had won. "You said come alone or don't come. You brought no one either. I find that interesting."

"I find it necessary. What I have to say doesn't work with witnesses." She gestured at the rail beside her. "Look at it. Go on. Really look."

He looked. The frozen harbor stretched from mole to breakwater, the fishing fleet locked fast in a lattice of grey plates, the channel out kept open only by the *Atlas*'s constant fretting and a borrowed ice-breaker that was due to leave any day. Beyond the breakwater, the open sea smoked dark under a low sky.

"You know what that is?" she said. "That's the last honest map anyone will draw this year. No borders on it. No seals, no stamps, no magistrate's signature worth the wax it's dripped in. Every government between here and the horizon has spent the last five years proving it can't protect anybody. The war saw to that. The peace is seeing to the rest." She turned to face him fully. "But the sea still keeps its oldest law. You get what you can hold."

"That's not a law," Cyrus said quietly. "That's weather."

"Weather is a law. It just doesn't lie about being one." She let that sit. Down on the ice, far off, two figures were dragging a sledge of firewood across the frozen inner basin, tiny and patient as ants. "You've been in my town eleven weeks, Mister Carpenter. You've mended nets, fixed crates, charmed Sef the fossil, and raised two children who call you uncle with a conviction that fools everyone but me. I've watched you lie so steadily the lies have become load-bearing. And I've watched you do it all while carrying something in your coat worth more than this whole frozen harbor."

His face did almost nothing. Almost. A tightening around the eyes, quickly smoothed. She could have told him the exact moment she'd first seen it — three days after his arrival, when he'd shifted his coat on the dock and the leather case had made a sound against his ribs no carpenter's tools make. She'd heard it across twenty feet of noisy pier. Some sounds you never forget. Dispatch cases had followed her whole life like dogs.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Don't insult us both." She said it without heat. Heat was for men who needed it. "Here's what I know, and I'll lay it out like cards, because I want you to see every card. One: there was a courier in this town carrying republican cipher material, and now there are two such sets in existence, because a dead man floated in off the tide with a case stamped in the same wax. Two: Magistrate Corven has one of those sets and doesn't know how to read it, which is making him sweat through his robes. Three: the man who can read both sets sleeps above a chandlery — or slept there until Corven's sweep frightened him into a net shed — and that man is standing in front of me pretending to be bored."

The ice groaned below them, long and low. Neither of them moved.

"Four," Rhea continued, "the *Atlas* sails in four days if the channel holds, with tickets for a hundred and sixty souls and demand from here to the bridge for ten times that. And five—" she smiled, and let it be a real smile, because the moment deserved one, "—I no longer need your codes to be rich. I need them only to be safe. Those are different needs, and I've finally sorted out which is mine."

For the first time, Cyrus looked genuinely uncertain. "Explain."

"Gladly." She leaned against the rail, coat collar up, and gave him the future the way she'd built it, plank by plank, in the small honest cabin of the *Kestrel's Mercy*. "The *Atlas* sails on schedule. Everything normal. Her second officer will have a change of heart about his wages around the river mouth, where the crew I've already placed take the wheelhouse and the engine room in the same five minutes. Nobody bleeds — I've promised Brann nobody bleeds, and Brann takes promises seriously. The passengers wake up in international waters on a ship with a new management structure and a new price list. Berths to the highest bidders. The hundred and sixty who paid already can stay aboard if someone ashore wires more money, which most of them can, because people who can afford steamship tickets have people. The rest wait at anchor while their families learn the difference between what a life is worth and what it costs."

She watched his face go through it: horror first, then arithmetic — he couldn't help himself, he ran the numbers even as he hated them — then the terrible question forming behind his teeth.

"The codes," he said.

"Are insurance. Nothing more. Whoever ends up holding cipher material from a dead republic makes enemies in four capitals. But whoever holds it *and* the man who can read it makes those enemies negotiate. I don't want to sell your secrets, Dunmore. I want to retire somewhere warm and untouchable, and your unreadable papers are exactly the kind of thing that keeps a woman untouchable. Held, not spent. A dowry, not a purse." She shrugged. "Or burned, if it comes to that. But burned by choice, on my timetable, not swept up by some magistrate or smuggled onto a steamer by a man with a price on him. Understand? Right now those codes are a bomb in your coat, and bombs don't care whose chest they're in. I'm offering to move it to mine. I've got a colder heart. It'll keep better."

He was silent a long moment. Out on the breakwater a lantern swung, signaling — she noted absently — one of hers, running the nightly check on the channel stakes.

"And the children," Cyrus said at last. His voice was very level, the way a rope is level just before it snaps taut. "Marta and Tomas. Where do they fall in your price list?"

"There it is," Rhea said softly. "There's the man." She straightened. "Children sail free. Both of them. And the girl too — Noor, the stowaway, the one who took Halversen's boot to the ribs on the quay and said nothing, which bought my respect and cost Halversen his place in my crew. All three sail free, with papers I'll have drawn by Thursday, on a ship where I decide who pays and who doesn't. Marta, Tomas, Noor — berths, food, landfall at a port of my choosing where nobody asks questions with badges on." She paused precisely. "One berth short."

The wind moved along the balcony. Somewhere below, inside the customs house, a shutter banged twice and went quiet.

"One berth short," Cyrus repeated.

"You've done the sum yourself. I've watched you doing it for a week — four souls, three names, and a ship that counts heads like coins. I'm not telling you anything you haven't already whispered to the first mate in the shadow of number two hold." She saw him flinch and felt no triumph about it; triumph was a luxury and she was past luxuries. "So here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, so listen the way you listen to ice. Come with me. Sail on the *Mercy*, not the *Atlas*. Be my clerk. You read six languages, you cipher like a machine, you're honest in the one way that matters — you don't steal from your own people — and you are wasted on carpentry the way a scalpel is wasted on kindling. The codes come to me, held in trust, unreadable without you. The children sail free and safe. Noor likewise. And you—"

"And I stand on your deck," Cyrus said, "while a hundred and fifty strangers learn what their tickets are really worth."

"A hundred and fifty strangers who will mostly live," Rhea said, "because I run a business, not a slaughterhouse. Compare that to what happens if you refuse. Corven gets pressure from above, the codes get seized, you get extradited to whichever capital hates your republic's ghost most, and the children go into an orphanage system that eats children like yours for breakfast. Or worse — Rhea Vance decides you're a loose end. Don't make me a monster in your head, Dunmore. I'm offering you the only clean exit left on this coast. The fact that it dirties my hands is my affair, not yours."

"And if I say no?"

She had prepared for that too, of course. She had speeches for it. But standing there in the cold with the frozen harbor smoking below and this exhausted, careful man looking at her with eyes that had already buried a sister and a country, she found she didn't want the speech. She wanted him to understand her, which was a weakness, and she let herself have it for exactly one breath.

"If you say no," she said, "then tomorrow morning you'll be an example. Not a corpse — corpses teach nobody. An example. I'll put your name, your true name, and your cargo on every lip from the fish market to the magistrate's bench. I'll let Corven hunt you openly. I'll let the town do what towns do to people who bring trouble into them." She held his gaze. "I'd regret it. I'll say that plainly, because you've earned plain speech. But regret is also weather. It passes."

Cyrus looked back out at the ice for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was quiet and strange, almost gentle.

"You think the sea is honest because it doesn't pretend. But I've crossed it, Captain. The sea doesn't pretend because it doesn't care. There's no country out there. There's just weather, and the people who choose each other while it kills them. That's the only nation I've ever held citizenship in, and I'm not surrendering the passport."

Rhea studied him. Then she laughed — short, real, surprised out of her. "God. If I'd met you ten years ago I'd have made you rich."

"If you'd met me ten years ago," Cyrus said, "you'd have made me dead. Good evening, Captain Vance."

He went down the dark stair without hurrying, which told her more than any answer: a man who runs shows you he knows he's prey. This one walked like he'd already chosen his ground.

* * *

Back aboard the *Kestrel's Mercy*, she did not sleep. She sat in the small truthful cabin and rewrote the third list in her head, and admitted to herself what she had known since the balcony, maybe since the fish cellars, maybe since the drowned courier came in on the tide wearing the same seal as the quiet carpenter: Dunmore would not take her offer. Men who carried other countries in their coats didn't take offers. They took chances.

Which meant the plan changed shape. She turned the crew list over and wrote on its blank back, in her private cipher, the new arithmetic:

*Atlas taken at sea. Codes recovered from Dunmore before boarding or after. Children and the stowaway girl aboard the Atlas — collateral, unharmed, bargaining stock. Corven neutralized by his own debts. The bridge stays broken; the town stays frozen; nobody leaves except through me.*

It was clean. It was even, in its way, merciful — fewer graves than war would dig. She told herself that, and mostly believed it, the way you mostly believe the floor will hold.

Brann would need two more nights to move the shore party aboard. The ice-breaker's departure was set for the day after the *Atlas* sailed — no. She stopped. Recalculated. The *Atlas* must sail first, into the channel, before the breaker closed it behind her. Four days. Maybe less, if the cold tightened its grip.

She blew out the lamp and stood at the porthole a while, watching lamplight flicker along the frozen mole where the whole trapped town walked its evenings, every one of them rehearsing their escape.

"Poor honest country," she murmured to the dark water under the ice. "You never lied to anybody. That's why they'll all come to you in the end."

In four days, the *Atlas* would weigh anchor at dawn. Which meant that tomorrow — tomorrow, before anything else could move — she had to send word to her man in the *Atlas*'s engine room to advance the timetable, and a message to the net shed's neighborhood that would put eyes on every path from Sef's door to the quay.

Because Rhea Vance had finally understood the one variable she could not buy: Cyrus Dunmore did not intend to board that ship at all. He intended to put everyone he loved on it and stay behind on the ice.

And a man planning to die is the only cargo in the world that cannot be weighed.
