## 24. Tomas Runs

The whisper came to him the way all terrible things came now: sideways, through a gap in the boards, in someone else's voice.

Tomas had gone down into the fish cellar for water — that was his one job, and he did it carefully, because careful was what Uncle Cyrus asked of them and because Marta had enough weight on her shoulders already without him spilling things or being seen. The net shed above sat on stilts at the marsh edge, and the cellar beneath it was half-dug into frozen ground, smelling of brine and old ice and the sweet rot of herring heads. He filled the jug from the rain barrel, which had frozen over twice and been broken open twice more with the heel of Sef's boot, and he was turning back up the ladder when voices drifted down through the floor seams.

"—registered, that's what Corven's clerks are calling it now. A census of minors. Names taken, ages taken, ports of origin taken."

"Ports of origin," said a second voice, older, dry as scale. "You know what happens when they write down where a child came from."

"You know what happens when they write down *who* a child belongs to."

A pause. The scrape of a crate being shifted. Then the first voice again, lower: "They put the list on the quay board tomorrow. Every unclaimed child in Verrigan goes on it. And every child with a name on that list gets looked at. Looked at closely. By men who get paid by what they find."

Tomas stood very still with the jug against his chest.

He was nine years old, and he had learned in the last two years — on trains, in ditches, in the back of a hay wagon crossing a border at night while his mother's cough got worse and worse — that *looked at closely* was never anything good. Men who looked at children closely were looking for reasons. Reasons to take them somewhere. Reasons to separate them from whoever was hiding them. His sister would have said, *Don't be stupid, Tomas, nobody means us.* But Marta wasn't here. Marta was up in the loft mending her own coat with thread she'd traded half her bread for, and Uncle Cyrus had gone out before dawn with Noor and hadn't come back yet, and Sef was asleep by the stove with his mouth open, snoring like an old boat engine, and the whisper kept going through the floor.

"And the Dunmore children?" the second voice asked. "The carpenter's nephews and niece, whatever they are?"

"They're on it."

The word went through Tomas like a fishhook.

*On it.* Written down. His name, Marta's name, in ink, on a board, on the quay, where anyone could read it — where *she* could read it, the captain woman with the smile like a knife being drawn slowly. Rhea Vance knew about them; he'd heard Noor say so, quietly, to Cyrus, weeks ago, thinking he was asleep. And if their names were written on a public board then it didn't matter how still they stayed, how quiet, how careful. Being hidden only worked when nobody knew where to look.

The jug was shaking. He made himself stop shaking it. He climbed the ladder as silently as he could, set the jug down inside the door, and stood in the cold dimness of the shed among the hanging nets, looking at the sleeping shapes: Sef by the stove, Marta curled in the corner of the big pallet with her back to the wall the way she always slept now, one arm out even in sleep, as if to bar the door.

Nobody meant them. That's what Marta would say.

But Marta hadn't heard the whisper.

Tomas pulled on his boots. He took nothing. Taking things slowed you down; he had learned that too. There was a loose board at the back of the shed where the marsh met the water, a gap wide enough for a boy his size that Noor used when she came and went, because Noor always used gaps instead of doors. He could slip out that way, follow the marsh edge south along the ice, get clear of the town before morning, and just — keep going. Somewhere. Anywhere that didn't have his name written on it. If he was gone by the time they read the list, then there would be no Tomas Dunmore to find. They couldn't register a boy they couldn't catch. He'd done this before. He'd run from worse places than a warm shed.

That was the thought he carried out into the dark, and it was a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and he went anyway.

* * *

The marsh at night in hard frost was a country made of glass and bone.

Reeds stood frozen mid-bend, each one cased in its own sleeve of ice, and when the wind moved them they rang against each other with a sound like tiny distant bells, thousands of them, so that walking through the marsh felt like walking through a church where everything breakable had been left out. Underfoot the ground was iron. Pools that had been water a week ago were sheets of clouded mirror, and Tomas had learned from watching Noor which ones held — the ones with reeds growing through, the ones dark with weed under the surface — and which ones were shells over black water, and he stepped the way she'd taught him without quite knowing he was doing it, testing each footfall before trusting it.

Behind him, Verrigan glowed. Not bright — the town hoarded its lamp oil like everything else now — but a low amber smudge against the sky, harbor side, with the darker bulk of the frozen Atlas somewhere inside it. Ahead of him, nothing. Marsh running out toward the sea walls, and beyond them, according to the maps Cyrus drew for him by lamplight, the long gray beaches, and beyond those, the road that didn't exist anymore because the bridge was broken, and beyond *that* —

He didn't actually know what was beyond that.

It occurred to him, four hundred yards into the marsh with his ears freezing and his breath icing his collar, that this was the difference between himself and everyone else in the shed: they had plans. Even Noor, who owned nothing but a stolen compass and three borders' worth of scars, had a plan, which was to survive until she could stop merely surviving. Cyrus had a plan. Sef had a plan, which seemed mostly to consist of soup. Marta had a plan for every hour of every day. And Tomas's plan, the whole of it, the entire architecture of his escape, was *away.*

He stopped on a hummock where the reeds grew thick, and let that settle over him.

Away. Away from the list, away from Rhea Vance's smile, away from the whisper through the boards. It sounded so much smaller out here, said aloud in his own head. Because the problem with *away*, when you were small and alone and the world was made of ice, was that away contained everything else too. Away contained the border guards he'd crossed once between his mother and his mother's grave. Away contained men who paid by what they found. Away was not a place. Away was just everywhere the shed wasn't, and the shed had soup in it, and Marta, and an old man who snored, and a girl named Noor who had taught him which pools held.

He turned around.

That was when the lantern came up the sea-wall path.

Not one light. Three, strung out in a line, moving fast and low, the way soldiers moved — he knew that walk, he'd watched it from hay lofts — and even before his eyes sorted the shapes around the flames he knew whose men they were, because there was only one person in Verrigan who sent scouts out into the marsh at midnight in January, and she smiled like a drawn knife. They were quartering the ground. Spreading as they came, one swinging wide along the frozen channel, one holding the path, one cutting straight across the flats toward — 

Him. Or the shed. Or both; it hardly mattered. They were between him and home either way, and the distance closed with terrible ease, lantern light sweeping the reed-tops, and the bell-sound of the ice-filled reeds rang on, indifferent, giving away his position to anyone who knew how to listen.

Tomas ran.

Not away — there was no away anymore, he understood that now with a clarity that felt like the frost going through his boots — but *anywhere that wasn't here,* which is a different thing and a much worse plan. He crashed through a frozen pool that held, then one that didn't quite, going in to the shin with a crack like a shot, cold climbing his leg like fire. Behind him a voice called out, not alarmed, almost bored: *"There. In the reeds, left of the channel. Small one."*

Small one. That's what he was to them. A size.

He ran along the channel bank until the bank ended in black open water steaming faintly in the cold, the one place in the whole white marsh where winter hadn't finished its work, and the lantern behind him swung closer, and the man holding it said something to another man, and there was laughter in it, and Tomas stood at the edge of the freezing water with nowhere left to go, nine years old, and thought with perfect calm, the way you think things right at the very end of your strength: *Marta is going to wake up and I won't be there.*

Then a hand closed on his ankle — no. On his collar, from below and behind, out of a hole in the bank he had walked straight past without seeing, and a voice he knew better than his own heartbeat hissed, *"Down,"* and the world tilted, and he was pulled backward through cold air into darkness and reek of peat and mud, and the last thing he saw before the turf-cover dropped back over the opening was lantern light sweeping the spot where he had been standing.

Noor's hand clamped over his mouth. Her other arm pinned him to her like cargo.

In the dark under the sea-wall turf, in a smuggler's cache that smelled of tar and old rope and Noor herself — woodsmoke and salt — they lay pressed together on packed earth and listened to boots come close. Very close. Close enough that Tomas could hear the wet sound of breath through a scarf, could see, through a finger-wide gap between turf and frame, a boot toe pointing at them not two feet away.

"He went in the water?" the boot asked.

"Or doubled back." That was the bored voice again. "Vance wants the little ones alive. Registered children make better leverage than drowned ones, that's the whole point of registering them."

The boot shifted. Mud trickled from the roof of the hide and landed, very softly, on the back of Tomas's hand.

"Check the shed side of the wall," the boot said, and moved away, and the light moved with it, and Noor did not relax for a count of two hundred — Tomas knew because he counted her heartbeats through her coat, fast as a bird's, then slower, then slower — and then, in the pitch dark, she began very quietly to laugh. Not much. Just a shaking of her shoulders and a huff of breath against his ear, and after a moment he realized his own mouth was doing something similar, some animal response to having not died, and she pressed her hand flat on top of his head like blessing a small dog.

*"Idiot,"* she whispered, with enormous affection.

* * *

They stayed in the cache until the lanterns gave up on the channel and regrouped on the path, and then Noor led him the opposite way from anywhere — deeper into the marsh, along routes she clearly had walked before, over frozen pans he'd have died on alone. She moved like the marsh was a house she'd grown up in. Where Tomas saw blank white sheet ice she saw the dark seam of a weed-choked pool and stepped around it without breaking stride; where the reeds closed into an impenetrable wall she found the gap with her hand before her eyes found it. Twice she stopped dead, tilted her head, listened to the ringing reeds, and changed direction, and each time, minutes later, came the far-off glow of a lantern crossing where they would have been.

"Why do they ring?" he whispered the third time, when they crouched together in the lee of a stranded rowboat frozen into the mud, hull up, its keel furred with frost.

"The reeds? Wind." She said it like it was obvious, because to her it was. "Everything frozen talks if you know its voice. Reeds ring. Lake ice booms when it grows — long note, like a whale. River ice cracks short. You learn the sounds, you can hear a man coming a mile off, because men are the only things in a frozen marsh that don't belong to the marsh." She looked at him, eyes catching what little light the overcast sky gave back. "You didn't hear them. You were loud as a cart."

"I was trying to be quiet."

"You were trying to be scared quietly. Different thing." She dug in her coat and produced a strip of dried fish, broke it, gave him the bigger piece without appearing to decide to. "Eat. Your hands are shaking from cold or stupid, doesn't matter which, both need fuel."

Tomas ate. The fish was salty and terrible and the best thing he'd ever tasted, because he was alive to taste it.

"Noor." His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "Why did you follow me?"

She was scanning the reed-line while he talked, always scanning, but she answered. "Sef woke up. Counted beds. Went straight to me, pointed at the gap in the boards, and said, 'Fetch him before the marsh does.' So I fetched you." A pause. The reeds rang. She watched the dark. "Also you're my brother now. That part wasn't Sef's idea."

Tomas looked at her. In the three months since they'd dragged her half-frozen out of the Atlas's hold she had never once said anything like that, never said anything soft at all, and she said it now facing away from him, matter-of-fact, checking the horizon, as if she were reporting weather.

"When they caught me," he said, "at the border, last year — before Uncle Cyrus — there was a girl in the wagon. Older than you. When they opened the doors she put me behind her and told them I was hers." His throat tightened. "They took her instead. I've never known her name."

Noor was quiet a moment. Then: "Now you know why she did it."

"Yes."

"Good. Don't waste it."

They moved on. The marsh thinned toward the sea wall, and the wind rose, and somewhere behind them the town's bells marked three in the morning, muffled and cracked by frost. Noor altered course toward a line of stunted pines on a hummock — shelter, maybe, or a landmark — and as they crossed the open flat beneath them, the ice under their feet spoke.

Not the short crack of river ice. A long, deep boom rolled under the ground and kept rolling, out across the flats, answered a mile off by another, lower, like two enormous animals calling to each other across the dark.

Tomas froze. Noor didn't. She stood on the booming ice with her head cocked, and slowly, wonderfully, she smiled.

"It's growing," she said. "New ice, pushing out from shore. Listen — it's the marsh telling the whole world it's getting thicker tonight." She crouched, laid her palm flat on the frozen flood, and beckoned him down until his hand rested beside hers on the freezing surface. Another boom rolled beneath them, up through their palms and bones. "Nobody can cross that quietly. Nobody can sneak up on this. Best night there is, for two people who don't want to be found."

They stayed like that, hands on the singing ice, while the marsh talked to itself in its vast slow language. And Tomas understood, in the way children understand things suddenly and completely and forever, that fear had been the thing making him small. Running *from* was small work. But this — listening, learning the voice of the ground under you, moving with someone who moved like the marsh belonged to her — this was running *with*, and it was a different animal entirely, and he was done with the first kind for good.

"Noor," he said. "Teach me the sounds. All of them. Everything you know."

She glanced at him sidelong, and something in her face — approval, or relief, or just recognition — flickered and settled. "Starts with reeds," she said. "You already know reeds."

They were halfway to the pine hummock when the ice spoke again — but wrong. A short, sharp report, close, from the direction of the sea wall, followed by another, and then not ice at all: a whistle, shrill and urgent, from the town. One blast, two, three. Lanterns bloomed along the quay like a rash. And thin across the frozen harbor, distorted by distance and cold but unmistakable, came the sound of Sef's voice — the old man shouting, defiant, and then the crash of a door and many boots, and Noor's hand closed hard around Tomas's wrist.

"The shed," she breathed. "Cyrus isn't back yet. Marta's alone."

Another boom rolled out under their feet, the marsh announcing itself to the world, indifferent and enormous, while down at its white edge the town lit up torch by torch, and Noor began to run, and this time Tomas ran exactly with her.
