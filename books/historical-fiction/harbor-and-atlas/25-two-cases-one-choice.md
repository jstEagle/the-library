## 25. Two Cases, One Choice

The first mate's name was Halvard Oss, and he kept his accounts in a grease-pencil ledger with a spine soft as an old boot. Cyrus had watched him work the gangway for weeks — the way Oss counted heads like a man counting his own heartbeats, the way he turned away a weeping merchant with one hand while slipping a coin back into the man's pocket with the other. That was the kind of officialdom Cyrus could negotiate with. Not the captain, who was a distant god of timetables and steam pressure. Not the purser, whose tickets were already spoken for twice over. Oss. The man who made the arithmetic real.

Cyrus found him at dawn on the Atlas's aft deck, chipping ice from the rail with the flat of a belaying pin, breath smoking in the gray light.

"You're the carpenter," Oss said without turning. "The one who fixed the purser's crates. Good joinery. Too good."

"Too good?"

"For a dockhand." Oss knocked a plate of ice loose and watched it shatter on the deck. "I've been twenty-two years on salt water, Mr. — what shall I call you today?"

"Dunmore is fine."

"Dunmore." The mate smiled without warmth. "That's this week's name, then. I don't care what you were. I care that you've been watching my ship like a man watching a woman across a crowded church. So. Say what you came to say before the watch changes."

Cyrus had rehearsed nothing. He had learned long ago that rehearsal showed in the voice like grain in cheap wood. He simply said it.

"One berth. I'm told there's one berth left after the cancellations from the bridge closure."

Oss set down the pin. "You're told right. One. Third-class, amidships, next to the engine housing so the whole crossing sounds like the end of the world. The purser wants forty crowns for it and would take sixty if anyone was fool enough."

"I want four."

For a moment the only sound was the harbor — ice groaning against pilings, gulls arguing over something dead on the tideline, the far-off ring of hammers where men broke up a sledge that had frozen into the mud.

"Four," Oss repeated. "On one berth."

"On one berth and whatever else you can find me. A hammock in the chain locker. A corner of the chart room. I'll sleep standing at the rail if I have to. But four names on your manifest, clean papers, no questions asked at the far end."

"And what does a carpenter offer for a miracle like that?" Oss picked up his pin again, turned it in his gloved hands. "Because I'll tell you what I see when I look at you, Dunmore. I see a man who folds his hands like a diplomat. I see a man who scans rooftops out of habit. I see a man the magistrate's office has been asking about, which means the smugglers have been asking about him too, which means anywhere near you aboard my ship is a hole in the hull. Four berths. You might as well ask me to carry plague."

"I know what I'm asking."

"Do you?" Oss leaned against the rail, studying him with the flat professional interest of a man appraising cargo that might be worth more than its manifest claimed. "Then let's do sums. There are two hundred and eleven people in this town who'd kill for your single berth. There's a magistrate who could close my ship with a signature if he thought I was carrying fugitives. And there's a woman named Rhea Vance who offered my bosun three months' wages to leave a stern light burning tomorrow night. You didn't know that last part, did you."

Cyrus felt the cold go through him, colder than the morning. "No. I didn't."

"No, you wouldn't." Oss straightened. "So come back when you understand the full price of what you're buying. And come back alone. Whatever household you've built up in that net shed — the fewer people who know their names are on my books, the longer they stay off other people's lists."

He walked away along the icy deck, sure-footed as a cat, and left Cyrus standing at the rail with the fog coming in off the water like a curtain being drawn on the whole world.

* * *

He did not go straight back to Sef's shed. He went instead to the seawall at the harbor's mouth, where the breakwater met the mole, and sat on a bollard furred with frost, and forced himself to do the thing he had been avoiding since the night the mate's words first came to him through the chandlery wall: the arithmetic.

It was simple. That was the obscenity of it. Simple as a child's sum.

One berth. Marta. Tomas. Noor. Himself. Four minus three.

He took out the stub of pencil he carried and, because his hands needed occupation or they would shake, he wrote it on the back of a scrap of sandpaper: *M — T — N — C.* Then he drew a line under the last letter, the way you strike a figure that doesn't balance.

There were arguments against. He catalogued them the way he'd once catalogued diplomatic assets, coldly, completely. Argument one: Noor was young and strong; she could survive another winter, another border, another road. Counter: she had survived three borders by luck that was running out; her face was known now in every fish cellar and tavern from the raid, and Rhea's men had already broken two of her fingers against a table edge for her silence. Luck was not a plan. Argument two: the children needed a guardian; sending them alone was abandonment dressed up as sacrifice. Counter: the papers Corven could forge would name a guardian — Sef, perhaps, or Ilsa Brandt if she stayed, or even Oss himself as ship's guardian pro tempore, a common enough arrangement for unaccompanied minors in the neutral ports. Children traveled under guardianship all the time. What children needed, at the far end of the sea, was not the man himself but what the man could buy them: names, papers, a start. And those things weighed nothing. Those things fit in an envelope.

Argument three was the one that mattered, and it was not an argument at all. It was Marta's face at lamplight, asking why he always looked at the door first. It was Tomas asleep with a knotted length of rope still in his fist, holding on even in dreams. It was Noor teaching Tomas the marsh paths, laughing for the first time Cyrus had heard, her splinted fingers clumsy and brave.

Against all of that he put one fact: he was worth more dead than alive to everyone still circling this harbor, and worth nothing at all beyond it. The codes died with the republic. His skills — languages, ciphers, the careful invisible life — were worthless in a neutral port where nobody cared who you had been. But a berth bought with whatever he had left of value, that was worth four lives if he spent himself instead of the money.

He looked at the sandpaper a long time. Then he rubbed the letters out with his thumb, one by one, until there was nothing written there at all.

* * *

Sef knew. Of course Sef knew. The old man said nothing when Cyrus came back smelling of open water and bad decisions, but that evening, mending a trawl net by the stove with hands that moved like slow birds, he began to talk without looking up.

"There's a kind of drowning," Sef said, "that doesn't leave a body."

"Sef—"

"I've seen it. A man decides he's the ballast. Throws himself over so the rest can make way." The needle flashed, dipped, flashed. "My son did it. Different sea, same arithmetic. He gave his berth on a boat to a woman with a baby, stayed behind to run the last ferry of fugitives out. They took him on the quay." The needle stopped. "The woman's boy is a dentist in Torshavn now. Good teeth, the whole family. And my Jonas is a name on a stone I've never seen. I've had thirty years to decide whether it was worth it. I still can't. You want to know what I think? I think that's the answer. If you can ever decide it was worth it, you didn't love them enough to be wrong about."

"That's not comforting, old man."

"I'm not in the comfort trade. I mend nets. Everything I fix is meant to hold weight, and I tell you true: grief holds weight too. Better than most rope. But a net with a hole where the maker should be — that's not sacrifice, that's just damage." Sef bit the thread. "You go talk to whoever you're going to talk to. But you talk to the children before you decide you know what they can carry. People always forget to ask the ones they're saving whether they'd rather have saved *you*."

Cyrus lay awake in the loft afterward between the warm shapes of Tomas and Marta, listening to the harbor ice boom and settle like some vast animal turning over in its sleep, and he understood that Sef was right, and hated him gently for it, because it made everything harder. It meant he couldn't just vanish nobly. It meant he owed them the truth, and the truth was a thing he had been rationing out in thimbles since the day they'd arrived at his gate with a note and their mother's eyes.

Not yet. First, terms. He would get the terms fixed — irrevocable, in writing, witnessed — and then he would tell them, and they would fight him, and he would hold, because holding was the one thing he had ever truly been good at.

* * *

He found Oss two nights later in the harbormaster's back room, where the mate went to drink coffee strong enough to strip varnish and argue timetables with a clerk who feared him. Cyrus closed the door and laid it out flat.

"The berth. Third-class, amidships. Forty crowns, the purser's price. I'll pay it. But the other three names travel as ship's company — supernumeraries. Wardroom mess, no pay, signed off at the neutral port. It's done on tramp steamers every winter when crews jump ship. You need bodies for the crossing anyway, and the ice has stranded half your deckhands ashore. My girl can work a galley — she's been feeding five of us for a month on scraps. The boys can haul line, chip ice, stand a lookout watch with an able seaman. They're not passengers, Oss. They're crew."

"They're children."

"They're survivors. There's a difference, and you've carried enough of both to know it."

Oss drank his coffee and said nothing for a long time. Steam curled between them. Somewhere below, the Atlas's engines turned over slowly, warming, like a beast dreaming of open water.

"Supernumeraries need a sponsor of record," Oss said at last. "Ship's articles. Someone accountable."

"I'm the sponsor."

"And when the port authorities at the far end ask why a carpenter is shipping three wards under articles, and one of those wards is a stowaway the whole waterfront knows by sight—" He set down the cup. "And here's my other problem, Dunmore. The purser sold your berth's option Tuesday. To Vance's agent. Provisionally. She's collecting berths the way she collects everything — quietly, in other people's names. Which means even your one berth is sitting on ground she thinks she owns. You're not negotiating with me anymore. You're negotiating through a war zone."

"Then I need to move fast."

"You need to move *smart*, and fast is usually stupid wearing fast's coat." Oss rubbed his jaw. "Here's what I can do, and it's all I can do. I can keep the articles blank in my cabin — unsigned, undated, unwitnessed. Blank articles, ready for four names or three or none, filled in the hour before the tide. Nothing on paper for anyone to steal or subpoena or buy off the purser's drunk assistant. When you walk up my gangway, whoever walks beside you goes in the book. Whoever doesn't—" He spread his hands. "Was never coming."

"And the berth Vance's agent optioned?"

"Is a fiction until the purser countersigns, and the purser won't countersign until boarding day, because he's a coward and cowards procrastinate. Which gives you—" Oss checked a pocket watch, snapped it shut "—nine days. Get me names I can defend to a port inspector. Get me a story that survives ten minutes of competent questioning. And get yourself out from under Vance's eye, because if she learns what you're doing, she won't bid against you. She'll just make sure you're not alive to board."

Cyrus stood. At the door he paused. "Why? Twenty-two years on salt water — why risk your ship for a carpenter and his strays?"

Oss looked into his coffee cup as if the answer were floating there.

"Because in 'twelve I ran a boat full of people past a blockade, and I made the count at the end and came up one short, and I have done the sum every night since trying to make it different." He didn't look up. "Nine days, Dunmore. Don't make me do the sum again."

* * *

Walking back along the mole in the blue dark, Cyrus let himself feel it — not triumph, because there was none, but the terrible clarity arriving at last, the way land arrives through fog: gradually, then all at once, immovable.

Three names. Maybe four, if fortune favored — maybe Oss's blank articles would stretch, maybe Corven's gratitude after the net shed would buy a fourth signature. He would fight for the fourth. He owed Noor that much and more; she had taken a beating with his sister's children's names locked behind her teeth. But he knew, the way you know a knot is sound without testing it, that when the hour came he would step back off the gangway himself rather than let the count come up short. He had fled a dying republic carrying secrets instead of children. He had promised his sister both of them across the sea, and promises made to the dying were the only contracts he still believed in. The third clause of that promise — the one no one had written down — was that he would see them onto the deck, and then he would become what he had always been training his whole life to be: unremarkable. Invisible. A man the sea forgot.

It didn't frighten him. That surprised him most. He stood at the end of the mole where the lantern swung and the black water breathed beneath the ice, and he tested the thought the way Sef tested a splice, and it held. Staying was not drowning. Staying was finishing the job. Every courier, every carrier, every man who moved precious freight knew the final rule: the cargo matters, the carrier doesn't. He had lived by that rule for years without knowing it was preparing him for this.

He was still standing there, cold settling into his bones like a tenant, when he heard footsteps on the frost behind him — light, quick, deliberate. He turned, hand moving to where a knife wasn't anymore.

Marta stood at the landward end of the mole, wrapped in Sef's spare coat, her breath silvering the lamplight. Twelve years old, half-starved two months ago, and now standing like someone who had followed him and was not sorry she had.

"I saw you leave," she said. "You walk different when you've decided something. Tomas says it's your shoulders. Mine too, he says." She came closer, chin up, eyes bright and frightened and fierce. "Uncle Cyrus. Whatever bargain you made tonight — whatever you think you're going to do at that gangway — you should know we counted too."

She held up her mitten. Four small fingers, folded down into a fist.

"We've been doing the same sum," she said. "And we got a different answer."
