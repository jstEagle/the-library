## 26. Ilsa's Bridge

The wrench had belonged to Henrik. It was too heavy for her hand, which was precisely why she carried it — the weight of it in her coat pocket swung against her hip like a second heartbeat, and she had found that walking with it made her walk differently. Straighter. Slower. As though she were going somewhere on official business, and perhaps she was.

Ilsa Brandt crossed the ice-glazed boards of the drawbridge for the hundredth time that winter and knelt at the mechanism housing with her skirts frozen stiff around her knees. The wind came off the harbor carrying the smell of tar and dead fish and woodsmoke, and beneath all of it the mineral cold of the sea turning to glass. She put her ear to the housing the way a physician puts an ear to a chest.

Then she listened.

Henrik had taught her this in their first year of marriage, back when being a bridge keeper's wife meant learning the machinery as well as any man. *The gears tell you what they need,* he'd said, his hands over hers on the great iron crank. *Most men listen with their eyes. Listen with your hands.* She had listened with her hands for eleven years after that, greasing cams and replacing pins while he climbed the tower to work the signals, and when he died — quietly, in his sleep, the luckiest death Verrigan had seen that decade — the magistrates had simply let her keep the post because no one else wanted it and no one thought a widow could do it anyway.

She had let them think it. That was the trick of invisibility: you let people underestimate you until the day you didn't.

Now she ran her gloved fingers along the broken gear train and felt what she had been refusing to feel for two months. Not a snapped mainshaft. Not a cracked housing. Not frost-heaved foundations or any of the catastrophic failures Engineer Polm had catalogued in his report, the one that declared repairs would take past the sailing season, past spring, past hope.

A sheared pin. Two, actually — the secondary one had gone when the first gave way under the season's last load. A shear pin was a two-hour job with the right stock. The counterweight chains were fouled but sound. The hydraulic accumulator needed recharging, which required pump oil and pressure gauges, both of which existed in exactly one place in Verrigan.

Ilsa sat back on her heels and looked out across the frozen inner harbor, where the *Atlas* rode at anchor like a black tooth in white gum, and understood, with the slow cold certainty of a woman who had spent her whole life listening to machines, that the bridge could be raised in four days.

Four days. And the town had been told months.

* *

She went first to Polm's office above the customs house, because a part of her still believed in reports and signatures and the ordinary machinery of honest administration. Polm was a small man made smaller by his desk, and he received her the way officials receive inconvenient women, with a smile like a door opening onto a wall.

"Mrs. Brandt. Still chipping at the mechanism, are we? Cold work."

"Engineer Polm." She set her assessment on his desk — she had written it out fair the night before by lamplight, every measurement, every part number, every estimate in Henrik's old shorthand that any harbor engineer worth his salt could read. "Your report says eighteen months minimum for the main drive rebuild. There is no main drive rebuild. The drive is intact. Two shear pins, chain fouling, accumulator charge. Four days with proper parts."

Polm picked up the paper, glanced at it, and set it down with the delicacy of a man handling something mildly soiled. "I appreciate a widow's enthusiasm for her late husband's work—"

"My husband's work kept this bridge open through two floods and the naval blockade. Don't you dare speak of him as a hobby." Her voice came out lower than she intended, almost gentle, and somehow that was worse; Polm actually flinched. "Where are the parts, Engineer? Because they aren't in your stores. I've checked. I check everything. There hasn't been a shipment of pump oil or gauge fittings through this port since November, and yet the *Lark's Mercy* — Rhea Vance's ship — took on engineering cargo three weeks ago. Crated, stenciled, heavy. I watched it go aboard. I know what crates of accumulator components weigh when they're swung on a davit, because I've been swinging them for twenty years."

"You watched from the bridge," Polm said carefully. "That's public access."

"Everything about this bridge is public access. That's what a bridge *is*." She took back her assessment. "Who ordered you to write that report?"

"I wrote my own report, Mrs. Brandt."

"Then someone dictated it slowly enough for you to copy." She stood. Her knees ached from the cold, and she was suddenly, furiously tired — tired of deference, tired of grief worn like a mourning band that excused everyone from taking her seriously. "You're a better engineer than a liar, Mr. Polm. I could hear it in your voice the day you announced the timeline. You sounded like a man reading his own eulogy."

She left before he could answer. In the stairwell she passed Corven's clerk coming up, and the young man stepped aside quickly, eyes down, and something about the way he wouldn't look at her — the way nobody in official Verrigan would look at her anymore — confirmed what she already knew.

* *

The manifest came to her by accident, or by the kind of accident that happens when you have spent weeks making yourself a fixture in places people forget to guard.

Ilsa had taken to drinking her one cup of coffee each afternoon at the Harborlight tavern, at the corner table nearest the stove, where she sat with her mending and said nothing. Widows who say nothing become furniture, and furniture hears everything. For two months she had listened to Rhea Vance's men talk over cards — their voices dropping only slightly, carelessly, because what threat did a gray-haired woman with darning needles pose?

On Thursday, the third of the month, two of them sat behind her arguing in whispers about berthing fees, and one of them slapped a folded paper on the table between them, and the paper slid, and neither man noticed when Ilsa's mending basket drifted six inches along the bench and the paper drifted into it, under a half-knitted sock, in the smoothest motion of her life.

She did not read it until she was home, shutters drawn, Henrik's lantern turned low.

It was a cargo manifest, stamped with a shipping company she'd never heard of, which meant it was stamped with a company that did not exist except as a name Rhea Vance used on paper. And it listed, in the dry language of freight, exactly what Ilsa had known it would list: accumulator cylinders, pressure gauges, pump oil in drums, shear-pin stock in three diameters, chain links rated for bridge loads — quantities far beyond any ship's needs, crated and warehoused, marked *HOLD FOR SPRING MOVEMENT.*

Hold for spring movement. Keep the bridge broken until the ice melted and the *Atlas* sailed and every refugee in Verrigan had paid whatever the market would bear for a way out — because a town that couldn't leave by land could only leave by sea, and Rhea Vance owned the sea.

And there, at the bottom of the page, in a clerk's careful hand, a note that made Ilsa's blood stop and then start again hotter:

*Polm compensated per agreement. Bridge file closed.*

They had paid the engineer. They had closed the file. The bridge wasn't broken by weather. It was broken by arithmetic.

Ilsa sat in the lamplight for a long time. Outside, the wind moved snow across the harbor ice in long whispering banners, and somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse at nothing. She thought of Henrik, who had loved the bridge the way other men love a wife — patiently, daily, without ever expecting it to love him back. She thought of what he would have done with that paper in his hand.

He would not have gone quietly to the magistrate. He would have gone loudly.

But then, Henrik had never been afraid. And Ilsa, folding the manifest into oilcloth and sewing it into the lining of her coat with the same needle she used for darning, discovered that she was terrified — properly, bodily terrified, her hands shaking so badly she pricked her thumb twice — and that she was going anyway.

Ordinary courage, she thought. It isn't the absence of fear. It's fear that keeps its appointment.

* *

She chose the public route deliberately.

Not the back stairs, not a whispered meeting, not any of the dark channels where Rhea Vance's leverage flowed. If Ilsa Brandt disappeared now, quietly, the way inconvenient people disappeared that winter, no one would ask questions. So she made herself impossible to disappear.

At noon on Friday she walked into the courthouse during open session — the weekly docket, half the town in the gallery freezing through a dispute about firewood rights — and she stood in line with petitioners like any widow with a grievance, and when her name was called she climbed the steps to the rail and stood before Magistrate Corven in front of forty witnesses and God.

"Magistrate. Ilsa Brandt, keeper of the drawbridge. I bring a matter of public safety, and I'll ask your indulgence, because it won't fit in a form."

Corven looked up from his papers. Up close he was worse than she remembered — grayer, thinner, a man wearing his own face like a borrowed coat. His eyes flicked once toward the gallery, once toward his clerk, and settled on her with something that might, in another life, have been gratitude. A drowning man recognizing another drowning man.

"State your petition, Mrs. Brandt."

"The drawbridge can be repaired in four days."

The gallery murmured. Corven's hand stopped moving.

"Engineer Polm's assessment—" he began.

"Is wrong, sir, and I believe he knows it's wrong, and I believe he was paid to know it." Her voice carried — twenty years of shouting over gale and machinery had given her a voice that filled stone rooms without effort. "Two shear pins, chain fouling, an accumulator charge. I've held the parts inventory of this port in my hands" — she drew the oilcloth packet from her coat and laid it on the rail with a soft finality, like a card played face-up — "because last night it was in my coat, and before that it was on a card table in the Harborlight, and it lists bridge components sufficient to raise the crossing by Tuesday next, warehoused aboard the *Lark's Mercy* under a false company flag, marked hold for spring movement. Marked, Magistrate, in the same hand that notes a payment to close the bridge file."

Silence, the special silence of a room full of people deciding simultaneously whether to be witnesses.

"Those are grave accusations," Corven said slowly. "Brought on evidence of — stolen property?"

"Borrowed," said Ilsa. "From men who stole a bridge from a town. You can charge me with theft, Magistrate, right here in front of everyone, and I'll plead guilty with pleasure, and we can all watch what happens to the case after dark. Or you can read that manifest aloud, order the *Lark's Mercy* held, and put my bridge back together before the ice decides for us." She leaned on the rail, and for a moment — just a moment — she let him see the fear under the iron, and the iron under the fear. "My husband kept this bridge for twenty years, and he never once asked who it was convenient to. I'm asking you to be half the man he was. That's all. Half."

In the gallery, someone began to clap. Then others. It was not loud, and it did not last, but it did not need to; it had been witnessed, and witnessing was the whole point.

Corven sat very still. Ilsa watched him the way she had learned to watch mechanisms — reading the load, the strain, the point of shear. She saw the moment the pin gave: his eyes went to the manifest, then to the gallery, then to some private ledger behind his own face where debts were tallied, and something in him visibly resolved, or broke, or both.

He drew the manifest toward him.

"This session will recess," he said, and his voice was steady, which cost him more than anyone in that room would ever know. "The court takes under review the matter of the drawbridge assessment, and refers the question of the *Lark's Mercy*'s cargo to... this court's direct inspection." He looked up at Ilsa, and his mouth made something that was almost a smile. "Mrs. Brandt. You will remain available to assist the court. Bring your wrench."

* *

She waited in the corridor while the gallery emptied, and that was where Corven found her — alone, away from eyes, suddenly just a tired woman and a frightened man.

"You understand what you've done," he said quietly. "She'll come for you. Not with law. With less."

"I've been a widow nine years, Magistrate. Everything since has come for me, and here I stand." She shouldered her bag. "Read the manifest tonight. All of it. There's more on that page than bridge parts — there's a whole economy written in pencil. And when you've read it, decide fast which side of it you're standing on, because Tuesday is closer than you think, and the ice doesn't wait on paperwork."

She left him there in the corridor, a man holding a piece of paper that weighed, she judged, approximately as much as a career, a conspiracy, and possibly a war.

Outside, the wind had dropped and the sun came through the fog like a coin behind frosted glass. Ilsa walked home along the quay, past the frozen harbor, past the black hull of the *Atlas*, and up the ramp onto her broken bridge, and at the top she stopped and put her bare hand flat against the cold iron housing, the way she used to when Henrik was alive and working the tower above her.

"Hold together," she told it. "We're getting you fixed."

Behind her, down on the water, a ship's boat was pulling for the *Lark's Mercy* with urgent oars, and word — she had no doubt — was already traveling ahead of it.
