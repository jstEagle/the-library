## 27. The Magistrate's Mirror

Corven had written four letters in his life that he meant to send and burned three of them. He thought of them now as he climbed the stairs behind the fish-cellar door — the fourth copy sat in his desk drawer like a loaded pistol with the safety off, and he could not decide whether it pointed at himself or at the man he had come to see.

The loft above the old cooperage was a place he knew from seizures years ago: smugglers had once stored brandy there, back when he still believed the law was a wall instead of a curtain. Now a single lamp burned behind the salt-fogged window, and beneath the door he could see the shadow of a man who did not shift his weight, did not pace, did not fidget. A man who waited the way stone waits.

Corven knocked twice, paused, knocked once — the signal his clerk had carried to Sef's shed that morning, written in a hand he had not used since his prosecuting days, when warrants went out under seal and meant something.

The door opened on a chain, then all the way.

"Magistrate Corven," Cyrus Dunmore said. His voice was flat as slate. "I wondered which of you would find me first."

* * *

Cyrus had known, of course, from the moment the raid missed him by six hours, that the summons would come. A town like Verrigan did not let a mystery walk to the edge of its sight and simply stand there. What he had not known was whether the hand reaching for him would wear Rhea Vance's rings or the magistrate's robes — and there was something almost restful, standing in this cold room with the lamp between them, in finally seeing which wolf had come.

Corven was not what he expected. He was smaller than his reputation and greyer than his portraits in the customs house, and his eyes had the particular exhaustion of a man who has been awake for years without ever quite sleeping. He set his hat on a nail with unnecessary precision, as if order were a thing he could still impose somewhere, even here.

"You'll want my head," Cyrus said. "Or my case. I'm told those are the only two currencies left in Verrigan this winter."

"I want neither." Corven lowered himself onto the one stool as though his knees had been negotiating with him all day. "What I want is to make you an offer. And before I make it, I want you to understand that I know exactly what I am when I make it."

He reached into his coat and laid a paper on the crate between them. Unfolded, it showed a page of cipher groups in a neat clerk's hand — a partial transcription, five lines only, each line annotated in red ink with dates and names. Cyrus's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world. He recognized the system at once: the republic's naval attaché code, the one sewn into the leather case against his ribs for eleven months.

"Where—" he began, and stopped, because the answer didn't matter. The drowned courier. The duplicate case Marta had carried home from the tideline like driftwood. The empty hiding place under the floorboards. Somewhere in this frozen town, half of his ghost was already in another man's hands.

"The raid recovered a dispatch case," Corven said. "Sealed. Stamped with the seal of a state that, as of last spring, no longer exists. My instructions — such as they arrive now, don't ask me from whom — are to seize any courier's papers *for inspection.* You may imagine for whose inspection." His mouth thinned. "Captain Vance has been generous to me over the years. Generous in ways I cannot repay and cannot survive refusing."

"So you're her dog."

"She thinks so." For the first time something moved behind Corven's face, something old and bitter and almost amused. "She believes she owns me because she holds the receipts. She is wrong about what the receipts prove. They prove I took money. They do not prove I stopped being what I was." He tapped the transcribed cipher lines. "This code can do more than buy passage, Mr. Dunmore. Deciphered fully, it contains routing orders, payment authorizations, and correspondence implicating every official on this coast who sold favors during the war's last year — including, if the pattern holds, a complete record of what was paid to me and what I did to earn it. It also contains, buried in the annexes, exculpatory material. Evidence that certain convictions I secured were obtained honestly. That certain men I sent to prison were guilty after all, whatever else was happening around me."

He looked up, and Cyrus saw the mirror in him plainly: a man holding a document that could save him only by damning everything near him.

"I am offering you immunity," Corven said quietly. "Full legal protection. Registered under whatever name pleases you, your person and papers untouchable, Captain Vance's interest in you dissolved by the full weight of this jurisdiction — such as it still is. In exchange, you give me the code. All of it. The key, the books, whatever exists. And you give me your silence: you never repeat, inside these walls or outside them, anything you learn about how Verrigan has been governed this past decade. The truth stays bought. That is my offer."

The lamp hissed. Out on the harbor, ice sang — that low groaning the freeze made at night, like a ship turning over in her sleep.

"And if I refuse?" Cyrus asked.

"Then tomorrow I issue the inspection warrant properly, with my own seal, and Captain Vance's men execute it, and you will be in a cell while they search everywhere you've been. Your children will be registered as wards of the state. The young woman at the chandlery will be returned across her third border." Corven said it without relish, without apology — a man reciting arithmetic he hated but refused to falsify. "I am not threatening you, Mr. Dunmore. I am describing Tuesday."

* * *

Cyrus walked to the window. Below, the fish cellars stood black against the white harbor, and beyond them the Atlas rode at her berth with her lamps lit, a small city of escape floating on frozen water. Four days. Maybe five, if the ice broke late. He had spent eleven months being unreadable, and here was a tired man in a good coat reading him anyway — not by cleverness, but because Cyrus had done the one thing an invisible man must never do. He had begun to love people where they could be seen.

He thought of Lena. *Both of them, Cy. Both, or don't come tell me goodbye.* He had told her yes. He had held her hand and lied to her the way you lie to the dying — with your whole heart.

"You're offering me safety," Cyrus said, turning back. "You're offering me what I came here to buy with eleven months of being nobody. And you're asking me to fund it by handing you a weapon that decides who governs this coast for the next twenty years. Yours or hers or whoever reads it next." He shook his head slowly. "I've been carrying that weight since the republic fell. Do you know what it's like, Magistrate? Every border, every checkpoint, every kind stranger — I weigh them. I calculate what my life costs against theirs. I have gotten very good at it. It is the only skill the war left me."

"Then you understand my offer precisely."

"I understand it perfectly. It's a good offer. It's the same offer I'd make, if I were you." Cyrus came back to the crate and sat opposite him, close enough that the lamp threw their shadows together on the wall behind — two shapes merging into one hunched figure. "But I'm going to make you a different one, and I need you to hear all of it before you say no."

Corven folded his hands. Waited.

"There are four of us," Cyrus said. "Marta, twelve. Tomas, nine. Noor — sixteen, and she has crossed three borders alone and taken a beating from Vance's men rather than give up children she'd known for a month. And me. The Atlas sails in four days with one berth unclaimed after the tickets I've already arranged. Your offer protects me. Mine buys passage for all four. Clean papers, real names or invented ones, whatever your office can stamp — and berths on that ship, confirmed tonight, witnessed by your own clerk so that Vance can't unwind them after you're gone."

"That ship answers to a shipping line," Corven said. "Not to me."

"Everything in this town answers to somebody, Magistrate, and you are the somebody everyone pretends doesn't answer to anyone. You can find the berth. Compel it, buy it, requisition it — I don't care how. I care that four names board and four names sail." He leaned forward. "And here is the part that should interest you more than the code does. I'm not selling it to you. I'm giving it away — with conditions attached that protect *you* too."

Something flickered in the magistrate's eyes. "Go on."

"When I give you the key, you decipher the whole book. Every name, every payment. And then you choose. Not Vance. Not the inspection warrant. You. You take the exculpatory pages and you use them — publicly, at the assembly Ilsa Brandt is calling, or in an inquiry, however a ruined man clears his ledger. And the incriminating pages — the ones naming you, the ones naming every bought official on this coast — those go into a stove. With me standing beside you. Because here is the truth you haven't seen yet, Corven, and I can see it because I've been carrying your mirror for eleven months: that code is worth nothing in your hands and everything in hers. As long as it exists anywhere, you are a lever waiting for a hand. Burn it, and you're just a man who took money once and spent the rest of his life paying it back. I know how that feels. I'm the world's leading expert."

The silence stretched so long that the lamp flame steadied entirely, a small fixed gold point in the cold room.

"You'd trust me with it," Corven said at last. "After everything you must suspect about me. After what my own warrant nearly cost your children."

"I don't trust you," Cyrus said. "That's the point. I trust the arithmetic. Vance needs that code to sell; you need it to survive; I need it to disappear. Only one of us needs it for nothing — and I'm the one holding it. So I set the price." He allowed himself, for the first time in the whole conversation, something that was almost a smile. "Besides. You wrote four confessions. Three burned. Don't look startled — a clerk who copies a man's letters for ten years learns his habits, and grief has a handwriting. You kept the fourth because you couldn't decide whether you wanted to be forgiven or executed, and you hoped writing it again would tell you. Am I close?"

Corven stared at him. Then, very slowly, he laughed — a short, rusted sound, like a gate opening after a hard winter.

"You're closer than anyone has been in years," he said. "It doesn't tell me. I've read it forty times. It tells me nothing except that I meant it."

"Then mean this instead." Cyrus drew open his coat and lifted out the leather case — the true one, worn soft as a dog's ear, warm from his body. He set it on the crate between them but kept his hand on it. "Two berths confirmed tonight. Two more by tomorrow's tide. Papers for four. And at dawn the day after, you and I feed every page of this into the chandlery stove together, and whatever we both were — courier, magistrate, coward, accomplice — ends right there in the ash. Nobody saves the republic. It's dead. We save what's actually alive: two children, one stowaway, one bridge keeper's widow, one old net-mender sitting in your lockup for a crime he committed by being kind to me. You want redemption, Corven? It isn't in the code. It's in the berth manifest."

The magistrate looked down at the case for a long time. Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere down the cellar stairs a rat moved through straw with the sound of a man dragging a coat.

"If I do this," Corven said, "Vance loses everything in a single night, and she is not a woman who accepts loss. She'll come for the ship. Possibly for you. Certainly for me."

"Then we'd better make sure the whole town is watching when she does," Cyrus said. "People like Vance can fight individuals. They cannot fight an audience."

For a moment neither man moved. Then Corven extended his hand across the crate — not toward the case, Cyrus noticed, but toward him. A handshake, offered first, before any paper changed hands. The gesture of a man who understood that documents were only as honest as the people who signed them, and who wanted, just once, to see honesty arrive ahead of the paperwork.

Cyrus took it. The magistrate's grip was cold and dry and did not flinch.

"I'll bring the berths' confirmation at first light," Corven said, retrieving his hat from the nail. "Signed. Witnessed. If you're lying to me, Mr. Dunmore, know that I have spent fifteen years learning exactly how far a determined man can fall."

"I'm not lying," Cyrus said. "I'm just finally falling in the right direction."

At the door, Corven paused, silhouetted against the stairway dark, and spoke without turning around.

"The fourth confession. When this is finished — if it finishes — I'd like you to read it. Before I burn it. A man should have one witness who knows what he's deciding."

Then he was gone, footsteps fading down the stairs, and Cyrus sat alone with the lamp and the case and the enormous lightness of a man who had, for the first time in a year, put his life on a table and named a price that wasn't his own survival.

He did not sleep. At some hour past midnight he heard the harbor ice crack — a sharp report, then another, like distant gunfire or a door unlocking — and by the time the sky went grey, word was running along the waterfront faster than any clerk could carry it:

Rhea Vance had boarded the Atlas before dawn, with thirty armed men, and taken the gangway.
