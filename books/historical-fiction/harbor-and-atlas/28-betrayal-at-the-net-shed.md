## 28. Betrayal at the Net Shed

Sef had been mending the same trawl net for three days, and he was in no hurry to finish it.

There was a pleasure, at his age, in work that could not be rushed. The mesh wanted a patient hand — each knot pulled snug but not tight, the twine dampened so it would not snap under the needle. His father had taught him the rhythm before Sef had learned his letters: *pull, twist, set. Pull, twist, set.* Sixty years of it had worn the motion into something below thought, and that was where he liked to be on cold mornings, down below thought, with the stove ticking and the smell of tar and old herring in the timbers, and four fugitives asleep in the loft above him like cargo too precious to declare.

He worked by feel more than sight now. The shed's single window faced east, and this morning the light came through it gray as fish scales, catching the frost ferns on the glass and doing nothing about them. The harbor outside had frozen hard enough to walk on, which meant the whole town was walking on it — you could hear their boots from the shore, a distant squeaking, people crossing ice where no person had a right to stand. Strange days. A man who'd spent his life reading water now lived beside a harbor gone silent as a church floor.

Upstairs, someone turned over in their sleep. The boy, probably. Tomas slept like a soldier — all at once, everywhere, then bolt upright at nothing. The girl slept like her uncle, Sef had decided; quiet, guarded, one ear always open. And Noor didn't sleep so much as lie there rationing it out, the way she rationed everything, bread, words, trust.

And Cyrus Dunmore — or whatever name was truest for him this week — slept least of all, and rose first, and was already gone up the mole before full light, because that was what that kind of man did. Sef had known his kind before. Men carrying something heavier than luggage learn to move early.

Sef set his needle, bit the twine, and thought about his son.

Not deliberately. It was just where his hands went when they slowed. Joren had been twenty-two the winter he'd run his last boat, ferrying people the law said were freight and his conscience said were neighbors. They'd taken him at the breakwater, and Sef had spent eleven years since telling himself the arithmetic was simple: *one boy, forty-three souls carried.* He had never once made the sum come out even. But he had kept the compass, and kept the shed, and kept, somewhere under the scar tissue, the belief that a man gets one great gamble in him and Joren had been his.

He was wrong about that, it turned out. Here he was, gambling again. Older, slower, four souls instead of forty-three, and this time he knew exactly what the stakes were priced in.

*Pull, twist, set.*

The knock came at nine.

Not a knock, really — three thumps of a fist on the door frame, the particular flat authority of men who did not expect to wait. Sef's hands went still on the net. Above him, the loft boards creaked once and went dead silent. Good. They'd heard it too.

"Open in the name of the magistrate," called a voice Sef half-knew. Dekker. One of Rhea Vance's, wearing whatever legitimacy Corven's warrant gave him the way a thief wears a stolen coat.

Sef laid down his needle with great care, as if he intended to come back to it. He looked around the shed — the coiled lines, the barrels, the stove, the ladder to the loft, the loose board near the north wall that he himself had pried up two nights ago at Cyrus's request and re-laid so cunningly that even he could barely find its seam. Under it, wrapped in oilcloth, the second case. The drowned courier's case. Empty now of everything except its own weight and the seal stamped on its leather, but the seal was the whole point; Rhea's people would know it on sight.

*Let them find it,* Cyrus had said, and there had been something in his face Sef recognized from mirrors — a man handing off the thing he couldn't carry anymore. *Let them find an empty box and believe they've found the prize. It buys us days.*

"It buys you trouble," Sef had answered.

"It's already bought," Cyrus had said. "I'm only deciding who pays."

Now the fist hit the door again, harder, and Sef crossed the floor at an old man's pace, unhurried, because hurrying told a story and he refused to tell this one. He drew back both bolts.

Dekker stood in the doorway with six men behind him and the frost burning pink on their cheeks, and past their shoulders, out on the white harbor, a whole crowd of early-morning walkers had stopped to watch, because a town that is trapped will always stop to watch a door being kicked.

"Morning, Dekker," Sef said. "You'll wipe your boots."

"We've a warrant for the search of these premises." Dekker held up a paper with a flourish that suggested he was proud of knowing how warrants worked. "Foreign correspondence. Sealed documents. Courier's effects."

"My premises hold nets," Sef said. "Nets are famously sealed."

"Step aside, old man."

Sef stepped aside. That was the whole art of it, he had decided in the night, lying awake listening to the children breathe: step aside. Be the obstacle that is not worth an obstacle's price. Let them tear the place apart. Let them take the empty case and crow over it. Everything that mattered was already climbing down through the fish cellar hatch if the children had done as they were drilled — and they had drilled it, God help him, they'd drilled it like gun crews, Marta showing Tomas the route twice, Noor walking it blindfolded once in the dark to prove it could be done. Down through the hatch, along the pickle-brine channel under the floor, out through the ice-door into the third cellar where the herring barrels stood in rows like a congregation. He'd hidden contraband in those cellars for twenty years and never once been caught, and now he was hiding children in them and had never felt less like a smuggler and more like a priest.

The men came in fast, and the shed filled with noise — boots, barrels kicked, the stove poker ringing on stone. One went straight up the ladder. Sef heard the loft swept corner to corner, the straw ticking slashed — he'd insisted they take every scrap of bedding with them into the cellars, and thank God for old paranoia. Nothing up there but cold straw and a broken pallet.

"Nothing!" the man above called down.

"Keep looking." Dekker came and stood before Sef, close, working a grin that didn't fit his face. "Where's your lodger, Brandt? The carpenter. Big fellow, careful hands. My captain hears he does beautiful work."

"I don't ask my lodgers' trades," Sef said. "I ask their rent. He paid it."

"He paid it Tuesday." Dekker held up a small canvas pouch between two fingers, and Sef felt the ground tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts. "Paid it to a gossip-woman on the fish quay, who paid it to my man, who asks fewer questions than I do. Funny money for a carpenter, wouldn't you say? Foreign mint marks. Somebody in this town has been selling your household piece by piece, old man, and we've been buying."

So that was it, then. Not the warrant first, not the magistrate — a child's coins, traded for bread. Sef thought of Marta's thin wrists, the way she counted everything, and understood the shape of it without needing to see it. Some part of him noted, coolly, that whoever had sold them had also saved them from worse: a raid surprised them in daylight, prepared-for, rather than a knife in the dark. He filed that away. Old habits.

"You've got your warrant," Sef said. "Search."

They searched. They were thorough in the way of men who have been promised a bonus, and they found the board within the hour — Dekker himself, kneeling at the north wall, prying it up with a boat knife, letting out a shout like a boy with a jackpot. The case came up dripping frost-smell, oilcloth torn away, and Dekker turned it in his hands, reading the seal, and his grin finally fit his face.

"Well," he said softly. "Hello, beautiful."

He tried the clasps. Locked, naturally — Cyrus had left it locked, deliberately, so that opening it would require either the key or an axe, and either act would happen in front of witnesses and before superiors. Dekker settled for weighing it in his palms, tapping it against his knee like a man appraising a melon.

"The captain will want this opened proper," he said to his men. "Anything else? Anything living?" His eyes went up the ladder, around the walls, and landed back on Sef with new attention. "The lodger. The children. Where do you keep them?"

"What children?" said Sef.

It was almost true. He'd gotten good at almost-true. Forty years of customs men and harbormasters had taught him that the best lies are load-bearing structures built entirely out of technicalities, and he leaned on this one now with all his weight. *What children.* There were no children registered in this shed. There were no children in any ledger anywhere in Verrigan bearing his name. On paper, the loft above him had been empty since autumn.

Dekker studied him the way gulls study a closed shellfish, and evidently concluded there wasn't time to pry. He jerked his chin at his men. "Take the old man. He can explain his empty house to the magistrate."

"And the case goes to the captain," one of them said.

"The case goes to the magistrate," Dekker corrected, with the tiny hesitation of a man whose instructions and ambitions did not perfectly overlap. "Captain's orders is it goes to the magistrate. I heard them myself."

Sef let them take his arm. He paused, at the threshold, to look back at the ruin of his shed — the slashed straw drifting down through the loft boards like dirty snow, the upturned barrels, his poor half-mended net flung across the floor with the needle still caught in its mesh. Sixty years in this room. He found, examining himself honestly, that he felt mostly the way you feel leaving a table mid-meal: mildly annoyed, and not very hungry anyway.

Out on the ice, the watchers had multiplied. Word traveled fast in a frozen town; half the waterfront stood out on the white harbor pretending to fish through holes, and among the black specks of them Sef picked out, he was nearly sure, the straight back and stillness of Ilsa Brandt, and further off, moving fast toward the fish cellars with a sack over one shoulder like a woman with legitimate errands, a slight figure he knew for Noor. *Good girl. Walk, don't run.*

Then the column moved off toward the magistrate's house, Sef in the middle of it with his hands bound before him in a fisherman's hitch — Dekker's little joke — and Sef laughing at it despite everything, because it really was a decent hitch, well-tied, and some student of knots should tell the boy someday that the law of Verrigan had finally tied something properly.

* * *

The cellars smelled of brine and decades, and Marta learned that she could be brave in the dark.

That discovery surprised her. She had assumed bravery was loud — her uncle's kind, standing in doorways, making bargains. But here, crouched behind the herring barrels with Tomas's hand crushed in hers and the raiders' boots thundering overhead like weather, she found a small, cold, steady thing in her chest that simply refused to be frightened past a certain point. She had spent her fear already, she realized. Somewhere between the crossing and the chandlery loft and this morning's bread, she had spent it all, and what was left was just a girl doing the next thing.

The next thing was: keep Tomas quiet. The next thing was: don't cough from the brine-mold. The next thing was: when the boots stopped directly overhead, don't imagine what happens if they find the loose flagstone.

They had heard it all — the door, the shouting, Sef's calm voice like a keel through chop, and then the terrible cheer of the finding. *"Hello, beautiful."* Marta had pressed both hands over her own mouth. The case. Uncle Cyrus's terrible, wonderful, empty case, the one he'd buried with such ceremony two nights ago, whispering to Sef through the floorboards that a hunted man's greatest gift to his hunters is something to hunt. She hadn't fully understood it then. Lying in the salt dark while the trap they'd built for the hunters snapped shut around an empty box, she began to.

Tomas's grip tightened. Through the floor came Dekker's voice, muffled: *"What children?"*

And then the old man's voice — and Marta would remember this until she died — not afraid, not pleading, just dry as tinder: *"What children?"* thrown back like a coin, and then laughter, low and unbothered, and the sound of boots turning toward the door, and the bolts, and the silence.

They waited an hour after that. Noor had drilled them: *an hour, always an hour, the first hour is the trap inside the trap.* Then the ice-door at the far end scraped, faintly, and Noor slid in with the frost still on her shoulders and a sack of rolls and a face like a drawn knife.

"They took Sef," she said. It wasn't a question; she'd watched it from the quay.

Marta nodded. Her eyes stung. "Because of me. The coins — I didn't know — I only—"

"I know what you only," Noor said, and sat down between them, and put an arm around each. In the dark her voice changed, softened by maybe a tenth. "Three borders, Marta. You know how I crossed them? People who were hungry sold me, every time. Every single time. And I'm alive. So we're going to be alive too, and then you're going to feel bad about it later, when feeling bad is safe. Not now. Now we move, because in about two hours Dekker figures out the case needs a key nobody has, and starts asking where the key-keeper's family went, and this cellar is the second place he'll look."

"Where's the first?" Tomas whispered.

"Somewhere he'll never find it," Noor said, and Marta heard in her voice the same cold steadiness she'd just discovered in herself, and understood that Noor had spent her fear years ago and had been living on this ever since. "Come on. Through the marsh line, then the tannery wall. We're going where I go when the world gets narrow."

* * *

They brought Sef to the magistrate's house by the front door, which was theater — Corven wanted the town to see the courier's accomplice delivered in daylight, wanted it witnessed, wanted the spectacle to do the work the evidence couldn't yet. Sef played his part. He shuffled, he looked appropriately diminished, he let the crowd on the quay read defeat into his stooped shoulders, and privately he counted windows.

In the magistrate's study, Corven dismissed the escort and stood a long moment by the cold fireplace, holding the leather case as though it might change temperature in his hands. Up close the man looked terrible — gray at the seams, a magistrate being eaten from inside by whatever had been slipped into his mouth months ago. Sef had seen hooked fish wear that expression.

"You are Sef Brandt," Corven said. "Net-mender. Formerly of the *Gull's Wage*, formerly of several vessels that do not appear in any registry I possess."

"I'm retired from registries," Sef said.

"So am I, effectively." Corven set the case on the desk. For a moment something almost human crossed the ruined official face — fatigue, or its cousin, honesty. "This was found buried in your floor. It carries the seal of a republic that was dissolved last spring. Its twin was carried into my town last autumn by a man who does not exist. Do you understand what you are standing in the middle of, old man?"

"A locked box," Sef said, "and a lot of wind."

Corven unlocked it.

He had a key — of course he did; Rhea's intelligence had gotten that far, or Corven's own locks had surrendered — and Sef watched the magistrate's hands as the lid came up. Watched them go still. Watched him lift out, one by one, the contents: oiled paper, wadding, a spare shirt folded with seamanly precision, a block of ship's biscuit gone soft, and a false bottom panel that came free to reveal absolutely nothing at all.

The silence lasted long enough to have a tide in it.

"It's empty," Corven said, not to Sef, to the room.

"Funny thing about floors," Sef said. "Things buried in them tend to move."

"Where is it?" And there it was — not the magistrate asking, but the other thing inside the magistrate, the leashed thing, the thing Rhea Vance held by a chain made of paper. "Where are the codes, Brandt?"

Sef looked at him for a while. He thought about Joren, about arithmetic, about the four souls scattered safe somewhere between the marsh line and the tannery wall, moving through the town like fish through netting because an old man had once taught a girl named Noor every hole in Verrigan's skin. He thought about the smile he could feel arriving on his own face and decided not to stop it.

"You're asking the wrong question," he said pleasantly, "in the wrong town, on the wrong side of the ice. Ask yourself this instead, Magistrate: when they dig through your whole life someday — and they will, I've watched men like you my whole career — what do you hope they find buried? Because somebody buried *nothing* in my floor, and it bought four people their lives. Best trade I ever made. What's yours going for, these days?"

Corven's hand twitched toward the bell. Stopped. Something in Sef's smile — the total absence of fear in it, the terrible contentment — seemed to stay the man's hand more than any argument could have.

"Lock him up," Corven said quietly, to the walls. "Solitary. No visitors."

"Visitors," Sef said, rising, "are the one cargo I never carried anyway."

They marched him down through the magistrate's house to the lockup beneath it, one lamp, one cot, a door with a viewing slit, and when it clanged shut behind him Sef sat down on the cot, tested it — adequate — and laughed again, softly, alone in the dark, the freest man in Verrigan.

Because the case was empty. Because the children were ghosts. Because the codes rode in a coat pocket up the marsh line in the hands of a man who no longer needed to be found, and the hunt now had nowhere to look but inward. Because they could hold his body until spring and it would contain nothing anyone wanted.

Above him, faint through stone, he could hear the town going about its panic — boots on the quay, voices raised over tickets and berths, the Atlas's departure breathing down everyone's necks. Two days, they said now. Maybe three, before the ice closed the road entirely and the ship with it.

Two days, Sef thought, settling back against the cold wall with his hands laced behind his head like a man napping on a beach. Whatever Cyrus Dunmore meant to do with his terrible arithmetic, he'd best do it quick.

And somewhere overhead, fainter than the boots, a bell began to ring — not the harbor bell, not the church bell, but the sharp, wrong, urgent hammering of the fire bell on the mole, and running feet, and someone shouting about smoke rising from the chandlery.
