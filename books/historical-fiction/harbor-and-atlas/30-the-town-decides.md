## 30. The Town Decides

Ilsa Brandt had stood on the bridge tower through forty-one winters, and she knew the sound a crowd makes before it decides anything. It was the sound of the river under early ice — not breaking yet, not still, but working at itself in the dark, testing where the weakness ran.

The fish market had not held this many people since the herring boom of '09. Lamplight swung from the roof beams on ropes and poles; every lantern in Verrigan seemed to have been requisitioned, borrowed, or stolen for the night, and their combined glow turned the hanging nets and coiled rope into the rigging of some vast vessel that had run aground indoors. The cold came up through the flagstones in a way that made standing still an act of endurance. Nobody sat. Sitting was for people who expected to be there long.

She climbed onto the auction block with the manifest folded against her ribs, inside her coat, where it had lived for two days like a second heartbeat. The paper had warped slightly from the warmth of her body. She could feel its edges through wool.

Magistrate Corven stood at the edge of the block, below her, among the crowd rather than above it — she noted that, and noted that others noted it too. He wore his robes, but unbuttoned, as if he could not quite decide whether he was officiating or confessing. His clerk hovered at his elbow with a ledger clutched to his chest like a breviary.

"Ilsa Brandt," Corven said, and his voice carried badly in the smoky air, so she stepped forward herself.

"You all know me," she said. "Some of you liked my husband. Some of you didn't, but you respected him, which is better." A low sound moved through the crowd — not quite laughter, but the shape of it. "For thirty years I kept the bridge, and I thought that was the work of my life. Then the bridge broke, and I learned something. I learned what this town is when the road south closes. I watched us become a place where tickets are hoarded like grain in a siege. Where a man can be beaten on the mole in broad daylight and no one asks who did it. Where children are registered like cargo."

At the word *children* a woman near the barrels crossed herself, and Ilsa saw Noor — standing with Cyrus Dunmore's youngsters pressed close on either side of her, the girl's split lip yellowing into a bruise — lift her chin a half-inch. Ilsa had spent the previous evening with the girl, sitting across the table in the net shed while Noor told her, flatly and without self-pity, exactly what Rhea Vance's men had asked and exactly how she had answered. *Nothing gets sent back across my bridge,* Ilsa had told her, and meant it in a way that surprised even her.

"Three days ago," Ilsa said, "I went looking for parts. Bridge parts. Chain links, pin stock, the gearing for the north leaf. Do you know why our bridge sits broken while an engineer shrugs and says spring? Because the parts exist. They were ordered in October. They arrived in November." She let that sit. "They are sitting in warehouse four, under seal, on a manifest signed by Captain Rhea Vance — the same manifest that lists eleven berths on a certain private vessel, reserved under false names, priced at four hundred crowns apiece."

The crowd's noise changed pitch. It was the sound she had waited her whole widowhood never to hear: the sound of arithmetic being done by several hundred cold, frightened people at once.

"Four hundred crowns," someone repeated from the back, incredulous. A dockhand — one of Sef's mates, face raw from the skirmish two nights past — began to laugh, a short ugly bark. "My brother offered them everything we had. Three hundred. They told him the boat was full."

"The boat isn't full," Ilsa said. "It was never going to be full. Read it yourself." She unfolded the manifest and held it high, and the clerk — Corven's clerk, moving as though in a dream — took it from her hand and read it aloud, name after purchased name, berth after berth, and beside each entry the price paid and to whom: initials, a hand stamp shaped like an anchor with a bar through it, sums that would have kept a family in bread and coal until thaw.

When he finished, the market was silent in the way a harbor is silent just before wind arrives — every sail slack, every face turned one direction.

Rhea Vance was not present. That was the first thing everyone realized, and the second thing they realized was worse: it didn't matter whether she was present, because her operation was everywhere — in the warehouse seals, the crew rolls, the tickets already sold and hidden in floorboards and Bibles and mattress ticking throughout this very crowd. Half the people in the fish market had bought something from her, at some price, in some order of desperation. And desperation, Ilsa knew, does not make people ashamed. It makes them loyal to whoever fed them.

A voice from the fishmongers' side — Halvard Pike, who sold the worst mackerel in three counties and had never once been accused of idealism — called out: "And what do we do about it, then? Burn her warehouses? With whose watchmen? The magistrate's men answer to—" He stopped himself, too late, and looked at Corven, and the look said everything the sentence hadn't.

Corven stepped forward onto the block beside her. Up close she could see what the lamplight did to him: he looked like a man who had been underwater for a long time and had only recently decided whether he wanted to come up.

"Say it," Corven said quietly, to Pike, to the room. "My watchmen have answered to Rhea Vance since before the freeze. Some of them are on that manifest. I will give you their names myself if the town demands it." He drew a breath that seemed to cost him. "What the town does with me afterward is the town's affair. But tonight I am asking you to believe that the law in this town has been for sale, and that I am the one who sold it, and that starting tonight the price has changed."

Ilsa watched the crowd take that in — suspicion first, then something more dangerous: hope. Hope was the thing she had been afraid of all evening. Anger burns out. Hope organizes.

* * *

It came apart twice before it came together.

The first time was old Maren Kessel, who had lost her husband to the sea and her son to the war and had been saving for a berth on Rhea's private boat since October — everyone knew it, no one had said it. She stood on a crate in her dead son's coat and said, "You'll forgive me, Ilsa Brandt, if I don't cheer. That manifest is the only thing standing between me and spending the winter watching my grandchildren cough. You want to tear down the only ladder out of this frozen hole because it's a crooked ladder. Fine. Show me the straight one. Show me one honest berth on that steamer I can afford."

"That's the point, Maren," Ilsa said. "There isn't one. That's not the ladder's fault—"

"It's somebody's fault," Maren said, "and it isn't mine," and the crowd murmured agreement, and for a moment Ilsa felt the whole assembly tilt toward her like a deck in weather.

The second time was worse. A voice from near the doors — a voice Ilsa recognized with a lurch, because it belonged to the harbor office — called out: "And what about the courier?" It was Quartermaster Doss, red-faced, pointing into the crowd generally and at nothing specifically. "Since we're naming names tonight. There's a man in this town carrying papers that started this whole rot. Republic seals. Codes. Why do you think Vance built her little empire in the first place — because there's treasure here and everyone knows it! Every smuggler between here and the capital is sailing toward this harbor, and you're worried about warehouse seals. Give up the courier, search him properly, hang whatever he's carrying in the square, and maybe this ends without blood!"

The word *courier* went through the crowd like a hook through a net. Ilsa saw heads turn — not toward the doors, not toward Doss, but sideways, quick furtive turns, dozens of them, each person checking whether the person next to them was watching them check. Cyrus Dunmore stood near the back with the children and Noor, and he did not move at all, which was either innocence or the finest performance Ilsa had ever witnessed, and she realized she genuinely could not tell which, and realized further that neither could anyone else, and that this — the whole town suddenly unable to tell friend from courier from smuggler — was precisely the country Rhea Vance had been building all winter.

"He saved your bridge," Noor's voice cut through, thin and hard. "He's why any of you know about the parts at all—"

"Did he?" Doss said. "Or is that the story he gave you?"

And that was when Magistrate Corven did the thing that Ilsa Brandt would remember for the rest of her life, the thing she would describe in letters until her hands were too old to hold a pen.

He raised his hand for quiet, got it, and then stood in the lamplight with his robes open and his clerk's ledger under his arm and said: "The courier's papers came across my desk two nights ago. I have examined them. I am prepared to rule on them publicly, tomorrow morning, in this hall, under oath, where every soul in Verrigan can hear the ruling and the reasoning both. If the papers are what the law says they are, the law will take its course. If the law says a thing the town cannot live with, then the town will see exactly which of us is following the law and which of us is hiding behind it." He looked directly at Doss. "But justice done by torchlight at midnight, on rumor, against a stranger — that is not justice. That is just Rhea Vance with more people. We have all seen what her version looks like. Two of you carried Sef out of the lockup with your own hands."

Silence again. But it was a different silence now — heavier, deliberative, the silence of a jury rather than a mob. Ilsa felt it settle over the market like snowfall, and understood that Corven had just spent the last of whatever he had left to buy this town one night of thinking instead of one night of burning, and that whether it would prove enough depended entirely on what happened next.

Maren Kessel spoke again, slower this time. "And the berths? While we deliberate, the ice comes in. The Atlas sails when?"

"Five days," said a voice — the first mate of the Atlas, who had come ashore for this, which meant the ship's officers knew, which meant the stakes had climbed again without anyone announcing it. "Maybe less. The captain's given up pretending otherwise."

"Five days," Maren repeated. "So. We've got five days, a broken bridge, a magistrate who confesses in public, a courier nobody can find, and a captain with eleven berths and a private army." She looked around the hall, and something in her old face shifted — grief rearranging itself into resolve, the way ice rearranges into a sheet over water. "Then here's what I say. I say we don't ask anyone's permission. Not hers, not his." She pointed a knuckled finger at the manifest in the clerk's hands. "I say every soul in this town who wants off reads that list tonight, learns it, remembers it. I say warehouse four doesn't open without a public count. And I say that when the Atlas loads, this town stands on that quay and sees fair boarding with its own eyes — every ticket checked in the open, every passenger named aloud, and if anyone jumps the line, rich or poor or wearing a captain's coat, we put ourselves between them and that gangway. All of us. Together. Let her try to board past six hundred people who've agreed on one thing."

Nobody cheered. That was the strange part, the part Ilsa would try and fail to explain in letters for years. There was no roar, no caps thrown, none of the theater of the mobs she remembered from her girlhood. Instead there was a long exhale, hundreds of breaths fogging together in the lamplight, and then people simply began to move — toward the clerk, toward the manifest, toward one another, forming lines and knots and small determined committees with the unhurried competence of people who had spent their lives loading boats and mending nets and keeping bridges, applying that competence now to the business of refusing to be prey.

Halvard Pike was organizing a watch rotation on warehouse four before Ilsa got down off the block. The net-menders had claimed a corner and were drawing the harbor approaches in chalk on the flagstones. Someone's daughter was going door to door with a list of names of everyone who had bought from Vance, gathered not to punish but simply to *know*, which in a town this size was nearly the same as holding.

Corven descended from the block and stood beside her in the emptying space where the auction block cast its shadow. For a while neither of them spoke.

"You knew they'd want the courier's head," Ilsa said at last.

"I did."

"And you bought them one night anyway."

"One night," Corven agreed. His eyes went to the far end of the hall, where Cyrus Dunmore was shepherding the children toward the doors, Noor walking rearguard with her bruised face turned back toward the crowd, counting exits out of pure habit. "Whether it's enough depends on what I rule tomorrow. And what I rule tomorrow—" He stopped. Adjusted the ledger under his arm like a man shifting a wound. "Tomorrow I either save this town or indict myself, and I no longer believe those are different things. Good night, Mrs. Brandt. Keep your wrench handy."

He walked out into the snow alone.

Isla stayed a moment longer, watching the lamps gutter in the draft from the opened doors, watching her neighbors carry fire home in their cupped hands — not torch-fire, not yet, though it might become that; the other kind, the kind you keep alive through a winter by feeding it slowly and agreeing, night after night, not to let it go out.

Outside, the snow was falling thick and straight over the harbor, erasing the mole, the masts, the black shape of the Atlas at her berth. Somewhere beyond the white curtain, out on the water, a ship's bell rang once — the change of watch aboard Rhea Vance's vessel, keeping her own count of the same five days.

Five days, Ilsa thought, turning her collar up against the wind. And tomorrow morning a magistrate takes the stand and rules on a dead republic's secrets, in front of the whole town, with Rhea Vance listening from wherever she listens.

She set off down the hill toward the bridge tower, and behind her the fish market's lights burned on, window by window, household by household, the whole waterfront waking in the dark.
