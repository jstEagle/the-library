## 31. Rhea's Reckoning

Rhea Vance had lost harbors before. She had lost them to storms, to magistrates, to the slow rot of a crew that stopped believing in her. She had never yet lost one without taking something out of it that made the loss survivable.

She stood in the stern cabin of the *Meridian* — her ship, still hers, whatever Verrigan thought it had taken from her — and counted what remained. Twelve men and three women she could trust to follow her onto a frozen gangway in the dark. Two of them had been with her since the Baltic runs. The rest were leftovers: deckhands whose wages she'd paid in advance, a bosun dismissed from two other ships before hers, a boy named Pell who was sixteen and looked at her the way she had once looked at no one, because looking at anyone that way was how you died.

Twelve and three. Against a steamer whose crew had locked its arms together like men barring a tavern door, and against a waterfront that had spent the last week learning, lamplight by lamplight, exactly who she was.

The manifest had done it. The Brandt woman and her stolen papers, read aloud in the fish market like scripture, and half of Verrigan had discovered overnight that its indignation had a body. Her warehouses had been emptied of everything portable by morning. Her name had been spat on the ice by children who ought to have been afraid of it. Corven had not moved against her directly — the magistrate was too busy drowning in his own paperwork to sign anything so honest as an arrest warrant — but he didn't need to. The town had decided, and towns were worse than courts. Courts could be bribed.

"Captain." Kessing, her second, filled the doorway. He had a face like a hatchet left out in weather, and he had never once asked her whether a plan would work. It was why he was still alive. "They're all aboard the *Atlas*. Watch posted at both ends of the mole. The mate has the crew sleeping in shifts in the hold."

"The passengers?"

"Berthed aft. Locked companionway. The Dunmore man is aboard already — went up with the family at first dark, papers clean as church linen."

So. He had bought his way out after all, or bargained, or begged — it didn't matter which. She had offered him a place at her side twice, and twice he had chosen the smaller country. There was a kind of man who would rather be nobody in a crowd than somebody on open water, and Rhea had spent her whole life despising him and, in the worst hours of the night, envying him.

"And Corven?"

"Still in his office. Lights burning."

Burning his career down around himself, she thought, one page at a time. She had made the magistrate and she had unmade him, and now he was going to make himself into something else entirely just to spite her. Men were astonishing. Give them ruin and they'd find a way to call it redemption.

She spread the harbor chart on the table and weighted its corners with coins.

"The *Atlas* warps out on the morning tide," she said. "After that tide, this harbor freezes solid for four months and I am finished here — finished everywhere, if the passenger lists reach the consuls. So we take her tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight."

Kessing studied the chart without touching it. "With fifteen?"

"With twelve and three," she said, "and none of them expecting us. A steamer at anchor is a building with everyone asleep inside. We don't board at the gangway. We come up under the stern, quiet, on the ice side where their watch won't want to stand. We take the bridge first, then the engine room. Once we have steam and steerage, the crew can argue all they like from the deck of a ship they don't control."

"And the dockworkers."

"There are no dockworkers at three in the morning. There are men asleep in houses with stoves in them." She said it with more certainty than she felt. That was the trick of command nobody wrote down: you spoke your own doubts in a voice loud enough to drown them, and eventually your crew mistook the volume for conviction, and eventually — if the sea was kind — you did too.

"And afterward?" Kessing asked. "Verrigan will burn behind us."

"Verrigan was always going to burn behind us." She folded the chart. "The only question was whether we'd be standing on the water when it did."

* * *

They came up the harbor at low moon, oars muffled with sacking, the hull of the launch scraping along the new ice at the harbor's edge where it was thin enough to break with a boat hook. The cold was total. It got into the joints of her hands and set up residence, and Rhea thought, absurdly, of her mother's kitchen in a port she had not seen in thirty years, and put the thought out with the rest of the night's garbage.

The *Atlas* sat at her berth like a fat black whale, riding lights dimmed, funnel breathing thin smoke — steam up, good, the engineers keeping her warm against the freeze. Along the quay the watch fires burned at intervals, and figures moved between them, and there were more figures than there should have been.

Kessing saw it at the same moment she did. His hand found her shoulder, a familiarity he paid for yearly with his life's savings in deference, and squeezed once.

"That's not a watch, Captain. That's a garrison."

She counted. Twenty at least along the mole, and more silhouettes on the *Atlas*'s deck, and — she narrowed her eyes against the wind — shapes stacked along the warehouse fronts like cordwood. Men sitting on crates in the dark, waiting. Not sleeping. Waiting.

"They know," she said. The words came out flat and calm, and she was proud of that, somewhere underneath where pride was still possible.

"They can't know. Nobody knew but us—"

"Pell." She said the boy's name and felt the shape of the mistake even as she named it. Sixteen years old, three weeks aboard, and she had let him carry the message to the bosun because her own legs were tired. Three weeks. "When we're clear of this, find me."

"Captain, if they're waiting for us, the smart move is—"

"The smart move," Rhea said, "is what I say it is."

But she looked at the mole a long moment, at the fires and the waiting men, and she did the arithmetic honestly, because she had never in her life done any other kind. Fifteen against forty on a quay they held, with the *Atlas*'s crew armed above them and the town waking behind. The smart move was withdrawal. The smart move was the *Meridian* and the open sea and a winter of running, alive.

The smart move was also the end of her. A captain who ran from a frozen harbor with fifteen men was a story told in taverns for a season and forgotten by spring. Everything she had built in ten years of Verrigan — the routes, the bribes, the careful architecture of fear and favor — rested on the belief that Rhea Vance did not retreat. Belief was the only cargo that couldn't be stolen, only spoiled.

And besides. Beneath the arithmetic, in the flooded hold of herself where she kept the things that actually steered her, there was this: they had decided, these dockworkers with their lamplight justice, that Rhea Vance was finished. They had read her manifest aloud and turned her name into spit. She would show them what they had decided to finish.

"Under the stern," she said quietly. "As planned. If they want to stop us, they'll have to come out on the ice to do it, and men who've spent a warm evening by a fire think twice about the ice." She pulled her collar up. "Quietly, Kessing. All the way in."

* * *

They came alongside the *Atlas*'s stern quarter at half past three, and for ninety seconds the plan worked.

The grapnels went over silently, padded hooks finding the rail, and Rhea went up the line third, boots finding the hull's seams in the dark the way she had climbed hulls since she was younger than Pell. The wind off the ice cut across the deck and no one shouted. The stern watch was a single man at the taffrail, stamping his feet, facing the wrong way, and Kessing took him down with a forearm and a wad of sacking before the man's breath finished fogging.

Then Rhea stood on the afterdeck of the *Atlas* with fourteen followers pouring up behind her, and the deck lights came on.

All of them. At once. Every lantern on the afterdeck, every storm lamp hung at the bridge wings, blazing down on her like the opening of some theater where she was the only playbill.

And the deck was full.

Not the skeleton watch she'd mapped in her head. Fifty men at least, maybe sixty, arranged in a crescent around the afterdeck with the easy patience of men who had been standing there for hours. Dockworkers mostly — she recognized faces, haulers and riggers and the winch crews from the east quay — and among them, here and there, the harder faces of seamen she'd bribed or bullied over ten years and who had evidently, in the last week, conducted their own audit of that account. At the center of the crescent stood three old men she knew down to the grain of them: Halvorsen, the rigger; Osten, who had worked her cargoes since the first winter; and Brakk, the steam-winch man, whose hands could crush a coconut and who had eaten at her table.

None of them carried guns. That was the thing that turned her blood to slush. They carried hooks and lengths of chain and belaying pins, the tools of their trade, and they stood in the light like men who had been asked by history to do one simple thing and intended to do it well.

"Evening, Captain Vance." Halvorsen's voice was unhurried, an old man's voice, pitched to carry. "You're expected."

Rhea let the silence stretch. Behind her, her fifteen came up over the rail and stopped, one by one, the way water stops when it meets a wall.

"Expected," she repeated. "How theatrical. Who told you — Corven? Or did the widow Brandt organize this between bridge repairs?"

"No one told us anything," Osten said. "We can count boats, Captain. Same as you can count men. You've been massing on the *Meridian* for two days. Everyone on this waterfront can see it. Question was never *if*. Only when, and who'd be standing here."

Two days. She had spent two days assembling her force in plain sight because she no longer had hidden places to assemble anything, and she had told herself the fog and the dark would cover the arithmetic. Fog and dark. On a harbor full of men whose livelihood was reading water and weather and each other.

"You're outnumbered two to one," she said, "and you're holding tools against sailors. Go home. Every one of you has carried my crates and taken my money, and I'll forget this evening ever happened — that's still on offer, tonight only."

"We took your money," Brakk said. His voice was very soft for such a large man. "My brother took your money too, year before last, ferrying a family up the coast for you. You promised him return passage and gave him to the revenue cutters instead, to cover your own wake. He's doing six years in the Keswick hulks." He shifted the winch handle in his hands. "We did the sums on you a long time ago, Captain. This week the rest of the town caught up."

Ah, thought Rhea. There it was. Not the manifest, not the widow, not the magistrate's conscience. Arithmetic again. Ten years of debts called in at once, with interest, on a frozen deck.

"Last chance," Halvorsen said. "Gangway's behind you. Boat's below. Take your people and go out on the tide, and Verrigan will let you. That's the assembly's word, passed down the line. No blood tonight. The town doesn't want your corpse, Captain. It wants you gone."

And that — God help her — that was the unbearable part. Not the odds, not the crescent of hooks, but the *permission*, delivered politely by an old rigger in a knitted cap, as though Rhea Vance were a stray dog to be shooed across a threshold rather than the woman who had owned this harbor's nights for a decade. She felt it rise in her, the old white heat, the thing that had taken her from a cabin girl to a captain while better-tempered women drowned, and she knew even as it rose that it was the exact mechanism of her destruction, and she let it rise anyway, because it was the only part of herself she had never learned to refuse.

"Gone," she said. "On your word. On a dockworker's word." She laughed, and heard how it sounded, and hated the sound, and kept going. "I have taken ships out of blockaded ports. I have bought magistrates and buried rivals. You think a knitting circle frightens me off my own prize? Take her," she said to her people, loud, ringing off the deckhouse. "Take the bridge. Anyone who stands in the way goes over the rail."

For a heartbeat nothing moved. The lamps hissed. Somewhere back in the town a dog began to bark, and was silenced.

Then Kessing surged forward toward the crescent with four men behind him, and the deck exploded.

It was not a battle; battles have lines and maneuvers. It was a brawl in a furnace room, close and hot and graceless, chains swinging at shoulder height, men going down on steel deck plates and not getting up quickly, the lanterns swinging so the shadows reeled drunkenly across the fight. Rhea drove for the starboard ladder to the bridge with Pell at her heels, and a rigger caught Pell's collar, and the boy twisted free with a knife she hadn't known he carried, and the rigger sat down hard holding his arm, and Rhea was on the ladder, rung after rung, the bridge door ahead of her, actual brass-handled victory ahead of her—

—and the engine telegraph rang.

Full astern. From the engine room. Someone down in the guts of the ship — one of her own? No. One of theirs, one of the *Atlas*'s engineers, warned, ready, sitting on his levers all night waiting for exactly this. The whole vessel shuddered and leaned, and the mooring lines snapped taut and sang, and everyone fighting on her deck went to their knees at once, a hundred bodies dumped sideways like crockery, and through the bridge windows Rhea watched the harbor swing crazily past as the great ship surged backward against her lines, and understood, in the space of one lurching second, that the ship itself had chosen a side.

The *Atlas* was not a prize. The *Atlas* was a fortress, and fortresses do not care who boards them.

She got to the bridge door anyway. It was barred — barred from within, iron pin driven home days ago, the glass fogged with the breath of men inside who were watching her through it. She saw a face in the fogged glass. The first mate. He did not gloat. He simply looked at her, the way you look at weather that has finally arrived, and shook his head slowly, once.

Below, the fight was dying. Not won — dying, smothered, pressed out between the crescent and the rail and the sheer weight of men who had slept beside their boots waiting for the whistle. Kessing was down on one knee with two riggers standing over him, hatchet-faced and defeated, making the wise calculation he had never once made about her plans. Her Baltic men were dragging wounded toward the rail. And Pell — Pell was on the deck, curled small, and Osten was standing over the boy not striking him, only standing, guarding him from the melee, which was somehow worse than any blow.

Rhea stood on the bridge wing of a ship she would never own, in light she had walked into with her eyes open, and made the last command decision of her Verrigan years.

"To the boat," she said. Her voice carried; it always had; it would carry over her own funeral. "All hands. We are done here."

* * *

They pulled away from the *Atlas*'s stern with seven unwounded, five limping, and Pell bleeding from a scalp wound, and the deck crew of the steamer let them go. That was the humiliation refined to its essence: they let her go, standing aside at the rail with their hooks grounded like soldiers at present arms, granting passage, granting nothing else. No shot pursued her. No shout. The harbor was already closing over the event, deciding what it would become in the telling — *the night Vance tried for the steamer*, someone would say, *and the docks stood together* — and in the telling she would shrink, decade by decade, until she was a story dockhands used to frighten dishonest captains.

Rhea sat in the stern sheets with the ice grumbling under the keel and did not look back at the harbor. Looking back was for people who still wanted things from places.

"Where to, Captain?" Kessing. Blood on his collar, voice level, loyal past all reason or accounting. "The *Meridian*?"

The *Meridian*. Her ship, yes. Also a hull anchored in a harbor whose every eye now knew her, under a sky that would freeze her in within the fortnight, in a town that had voted her gone and had the numbers to enforce the vote. To stay aboard the *Meridian* was to be besieged, then trapped, then taken, in that order, by spring.

"North channel," she said. "Out on this tide, while the ice still has gaps."

"North channel's not cleared, Captain. And the *Meridian*'s short-handed. Fifteen souls can't work her, and half of those will jump at the first port."

"Then they can jump." She heard her own voice go quiet, which frightened her crew more than shouting ever had. "We'll take what crew we can buy at the northern ports. There are always men who want what Verrigan doesn't anymore."

Pell lifted his head from the gunwale, young face gray in the starlight. "Captain — the others. The ones they took off the quay. Halloran and the Voss brothers—"

"Are Verrigan's problem now." She said it without heat, and watched the boy understand her, and watched something go out of his face that she suspected, dully, she would miss.

The launch threaded the black leads between the ice pans. Ahead, at the harbor mouth, the north channel opened its narrow dark throat toward the open sea, and beyond the sea there were other harbors, other winters, other towns that had not yet learned her name. She would start again. She had started again before. That was the whole of her biography, written in salt: begin, build, lose, begin.

Except.

Except that this time, for the first time, the losing had been public. This time the ledger was known. She could outrun ice and magistrates and even memory, but she could not outrun arithmetic, and the arithmetic now read: Rhea Vance, stripped of harbor, stripped of crew, stripped of the belief that had been her only un-stolen cargo, fleeing north on the year's last tide with a bleeding boy and a hatchet-faced man and eleven strangers.

At the mouth of the channel she paused at the tiller, one moment only, and let herself look back. Verrigan lay under frost and lamplight, small as a toy port, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, and far down the quay a single figure stood at the edge of the mole watching the launch go — a woman, broad-shouldered, unmistakable even at this distance. The Brandt widow. Rhea could not see whether the woman raised a hand, and decided she had not, and steered for the open dark.

Behind them, faint across the water, came the sound of the *Atlas*'s bell ringing eight times, and then — she refused to believe it, and heard it anyway — a ragged cheer from the docks, fading as the channel walls closed around the launch like a book shutting.

By dawn, she knew, the harbor would be sheeted white from shore to shore, smooth and sealed and utterly indifferent, the way a page looks after the ink has dried.

"Make for the northern ports," Rhea Vance said to the frozen dark, to no one, to the sea that had taken everything else she'd ever owned and given back only the next horizon. "There's always another harbor."

But the dark did not answer, and the tide ran north, and behind her the lights of Verrigan went out one street at a time, going to bed, going on without her.
