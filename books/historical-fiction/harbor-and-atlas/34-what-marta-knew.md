## 34. What Marta Knew

The confession had been sitting in Marta's chest for eleven days, and by the twelfth it had grown teeth.

She felt them every time Cyrus smiled at her across the loft, every time he handed her the larger half of the bread without being asked, every time Tomas fell asleep against his shoulder like it was the most natural thing in the world. The guilt had started small — a pebble in her boot — and now it was a stone wall she had to climb each morning just to look at her uncle's face.

She had traded him. Not for money. That would have been simpler, cleaner, something she could hate herself for cleanly. No — she had traded him for fish stew and a ribbon and the warm, swelling feeling of being *someone*, of knowing a secret worth two bowls and a length of blue ribbon. Greta at the fish stall had asked such simple questions, with such kind eyes, and Marta had been so hungry for an adult who treated her as interesting rather than invisible.

*There's a lodger at the chandlery,* she'd said. *A man who mends crates but has hands like a gentleman. He burns papers at night when he thinks no one watches.*

That was all. That was everything.

She had watched the words travel after that — seen them move through the docks the way gossip moves through gulls, one beak to the next — and then came the raid on the chandlery, the sweep of waterfront lodgings, the flight to Sef's net shed, and finally the raid on the shed itself, where they'd taken Sef away in irons while the second case — the drowned man's case, the one buried under the floor that Marta wasn't even supposed to know about — was found empty because Cyrus had moved it hours before.

But Sef was still in the lockup. Noor's lip was still split from where Rhea's men had worked her over on the docks. And last night, over the fish cellars' smell of brine and darkness where they now slept, Marta had heard Noor say quietly to Cyrus: *Someone talked. Rhea's scouts don't find sheds by accident.*

Marta had lain awake until the lamps on the mole went out, doing arithmetic she couldn't finish.

Now it was morning, and the household had gathered in the chandlery loft — Cyrus had won back access somehow, some arrangement with the chandler whose name nobody said aloud — to hear the news. One berth. That was what they kept saying, in the low voices adults used when they thought children weren't listening, which was a joke, because children heard everything; hearing was the one skill starvation sharpened.

One berth left on the *Atlas*. Three names cleared: Marta, Tomas, Noor. Four people. Cyrus intended to stay behind.

She knew it the way you know weather coming — not from any single proof but from the pressure, the way he'd begun looking at things too long, memorizing them. Last night he had looked at Tomas sleeping for nearly a full minute, the way you look at a harbor you're leaving.

So the confession came up her throat like a fish bone, and this time she didn't push it down.

* * *

Cyrus was repairing the latch on the loft window when she did it — always fixing things, always making their hiding places tighter, as if his hands couldn't stand still — and Marta stood in the middle of the room with her fists balled at her sides until Noor glanced up from the map she was copying and nudged Tomas, who nudged back, and then there was silence, the particular silence of three people noticing the fourth.

"I have to tell you something," Marta said.

Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. She tried again, louder, the way she'd rehearsed it against the cellar wall at dawn.

"It was me."

Cyrus set down the latch. He didn't ask *what was you*. That was the worst part — he didn't make her explain the crime before convicting her, he simply waited, and his waiting had no cruelty in it, which was unbearable.

"The woman at the fish stall. Greta. She gave us stew, remember? Back at the chandlery, before the freeze." The words tumbled now, all of them, the whole ugly ledger. "She asked about you and I told her. I told her you were a lodger who burned papers at night and had gentleman's hands and I took the stew and I took a ribbon and I — " Her breath hitched. "I liked it. I liked that she thought I knew something. And then the raiders came to the shed and they took Sef and hurt Noor and it's because of me. All of it is because of me."

Tomas had gone very still by the stove. Noor had risen halfway off her crate, hand out as if to reach for Marta, and stopped.

"And I know what you're going to do," Marta went on, because if she stopped now she never would, and the wall had to come down or crush her. "You're going to leave me here. You should. You've got one berth and three names and you were going to give your seat up anyway, so now you don't have to feel bad about it, you can just — leave the one who sold you. That's fair. I did the sums myself." Her voice cracked on *fair* and she hated it. "I just wanted to say it to your face first. So you'd know."

The loft was quiet. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the chandler rolled a barrel across the floorboards, and the sound passed under them like a slow wave.

Cyrus crossed the room. Marta braced — for the shout, the cold nod, the turning away, whatever form banishment decided to wear — and instead he sat down on the floor. On the floor, in the dust, in front of her, so that she was taller than him. He looked up at her with tired eyes that had seen, she understood suddenly, far worse confessions than hers, from far more powerful mouths.

"Sit down," he said gently. "All of you. I want to tell you something true, and I'm tired of telling it standing up."

* * *

They sat in a rough circle on the floorboards, the four of them, like children around a campfire, except the fire was a stove and the story was a wound.

"You're right about the berth," Cyrus began. "One left. Your name, Tomas's, and Noor's are cleared. Mine isn't on the list because I took mine off." He raised a hand before Tomas could speak. "Let me finish. I promised your mother — " his voice caught on the name and recovered, "— I promised her I'd get both of you across the sea. I didn't promise to go with you. I decided a long time ago that those were the same thing, and I was wrong to decide it alone."

"Uncle Cyrus—" Tomas started.

"Two weeks ago I sat with the first mate of the *Atlas* and worked out the arithmetic like a merchant," Cyrus said. "Three sail, one stays. And do you know what I felt?" He laughed, short and hollow. "Relief. Because staying meant finishing it. Finishing *me*. The man I was before Verrigan — the courier, the carrier of other people's secrets — he could die here on the dock and the children could live, and everyone would call that noble." He shook his head. "It isn't noble. It's just another way of running. I ran from the republic, I ran from your mother's grave, and I was going to run from this family by calling my leaving a sacrifice. That's the truth. You wanted truth from me, Marta — there's mine. You sold gossip for a bowl of stew. I sold my family's future for a clean conscience. Which of us got the better price?"

Marta stared at him. Something enormous was happening inside her chest and she didn't have a word for it yet.

"Greta didn't sell us," Cyrus went on, quietly. "I've spent these eleven days tracing the leak, because I needed to know how badly we'd been sold. Do you know what I found? Greta told a dockhand. The dockhand told a tavern keeper. The tavern keeper sold it to one of Rhea's men — *he* chose to sell it, for coin, to a woman who used it to put an old man in irons. Your words traveled, Marta, but words aren't knives until someone pays for the forging. Greta didn't know what she was asking. You didn't know what you were answering. You were nine years old and hungry and someone made knowing things feel like being loved. That's not treachery. That's a trap, and you walked into it the way children walk into traps — because the bait was food."

"But Sef—"

"Sef knew the risk when he opened his door. So did Noor." Cyrus looked at Noor, and Noor, lip still scabbed, shrugged one shoulder and said, "I'd take the beating again. Ask me tomorrow too, I'll say it again." And Tomas, who hadn't spoken, suddenly said in a fierce small voice, "Sef would say it was worth it. He'd say it *exactly* like that. 'Worth it, girl, stop making that face.'" And for a moment, impossibly, they almost laughed, grief and laughter being close cousins in cold rooms.

"No," Cyrus said. "What you did mattered, Marta. It frightened me, it cost Sef his freedom for a night or a week — we'll get him back, that's settled, that's already moving — but it also taught me something I refused to learn any other way." He looked around the circle. "It taught me that secrets keep no one safe. Only people keep people safe. This town is going to keep Sef's door open, and keep Rhea off our gangway, and keep the magistrate honest for exactly as long as they decide we're theirs — and we are theirs, a little, now. We belong to Verrigan whether we sail or stay. You can't buy that kind of belonging. Apparently you can only get it by making terrible mistakes in public."

He turned back to Marta, and his voice dropped to the register he saved for the important things, the things he said only once.

"I'm not leaving you behind. Not for the stew. Not for the raid. Not ever. If there is one berth and five of us, we will find a way to make it six names or no sailing, but nobody gets traded. Nobody gets weighed." A pause. "I almost weighed you. In my head, that first night, when you both arrived at the gate — I did the sums. Two children, one courier, one mission. I chose you, but I chose you the way a man chooses cargo. I've been trying to learn the difference ever since."

Marta found her voice somewhere near her shoes. "Then why are you still planning to stay?"

And Cyrus Dunmore, who had answers for magistrates and smugglers, had none for a nine-year-old. He opened his mouth, closed it, and finally spread his hands — empty, calloused, unremarkable hands that had carried the fate of a dead republic sewn into a coat lining.

"Because I'm afraid," he said simply. "And fear does its sums in the dark."

* * *

It was Noor who broke the shape of the argument — Noor, who had survived three borders and had therefore developed, Marta thought, an instinct for when a conversation was circling a hole instead of crossing it.

"So don't do sums in the dark," she said. "Do them out loud. Right now. All four of us." She looked around the circle with her sharp dark eyes. "Everyone keeps deciding things *for* everyone else. He decides to stay. You decide to confess alone. I decide to take beatings without asking permission first." She touched her split lip, almost absently. "Maybe that's how we keep losing pieces of ourselves. Everyone's making choices alone."

Tomas, who was ten and mostly quiet and missed his mother with a completeness that scared the adults, said into the pause: "What's family mean?"

Nobody answered right away. The stove ticked. Outside, the frozen harbor creaked and boomed as the tide worked under the ice, that great slow breathing sound that Marta had come to think of as the town's own sleep.

"Blood," Marta offered at last, half-heartedly, because that was the church answer.

"Mother's blood is back there," Tomas said. "Under the ground. That doesn't help us pick berths."

"Protection," said Noor. "Family protects."

"We can't protect each other," Marta pointed out. "We keep getting raided."

Cyrus was watching them all with an expression Marta had never seen on an adult before — not patience exactly, and not amusement, but a kind of open-handed attention, as if the answer mattered more coming from them than from him, as if whatever they built in this moment he intended to live inside for the rest of his life.

"Then what?" he asked softly. "What does family mean, if it isn't blood, and it isn't perfect protection?"

Silence again, but a working silence this time, four heads bent around the question like hands around a knot. Marta stared at the floorboards where a case full of a dead republic's secrets had been hidden, and thought about the fish stall, and the ribbon — blue, she'd hidden it, she'd never even worn it — and about Sef's big ruined hands showing Tomas knots, and Noor standing between Rhea's men and questions, taking the boot without giving up two children she'd known for three weeks.

And it arrived, the way real things arrive: all at once, obvious, already true before she said it.

"Nobody gets left to make the choice alone," Marta said.

She said it again, louder, testing it, and found it held weight.

"That's it. That's what family is. It's not that nobody gets left *behind* — sometimes somebody has to stay, maybe, sometimes the boat only holds so many — but nobody gets left *alone* with the choosing. If Uncle Cyrus stays, we all stay part of the deciding. If I make a mistake, I don't carry it by myself in a cellar at dawn. Nobody gets to be the one who sacrifices in secret and nobody gets to be the one who's sacrificed in secret. Everything out loud. Even the arithmetic." She looked at Cyrus, defiance and hope fighting in her chest. "Especially the arithmetic."

Noor nodded slowly. "Out loud," she agreed. "First rule of crossing borders. You travel in a group, you decide in a group, or the border picks you off one at a time. Borders love people who are alone with their choices."

Tomas reached over and took his sister's hand, awkwardly, the way boys do, glaring at the middle distance as if daring anyone to remark on it. "Then it's decided," he said. "That's the rule. Nobody decides alone."

Cyrus didn't answer at first. Marta watched him the way she'd watched him since the gate — cataloguing, wary, hungry for proof — and what she saw was a man setting something down. Not the codes; those were already promised away, pages destined for a stove. Something heavier. The habit of solitude. Eleven months of it. A lifetime of it. His eyes were wet and he let them be wet, which told her more than any speech.

"Then here's my decision, out loud, subject to the rule," he said at last. "I was going to stay because staying was the only way I knew to matter. Now I'm asking you instead: what should I do?"

"Come with us," Tomas said instantly.

"If there's a way," Noor said — practical, always practical — "take it. And if there isn't, then we decide *that* together too. But first we make there be a way. Corven owes you terms. The mate owes you honesty. Make them both say out loud what they said in the dark."

Marta stood up. Her legs felt strange, lighter, as if the stone wall in her chest had come down and the materials were being repurposed on the spot — into a rule, a rope, a family stitched out of confession and consequence.

"Then let's go ask," she said. "All four of us. Out loud. Before the tide turns."

Cyrus looked around the circle — his sister's children and a stowaway, his whole visible supply of family, his whole new country — and something in his face that had been winter for a very long time finally, cautiously, began to thaw.

"Together, then," he said, reaching for his coat. "But if we're walking onto Rhea Vance's docks as a family, you should know — " and here, impossibly, he smiled, tired and real — "there's a manifest missing from her office, a bridge that needs her hoarded parts, and a magistrate who owes me a signature. We may be about to become famous."

From below came the chandler's shout that a boat was at the ladder — and through the frost-rimed window Marta saw, standing on the quay in the pale noon light, Magistrate Corven himself, hat in hand, looking up at the loft like a man who had finally, at terrible cost, decided to keep his word.
