## 35. The Codes Burned

Corven came at dusk, alone, wearing no robes and carrying his fear like a man who had learned to carry it close to the body, where it made less silhouette.

Cyrus had watched him cross from the magistrate's office along the frozen quay — a straight, unhurried line through the lamplight and the long blue shadows of stacked crates, past dockworkers who nodded to him because they did not yet know what he was about to become. The ice in the harbor groaned beneath its snow like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Beyond the mole, the *Atlas* rode at her berth with steam up, black funnels against a sky the color of ash and iron, three days from sailing.

He let Corven knock twice before he opened the chandlery door.

"You came without your clerk," Cyrus said.

"I came without anyone." Corven stepped inside, stamping snow from his boots. He looked older than he had in the autumn — not weaker, Cyrus thought, but hollowed out, the way timber is hollowed to make a drum. "You said you'd know by today whether I could be trusted. I dismissed my clerk an hour ago and told him to go home to his mother in Aldenmark. If this goes wrong, he'll testify I acted alone."

"That's not what I asked."

"No." Corven removed his hat. His gray hair was flattened by it, giving him briefly the look of a schoolboy called before a board of inquiry. "It isn't. But you should have the answer anyway. Everything I do from tonight is mine alone. That is the only kind of trust I have left to offer."

The loft above was warm. Marta had fed the stove until its iron sides ticked like a clock, and Tomas had laid the table for four with the ceremony of a boy who had eaten from garbage barrels two winters running and still found abundance miraculous: bread, salt fish, the last jar of Noor's pickled plums. Noor stood by the window with her arms crossed, watching the street the way she always watched it — not nervous, exactly. Inventorying.

"Sit," Cyrus said, and the word came out rougher than he intended, so he said it again, softer, and pulled out a chair with his own hand.

They ate almost in silence. Tomas asked Corven whether magistrates were allowed to eat pickled plums, and Corven considered the question with such gravity that Marta laughed for the first time in days, and Cyrus understood that the children were doing what children do in rooms full of dangerous adults: they were building a small fire of normalcy and standing around it. He let them. He even let himself stand near it, briefly, feeling its heat on his face.

Afterward he sent them up the ladder to the sleeping loft. Tomas went reluctantly, looking back; Noor went without looking back at all, which was its own kind of trust. Then it was just the two men and the stove and the sound of the harbor ice shifting in the dark.

Cyrus went to the corner where the floorboard lifted, worked it free with his thumbnail — the nail hole worn smooth now, a thing his fingers knew blind — and brought up the oilcloth bundle. It was lighter than it had been in October. It was always lighter every time he lifted it, as though the paper inside slowly sublimated into dread, leaving only paper behind.

He set it on the table between them.

"The duplicate case we took from the drowned courier," he said. "You've read it."

"I've read what I could read." Corven sat down slowly, as if his joints had opinions about honesty. "Column ciphers, rotating keys, a seal I prosecuted men for forging eleven years ago. Without the key matrix, it's expensive wallpaper. With it—" He stopped.

"With it, it's your exoneration. Proof of what Rhea held over you. Dates, amounts, the names of the men she bought. A record of every favor you refused and every one you didn't."

"Yes." Corven's voice was very quiet. "And also a weapon in any hand strong enough to hold it. That's what you've been deciding all winter, haven't you? Not whether to give it to me. Whether it should exist at all."

Cyrus sat down across from him. Between them the bundle lay like a body at a wake.

"When my sister was dying," he said, "she made me promise two things. Get the children across the sea. And never become the kind of man who could explain to them later why someone else had to die for it." He turned the bundle over once with his palm. "I've kept the first promise badly and the second one barely. There are men in the harbor jail because of me. There's a woman on a ship in your harbor who will kill me if the tide leaves her nothing else. Every day these papers stay in the world, I'm worth more dead than alive to somebody. Do you understand what that does to a person, Magistrate? It teaches him to count exits instead of days."

"I have some familiarity with the condition."

"I know you do. That's why it's you." Cyrus drew a breath. "Here is my offer, and it is the last one, and after tonight there is no other version. I give you the key matrix — here, now, memorized or written, whichever lets you sleep. You use it once, in private, to build your defense against Rhea. Then you burn the transcript and the original together, and you swear to me on whatever you still believe in that when the inquiry comes — and it will come — you tell them the codes died with the republic they belonged to. Because they did. That's the truth, Corven. That's the part nobody in this town has bothered to say out loud all winter. The country that issued these papers doesn't exist. There's no government left to receive them, no army left to act on them, no cause left that a single page of this can serve. All that's left is what they're worth to hungry people. And I am done feeding people to them."

Corven was silent for a long time. The stove ticked. Out on the water, something cracked — a lead opening or closing, impossible to say.

"You could have sold them," he said finally. "Rhea offered you a boat. I offered you immunity. Either bargain would have saved your skin and cost you nothing you couldn't have slept through."

"They would have cost everything. Just later." Cyrus smiled, and it hurt his face, because it was the first smile he'd given anyone in authority since before the republic fell. "Besides. You wrote four confessions this month and burned three. My clerk tells me things too."

Corven stared at him. Then, slowly, he began to laugh — a dry, astonished sound, half cough, the laugh of a man hearing his own sentence read and finding it lighter than feared.

"Your clerk," he said, "is a menace."

"She is. She's sixteen and she's survived three borders, and I'd trust her before I'd trust most ministries." Cyrus rose, went to the drawer, and took out paper and the stub of pencil Marta used for her sums. He sat again and began to write — columns of figures, letter-pairings, the rotation intervals he had carried in his head for eleven months like stones in his boot. His hand did not shake. He noticed that it did not shake, and was surprised, and then was surprised at being surprised, as though grief and relief had spent so long sharing the same room in him that they'd forgotten which was which.

When it was done — two pages, small, exact — he slid them across the table.

"Read it here. Memorize what you can. Then we do the rest together, or not at all."

Corven read. His lips moved slightly, counting. Twice he stopped and closed his eyes, and Cyrus watched a man walk through his own ruin and find, somewhere in the wreckage, a door marked *out*. When he finished, he folded the pages with great care, as one folds a letter that will be reread, and put them inside his coat against his chest.

"Now," he said. His voice had changed. It was steadier and somehow younger. "Show me what dies."

* * *

They opened the bundle together at the table, and for a moment neither of them moved.

Inside the oilcloth lay the code pages of a country that had lasted nineteen months and been mourned ever since as if it had lasted a generation. Cyrus had not looked at them whole since the autumn. He had checked their edges under floorboards, counted them by touch in the dark, weighed them in his hand a hundred times — but he had not *read* them, because reading them meant admitting they existed, and not admitting they existed had been the whole architecture of his survival.

Now he spread them out, and the stove-light moved across the fine cipher columns, and he saw his own handwriting in the margins from another life — annotations in a ministry shorthand, notes to colleagues who were dead or scattered or worse, a small tidy universe of men who had believed that paper could outlive guns.

"There's the naval table," he said, pointing, his finger steady. "Harbor authentication protocols. Useless — both harbors are rubble. Diplomatic channel keys. Dead — the missions were closed in the spring. This one—" he touched a page near the bottom and his throat tightened unexpectedly, "—this is the courier rotation for the southern rail line. I helped design it. A friend of mine named Ostrander ran the first circuit. He was shot at Halvet Bridge in the second week of the war."

"You knew him."

"I knew everyone. That was the problem with being good at the work. You knew everyone, and then you knew their names read off a list, and then you stopped going to the lists." Cyrus gathered the pages square. "There is no one left alive who can act on a single line of this. Only people who can profit from it. That's all it is now. Profit. For Rhea, for whoever buys her, for any magistrate weak enough—"

He stopped, because Corven had flinched, and it would have been cruelty to finish.

"Forgive me," Cyrus said.

"No." Corven shook his head once, firmly, like a man refusing a dish. "Don't forgive me in advance of anything. Let me earn the ordinary courtesies as I go." He stood, rolled his sleeves, and looked at the stove with something approaching appetite. "Well, Magistrate Corven. Here is your jurisprudence at last. No warrant. No witnesses. No appeal."

"Two witnesses," Cyrus said.

"One of whom is a ghost," said Corven, "and one of whom intends to become one."

They knelt together at the stove, which struck Cyrus afterward as either comedy or liturgy, depending on how much a man needed it to be holy. Cyrus opened the firebox door. The coals within were banked low but living, a red country of embers no bigger than a hearth, and he thought, absurdly, that all winter he had treated this little iron nation as the only safe border left to him.

He fed the pages in one at a time.

The first caught immediately — dry paper, hungry heat — and curled into a black blossom that broke apart into sparks. The naval table went next, its columns glowing briefly legible, numbers shining through the char like bones through smoke, then collapsing inward. He fed the diplomatic keys and watched the seals' ink flare greenish, some chemistry of old iron-gall burning off its last authority. Each page made a different death: some went fast and bright, some smoldered and had to be pushed into the coals with the poker, and one — the southern rail rotation, Ostrander's circuit — he held longer than he needed to, reading the margin notes of his former self, until Corven said quietly, "Let him rest too," and Cyrus set it on the coals and closed his eyes while it burned.

Smoke went up the flue. Somewhere beyond the roof, above the frozen harbor, it joined the night.

His hands were empty.

That was the thing he had not prepared for. Not the heat, not the smell of scorched ink, not the strange grief of it — but the sheer lightness after, the way his coat no longer hung wrong on his shoulders, the way the floorboard corner no longer called to him from across the room like a debt. Eleven months he had been a man shaped around an object, a keyhole-shaped absence walking around in a stolen coat, and now the absence filled with plain air, ordinary and cold and entirely his own.

He sat back on his heels and found, to his embarrassment, that his eyes were wet.

Corven pretended, with the skill of a lifetime in courtrooms, to be absorbed in banking the fire.

"It's done," Cyrus said, when he trusted his voice.

"It's done." Corven closed the firebox and latched it, sealing the little red country. "Tomorrow I'll write out what I remember of the key from memory, verify it against my notes, and destroy the transcription. By noon the codes exist in exactly one head in the world, behind no lock but mine." He paused. "That makes me the most dangerous man in Verrigan, I suppose."

"It makes you the most *free* man in Verrigan. There's a difference, and you'll feel it tomorrow, and it will terrify you."

"It already does." Corven rose, retrieved his hat, and turned it in his hands. At the door he stopped. "Dunmore. The inquiry will want a name for you. What the papers call you, when this all comes out. What shall I write?"

Cyrus looked at the stove. Behind its iron door, the last embers of the republic were settling into ash that would be cold by morning and scooped out with the cinders like anything ordinary.

"Nothing," he said. "Write nothing. Or write *unknown civilian, traveling with family.*" He tried the phrase in his mouth and found it fit better than any name he'd worn since boyhood. "Yes. That one. *Traveling with family.*"

Corven nodded once — a judge's nod, brief and binding — and went out into the dark.

* * *

They woke the children to tell them, because it seemed wrong to let them sleep through their own liberation.

Marta came down the ladder with her hair loose and her suspicion fully armed, and when Cyrus told her — plainly, without ceremony, the way she had demanded he tell her things ever since the night she confessed her gossip — she looked at the cold stove for a long moment and then at him.

"So nobody wants us anymore," she said. "Nobody's hunting us."

"Nobody's hunting the papers. Some fools may still come asking about us. But we're not worth money now, sweetheart. We're only worth ourselves."

Tomas, wrapped in his blanket, considered this with the ferocious literalism of an eight-year-old. "Then can we stop practicing the escape route?"

Noor laughed from the shadows by the window — a short bark of a laugh, quickly strangled, the way she still laughed when something caught her genuinely off guard. "He's been timing you," she told Cyrus. "Every night. Through the cellar. Fourteen seconds."

"Twelve," Tomas corrected.

"Twelve," Noor conceded gravely, "if the cat cooperates."

Marta climbed into Cyrus's lap despite being nearly too big for it, the way she still did on nights that mattered, and put her mouth by his ear as if the walls had been listening all winter and might need correcting.

"Does this mean we won?"

Cyrus thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and had paid for it in gossip and guilt and fourteen-second escape drills. He thought about Sef in the cellars smiling as they led him away, about Noor's split lip healing crooked, about Ilsa Brandt standing on her bridge in the wind, about a magistrate who had signed away his career with a steadier hand than he'd ever signed a verdict.

"It means," he said, "that we don't have to win anymore. Which is what winning turns into, if you're lucky, if you quit while everyone's still breathing."

Marta accepted this with a child's shrug, which is to say she filed it among the many adult mysteries she was collecting against future use, and announced she was going back to sleep, and that everyone else should too, because the *Atlas* sailed in three days and packing required rest.

They banked the stove. The loft settled into darkness, the four of them breathing in the rhythms that families drift into without noticing — Tomas's slow deep tide, Marta's smaller quicker one, Noor's careful silence that wasn't quite sleep, Cyrus's own ragged thing gradually, gratefully falling into step with the others.

Before sleep took him he lay a while with his eyes open, doing the thing he had done every night since October: checking exits, counting threats, running the arithmetic of flight.

And found the sum coming up empty. No case under a floorboard. No name worth killing for. No weight sewn into any coat. Just a loft, a stove gone gray, three children not all of them his by blood and all of them his by choice, and outside the frost-white window the masts of the *Atlas*, three days out, taking the moon on their yards like spears laid down.

He was no one. He was unreadable. He was, as far as any ledger in the world was concerned, an unknown civilian traveling with family.

Cyrus Dunmore — or whatever the man in the loft was called now — slept better than he had in eleven months, and dreamed of nothing but water, moving, unhurried, going somewhere green.

* * *

He woke before dawn on the third day to the smell of the sea and the sound of the town already awake.

Below the window the waterfront was coming alive for departure: cart wheels on frozen cobbles, gulls screaming over the fish stalls, the *Atlas*'s bosun's whistle cutting thin and bright through the cold. Steam stood up from her funnels in a white column that leaned east with the wind. On the quay, small figures were already gathering — passengers with their bundles, dockworkers with their hooks, and one straight figure at the foot of the gangway who could only be Corven, arriving early, holding documents.

Noor was dressed. Of course Noor was dressed; she had probably never fully undressed. She handed Cyrus his coat without a word, and when he shrugged into it his hand went automatically to the old seam where the case had been sewn and found only flat cloth, and the flatness of it felt like a hand letting go of his.

Tomas was at the window on his knees, counting masts. Marta had already packed and repacked the single bag twice and was now supervising everyone else's packing with the air of a harbor master. Down in the street, Sef was waiting with his cart, having appointed himself, over all objections, the official porter of the expedition.

Cyrus took one last look around the loft — the knots Tomas had nailed to the beam for practice, the tally marks Noor kept by the window, the smooth-worn floorboard lying flush and innocent, keeping nothing.

Three days had become one morning. The tide was in, the papers were ash, and the whole town, apparently, intended to see them to the gangway.

"Marta," he said, picking up the bag. "Children. Let's go home a different way."

They went down into the cold together, toward the ship, and the frost-white mole stretched out ahead of them like a road being built as they walked it.
