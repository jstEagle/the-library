## 37. The Morning Tide

### I.

Corven had dressed before dawn out of habit, and because there was nothing left in the house worth sleeping for.

He stood at his window while the town below him assembled itself along the quay — lamplighters still working their rounds through the blue-grey dark, dock crews drifting toward the mole like iron filings toward a magnet, and everywhere the smell of frost and tar that he would carry in his nostrils for the rest of his life, whatever shape the rest of his life took. The Atlas rode at her berth with steam already feathering from her stacks, a white ghost breathing against the black water.

On his desk behind him lay the letter he had written at midnight, addressed to the tribunal convening in the provincial capital. Four pages. He had burned three drafts of a confession over the winter, and the fourth — the one his clerk had copied without being asked, the one the boy had pressed back into his hands two nights ago with eyes like a man handing over a live coal — that fourth version was now on its way south in the mail pouch, whether Corven willed it or not. So he had written this last letter himself, plainly, without art: an accounting of Rhea Vance's operation, of the bribes and the ledgers and the names, and at the bottom, in his own hand, a full confession of what he had done to let it grow.

*I served the law until it was convenient not to,* he had written. *Herein is the record of what that cost.*

He had signed it. That was the whole trick of it, he thought — signing your name after years of forging everyone else's.

Below, a gull screamed once and went silent, as if embarrassed by the noise on such a morning. Corven put on his coat of office for the last time, looked at himself in the small mirror above the washstand, and found that the man looking back was a stranger he did not entirely dislike.

He left the robe on the hook by the door. Whatever happened on that quay, he would face it as Corven, not as the robes. The robes had done enough damage on their own.

* * *

### II.

The bridge sang when it moved now.

Ilsa Brandt felt it through her boots — a low, sweet vibration running up through the iron of the new gears and into her ankles, the sound of machinery turning the way machinery was meant to turn, oiled and true and *hers*. Six days it had taken. Six days of hauling parts out of Rhea Vance's hoarded warehouse manifest by manifest, six days of her own cracked knuckles and the fitter's boy's small strong hands and Sef standing by with a kettle of coffee and opinions about everything, and the Kessel Bridge stood whole again over the frozen channel, its counterweights balanced, its chains taut as harp strings.

She had told herself she climbed the tower every morning to check the mechanism. She was done lying to herself about small things; she'd spent her courage on large ones.

From the tower platform the whole harbor lay open beneath her like a coin at the bottom of a grey pool. The ice sheet that had caged the town all winter was broken now in a long channel from the mole to open water, black water steaming faintly where the pilot boat had cut it at first light, and there at the berth sat the Atlas, tall and patient, taking on her final souls.

She could see them from here — four small figures on the frost-white mole, walking in a line. Even at this distance she knew the shape of them. The tall man who mended nets too carefully. The girl who walked like she was counting her steps. The boy between them, and beside him the stowaway with the brand-new papers in her coat, walking like someone who had just been told the ground would hold her weight and didn't quite believe it yet.

"Go on, then," Ilsa said to the wind. It took her hat brim and bent it back, and for a moment she let it, leaning into the cold the way she once leaned into Henrik's shoulder at exactly this hour, watching the fishing fleet go out.

The grief was still there. It would always be there. But it had changed shape over the winter, the way ice changes shape — hard and enclosing at first, then cracked, then finally broken into pieces small enough to carry, one in each hand.

She checked the gauge. Steam pressure adequate. Hydraulic reservoir full. The parts Rhea had hoarded sat in their cradles doing honest work at last.

There was one thing left to do, and she intended to do it properly.

* * *

### III.

Sef had been on the quay since before the lamplighters, which for a man of seventy-odd was less devotion than insomnia, but he chose not to correct the impression when the dock boys began arriving and found him already there, mending a net nobody needed mended, because a man's hands wanted something to do while his heart did other work.

They were coming down the mole now. The whole ragged household of them — Dunmore with his collar up and his shoulders finally, *finally*, down where shoulders ought to sit; Marta with her chin up like a duchess though Sef could see she'd been crying; Tomas glued to Noor's side the way a burr sticks to wool; and Noor herself, walking like the pier might be pulled out from under her at any moment, which to be fair it had been, more than once, in every way that mattered.

Sef set down the net.

He had rehearsed nothing. He was an old man who had learned that the important things couldn't be rehearsed, only endured or missed, and he refused to miss this one.

"You," he said as they reached the head of the quay, pointing his needle at Cyrus. "Your clove hitches are still an insult to rope. Practice on the crossing."

"I'll make a study of it," Cyrus said. His voice was rough. Sef pretended not to notice, in the way of old men, which is to say he noticed everything.

Marta hugged him first, quick and fierce, and whispered thank you against his coat in a voice meant for no one, and Sef patted her shoulder twice because twice was what his arms knew how to do. Tomas shook his hand with enormous formality, having been taught knots by this man and understanding, in the way of boys, that a handshake was the knot you tied to people you respected.

Then Sef turned to Noor.

She stood a little apart, as she always did, hands in her pockets, three borders' worth of caution still living in her spine. And Sef felt the old ache open in his chest — the exact size and shape of a boy who had ferried strangers across a border one autumn night twenty-two years gone and never come back — and he let it open, because he had spent a winter learning that the ache was not a wound anymore. It was a door.

"Here." He took her hand and pressed the object into it, closing her fingers over it with both of his own gnarled ones. "It wants oiling monthly and it doesn't lie. Two virtues rare in this world."

Noor opened her hands.

The compass was brass, greened at the seams, worn smooth on the hinge where a thumb had worried it across forty years of charts. His son's compass. The one he had carried home in his pocket instead of burying, because some things you keep and some things you keep carrying until you find out what they were for.

"No," Noor said — but it was the wrong kind of no, the thin kind, and Sef had survived smugglers and magistrates and one very bad winter by knowing the difference between a no and a wish.

"It pointed me north out of this harbor when I was younger than you and twice as stupid," he said. "It pointed my Joren home the night he didn't reach home. It's been sitting in my drawer pointing at nothing for twenty years, and a compass that points at nothing is just a paperweight with pretensions." He folded her fingers shut again. "You crossed three borders on luck and stubbornness. Luck runs out. Stubbornness needs instruments."

Her jaw worked. She had cried in front of no one all winter — he'd watched her take a beating from Rhea's men without shedding a tear — and she clearly intended to hold the line now, and Sef loved her precisely for the attempt.

"What do I—" she started.

"You point true," Sef said. "That's all any of us manage."

She looked down at the compass in her cupped hands, and the needle inside swung once, settled, and held steady on the harbor mouth, and whatever she said next she said too quietly for anyone but the wind, and Sef decided that was between her and the wind.

The bosun's call sounded from the Atlas. Five minutes.

"Off you go, all of you," Sef said gruffly, flapping his needle at them. "Tide won't hold its breath."

Cyrus stopped. Turned back. And for a moment the two men looked at each other across the frost-silvered planks of the quay — the ferryman of fugitives who had quit, and the carpenter who had never been a carpenter, each of them recognizing in the other the same ridiculous, unfashionable thing.

"Sef," Cyrus said. "When this is over — the inquiry, all of it — write to us. We'll want to know how the nets are."

"The nets," Sef said, "will be scandalous without you."

And then, because sentiment is a tide and it comes whether you invite it or no, he gripped the man's forearm hard, once, and let go, and turned away first so that no one would see what his face was doing, and busied himself immediately and violently with a coil of rope.

* * *

### IV.

From the bridge tower, Ilsa saw the gangway come down like a tongue, and she put her hand on the lever.

The Atlas's captain had been notified — a note delivered the previous evening, unsigned, in a woman's handwriting: *At seven bells, watch the bridge.* He probably thought it was a threat. Let him. Half the pleasure of the past week had been watching powerful people try to guess what Ilsa Brandt would do next, when the answer, every time, was simply: the obvious right thing, loudly.

The passengers filed up the gangway. First class, then ticket holders, then — last, at the very end, waved aboard by a first mate whom Ilsa would have hired on the spot — the four of them. She saw the children turn at the rail. She saw the tall man stand there a moment with his hand raised, not waving exactly, just raised, like a man taking an oath he'd already taken.

Seven bells.

Ilsa Brandt pulled the lever.

The Kessel Bridge came alive under her hands. Gears caught, chains paid out singing, and the great double span of the bridge rose slowly into the pale morning sky — the first time it had opened since the first frost killed it in November, the first time it had opened for anyone in a season when opening anything had seemed impossible. The sound rolled across the harbor, iron and triumph, and she saw heads turn along the whole length of the quay, saw the Atlas's officers stare, heard even at this distance the murmur run through the crowd.

The ship's whistle answered. Three long notes, low and formal — a salute, the kind given to naval vessels and dignitaries and, apparently, to a bridge keeper's widow standing alone in a tower with grease on her cuffs.

Ilsa laughed. It came out half a sob, and she let that be too.

*Go well,* she thought at the ship, at the small figures at her rail. *And write.*

* * *

### V.

Corven watched the bridge rise from the foot of the quay, and understood it at once for what it was — not engineering but oratory — and had to press his lips together against a smile that would have looked deranged on a man in his circumstances.

His circumstances: at noon, the provincial marshal's launch would arrive. At one o'clock, give or take, he would be formally suspended. By evening, in custody. Within the month, most likely, tried — and the confession already traveling south would either save him or hang him, depending on how the tribunal weighed thirty years of service against two of cowardice and one of, at last, the opposite. He had made his peace with the arithmetic. It was, he reflected, the first arithmetic of his life that balanced cleanly.

"Magistrate Corven."

He turned. The first mate of the Atlas stood there, cap in hand, wind-reddened and awkward.

"Mr. Okafor sends this," the man said, holding out an envelope. "Asked me to say—" he glanced at the paper as if checking, then evidently decided to paraphrase— "he says you'll find the terms honored. Berths and papers, all four. And he says—" the mate hesitated— "he says a man who burns his career to legitimize a rescue has earned the right to watch the tide come in without looking over his shoulder. Or words to that effect. Sailors mangle prose."

Inside the envelope, a single sheet. Corven read it there on the quay, wind worrying the corners.

*Magistrate — Enclosed is my deposition regarding the night of the lockup escape, stating plainly that you acted under duress from Vance's operation and in the interest of public order throughout. It is the truth as I can swear it, which is, I have found, the only kind worth swearing. If it is of use to you, use it. If it is not, burn it; you've had practice. — C.D., lately of no fixed address*

Corven read it twice. Then he folded it, and put it inside his coat, over his heart, which was a location he had not used for documents in some years and which seemed suddenly the only filing system that mattered.

"Tell Mr. Okafor," he said, and found his voice needed clearing, "tell him the terms were honored on both sides, and that I will testify to the character of his papers before any tribunal in the province."

"He said you'd say something like that," the mate said, and grinned, and ran for the gangway.

The lines came off. The tug nosed the Atlas's bow. Slowly, with all the ponderous grace of a cathedral deciding to move, the ship swung out into the broken channel, her screws churning the black water white, and slid toward the gap in the ice and the grey sea beyond it.

Corven stood at the very edge of the quay — the same spot, he realized, where weeks ago Rhea Vance's men had stood smoking and he had walked past them with his eyes down. He stood there with his eyes up.

At the stern rail, four figures. One of them raised an arm — he couldn't tell which — and though no words could cross that water, he inclined his head, formally, as one magistrate acknowledging another court's verdict.

Then the ship passed the risen bridge, passing literally beneath Ilsa Brandt's lifted spans, and Corven thought: *there's a sentence for a novel, if anyone survives us to write novels.* The Atlas went through the gate of her making and out onto the open sea, and the morning fog took her gradually, stack first, then funnel, then hull, then nothing but wake and gulls.

He turned from the water. Up the hill, the tribunal's offices waited, and the marshal's launch, and the rest of it — inquiry, trial, ruin, possibly worse. He had feared that walk his whole life. He discovered, climbing the quay steps with the letter against his chest, that fear had simply failed to renew its lease.

Behind him the bridge lowered with a long exhalation of steam, closing the road south, opening the road south, doing its honest work.

* * *

### VI.

Six hundred miles of cold ocean away, at the stern rail of the *Atlas*, Noor opened her hand.

The compass sat in her palm, needle swinging, settling, swinging — finding itself each time, always returning to the same fixed truth regardless of what the sea did underneath it. She watched it for a long time. Behind her, Tomas was asleep against Cyrus's coat, and Marta was teaching Cyrus a clapping game badly, and the gulls were keeping pace with the ship as if hired for the occasion.

"Joren," she said quietly, to the compass, to the man she'd never met whose brass and glass now lived in her pocket. "Point true. Got it."

The needle quivered and held.

Ahead, three weeks of grey water, and then a neutral coast, and then whatever name they would invent for themselves at whatever desk demanded one — because Noor had heard them arguing about it in the corridor the second night out, heard Marta insisting on *Vance* purely to scandalize everyone, heard the tall careful man laughing in a way she hadn't heard all winter, and she understood they were not arguing about a name at all. They were arguing about how to build a thing that could not be taken from them.

She put the compass away. She turned to face the bow.

The sea ran out ahead of the ship, endless and unmarked, and for the first time in three borders and one terrible winter and one whole lifetime, Noor found she was not looking over her shoulder to see what followed.

What followed was a wake, closing gently over itself, leaving the water smooth.
