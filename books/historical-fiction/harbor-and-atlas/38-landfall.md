## 38. Landfall

The fog lifted on a Tuesday, and there was land.

Cyrus felt the change before he saw it — the ship's motion altered, the engine note dropped half a tone, and above him on deck came the sound of feet running to the rail, dozens of feet, the whole steerage complement waking at once as if someone had shouted. He climbed the ladder with his coat collar up out of habit and found the morning already claimed: passengers pressed shoulder to shoulder, pointing at a gray-green smear on the horizon that resolved, minute by minute, into hills, then rooftops, then a harbor with cranes standing in it like patient herons.

A neutral port. He said the words to himself twice, testing them, the way he'd tested every word for eleven years. *Neutral* was not a country or a flag; it was an absence — of warrants, of seals, of men who could look at your papers and see through them to the man underneath. He had been living inside absences so long that abundance felt like a trick.

"Uncle." Marta appeared at his elbow, breathless, Tomas behind her with Noor's hand locked in his. All three of them had the same salt-stiffened hair, the same wind-burned cheeks, the same unbearable aliveness. "Is that it? Is that where we get off?"

"That's where we get off."

"It's small," Tomas said, disappointed.

"Harbors always look small from the water," Noor said. She had crossed three borders in her short life and stood at rails like this one more times than any child should. "The town's behind the town. There's always a town behind the town."

Cyrus watched her say it and thought, not for the first time, that she had earned her cynicism the honest way, coin by coin, and that someday — not yet, but someday — she might let herself spend some of it on hope instead.

* * *

They came into the harbor slowly, the Atlas wallowing a little without her full cargo of confidence, and the port revealed itself in layers. A customs house with a green dome. A breakwater with painted stripes gone chalky. Fishing boats, coal barges, a liner twice the Atlas's size flying a flag Cyrus didn't recognize and didn't need to — that was the whole point of neutral flags; they were furniture, they asked nothing of you. On the quay, porters and money-changers and women selling bread out of baskets, and none of them looking at anyone's face, because here faces were not evidence.

He had forgotten what that was like. Or rather, he remembered Verrigan, where for one winter people *had* looked at his face and seen a dockhand, a lodger, an uncle, a man — and the memory of it rose in him now with a sweetness so sharp it was nearly grief.

"You're doing the thing," Marta said.

"What thing?"

"The staring-at-nothing thing. The thinking-too-loud thing." She tugged his sleeve. "Tomas wants to know if we'll have a house."

"We'll have rooms," Cyrus said carefully. "First rooms, then work, then — " he heard himself about to promise, and stopped, because promises made in harbors were cheap. "Then more rooms, probably. Better ones."

"A house," Tomas insisted, "with a door that locks from the inside."

Noor looked at him sideways. "You've been thinking about doors."

"I've been thinking about them my whole life," Tomas said, with the gravity of nine going on ninety, "I just never had one."

* * *

The paperwork began at two o'clock and took the rest of the day.

The immigration hall had once been a customs warehouse; its vaulted ceiling still held iron rings where bales had hung, and the clerks sat at long scarred tables beneath them like men playing an endless, joyless game of cards. The queue moved at the pace of institutional mercy, which is to say barely, but it moved, and no one in it was beaten, and no one was taken away. Cyrus kept cataloguing these facts like a man counting coins he didn't trust.

At last their turn came. The clerk was a woman past middle age with spectacles pushed up into gray hair and ink on three fingers, and she pulled their forms toward her without ceremony.

"Names."

And there it was. The old cold, the old arithmetic, starting up in him like an engine in cold weather. He opened his mouth to give the forged name he'd prepared in the second week at sea — he had drafted four, tested them for plausibility the way he'd once tested codes — and then Marta stepped forward.

"We're choosing a new one," she announced.

The clerk raised her eyes over the forms. "Choosing."

"On the ship we voted." Marta said this with the absolute authority of a child reporting constitutional law. "It's allowed. It says on the form, right there — *name under which the party wishes to be registered.* Wishes. That means we can wish anything."

Cyrus looked at the form. She was right. He had read that line forty times and seen only a field to be filled strategically; she had read it once and seen a door.

"The name is Brandt-Dunmore-Vance," Tomas said, all in one breath, like diving off a pier.

The clerk's pen stopped. "That's three names."

"It's our three names," Marta said. "Brandt is Ilsa — she kept the bridge. Dunmore is us, even if Uncle hates it. And Vance is — " she faltered, just slightly, the first crack in the marble — "Vance is complicated."

"Vance is Rhea Vance?" the clerk said. "The smuggler?"

"Noor says villains should be remembered honestly," Tomas put in, "or else they come back as rumors, and rumors are worse."

Cyrus opened his mouth to explain — to smooth this over, to produce the reasonable forged name, to be unremarkable one last time out of pure muscle memory — and found Noor watching him. Not the children. Him. With that level, unblinking patience she used like a tool.

"You don't have to," she said quietly. "But I think you want to. You keep almost smiling."

He wasn't, quite. But something in his face was trying.

"They'll laugh at it," he told the children. "Clerks. Officials. People who ask your name at hotels. Three surnames hyphenated together — it's absurd. It sounds like a law firm founded by accident."

"Good," said Marta. "Let them laugh. Then they remember us."

The clerk turned the form around and slid it across the table with the pen on top. "Sign it or don't," she said, with the weariness of a woman who had processed a thousand escapes and knew which details mattered and which were simply human beings being strange at her. "The republic doesn't care what you call yourselves. That's rather the point of it."

*Cyrus Dunmore* — he wrote it, and the pen hesitated only at the D, and then did not hesitate at all. Below it, in smaller hands: Marta, Tomas, Noor. Brandt-Dunmore-Vance. Four signatures, one ridiculous stitched-together name.

It looked, he thought, like a net. Knotted from whatever rope came to hand. Ugly, and strong, and made to hold things that mattered.

"There," the clerk said, stamping it. "You exist."

* * *

They existed, as it turned out, in a rented room above a bakery that smelled so aggressively of yeast and cinnamon that Tomas declared it the finest building in the world and refused, for two days, to leave it.

Cyrus went out alone on the third morning, because some habits were load-bearing. He walked the harbor the way he'd once walked Verrigan's — mapping, timing, noting the pawnshops and the shipping offices and the queue outside the consular annex — before catching himself, mid-calculation of sight lines and escape routes, and stopping dead in the middle of a fish market until a porter swore at him.

*No one is coming*, he told himself. *There is nothing left to carry.*

The codes were ash in a stove in Verrigan. The key was in Corven's keeping, dissolving whatever use the ashes might have had. Rhea Vance was a story now, half legend, half cautionary tale, told on both sides of the frozen sea. He was a man with legal papers, three dependents, sixty-one crowns, and a carpenter's hands that were finally, after years of pretending, developing honest calluses.

He bought bread, herring, a bundle of pencils for Marta, and — after a long internal negotiation — a secondhand atlas, cloth-bound, its maps annotated by some earlier dreamer in faded violet ink. He carried it home under his arm like contraband and set it on the table without comment, and the room went silent, and then erupted, and he understood that he had just spent the best money of his life.

They spread it across the floor and lay around it on their stomachs like cartographers dividing a new world.

"This is us." Tomas stabbed a finger at the neutral port. "This is Verrigan — see, if you go straight across, it's not even far, the sea just makes it look far. This is where Noor was born — show them, Noor."

"Later," Noor said, and Cyrus noted it — the place she kept folded away, not hidden exactly, just hers.

Marta traced coastlines with a pencil stub, naming everything, renaming half of it. "When I have a boat," she said, "I'm going everywhere on this page. Everywhere. Even the blank parts."

"Especially the blank parts," Cyrus said, and then heard his sister's laugh in his own voice, so sudden and so clear that he had to stand and go to the window.

Below, the street went about its business. Above, three children bent over the map of the world, arguing happily about distances. Somewhere between those two facts was the life Lena had asked him to build, assembled at last out of nothing — no inheritance, no homeland, no name anyone would recognize — and holding.

* * *

On the fifth evening there was mail. Not letters yet — too soon for letters — but a parcel, forwarded with bureaucratic slowness through three ports, wrapped in sailcloth and tied with net twine in a knot so particular Cyrus laughed out loud before he'd cut the first line.

Inside: a brass compass, old, dented, its needle cage repaired with wire — Sef's son's compass, which Sef had pressed into Noor's hands on the quay at Verrigan while the Atlas warped out. But this was a second package within, and this one held paper. A sheaf of it, in different hands.

The first was Ilsa's, square and unhurried:

*The bridge stands. It took six days once the parts stopped being stolen and started being borrowed — you will appreciate the distinction. The council has made me Keeper of Works, which mostly means I yell at men younger than my late husband and they pretend to mind. The harbor thawed in March. The first ship through paid a toll of one barrel of herrings to the town, by unanimous vote, and we ate them on the mole. Corven presided. He has lost everything except his spine, which appears to have grown back stronger than before. He asks after you in the way men do when they will not write first.*

The second, cramped, precise, unmistakably a lawyer's:

*I am instructed by no one to record the following. The inquiry into my conduct concluded with a censure and my resignation, which I accepted with gratitude, as both were cheaper than prison and more honest than acquittal. I practice conveyancing now. Deeds, leases, boundaries. It turns out that defining where one man's ground ends and another's begins is the only law I was ever good at. Tell the boy the lock on your door is sound, if he still worries about such things. Tell the girl that documents matter less than the hand that signs them, and choose hands accordingly. — E.C.*

The third, on wrapping paper, in a fist like a cleat hitch:

*So. Still alive, are you. Good. Half my crew signed onto honest ships out of sheer spite toward me, and the other half won't stop telling the story of the night the whole dock stood against me, so I have become a fable, which is worse than being a fugitive — at least fugitives can change ports. I write to say only this: the name was well chosen. I laughed for an hour. Rhea Vance, honest citizen of nowhere, patroness of a family of nobodies — there are worse epitaphs. If the sea ever brings you near my new venture (legitimate, more or less, don't tell the children), the drinks are on the house you'll refuse to enter. — R.V.*

And at the bottom of the packet, folded separately, a scrap in Sef's own hand, five words long, which Noor read aloud because it was addressed to all of them and none of them:

*A net holds if knotted true.*

Cyrus sat back. The lamplight flickered over the table — compass, map, pencils, letters from a frozen town that had decided, one ordinary person at a time, to be worth saving. Marta was already writing a reply in her head; you could see the sentences forming. Tomas had fallen asleep sitting up, the atlas open on his chest to a page of islands. Noor hung the compass by its cord around her neck and tucked it under her shirt, over her heart, where it would live from now on.

"Uncle," Marta said, pen poised, "they'll want to know what to call us in the letters. What do we put?"

Cyrus looked at the registration copy pinned to the wall — *Brandt-Dunmore-Vance*, stamped, official, absurd, theirs — and at the three faces waiting on his answer, though it wasn't really his answer anymore, hadn't been since the ice.

"Put the whole thing," he said. "Every letter of it. Let them practice writing it until their hands ache. We're going to be using it a long time."
