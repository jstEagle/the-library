## 39. Letters to Verrigan

*From the notebook of Marta Dunmore, kept in the second drawer of the desk in the house on Ropewalk Lane, neutral port of Saldhaven — spring, 1920*

Cyrus says a letter should be built like a net: every knot tied twice, nothing left to strain against alone. He says this because the first letter I ever wrote him had holes in it big enough to lose a whole secret through, and he has never once let me forget it, though he laughs when he says it now, which is new.

So here is my net, tied twice:

Dear Ilsa —

Tomas says the sea here is the wrong color and I told him the sea is the sea and he said that is exactly what someone would say who had never seen the right sea, which is nonsense, but I am including his nonsense because you liked his last letter better than mine and I know favoritism when I see it.

The house on Ropewalk Lane has a stove that draws properly and a window that looks at the harbor instead of away from it, which Cyrus says is luck and Noor says is because we looked at forty houses before we picked it. Both are true. We have taken a roomer, a widow named Frau Ostmann who pays in bread and gossip in roughly equal measure and reminds me of nobody in Verrigan, which took some getting used to. In Verrigan everyone reminded me of everyone.

Noor has work at the harbor office copying manifests, which she finds funny for reasons she will only explain after dark, and Tomas has started school and lies about it fluently. Cyrus fixes crates again. He says a man should do the thing he is worst at until it stops mattering, and then find another thing. I think he is talking about more than crates.

Tell Sef his compass has not left Noor's pocket since the morning we sailed. Tell him also that she has already dropped it in the harbor once and fished it out with her coat sleeve still on, so it is carrying on the family tradition of nearly drowning.

Write us about the bridge. Tomas checks the post slot four times a day and pretends he doesn't.

Your Marta

* * *

*Verrigan, from Ilsa Brandt to the household on Ropewalk Lane*

Dear children all, because I refuse to rank you,

The bridge went up yesterday at noon for the first time since the freeze, and I will tell you honestly what it was like, because you were owed honesty before you left and there was never time enough to give it.

They ran the gears slow. Half the town stood on the quay to watch, and nobody spoke, which in Verrigan is a kind of music. The counterweights took the strain the way they were always meant to, no shudder, no complaint, and when the span locked into its housing there was a sound like a door closing gently in a far room, and then everyone shouted at once and a man I have known my whole life wept openly onto his herring cart, and I did not, because I was too busy checking the locking pins, which is what he would have wanted.

The parts came from Rhea Vance's seized warehouse, which the inquiry released to me by name — my name, Ilsa Brandt, widow, bridge keeper, on government paper. Corven signed it himself. I made him sign it in front of witnesses and he did not flinch, though his pen pressed hard enough to dent the desk. There is more iron in that man than the bridge has, and most of it rusted through before this winter. Some of it, I believe, has been replaced.

Sef came home Tuesday. Do not ask me how; ask him, when you write, and enjoy whatever he answers, because it will be a lie shaped like the truth, the way his jokes always are. He walked past the lockup on his way to the chandlery and tipped his hat to the empty cell window. I am told this is called style.

Rhea Vance is gone. Her ship left the harbor on an ebb tide with a skeleton crew and no berth waiting anywhere that will have her, and the word that comes back from the coastwise captains is that her name travels ahead of her like weather. She took the sea, which was the only country left to her, and the sea does not write letters.

The harbor ice broke in March with a crack they heard in the next valley. It sounded, Sef said, like the winter clearing its throat before it finally said something worth hearing.

I am keeping your room aired, Marta, though I know you are not coming back to it. Some rooms you air anyway. That is most of what I know about grief and all of what I know about hope.

Yours,
Ilsa

* * *

*From Noor, undated, written on harbor office paper with a manifest stamp in the corner because good paper should not go to waste*

Dear Sef,

You asked me to describe the compass working, so here it is, described: it points north. You asked me to describe how it feels to hold it, and that one I have been three drafts on.

It feels like being handed something that was carried by someone who didn't finish carrying it. You told me once your son ferried people across a border and the border crossed him instead, and you gave me the compass because you said instruments should stay in motion or they die in the drawer. So I keep it moving. North, mostly. Sometimes I just hold it and let it argue with itself near the stove, and I think about a boy I never met teaching me directions from inside a brass case.

Here is what I have decided, and you may laugh: I am going to sit the pilot examinations. The harbor master laughed too, until I showed him my charts. Three borders taught me coastlines the way hunger teaches bread. I know water by the sound it makes against different hulls. I know which fog tells the truth and which fog lies. And this office work means I read manifests all day, so I already know where everything in this harbor is going before the ships do.

Marta says a woman pilot is a scandal. Marta says this while sharpening her own pencils to apply for the same thing in five years, so weigh her opinion accordingly.

Cyrus has a new name and wears it like a coat two sizes too large — roomy, warm, not yet his. Give it time. Tomas grew a full inch and attributes it entirely to your knots having strengthened his character. I did not have the heart to tell him it was the bread.

Repair the bridge keeper before she repairs any more bridges. She pretends her hands don't hurt. I know pretending when I see it; I invented half of it.

— Noor

P.S. Your son's compass points true north within two degrees. Whatever else you lost, you kept the part that finds its way.

* * *

*From Sef Brandt, fisherman, to "the whole nest of you," dictated aloud in the chandlery and written down by Ilsa over his objections regarding spelling*

Listen. I will keep this short because paper costs money and Ilsa charges rent on the table.

First: Marta, your net-tying advice worked. The new nets hold. This proves only that even bad advice works if you tie it twice, which is your whole philosophy and probably Cyrus's too, so between you, you've got one entire sound mind.

Second: Tomas. The knot you couldn't master is called a carrick bend and the trick is that you were fighting it. Knots aren't caught, boy, they're persuaded. When you come back — and the town will still be here, we're too stubborn to sink — bring your hands and I'll show you. A man who can mend a net is never entirely without a country.

Third: Noor. Pilot exams. Good. When you pilot your first ship, I'll stand on the mole whether I can see you or not, because standing on moles is what old men are for. My eyes are going but my standing is excellent.

Fourth: Cyrus. I won't use your new name in case this letter gets read by the wrong stove. Here is what I want to say and Ilsa says I have to say it plainly at least once per correspondence, so brace yourself.

You came here invisible. I've been invisible myself; it's a common trade in port towns. But invisibility is a story too — the story of a man who has decided the world can't be trusted with his outline. Somewhere between the net shed and the gangway you stopped telling that story. I watched it happen. It happened the night you let the children be seen with you in daylight. Everything after that was just paperwork.

A man who mends nets beside other men and lets them learn his hands — that man belongs to a harbor, whatever his papers say. Papers float. Hands remember.

Fifth and last: the lockup was cold but the company was quiet, and the magistrate who sprang me apologized so formally I thought he was proposing. Tell him, if you write him, that I accept.

That's all. Ilsa says I've used both sides of the page like a barbarian.

— Sef

* * *

*From Magistrate Corven, written at night, in the hand of a man relearning how to hold a pen without pressing through the paper*

To the household on Ropewalk Lane,

I will begin with the news, because beginning with anything else would be evasion dressed as courtesy, and I have worn that costume long enough.

The inquiry concluded Thursday. I was cleared. You should understand precisely what that sentence is worth, because I do: it is worth nothing, and it cost everything, and those two facts together are the first honest arithmetic of my life.

I was cleared because the manifest Ilsa brought me proved Rhea's operation beyond dispute, and because a magistrate who cooperates fully with an inquiry into his own conduct — who hands over his ledgers, his correspondence, and, yes, the fourth confession his clerk copied without my knowledge and against my explicit instruction, that boy has a future in inconvenient integrity — cannot plausibly be prosecuted as an accomplice to the enterprise he dismantled. The law is a machine for reaching correct outcomes through incorrect reasons. I know the machine better than anyone in this jurisdiction. I fed it carefully.

But I did not want merely to avoid the dock. So I stood in the public session and stated, on the record, that I had failed this harbor for years under pressure I was too weak to refuse, and that the evidence against Captain Vance was admissible despite its provenance because justice does not purify itself by refusing dirty hands, it simply stays dirty and does nothing. The gallery was silent. Then Ilsa, in the front row, nodded once, and I found I could breathe.

I resign my office at the end of the term. I am asked, frequently, what I will do afterward, and I have begun to answer: whatever the harbor needs done that no one honorable wants to do. There is always such work. I have qualifications.

Now the private matters.

To Cyrus, whom I will not name correctly even in my own letters, some habits being load-bearing: you asked me once, across a table in a room that smelled of lamp oil and fear, whether a man could trade his way back to a soul. I told you the exchange rate was ruinous. You proceeded anyway. I have since completed the transaction myself and can report the terms were as advertised: total loss of position, reputation, and comfort, in exchange for the ability to sleep without counting. I got the better end of the bargain, and I suspect you did too. We should form a club. We would be its only shamefully relieved members.

To the children: I have entered your names — your true ones, given under seal and then sealed again — nowhere except my memory, where they are safe as anything I possess, which is now very little and therefore very secure.

One enclosure. The harbor master's office announces, over my signature as a courtesy, that the pilot examinations open to all candidates regardless of sex or origin this autumn. The announcement required eleven drafts and one threat of resignation. It passed. Miss Noor should apply. Tell her the examining board includes one member who owes this town more than he can repay and intends to examine candidates with terrifying fairness as partial payment.

Yours in arrears,
Corven

P.S. Sef's account of my apology being mistaken for a proposal requires correction. I apologize formally, at length, and in writing. It is the only kind of apology I have ever been good at, and the only kind he deserved, and he knows it.

* * *

*From Tomas, age ten and a half, to Sef, in careful handwriting with the carrick bend drawn in the margin, incorrectly*

Dear Sef,

I tried the bend the way you said, persuading instead of fighting, and it worked three times out of ten, which Marta says is bad and Noor says is a start and Cyrus says is the exact success rate of everyone's first week, including his, which I don't believe but appreciated.

School here is fine. I lied to Marta about it in my last letter, which she has definitely figured out because she figures out everything, so officially: school is fine and I hate arithmetic slightly less than before, which everyone says is growth.

I have nightmares less. I am writing this down because you said things shrink when you say them and I have tested it and it's true, the nightmare from the crossing is about half the size it was in Verrigan, and I expect by summer it will fit in a drawer with the compass if I ever get the compass, which Noor says is hers, fair, but I get to hold it sometimes.

Question: when a ship pilots into a strange harbor, how does the pilot know where the deep water is if the charts are old? Cyrus says somebody has to go first and take soundings. I think about that a lot. Somebody always has to go first and take soundings, in harbors and everywhere else. I think our whole winter was people taking turns going first — Ilsa, and Noor, and you, and even the magistrate. Even Marta, though what she did almost sank us. Cyrus says almost-sinking is how crews learn each other's weight.

Tell Ilsa the bridge going up at noon made me cry in the kitchen when we got the letter and I am not ashamed, I have decided.

Your friend,
Tomas

* * *

*The last letter in the drawer that spring, in Cyrus's hand, addressed to no one, unsent, folded around a scrap of burned paper*

I have taken to writing letters I do not send. Corven burns confessions; I compose mail to ghosts. Between us we keep the stationery trades solvent.

This one is to Verrigan entire — the town, not the persons, since the persons get their share above — and it goes like this:

I arrived in you as a lie wearing a carpenter's coat, and you never asked me to prove the coat was mine. That is either carelessness or grace, and after a year among you I have ruled out carelessness. You knew. Ports always know. Sef knew by my hands in a fortnight; Ilsa knew by my silence; Rhea knew by everything; even Corven knew before he summoned me, and chose, for reasons he is still paying for, to let the knowing stay unspoken. A town that can see a man clearly and shelter him anyway — that is not a hiding place. That is a home that hasn't demanded paperwork.

We are settled here. The stove draws, the children grow, Noor studies charts by lamplight with my old hands guiding hers only when she permits it, which is rarely, which is right. Marta keeps this notebook and therefore keeps all of us. I have a name now that three people voted on and a fourth insisted upon, and some mornings I answer to it before I remember it isn't the one I was born with, and those mornings are the best proof I have that identity is a net and not a chain: it holds because many strands bear the weight, and no single strand matters alone.

I thought I spent the winter choosing between the children I promised and the girl who saved me. The truth, which took the whole sea voyage to surface, is that they chose each other first, and chose me, and left me nothing to decide except whether to stop struggling. I stopped. It was the hardest and easiest act of my life, ranking somewhere alongside burning the codes, and I commend both to anyone still carrying a republic in his coat.

The ice is breaking here too. The harbor master posted the season's pilot schedule this morning, and Marta stood in front of it so long I thought she'd rooted. She pointed at a name on the autumn list — the examinations — and didn't say anything, and didn't need to. There is a ship due next month flying colors I recognize from another life, and she has already announced, publicly, at breakfast, with the certainty of a magistrate signing in front of witnesses, that when she is grown she will be the one to bring ships like it safely in.

God help the shipping lanes. God help me. God, whoever is keeping the ledger now that Corven has resigned, help us all — and thank you, Verrigan, from a man whose name you never knew, for knowing him anyway.

* * *

*Final entry, pasted into the notebook by Marta, cut from the Saldhaven maritime gazette, summer 1920*

HARBOR NOTES: The pilot examinations open this autumn to all qualified candidates. Among early applicants: N. ——, formerly of Verrigan, whose references include a harbor master, a bridge keeper, and — in what the board describes as "an unorthodox but compelling testimonial" — a fisherman, who wrote in full: *"She reads water the way her own history reads her. Admit her before some other harbor steals her."*

Applications close at month's end.

In other notes, the steamer *Atlas*, refitted and reflagged, is expected at Saldhaven within the month, her master reportedly asking after a family of four who sailed with her last — as she wishes to log them, by name, among the crew of her first honest crossing.
