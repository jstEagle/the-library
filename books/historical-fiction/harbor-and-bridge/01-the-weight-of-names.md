## 1. The Weight of Names

The wind came off the water the way it always did in October—sideways, salt-heavy, carrying the smell of kelp and cold iron—and Elodie Winterbourne climbed the ninety-one steps of the Greyhaven light with her shoulders bent into it, as she had every evening for nine years.

Nine years since Nathaniel's lungs had filled with sea-spray one winter and never emptied again. Nine years since she had taken up the wick-trimmer and the oil book and the long watch that no one had ever formally given her, because the Crown paid a keeper to tend this light and had not seen fit, in all that time, to notice that the keeper was dead and his widow had simply continued in his place without asking permission or pay. She had written twice to the Trinity Office in London. The first letter went unanswered. The second received a reply so delayed and so brief—a single line acknowledging receipt—that she had framed it, half in bitterness and half in amusement, and hung it inside the pantry door where she saw it each morning when she went for the flour.

*Received with thanks.* Thanks. The lamp burned; the ships lived; thanks enough was the doing of it.

Tonight the glass of the lantern room was fogged at its lower edge, and she wiped it with the wool sleeve of her coat before she knelt at the lamp. The flame stood steady, a foot tall, drinking from the reservoir that held four gallons of sperm oil and cost more per month than Mrs. Quill earned delivering half the babies in the parish. Elodie trimmed the char from the wick with the small brass scissors Nathaniel had bought in Falmouth on their wedding journey, and the light steadied, brightened, threw her shadow enormous and wavering against the whitewash of the tower wall.

She talked to him sometimes up here. Not aloud—not usually—but in the silence behind her teeth, a running commentary: *the wick is wearing unevenly, I think the burner wants cleaning; there is weather coming out of the southeast, my knees knew it before the glass did.* It was not prayer. She had tried prayer after he died and found it like knocking at a door in a house she no longer believed anyone inhabited. But the talking helped. A habit of two voices does not die quickly.

From the gallery she could see all of Greyhaven: the crooked stack of houses climbing down from the church to the harbor, their windows already gold with peat-fire light; the stone bridge across the tidal channel, drowned now at high water, its crossing stones showing only as a broken line of foam; beyond it the marsh and, farther, the dark rise of the inland hills. Forty-one households. Two hundred souls, give or take, counting those who counted themselves nowhere at all. She knew them. That was her other work, the work no requisition had ever mentioned and no office paid for.

She went down the stairs with the lamp glass rattling faintly overhead and let herself into the kitchen of the keeper's cottage, where a pot of barley soup had kept itself warm on the banked fire and her second ledger lay waiting under its oilcloth.

There were, in truth, two books in Elodie Winterbourne's keeping, though only one of them was official. The official one—the harbor register, board-backed, brass-cornered—sat on the shelf above it and recorded what the Admiralty cared about: tonnage, cargo, customs entries, harborage fees, wrecks reported in the proper form with the proper columns. It was a ledger of things.

The other was a ledger of people.

It had begun, she could not now deny, as grief. In the first winter after Nathaniel died, unable to sleep, she had sat by the fire with a cheap stitched copybook and written down the names of everyone who had come to the house with food, with fuel, with silence, with their hats off—everyone who had stood at the graveside in the sleet—and then, because the exercise did nothing to ease the night, she had gone further back. Names of the drowned these thirty years. Names of children christened privately because their mothers could not afford the parson's fee. Names of men who had jumped a press-gang's boat at Greyhaven quay and been hidden in attics until the revenue men gave over searching. Her hand had moved and moved, and somewhere past midnight she understood what she was making: not a memorial but an account. A true one. The kind the parish register pretended to be and was not, because the parish recorded only those who could pay the fee, and the customs house recorded only those it meant to tax or hang, and the Crown recorded only what it owned.

No one recorded the rest. So she would.

The copybook had become two copybooks, then three, sewn together at the spine with waxed thread and covered in sailcloth. She kept them in no order a stranger would recognize—her own system, part chronology, part alphabet, part pure association—and she doubted any historian alive could make sense of it in under a year, which suited her fine.

* * *

The knock came just after nine, three soft raps and then, immediately, a fourth—Mrs. Quill's knock, unchanged in forty years, impatient even when there was no emergency. Elodie opened the door on the midwife's round face streaming with sea-fog, her basket over one arm.

"Wreck," Mrs. Quill said, before Elodie could speak. "The *Merry Alice*, on the Teeth. Tam brought word up through the marsh—he's below at the quay with the boy's boat, wet through, poor creature, and won't come to the fire till you go down yourself. Three fishermen out of Polperro. Two got off on the rocks. One didn't."

"Who?"

"Old Pentreath's boy. The young one. Tomas."

Elodie closed the door behind her without locking it. There was nothing in a keeper's cottage worth stealing except the ledgers, and no thief in Greyhaven would touch them—they were better guarded by common consent than if she had kept them behind the church altar.

The walk down to the quay took eleven minutes by the cliff path, and the wind pushed at her back the whole way as though it too wanted the news confirmed. Tam was where Mrs. Quill said, hunched in the lee of the fish-cell wall, his little punt tied beneath him, bailing with a wooden scoop because the boat had shipped water coming around the point in the dark. He was fourteen, perhaps—no one knew precisely, least of all Tam, who had appeared on the tidal bridge one spring morning six years ago, thin as a gull's leg, with no name anyone could learn and no voice he would or could use. The harbormaster had wanted him whipped off the bridge. Elodie had wanted to know if he was hungry. That settled the arrangement of both their lives more thoroughly than any contract: he slept in the net-loft behind her cottage, mended her fences, ferried anything she asked across the channel at any hour, and had not spoken a word in six years. He spoke constantly, all the same—with his hands, his eyebrows, the set of his mouth. Tonight his hands told a story of black water, white rock, and a scream the wind took.

"Two safe," Elodie said. "Young Pentreath and—" Tam held up fingers: *the Kellow man, the one with the bad arm.* "And Tomas Pentreath lost."

Tam's jaw tightened. He nodded once, then reached into his shirt and drew out something wrapped in oilskin, holding it out to her: a boot, sodden, small, the heel stamped with a cobbler's mark. She knew it without being told whose it was. She had logged that cobbler's mark before, in another context, in another life almost—when young Pentreath had come to have his boat's name repainted and stood about in the yard boasting in his father's hearing.

"You carried him?" she asked gently.

Tam shook his head. *Found.* He mimed the wave, the receding water, the still shape on the shingle below the Teeth. Then, with terrible economy, he folded his hands and closed his eyes, and she understood: they had not been able to bring him up tonight. The sea was still working on the rocks; the bodies that could be recovered would be recovered at slack water, toward morning. He had brought the boot instead. Proof. Something for the record.

She put her hand on his wet hair. "You did right," she said. "Now you'll come up to the fire, and you'll eat, and Mrs. Quill will bully you into dry stockings, and none of us will say one word about boats." His look answered—*the pages, though*—and she nodded. "And afterward, yes. Afterward we write it."

* * *

By midnight the cottage was full. Mrs. Quill had commandeered the hearth and was feeding Tam soup in stages, watching him the way she watched women in labor—for the moment things turned. Joss Renner arrived last, letting himself in with the particular knock that was not a knock at all, a fingernail drawn once along the doorframe, because a man in Joss's line of work did not knock anywhere if he could help it. He was a big man gone quietly gray at the temples, with hands like mooring rope and a smile he spent carefully. Behind him came Wren, seven years old and furious with sleepiness, dragging a carved wooden gull by one wing.

"She wouldn't stay abed once Tam went past the window," Joss said, unwinding his scarf. "Girl hears everything in this town before the wind does."

"She gets it from her father, who also hears everything." Elodie poured tea for them both, and Joss's eyes went, as they always did, briefly to the shelf where the sailcloth-covered books sat. Not covetously. Warily. A smuggler looks at records the way a fox looks at a dog's kennel—even empty, even friendly, it smells of teeth.

"Three names tonight," Mrs. Quill said into the quiet. "You'll want them proper."

They gathered, as they always gathered on such nights, around the kitchen table, and Elodie opened the ledger to the current section, and dipped her pen, and waited for the room to give her the truth in order.

"Tomas Pentreath," she wrote. "Aged nineteen years. Second son of Tomas Pentreath senior, fisherman, and Grace Pentreath, born Vosper. Lost on the Teeth, the fishing smack *Merry Alice* of Polperro, in the night of the fourteenth of October, 1747. Body to be recovered at slack water." She looked up. "Was he married? Courting?"

"Courting Tamsin Lanyon, the cooper's girl," Mrs. Quill said. "Not promised. But near enough."

"Then that goes in. Names are debts, Quill—if he courted her, she has a claim on his memory, and someone should know it." She wrote: *He leaves a sweetheart, Tamsin Lanyon, who should be told kindly.* Beside the entry she left a margin space, as she always did, for what might be learned later—a burial date, a child no one yet knew of, a correction. The margins were where the ledger breathed.

"The other two?"

"Silas Kellow, forty and more, wife living, the bad arm from a winch three years back—I set it myself, he owes me for the splint and has never once mentioned it, which is his way of paying, some men's gratitude lives entirely in their silences." Mrs. Quill sniffed. "And William Pentreath, the elder boy, twenty-six, wife Sarah, two children, a girl and a baby."

Elodie wrote them all, dates of birth where she knew them—she knew nearly all of them, because Mrs. Quill remembered every birth she had attended back to the reign of the old queen, and had a memory for dates that put the parish clerk to shame—debts, dents in boats, quarrels reconciled, the whole unprofitable tissue of lives that official paper found too small to hold.

Wren had climbed onto the bench beside Elodie sometime during the writing and sat with her chin on her fists, following the pen. She was learning her letters from that ledger—Joss had grumbled about it once, *not that book, Ellie, teach her off the Bible like a Christian*, and Elodie had answered that the Bible did not contain the name of Wren's mother, and this book did, and a girl should learn to read first in the place where she herself was written. Joss had not raised the objection again.

"Read me mine," Wren said now, the way other children asked for stories.

Elodie turned back two sections, to a page dated eight years past, and read: "*Margery Renner, called Meg, born Marazion, daughter of a tin-miner, died of childbed fever on the ninth of June, 1740, in the house of Mrs. Quill, midwife, having lived honestly and loved well and been married according to the custom of the coast, though no church holds the record of it. She leaves a husband, Joss Renner, and a daughter, Wren, born the twenty-ninth of May, 1740.*" She paused at the line beneath, written in younger ink, in a harder hand—her own hand, the year she had begun to understand what the book was for. "*Let it be noted that this marriage was true. Witnessed here so that no priest nor clerk nor officer may hereafter say otherwise.*"

"Every time," Joss murmured. He was looking into his tea. "You say it the same every time."

"That's the point of a record, Joss. It says the same thing every time. That's how it beats the people who want it to say something else."

He glanced up sharply at that—at the words themselves, perhaps, or at something underneath them—and Mrs. Quill, who missed nothing that happened in any room containing other people, set down her cup with deliberate care. But Wren had already claimed the next request, and the moment folded itself away like a letter into a pocket.

"Read the wreck ones," Wren said. "The old ones."

So Elodie read: the brig *Providence*, lost with eleven hands in the great gale of '31; the unnamed foreign sailor washed ashore in '36 whom the town buried anyway and whom she had entered as *a stranger, given to us by the sea, whom we made our own for the length of a grave*; the press-gang deserters hidden in the salt cellar at the Angel Inn, listed only by description and kindness (*one lame, one red-haired, both frightened, fed by Mrs. Trink at her own cost*). Tam leaned in for all of it, though he had heard it a dozen times, his dark eyes moving from her mouth to the page and back, learning letters by firelight the same as Wren, in his own silent fashion.

Outside, the wind rose a degree and settled, rose and settled, like something breathing in its sleep. The lantern above turned and flashed, turned and flashed, faithful as a heartbeat, and the light swept the water and found nothing yet—but Elodie, pausing between entries, felt the night press at the window with a weight she could not name. Early storms were one thing. This felt like a season turning over in its bed, deciding which way to lie.

"One more thing tonight," she said, and dipped the pen again, and added at the bottom of the page, in the plain column she reserved for arrivals: "*Also entered, this fourteenth of October: a navy cutter sighted at dusk off the eastern head, standing offshore, showing no colors. Out of season by many weeks. Reason unknown. Watched by J. R. and noted here for whatever comes of it.*"

Joss read that upside down, as he read everything, and his careful smile did not appear.

"A bit early for the King's boats," he said lightly, too lightly.

"A bit early for most things the King sends," Elodie said, and blotted the page, and closed the book on three new names and one unease.
