## 2. What the Tide Brings

Wren woke to the sound of the sea wearing a different voice.

She knew the harbor's moods the way other children knew their mothers' footsteps — the flat grey mutter of a working morning, the long silver hush of a calm tide, the snarling argument the water made against the breakwater when autumn came sniffing around. But this morning the sea sounded hungry. She lay in her narrow bed beneath the eaves of her father's house and listened to it chewing at the shingle below the cliff path, and she thought: something has broken.

She was dressed before the thought finished forming. Her father was already gone — his boots' prints in the cold ash of the hearth, a heel dragged where he'd paused, which meant he'd heard it too and gone down quick without waking her, which meant he expected work and didn't want her underfoot, which meant she would follow him anyway. That was the shape of their love: he left, and she followed, and neither of them said so out loud.

The sky over Greyhaven was the color of a bruise going yellow at its edges. Gulls wheeled in ragged spirals above the point, screaming the particular frantic joy they saved for wrecks. Wren ran the cliff path with her skirts knotted up in one fist, and when she crested the rise above the cove she saw it: a fishing smack, half-swallowed by the shingle, lying on its beam like a dead animal with its belly torn open. Its mast leaned at a drunken angle. Nets trailed from it into the surf like drowned hair.

And there, small against the wreck's black hull, stood Mrs. Elodie Winterbourne with her lantern still lit in broad daylight, as if the light itself refused to believe the night was over.

* * *

"You shouldn't be here alone," Wren said, sliding the last few feet of loose shingle to reach her. The stones shifted and sang under their feet, that strange wet groan the beach made when the sea rearranged it in the night.

"I'm not alone anymore." Mrs. Winterbourne did not turn. She was watching the wreck with an expression Wren had never quite learned to read — not grief exactly, and not fear, but something with the posture of both. "Three men out of Harrow Point. They ran for the harbor mouth in the dark and misjudged the channel." She said it plainly, the way she wrote things down, and Wren understood suddenly that saying-it-plainly was how Mrs. Winterbourne kept herself upright. "Two made shore on the point. The third—" She stopped. Adjusted her grip on the lantern. "Tam is up at the light house signaling the salvage crew. Go and help him bring the lines down."

Wren did not go. "Is he drowned?"

"The sea will tell us," Mrs. Winterbourne said. "It tells me everything eventually. That's most of what I do, Wren — I write down what the sea tells me." Then she looked at the girl properly for the first time, and something in her face softened by one degree, which from Mrs. Winterbourne was a great warmth. "But you can start on the cargo before the next tide takes it. There'll be barrels in the hull. Anything with a maker's mark we log; anything else the town may share."

They worked as the morning brightened from bruise-yellow to pale washed blue. Tam came down the cliff path with coils of line over his shoulder, moving quick and sure-footed as a gull himself, and though he never spoke — Wren had known him all her life and had stopped thinking of his silence as a lack years ago — his dark eyes spoke plenty. He looked at the wreck, then at the sea, then spat carefully into the foam, which Wren took to mean the third fisherman was still unaccounted for and Tam did not like the odds.

By midmorning they had the hull cracked open like a nut. Barrels came up slick and gleaming: salt pork, tar, a cask of nails, and — Wren found this one herself, hauling at the rope while Tam steadied it — a small keg that sloshed with a sweetness she could smell through the wood.

"Brandy," Mrs. Winterbourne said, when Wren cracked the bung and breathed it in. "French." She was quiet a moment. "Your father will want to know about this one before anyone else does."

Wren grinned. Everyone in Greyhaven knew what Joss Renner did, and no one in Greyhaven ever said so, least of all to Mrs. Winterbourne, who kept the harbor records and was therefore officially blind to certain tides. But Mrs. Winterbourne only wrote *one keg brandy, French, condition poor* in her little book with its stiff covers, and there was — Wren was almost sure — the smallest twitch at the corner of her mouth.

Then a shout came down from the point, thin across the water, and a man's shape appeared on the rocks waving both arms, and Tam dropped everything and ran, and Mrs. Winterbourne closed her eyes for one breath and said, quietly, as if filing it away even before it was confirmed: *"Third man alive."*

Later Wren would remember how she'd written it first, and the man second. As if the record came before the world.

* * *

The rain drove them up to the lighthouse in the afternoon, and that was how Wren came to sit at Mrs. Winterbourne's kitchen table with a wreck's salt drying white on her hands, learning to read.

It had started the way it always started now. Wren could pick out letters well enough — her father had taught her the shapes of them, grudgingly, by carving them into driftwood with his knife during slow watches — but letters were just animals in a pen until someone showed you how they ran together. And Mrs. Winterbourne, who owned perhaps eleven books in the world and kept every one wrapped in oilcloth, had discovered two winters ago that the girl who turned up at her door in every weather had a hunger in her that books alone could not feed fast enough.

"Here." Mrs. Winterbourne laid the ledger open on the table between the bread board and the lamp. Not the great private book she kept locked in the drawer beneath — Wren had seen that one only once, glimpsed its dense pages before the lid came down — but the working register, the day-book of arrivals and cargo and weather. "Read me yesterday."

Wren bent over the page. The ink was brown-black, the hand small and upright and tireless, row after row of it, and she felt the old familiar vertigo — all those words standing so still and meaning so much, like a flock frozen mid-flight.

"*On* the ninth day of October," she began slowly, tracing with one finger, "the *Smack* Grace of Harrow Point, three men aboard, came to grief upon the west shingle at the turning of the tide—"

"Good. What does *grief* begin with?"

"G."

"And end?"

"*F.*" Wren frowned at it. "No — *f* has no tail here. It's..." She looked harder. "*H.*"

"*Ea-f*. You know this word. Your father uses it often enough about you."

"*Grief,*" Wren said again, tasting it whole now instead of letter by letter, and something opened in her chest, the way it always did, the moment the animals escaped their pen and became a running thing. "Mrs. Winterbourne — why do you write all of it? Papa says the Crown keeps its own registers at the customs house, and the parish keeps its rolls, and nobody needs another."

The rain ticked against the glass. Downstairs, in the round stone throat of the tower, Tam was oiling the lamp mechanism, and his cloth made a soft rhythmic whisper, like the sea far off.

"Because," Mrs. Winterbourne said, "registers are written by men who are paid to write them, and rolls are written by parsons who baptize only what pleases them, and neither of them writes down what happens to a smack called the *Grace* at four in the morning when no one official is awake to see it." She turned the page back, back, back, weeks and months of entries flowing under Wren's eyes like water under a bridge. "I keep this because the sea forgets nothing and tells everyone, and somebody must be listening when it does."

"Read me one," Wren begged. "From the beginning. From before I was born."

Mrs. Winterbourne was quiet so long that Wren feared she'd asked wrong. Then the older woman drew the lamp closer, and her finger came down on a page near the front, and she read in a voice like the recitation of a psalm:

"*Midsummer, 1731. The lugger Kestrel, Renner master, cleared the harbor at dawn with a fair wind. Aboard besides the master: his wife, Marion, big with child and determined to sail the last leg home regardless, the master being unable to persuade her otherwise. Wind freshened in the afternoon. Child born aboard off the Mouse Light, delivered by the mother herself with the master holding her steady against the rail. A girl. Seven pounds by Mrs. Quill's guess when they made harbor. They will call her Wren, for the size of her and the noise she made.*"

Wren sat perfectly still.

"That's not in any parish roll," Mrs. Winterbourne said gently. "Marion Renner was born on the mainland and never churched here; the parson wanted a fee paid in coin your father didn't hold. So the only place in the world where your birth is written down, Wren Renner, is in my book. Yours, and your mother's, and the exact hour of it — the sixth bell of the afternoon watch, which your mother swore by and no one dared dispute."

"She read it aloud twice more, silently, following with her finger, and each time the words did the same impossible thing: they made her mother present. Not the mother in the stories her father told in fragments, not the absence shaped like a woman at the edge of every memory — but a person with a name, big with child, refusing to go below, laughing probably, swearing certainly, arriving in ink at the sixth bell of the afternoon watch. Wren had existed for eleven years without knowing she had been *written*. Now she could point to the place.

"Can I copy it?" she whispered. "Just mine?"

"When your letters run smoother." Mrs. Winterbourne closed the book, but softly, the way one covers a sleeping thing. "Every name in here, Wren — every one — I wrote because someone might someday need proof they were alive. Debtors and deserters and drowned men and girls born on luggers. Paper is how the living testify for each other. Remember that."

Wren remembered it the way children remember prophecies: word for word, meaning for later.

* * *

The storm passed off in the night, and the next day came in clean and gold, the whole harbor rinsed and glittering, the *Grace*'s crew alive and drinking their rescue at the Anchor with the third man telling lies about his own courage. Wren spent the morning carrying split fish to the flakes and the afternoon on the beach below the light, hunting whatever else the wreck had given up — a ship's bell, half-buried; a boot; a coil of rope worth saving.

She was kneeling in the wet sand, prying at something that might have been a chest strap or might have been kelp, when the shadow fell across her.

Not a cloud's shadow. Tam's. He stood over her pointing out to sea, and he was not making his usual quick signs. His arm was rigid. His eyes were very wide.

Wren got up and looked.

A sail, far out past the point, standing in toward the coast with the patient certainty of a thing that owns the water it floats on. Even at that distance Wren knew it at once for what it was — her father had taught her rigs the way other fathers taught psalms — and knowing, she felt the first cold thread of unease go through the warm gold day.

A cutter. Square-topsailed, flush-decked, lean as a knife. Navy blue at the waterline where the light caught her.

"Navy," Wren said aloud, and heard how small her voice was. "But it's not the season. The revenue boats don't come round till spring — Papa said — he *said*—"

Tam's hand closed on her shoulder. He pointed again, more urgently, and now Wren saw what he meant: the cutter was not standing off to patrol, wasn't making her lazy circuit of the coast. She had her yards braced round and her head aimed straight at Greyhaven harbor like a finger pointing at a name.

Together they ran for the cliff path. Halfway up Wren stopped once and looked back, because the light was still golden and the gulls were still wheeling and everything looked exactly as it always had, and it seemed important somehow to fix that ordinary world in place before anything changed it.

At the top of the path, outside the lighthouse door, Mrs. Winterbourne stood with a spyglass to her eye, absolutely motionless, her ledger tucked under her arm. Wren reached her breathless and grabbed her sleeve.

"What is it? Who is it?"

"A cutter," Mrs. Winterbourne said. "Navy. Six guns, if she carries her full fit." She did not lower the glass. "Coming in with the flood tide."

"Is it Papa? Did someone—"

"It isn't your father they're sailing for, child. Cutters don't chase smugglers in flotillas." At last she lowered the spyglass, and Wren saw a thing she had never seen in Mrs. Winterbourne's face before: it had gone careful, like a page smoothed flat, every feeling pressed down hard beneath a surface held deliberately level.

"They're flying requisition colors," Mrs. Winterbourne said, half to Wren, half to the sea, half perhaps to the book under her arm. "That pennant means the Crown wants something. And the Crown wanting something in October, a full season early—" She stopped. Put her hand, briefly and lightly, on top of Wren's head, as if sealing something. "Go and find your father, Wren. Run now. Tell him the navy is come to Greyhaven, and tell him Mrs. Winterbourne says he should think carefully about what he keeps in his house."

Wren ran. Behind her, at the top of the cliff, the keeper of the light stood a moment longer watching the cutter grow on the horizon — watching, Wren would understand much later, the way a person watches weather that is going to change everything, and begins, even then, to count what can be carried.
