## 3. Orders in a Sealed Hand

The packet came on the Tuesday tide, riding in the pocket of a navy courier who smelled of tar and contempt. Elodie saw him first from the lantern gallery—a single figure picking his way along the harbor road where the cobbles gave way to shingle, his boots too fine for the ground they crossed. By the time he reached the foot of the light tower she had come down the ninety-one steps and opened the door before he could knock, which seemed to displease him.

"Elodie Winterbourne?" He said her name as though reading it from an inventory.

"Keeper of the light," she said. "And what else depends on who's asking."

He did not smile. He drew from his coat a document in a heavy envelope, sealed with a wafer of red wax stamped with the royal arms, and held it out at arm's length as if it might be unclean. "For the harbormaster, the customs officer, the parish clerk, and—by direction—the keeper of the harbor light at Greyhaven. You are named."

"I'm named." She took it. The paper was heavier than any letter she had ever held, weighted like something drowned. "Named in what?"

"You'll want to read it sitting down, madam, I'm told." Now he almost smiled. "I've four more deliveries on this coast before dark." And he turned and went back the way he had come, his boots striking the shingle with the crisp, unhurried rhythm of a man whose business was finished the moment it was spoken.

Elodie stood in the doorway a long moment with the envelope in both hands. The wind came off the water carrying the smell of the ebb tide—that brown, mineral breath of exposed weed—and beneath it the ever-present pitch-and-salt of her own walls. Above her the lamp room sat dark against the pale morning, waiting for evening as it always waited, patient as a dog.

She broke the seal inside, at her table, with Tam perched on the opposite stool watching her hands. The boy never spoke—he had not spoken in the six years since she'd pulled him half-drowned from the shallows below the tower—but he had a hundred ways of making himself understood, and just now his stillness was one of them. He knew sealed documents. He knew what wax meant. He had watched enough letters arrive at this table over the years to understand that some of them changed weather.

*By order of His Majesty's Commissioners for the Admiralty,* the document began, in a hand so regular it might have been printed, *the harbor and town of Greyhaven are hereby designated a port of supply and victualing for the term of the present war...*

She read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, moving her lips over certain phrases the way one probes a sore tooth. *All registers, rolls, manifests, ledgers, and records of what kind soever pertaining to the traffic, tonnage, persons, debts, and dwellings of the said harbor and town* — there it was, the phrase that made the room go cold around her, though the fire was lit and the kettle ticking on the hob — *shall be surrendered to the Commissioner appointed for the purpose within thirty days of the date hereof, upon pain of forfeiture and such further penalties as the law provides.*

Thirty days.

Tam leaned across the table and touched the paper, once, with one finger, as if testing whether it was wet.

"I know," Elodie said. "I know."

She put the kettle on properly and made tea she did not drink. Outside, the harbor lay glittering and ordinary under a high scud of cloud; the fishing fleet was out beyond the point; Mrs. Quill's washing hung like signal flags between the cottages on the strand. Nothing in Greyhaven's face had changed. Only the paper on her table had changed everything underneath, the way a stone dropped in still water changes the whole pond without troubling the surface for more than a moment.

All records. All records of what kind soever.

She thought of Joss Renner's name in those pages—not once but dozens of times, entered in her own careful script: cargo run, debt paid, debt owed, daughter born, wife buried. She thought of Wren, who had no other record of her mother in all the world than three lines in Elodie's ledger. She thought of the drowned crew she had logged only last week, men whose parish had never heard their names, whose existence now lived entirely in her hand on her page in her tower.

And somewhere out on that grey water, sooner than any season warranted, a cutter was coming to collect them all.

* * *

The harbormaster came up to the light at dusk, which he rarely did; Aldous Pettibone was a man who believed people should come to him, and usually did. He arrived breathing hard from the stairs, his official chain of office glinting—worn, Elodie suspected, precisely for occasions like this, armor disguised as dignity.

"Well then," he said, settling onto the stool Tam vacated without being asked. "You've read it."

"I've read it."

"And?"

"And it says what it says." She poured him tea because refusing hospitality to Aldous Pettibone was a war she didn't have the ammunition for. "Thirty days."

"Twenty-nine now. The courier lost a day at Marsh End." He wrapped both hands around the cup though the tea was hot enough to scald, and she saw that beneath his pomposity the man was frightened. That surprised her. Pettibone frightened was a thing she had not seen since the great storm of '39. "Elodie. Listen to me. I've been in correspondence with the port authority at Kingsbridge since the rumors started in the spring. This is not a negotiation. The Crown wants a supply port on this coast, and Greyhaven is the only anchorage between here and Wexford Deep that will take a transport in all weathers. They will have it. And they will have every scrap of paper in this town that says who owes whom what, because you cannot victual a navy out of a town full of private debts and smugglers' arrangements and—" he waved a hand vaguely, "—whatever else you people keep written down in corners."

"*You people keep written down in corners.*"

"You know very well what I mean." He drank, wincing. "The requisition names me. It names the customs officer. It names the parish clerk. And it names you, Winterbourne, by title and by name, which means somebody—somewhere—has heard that the keeper of Greyhaven light keeps accounts of her own."

The fire popped. On the shelf above it, the working ledger sat in its usual place, spine worn pale where her thumb rode it daily, fat with six years of entries and twenty before that in her husband's hand. She did not look at it. She looked at Pettibone instead, and found him looking at it too.

"Compliance is the whole of wisdom here," he said quietly. "I'll say this once, woman to official, and then never again: give them everything. Everything, promptly, in good order, with a list of the list. Men who hide papers from the Crown end up watching those papers burn in a square while they stand in the stocks, and I am too old for the stocks, and you are too useful to waste."

"Useful."

"The light is useful. The town trusts the light. When the garrison comes—and it is coming, Elodie, ships and soldiers and God knows what clerks behind them—this town will need someone it trusts standing somewhere it can see." He set down the cup and rose, smoothing his coat, the chain catching the lamplight. "Be that someone. Not a martyr. A keeper."

He let himself out. She heard him descend the stairs slowly, counting them under his breath the way old men do, and the door boomed shut behind him, and the house settled into its familiar sounds—the sea, the beam turning above, Tam's careful feet crossing to the window to watch the harbormaster's lantern dwindle down the harbor road.

Elodie sat alone at the table with the requisition spread before her and the two ledgers on their shelf, and for a long time she did nothing at all.

Then she took the working ledger down, and the older one beneath it—her husband's, its boards stained with salt, its entries in a hand firmer than hers would ever be—and set them side by side, and turned to the first blank page at the back of her own, and dipped her pen.

Not to write a name. To write a question.

*What can be carried?*

She looked at the words a long while. Then she blew out her reading candle, because some sums were better done in the dark.

* * *

She did not sleep. This was not unusual—keeper's nights were broken things by trade, her sleep taken in slices between trimmings of the lamp—but tonight the waking had a different texture. She lay in the narrow bed beneath the sloped ceiling of her room and listened to the beam sweep overhead, that slow heartbeat of glass and flame, and in the intervals of its turning she counted.

It was what she did. Her whole life, she realized, lying there in the dark, had been an act of counting. Tides counted. Hours counted. Names counted—entered, weighed, given their due portion of ink. Her mother had taught her figures; her husband had taught her that a number without a name attached was a cruelty; the sea had taught her that everything unrecorded was lost twice, once to the water and once to forgetting. The ledger was not a book. It had never been a book. It was the difference between those fishermen logged last week existing and not existing, between Wren's mother having lived and having been nobody, between Joss Renner being a man with a history or merely a body at the end of a rope with a charge sheet.

*All registers, rolls, manifests, ledgers, and records of what kind soever.*

She got up, wrapped her shawl, and went down to the kitchen, where the banked fire gave a low red light sufficient for thinking. She stood at the table and laid out the problem the way she laid out anything: piece by piece, in order, counting.

What could be carried?

Her mind went first, absurdly, to practicalities. The working ledger weighed—as near as she could judge by hefting it in memory—some four pounds. The old one, three. Wrapped in oilcloth, sewn tight, they would survive a wet crossing. Tam could carry them in the boat; Tam could carry anything, that boy moved through the world like water deciding its own course. There was the sea cave below the eastern cliff—she and Joss had stowed goods there in lean years, though neither of them had ever said aloud that this was what they were doing. Dry at low water except in the highest springs. Reachable at night. Known to exactly three living souls, herself included.

But carried where? To whom? A record hidden was a record hostage. If she died—and keepers did die, fell on stairs, drowned in tenders, simply stopped one winter night between trimmings—who would know to look? Who among the people in those pages could read well enough to use what they found? Wren, she thought, and stopped, and stood very still by the dead fire.

Wren, who was learning her letters from those very pages, tracing the names with a grimy finger while Elodie sounded them out. *Joss Renner. Smuggler. Honest at heart, which is worth noting.* The girl read those lines like scripture, because to her they were—they were her father vindicated, her mother resurrected, herself made permanent in ink. If the Crown seized that book, it seized the only church Wren Renner had ever entered.

There was another answer, quieter, forming at the bottom of her mind like sediment settling. If a thing existed in only one place, it existed nowhere. She had said as much to young sailors about charts. One chart, one error, one rock. But a coast described in two pilot books, kept by two hands—

A copy.

The word arrived in her head and sat there, plain and enormous. A copy, made by candlelight, page by page, night by night, in twenty-nine days. Her eyes were forty-four years old and had begun their long surrender; the working ledger ran past two hundred pages, dense as a herring shoal. It could not be done alone. It would need hands she trusted absolutely, memories besides her own to check against, a place to work unseen, a way to move pages that no clerk of the Crown would think to search.

Mrs. Quill, who remembered every birth and burial on this coast for forty years because midwives cannot afford to forget. Tam, who could carry anything anywhere without a word that could be overheard. And Joss—Joss, whose neck the seized papers might very literally shorten, and who had spent his whole life proving that the Crown's records and the truth were different animals.

It was conspiracy. She heard Pettibone's voice saying *martyr* and pushed it away. It was also, she thought, laying her palm flat on the table where the ledgers had lain, simply keeping the light. That was all the ledger had ever been: the lamp turned inward, burning over the harbor's people instead of its waters, so that no one need be lost in the dark for lack of someone watching.

Above her head the beam swept round, and round again. She counted its turns the way she had counted everything else, and found that she had reached no decision at all, only arrived at the beginning of one—a small hard seed of resolve planted somewhere below thought, already rooting.

Twenty-nine days. She would need every one of them, and every friend the light had ever earned her.

Through the kitchen window, far down the harbor where the water met the dark, she saw a movement that was not movement of tide or bird—a small boat sliding out from behind the breakwater, running dark and swift toward the open sea. Joss, going out on business he would never name to her. Or perhaps not Joss. Perhaps, already, the beginning of the season's first strange traffic, the town's quiet life stirring ahead of the Crown's loud arrival.

She watched until the darkness closed over the water, and climbed back to bed, and lay listening to the beam turn.

Out on the horizon she could not see, past the point and past the deep-water grounds, a cutter was surely making its way up the coast with its orders in a sealed hand and its commissioner aboard—someone paid to write down what Greyhaven was, officially, forever, as the Crown wished it remembered.

Let him come, she thought, surprising herself with the steadiness of it. Let him bring his fine regular hand. He would find a keeper at her post.

He would find, whatever else he found, that this town had been writing itself down for years—in a hand he had not yet learned to read.
