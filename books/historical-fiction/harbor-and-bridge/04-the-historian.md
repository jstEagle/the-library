## 4. The Historian

Mr. Aldous Vane had never in his life been seasick, a fact he regarded as one of the minor proofs of his own superiority. The cutter *Swiftsure* came about in the grey morning with her canvas cracking and her rails weeping salt, and Vane stood at the bow with his greatcoat buttoned to the throat and his ledger-case strapped across his chest like a breastplate, watching Greyhaven assemble itself out of the fog: first the black spine of the breakwater, then the crooked roofs climbing their hill like barnacles on a hull, and last of all the lighthouse, white as a held breath against a colourless sky.

He took out his pocketbook and wrote, in a hand so small and regular it might have been printed: *Arrived 14th September, 1747. First sight of harbour — light visible through haze at approx. three miles. Town appears poor, defensible, useful.* He considered the last word. *Useful* was what the Crown thought of Greyhaven; he had read the requisition four times on the crossing and could recite its clauses the way other men recited psalms. But a historian's business, he reminded himself, was not merely to record what the powerful thought. It was to make the record so complete that nothing escaped it.

Nothing. That was the whole of his ambition, and he did not blush at it. Other men wanted estates, commissions, the ear of ministers. Vane wanted the past itself, collated, indexed, and bound in calfskin with his name stamped on the spine. He was thirty-four years old and had spent eleven of them in the archives of the Admiralty and the Home Office, learning that history was not written by victors but by the men who arrived afterward with clean paper and patience. The war had made a hundred small harbours suddenly important, and where importance went, documents followed, and where documents went — Aldous Vane followed them.

"Shoal water off the north mole," the sailing master said beside him, uninvited. "Tide's half out. You'll want to watch your boots on the landing, sir."

Vane watched his boots. He noted the shoal too.

* * *

The harbour at low water smelled of weed and tar and old fish, and beneath these, faintly, of woodsmoke from the houses stacked up the hillside. A customs skiff met them at the stairs, rowed by a boy who did not speak — not would not, Vane judged, watching the shape of him, but *could not*, for when Vane thanked him the boy only looked at him with wide dark eyes and put his hand briefly flat over his own heart, which Vane understood, after a moment's study, to be some local species of acknowledgment. He wrote this down too. *Mute boatboy. Gesture = thanks/greeting? Verify.*

The harbormaster, a broad man named Codd whose waistcoat had surrendered some years ago to his stomach, met him at the quay with the requisition order already unfolded in his fist as though it were a talisman he hoped might ward off whatever the Crown had sent.

"Mr. Vane," said Vane, extending a card he had printed at his own expense. "Historian attached to the Commission for the Supply Ports. I'm to write the account of the occupation — the bringing-in of the garrison, the works, the shipping. All of it."

Codd read the card upside down, which told Vane something about him. "We've had soldiers' letters and a colonel's compliments," Codd said. "First time anyone's sent a man to *write us down*."

"Every town gets written down eventually, Mr. Codd. Some by fire, some by plague, some by ballad-singers. You have the good fortune to be written down accurately." Vane smiled, and it was a genuine smile, which made it worse. "I wonder if you might tell me who keeps the harbor records."

* * *

The keeper of the light came down from the headland herself, and Vane knew her before she reached the bottom step, because Codd had described her and because there are people whom a place cannot be imagined without. Elodie Winterbourne walked like a woman who had measured every path in the town with her own feet: unhurried, upright, her shawl pinned against the wind, her face carrying the particular sunburned composure of someone who spends her nights alone with a flame. She was perhaps forty. Her eyes went to his coat first — to the quality of it — and then to his face, and Vane had the distinct sensation of being entered into an inventory.

"Mrs. Winterbourne," he said, bowing. "Aldous Vane. I've come to write the official history of what is about to happen to your town."

"And here I thought you'd come to help prevent it." She said it mildly, almost kindly, and offered her hand. Her grip was a working grip. "You'll want the shipping lists. Codd keeps those. Or thinks he does."

"I'm told you keep records of your own."

The wind moved between them for a moment, smelling of the open sea.

"Codd says that?" she asked.

"Codd says there is no record in Greyhaven worth the name, which any experienced man knows means precisely the opposite." Vane kept his tone light, conversational, the tone of a man discussing weather or the price of candles. "I've spent eleven years in government archives, Mrs. Winterbourne. I have learned that wherever an official register is badly kept, a private one is beautifully kept. It is practically a law of nature. Where is yours?"

She did not lie to him. He noticed that at once, and it interested him more than any document yet. Most people lied reflexively, badly, and the lies were themselves evidence. Mrs. Winterbourne looked at him with the frank assessment of a lighthousekeeper deciding whether a vessel on her horizon was weather worth noting.

"My records are the harbor's records," she said. "Kept where the work is done. If you want to know what comes into Greyhaven and what goes out, you'd best see how the light is kept, because everything that passes this coast passes under it. Will you climb, Mr. Vane? It's a hundred and six steps."

"A hundred and six," he repeated, delighted. "You've counted."

"I count things," said Elodie Winterbourne. "It's a habit of widows and keepers. Come."

* * *

The stair wound up inside the tower like the interior of a shell, and Vane climbed it counting without meaning to, arriving at the lantern room on the ninety-ninth step and correcting himself aloud. She laughed — a short, real laugh — and said, "There's a false landing halfway. Everyone miscounts. I did for the first year."

The lantern room was immaculate. That was his second observation, and it mattered more than the first, because order of that quality is never accidental. The great lens sat in its frame like the eye of something patient; the brasswork had been polished until it gave back the grey daylight; the oil store was racked and labelled in a hand that was small, upright, and regular — a hand, Vane noted, very like his own. On a desk built into the curve of the wall lay a logbook, open, weighted at the corners with two flat stones from the beach.

"May I?" He did not wait entirely for permission; he had found in life that asking while reaching produced better results than either alone. The logbook was exactly what a harbor log should be — dates, winds, vessels, tonnages, arrivals, wrecks — but it was kept with a thoroughness that embarrassed anything he had seen in the Admiralty's own offices. Every entry signed. Weather recorded twice daily. And in the margins, in the same precise hand, small annotations: *master drunk, cargo short 3 cwt, paid in kind — flour.* A wrecked fishing crew logged eleven days prior, each man named, with the note: *all hands lost except T. Brine, boy, landed at Porthmoor, walking home.*

"You name the dead," Vane said quietly.

"They're easier to carry with names on." She said it without drama, trimming the wick-trims into a dish as she spoke, housekeeping the light even now, in front of a stranger. "A drowned man without a name is a statistic. With a name he's somebody's grief, and grief gets tended. Names are practical, Mr. Vane."

"Names are power," Vane said. "Whoever holds the names holds the past. Whoever holds the past—" he closed the logbook gently, reverently, the way another man might close a bible, "—holds the argument about the future. That is my entire profession in two sentences, and I confess I've never before said it aloud to anyone who seemed likely to understand it."

"Flattery is also practical," she said. "But go on."

He went on. He interviewed her for two hours in the lantern room while the afternoon thickened toward rain, and he filled nine pages of his pocketbook. She knew every coaster by its skipper's habits; she knew which masters watered their crews' beer and which ones short-paid; she knew the tonnage of the *Merrow* to within a quarter-ton and corrected his figure without apology. She spoke of shipping traffic the way a confessor speaks of sinners — specifically, exhaustively, with a certain fond exasperation. Vane had interviewed admirals who knew less about their own squadrons than this woman knew about the fish-boats of a dying village.

And underneath all of it, running like a current beneath a swell, he felt the thing she was not saying. There was more paper in Greyhaven than a logbook. He was sure of it the way he was sure of a forged signature: not by any single feature but by the aggregate. Her memory was too complete. Men and women who keep perfect records in their heads have usually got paper somewhere as backup, because even the finest memory distrusts itself, and Mrs. Winterbourne did not strike him as a woman who trusted anything so fallible as remembrance when ink would do.

"There will be seizures," he said at last, carefully, watching her face. "The requisition requires all registers, rolls, and records surrendered within the month. Parish books, customs papers, harbor logs. Everything written, gathered, inventoried, and shipped to the commission in Portsmouth. My clerks will index it. I will draw on it for the history." He paused. "You should know that I am not the instrument of that order, Mrs. Winterbourne. I am merely the man who arrives after it. But I would rather arrive knowing what was lost."

"Would you?" Something moved behind her eyes — not fear, he thought. Reassessment. The look of a keeper re-rangging a light she had previously aimed elsewhere. "Then here is what will be lost, Mr. Vane, and you may write it in your little book. This town has never once appeared correctly in any document the Crown holds. Not the census, not the customs rolls, not the parish registers. Half the souls here were born outside the church's bookkeeping and live outside the excise's. When your soldiers gather up our papers, they will gather up a fiction, and burn it, and call the ash complete. That is what will be lost. Nothing. Less than nothing."

"And yet," Vane said softly, "you keep such careful logs yourself. For a town that exists nowhere officially, you seem very anxious to fix it in ink."

The rain arrived then, sudden and slanting against the lantern glass, and she turned to check the lamp — not lit yet, but inspected, touched, confirmed — and when she answered him her back was to him and her voice was perfectly level.

"Because someone has to remember us properly," she said, "if only the tide."

Vane wrote nothing for a full minute. Then he wrote: *Keeper E. Winterbourne. Memory: exceptional. Records: exist beyond the log. Location unknown. Sentiment: formidable.* And beneath that, after consideration, in the small regular hand: *Do not underestimate.*

* * *

He dined at the harbormaster's table that evening on mackerel and boiled potatoes, enduring Codd's anecdotes about the militia and the price of coal, and all the while his mind worked the problem the way his fingers, in idle moments, worked a knot. Upstairs in the room lent to him he laid out his papers by candlelight: the requisition, his instructions, the nine pages of notes, and a fresh sheet headed in his neat capitals — *INVENTORY OF GREYHAVEN RECORDS, KNOWN AND SUSPECTED.*

Known: harbor log (Winterbourne). Customs duplicates (office, mostly rotted, per Codd). Parish register (church — soon to be a storehouse, God help the architecture).

Suspected: a private ledger. Contents unknown. Kept by the same hand that kept the log.

He dipped his pen and sat for a long time without writing, listening to the rain and, beneath the rain, the sea working patiently at the breakwater. He thought about her face when she had said *someone has to remember us properly* — the absence of appeal in it, the plainness of it, a sentence spoken not to move him but simply because it was true, the way she stated tonnages. He had met idealists before; they bored him, because they mistook feeling for evidence. This woman was not an idealist. She was a professional. She kept records the way he did — completely, jealously, against the world's talent for loss — and that meant, he realized with a pleasure that startled him, that he had finally met his counterpart.

The Crown would take whatever papers the town surrendered. His clerks would index them. His history would be built on them, and it would be accurate, and praised, and bound, and it would contain, somewhere in its appendix, a list of Greyhaven's registered souls. But if Aldous Vane had learned one thing in eleven years of archives, it was this: the official record is always the second draft. Somewhere in that crooked town, in a tower or a cellar or a seam in a wall, sat the first.

He blew out his candle, and in the dark he smiled — courteously, precisely, at no one.

*Find it,* he thought, and slept well.
