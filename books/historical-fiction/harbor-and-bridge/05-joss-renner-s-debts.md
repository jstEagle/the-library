## 5. Joss Renner's Debts

The *Kestrel* sat low in the water on the last leg home, and Joss Renner sat lower still in the stern, one hand on the tiller, watching Greyhaven's light swing its slow white arm across the dark. There was a comfort in that rhythm he had never found anywhere else—not in his father's boat, not in the king's service he'd fled at nineteen, not in any bed he'd paid for or been given. The light swept, and swept again, and meant the same thing every time: *you are nearly home; someone is awake.*

He did not let himself think of it as Elodie's light. He had learned, over eleven years of running cargo through other men's bays, that a man who names a thing begins to owe it.

And he owed enough already.

The customs seizure had happened three weeks ago at Falmouth—a revenue cutter too quick by half, taking the *Kestrel's* sister vessel with her papers aboard. Not his papers, thank God and the devil both, but papers bearing his name. He'd heard it from a fisherman in Pentruth who'd heard it from a tide-waiter who'd been drunk enough to talk: among the documents carted off to the Collector's office was an old manifest, five years stale, listing one *J. Renner* as master of record for two runs the Crown had never proven but had never forgotten. Free trade, they called it when they were being polite. Smuggling, when they wanted a man's neck.

Hanging was still on the books for it. Not often carried out anymore, the fisherman had said quickly, seeing Joss's face—more often transportation, seven years across the water. As if seven years in a fever-ridden hulk bound for Botany Bay were a mercy a man should weep with gratitude for.

Joss had smiled and bought the man a drink and walked out into the dark with his hands steady and his heart going like a gull in a closed room.

Now the harbor bar passed under his keel, and the light swept overhead, and he steered for the staithe because there was nowhere else in the world to steer for.

* * *

Elodie Winterbourne kept late hours, which was one of the ten thousand things about her that Joss had stopped pretending not to notice somewhere around his thirtieth year. When he came up from the staithe, boots wet to the ankle, her kitchen window still burned yellow against the night, and the door opened before he could knock. She always knew his step. He'd stopped wondering how.

"You look like a man carrying something heavier than his coat," she said, standing back to let him in.

"That's just my charm. It weighs a great deal."

"Sit down, then, and put it on the table where I can see it."

Her kitchen smelled of bread gone warm again in the hearth and lamp oil and the faint salt-tang that lived in everything in Greyhaven, in the grain of the wood, in a body's hair after a month. A pot of tea stood on the hob as though she'd been expecting him, which perhaps she had; word traveled fast along a coast where everyone watched everyone else's sails. The ledger lay open on the dresser—the ledger, the famous one, the town's second scripture—and Joss looked at it the way he supposed a condemned man looks at the Bible they offer him, wanting its comfort and fearing what it might require him to confess.

She poured the tea without asking what he took, because she knew that too, and sat opposite him, hands folded, waiting. That was Elodie's gift. She could wait like the sea waits—no impatience in it, only a patience older than any single thing that moved upon it.

So he told her. All of it, in order, the way you take a wound apart bandage by bandage when you've finally decided it has to be cleaned.

The seized papers. The name on the manifest. The Collector's office at Falmouth, now stuffed with documents that could be read any way the Crown pleased, and the Crown these days pleased itself to read everything the worst way. The requisition notice posted on the church door that same week—Greyhaven claimed as a supply port, all registers and records to be surrendered within the month—which had seemed until tonight like a matter of ink and inconvenience, and now sat in his chest like swallowed anchor.

"They'll cross-reference," he said. "That's what clerks do, isn't it? They sit in rooms with candles and they match one paper to another paper, and where two papers agree, a man hangs." He turned his cup on the table, watching the tea shiver. "My name's in those customs papers now. And I'm willing to bet my name is in your ledger, Elodie Winterbourne, a good forty times over. Landings, dates, tides, what I carried and when I carried it. You wrote it down because that's what you do. Because somebody ought to know a thing truly, even if nobody official ever asks."

She didn't flinch. He hadn't expected her to.

"Yes," she said simply. "You're in it. So are half the able-bodied men of this coast, if the truth were known, and half their fathers before them. I recorded what happened at this harbor, and what happened was you."

"And now what happened at this harbor belongs to the King."

"Now," she said, and her voice was level as a carpenter's rule, "we have not yet surrendered anything."

* * *

There was more. There had been more for years, pressed down under his tongue like a coin saved, and he found, sitting in her warm kitchen with the light sweeping past the window in its patient arc, that the coin had grown too heavy to keep biting down on.

"Wren's mother," he said.

Elodie went very still. Stillness, in her, was not emptiness; he had learned that much. It was attention, total and unblinking.

"Maren," he said, because saying the name aloud was a kind of duty he'd neglected too long. "Maren Oduya. You knew her. You—"

"I delivered her daughter," Elodie said quietly. "On the fourth of March, 1736. A bad night. Ice on the channel, of all things. She held my hand near to breaking and never made a sound until Wren cried out, and then she laughed." Something moved behind Elodie's eyes, old grief wearing the thin garment of memory. "I remember everything about that night. It's written in the ledger. Not the parish register—the ledger."

"That's just it." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "It's written nowhere else. Do you understand me, Elodie? Maren was born in Pentruth to a father who wouldn't pay for a parish entry because he didn't hold with the Established Church, or didn't hold with paying, which amounted to the same creed. She married me on the sly at a dissenting chapel inland—no license, no banns the vicar would recognize, a piece of paper signed by a minister who's dead now and whose chapel burned five years back. She died of childbed fever eight days after Wren came, and the parish register lists her as—" He had to stop. Eleven years, and it could still stop him mid-sentence like a fist. "As *Wren Renner, base-born, mother unnamed.* That's the whole of her, in the eyes of the law and of God as administered from the vestry. One line. Mother unnamed. Like she was weather. Like she was something that happened to me instead of someone who happened in the world."

The fire ticked. Upstairs in the lantern room, faint through the ceiling, the clockwork of the light turned its slow revolutions. Elodie reached across the table and laid her hand over his wrist—not a gesture of passion, nothing so complicated, only a woman's hand on a man's arm, holding him to the earth.

"She was not weather," Elodie said. "I knew her for six years. She could gut and salt a mackerel faster than any man on the staithe, and she sang in a language I never learned, songs her grandmother brought from somewhere over the water, and she taught them to Wren before Wren could walk. She once argued old Dr. Penhale to a standstill over whether a drowning man feels cold before he feels fear, and won, and made him buy her a brandy to concede." Elodie's mouth curved. "I wrote all of that down, Joss. Not the official parts. The true ones. What the ledger says about Maren is that she existed, and that she mattered, and that a girl named Wren Renner came into the world through her on the fourth of March in the year '36, and that her mother's name was Maren Oduya, and that no power on this coast can make that untrue."

"But it can be made unread." He said it flatly, the way you state the draft coming under a door. "That's what I came to ask you, and God forgive me for asking it. These orders on the church door—registers, rolls, records. If your ledger goes into a clerk's crate with the rest, and some clerk with a steady hand copies out the shipping entries and burns or bins the rest as irregular—" He looked up at her, and found he could not dress the question in anything. "Elodie. What does the ledger say about us? About me and my girl? Is there enough of us in it to hang me? Is there enough of Maren in it to keep her from vanishing altogether?"

For a long moment she said nothing, and the light swept the window, and swept again, and he counted the sweeps because counting was the only discipline he had left.

Then she rose, took the lamp from the table, and lifted the ledger down from the dresser.

* * *

She did not read to him. She turned the pages and let him see—that was different, and he understood even in that moment that the difference was the whole point. Reading aloud would have been testimony. This was showing a man his own face.

*J.R., landed brandy and tea, north cove, 12th Sept.* In her neat, upright hand, unashamed. *J.R., brought word of wreck on the Gull Rock, aided salvage, honest in the division—note this, for it is not always so with him.*

"I wrote that last part," she said, "because a record that tells only a man's crimes is a lie of omission. You're a smuggler, Joss. You're also the man who pulled the Tregarrick boys out of the water in '41 when no one else would launch. Both facts go in the book or neither does."

Page after page. Dates and landings, yes, enough to hang him a dozen times if a hanging judge read them. But threaded through, dense as net-mesh: *J.R.'s dghaisa carried Mrs. Quill to the Pentruth fever cases through the ice.* *J.R. gave his winter's profit to the Widmow family when their boat was lost.* *Wren Renner, aged 6, first word read aloud at this light: her own name.*

And there, in the middle of the book, in ink darker than the rest because she had gone over it twice:

*Maren Oduya, wife of Joss Renner though no church holds their bond, died 12th March 1736, of childbed fever, eight days after the birth of her daughter. She sang to the child every day of those eight days, though it cost her breath she could not spare. She asked that the girl be called Wren, for the small brown bird that sings through winter. She was twenty-six years old. She is mourned.*

Joss discovered his eyes were burning and did not trouble to hide it. There was no one in the world whose seeing of it would shame him.

"You kept her," he said, when he could speak. "All this time. You kept her alive in ink."

"I keep everyone," Elodie said. "That is the whole of my religion, such as it is. Names outlive paperwork, Joss. Paper can be seized, burned, drowned, misfiled by a lazy clerk—but only if it exists in one place only." She closed the ledger and rested both hands upon it, and looking at her face in the lamplight, Joss felt the first cold thread of something that was not quite dread and not quite hope. "Yours and Wren's exist in one place only. Mine too, come to that. Tam's. Mrs. Quill's forty years of mothers and babies the parish never bothered to count. We are all of us, on this coast, one crate in a requisition wagon away from never having been."

"What do we do?"

"We do what people have always done when the powerful come to rewrite the story." Her voice stayed gentle, and somehow that gentleness was the most frightening thing in the room. "We make certain the story survives them. But not tonight. Tonight you'll finish your tea, and you'll sleep in the boat shed, and tomorrow—" She stopped. Below them, faint but unmistakable across the sleeping town, came the sound of boots on the harbor road, unhurried, numerous, in step. Neither of them moved. The light swept the window. Somewhere out on the black water, early in the season and earlier in the night, a navy cutter's riding lights hung at anchor like a handful of dropped stars.

"Tomorrow," Elodie finished softly, "I think we had best find out exactly what Mr. Harbormaster signed for."

Joss looked at the ledger under her hands, at the lamp, at the small stubborn warmth of the window against all that dark water, and understood that whatever came ashore next, the quiet years were over.

Outside, the boots drew nearer, and the light swept on, keeping watch.
