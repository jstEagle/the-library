## 6. Copying by Candlelight

The decision came to her not as thunder comes but as tide comes—slowly, and then all at once, until there was no shore left to stand on.

Elodie Winterbourne had lain awake most of the night after the requisition notice arrived, counting what could be carried. Not chests of silver, not the good china from her mother's side, not even the house itself, which she had shared with Nathaniel for eleven years and with his ghost for six more. What could be carried was thinner than all of that. It weighed almost nothing. It was ink.

She rose before dawn, as she always did, and climbed the stair to the lantern room to trim the wick and scrape the soot from the glass while the flame still burned low and blue. The sea below was the color of unpolished pewter, and the wind had a new edge to it, the first true taste of autumn. Storm season was coming. The herring boats would feel it first.

When she came down, she did not go to the kitchen for her bread and tea. She went instead to the narrow closet beneath the stair where Nathaniel had kept his charts, and she knelt on the cold flagstones, and she drew out the ledger.

It was not a grand thing to look at. A mariner's ledger, bound in salt-stiffened leather, its spine held together with tarred twine. Her husband had begun it as a harbor book—tonnage, fees, arrivals—and when he died, she had simply gone on writing in it, and then gone on past what any harbor book ought to hold. Seventeen years of Greyhaven, entered in her small upright hand: every crew that sailed out and came home short, every debtor who settled and every one who never would, every stranger sheltered a night in the lamp room, every birth Mrs. Quill attended that the parish clerk never heard of, every drowning the sea confessed to. Some entries were three lines. One—the foundering of the *Mercy of Whitby*—ran four pages, because she had written down not only the lost but what each man had been carrying in his pockets when they washed ashore, so that someone might know them by more than their number.

She set it on the kitchen table in the full gray light of morning and looked at it for a long time.

Then she fetched the second-best quill, the good ink, and a ream of thin paper she had bought two winters ago against just such an instinct she had not yet understood, and she opened the ledger to the first page, and she began to copy.

*Peter Amsel, master of the ketch Dorcas, owed the harbor fund three shillings at his death, forgiven, his son having served the light faithfully one winter.*

Her hand shook at the first stroke and steadied at the second. That was how grief worked too, she thought. And courage. Everything true arrived shaking and settled only through repetition.

* *

Mrs. Quill came at noon, as she came most days, carrying a basket that smelled of rosemary and, faintly, of the tinctures beneath the rosemary. She was seventy if she was a day, small and dry as driftwood, with eyes that had looked into more beginnings and endings than the parish register would ever admit. She let herself in without knocking, hung her cloak on its peg, and stopped dead when she saw the table.

"Well," she said. "So you've finally gone mad, and sensibly."

"I'm copying it," Elodie said. "All of it. Every name."

Mrs. Quill set down her basket slowly. For once in forty years she did not have something sharp to say at once, and the silence between them filled up with the sound of the sea against the tower.

"The garrison will want this table searched," the old woman said at last.

"They'll search what I surrender. They won't search under my floorboards for paper that doesn't exist—if no one knows it exists." Elodie turned the ledger toward her. "But I can't do it alone, Meg. My memory is good, but yours is older. You were at births my hand never recorded until weeks after. You buried people whose names I only ever got secondhand. I need you to sit with me and remember."

Mrs. Quill lowered herself into the chair opposite, joints cracking like green kindling. She pulled the copied pages close and read them over the tops of her spectacles, lips moving, and Elodie watched forty years pass behind the old woman's eyes.

"Sal Renwick," Mrs. Quill said abruptly. "You've got him down as born in March of '31, because that's what his mother told the harbor folk. He was born in January. In my bed, during the great frost, three weeks early, and I broke ice in the wash basin to bathe him. His mother wanted him counted in March because January was the month her own father died and she'd have bad luck on him otherwise." She tapped the page. "Change it. If a thing is worth risking the rope for, it is worth doing accurately."

Elodie struck the line and rewrote it. Something in her chest unlocked, some door she had been holding shut with her shoulder since the notice came.

They worked until the light failed. Mrs. Quill remembered in a strange order—not chronological but associative, one name pulling another up out of the dark like anchor chains. The midwife would say a name, and Elodie would find it or fail to find it, and when she failed, Mrs. Quill would dictate the entry whole: dates, kin, debts, the little human freight that no official record carried. *Hester Vane—"no relation to any Vane of quality, mind"—died of the lung fever aged nine, mourned by the whole quay, who flew their nets at half-mast though it cost them the day's catch.* Elodie wrote it. Somewhere in the Crown's great archives there was no Hester Vane at all. Now there would be twice.

"Tam," Mrs. Quill said, near dusk. "What will you do about Tam? He can't read enough to know what he's carrying, which may be a mercy, but the boy sees everything. He'll work it out."

"He already has," said a voice from the doorway.

Tam stood there with a bucket of mussels he had plainly forgotten he was holding, looking at the papers with an expression Elodie had learned to read better than speech: wary, then resolved. He set the bucket down, crossed the room, and tapped the ledger twice with one forefinger, then pointed toward the window, toward the headland north of the light.

"The sea cave," Elodie said.

He nodded once, brisk as a harbormaster confirming a berth.

"Can you reach it in weather?"

His answer was to look mildly insulted. Mrs. Quill laughed—a dry rustle like pages turning—and reached up to grip his ear affectionately. "Mind you don't get caught, gull-boy. Soldiers can't question what you won't say, but they can still hurt you trying."

Tam went very still. Then he touched his own chest, flat of his palm, over the heart, and looked at Elodie with such level gravity that she understood he was making a vow in the only language he had, and that the vow was accepted before it finished being made.

* *

The cache was Tam's idea in practice, though the cave had been hers to find years ago, on an afternoon of low spring tides when she had walked the headland grieving and let the sea lead her. It lay under the black rocks north of the tower, mouth half-hidden at high water, and inside it a shelf of dry stone above the spray line where she had once, foolishly, romantically, hidden a birthday gift for Nathaniel he never lived to open. The cave remembered things. Perhaps it could be persuaded to remember this.

Tam ferried the first bundle on the third evening, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with tarred string, timed to the ebb. Elodie watched from the lantern gallery, small and sharp with fear, until his figure appeared again on the causeway path below, unhurried, whistling soundlessly at the gulls. Afterward he showed her the trick he'd devised: the packet slung flat against his chest beneath his coat, tied with a fisherman's loop he could shed into the water in three seconds if hands grabbed him. The pages would drown. The boy would not.

"You are braver than is good for you," she told him, and he shrugged, and stole a heel of bread, and was gone.

* *

The first storm of the season arrived five nights later, and it arrived without manners.

Elodie felt it coming in her teeth all afternoon—a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the swell, the gulls sitting sullen on the water instead of working the tideline. By dusk the horizon had gone the color of a bruise and the wind had swung round twice, and she knew the signs the way she knew her own hands. She lit the lamp early. She doubled the storm shutters. She put soup on to warm, because whoever came off that sea tonight would come to her door cold.

The garrison had other ideas. A corporal and two men came up the cliff path at full dark, hunched and swearing into the rain, hammering at her door with the flat of a sword.

"Lieutenant's compliments," the corporal shouted over the wind. "Every able body on the quay is wanted—cargo boat dragging anchor, they need men on the capstan lines!"

"Send them here and I'll feed them after," Elodie said, "but I don't leave the light. There's a blow coming out of the northeast that hasn't properly started yet, and when it does, every keel between here and Marrow Point will want that lamp."

"The lieutenant's orders—"

"Are your lieutenant's," Elodie said. "This light is mine, and it stays tended. Tell him so."

The corporal stared at her—at this widow woman in oilskins standing square in her own doorway with the wind tearing her words to ribbons—and whatever he saw made him touch his hat and turn back down the path at a run.

The blow came at midnight, exactly as promised.

It came down the chimney first, then across the gallery, then in one long scream off the sea that set the whole tower humming like a struck bell. Elodie fought the lamp through it. The glass panes flexed in their leads; twice she thought the gallery rail would go; spray came over the top of the tower itself, sixty feet above high water, and froze on the lantern roof. When the draft guttered the flame she cupped her own hands around the wick like a woman sheltering a sparrow, and when the oil thickened in the cold she warmed the reservoir with her breath and patience. There was no one to relieve her. There never had been, not really. She had stood this watch alone for six years, and she stood it now, and the light swept out over the black violence of the water, patient and stubborn, saying the only thing she knew how to say: *here, here, here.*

Near three in the morning, feet on the stair. Tam burst into the lantern room streaming seawater, white-eyed, hauling the door shut against the gale. He gripped her arms and pointed down, toward the harbor, and mimed—quick, urgent—a hull plunging, a line snapping, men hauling.

Below, in the fitful lightning, she could see it: a coaster dragging toward the outer rocks, and on the quay a cluster of lanterns, soldiers and fishermen together on the capstan, and beyond them the church tower, and beyond that, far out, a second light where no light should be—a distress flare, red and brief, swallowed by the rain.

Tam took her sleeve and pulled toward the stair. He wanted her on the quay. He wanted everyone, everywhere, doing everything at once—that was Tam in any crisis, the boy who could not speak spending himself in motion instead. But Elodie put both hands on his shoulders and turned him to face the lamp.

"Not me," she shouted over the storm. "Somebody holds the light, Tam, or nobody gets home. Go—go help the lines—but you come back and tell me everything, you hear? Names! Get me names!"

He went. She returned to the glass, and the long night, and the flame.

The coaster came off the rocks sometime before dawn—the capstan lines held, barely, and the sea, having considered the matter, relented. Two men were hurt, none dead. Tam crawled back up the tower at first gray light, soaked through, exhausted, grinning around his silence, and pressed into Elodie's palm a scrap of paper on which he had written, in his careful schoolroom letters, four names: the injured men, so she could enter them.

She sat down right there on the lantern room floor, amid the frozen spray and the smell of burnt oil, and laughed until she cried, because the boy had understood it before she'd taught him. Everyone could keep the ledger. Everyone was the ledger.

That afternoon, when the sky cleared to a rinsed pale blue and the garrison counted its bruises, Elodie copied the four names into the duplicate, verified them against Tam's memory and her own watching, and sent the page out with him under his coat, against his heart, to the cave. Mrs. Quill came for supper and stayed to dictate. The candle burned low. Outside, the sea was already gathering itself for the next blow, and the next after that; the season had begun, and there would be no quiet now until spring.

It was Mrs. Quill, shrugging into her cloak at the door near midnight, who paused with her hand on the latch and looked back with those ancient unsentimental eyes.

"One thing you haven't thought on," she said quietly. "Vane. The historian. He'll want to interview the town—he'll start with the harbor records, and the harbor records start with *you*. When that man sits across this table asking after names and dates, girl, you'll need a face that doesn't know what's under your floorboards." She fastened the clasp. "Best begin practicing your lies now. You've less time than you think."

Through the window, far down the dark water, Elodie thought she saw—though it might have been a star, though it was probably nothing—a light moving steadily closer along the coast.
