## 7. The Garrison Takes the Town

Wren woke to the sound of boots.

Not the soft, scattered footfalls of fishermen heading down to the quay before dawn—she knew those the way she knew her own heartbeat, a rhythm so familiar it had become part of the house's silence—but the hard, measured percussion of many men marching in step, each heel striking cobbles like a nail driven into wood. The sound came up through the floorboards, through the frame of her cot, into her teeth. She lay still in the grey half-light and counted the beats until she lost count, and then she gave up counting and simply listened to Greyhaven being walked over.

Her father was already gone from his bed. That was not unusual; Joss Renner kept hours that belonged to no honest trade, and he came and went like weather. But this morning there was a tension in the empty room he left behind, in the way his coat hook hung bare, in the smell of tar and tallow where he'd worked at something by candlelight after she'd gone to sleep. She dressed quickly, cold fingers fumbling buttons, and went out into the street to see what the tide of soldiers had brought in overnight.

They were everywhere.

They came up from the quay in columns of eight, red coats dulled by sea-fog to the color of old brick, muskets slung and bayonets fixed as though Greyhaven might rise up against them between one step and the next. Officers rode at the column's head on horses that seemed offended by the salt air. Wagons followed, heavy-axled and mud-splashed, their canvas covers lashed down over shapes that could have been anything: grain sacks, gun barrels, coffins. Wren stood against the wall of the cooper's shed with her hands in her armpits and watched them pass, and the townspeople gathered beside her one by one—Mrs. Quill with her shawl drawn tight, the cooper and his sons, old Tam-asleep-on-his-feet Tam who was never actually asleep—all of them silent, all of them wearing the same expression, which was not fear exactly but something older and more practical: the look of people measuring a thing they could not fight.

The soldiers did not look at them. That was the strangest part. Wren had expected eyes, curiosity, perhaps cruelty; instead the red coats marched through her town as if it were empty, as if the people pressed against the walls were no more substantial than fog, and this indifference frightened her more than any glare would have done. You could argue with hatred. There was nothing to argue with in being unseen.

By noon the billeting officers had come through with paper lists and chalk, marking doors. Two soldiers were assigned to the Widow Marsh's front room; four to the tavern; six to the great drafty house on Chapel Lane that had stood empty since the Hendersons emigrated. And two to the Renner cottage—which meant, Wren understood with a lurch, that Elodie's little stone house beneath the light must have been passed over, or perhaps claimed for the lamp itself, because the requisition notice pinned to the church door named the harbor light as "a navigational aid of naval significance" and its keeper as essential personnel. Essential personnel. Wren read the notice three times while the crowd eddied around her, mouthing the unfamiliar official words, sounding them out the way Elodie had taught her to sound out the ledger's entries. *All registers, rolls, manifests, parish books, customs papers, and other records whatsoever to be surrendered.* Other records whatsoever. The phrase sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

* * *

The church changed first.

Wren noticed it on her way to Elodie's the next morning, because the path from the Renner cottage ran along the churchyard wall, and the wall now had sentries. Inside, the transformation was already far advanced: pews carried out and stacked in the yard like coffins awaiting burial, the stone floor swept and chalked into lanes, and sailors and soldiers working together in an eerie cooperation to stack casks from the wagons—salt pork, powder kegs, bundles of pike handles, everything the war needed to keep itself fed. The altar remained at the east end, incongruous among the barrels, and someone had draped the communion cloth over a crate of ship's biscuit as though modesty could be preserved by inches of linen.

Father Ainsley stood in the yard among his homeless pews, holding his prayer book against his chest the way Mrs. Quill held her satchel, and when Wren paused beside him he said, without looking at her, "They asked for the parish registers yesterday."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I would surrender what the law required." He said it carefully, each word set down like a coin on a scale. "The law requires a great deal, it turns out. Births. Marriages. Burials. Forty years of my hand." His thumb moved across the book's worn cover. "I gave them the fair copies, child. The ones written out neat for the bishop's visitation." He did not say what else he might have given them, or what else existed, and Wren was wise enough—or becoming wise enough—not to ask in the open air with sentries twenty feet away. But something passed between them in that moment, priest and smuggler's daughter, an understanding without terms, and she walked on toward the light with her heart beating strangely fast.

The school closed that same week. There was no announcement; the schoolmaster simply stopped ringing his bell, because the schoolroom had been taken for a guardhouse and Master Pellow himself assigned to inventory duty in the church-storehouse, counting biscuit for the Crown instead of teaching letters to eleven children of Greyhaven. Wren had only been attending irregularly anyway—her father's trade made regularity dangerous—and Elodie had taught her more at the lamplight table than Pellow ever had. But standing outside the guardhouse window, watching a corporal use the ledge where she'd once carved her initials to rest his musket barrel, she felt the loss open under her feet like a hole in sand. It wasn't the lessons she mourned. It was the bell. A town rang its bell for fire, for wreck, for worship, for school. Now the only bells in Greyhaven would be ship's bells and watch bells, the Crown's time replacing the town's.

* * *

She spent what hours she could at the light, and those hours were the eye of the storm.

Elodie behaved as though nothing had changed. She trimmed the wicks, wound the clockwork, logged the weather in her working ledger with the same unhurried hand, and cooked enough for five even though the garrison ate from its own stores. Tam came and went as always, carrying messages nobody spoke aloud, his carved gull riding his collar. Joss appeared at odd hours, slept badly, disappeared again. But Wren watched her father now with new eyes, and she watched Elodie too, and she saw the things they did not think she saw: Elodie sitting late with the ledger open before her, not writing but reading, running her finger down columns of names as a woman counts her children before a journey. Joss burning small papers in the cottage hearth, one at a time, feeding them to the flames with tongs so they wouldn't flare and show through the shuttered window. Her father destroying something page by page, and never meeting her eyes when she came downstairs and found the ash.

"Why are they here?" she asked him one evening, when the billeted soldiers were out on the mole and the cottage was theirs alone. "Truly. The war's in the north. Nobody fights over Greyhaven."

Joss looked up from his whetstone. He had her mother's way of going still when he thought, and seeing that stillness always did something to Wren's chest, because she could not remember her mother's face except reflected in his.

"Greyhaven has a harbor deep enough for supply ships," he said. "And a road inland that doesn't drown at spring tide. That's all we are to them, bird. A funnel. They'll pour the war through us and move on." He tested the blade with his thumb. "But a funnel has to be clean. No unrecorded cargo. No names off the books. They want every soul in this town accounted for, stamped, and filed where they can find it."

"Like the church registers."

"Like everything." He set the knife down. "Listen to me. If ever I'm not here, and anyone in a red coat asks you questions about your father, about his family, about—" he paused, and Wren heard the pause hold a name he almost never said, "—about your mother, you answer dull and short. *I don't know. I was young. My father never spoke of it.* Dull and short. Do you understand why?"

"No," Wren said honestly.

"No," Joss agreed softly. "But you will."

* * *

She understood on a Thursday morning, nine days after the landing, in the square.

It began with carts. Soldiers went door to door with the billeting lists and came back pushing handcarts, wheelbarrows, anything on wheels, piled with paper. Wren was coming back from the well when she saw the first load cross the square—loose sheets, bound volumes, rolls of vellum, a merchant's daybook with its covers flapping—and behind it another cart, and another. Word ran through the town faster than the carts did, and by the time the soldiers had built their platform of fish crates in front of the church-storehouse, most of Greyhaven had found reasons to be in the square with baskets and idle hands.

Mr. Vane was there.

Wren knew him by sight now—the Crown's historian, tall and neat in dove-grey rather than red, the man who had come aboard the cutter with more trunks than any soldier and fewer weapons than any officer. He stood a little apart from the platform with a notebook in his hand, watching the piles of paper grow, and his expression was not gloating and not regretful. It was attentive. He looked at the documents the way Elodie looked at weather coming in off the water: cataloguing, assessing, recording. Once his gaze crossed the crowd and touched Wren herself—a brief, weighing glance, the look of a man checking a name against a list—and moved on. She felt it on her skin like the touch of cold iron.

A sergeant read the order aloud. Wren caught fragments over the wind and the murmur: *surplus documentation... duplicates and defunct records... by authority of...* Then the soldiers began feeding the fire.

They burned the old harbor toll-books first, the ones predating the new requisition forms, because the Crown had new forms and old ink was clutter. They burned shipping notices from years past, apprenticeship indentures whose terms had expired, a bale of letters seized from the customs house marked *correspondence, non-germane*. Each armload went onto the pyre and the flames took it with a sound like a long exhale, and the smoke rose straight up in the windless morning, white and then grey, carrying flakes of ash over the rooftops like black-winged gulls.

Wren stood in the crowd and told herself it was only paper. Old paper, dead paper, paper nobody wanted. The sergeant had said so. *Surplus.*

Then a soldier dragged a chest from the last cart and tipped it, and what fell out was not loose sheets but bound volumes—dozens of them, decades of them—and among them, tumbling open on the cobbles with its pages fanning, a register written in a hand she knew.

Not Elodie's hand. But a hand like it. A careful, cramped, patient hand, the hand of someone who had written *born to* and *wife of* and *departed this life* ten thousand times, and every one of those ten thousand entries had been somebody's whole existence—their entire proof, the only place in the world where the fact of them was written down. Wren watched a soldier pick the volume up by its spine, idly, the way you'd pick up kindling, and shake it shut, and the pages clapped together around all those lives like a book closing on fingers.

The volume hit the fire and curled. She could not see the names. She told herself she couldn't see the names because of the smoke, not because there were no names left to see.

Something shifted in her then, quietly and permanently, the way a keel shifts when the ballast breaks loose. She had known, in the wordless way children know things, that the ledger mattered—that Elodie's book of names was precious the way the lamp was precious, a small defiance of the dark. But knowledge and understanding were different animals, and understanding arrived now with the smell of scorched vellum: *this is what surrendering means.* Not losing a book. Losing the people in it. If the ledger went into a fire like this one, then Mara Renner had never been born. Then Tam had no name, only a mute boy the garrison fed or didn't. Then Mrs. Quill's forty years of delivered children and closed eyes became nothing, became smoke, became the absence the Crown preferred. Then Joss Renner was whatever the customs papers said he was, and Wren herself was nobody's daughter, because there would be no line anywhere in the world that said otherwise.

Her mother lived in Elodie's ledger. Wren had learned her letters from that ledger, tracing *Mara* again and again in the margin where Elodie had entered her, and now soldiers were burning books in the square and Mr. Vane was writing in his notebook, and somewhere in the harbor light, under a loose floorboard or behind a stone she hadn't yet been shown, the true record of everyone Greyhaven had ever held was hiding from exactly this.

Across the fire, Elodie stood at the crowd's edge with Mrs. Quill beside her and Tam's hand gripped small in hers. Her face was composed, keeper-calm, the face she wore in storms. But she was looking at the flames the way other people look at graves, and when the heat gust shifted and drove ash across the square she did not step back, did not blink, only tightened her grip on the boy's hand until Wren, twenty feet away, saw the knuckles whiten.

Then Elodie turned her head, deliberately, and found Wren through the smoke.

She did not beckon. She did not speak. She only held her daughter-of-the-heart's gaze for one long breath, and in that breath Wren read everything the older woman would not say in the open square, by the fire, under the soldiers' eyes: *Now you understand. Now you know what we are keeping. Now you are one of us who keeps it.*

The fire burned until midday. When it was done, the soldiers raked the ashes and the town drifted home to its occupied houses, and Wren walked the long way, past the churchyard wall, past the sentries, down to the shingle below the light where the sea could reach her boots. She stood a while looking at the tower, white stone, black lantern, patient against the sky, and she thought about her mother's name written safe in ink, and about her father feeding papers to the hearth by tongs, and about dull short answers, and about the historian's grey eyes checking her against his list.

Then she picked her way up the cliff path, knocked twice on the lamplight door the way Tam had taught her, and waited to be let in on the secret.

Elodie opened the door before her second knock finished. She had known, of course. She had probably known since the square. She stepped aside to let Wren enter, and behind her, spread across the lamplight table, lay the ledger—open, alive, its pages whispering under the draft from the door—and beside it, fresh-cut and stacked, a quire of new paper, blank as snow, waiting for copies.

"Shut the door, love," Elodie said. "There's work, and we've less time than I hoped."
