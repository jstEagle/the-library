## 8. An Interview by Lamplight

Aldous Vane had conducted forty-one interviews since coming ashore at Greyhaven, and he ranked them all, privately and without apology, the way other men ranked horses or vintages. The harbormaster had been a bore—obsequious, imprecise, forever rounding tonnages to the nearest flattering figure. The garrison paymaster had been worse: a man who mistook vagueness for discretion. The fishermen had been sullen, the churchwarden pious, the customs clerk a ruin of drink who could not distinguish 1743 from 1744 without weeping.

Mrs. Elodie Winterbourne was none of these things.

He had interviewed her twice already—the first time formally, in the requisitioned parlor of the harbor house with a soldier at the door, the second informally, on the gallery of the light itself while she trimmed wicks around him as though he were weather to be worked around rather than a gentleman of the Crown to be deferred to. Both times she had answered every question put to her. Both times he had left with the uneasy conviction that he had asked far fewer questions than she had permitted him to believe.

Tonight would be the third. He intended it to be the decisive one.

* * *

The harbor register had come into his possession eleven days ago, surrendered among the first tranche of documents under the seizure order—early compliance being itself a small performance, Vane thought, though whether of honesty or theater he could not yet say. It lay now on the table before him in his quarters above the seized customs house, bound in salt-stiffened leather, its pages ruled in a careful, narrow hand. He had read it three times through. He had made indices. He had cross-referenced it against the naval convoy manifests, the surviving customs rolls, the militia levies, and his own interview notes from forty-one conversations, and out of that labor a single conclusion had emerged with the slow, cold clarity of frost forming on glass:

Elodie Winterbourne's memory was better than her book.

That should have been impossible. Registers were written at the moment of events, when facts stood nearest to hand; memory was written afterward, subject to erosion, embellishment, and wish. In every town he had ever documented—and there had been four—the reverse relationship held. People forgot what their papers remembered. Their recollections drifted, softened, reorganized themselves around regret.

But ask Mrs. Winterbourne how many barrels of salt herring had gone out on the *Merrow's Purse* in October of 1745, and she would tell you sixty-two; the register said fifty-eight. Ask her which crew member failed to return from the herring grounds that autumn, and she gave you the name instantly, unhesitatingly—*Dannock, Peter, drowned the ninth, body recovered the twelfth, buried by Mrs. Quill's brother, no stone*—while the register recorded only a blank line where the name had been entered late, in different ink, and then struck through by some earlier keeper's pen.

Fourteen such discrepancies so far. Fourteen places where the woman knew more than the ledger did. And in not one instance had she been vague. She corrected the record the way a jeweler corrects an apprentice's setting—precisely, without apology, and with the calm of someone who has long since stopped expecting the object to argue back.

Vane found this fascinating. He also found it, in a word he did not permit himself to use in reports, alarming.

He dressed carefully for the evening—not in his best coat, which announced ambition, but in his second-best, which announced reasonableness. He carried no soldiers. He took instead his notebook, his pencil, a copy of the register, and a bottle of decent claret that had survived the crossing, because a man learned more across a shared glass than across a drawn sword, and Aldous Vane had never once in his career needed the sword.

The walk to the light took him past the square, where the army's burning continued in a desultory way—a brazier of "surplus" papers, parish duplicates mostly, their ash rising on the wind like grey moths. A boy watched him pass. Mute, the garrison said, the boatboy who lived half-wild around the harbor steps. The boy looked at Vane with an expression he could not classify and did not enjoy failing to classify, and then looked away toward the lighthouse with an expression he could: it was the way men look at a lit window when they are walking home through cold.

*You are going somewhere,* Vane thought, watching the boy go. *Everyone in this town is going somewhere, and none of you will tell me where.*

He filed the thought away, as he filed everything, and climbed the causeway path.

* * *

She admitted him to the lantern room's lower chamber—the small round room beneath the lamp where the oil was stored and the great logbook kept—and she did it without surprise, which told him she had seen him coming from the gallery, and without ceremony, which told him she had decided what he was worth in advance and saw no reason to revise upward.

"Mr. Vane." She set a second chair beside the stove without being asked, which was either hospitality or strategy. "You'll take tea. The claret I'll decline on your behalf—it fuddles the memory, and yours is the one that needs keeping clear tonight."

"You anticipated my errand."

"I anticipate the tide." She poured. "It comes twice a day and brings the Crown with it lately."

He laughed despite himself, and sat, and opened the register on the small table between them. Her eyes went to it—he watched them go, deliberately, the way a card player watches eyes—and they did not widen, did not flicker, did not do any of the hundred small betrayals he had catalogued over six years of documentary work. They simply rested on the book with the mild interest of a woman looking at a neighbor's fence.

"Mrs. Winterbourne. I want to begin with a compliment, and I'd ask you to receive it honestly, because I intend to build an evening on top of it." He laid his hand flat on the register. "This is the finest harbor record I have encountered in four provincial administrations. Ruled columns consistent across twenty years. Weather notation that a Royal Society observer wouldn't blush at. Crew lists complete down to boys and bondsmen. Whoever kept this book understood that a record is a kind of keel—everything else in a port balances on it."

"That was my husband's doing," she said. "I only kept the habit after him."

"With respect, madam, the hand changed in 1741 and nothing else did. Not the ruling, not the abbreviations, not the discipline. Habits don't survive grief intact unless they've become something sturdier than habits." He let that sit for a moment. She drank her tea.

"So here is my difficulty." He turned the register to face her, open to the autumn of 1745. "Your book says fifty-eight barrels of herring went out on the *Merrow's Purse* in October of that year. You told me sixty-two. I assumed at first you misremembered—I have stopped assuming that. So I checked the customs duty paid at the next port of transshipment, which is a thing I am quietly good at, and the duty corresponds to sixty-two. Your book is wrong, Mrs. Winterbourne. Your memory is right. And I have found fourteen instances of exactly this pattern."

The stove ticked. Above them, separated by the whole machinery of the lantern, the great light swept its beam across the water and gathered it back again.

"What is my question?" she said at last, and it was such an odd, honest question that he almost smiled. "You came here with fourteen discrepancies and a compliment, Mr. Vane, and I don't yet hear the question inside them."

"The question," he said, leaning forward, "is what happened to the missing four barrels. Because I don't believe they're missing. I believe they were smuggled, that the duty was quietly paid later through Falmouth to square the books, and that you—keeping this register—knew about it at the time and chose, for reasons of your own, to write the true number in your head and the safe number on the page." He paused. "And the larger question is: if that is true of herring barrels, what else lives in your head that does not live on this page?"

He had rehearsed the phrasing on the walk over and still found it too blunt, but bluntness was sometimes a tool; you used it to see what shape the hole it made was.

Elodie Winterbourne considered him for a long moment across the teacups. When she spoke, her voice was level and unhurried, and it frightened him a little, because it was the voice of someone deciding something.

"You think I keep two ledgers, Mr. Vane. One in ink and one in memory."

"I think everyone keeps two ledgers. Most people are simply careless about both."

"Then you've misunderstood me, and I'd rather be understood than agreed with, so attend." She set down her cup. "The four barrels. Joss Renner ran them—that much of your arithmetic is right, and you may write it down if it comforts you, because Joss has run half the cargo on this coast since he was fifteen and everyone knows it, including the excise men who buy their tobacco from him. But the duty wasn't squared later. My husband squared it *before*, out of his own pocket, because the *Purse* was short-crewed that season and Renner's men filled the gap on the herring grounds, and my husband would not have a debt stand between neighbors. He wrote fifty-eight in the book because fifty-eight was what the *Purse's* own hands had landed. The other four were Renner's hands. Separate account. Paid in full, witnessed by me." She folded her hands. "The page records what the page is for. The rest I keep because someone ought to."

Vane held very still. It was a beautiful answer. It was also, he suspected, almost entirely beside the point, and constructed to be—every element of it plausible, generous, verifiable in spirit if not in paper, and arranged with the artlessness of a very fine piece of carpentry. He could not find the joint. That was what disturbed him. In forty-one interviews he had always found the joint.

"And the drowned man," he said. "Dannock. Your book strikes his name; your tongue restores it."

"My book doesn't strike anything. My husband struck it, in blue ink, the week after—there was a dispute over the burial, the parish wanted a fee the family couldn't pay, and he lost patience with the whole arrangement and crossed the poor man out rather than enter him into a system that would charge his widow for the privilege of grieving. I told the story because you asked after crews, and Dannock was crew. If you want the truth of this coast, Mr. Vane, you must accept that some entries are omissions and some omissions are entries."

*Some omissions are entries.* He wrote the phrase down before he could stop himself, and heard her make a sound that might have been a laugh pressed thin.

* * *

They talked for two hours.

He walked her through the register season by season, and she answered everything—tonnages, names, debts, quarrels, the fire of '44, the fever winter—with a fluency that should have satisfied him completely and instead deepened his unease, because her fluency never varied. Memory stumbles; hers glided. Memory reaches; hers delivered, pre-weighted, pre-measured, like coin counted out of a purse. Twice he changed the subject abruptly to test her and twice she followed without a ripple. Once he invented a ship—the *Providence*, out of Weymouth, March last—and she said, gently, without triumph, "There was no *Providence* in March, Mr. Vane. There was a *Provident*, and she was from Poole, and you know it, because it's in your notes; you're testing whether I'll agree with whatever you put in front of me." And he had looked down at his own notebook and found that yes, *Provident*, Poole, was there, in his own hand, three pages back.

It was the most unsettling moment of the evening. He had been tested, caught, and corrected—and corrected *kindly*, the way one corrects a promising child—and she had done it without ever once appearing to withhold anything.

"You are admiring me again," she observed, near the end. "You do it with your chin. It's a dangerous habit in an occupied town."

"It is a professional assessment," he said, with dignity. "I will confess to it openly: you are the most precise witness I have ever examined. In any ordinary history, madam, you would be its backbone. I would build the entire account of this occupation on your testimony and note in the preface that every fact had been checked against a living memory and found truer than its paperwork."

"Flattery."

"Inventory." He closed the register. "Which leaves us only the matter I came to end with, and I'll ask it plainly, because you strike me as a woman who pays plainness its due. Under the seizure order, all registers, rolls, and records of this port are to be surrendered. This book is surrendered—I hold it. But a record, as you have taught me tonight, is not only a book. Is there anything, Mrs. Winterbourne—in any form, in any hand, in any place—that purports to record the traffic, people, debts, births, deaths, or comings and goings of Greyhaven, which has not yet come into His Majesty's possession?"

The lamp above them turned. Its beam crossed the windows, swept the black water, and returned.

"No, Mr. Vane," said Elodie Winterbourne, looking him directly in the eye with the perfect steadiness he had already learned to admire and was beginning, very quietly, to fear. "Everything this town remembers is either on your table or in my head. And my head is at your service whenever you want it."

* * *

He climbed down the causeway path with the claret unopened under his arm and the wind pushing at his back, and he did his best thinking walking, and by the time he reached the customs house he had assembled the evening into its components and found each one clean.

Every answer had been plausible. Every answer had been verifiable in principle. Every answer had arrived too fast and fit too well, like a key cut by someone who had studied the lock through a keyhole. She had confessed to smuggling without naming it smuggling. She had explained two discrepancies with a generosity of narrative detail that no innocent witness could produce on demand—and no guilty one, either, unless she had rehearsed. Unless she had rehearsed for years.

Unless, said a colder voice in him, the discrepancies are not evasions at all. Unless the register is the evasion, and the woman is the record.

He sat at his desk, lit his candle, and pulled his index cards toward him. Fourteen discrepancies, and tonight perhaps twenty. He would need thirty. He would map them all—every place her voice outran the page—and see whether the pattern pointed toward concealment or toward something stranger: a keeper who had spent twenty years writing down less than she knew, on principle, the way a wise man spends less than he owns.

At the bottom of the stack, half-buried, was the note he had made on his first day: *Harborlight keeper maintains private knowledge of port traffic beyond official register — source of local deference? Investigate.*

Underneath it, in fresh ink, he added: *She is not hiding from the record. She is the record. Find what she has sworn to carry, and you find the true history of Greyhaven.*

He blew out the candle. Through his window he could see the light turning above the harbor, patient, exact, sweeping the dark on its endless rounds—and he thought, with the particular pleasure of a man who has finally identified the shape of his real work:

Tomorrow he would request the garrison's list of every person who had set foot in the keeper's household since the seizure order. Servants, visitors, boatboys, midwives. No record lives entirely in one head. Heads talk to heads. Somewhere in this town, Mrs. Winterbourne's memory had touched other memories—and wherever two accounts touch, Aldous Vane had learned, a seam opens.

In the morning he would send for the midwife.
