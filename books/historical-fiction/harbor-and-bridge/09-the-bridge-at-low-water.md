## 9. The Bridge at Low Water

Tam Hearn had never spoken a word in his life, and he had learned early that the world did not wait for boys who could not ask it questions. So he watched. He watched the gulls wheel and drop their shellfish on the harbor rocks; he watched Mrs. Quill's hands fold linen into shapes that meant *coming* or *gone*; he watched Elodie Winterbourne's face when she trimmed the great lamp each evening, the way her mouth moved around names she never said aloud. He knew more than anyone in Greyhaven knew, because knowing was the only voice he had.

And now they wanted him to carry something across the bridge.

He stood in the sea cave at dawn with the tide still low enough to show the black teeth of rocks below the lantern, and he looked at what Elodie had given him: pages folded into an oilcloth packet no bigger than a hymn book, sewn shut at the corners with waxed thread. Forty-one names. Births. Debts forgiven. A drowning from 1731 written in Elodie's small, upright hand. He could not read them — Wren was learning, but Tam had never been taught, and letters stayed closed to him like doors without handles — but he understood their weight anyway. He held them the way you hold a sleeping bird.

*Mrs. Prewitt's house,* Elodie had mouthed at him that morning, her fingers shaping the words slowly so he would not mistake them. *Past the mill. The blue door. Give it only to her.* And then she had taken his chin in her rough palm, the way she did when she wanted him to truly hear her, and said: *"If anyone stops you, Tam, you know nothing. You're going to sell eggs."*

There was a basket waiting by the door of the cottage, lined with straw, holding eleven brown eggs and a heel of bread. He was to be a boy going to market. It was such a simple lie, and he turned it over in his mind as he walked, testing its seams. He could not say *I am carrying nothing*. He could not say *these are my mistress's papers*. He could not say anything at all, which meant that for once in his life his silence was not a wound but a wall. The soldiers could search his mouth and find nothing there. They would have to put their hands on the packet itself, and the packet was flat against his chest, and his heart was hammering against it hard enough, he thought, to give them away by sound alone.

The tidal bridge began at the end of the harbor road, where the cobbles gave way to wet stone that ran a quarter mile across the estuary to the mainland path. At low water it stood dry and pale as a scarred forearm, studded with barnacle clusters and the long dark tidelines of previous drownings. At high water it vanished entirely, and men had died trying to cross it at half-tide, misjudging the speed of the returning sea. Tam knew the bridge better than anyone alive. He had crossed it since he was four years old, when old Marta Hearn — who was no true relation but had claimed him off the beach all the same — used to set him on her shoulders and walk the crown of the stones while the water sucked and gurgled on either side. She had drowned there six winters ago, coming back from the mainland with medicine for a fever that took three children anyway. The town said she had been foolish to cross so late. Tam knew she had crossed late because the fever could not wait for the tide, and he had never needed words to hold that knowledge, or her.

This morning the estuary lay open and silvered under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. The season's first storm had come and gone two nights past, taking roof slates and one of Joss Renner's boats with it, and the air still had a scoured, rinsed quality, everything washed too clean. Tam could see the whole span of the bridge ahead of him, empty from end to end.

That should have comforted him. Instead it made the skin crawl between his shoulder blades. Empty water meant fish. Empty roads meant patrols.

*

He had seen the soldiers come ashore eleven days ago, and he had spent those eleven days learning them the way he learned everything: silently, obsessively, with the patience of a stone being shaped by tides. There were thirty-two of them encamped behind the church, though only twelve wore the red coats in any given rotation. Their sergeant was called Hobbes, and he had a habit of standing with his thumbs in his belt and his weight on his heels, which made him slow to turn. The young one with the freckles — Tam thought of him as Sparrow — was nervous and quick and dangerous precisely because he startled easily. Two of them were billeted in the Widow Crane's front room and could be heard through the floor arguing about dice past midnight. One had already begun courting the cooper's eldest daughter, badly. They were not devils. That was the worst of it. They were tired boys far from home, following orders none of them fully understood, and orders were worse than devils because devils at least acted out of appetite rather than duty.

Tam catalogued all of this in the ledger he kept behind his eyes, and every morning he walked the harbor road with his net and his bucket, doing nothing, being nobody, watching.

So when he stepped onto the first stone of the bridge and felt the morning hold its breath around him, he already knew what he would do if the patrol came. He had rehearsed it lying awake in the loft above Elodie's kitchen, staring at the rafters: keep walking. Do not stop suddenly. Do not look at your own chest. Boys carrying eggs do not clutch themselves like misers. Let them see a dull child on a dull errand, and let their eyes slide off him the way water slides off oiled cloth. His silence would do the rest. Silence was the most convincing testimony in the world. No one suspected a boy of lying when the boy could not speak at all.

Halfway across, the wind came up off the estuary and pushed at him sideways, smelling of salt and dying kelp and the far cold of open sea. Below him, between the stones, the shallows ran glass-clear over ribbons of sand, and a shoal of small silver fish broke apart and reformed beneath his shadow. He stopped — he could not help it — and looked down at the water, because looking at water had always been the closest thing he had to prayer. Somewhere under that sand lay the anchor stone of a wreck older than the town. Somewhere near it, Marta's shawl had washed ashore, torn and heavy, and he had carried it home himself, aged seven, and dried it by the fire, and kept it folded in his chest until Elodie found it there years later and understood without asking.

The packet pressed warm against his sternum. Forty-one names, riding just above his heartbeat.

He was fifty yards from the mainland side when he saw the red coats.

*

They came out of the gorse track that led down from the tollhouse — three of them, boots crunching on the shingle, moving without urgency the way men move when the day belongs to them. Tam's body went cold from the feet upward, a tide of its own, and for one white instant his mind offered him nothing at all: no plan, no rehearsal, no words even in the silent place where his words lived. Just the sight of red against green, and the eggs sweating in their straw, and the terrible knowledge that he could not call out, could not explain, could not say *please* in any language the world accepted.

Then his feet kept walking. That was the miracle of his own body, he realized later — that it knew the script even when his mind had fled. He did not slow. He did not speed up. He came on along the crown of the bridge with his basket swinging in its steady rhythm, and he counted his steps the way he counted waves, and inside his coat the oilcloth packet lay against his heart like a second, braver heart.

"Hoy. You. Boy."

Sparrow's voice. Of course it was Sparrow. Tam turned his head, arranged his face into the slack, slightly frightened blankness that adults expected from him — he had worn that face his whole life and knew every crease of it — and stopped walking. Not abruptly. A boy stopping because a soldier hailed him stops like that: uncertain, shoulders climbing toward his ears.

The three of them came onto the bridge to meet him, which was a small victory, because men on the bridge had to walk carefully, single file in places, and careful men asked fewer questions. The sergeant — not Hobbes; a thick-necked man Tam privately called Anvil — reached him first and looked into the basket without asking.

"Eggs," Anvil said, as though accusing him of something.

Tam nodded. He opened his mouth and let it stand open, and made a small motion in his throat, the gesture he had refined over ten years: the shape of speech with nothing in it. He pointed at his own ear, then his tongue, and shook his head with the practiced weariness of long habit.

"Deaf and dumb," Sparrow said, half to the sergeant, with the faint disgust of a boy who has been told this is possible but never quite believed it. "Crane's lot say the lightkeeper keeps one. A mute. Does the boats."

"The lightkeeper." Anvil said the word the way he'd say *smuggler*, and Tam felt the cold come back into his feet. "Who's this then, her errand dog?" He looked at Tam harder now, and Tam watched the man's eyes travel — basket, face, basket — and prayed to whatever governed tides and soldiers that they would keep traveling in exactly that shallow arc.

He thought of the pages then. Not abstractly — he thought of them physically, the waxed thread, the oilcloth, Elodie's small upright hand, the name *Maren Renner* written at the top of the third page because Elodie had shown it to him before sewing the packet shut, tapping it twice with her finger, and he understood without letters that this name was the reason for all of it. A girl's mother, erased before the girl could remember her. If they cut his coat open they would find her there, folded in quarters, riding over his heart. He thought: *they will have to take her out of me.* And something happened in his chest that he had no word for, some iron settling, some door closing and barring. He was afraid — his hands were shaking inside the basket's shadow — but the fear had changed its shape. It was no longer fear of being caught. It was fear for something, and fear for is a different animal altogether. Fear for has teeth.

"Where's it going, then? Eggs to the mainland?" Anvil glanced at his companions. "Lightkeeper sends her boy across with eggs and comes back with what, I wonder. Brandy? Tea? Leaf tobacco, maybe." He smiled without warmth. "Renner's whelp runs that cargo, don't she. And Renner's whelp spends half her days at the light."

Tam let his face go stupid. He pointed at the basket, then down the mainland track, then made the small shuffling gesture everyone understood to mean *market*, and added a shrug that said *I am only a tool that walks*. Inside, his heart slammed so hard he was sure it must move the fabric of his coat. He thought of Mrs. Prewitt's blue door. He thought: *not today. Not these pages.*

Anvil stepped closer. Close enough that Tam could smell the garrison on him — woodsmoke, bad soap, the sour ghost of army beer. The man's hand rose, and time went strange and syrup-slow, and Tam saw the hand as if drawn in Elodie's ink: reaching, opening, coming down —

— and landing on the basket's rim, tilting it, counting eggs with one blunt finger. Eleven. The finger withdrew.

"Take one," Anvil told Sparrow, peeling away, already bored. "Tax."

Sparrow took an egg with clumsy ceremony, nearly dropping it, and the three of them crunched off along the shingle toward the tollhouse, their laughter thin and carrying over the water. Tam stood on the crown of the bridge and did not move until the gorse swallowed the last flash of red. Then his legs announced their opinion of the entire morning by nearly setting him down in the shallows, and he sat on a barnacled stone and breathed, long and ragged and silent, while the estuary glittered at him without sympathy.

One egg. They had taken one egg for the Crown, and missed forty-one names by the width of an oilcloth and a heartbeat. He laughed — no sound came out, but his shoulders shook with it, a laugh like a fish fighting air — and wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist, and got up, and walked the rest of the way across with the packet pressed to his chest and his other hand steadying the ten remaining eggs as though they were the treasure.

*

Mrs. Prewitt's house stood a mile inland where the coast road bent around the millpond, low-roofed and stone-walled, with ivy eating one chimney and the promised blue door faded almost to gray. She had been the excise officer's wife for thirty years, the town said, and had learned from her husband every trick the free-traders knew, and had loved him enough to forget most of them after he died. When Tam knocked — twice, then once, the signal Elodie had given him — the door opened on a small woman with flour to her elbows and eyes that weighed him in a single glance, basket to face and back again.

"You'll be the Winterbourne's boy," she said. Not a question. She stepped back to let him in, and the door closed behind him on the bright morning, and the kitchen was dim and warm and smelled of bread and dried lavender and, faintly, of lamp oil — the same smell as Elodie's cottage, and Tam felt his throat go tight at the kinship of it.

She did not ask him to speak. That undid him more than anything else that day. She took the basket, set it on the table, and waited, her hands folded, while he worked the packet out of his coat — the oilcloth warm now, damp at the edges where his sweat had reached it — and laid it on her scrubbed table between the flour and the eggs. Then, because his body insisted on some completion, he tapped the packet twice with his finger, the way Elodie had tapped the page, and pointed south, toward Greyhaven, toward the light.

Mrs. Prewitt looked at the packet for a long moment. Then she looked at Tam, and her eyes had gone bright and wet in the lamplight.

"Maren Renner's in there," she said quietly. "I'd wager my husband's watch on it." She did not wait for confirmation. She lifted the packet and held it a moment against her own breast, mirroring without knowing it the way he had carried it, and Tam felt something pass between the three of them — the boy, the widow, the woman at the light — some chain forged of paper and stubbornness that no requisition order had the power to cut.

"She'll have it all copied by the seizure date, or I'm a Dutchman," Mrs. Prewitt went on, more to herself than to him, already moving to the dresser, pulling open a drawer where loose floorboards gaped like a ready mouth. "My husband hid excise ledgers in this very hole from men no cleverer than the ones in your harbor now. Thirty years, and the Crown never once found them. Women keep things, boy. That's the whole of history, if you want it straight." She knelt, fitted the packet into the dark, and replaced the board so seamlessly that Tam, who prided himself on seeing everything, could not afterward have said where the join was.

She fed him bread and honey and milk before he left, sitting him at her table like company instead of a courier, and talked to him the whole time in an easy, unhurried voice, not because she expected answers but because she understood — as few speaking people ever had — that being talked *to*, plainly and without pity, was all the conversation a mute boy ever needed. She told him about her husband. She told him about the year of the great wrack, when the customs men and the free-traders had fought a running battle through the millpond shallows and both sides had afterward drunk together in her parlor while she bandaged them. "Men make such noise about their differences," she said, "and underneath it's all the same business: keeping what matters from whoever means to take it."

The tide was turning when he started back. He knew it in his bones before he knew it in his ears — the changed pitch of the water talking under the bridge, the first thin sheets of sea sliding up the channel on the estuary's southern flank. He had hours yet, plenty, but he walked fast anyway, because the sky over the western water had begun to assemble something large and gray, and the wind had a new edge to it, and the season was turning the way old sailors said it turned: not gradually but all at once, like a door swinging.

He was halfway across the bridge, the sea lapping at the lower stones on either side now, when he saw the boat.

It came around the harbor mole under a scrap of reefed sail, running hard for shelter ahead of the weather, and even at that distance Tam knew the cut of her rig the way he knew his own hands. One of Joss Renner's. But she flew no signal, and she was sailing alone, and as she closed with the harbor he made out figures in her stern that did not move like smugglers — they moved like soldiers, red coats darkened with spray, and one figure at the rail who stood too still, hatless, his arms not tied but held, in the particular posture of a man being kept rather than escorted.

Joss Renner.

Tam stopped dead on the crown of the bridge with the rising tide swallowing the stones behind him, and the wind tore at his hair, and the packet's twin — its sisters, its daughters, page after page still growing by candlelight in Elodie's cottage — seemed suddenly to burn against a chest that had gone hollow. He watched the boat slide into the harbor, watched the soldiers leap to the quay, watched the small crowd begin to gather the way crowds gather, fast and fearful. And far above them all, in the lantern room of the light, a figure appeared at the gallery rail and stood motionless against the graying sky, watching the same boat come in.

The first drops of the storm struck the stones around Tam like thrown gravel, and he began to run for home.
