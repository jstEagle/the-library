## 10. Storm Damage

The storm came up from the southwest the way the old pilots always said the worst ones did—without manners and without mercy, arriving hours ahead of any forecast a sensible woman might have trusted.

Elodie felt it in her teeth before she saw it in the sky. She had been mending the wick-trimmers at the kitchen table when the ache began, a dull pressure behind her eyes that her mother had once called *the sea's knock*, and by the time she climbed the tower stairs to look southward, the horizon had gone the color of a bruise gone bad. Green-black water. A sky stacked with cloud like slate waiting to be roofed. And running before it all, the first white horses of the swell, already breaking over the outer shoal in long, ragged lines.

She did not waste time on dread. Dread was a luxury for people whose work could wait.

By four o'clock she had the storm shutters half-latched on the seaward gallery, the oil stores moved to the driest corner of the store room, and Tam standing at her elbow with his arms full of spare glass panes wrapped in sacking, his dark eyes asking the question he had no voice to ask.

"Yes," she told him. "It'll be one of those."

He nodded once and went back down the stairs without complaint. The boy had been with her three years now, washed ashore half-drowned off a collier that no one else survived, and in all that time she had never heard him make a sound—not grief, not fear, not pain. Mrs. Quill said the voice had drowned with the crew. Elodie thought perhaps it was only that he had found nothing yet worth saying that could not be said better with his hands.

The wind arrived at dusk.

It came not as a gust but as a wall, a single sustained shove against the tower that made the whole structure groan like a ship working in heavy seas, and after it the rain came sideways, needling, cold as flung gravel. Elodie stood in the lantern room and watched the light sweep its great slow circle out into the chaos, and she thought, as she always thought on such nights: *somewhere out there is a man at a tiller who will live or die by whether this lamp is burning when he needs it.*

That was the whole of the work. That was the whole of the faith. Everything else—the ledger, the names, the quiet war she was waging against the Crown's memory—was built upon this simpler foundation: keep the light. Keep the light, and everything kept by its light has a chance.

* * *

The crack came at half past nine.

She heard it even above the wind—a sound like a musket shot, sharp and wrong—and then a sound worse than the crack: the whistle of wind finding a way in where it should not be. She turned from the lamp mechanism in time to see the leeward pane of the lantern room shudder, flex, and burst inward in a spray of glittering fragments that the gale snatched and scattered across the floor like a handful of stolen stars.

Cold air roared through the gap. Rain followed it in sheets. The flame of the lamp bent flat, guttered, and for one horrible heartbeat the entire harbor went dark.

Elodie moved without deciding to move. She threw herself between the breach and the lamp, spreading her oilskin wide like a sail, and pressed her back to the broken frame so that her body took the storm's blow instead of the flame. The wind hammered her. Rain drove against her hood with the force of thrown stones. But behind her shoulder, shielded in the pocket of calm her body made, the flame steadied, straightened, and burned.

"Tam!" she shouted into the dark below. "Tam! Glass!"

His footsteps on the spiral stair were faster than her shout. He came up into the lantern room soaked to the skin, took one look at the breach, one look at her braced against it, and understood everything at once. He wedged himself beside her, pulled a wrapped pane from the sacking, and began to fit it into the frame with hands that did not shake.

They worked like that for an hour—she holding the line against the wind, he setting glass, both of them communicating in grunts and gestures and the wordless fluency of people who have shared too many hard nights to need language. Two panes went in and blew out again before they found the trick of it: set the pane low, seal the edge with tallow and canvas, let the frame's own iron take the strain. By eleven o'clock the breach was closed with mismatched salvaged glass, ugly as a patched coat, but closed.

Elodie sagged against the wall and discovered she was bleeding. A shard had opened a line across her left forearm somewhere in the first moments; she had never felt it. She bound it with a strip of her petticoat, tight, without ceremony, and went back to the lamp.

* * *

The knocking at the lightkeeper's door came at midnight, and it was not the wind.

She descended the ninety-one steps with legs gone wooden from the climb and the cold, and opened the door on two soldiers of the garrison, their red coats black with rain, a sergeant's voice fighting through the gale.

"Mistress Winterbourne. Orders from Captain Hesse." He thrust a paper toward her, sodden at the corners. "The harbor mole's taking damage and the transport *Providence* is due on tomorrow's tide with powder and provisions. Every able hand is wanted on the quay to reinforce the pilings. You're to come down now."

Elodie read the order by the light of the storm lantern she held. Then she looked up at the sergeant—a young man, broad, competent-looking, rain streaming off the brim of his hat—and she saw that he was not a cruel man, merely an obedient one, and that obedience had brought him out into this night to ask a woman to abandon the only thing between every ship in those waters and the rocks.

"The lamp is short-handed," she said. "The lantern room's breached. I've one boy and two hands, and one of them is mine."

"That's not my concern, mistress. The captain says—"

"I know what the captain says. I can read." She folded the order along its wet crease and handed it back. "Tell Captain Hesse that if the light goes dark tonight, there will be no mole left to reinforce and no transport to unload, because the *Providence* will open her hull on the Teeth Shoal with all her powder aboard, and Greyhaven will be a hole in the coast where a town used to be. Tell him the light comes first. It came first before his soldiers ever landed here, and it will come first after they've gone."

The sergeant's jaw tightened. Behind him, the younger soldier shifted uncomfortably, glancing up at the tower where the beam swept its stubborn circle through the rain.

"Mistress," the sergeant said, and something in his voice changed—not softer exactly, but more careful, the tone of a man stepping onto uncertain ground. "I've orders to impress laborers. If you refuse, I'm to note it."

"Note it," Elodie said. "Spell my name correctly. W-i-n-t-e-r-b-o-u-r-n-e. I'd hate to be misremembered."

He stared at her a moment longer. Then, unexpectedly, he touched his hat brim—a small gesture, almost involuntary, the courtesy of one professional acknowledging another's competence—and turned back into the storm.

* * *

They came twice more that night.

At two o'clock it was a corporal with six men, sent to take the laborers by force if necessary, and Elodie met them in the doorway with a boat hook in her hands and Tam at her shoulder holding the storm lantern high so the soldiers could see her face. She did not raise the weapon. She simply stood, small and gray-haired and immovable as the tower itself, and asked the corporal whether he proposed to explain to Captain Hesse why the harbor light had failed during the worst storm in nine years because its keeper had been dragged away to pile stones.

The corporal looked at the tower. He looked at the light turning above them, steady as conscience. He looked at the sea exploding white against the outer rocks, and whatever arithmetic he performed in that moment, it came out in her favor. They withdrew without touching her.

At four o'clock, alone again in the lantern room with the wind screaming its endless note, Elodie allowed herself one moment of exhaustion. She sat on the iron floor with her back against the lamp housing, feeling its warmth through her oilskin, and she let her eyes close for exactly the length of one rotation of the lens. Ninety seconds. No more. Then she rose, trimmed the wicks, checked the oil feed, and resumed the watch.

Her arm throbbed. Her shoulders burned from bracing against the breach. Somewhere below, Tam slept in the store room among the spare glass, curled like a compass needle pointing toward the stairs, ready to wake at her first call. And out in all that darkness, she knew, were boats—fishing crews caught out, a kelp-gatherer who never had the sense to run for harbor early, God knew what else—and every one of them would be steering by her fire.

Let the garrison have the mole. Let them stack their stones and count their barrels and write their requisitions. There was only one thing in Greyhaven tonight that mattered, and it was burning above her head, and it would go on burning if she had to hold the flame lit with her bare hands until dawn.

* * *

Dawn came the color of pewter, and with it the accounting.

The storm blew itself out by mid-morning, retreating southeast in long gray ranks, and the town emerged to count its wounds: the mole breached in two places despite the soldiers' night of labor, three fishing boats driven onto the sandbar and splintered, the *Quaker's Fortune* dragged her anchor and lost her bowsprit, and the sea cave where Elodie's copied pages lay hidden swallowed by a surge that had reached higher than any tide in living memory—though the cache sat above the surge line, and when Tam went to check at low water, he came back and gave her the small double nod that meant *safe*.

But the tower had taken its wound too. When the light finally failed at daybreak and Elodie climbed down into ordinary morning, she stood in the yard and looked up at the lantern room and saw the patchwork of salvaged glass gleaming unevenly in the sun, and beneath it, worse, a long diagonal crack running through the main frame of the roof, split by the wind's leverage, weeping a thread of rust-red water down the glazing bars.

Mrs. Quill arrived within the hour, as midwives always arrive, drawn by some instinct tuned to trouble. She clucked over Elodie's forearm, cleaned and bound it properly with linen and a sting of spirits, and pronounced it shallow while looking deeply unconvinced.

"You'll want a glazier from Kingsbridge for that roof," she said, nodding upward. "And you won't get one. Not with the roads what they are and the garrison commandeering every cart."

"Then I'll want pitch and canvas and a ladder," Elodie said. "And someone with steady hands who isn't afraid of heights."

"Steady hands." Mrs. Quill sniffed. "You mean the boy."

"I mean whoever I can trust. Which narrows the list considerably these days."

The old woman was quiet a moment, folding her bandages with the economy of forty years' practice. "You refused them last night. The laborers. All night, they say—you stood them off three times."

"Someone had to stand."

"They're saying more than that, down in the town." Mrs. Quill's voice dropped, though there was no one to hear but the gulls. "Sergeant tells the corporal, corporal tells the cook, cook tells everyone. The lightkeeper wouldn't leave her post. The lightkeeper called the captain's order a death warrant for the transport. Some of the soldiers are saying it with respect." She paused. "Some are saying it to their officers. With a different kind of respect."

Elodie looked up at the cracked lantern room, at the light she had kept burning through the worst night of the season, and felt no triumph in it—only the tired clarity of a woman who has been seen doing something worth remembering.

"Mr. Vane," she said quietly. "Is he asking about it?"

"He's asking about everything. That's his trade, isn't it? Asking." Mrs. Quill gathered her basket. "Word is he walked the mole at first light in that fine grey coat of his, taking notes while the waves were still coming over it. And word is—" she hesitated, and Elodie knew the hesitation cost her, for Mrs. Quill did not hesitate, "—word is he asked specifically how long the light had stayed burning. Asked whether the keeper had left her post even once. Asked it very carefully, as though the answer were going into a book."

Elodie was silent. Through her mind moved the image of the historian's neat hand, his precise pen, recording her endurance in whatever ledger he kept—the official one, the one written by the victors, the one that could make a loyal servant or a marked woman with the same stroke of ink.

"Well," she said at last. "Then let him write it down. A woman who keeps her light in a storm is no secret worth hiding."

"No," Mrs. Quill agreed, moving toward the gate. "But a woman who keeps other things besides her light—that's another matter entirely. Mind your arm, Elodie. And mind your tongue. Storms pass. Men with notebooks don't."

She went out through the gate and down the cliff path, and Elodie stood alone in the yard with the salt wind drying on her face, looking up at the wounded tower she would spend the next week repairing with pitch and canvas and prayer.

Above her, the great lens waited in its patched cage of glass, dark now, patient, gathering itself for nightfall. Below her, in the house, the true ledger lay hidden under the loose hearthstone, its pages heavy with the names of everyone the Crown wished had never existed.

Two records. Two storms. And somewhere inland, riding toward her through the wreckage of the season, a historian with a careful pen and a question he had not yet thought to ask.

Elodie went inside to boil pitch.
