## 11. What Vane Found

The rain had found the seam in the cutter's boat long before it found the shore, and by the time Aldous Vane stepped onto the quay at Greyhaven his left boot was full of seawater and his notes were saved only by the oilcloth he had wrapped them in twice, the way a man wraps a thing he cannot afford to lose. He stood a moment on wet stone, taking inventory of the town the way he took inventory of everything: harbor to the south, garrison flag snapping above the old customs house, the light station on its promontory like a held breath. Forty-one roofs. Three lanes. One church, now a storehouse. One woman who kept a ledger better than any clerk he had employed in London.

He was thirty-four years old and he intended, before this occupation ended, to write the definitive account of the Crown's administration of this coast. He intended it with the calm of a man who had wanted very little in his life and had gotten most of it, and who had learned that wanting quietly was more effective than wanting loudly.

"Mr. Vane." Sergeant Hallow, waiting under an umbrella that did him no credit. "Mrs. Winterbourne will see you at the light. She says come after the tide turns."

"She sets the hour, then."

"She sets every hour in this town, sir, if you ask me." Hallow said it without malice, which was the troubling part. "Begging your pardon."

"I don't pardon you, Sergeant, I merely decline to log it. Come along."

They climbed the path together through wind that smelled of kelp and cold iron. The storm three nights past had left its signature everywhere—trawling nets strung up like gutted animals, a dinghy deposited in a garden as casually as a hat set on a table, the low stone wall along the cliff path opened in two places as though something enormous had stepped over it and grown bored. Vane noted all of it. He noted the boy too, the mute one called Tam, who sat on the breakwater mending something small and watched them pass with eyes that missed nothing and gave nothing back.

* * *

She received him in the kitchen of the light station because she always received him in the kitchen, and Vane understood the choice precisely: no formal parlor, no pretense of rank, only bread and tea and a table between them on which anything could be spread out and examined. Elodie Winterbourne poured his tea herself, her hands steady, her grey hair pinned back with what appeared to be a fish bone carved into the shape of a feather. She was fifty-one years old and she looked at him the way a lighthouse looks at a ship—not hostile, not welcoming, merely measuring how much water lay beneath him.

"Mrs. Winterbourne. You've weathered the storm, I see."

"The lantern cracked. I've patched it with pitch and prayer, in reverse order of effectiveness." She sat. "You've come about the register."

"I've come about several things." He unwrapped his oilcloth packet and laid out his materials with the ceremony he could not suppress: his notebook, his pencil, the fair copy of the harbor register that his clerks had produced from the original surrendered weeks ago—a copy made because the original had been damaged, water-stained along its lower third where some careless soldier had left it in the rain outside the customs house, and because Vane did not trust damaged documents to travel. "First, a formality. The requisition of labor for repairs to the east wharf—"

"You'll have your twelve men. Tam can name them faster than I can; he keeps his own count."

"The mute boy keeps a count."

"He keeps everything," she said, and there was something in her voice when she said it, some warmth banked like a stove overnight, and Vane wrote: *lightkeeper speaks of the boy Tam with affection; boy is regarded locally as hers.*

"Second," he said, "the shipping returns for October. Your figures differ from the customs house abstracts by four vessels."

"They would. Four vessels ran unlit into the harbor during the storm nights rather than wait for dawn, and I logged them and the customs men didn't, because the customs men were hiding from the rain like sensible creatures. I'll give you names and tonnages if you want them."

"I want them," he said, and she gave them, unhurried, precise to the last man's initials, and he took them down and felt again the particular pleasure he felt in her exactness—the pleasure of finding a well-made thing in an ill-made world. It was not admiration, he told himself. It was professional recognition. The distinction mattered to him, though he could not have said why.

* * *

It was the third item that undid him, and it undid him slowly, the way tides undo sea walls.

When the formal business was done—and he knew, and she knew, that they both lingered over the forms the way duelists linger over the code before the exchange—he drew from his packet the damaged original itself. He had brought it against regulation, wrapped in three layers of oilcloth, because he had reached the stage in his work where he needed to touch the thing he was writing about.

"The water damage," he said. "I've been reconstructing the affected pages. Ink holds differently depending on its iron content; the entries from 1741 onward fade but remain legible under raking light. Yours are remarkably consistent—you mixed your own ink?"

"Oak gall and rainwater. My husband's recipe."

"Your late husband appears throughout these pages like a second author."

"He was. For nine years. Then the sea edited him."

Vane paused with his pencil above the page. It was the nearest she had ever come to grief in his presence, and he marked it, and then reproached himself for marking it, and marked that too.

He turned to the stained section, running his finger down columns of names softened to brown ghosts. "Here. The winter of '43. You recorded eleven souls landed from the wreck of the *Providence*—wait, no. Ten in the official column here, and yet your daily memoranda reference provisions issued to eleven persons. I assumed a clerical correction, one entry superseded."

"One of the ten drowned reaching shore," Elodie said. Her voice had not changed. "The eleventh came ashore later. A man named Corliss. He stayed the winter."

"There is no Corliss in this ledger."

"No," she agreed. "There wouldn't be. He'd have been taken up for debt if any record of him surfaced. I wrote him in my other book."

And there it was—the sentence Vane had been circling for six weeks without knowing it, delivered so plainly it might have been about the weather. *My other book.* She said it the way one admits to keeping chickens.

"You keep two ledgers," he said.

"Every keeper of records does. One for the Crown, one for the truth. Mine happen to be more thorough than most."

He should have written it down. Later, examining himself as he examined everything, he would identify that moment precisely: the moment he set down his pencil instead of picking it up. "And this other book. Where is it?"

"Gone, I'm afraid, in the flooding. Paper and seawater make poor companions. What survived, I copied into the official record where it seemed proper; what didn't—" She lifted her teacup. "The sea keeps its own archives, Mr. Vane."

It was elegantly done. He almost applauded. But his mind had already gone back down the stained pages, to a different absence, one that had been nagging at him since morning in a way he could not shake—the way a word sits on the tip of the tongue. He turned the leaves carefully, past the wrecks and the births and the debts, to the marriage registers she maintained in parallel, unofficial, invaluable.

"Mrs. Renner," he said.

Elodie's face did nothing at all, which told him everything.

"Joss Renner married in — let me see — June of 1738, according to the parish abstracts seized from St. Brendan's. Yet the harbor register lists the birth of his daughter, Wren, in May of 1739, and here—" his finger stopped on a faded line "—here the mother's name has been left blank. Not crossed out. Not amended. Blank, from the first writing, with space left around it as though awaiting a name that never arrived. Every other birth in eleven years of your hand carries a mother's name, Mrs. Winterbourne. Even stillborns. Even foundlings. Who was Wren Renner's mother?"

The fire ticked. Outside, the patched lantern creaked in its cage, turning to face the darkening water. Vane watched the woman across from him and saw her decide something—not whether to lie, he thought, but which truth to spend.

"Her name was Sefeya," Elodie said at last. "Sefeya Renner. Born Sefeya Oduya, out of a trading family that put in here twice a year when I was a girl. She died of childbed fever eight days after Wren came, in this house, with Mrs. Quill attending. There was no parish record because the parish wouldn't take her—wrong church, wrong color, wrong everything, in their judgment. So I took her. Birth, death, the eight days between, all of it. In my other book." She set down her cup. "You asked. That's the answer."

"And why does the blank remain in the register you surrendered to the Crown?"

"Because a blank protects no one. A name would have hung her posthumously beside her husband, whose name, I'm told, appears in certain customs papers under charges that carry rope. I left the space empty the way you leave a window open for someone expected home."

Vane sat very still. He was aware of several things at once, which was usual for him, and of one thing more powerfully than the rest, which was not: that he wanted her to keep talking. Not for the history. Or not only.

"Mrs. Winterbourne," he said, "do you know what I am instructed to do with discrepancies?"

"I imagine you resolve them."

"I resolve them in favor of the account I am commissioned to write, which is an account of orderly administration. Discrepancies are disorder. Persons absent from records are, administratively speaking, persons who never existed—which makes them, administratively speaking, infinitely arrestable, deportable, erasable. A girl of seven whose mother never officially existed is a girl with no claim, no standing, no protection under any document in this country. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

"I understand perfectly," Elodie said. "You're offering to write her in."

"I am offering," Vane said slowly, feeling his way along a ledge in the dark, "to reconcile the record. To enter Sefeya Renner, née Oduya, into the official history I am compiling—birth, death, marriage, the whole architecture of a life—with my own authority behind it, such as it is. Once a name is in a published history endorsed by the Crown, striking it out becomes an act of vandalism against the state. No one hangs beside a name that official. And in exchange—" he met her eyes "—you tell me where the other book is, or what became of it, and you tell me true."

The silence went on long enough for the lamp above them to complete a full revolution, its beam sweeping the kitchen window like a slow white finger.

"You misunderstand what you're bargaining for," Elodie said quietly. "That book isn't mine to sell, Mr. Vane. It belongs to everyone in it. Including you, now—I'll write tonight's conversation in it, word for word, and someday someone will read what you offered and what you asked in return. You'd be buying a place in the very record you're trying to close."

"Then write me fairly."

"Oh, I always do." She rose and gathered his papers, squaring their edges with small precise movements, and handed them back across the table like a surgeon returning instruments. "You'll have your twelve laborers at dawn. And Mr. Vane—when you write your history of us, write Sefeya whole. Not as a discrepancy resolved. As a woman who crossed an ocean twice a year and chose, in the end, to die in a stranger's house rather than a ship's berth, because the stranger's house smelled of bread. She deserves better ink than reconciliation."

She walked him to the door. Below, the tide was coming in over the tidal bridge, black water climbing the stones, and somewhere beyond it the sea cave where—if half his instincts were correct—his answer waited, patient as the drowned.

At the bottom of the path he turned. She stood in the doorway with the light wheeling behind her, and she raised one hand, not in farewell exactly. More like a warning given politely.

Vane pulled his collar up against the wind and began composing, in his head, the sentence that would open Sefeya Renner's entry—and found, to his considerable professional alarm, that he could not decide whether he was writing it to protect a dead woman, or to bait a living one, or simply because some part of him, some part he had never before had occasion to log, refused to let the blank stand.
