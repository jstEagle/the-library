## 12. Mrs. Quill's Testimony

Mirren Quill had delivered her first baby in the winter of 1707, in a fishing hut that smelled of tar and bracken, with the mother's sisters holding candles whose tallow wept down their wrists. The child had been a girl, born blue and furious, and Mirren had slapped her tiny back until she howled loud enough to be heard over the wind, and the father had wept into his beer, and nobody had written anything down. There was no one to write it. There was no paper in the house, no ink, no quill. The girl's name was Sorrel Tice, and she lived to be sixty-one, and she died in the fever year with no stone over her, and the only place her birth was ever recorded — the only place in all the world — was in the memory of an eighteen-year-old midwife who now, forty years on, could not always remember where she'd set her spectacles.

She remembered Sorrel Tice, though. She remembered every one of them.

That was the thing people didn't understand about midwives. Priests kept registers, yes, when they could be troubled; harbormasters kept manifests; the Crown kept its ledgers of taxes and tonnage and men. But the parish register began at the christening font, and half of Greyhaven never reached the font. Born at low tide in a loft above the cooperage. Born in a boat halfway to the herring grounds. Born in the gap between one breath and the next, arriving so fast there was no time for anything but catching. Those children came into the world without witnesses except Mirren Quill, and if she did not carry them, they were not carried at all.

She was thinking about this as she climbed the cliff path to the light in the last grey hour before dark, her basket heavy against her hip with a jar of goose grease, a twist of dried thyme, and — wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, beneath the remedies, where her hands knew they were though she hadn't looked at them since the cottage door — four pages torn from a copybook, covered in Elodie Winterbourne's small upright hand.

The wind had teeth in it now. First storm of the season had come through three nights past and taken slates off the church roof and half the garrison's new timber stock into the harbor, and the air since then had the bruised quality of weather not yet finished with them. Below, the town showed its lights one by one, mean little sparks, and beyond them the water lay black and enormous. Soldiers moved along the quay in pairs, lanterns swinging. They had been counting boats again. She could tell by the way the fishermen stood in their doorways watching them, arms folded, faces shut as mussel shells.

Mirren climbed the way she had climbed for forty years, slowly and without apology, resting twice and pretending both times to be admiring the view.

* * *

Elodie met her at the door with tea already poured, which was hospitality, and with the ledger already open on the table, which was need. Mirren had known Elodie's husband Nathaniel, God keep him, and had known Elodie as a bride with salt-stung cheeks who talked to gulls as though they might answer, and she had watched her become what she was now: a woman who kept the light and kept the town's memory in the same breath, as if the two duties had grown from one root. The lamp turned above them in its glass room with a sound like slow breathing. The fire ticked. On the table, the ledger lay open to a page dense with names, and beside it sat a second book — plain, cheap, unruled — with perhaps a quarter of its leaves filled in the same careful hand.

"You've been copying," Mirren said.

"I've been copying." Elodie pushed the teapot toward her. "Tam ferries pages to the cave when the tide allows. Mrs.—" she stopped herself, corrected, "—when the tide permits, I should say."

"The tide doesn't permit or forbid. It only does what it does." Mirren lowered herself into the chair by the fire, accepted the cup, and let her fingers warm around it. Her knuckles had gone knotted as driftwood these last years; they ached ahead of weather, and they were aching now. "You want my memory."

"I want your memory."

"And you want it written, not just told. Because told fades." Mirren looked at the duplicate ledger, at its patient empty leaves. "You want me to pour forty years through a funnel made of paper."

Elodie didn't pretend otherwise. That was one of the things Mirren loved about her — she had never once, in all the years, tried to make a kindness out of a lie. "The seizure is in eleven days," she said quietly. "Every register, every roll, every record in Greyhaven goes into a crate and onto a cutter for the Crown's clerks to sort. What survives will be whatever serves them. Births of taxable men. Deaths of debtors. Nothing else." Her hand rested flat on the old ledger. "This is everything else."

Mirren drank her tea and said nothing for a while. Outside, the lamp's great lenses wheeled their light across the water, around and around, patient as prayer.

"My mother couldn't write," Mirren said at last. "Did I ever tell you that? Neither could my father, nor any soul in the house they kept on Fennick's Row. When I learned my letters, from the minister's wife, God forgive us both, I thought I had been given something better than gold. Words that stayed. You could put your grandmother's name on a page and it would still be there after you were dust." She set the cup down. "I was a fool about it for years. Thought paper made things true. Then I grew up, and buried children I'd caught with my own two hands whose names never touched paper anywhere, ever, and I understood."

"Understood what?"

"That paper doesn't make things true. Things are true first. Paper only remembers — and only some things, and only when someone powerful wants them remembered." She leaned forward. The fire put orange light along the deep lines of her face. "The Crown can seize every register in Greyhaven, girl, and it will change nothing about what happened here. Bess Mowbray will still have been born on the herring boat in '31. Old Tam's mother — the first Tam, the boy's grandmother — will still have died in the hard frost with her hands folded, asking after a son the sea took twenty years before. Whether anyone's paperwork says so is a matter between the Crown and its own conscience. Names outlive paperwork. They always have. They live in mouths, in stories, in the telling at funerals and the scolding of grandchildren. You cannot seize a memory."

"No," Elodie agreed. "But you can starve one. A story told once dies with the teller. A story written down can travel. Can argue. Can prove." She turned the ledger toward Mirren, and her finger came down on the page, on a name near the top in ink gone brown with age: *Maren Renner, arrived spring '38, no vessel listed.* "Wren's mother is in here because I wrote her here. Nowhere else in England does that line exist. Not in a parish roll, not in a customs book, not in any record the garrison will honor. When they take the registers, they take the last official trace that Maren Renner ever breathed. And Vane—" Elodie's voice caught slightly, and steadied, "—Vane has already begun indexing. His clerks work by daylight in the customs house. If Joss's name appears in the seized papers under the old charges, and Wren's mother appears nowhere at all, then the Crown's account of this town will say that Joss Renner was a smuggler with an unexplained daughter and no wife, no history, no people. It will make Wren a rumor in her own life."

Mirren looked at the name a long moment. Maren. She had not attended Maren's birth — that had happened far inland, in country Mirren never saw — but she had been there for the other end of her, the ending, nine years ago now, in the little house above the shingle with Joss pacing the garden like a man waiting for a verdict. She had held Maren Renner's hand at the last, and promised her, because it was what you promised the dying and it was even mostly true, that she would not be forgotten.

"Then we had better begin," Mirren said, "because my memory is the only register left, and I am seventy-six years old, and I do not intend to take it into the ground unspent."

* * *

They worked until the candle burned down to its collar and Elodie lit another, and then until that one followed.

It went slower than Elodie wanted and faster than Mirren expected. The method was simple enough: Elodie read aloud a name from the old ledger, and Mirren closed her eyes and walked backward into the year of it, and gave back whatever the ledger could not hold. The date of a birth became the room it happened in, the weather, who held the mother's other hand. A death entry became the words the dying woman said, the smell of the sickroom, the thing the family needed done afterward that no one else would do. Elodie's pen hurried after it all, condensing lives into lines, and Mirren marveled and mourned at once to hear forty years come out of her own mouth as though she had been carrying it in baskets all this while and had simply been waiting for somewhere to set it down.

"Annis Corve," Elodie read. "Born March the fourth, 1729. Mother: Tamsin Corve. Father unnamed."

"Twin," Mirren said, eyes shut. "Her sister died before dawn. Never named — too weak to cry, and gone by the time the cock crowed. Tamsin wouldn't have the child told until she was seven, and then the girl grieved like she'd lost her own shadow, which in a manner of speaking she had. Write it: *twin sister, unnamed, same night.* If the Crown won't count her, we will."

Elodie wrote it. *Unnamed twin, same night.*

"Peter Lunney. Drowned June the eleventh, 1733."

"Not drowned," Mirren said sharply, and opened her eyes. "That's exactly the sort of thing I mean, Elodie. The parish roll says drowned because his body washed up under the bridge and drowned men are easier than lost men. He fell. Off the north jetty, drunk, in July — the date's wrong too, it was August, the sixth, because the herring fleet was in and I remember the smell of the catches rotting on the quay in the heat. Peter Lunney slipped and fell, and the tide took him, and calling it drowning makes it sound as if the sea chose him. It didn't. Write *fell.* Let his grandchildren know the difference, if any of them ever get to read this."

*Fell,* Elodie wrote. And then, after a pause, in the margin: *not taken by the sea. Fell.*

They went on. The candle guttered. The lamp overhead turned its endless circles. Somewhere below, a patrol's boots rang on cobbles, faded, were gone. Names surfaced and sank: births the parish never saw, deaths the parish never bothered with, marriages made common-law over a shared hearth because no priest would wed a debtor, baptisms performed by a layman with river water and a good heart during the year the incumbent fled his living. Mirren gave each one its witness. Some she told plainly. Some she could barely speak — the infant in the winter of '41, the third lost to that mother, whom Mirren had wrapped and carried herself through snow to the churchyard's unconsecrated edge because the sexton refused the ground inside the wall to a child unbaptized. She had buried that baby with her own hands, sixty-eight years old and swearing, and she had never told a living soul where, and now she told Elodie, and Elodie wrote it, and the place existed at last outside of one stubborn old woman's keeping.

"There," Mirren said finally, when the telling of it had stopped stinging. "Now it's two places. Do you see what you're doing? You're not making a copy. You're making *witnesses.* A thing seen once is fragile. Seen twice, argued over, carried in two directions — that's harder to kill."

"That's the whole design," Elodie said softly. "The original stays hidden. The duplicate travels. Tam carries pages to the sea cave; from the cave they go inland, piece by piece, to the widow Harsent. By the time the garrison opens the crates, the truth of this town will already be scattered in a dozen hiding places, and no clerk alive can gather it all back."

Mirren studied her across the table — the shadows under her eyes, the ink on her fingers, the set of her jaw. She had watched this woman bury a husband and keep a light through the worst night in thirty years, and she had thought she knew her measure. She revised it upward.

"And Wren?" she asked. "Where does Wren sit in this design?"

Elodie was quiet. The pen rested in her fingers, dripping a small dark bead onto the blotter.

"Wren learns to read from this ledger," she said at last. "I've been teaching her with it. She traces the letters of names she knows — her father's, Tam's, yours. She asked me last week why some entries had lines drawn through them, and I told her the truth: those were corrections. Mistakes owned. And she said—" Elodie's mouth bent, not quite a smile. "She said, *Who checks your mistakes?* Seven years old, and she asked the question Vane will ask me."

"And what did you tell her?"

"That the sea checks them. The tide brings back what it takes." Elodie blotted the bead of ink carefully. "But I'll tell you what I didn't tell her, Mirren, because you'll understand it. When this is over — when the crates go and the decoy sits in some archive and the true ledger is safe — I mean for Wren to carry it. She's the one it was written to protect. Maren's line, Joss's daughter, a girl the Crown's account would erase entirely. Who better to carry the proof of herself than herself? The pages sewn into a coat. Inland, to safety, to the widow. To a future where she can open it and read her mother's name and know that somebody, once, refused to let her be nothing."

Mirren felt her throat tighten. She was too old to hide tears and too sensible to waste them, so she let the one come and wiped it away briskly with the heel of her hand.

"Maren asked me," she said. "At the end. She made me promise she wouldn't be forgotten, and I promised, and I've kept it thin — a mention here, a story told there, a name kept alive in Joss's mouth on bad nights. This—" she laid her knotted fingers gently on the fresh page, on the wet ink of the newest entry, "—this keeps it whole. Finish it, Elodie. Whatever it costs, however many candles, however many nights. Eleven days is nothing. I've caught twins in less."

"We'll finish," Elodie said. "We have to. Because once the seizure date passes, everything not yet copied is only memory again — yours, mine — and memory alone can't cross a border or survive a search. It needs paper. It needs to *travel.*"

* * *

Mirren left well after midnight, declining company on the cliff path on the grounds that she had walked it longer in worse weather and would thank nobody for treating her as cargo. Elodie lent her a hooded lantern anyway, and Mirren took it anyway, and they parted at the door with the particular gravity of conspirators — a nod, a squeeze of inked fingers, nothing said that a listening soldier could have used.

The walk home took her past the square. She had meant to keep her eyes forward; she did not manage it.

In front of the church, by the light of a watch fire, soldiers were feeding papers into a barrel. Surplus records, they called it — duplicates, obsolete rolls, matters already entered elsewhere in the Crown's books. She could not see from the lane what the papers were. She only saw the flames take them, leaf by leaf, curling orange and going up, and she saw the young corporal feed the fire with a kind of boredom, page after page, the way a boy feeds sticks to a bonfire, without reading, without knowing, without caring, that he was burning somebody's entire existence.

Mirren Quill stood in the cold lane with her lantern shuttered and watched until her eyes stung, and then she went on home through the dark, faster than she had come, her basket bumping her hip, the four pages of her testimony riding safe in oilcloth at the bottom.

Names outlive paperwork, she had told Elodie, and it was true, and she believed it.

But watching the fire eat, she understood that it was only true if somebody was left alive to carry the names — and that paper, for all its fragility, for all its lies, was the only raft yet invented that could bear a name across the water between one generation and the next.

Eleven days. She counted them on her fingers all the way up Fennick's Row, and behind her in the square the barrel-fire burned on, and above it, wheeling its steady light over the black water, the lamp kept turning — as if it already knew what was coming and meant to see it through.
