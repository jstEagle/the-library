## 13. The Decoy

The rain had come in from the east that morning, a fine grey sift that made the harbor sound like breathing, and by the time Joss Renner climbed the stair to the lantern room, his shoulders dark with it, Elodie had already laid out the tools of her deception on the trestle table: two quills, the good ink and the poor, a sheaf of paper bought from the stationer in Fennwick with money she had told herself was for lamp oil, and a ruler borrowed from Mrs. Quill's late husband's sea chest, its brass edge worn smooth as a knuckle.

"You look like a woman about to commit a crime," Joss said, wringing water from his cap.

"I am one. Or I have been for thirty years, if you count keeping names the Crown never asked me to keep." She did not smile. "Sit down, Joss. We have eleven days."

He sat. He was a man made uneasy by stillness, Joss Renner—his hands always wanting rope or tiller or the warm neck of a horse—and watching them now, hovering above the clean paper as though the blankness itself might accuse him, Elodie understood that what they were about to do troubled him more than any run along the coast ever had. Smuggling he could justify to himself. Forging a memory could not be justified. It could only be done.

"Show me again," he said. "What goes in. What stays out."

She turned the true ledger toward him, open to the page where, four years past, she had written in her careful upright hand: *Joss Renner, boat *Merrow*, brought in French brandy and salt, paid half his debt to Widow Tarcott. Daughter Wren, born spring tide, March.* Beside it, the name that was not there at all—the name that existed only in Elodie's memory and in Mrs. Quill's, because no priest had been fetched, no parish register opened, and the mother had been dead within the year: *Lian Renner*, whom everyone in Greyhaven had called Lian Renner anyway, because a town decides who a woman is regardless of what the paperwork says.

"The Crown wants shipping records," Elodie said. "Tonnage, customs, wrecks, debts owed to licensed merchants. That is what Vane will look for. So that is what we give him—all of it, true down to the last shilling, because a lie is caught by its seams and I will not have my work called false." She drew the second ledger toward her. "But the people—the ones the garrison means to erase—we leave out. Wren stays off these pages. Lian was never on them. Tam's history goes unwritten. Mrs. Quill's forty years of births and deaths, all the souls the parish never bothered with—" her voice caught, and she mastered it, "—they stay where they have always lived. In here." She touched her temple. "And in the copy that sleeps in the sea cave."

"And if Vane asks why your ledger is thinner than your memory?"

"Then I will tell him what old record-keepers always say when their books fall short: that some things were entered in the parish rolls instead, and the parish rolls went with the curate when the curate went." She met Joss's eyes. "It is even partly true. That is the trick of it, Joss. The best lie is a truth with a door closed in it."

He was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the glass behind them; below, the harbor muttered against the seawall.

"My name," he said finally. "The customs papers they seized—they'll have me on charges that could hang me. Is my name in the ledger we're giving them?"

"It is. It must be. A ledger with no Renner in it would be a ledger with no *Merrow* in it, and no *Merrow* means no fishing fleet, which Vane would notice before he noticed anything else." She reached across the table and put her hand over his restless fingers. "Your name goes in as I wrote it: debts paid, cargo logged, nothing said of where the cargo came from. The ledger does not hang you, Joss. The customs men already have what hangs you. The ledger is only proof you lived here, worked here, paid what you owed. Proof may be the only thing standing between you and a rope."

"Or the proof that finds me," he said. But he pulled a sheet of paper toward him and uncapped the poor ink, because she had taught him this much already: the decoy must be written in many hands, not one, or Vane—who had spent three evenings studying her script as a man studies a face across a crowded room—would see forty years of entries appear in an afternoon.

They worked until the light failed and Elodie lit the lamp overhead, and then they worked by its spilled glow, the two of them bent over the trestle like conspirators in a painting she had once seen in a merchant's parlor and never thought to be living.

* * *

The forgery was a stranger's craft, and she was slow at first. A ledger is not merely writing; it is weather. Ink fades differently in a damp year than a dry one. Hands change—an injury to the wrist, a new quill, the simple settling of age into the joints. Her own entries in the true ledger spanned three decades of small calamities, and to counterfeit them she had to become, in effect, every woman she had ever been: the young bride with her sharp unpracticed strokes, the widow whose hand had gone careful and economical, the older keeper whose letters had begun, she saw now with a pang, to lean eastward like wind-stunted trees.

She aged the paper with cold tea and set pages to dry on lines strung near the stove, and she made herself write errors and correct them, because a perfect ledger is a lying ledger. She blotted. She smudged with a thumb greased deliberately with mutton fat. On the fourth evening she took the razor to the corner of a page and burned a notch into it as though a candle had once leaned too close, and felt, doing it, a horror entirely out of proportion to the act—as though she had singed something living.

Guilt, she learned, does not argue with you. It simply sits down nearby and stays.

On the fifth night Joss found her stopped mid-entry, quill raised, staring at the line she had just written—a false record of grain landed from the *Providence*, entirely accurate, entirely harmless—and unable to remember why it had seemed necessary to make it feel real.

"Elodie." He said her name the way he'd have said *rocks ahead.*

"I am deceiving a man," she said, "whose whole life is deciding what is true. Do you understand what that man does? He reads a town the way I read weather. And I am building him a fog and asking him to sail through it and call it honest sky."

"Aye," Joss said. "And on the other side of that fog is a girl who doesn't exist officially, and a boy who can't speak for himself in any court, and a midwife who's delivered half the children in this county without one entry in any book but yours." He tapped the true ledger, wrapped now in oilcloth and hidden beneath the loose hearthstone they had chosen weeks ago. "You're not lying to save yourself, Orsen—Elodie. You're lying the way a light lies to a ship. It says *safe passage here* so the ship doesn't find the rocks. Let him read the fog."

She laughed then—short, surprised, half grief—and picked up the quill.

* * *

Mrs. Quill came on the seventh day, bringing bread and a jar of goose grease and the particular silence of a woman who knows more than she has been told and has decided to be told nothing.

"He was at the harbor office again yesterday," she said, setting down her basket. "Vane. Asking after birthings. Asked me how long I'd been in practice and whether I kept notes of my own." Her mouth folded inward. "I told him midwives keep their records where God keeps His."

"What did he say to that?"

"He wrote it down." Mrs. Quill unfolded herself onto the bench, joints cracking like green kindling, and looked at the drying lines of tea-darkened paper with the expression of a woman appraising fish at market. "So that's the false one. Which is which?"

"This is the one we give him."

"Hm." She rose, crossed to the lines, and held a page to the light from the lantern room, turning it as though checking a hem. "The ink's too even on this one. See—the third entry down. Nobody's ink dries that evenly on a damp day, and this has been damp weather."

Elodie came to look, and saw it: a column of figures in her own simulated younger hand, uniform as fence posts, while every true page around it in her memory carried the small irregularities of breath and cold and hurry.

"You should have been the forger," she said.

"I've spent forty years writing down babies' weights and mothers' lies," Mrs. Quill said. "I know what an untrue record looks like. It looks tidy." She handed the page back. "Do it again. Make your hand tired. Make it cold. You weren't tidy in 1731 and you know it."

They worked together through the afternoon, the old woman supplying from a memory like a hold full of ballast the details that make a ledger breathe: which winters the ink had frozen in the pot, which year the paper had changed because the Fennwick stationer died and his son stocked a cheaper stock, the fact that Elodie had broken her right wrist in the autumn of '39 and her entries for a month afterward ran left-leaning and cramped. Each detail was a stitch. By dusk the decoy had begun to acquire the one quality Elodie feared most in it: plausibility. It looked like a thing that had been lived in. It looked almost like her life.

"That's enough for today," Mrs. Quill said, gathering her basket. "You've been looking at it too long. A woman can't see her own forgery clear after dark—it starts looking like confession." At the door she paused. "That historian asked me one thing more. Asked if you'd ever lost pages. Fire, water, anything." Her eyes were steady on Elodie's. "I told him the sea takes what it likes from everyone in Greyhaven, and you're no exception."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I lied to the Crown for you. That's a sin I'll settle for at my death, and I'll want it worth the price." And she went down the stair, her footsteps fading into the rain.

* * *

The eighth night, Elodie practiced lying to Aldous Vane's face, though he was twelve hours and half a mile away, asleep presumably in the harbormaster's best bed with his notes squared beside him. She practiced aloud, to the empty lantern room, because Joss had said—wisely, she thought—that a lie unrehearsed is a rope unrepaired.

"The ledger, Mrs. Winterbourne. You are certain this is complete?"

"It is the ledger I have kept since my husband's death, Mr. Vane. Entries before that were in his hand and were lost when the *Grace Alming* went down—his sea chest went with her." True. The old ledger had drowned; she had watched it sink in every sense but the literal.

"And the years before you married? The harbor must have had records."

"Harbor records belong to the harbormaster. My husband's appointment came with the light, not the port." True again. The door closed softly on its true hinges.

"Mrs. Quill tells me you have served this town in other ways. Births. Deaths."

"I am a keeper of lights, Mr. Vane. I keep what is given into my keeping. If neighbors have trusted me with their occasions, those occasions belong to them, not to me, and certainly not to a requisition order." There. The boldest sentence, the one that would either pass as principle or ring as evasion. She said it six times, adjusting tone, until it sounded less like defiance than like a woman stating the plain boundaries of her office.

She stopped, throat aching. Below her the great lens turned, throwing its beam out over the black water, and she thought—not for the first time, but with new bitterness—that a lighthouse is itself a kind of liar. It says *here is safety*. It says nothing of the reefs. Every sailor knows this and trusts it anyway, because the alternative is darkness, and darkness kills faster than deception.

Perhaps Vane would understand that arithmetic. Perhaps that was the most frightening possibility of all: that he might understand it perfectly, and admire it, and forgive her nothing.

* * *

On the tenth evening Joss brought the binding.

He had traded for it in Fennwick—a blank ledger of the same size and make as hers, its cover scuffed convincingly, its spine already softened—and he set it on the table with the odd gentleness of a man laying down a weapon.

"Sewed the signatures loose in two places," he said. "Old books come apart. New-looking bindings raise eyebrows."

Elodie ran her hand over the cover. Then she gathered the finished quires—the product of ten nights, three hands, one old woman's merciless eye—and began to fold them in. Halfway through, her fingers stopped on a page she had nearly forgotten: the entry recording the wreck of the *Kindly Charlotte*, three fishermen drowned, their names written in the true ledger with the date of the finding of the bodies and the depth of the mourning in the town. Those names were in the decoy too. They had to be; a wreckless winter would have been suspicious. But copying them had felt different from omitting Wren. Omission was protection. Copying the dead felt like selling them.

"They're still in here," she said quietly. "The drowned men. In the book I'm handing over."

"Where else would they be?" Joss said. "Dead men need their names in official books worse than living ones do. Living ones have us." He nodded at the oilcloth bundle waiting beneath the hearthstone—the true ledger, destined for Wren's coat and the road inland. "The dead can't run."

She sewed the last signature, broke the thread unevenly, rubbed candle wax into the binding, and set the decoy on the shelf where the true ledger had lived for thirty years, in its place, wearing its shape, ready to answer to her name.

It looked back at her from the shadow of the shelf, and it looked like home, and that was the moral cost of it entire: that she had built a false thing and hung her own likeness on it, and that from tomorrow forward, anyone who looked through that book would believe they knew her.

* * *

Vane came on the twelfth day, earlier than she expected, while the decoy still smelled faintly of the tea she had used to darken its earliest pages and Elodie's hands still bore the faint brown stains of the same. She saw his cutter's boat ground on the shingle and had time for exactly three breaths before his knock came at the door.

"Mrs. Winterbourne." He removed his hat, dripping. Rain stood on his shoulders like a second coat. "I regret the hour. But the seizure is scheduled, and I am instructed to inspect records privately before the formal collection, to spare the town any public confusion."

"Of course." She stepped aside. "There is tea on the stove. Sit by the fire; you're soaked through."

He declined the chair at first, standing with his hands clasped behind him, surveying the room with those quick grey eyes that catalogued everything and confessed nothing—the drying lines (empty now, she had struck the lines the night before), the trestle table (bare), the shelf. Then he sat, accepted tea, and said, without preamble:

"Your ledger, madam. When it is convenient."

Elodie took it down from the shelf. The book weighed almost nothing and felt like lead. She had rehearsed this moment eleven times in the empty lantern room, and none of the rehearsals had included the way his hands received it—carefully, respectfully, the way a priest receives a confession or a thief a purse—and the way he opened it not to the first page but to the middle, as though he already knew the geography.

His eyes moved. Line, column, date. The fire ticked. Somewhere below, Tam's boat knocked against the pilings, once, twice, patient.

"You had a wet autumn in '44," Vane said at last, mildly.

"Drowned the low path for a month. We rowed to church."

"Mm." He turned a page. Turned another. His finger paused—I watched it pause, Elodie thought later, the way one watches a compass needle find north—on the entry for the *Kindly Charlotte*. He read the three names. Read them again, she was nearly certain. Then his finger moved on, and she breathed again, and hated herself for the relief, because it meant she had wanted to fool him, and wanting was halfway to having, and having would make the lie complete.

He closed the ledger at last and set both hands flat upon its cover, as upon a Bible about to be sworn on.

"There is a discrepancy," he said pleasantly, "between this book and my notes. Nothing large. A matter of absence rather than error." He looked up at her, and his eyes were not unkind, which was the worst of it. "I find, Mrs. Winterbourne, that your ledger contains no entry whatsoever for a girl named Wren."

The fire ticked. The beam of the lamp swept overhead, around and around, patient as conscience.

"She is a child," Elodie said, and heard her own voice hold—steady, warm, exactly the voice of a woman telling the plain truth. "Children are not cargo, Mr. Vane."
