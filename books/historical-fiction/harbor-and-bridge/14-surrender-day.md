## 14. Surrender Day

Wren woke before dawn with her father's name caught in her throat like a fishbone.

She had dreamed of the square — she had been dreaming of the square for a fortnight now, every night some variation of it — and in the dream the soldiers' boots made a sound like surf on shingle, and the ledger burned in a barrel, and the names rose up out of the smoke the way gulls rise off a midden, screaming. She lay still in the narrow bed above Mrs. Quill's kitchen and listened to the real harbor breathing below the window, and told herself the thing Elodie had told her every night since Joss was taken: *He is alive. He is whole. We are doing what we said we would do.*

Today was what they had said they would do.

She dressed in the dark, fingers finding the laces by habit, and paused with one hand flat against her ribs. Beneath her shift, beneath her stays, sewn into the lining of her coat where no needle's work would ever be guessed at, lay eleven pages of the true ledger — not all of it, only the heart of it: the births, the drownings, the debts forgiven, and the page that mattered more than all the rest, the one where her mother's name stood written in Elodie's careful hand. *Marin Renner, born of no parish, wife to no record, mother to one daughter living.* Wren had slept in that coat for nine nights. She had learned to sleep without turning over.

Downstairs, Mrs. Quill had already lit the fire and set out bread and cold fish without comment. The old midwife moved slowly these days; her hands ached in weather, and there had been nothing but weather since the equinox. But her eyes were sharp as ever when Wren came down.

"You'll stand where I can see you," Mrs. Quill said. It was not a question.

"By the well," Wren agreed. "Third from the lamp post."

"And if anything goes wrong—"

"Tam takes me out along the quay, and we go to the cave, and I don't come back."

Mrs. Quill nodded once, satisfied, and pushed the bread toward her. "Eat it all. Fainting girls make poor liars."

* * *

Greyhaven had never held so many people.

They began coming down from the garrison at first light, columns of red moving through the grey morning like thread through cloth, and by the time the church bell — the garrison's bell now; the sexton had been made to surrender the key a month past — rang eight, the square was thick with them. Soldiers in two ranks along the north side. Townsfolk pressed together on the south, silent in a way that Wren had never seen a crowd be silent: not the hush of awe or fear exactly, but the held-breath quiet of people watching a surgeon's door. A wagon stood at the center of the square, its bed stripped and lined with oilcloth, ready to receive everything Greyhaven had ever written down about itself.

The seizure had been announced three days after Joss was taken. All registers, rolls, records, and writings of any kind pertaining to trade, tonnage, persons, or property, to be delivered into His Majesty's custody upon pain of fine and imprisonment. The harbormaster had posted it himself, his hand shaking so badly the ink had blurred, and Elodie had read it aloud at the kitchen table in a voice like reading a shopping list, while Tam sat with his fists clenched on his knees and Wren felt the floor tilt under everything she knew.

Now the clerks were setting up trestle tables beside the wagon, and an officer with a list was calling names of institutions — the parish, the customs house, the school, the harbor trust — and men were filing past carrying boxes, armfuls, entire drawers carried between two sweating parishioners who looked as though they were carrying coffins.

Wren stood by the well, third from the lamp post, and did not look at the lighthouse.

It was the hardest thing she had ever done. The light tower stood at the end of the quay the way it had always stood, patient and pale against the low clouds, and she wanted to look at it the way you want to look at your own house when it is burning. Because this morning the working ledger — the decoy, the false book, the lie Elodie had spent six weeks building line by lying line — sat upstairs in the lantern room under a canvas, waiting to come down.

* * *

Elodie came through the square at half past eight, and the crowd parted for her without being asked.

Wren watched her guardian walk the length of it — small, straight-backed, grey hair pinned tight, carrying the ledger wrapped in sailcloth against her chest the way she carried everything, as though weight were simply what arms were for. Wren had seen her carry a drowning man that way once, two winters gone, up from the tideline with the man's vomit soaking her sleeve, and she had not set him down until the surgeon took him, and then she had walked back out and finished trimming the lamp.

This morning her face was nothing at all. That frightened Wren more than any expression could have.

The officer with the list — Captain Hesse, Wren had learned; a man built like a door, with a voice that expected obedience the way stone expected rain — stepped forward. "Keeper Winterbourne. The harbor register."

"Here." Elodie's voice carried further than it should have. She unwrapped the sailcloth with unhurried hands.

Wren knew that book. She had helped make it. Every third entry in it was true, because Elodie had insisted — *a forgery too perfect is a confession*, she'd said, copying by candlelight with her bad hand aching — and every other entry was quietly, carefully wrong. Dates shifted by days. Tonnage rounded down where it would flatter the Crown's expectations and up where it would confuse them. And the margins, which in the true ledger held forty years of lives — *childbed fever, lost; debt to Quill paid in mackerel; drowned off the Bridge, 12th March, God keep him* — the margins of this book were clean as a swept floor. No Wren. No Marin. No Joss Renner at all.

Elodie laid it on the table. The clerks swarmed it like gulls on a dropped fish — weighing it, thumbing pages, counting entries against some master list of their own. Wren's stomach turned itself over and over. Somewhere behind her in the crowd Mrs. Quill stood very still, and Tam stood beside her with his jaw set and his eyes fixed on the middle distance, and neither of them looked at Elodie, because none of them dared.

The senior clerk frowned at a page. Turned back. Frowned again.

Wren stopped breathing.

Then the clerk shrugged — a small, professional shrug, the shrug of a man deciding a discrepancy is within tolerances — and chalked a mark on the crate, and the ledger went into the wagon with the rest of Greyhaven's memory, and the crowd let out its breath all at once, a long ragged sound like the tide going out.

Except Wren wasn't watching the wagon. She was watching Mr. Vane.

* * *

The Crown's historian stood apart from the clerks, the way he always stood apart from everyone, slightly to the left of whatever was happening, in a good coat that the salt air had begun to ruin. He had been in Greyhaven four months now, long enough that Wren had stopped flinching when she saw him in the lane, long enough that he had eaten supper twice at the lighthouse and complimented Mrs. Quill's nettle soup with what might have been sincerity. He was the only soldier-adjacent man in town who took off his hat indoors. He was also, Wren understood now, the most dangerous person she had ever met, because he was the only one who wanted the same thing she wanted — every name, every date, everything remembered — and wanted it for the Crown instead of for the dead.

Vane did not watch the ledger go into the wagon. He watched Elodie.

And Elodie watched the wagon, her hands folded in front of her, steady as a mantelpiece, and gave him nothing.

But Wren was fourteen years old and had spent those years learning to read water, weather, faces, and silences, standing beside a woman who kept a book of everyone — and standing there by the well, third from the lamp post, something opened in her like a door swinging wide. She looked at the square and saw it whole for the first time. Saw the schoolmaster who had taught her letters standing with his empty hands hanging, because his school's records were in the wagon and his school was closed and his pupils scattered. Saw the harbormaster, grey-faced, delivering his own register like a man handing over a hostage. Saw Mrs. Quill, who had buried children the parish never counted, watching forty years of testimony ride away in a cart. Saw Tam, mute since the fever took his words at six years old, whose whole self lived in his hands and in the few lines Elodie had written for him in the true ledger — *Tam, boatboy, found on the tideline Midwinter's Eve, taken in, kept* — and who would have ceased to exist on paper this morning, erased with a chalk mark and a shrug, if one woman hadn't spent six weeks lying by candlelight.

Elodie had built all of it. Not the light — the other light. The one nobody could see. She had spent years writing down every soul that washed through Greyhaven, not because anyone asked her to, not because it profited her a farthing, but because someone had to, because paper was the only country where some of these people had ever been allowed to live. And when the Crown came to burn the country, she had not raged, had not run, had not surrendered. She had built a second one, a false one, and handed it over with steady hands, and stood there in the wind looking bored.

Wren felt her eyes sting and blinked hard and did not cry, because crying in the square today would be a confession too.

*I am going to carry it,* she thought, hand pressed flat against the lining of her coat, against the pages that rode above her heart. *I am the proof. She wrote us down so we would exist, and I am the one who exists.*

* * *

The collection took until noon.

Parish rolls. Tithe books. School registers. The customs house ledgers, which everyone knew contained Joss Renner's name spelled out in evidence, and which went into the wagon without ceremony while Wren stood very still and thought about her father in his cell at the fort and made her face stay smooth. A clerk called the harbor trust's pilot logs. Another collected the gravediggers' book. By the time they finished, the wagon sat deep-laden, and the oilcloth went over it all, and the captain ordered it escorted up to the fort, and the crowd began, slowly, like ice breaking up, to move and murmur and come apart.

That was when Vane crossed the square to Elodie.

Wren saw it happen and could not hear it — the square was full of departing noise, boots and wheels and voices unclenching — but she saw the historian remove his hat, which he did not do for officers, and she saw him bow slightly, and she saw him speak. Elodie answered. Her face remained nothing at all. Vane said something else, longer, and glanced — briefly, professionally — at the wagon disappearing up the hill road, and then back at Elodie, and there was something in the way he tilted his head that made the hair rise on Wren's arms.

He lingered.

He lingered the way a tide lingers at its turn — that moment when the water stops going out and has not yet decided to come in, when everything hangs balanced and breathless. The clerks were packing their tables. The soldiers were forming up. The square was emptying around the two of them as though they stood in the eye of something, and still Vane talked, quietly, courteously, hat in hand, and still Elodie answered in her flat even voice, and Wren, pressed against the well with her heart hammering against the hidden pages, could bear it no longer and moved closer, close enough to catch fragments across the thinning crowd.

"—remarkably consistent work, Keeper. Forty years, and barely a hand's change in the script."

"I write slowly, sir. Always have."

"And yet—" Vane paused, and turned his hat once in his long fingers. "Yet the entries in the final months have a certain… freshness. An eagerness, almost. As though the book were being written *toward* this day rather than away from it. Ink does age quickly in sea air, of course."

"The air ruins everything here, Mr. Vane. Paper most of all."

"Mm," said Vane.

He put his hat back on. He bowed again, deeper than before, and Wren could not tell — she would spend the whole night unable to tell — whether the bow was mockery or respect or both braided together like rope. Then he said one thing more, so quietly that only Elodie and the wind heard it, and whatever it was, it made Elodie Winterbourne's folded hands tighten, just once, just enough for the knuckles to show white before they smoothed again.

Vane turned and walked away up the hill road after his wagon, unhurried, and the sound of his boots on the cobbles was very soft, like surf very far away.

* * *

Elodie did not speak until they reached the lighthouse. She climbed the stair ahead of Wren, all ninety-one steps, and only in the lantern room, with the great lens turning its slow patient circles overhead and the grey afternoon light broken into drifting shards around them, did she stop and lean against the rail and let out a breath that shook, just once, at the very end of it.

"Well," she said. "It's done."

"He knew." Wren's voice came out smaller than she meant. "In the square. He knew."

Elodie was quiet for a while. Below them the harbor lay flat and pewter-colored, emptied of the soldiers, the wagon a dark speck crawling up the coast road toward the fort. Tam was somewhere on the water in his boat, keeping his appointed distance, and Mrs. Quill was at her window with the shutters cracked, watching the road, and somewhere inland a girl named Wren was supposed to be walking toward safety with a book inside her coat — except that the girl was here, and the book was here, because the departure had been set for tomorrow's tide, and today they had all just had to stand in a square and watch a lie get carried away in triumph.

"Mr. Vane knows many things," Elodie said at last. "Knowing is his profession. What he does with what he knows—" She stopped. She looked out at the horizon, where the sky had begun to bruise along the east, another storm gathering out past the shoals, the season not done with them yet. "He asked me a question before he left. You didn't catch it."

"What question?"

"He asked me," Elodie said, "how many pages a book like mine holds. How many, precisely. He'd counted the leaves in the ledger they took to the fort. And mine comes up short by eleven."

The lens turned. Light swept the room, and passed, and left them in the grey.

"Wren," Elodie said gently, turning to face her, and her face was no longer nothing at all — it was old, and tired, and fierce, and it was the only face in the world Wren trusted. "Get your things, child. You leave on tonight's tide, storm or no storm."
