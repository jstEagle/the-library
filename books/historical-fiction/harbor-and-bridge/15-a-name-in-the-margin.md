## 15. A Name in the Margin

The requisitioned customs house on Quay Lane had been built for a smaller ambition than the Crown now brought to Greyhaven. Its single window admitted a grey, grudging light that did nothing for reading, and so Aldous Vane worked by candle even at midday, three tapers set in a brass stick he had carried from London and would not surrender to the quartermaster's general appetite. Around him rose the paper carcass of a conquered town: harbor registers bound in cracked calf, tithe rolls, parish books with their vellum gone the color of old teeth, manifests copied in hands that ranged from clerkly elegance to a fisherman's drunken scrawl. He had catalogued eleven crates of it in nine days. His clerks—two lieutenants detailed to him more for their legible penmanship than their intellects—catalogued by rote. Vane catalogued by appetite.

He had wanted this work since he was a boy in Norfolk, transcribing gravestones while other boys threw stones at crows. There was a pleasure in it that he had never confessed to anyone, least of all to himself in plain words: the pleasure of standing at the last hour of something. Every record was a life that had stopped being lived and started being written. The victor's historian did not make history; he embalmed it, and Vane took an artisan's satisfaction in good embalming.

The Winterbourne ledger lay before him under his own clean hands.

It had come to him among the surrendered records on Collection Day, delivered into Lieutenant Harkess's canvas sack with all the others, and Vane remembered the moment precisely because he had been watching Elodie Winterbourne rather than the ledger itself. She had carried it down the church steps herself—not the harbormaster, who surrendered his registers with the relieved obsequiousness of a man handing over a debt; not the priest, who wept quietly over his parish book until a corporal took it from him with embarrassed firmness. She had descended each step evenly, the ledger held against her hip like a child accustomed to being carried so, and had placed it in the sack without comment. Her hands had been steady. That steadiness had pleased Vane then, as a craftsman's work pleases another craftsman, and he had recorded it in his private notebook: *Keeper surrenders her own register without protest. Either resignation or discipline. Determine which.*

Nine days later, he believed he had determined which, and the answer troubled him the way a single wrong note troubles a musician—not loudly, but ceaselessly.

He turned to the first leaf and read again the inscription he had already committed to memory: *Greyhaven Harbor, Being a True Account of Vessels, Cargo, Souls, and Weather, Kept by E. Winterbourne, Widow, from the Year 1741.* Beneath it, in the same hand: *What is written is owed.*

*What is written is owed.* He had found the phrase in three other places through the volume, always at the head of a new year's section, like a motto renewed. It was not scripture. He had checked. It was hers.

Vane dipped his pen, thought better of marking the page, and blotted the nib on the cloth instead. He would write nothing in this book. Some objects deserved better than annotation, and besides—an instinct older than his training told him that this particular document rewarded the reader who touched it lightly.

He began at the beginning and went through slowly, the way he had once walked a battlefield after the guns fell silent, reading the ground.

* * *

The ledger was a marvel, and Vane resented it slightly for being one. Official histories pretended that the past was composed of great men moving great events; any honest archivist knew it was composed of weather, debts, births, and drownings, and here all four were entered in a single consistent hand with the economy of a woman who had no candle wax to waste. Fishing crews out and home. The brig *Providence*, lost on the Teeth with seven souls, their names given individually—given *individually*, which no harbor register in England bothered to do—with what each man had owed the chandlery struck through in careful strokes and annotated *forgiven, Michaelmas*. A deserter from the *Hector* sheltered through a February, logged only as *the Irishman, mends nets, eats little*. Births. Deaths. A running census of a town the Crown had never considered worth counting.

Vane had interviewed her twice now, once aboard the cutter and once in the lamp room with the gale still rattling in the glass behind them, and both times she had answered his questions about shipping traffic with a precision that had seemed almost flirtatious in its perfection—tonnage recalled to the half-last, dates fixed by tides. He had admired it. Admiration was dangerous in his profession; he knew that better than most, having watched two colleagues rise on flattery and one fall for it. But he had admired her the way he admired a well-built breakwater: professionally, at a distance, expecting nothing from it but continued function.

Now, at the bottom of page forty-one, he stopped turning leaves.

The entry concerned a vessel called the *Merry Alice*, a coaster, arrived from Falmouth in the spring of 1744 with salt and leather. Ordinary in every respect except that the ink was wrong.

Not obviously wrong. Vane had spent eleven years learning inks the way other men learned faces—the slow browning of iron-gall, the way oak-gall aged toward rust while logwood faded toward grey, the particular bloom of a fresh entry against the matte patience of an old one. The *Merry Alice*'s line sat on the page with its neighbors plausibly enough. But where every surrounding entry had settled into the paper, its fibers long since grown around the writing like flesh around a splinter, this line still floated faintly upon the surface. And the color was a shade too warm. Someone had matched it carefully—had tested, perhaps, or possessed a very good eye—but iron-gall blackens with time, and time does not negotiate.

He fetched his magnifier from the instrument case, a heavy French lens he had bought in Boulogne with money he should have spent on coats, and bent close.

Under magnification the matter ceased to be conjecture. The pen pressure of the *Merry Alice* entry differed subtly from the entries above and below—a fraction lighter, the down-strokes less bitten into the page—and there, at the termination of the final letter, a tiny pooling of ink where a hand had paused a heartbeat too long before lifting. Fresh ink pools when it hesitates. Old ink has finished hesitating years ago.

Vane sat back. The candle flames leaned in the draft from the ill-fitting shutter and righted themselves.

One anomalous entry proved nothing. Clerks re-inked damaged pages; damp blurred lines and owners retraced them; he had seen a hundred legitimate restorations in a hundred records. He forced himself through the whole volume again, slowly, hunting with the lens, and found five further entries with the same warm cast and floating quality, scattered across three years, none adjacent to another, all concerning vessels or persons otherwise unremarkable: a ketch, a coal hoys' cargo note, two fishermen's names added to a crew list, and—

He stopped.

A name. Not a vessel. A single name entered alone at the foot of a page dated March 1743, in the space usually reserved for remarks on weather: *Marren, Lise, of this place.* No surname beyond it, no vessel, no purpose. Just the name, set down where weather should be, in ink that had not dried in 1743 because it had not been spilled in 1743.

Vane knew the name. He had met it four days earlier, going through the seized customs papers with Lieutenant Pell, who indexed with the diligence of a man who believed indexing was patriotism. Among the depositions against one Joss Renner, smuggler, of Greyhaven—charges sufficient to hang him if the Admiralty cared to press, and the Admiralty generally cared—was a deposition sworn by a revenue rider naming one *Lise Marren* as Renner's woman and accomplice, deceased these six years or more. Pell had flagged the name because the rider's papers were the only place in the entire documentary record of Greyhaven where it appeared. No parish entry. No burial roll. Nothing. The woman existed in ink exactly once, in an accusation.

And now here she was a second time, in a ledger supposedly kept continuously since 1741, in a hand pretending to be four years old.

Vane closed the ledger and placed both palms flat upon its cover, as if the book might breathe.

* * *

He walked out along the quay that evening without deciding to. It was a habit he permitted himself—one circuit at dusk, counting masts, noting what the garrison's horses had trampled and what the tide had left—and he told himself the direction of his walking was incidental. The lighthouse stood at the harbor's elbow on its black shoulder of rock, connected to the town by the tidal causeway the locals called the bridge, though it was neither bridge nor quite causeway but a spine of stone the sea crossed twice a day like a landlord collecting rent.

She was on the gallery. He saw her silhouette against the first lamps of the town, small and upright, and he did not approach. He had learned more from not approaching in his career than most historians learned from inquiry.

Instead he let his mind do what it did, which was arrange.

Consider, he told himself, leaning on the seawall while the water worried the pilings below. A widow keeps an unofficial record for six years. The Crown announces seizure. She complies without protest—too readily, he had noted it himself. Nine days later the surrendered volume contains eight entries in recent ink, scattered and deniable, including the sole other appearance of a name that exists nowhere else except in papers that could hang a local man. Conclusion the first: she had reconstructed the volume from a duplicate or memory and introduced errors. Conclusion the second: she had surrendered a forgery entire. Conclusion the third—

Conclusion the third was the margin mark, and it was the one that kept him at the seawall long after his fingers went cold.

He had found it on the second pass. At the foot of page forty-one, beside the *Merry Alice*, a small tick of ink: a short downward stroke crossing a horizontal, like a keel cutting water—or like a check mark, though she could not have known anyone would check. But it was not the mark itself that arrested him. It was that he had seen its siblings. Throughout the volume, at irregular intervals beside certain entries—always entries concerning people rather than cargo: the drowned crew of the *Providence*, the Irishman who mended nets, births, deaths, forgiven debts—recurred a small signature of habit, a tick drawn in the same motion every time, quick and decisive, the stroke of a woman who made her mark and moved on. Vane had catalogued thirty-one of them before he consciously understood he was cataloguing anything. Habits of hand were as identifiable as habits of face; he had once unmasked a fabricated regimental history in Chatham because the forger drew his flourishes left to right and the genuine hand always right to left.

The tick beside the *Merry Alice* was Elodie Winterbourne's tick. Recent. Deliberate. Beside an entry that should not have existed in a book she claimed never to have rewritten.

Which meant she had not surrendered a forgery. She had surrendered the true ledger *edited*—entries added, perhaps, or restored, the false made native to the true. And the only rational reason to forge additions to a genuine record, to bury fresh ink among old, was to establish that certain facts had always been known. That certain people had always been recorded.

*Marren, Lise, of this place.*

Vane looked up at the light. It had kindled while he stood there—he had watched without seeing the shutters open—and the beam swung out over the darkening sea, patient and impartial, touching everything equally: the fishing fleet, the garrison's anchored transport, the horizon where nothing yet showed. A woman kept that light. A woman also, evidently, kept something else, and the Crown had taken the wrong thing, or rather had taken exactly what she had chosen to give, and called the taking complete.

He should report it. That was the arithmetic of his position, simple as a merchant's column. He wrote the official history; Colonel Ashe commanded the occupation and wanted, in the Colonel's phrase, *a clean paper town*; a keeper falsifying surrendered records was insubordination if not sedition, and the man whose fate hung on the name in question—Renner, the smuggler—was already in the cells beneath the old granary awaiting the Assizes that would arrive with the spring packets. One sentence in one dispatch and Mrs. Winterbourne's precision would acquire a very different value, and the name Lise Marren would return to the only place the official record had ever held it: a deposition, an accusation, a warrant.

He should report it, and he stood at the seawall understanding with uncomfortable clarity that he did not want to.

This was the trouble with reading everything, Vane thought. You learned what the omissions cost. Pell and his indexes saw names as charges. Vane had spent thirty years among gravestones and gutted archives, and he knew that a person unpainted by any record simply stopped, retroactively, at the edge of their last document—that Lise Marren, dead six years, existed now only as an accusation, and that somewhere a child—there was a child, the Renner girl, Wren, whom he had seen twice on the quay, sharp-eyed and silent—would grow to womanhood with a mother attested solely by a revenue rider's oath. The ledger said otherwise. The ledger said, in ink pretending to age, *she was of this place*. Someone had decided that a lie about the past was the only honest way to keep a truth in it.

It was elegant. It was, he admitted, leaning there in the salt wind, almost unbearably elegant. He had spent his career among forgers and knew the species: the vain ones, the greedy ones, the frightened ones. This was none of those. This was forgery as memorial—as testimony—the record weaponized against its own seizure. If he had encountered it in the archives of a defeated enemy he would have written it up for the Society of Antiquaries with genuine delight.

But she had deceived him. That was the fact under all the admiration, and Vane did not forgive deception, principally because he could not afford to; a historian who forgave it was a historian who would eventually commit it. And there was the deeper question, the professional question, the one that mattered to what he was building: if eight entries were restored, what else was absent? What had she *left out* of the surrendered book? A ledger edited to include the dead might also have been edited to exclude the living. Names that could hang. Routes that could convict. The true account of Greyhaven's commerce, which everyone on this coast understood had always run on two ledgers, the legal and the liquid.

He turned from the seawall at last. Behind him the light swept round, round again, keeping its indifferent time.

He would say nothing to Colonel Ashe. Not yet. A premature report would only produce soldiers, and soldiers solved documents with fire—he had watched the surplus burning in the square with his jaw tight and his hands in his pockets, saying nothing, hating his own silence and unable to justify ending it. No. This required a subtler instrument. This required the thing the Crown paid him for and rarely understood: not the record itself, but the interrogation of the person who kept it.

He climbed Quay Lane to his lodgings, lit his candles, and drew from his case a sheet of his own good paper. He wrote three lines requesting the honor of a further interview with Mrs. Winterbourne regarding discrepancies between the harbor register and the customs depositions—*discrepancies*, a word chosen to tell her everything and prove nothing—and signed it with the full formality of his office. Then, before sealing it, he turned the sheet over and, on the verso, in pencil, in a hand far less formal, wrote a single note to himself that no clerk would index:

*Ask her not what the ledger hides. Ask her why she thought hiding was necessary. Then listen.*

He sealed the letter, set it for the morning packet, and snuffed the candles one by one until only the street lantern's glow came through the shutters, thin and yellow as old ink. In the dark he could see the lighthouse beam pass the gap, again and again, and he lay listening to the sea work the bridge at low water and thinking, with the particular excitement he had never learned to distrust, that he had finally found in this shabby occupied town a document worthy of his whole attention—and that the woman who made it had known, from the moment she placed it in the canvas sack with steady hands, exactly what his eye would find and how long finding it would take.

She was not merely ahead of him. She was measuring him.

Mr. Aldous Vane, victor's historian, fell asleep composing the questions he would ask her, and woke once in the night with the unreasonable conviction that somewhere across the harbor she was awake too, trimming a wick, adding a name.
