## 16. Joss Is Taken

They came for him on a Tuesday, in the grey hour when the tide was out and the harbor mud lay exposed like something the sea had disowned.

Joss Renner had always known they would come. A man who lived half his life outside the law made a kind of peace with that knowledge early—wore it close, the way other men wore a knife or a charm against drowning. He had imagined the moment a hundred times: soldiers at his door, Wren's face turning toward him in question, and himself saying something steady and fatherly even as the irons went on. In his imaginings there was always time for a last word, a blessing, a joke to soften it. There never was, in life. That was the first thing the cell taught him: imagination was a liar with a soft heart.

The second thing the cell taught him was how long a night could be when you had nothing left to guard except your silence.

* * *

It began ordinarily enough, which he would think about afterward and understand was part of the design of it. He had come up from the boat at first light with a crate of salt herring, honest catch for once—he'd been running clean since the cutter arrived, since Vane's clerks had begun their patient indexing of the customs house papers—and his shoulders ached from the carrying, and the gulls were making their usual argument over the quay, and he was thinking about breakfast and about whether Elodie would want the good fish or whether he should sell those and bring her the bruised ones she'd only pretend not to notice.

He saw the red coats come around the fish sheds and his body knew before his mind did. Four of them, moving without hurry, spreading slightly as they came the way men spread who have done this before. A sergeant in front with a paper folded in one hand, and behind him—a detail Joss's stomach registered with cold finality—one of Vane's clerks in civilian black, thin as a rule line, carrying a ledger under his arm like a hymnbook.

"Joss Renner," the sergeant said. Not a question.

"That's me." He set down the crate of herring carefully, because his hands wanted to do something else with them and he knew what that something would cost him. "If it's about the excise stamp on the herring, I've got the paper—"

"It isn't about the herring."

The clerk opened his ledger. Joss watched the pages turn—watched, with a strange floating clarity, his own history being read back to him by a stranger's finger. The Renner name. The Renner name again. Entries dated years back, from seized customs papers he had assumed were rotting in some basement or burned by now, arranged instead into neat columns by patient hands. Cargo landed unmanifested. Cargo landed unmanifested. The Brindle Girl, twice, in '43. The Merrow's Debt. Names of buyers, some of them dead, two of them magistrates. Dates cross-referenced against tide tables—which meant someone had gone to the trouble of proving where his boat *could* have been on a given night by consulting the moon and the water, and Joss felt something between horror and unwilling admiration for the industry of it. They hadn't just found his crimes. They had reconstructed them, tidied them, given them an architecture.

"There are eleven counts here," the clerk said. His voice was mild, unhurried, the voice of a man reading a shopping list. "Several carry the capital penalty. The Crown's attorney will wish to ask you about your associates."

"My associates." Joss almost laughed. "I worked alone mostly. Smuggling's not a committee business."

"The papers disagree." The clerk turned a page. "There is also the matter of your daughter."

The quay seemed to tilt. Joss kept his eyes on the ledger, on the neat black entries, and did not let them rise. "My daughter's got no more to do with smuggling than the sergeant's mother does."

"No charge against her," the clerk said smoothly. "Merely a question of her whereabouts. She is not enrolled in any parish register. No school record. No baptism. As far as the Crown's records extend, Joss Renner has no daughter at all—which raises the natural question of where the child actually lives, and with whom, and under whose care. The garrison has a duty to establish the status of persons residing in occupied territory. Unregistered persons especially."

He understood then, standing in the fish-smelling morning light with his hands finally free and useless at his sides, that this was the trap entire. The smuggling charges were real—they could hang him on those alone—but they were also the lever. What they wanted was not his confession. Confessions hung men and closed files, and files were not what Mr. Aldous Vane was here to write. What Vane wanted was the town, whole and complete and accounted for, every soul entered into his official ledger, every gap explained, every person who had lived outside the paper walls of Empire brought inside or erased. And a girl who existed nowhere in any record but one old woman's private book—that girl was a hole in the historian's map, and holes were the one thing men like Vane could not abide.

"Where is Wren?" the sergeant asked, and it was plain from his voice that the question had been scripted for him, that the clerk stood behind it the way a hand stands behind a puppet.

"She's with her aunt. Up the coast." The lie came easily because he had prepared it years ago, laid it down like ballast. "Her mother's people."

"What was her mother's name?"

"Maren." It was true, at least. Everything else about Maren was nowhere but in Elodie's book and in his own chest, and he would keep it there.

* * *

The cell was under the old customs house, which amused him in a hollow way—he'd walked past that building a thousand times with a cargo hold full of brandy and felt nothing but professional satisfaction. Now he lay on a plank bed and listened to the harbor sounds come through the high barred window: the slap of water, the cry of gulls, the groan of mooring lines. Sounds he had lived by for forty years. They sounded no different heard through stone. That was somehow the worst of it. The world went on being the world, indifferent as tide.

They questioned him three times the first day. He gave them his prepared answers—the aunt up the coast, the dead wife's family, a childhood fever that explained why the girl had never been baptized, God rest whichever lie needed resting. He gave them nothing new. By the third session the sergeant's patience had thinned to wire, but the clerk remained courteous, and courtesy frightened Joss more than shouting ever had, because shouting was weather and courtesy was climate.

"You're protecting someone," the clerk said on the second evening, when they brought him bread and small beer and left the lamp burning. "That's understandable. Fathers protect daughters. But consider what you protect her *from*, Mr. Renner. At present she exists in a legal void. If anything happened to you—and I say this with regret, but the charges are what they are—she would be nobody's child in the eyes of the law. No inheritance. No claim. No name. Whereas if she were properly recorded—if her circumstances were documented, if she were attached to a household of good standing—" he smiled faintly at some private calculation— "the Crown can be generous. Registration is protection, Mr. Renner. We are offering to make your daughter real."

"She's been real for thirteen years," Joss said. "She was born on a Tuesday in March in a storm that took the roof off the Quill's woodshed, and Mrs. Quill caught her, and Elodie Winterbourne logged her birth in a book she keeps because the parish couldn't be bothered to sail out here most Sundays. My daughter doesn't need your ink to exist. She needs you to leave her alone."

The clerk wrote that down. Joss realized with a lurch that everything was being written down, every word, every refusal, and that somewhere across the town Vane would read it in the morning and adjust his instruments accordingly.

* * *

On the fourth night they moved him to the church, because the cells were wanted—more arrests coming, apparently; the occupation was finding its rhythm—and a makeshift brig of hurdles and rope had been set up in what used to be the vestry. He could hear soldiers beyond the wall, dice and grumbling, and through the east window, above the storehouse crates stacked where the pews had been, a sliver of harbor. If the night was clear he could see the far-off pulse of the Greyhaven light, sweeping its patient arc, and he fixed his eyes on it the way he had once fixed them on stars, and told himself the same things sailors tell themselves: steady, steady, it comes round again.

Elodie came to see him on the sixth day. He heard her before he saw her—the particular sound of her boots, decisive even on stone—and then Mrs. Quill's slower step beside hers, and the soldier at the door consulted somebody and let them into the nave as far as the hurdle barrier, on the pretext that the prisoner required spiritual consolation. Perhaps he did. He stood at the barrier and looked at the two women who were the nearest thing he had to family, and neither of them pretended this was a pastoral visit.

"They've set the hearing for the assizes," Elodie said quietly. "Three weeks. Michaelmas sessions, sitting at Harwick."

"Harwick." He turned the word over. Harwick meant a real court, a hanging judge if the docket was ugly, and eleven counts of unmanifested cargo with witnesses already indexed was ugly. "Well. I've beaten worse odds in weather."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it and reformed. "Don't do that. Not with me."

He reached through the barrier and took her hand. Her fingers were cold, lamp-oil scented, steady as ever, and he held them the way he might hold a line in heavy sea. "Listen to me, Elodie. Whatever they offer me—whatever terms—they'll phrase it so it costs you something. They know the book exists. The clerk asked me about it straight out, day one. I told him I'd never seen it. They don't believe me, which is fine; I didn't expect them to. But hear this: whatever they put to you about me, about Wren, about the ledger—the answer is no. You keep your lamp lit and your book shut and your mouth shut, and you get that girl inland where no subpoena reaches. Swear it."

"I won't swear to leave you here to hang."

"You'll swear it anyway." He was surprised how calm his voice came out, how much it sounded like his father's voice giving orders in a gale thirty years gone. "Elodie. Look at me. Eleven counts. Even if I named every buyer living and dead it wouldn't clear five of them, and it would pull half this coast into the net with me—Mrs. Quill's cousin, the harbormaster's brother, men whose grandkids are sitting in your kitchen eating my fish right now. My neck isn't worth that arithmetic, and I'm the only one in this room allowed to do the sums on it."

Mrs. Quill leaned close then, and her old voice was dry as seed husks but her eyes were wet. "Your mother birthed you in my hands, Joss Renner, and I'll not watch them bury what I caught. We'll think of something."

"There's nothing to think of. There's only what to refuse." He squeezed Elodie's fingers once and let go. "Now go on. The light doesn't keep itself, and neither does everything else you keep."

* * *

But it was later—much later, deep in the eighth night, lying on the plank bed in the dark with the harbor light sweeping past the window in its slow metronome—that the true shape of his situation came to him whole, the way a coastline comes up out of fog all at once when the wind shifts.

It was not the sergeant who held his life. Sergeants were weather. It was not even the Crown's attorney at Harwick; attorneys argued what papers showed, and papers showed plenty, but papers could be argued with, delayed, lost. No. The man who held Joss Renner's fate was the quiet one with the ledger under his arm and the courteous voice and the hunger behind his spectacles. Aldous Vane. Historian. Because Vane was writing a book about Greyhaven—its capture, its occupation, its transformation from den of smugglers into port of the Crown, that was surely how the chapter would run—and in such a book, what was a convicted smuggler? A footnote. An illustration. A cautionary example, useful precisely *because* he swung. Unless—

Unless Vane decided he was worth more alive. Worth more as a source. Worth more as a trade.

And that thought, which should have been hope, landed in Joss's chest like cold iron, because he understood what Vane would price him at. Not testimony. Not names of buyers—those the clerks already had. Vane would price him at Wren. At the gap in the record. At the one unregistered soul in Greyhaven and the woman who had written her into existence in a private book. Joss Renner's life was currency now, and the mint that stamped it wanted exactly the thing Joss had spent thirteen years building a fortress around.

He lay in the dark and did the hardest work of his life, harder than any run through excise cutters in foul weather: he searched himself for the place where his resolve might crack, and found it immediately, because of course it was shaped exactly like his daughter. Thirteen years old. Freckled. Read now, thanks to Elodie—read actual books, argued about them, had opinions about Captain Cook. A girl the law insisted did not exist. If they came to him and said, confess the ledger's location and her guardianship and we will commute the sentence to transportation—seven years, Australia, survival odds fair, and you could even see her before the ship sails—

He turned his face to the wall.

"I'm sorry," he said aloud, to no one, to everyone—to Elodie asleep in the tower, to Wren wherever Tam's ferrying had hidden her, to Maren ten years drowned and still the loudest voice in his head. "I'm sorry. Forgive me. Whatever I have to be to keep her safe, I'll be it."

The harbor light swept past the window. Paused in its arc, the way it always seemed to pause, though he knew it never did. Came round again.

And far across the sleeping water, in a stone tower he could not see, Elodie Winterbourne sat awake over her working ledger with the pen dried in her hand, because she too had done her arithmetic that night, and had arrived by her own road at the same cold sum: that the case against Joss Renner would never go to Harwick at all unless someone wanted it to; that the historian's clerks indexed papers but historians chose which pages to act upon; and that somewhere in the requisitioned customs house, in a neat hand, Aldous Vane was holding a man's life in suspension the way she held a flame in glass—against wind, against darkness, waiting to see what it would illuminate, or what it could be made to purchase.

She blew out her candle and sat in the dark, and did not sleep.
