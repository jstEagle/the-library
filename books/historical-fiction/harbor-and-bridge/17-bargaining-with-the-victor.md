## 17. Bargaining with the Victor

Elodie had rehearsed the walk a hundred times in her head, and still her feet nearly turned back at the door of the customs house. The garrison had claimed it for their own within a fortnight of landing—the old ledgers' shelves emptied and restacked with muster rolls, the smell of tallow replaced by lamp oil and boot blacking—and now it was Vane's kingdom, this room of paper where men were reduced to tonnage and cargo.

She stood in the doorway a long moment, sea wind still in her shawl, and let herself be afraid. Fear was an instrument like any other; she had learned that at the lamp. A steady hand did not mean an untroubled heart. It meant a heart that knew how to burn without shaking.

The soldier on duty announced her—*the lighthouse keeper, sir*—and Mr. Aldous Vane looked up from his desk with the particular stillness he always showed her first, as if each visit required him to reassemble his face before wearing it.

"Mrs. Lockridge." He rose, gestured to the chair across from him. "You've come at a strange hour."

"The tide decides most of my hours." She sat. Her hands folded themselves in her lap without instruction, and she was grateful to them for it. "But I came about something else. About Joss Renner."

Vane resumed his seat. On the desk between them lay the indexed papers—she could see the tabs of Joss's name, the neat clerk's hand, the little crosses in the margins where charges had been tallied against a man like entries in a grocer's book. *Running unlit cargo. Conspiracy with persons unknown. Resistance to His Majesty's officers.* Enough, on paper, to hang him. Paper had always been able to do what rope could not: reach backward through years and close around a throat.

"You are acquainted with Renner," Vane said. Not a question.

"He's fished my stretch of coast for eleven years. He's carried wood up the cliff path when my hands were too split to hold a hammer. He has a daughter."

"He has a daughter," Vane agreed, softly, in the tone of a man confirming a figure in a column. "Who is, I understand, no longer residing where one would expect to find her."

Elodie kept her face still. Wren had gone inland over a week ago now, Tam's hands on the tiller before dawn, the girl's coat heavy with sewn pages. Only Mrs. Quill and the widow knew. And now this man suspected—or worse than suspected.

"She's been sent to family," Elodie said. "Children are sent to family. That is what children are, in part: things that can be sent."

"And you are the family she was sent from, or the family she was sent to?" He asked it gently, which was the worst of him. Vane's cruelty, if it was cruelty, wore the dress of courtesy. He poured her tea without being asked, and she watched his hands—precise, unhurried, a historian's hands—and understood that everything in this room was arranged the way he arranged an archive, each object placed to produce an effect upon its viewer.

"I came to make you an offer, Mr. Vane. I'd rather not spend the hour fencing."

Something moved behind his eyes—interest, quick and genuine, the scholar's hunger showing through the officer's armor. He set down the pot. "Then I am listening."

* * *

She had chosen the customs house because it had tables, and tables meant documents, but as she began she found herself speaking the way she spoke at the lamp when the weather closed in—plainly, steadily, with the certainty of someone who had decided there was nothing left to lose by truth.

"You have the harbor register. You have the customs seizures. You have your clerks indexing names into columns." She laid her palm flat on the desk, over the tabs. "What you do not have is Greyhaven."

"Mrs. Lockridge—"

"Let me finish. You'll want to, when I'm done, because I think you already know I'm right." She leaned forward. "Your register says a fishing crew of five went down off the Teeth in March of '41. Five names. I can tell you that the youngest, Peter Amsel, was seventeen and had a habit of whistling while he worked that drove his brother mad, and that the brother, Daniel, never whistled again after that March, and died himself two winters later—not of grief, the town said, though the town said it quietly. Your register says five drowned. I can give you the shape of every one of those lives, because I wrote them down. Not the Crown's version. Mine."

Vane had gone very still. She pressed on.

"There are forty-one souls buried on the north point whose births were never entered in any parish book, because the parish priest drank and then died and was replaced by another who cared more for his tithe than his flock. There are women married on the beach by common consent who exist nowhere in law. There are children—" her voice caught, and she let it, deliberately; she had decided that if she must bargain, she would bargain with everything, including her own griefs "—children born in storms by lamplight, whose mothers' names appear nowhere but in a ledger I have kept for twenty-two years. Debtors. Deserters. Drowned sailors. A whole town's memory that the Crown is burning in your square by the sackful, and calling it order."

The fire in the grate popped. Outside, a sentry called the half-hour.

"You burned 'surplus' papers last week," Elodie said. "I watched from the cliff. Do you know what was in them? Baptismal records. Widow's petitions. A man's letter home, unread, from a transport that sank. Ash doesn't argue. Ash doesn't testify. When your history is written, Mr. Vane, Greyhaven will begin the day your cutter anchored, and everyone who lived here before will be either numbers or silence."

Vane's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "The requisition orders were clear. Unofficial records breed sedition—"

"They breed people," Elodie said. "That's what they breed. People. Which is precisely what an occupation cannot afford, so it burns them down to tonnage and headcounts and calls the ash tidy."

Silence stretched between them. Then she played her card, and her hand was steadier than she had any right to expect.

"I know what you're building. I've read enough of your kind of writing to know the architecture. A history of the occupation—victorious, orderly, complete. And I am offering you the only thing that will make it true instead of merely finished." She drew a breath. "My testimony. All of it. Every name, every birth, every debt, every drowning, sworn before witnesses, entered into your official record where no one can strike it out—because you told me once yourself, Mr. Vane, and I believed you, that a thing written into a Crown history is harder to erase than a thing carved in stone. Give me that. In exchange—"

"Renner," Vane said quietly.

"Renner walks free. The charges die or drown or whatever happens to charges when a historian loses a page—which I imagine is a thing you know how to arrange." She held his gaze. "And my—*and the girl* is left alone. No inquiries. No follow-ups. No clever questions asked of mute boys on bridges. Safe passage, safe silence, and when your book is published, she appears in it if she appears anywhere—as a child of Greyhaven, raised at the light, loved by the town. Not as contraband. Not as evidence."

The word *daughter* she had swallowed, and saw by his eyes that he noticed the swallow, catalogued it, filed it away in whatever inexhaustible archive the man carried behind his face.

* * *

He did not answer at once. Instead he rose and went to the window, and Elodie studied him the way one studies weather—a thing that might turn.

She had thought, walking down, that she knew what she would see in him: ambition, smooth and self-serving, a man who would weigh a smuggler's neck against a footnote and find the arithmetic pleasing. But what she saw in his shoulders, framed against the grey harbor light, was something more uncomfortable. Doubt. Real doubt, the kind that costs.

"You should understand my position, Mrs. Lockridge." His voice, when he turned, had lost some of its drawing-room polish. "I was sent here to write a history. Do you know what that means, practically? It means I arrive after the winning is done, and I make the winning look inevitable. That is the trade. My patron expects a certain book. My career rests upon delivering it. If I fill it with—what did you call them—debtors and deserters and unparished babies—"

"If you fill it with people," Elodie said, "it becomes the only honest book ever commissioned by a war."

He laughed then—a short, startled sound, quickly suppressed, as if laughter too were subject to requisition. "You are the most dangerous witness I have interviewed."

"You've said so before. I've decided to take it as a compliment."

"It wasn't entirely." But he was smiling faintly, and she marked it: the first crack in the courteous surface, and through it, something almost young. "You must understand what you ask. Renner's papers are indexed under charges endorsed by the collector at Kingsport, not by me. To quash them requires influence I would have to spend, and spending it leaves fingerprints. If it goes wrong, it is not your reputation that burns. It is mine. Possibly my liberty."

"Yes," Elodie said. "I had assumed you'd want something in return beyond testimony, and I've brought all I own: the truth, in bulk."

"That isn't all you own." His eyes went, deliberately, to the window—to the cliffs, where the great lamp stood dark against the afternoon, waiting for dusk. "There is a duplicate ledger somewhere in this town, Mrs. Lockridge. I am nearly certain of it. I have been nearly certain for some weeks. And you are asking me to negotiate for testimony while the original of that testimony sits in a sea cave or a flour barrel or a widow's mattress, beyond any power of yours to deliver and beyond any power of mine to destroy."

Her heart struck hard against her ribs, once, twice. She made herself breathe the way she breathed in storms—in through the fear, out through the fear, the lamp above all.

"What makes you certain?" she asked, and was proud that her voice barely moved.

"Water damage," Vane said simply. "The surrendered ledger was soaked at three edges in a pattern inconsistent with storage in a dry house. Ink at the spine bled in recent copying sequences. And there is a margin mark—a small cross, tilted left—that appears on pages of personal annotation but in no period register I have ever seen." He paused. "It appears also, I notice, in the way you annotate everything. Your mind keeps a ledger, madam. Your handwriting confesses it."

For a moment neither spoke. Down on the quay, soldiers hauled crates toward the church-storehouse; a gull screamed once and was done.

"So," Elodie said at last. "You hold a smuggler's life in one hand and a suspicion in the other, and you're telling me the price just went up."

"I'm telling you," Vane said, "that I cannot buy what you're selling with coin I don't control. Renner's release I might manage—it is ugly, but it is paperwork, and paperwork is my native country. The girl's safety I can promise with reasonable confidence, because promising is also paperwork. But if I do all this, and it emerges afterward that I was played—that the surrendered records were a forgery in part, that the true ledger of an entire occupied port traveled out of my jurisdiction sewn into a child's coat—" He stopped. Drew breath. "Then I am not a historian with a tarnished reputation, Mrs. Lockridge. I am an accessory. Everything I have built, everything I might build, hangs on trusting the woman who has spent this season deceiving me."

"Would it help," Elodie said, "if I told you I've spent the season being precise? I lied to you about exactly one thing. Everything else I told you was true, and you knew it, because you checked. That's the only kind of trust on offer in this world, Mr. Vane—the kind that's been tested. I'm offering you the tested kind."

He looked at her for a long time. The light was failing outside; a soldier came to trim the lamps in the room and withdrew at Vane's small motion. In the new, lower glow, the historian seemed older, and less armored, and Elodie understood suddenly that he was not deciding whether to help her. He was deciding whether to become, in his own private account of himself, a different man.

"One night," he said finally. "I'll require a night. There are—arrangements. Letters I may need drafted in proper form. A conversation with the provost, conducted carefully. If I do this, it will be done properly or not at all; a botched mercy is worse than none." He came around the desk, and extended his hand—an anachronism, almost, in that room of seals and wax—and she took it. His grip was cool and dry. "Come at dusk tomorrow. Bring nothing. If the answer is yes, Renner will be released within the week and the girl's file will close itself. If the answer is no—" he hesitated "—then leave the ledger where it lies, and tell no one we spoke, and I will do what I can for the child regardless. That much I can promise unconditionally, and you may test it if you like."

"Why would you promise that part regardless?"

Vane released her hand and turned to the window again, to the darkening water, and when he answered, his voice was very quiet.

"Because my mother's marriage was never entered in any parish book either. My father's family saw to that. I have spent thirty years making myself legible to institutions that refused to record her, and I have sometimes wondered whether I have been writing histories, all this while, or merely applying for membership." He straightened his cuffs—a small gesture, fastidious, terribly human. "Good evening, Mrs. Lockridge. Mind the cliff path. The wind is rising."

* * *

She walked home in the blue hour with the wind at her back, pushing her up the hill as if the coast itself wanted her swift, and all the way she turned his words over like stones at low tide, looking for what lived underneath.

*A night to decide.* She knew what rode on that night—Joss in his cell, Wren inland with her coat full of the town's soul, Tam and Mrs. Quill and the widow all tethered by invisible thread to a historian's conscience. She knew, too, what she had not said aloud: that in offering her testimony she had offered the one thing she could not take back. Once the names entered his official book, they belonged to the Crown forever—for keeping, yes, but for keeping on the Crown's terms. She had handed the victor the keys to the only vault she owned and prayed he was the sort of man who respected what he was given more than what he seized.

At the top of the cliff path she stopped and looked back. The customs house windows glowed below, and in one of them, small with distance, a figure bent over his desk, writing by lamplight. Whatever he was drafting—letters, or excuses, or the sentence in his own private ledger that would say what kind of man Aldous Vane had chosen to be—he was working at it late.

Elodie climbed the tower stairs and opened the lantern and began the ritual: wick, oil, glass, flame. The great lens caught the spark and threw it far out over the blackening water, and she stood in the turning gold and thought of the words she would write tonight, in the working ledger, in the margin, in her own small tilted cross:

*Bargain struck, pending. Prayed for the first time since Thomas died. Not sure to whom. Light kept.*

Far to the east, low under the cloud bank, the barometer glass in the lantern room had begun to fall—and she watched the horizon with a keeper's eye and did not like the color of the sky at all.
