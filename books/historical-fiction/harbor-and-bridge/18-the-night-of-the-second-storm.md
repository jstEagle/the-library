## 18. The Night of the Second Storm

### Elodie

Elodie had known storms the way a wife knows a husband's moods—by the first small signs, long before the violence. This one announced itself at dusk as a bruise along the western horizon, a color she had seen only twice in thirty years and both times had knelt afterward among wreckage. The barometer Mrs. Quill kept in her surgery—salvaged from a Spanish prize decades ago—had fallen so far that morning that the midwife tapped it twice with one knuckle and said, "Well. Best fetch your boards down."

By full dark the wind came off the sea like something with intent.

She had lit the lamp early. She had trimmed it twice. And she had done one thing more, before any of it began, which she would not tell Vane until much later: she had taken Wren by the shoulders in the cottage that smelled of salt and tallow and said, *Whatever you hear tonight, whatever you see—you go to the cave when I send Tam, and you don't come back for me.* The girl had stared at her with Joss Renner's gray eyes and then, instead of arguing, had pressed something into Elodie's hand—a carved gull, small and smooth, that Tam must have given up from his own pocket—and gone upstairs without a word. That frightened Elodie more than the barometer. Children know when adults are lying about safety. They always have.

The exchange was set for nine o'clock. Vane's terms, delivered through a nervous ensign: the transport *Larkspur*, riding at anchor in the roads, would carry Joss Renner to Portsmouth under writ of removal; Elodie would come to the lantern room at nine with her testimony prepared, and Vane would sign the order commuting Renner's sentence before she spoke a word. He had insisted on that sequence being his idea. She suspected he knew she would insist on it too, and had yielded it deliberately, the way a chess player gives up a pawn to watch what the other hand does with the freedom.

At half past eight the wind took the harbor bell and shook it until it sounded less like a warning than an animal in pain.

At quarter to nine, just as she wrapped her cloak and lifted the hood, the light above her head went out.

Not guttered. Not dimmed. It died between one heartbeat and the next, and the darkness that filled its place was total, a blackness with weight, pressing down through the stairwell like water finding a breach. Elodie stood in it and felt the whole architecture of her life tilt. Thirty years she had kept that flame. Ships steered by it. Men alive tonight were alive because of it.

"Tam," she called into the dark, and heard him already on the stairs, coming down to her, because he always came down to her.

* * *

### Vane

Aldous Vane had dressed carefully for the interview, which later struck him as either vanity or instinct—he never decided which. His good coat, the ink-stained cuff hidden beneath the sleeve. His notebook. The commutation order folded once in the breast pocket, signed but undated, dated but unsigned, he had not yet determined; he had written it both ways on separate drafts and could not make himself choose.

Then the lamp died, and all such small arithmetic drowned at once.

He was at the harbormaster's house when it happened, and he watched the whole town notice. From the window he saw doors open along the crescent, saw lanterns bloom in doorways like startled flowers, saw men come out into the rain and stand looking up at the black tower on the headland the way people look at a body. A naval officer beside him swore softly and said what everyone was thinking: there was a transport in the roads with two hundred souls aboard, and no light to bring her in.

Vane did not run—he was forty-six years old and had never run anywhere in his life—but he walked faster than he had ever walked, through rain that came sideways and stung like thrown gravel, up the cliff road with his hat crushed in his fist, and found the tower door open and a boy standing at its foot holding a shuttered lantern, and above them both, descending, a woman whose face in the boy's light was calm in a way that frightened him more than the storm.

"The oil froze," she said before he could ask. "In the feed lines. It happens when the temperature falls this fast. There is a way to warm them, but it takes hands."

"How many hands?"

"Three." She looked at him without apology, without gratitude, with only the flat appraisal of a woman counting tools. "You wanted my story, Mr. Vane. Tonight you may work for it."

He should have refused. Some part of him, the part that had climbed from nothing on exactly such refusals, catalogued every reason—the rank, the record, the sheer impropriety of the Crown's historian hauling oil drums in a gale while the subject of his history gave him orders. He noted it all, filed it, and took off his coat.

* * *

### Tam

The cold in the feed line was a snake in the wall, Tam thought. You couldn't see it, but you could feel where it lay, coiled somewhere between the cistern room and the lamp itself, and you had to warm the pipe a finger's length at a time or the metal would crack and then there would be no light until spring and ships would die in the dark and it would be their fault, his fault, hers.

He carried what could not be asked of him in words because no one had ever needed words for it. He knew the tower the way he knew his own mouth—every step, every shelf, every joint of iron. When Elodie pointed, he moved. When she held up three fingers, he brought three things. The wind came through the lantern glass in long shuddering moans and the whole gallery rocked on its great stone neck, and Tam, who feared nothing on water and little on land, discovered he feared this: the tower bending. Things made to stand, bowing.

Between runs he crouched at the little window over the sea and looked for the *Larkspur*.

He had seen her anchor lights earlier, two pale points in the black roads. Now he saw one of those points swing. Then the other. Then neither, and after a while—how long? his sense of time had gone liquid—a sound under the wind that was not wind. Wood. Wood in large quantities, complaining.

He pulled Elodie's sleeve and pointed. She looked, and he watched her face do a thing he had seen perhaps four times ever: go entirely still, all at once, like a bird landing.

* * *

### Elodie

The transport was dragging her anchors.

Through the streaming glass Elodie could see her now when the lightning obliged—broad on, beam sea, her topmasts bare, driving toward the northern spit where the water went white over broken ground. Two hundred men, the ensign had said. Conscripts mostly, farm boys out of the inland counties, the kind of cargo the war consumed by the thousand and called expenditure.

And in the tower behind her, warming a frozen pipe with his bare hands wrapped in her apron, knelt the man whose signature could save or hang Joss Renner, working without complaint, his fine coat soaked black, ink stains showing at the cuff when he reached.

"Mr. Vane." Her voice carried strangely in the wind; she had to shape each word like a nail driven home. "There is a boat crew at the harbor steps. Fishermen. They will not launch while the light is dark—they'd be blind out there. If we get the lamp burning, they'll try to put off and take lines to the transport before she strikes. Every minute is lives."

"And if we don't?" He shouted it up from the floor, not accusing—asking to be told.

"Then tomorrow you will write two hundred names in your official book," she said, "and I will write them in mine, and neither of us will ever be finished."

Something crossed his face that she would remember for years: not resolve exactly, but the collapse of hesitation, the moment a man stops weighing himself and simply acts. He thrust the oil drum aside and climbed.

They worked the lamp together, the three of them, and it was labor from some older, harder century—warming the feed pipe with cloths wrung from water heated on the brazier, Tam holding the lamp assembly steady against the tower's sway while Elodie cleared the charred wick, Vane pumping the warmed oil through with a hand force-pump meant for one pair of arms and suffering three. Twice the wind tore the gallery door open and nearly took it off its hinges; twice Tam fought it shut and won. Rain drove horizontally through every gap in the glazing and the lantern room became half ocean, ankle-deep, sloshing with each gust.

And somewhere below them all, in the chaos at the harbor steps, in the black roaring roads where a foundering transport screamed against her anchors, a cell door stood unlocked.

* * *

### Joss

Joss Renner had spent eleven days in that cell learning its grammar: the drip at the northeast corner, the rhythm of the sentry's boots, the hour the cook's boy passed the grate and could sometimes be bribed with a song. He had expected to die in it. He had made his peace the way he made most things—badly, and late, and mostly for Wren's sake rather than his own, because a hanged man's daughter carries the rope in her name forever.

So when the guard bolt went quiet at half past eight, and the sentry's boots vanished toward the square where the whole garrison had been called out with lanterns and axes because the light was out and the *Larkspur* was going ashore, Joss did not at first understand what he was hearing. Then the cell door swung, and Elodie's plan—or Tam's, or Mrs. Quill's; he'd never learn which—revealed itself in the person of the excise widow's ancient boatman, holding a shuttered lantern and a key ring and looking profoundly unhappy about the entire enterprise.

"Boathouse," the old man rasped. "Grey sail. Don't ask me nothing else, I'm too old to lie well."

Joss asked him nothing. He went.

The harbor was bedlam. Soldiers everywhere, torches, ropes, a chain of men passing coil after coil down the steps to the boats, officers shouting themselves hoarse against a wind that ate sound whole. He kept to the shadow of the storehouse wall, then the fish sheds, moving in the intervals between lightning the way he'd moved through customs cutters for twenty years. His body remembered everything his conscience had tried to make him forget: the weightless step on wet planking, the breath held through a torchlight sweep, the trick of becoming furniture.

The grey sail was where it should be. So, unexpectedly, was Wren.

She was sitting in the stern sheets with her knees drawn up, soaked through, absolutely still, and when Joss dropped over the gunwale she looked at him without surprise, as though she had known since the cell door opened who would come.

"I'm not going," she said.

"Yes, you are."

"Mama isn't in any book they burned," she said, and her voice cracked only once, and only slightly. "I'm the book. You said so. He said so. So I have to get where they can't burn me."

For a moment Joss Renner, who had faced press-gangs and excise men and the inside of a condemned cell without flinching, could not speak at all. Then he laughed—one short, broken bark the wind stole—and hauled the tiller across, and the grey sail filled, and the boat went out through the harbor mouth into the worst night of the century.

Behind them, above the drowning transport, above the town, the great lamp of Greyhaven caught, flickered, steadied, and blazed.

* * *

### Vane

Light burst from the tower like a decision.

Vane, sprawled against the brazier with scalded fingers and lungs full of oil-smell, watched Elodie close the wick mechanism with hands that did not shake even now, and felt something shift in his chest that he would spend the rest of his life trying to name. It was not admiration, though he had admired her since the first interview. It was closer to recognition—as if he had spent forty-six years assembling an argument about what human beings were worth, and here, in a leaking lantern room, stood the refutation of it, working.

Below, the fishermen launched. He saw them go, five boats, mad, magnificent, driving out through the harbor mouth toward the transport with the new light silvering their wakes. He saw the *Larkspur*'s crew answer with signal guns. He saw, farther out, past the stricken ship, a small grey sail standing north-northeast under storm canvas, running clean—

He saw it. Of course he saw it. He had indexed every boat in that harbor; he knew the Renner craft by its patchwork jib. And he understood in one cold, complete instant what had been done to him: the exchange had been scheduled for this night by people who could read weather better than he read men. The escape was not despite the storm. It was built of it. He had been given oil to pump and a lamp to light so that he would be here, usefully occupied, while the prisoner he had come to trade walked out of his custody on the back of the very catastrophe he'd been dispatched to chronicle.

He should have been furious.

Instead Aldous Vane began, absurdly, helplessly, to laugh—a short, ungentlemanly sound torn out of him by the wind—and Elodie Winterbourne turned from the lamp and looked at him with those clear gray eyes, waiting to see what he was.

"They're away," she said. It was not quite a question. She had offered him the chance to denounce it, and herself, and everything.

"A boat is missing from the harbor muster," he said slowly, "which I will be required to account for. A prisoner has escaped, which I will be required to explain. And a transport is going ashore in front of witnesses, which no one will require me to explain, because no explanation survives that." He looked out at the wreck driving toward the spit, at the five tiny boats reaching her. "Mrs. Winterbourne. I find myself in need of a version of events."

"You'll write the true one," she said. "It's the only kind worth keeping."

"The true version includes your smuggling partner's daughter fleeing north under my supervision."

"It includes that you chose," she said, "in a lantern room, with your own hands, to keep two hundred strangers alive instead of catching one girl. I've kept a ledger for thirty years, Mr. Vane. I know exactly how heavy a true page is." She turned back to the wick, adjusting, trimming, her voice almost gentle. "You wanted to write history no one could strike out. Here is your material. A light kept by enemies. Boats that went out for conscripts they owed nothing to. Write *that*, and Joss Renner is a footnote you can afford to pardon."

The lightning showed him her face, and the lamplight took it back, and Aldous Vane—who had dined with ministers and declined to flatter them—took out his notebook, opened it against the wind, and wrote the date, and beneath it: *The lamp failed at 20:44. It was relit by three hands. All survived who could be saved.*

Below them, the first of the fishing boats reached the *Larkspur*'s lee side, and men began to jump.

* * *

### Tam

Dawn came the color of pewter and found them still in the tower, because the lamp could not be left until the sea gave back its dead or its living, whichever it chose. Ninety-one men came ashore alive. The count went up through the morning, one dripping soldier at a time, carried up the cliff road on doors and ladders, and Mrs. Quill's surgery filled, and the church filled, and the town that the garrison had occupied for a season worked side by side with it in the wreckage and nobody remembered, for a few hours, who commanded whom.

Tam stayed at Elodie's side. He counted the rescued in his own way, a notch of attention for each, and stopped at ninety-one because ninety-two did not come.

Near noon, Vane came down the tower stairs last, gray-faced, oil-burned, his notebook swollen with rain but legible. At the door he paused and looked at Elodie a long moment.

"My superiors will want the transport accounted for within the week," he said. "I can manage the accounting. What I cannot manage—" he stopped, and started again, and Tam saw that the man was choosing words with visible effort, like a climber choosing holds, "—is the remainder. The names. The ones the registers lost. You said once I might hear them. I am ready to hear them now, Mrs. Winterbourne. Formally. On the record. Before I lose my nerve or my posting, whichever comes first."

Elodie looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at Tam, and something passed between them that had no words in it at all.

"Come at dusk, then," she said. "Bring your own paper. We'll begin with a woman called Maren Renner—who was born, and loved, and died, and will be written down tonight if I have to hold the pen in your hand myself."

Above them the lamp, extinguished at last in the daylight, waited in its glass house for the dark.
