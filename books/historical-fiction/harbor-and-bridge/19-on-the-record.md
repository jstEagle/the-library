## 19. On the Record

The storm had spent itself by dawn, leaving the harbor strewn with its temper — spars, rope, a ship's door no one could name the vessel of — and leaving the lantern room glass starred in two panes and the wick-gears fouled with salt. Elodie had not slept. None of them had. But when the sun came up thin and rinsed-clean over Greyhaven, and the transport *Merrow* lay hard aground on the shingle with most of her people somehow alive because a light had burned all night, something in the town had shifted on its axis, quiet as ballast.

It was Vane who named it.

He came to the light at dusk, when the gulls had gone to their ledges and the tide was drawing back off the bridge stones. He came alone, without his clerks, without the escort of redcoats that had shadowed him since the cutter first dropped anchor. Under one arm he carried a leather case; in his hands, offered forward like a man approaching an altar rather than a lighthouse, he carried paper, ink, and two quills.

"Mrs. Winterbourne." He stood at the foot of the stair, salt-stained still from the night's work, his precise coat ruined beyond what any valet could answer for. "I would take your testimony. If you'll give it."

Elodie looked at him for a long moment. She was tired down to the marrow, her hands still smelling of lamp oil and burnt wick, her shoulder aching from hours at the crank Tam's arms had been too small to turn alone. Behind her, through the open door of the dwelling, the fire ticked and Mrs. Quill's kettle murmured. Somewhere inland, Joss Renner was free — slipped from a cell that the storm had cracked open like an egg, vanished into the hills before the first patrol could count their prisoners twice. That debt was not yet paid. They both knew it.

"Testimony," she said. "For your history."

"For the record." He said it carefully, and she heard what he was doing: separating the word *history*, which could be commissioned and edited and bound in gilt for a minister's shelf, from *record*, which was older and harder and belonged to no one. "What went into my reports these six months is — incomplete. I find I am unwilling to leave it so." A pause. His eyes, which missed everything, missed nothing now. "You taught me the difference, whether you meant to or not."

"And if your masters don't like what I say?"

"Once it is entered under oath in the official narrative, striking it out requires a new act of Parliament and a scandal." Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might live. "Ministers will endure a great many drowned fishermen before they'll endure a scandal."

She should have hated him. For six months she had practiced hating him, neat and disciplined as her penmanship, and it had never once taken hold. He was the enemy's instrument, and she had used him as he had used her, and somewhere in the long black night just past — the three of them at the lamp, Vane's fine hands cranking the gear mechanism until they bled, shouting coordinates of the foundering transport across the wind while Tam held the shutter glass steady — somewhere in that, the shape of enemy and ally had blurred past any hope of recovery.

"Come up, then," she said. "The light needs tending at eight bells, and I'll not stop for ministers."

* * *

They worked at the table in the dwelling room, where she had written every private entry for eleven years. Mrs. Quill set out bread and cold fish and then, with the tact of a woman who had attended half the town into the world and the other half out of it, withdrew to the doorway with her mending, near enough to hear, far enough to be only an old woman sewing.

Vane opened his case. The paper inside was good stock, the government's own, watermarked with the Crown — and Elodie felt the old fear rise in her throat, the same fear that had walked her sleepless through the whole of the storm season. Paper with the Crown upon it had come to take things away. But Vane turned it toward her and dipped the first quill, and she saw that this time the paper had come to receive.

"I'll begin with the form," he said. "'A true and particular account of the inhabitants, births, debts, deaths, and passages of the harbor of Greyhaven, given under oath by Elodie Winterbourne, keeper of the light, this ninth day of November, in the year 1747.'" He glanced up. "Amend it as you see fit. It is your account. I am only the hand."

"The hand," she said, "that wrote the requisition order."

"Yes." He did not flinch from it, and she gave him credit for that. "I have been the Crown's hand for eleven years, Mrs. Winterbourne. Tonight I would like to try being its memory instead. Begin wherever you like. I will write everything. Slowly, if you need. We have until the tide turns and some hours beyond."

She thought of all the places she might begin — the requisition, the seizure, the burning papers in the square, Joss in his cell — and found she did not want any of them. Those were grievances, and grievances were for petitions. What she had was older than grievance. What she had was names.

So she began where she had always begun.

"In March of 1736, a fishing crew out of Pellow's Reach were lost on the Teeth — Matthew Oakes, master; his sons Daniel and Hugh; Silas Cray; and a boy whose name was never learned, called only Tam until such time as he could tell us better. He never told us better. Tam is his name now, and it stands."

Vane wrote. His hand, she noticed, was beautiful — the hand of a man who had been trained to think that how a thing was set down mattered as much as what was set down. He wrote slowly, exactly as promised, and read each line back to her before the ink dried.

"Silas Cray's widow, Prudence, took in the boy," Elodie went on. "When she died of the fever in '39, he passed between Mrs. Quill's house and mine, fed by whoever had a pot on. No parish ever recorded him. Enter him now."

"Entered. Tam, of Greyhaven, alive, in the fourteenth year of his age or thereabouts, keeper's boy." Vane's quill paused above the page. "Alive. You mark the living?"

"I mark everyone. That's the whole of it, Mr. Vane. The parish marks the baptized and the customs marks the taxed and the army marks the enlisted, and everyone else falls through the net. I keep the net." She pulled the working ledger from its drawer — the decoy had gone to the army in October, surrendered with steady hands in the square while the true book traveled sewn into a girl's coat, and this was the honest successor, begun fresh, kept anyway, because a keeper keeps — and laid it open beside his official pages. "We'll go year by year. You'll want dates. Mrs. Quill has them."

From the doorway, without looking up from her mending, Mrs. Quill said: "Maren Renner, born the third of May, 1719, at the house on the strand. Daughter of a fisherman named Aldous—" she said the name with dry relish and Vane's quill jumped — "Pell, who denied her at the font and left the coast the same season. Married Joss Renner in the spring of '37, no church, no license, married before God and forty witnesses at the Midsummer fire. Birthed one daughter, Wren, on the ninth of September, 1738. Died of childbed fever the twelfth of September, though the child lived."

The room was very quiet. The kettle murmured. Elodie found her own hands had gone still on the table, and she made them move again, smoothing the ledger's page.

"Say it once more," Vane said softly. "All of it."

Mrs. Quill said it again, every date exact, forty years of midwifery stored in a head that forgot nothing it had witnessed at the doors of life and death. And Vane wrote *Maren Renner* into the government's paper — birth, marriage, motherhood, death — each element sworn and dated and attested, each one now a fact with the weight of the Crown behind it, indelible, actionable, a person restored to the world by the enemy's own instrument.

When he finished the entry he sat back, and for a moment the historian's composure cracked along some seam Elodie had never seen before.

"There is no file for her anywhere," he said. "I searched. In London, in the customs rolls, in the parish registers of three counties. It was as if she had never—"

"She was never legally recorded at all," Elodie said. "Which is why they could plan to erase her daughter. A girl whose mother doesn't exist is halfway erased already. All the garrison had to do was finish the sentence." She leaned forward. "Enter her, Mr. Vane. Enter her properly. Not as an addendum, not as a marginal note. As a woman of Greyhaven who lived thirty years and bore a child and is owed her name in ink."

"Maren Renner," Vane repeated, writing it in a fair round hand, and then, beneath it, in smaller characters: "*recorded this day, that she may never again be unrecorded.*"

"That last line isn't testimony," Elodie said.

"No." He did not strike it. "Let it stand."

* * *

They worked until the candle had burned down twice and been replaced, until the moon crossed the broken panes of the lantern room overhead and the tide turned below the headland. Name by name, year by year, the dead and the living of Greyhaven marched into the official history of the occupation: the drowned and the delivered, the debts forgiven and the debts unpaid, the deserters sheltered in lofts and the press-gang victims hidden in the sea cave through a whole winter, the wrecks salvaged and the widows pensioned by common consent out of common purse. Vane asked questions like a man who intended to defend every line before a tribunal — dates, witnesses, corroborating circumstances — and Mrs. Quill supplied dates like a well supplying water, and Tam appeared sometime past midnight with hot barley broth for three and stayed to watch the writing with his whole body, leaning on the table's edge, following the quill's progress with fierce attention.

At the entry for himself — *Tam, alive* — he put one blunt finger on the page and held it there a long time.

"He wants to see it's real," Elodie said. "That it counts. That the King's own paper knows his name."

"It counts," Vane said, to the boy directly, and something passed between them that needed no words, which was fitting, since Tam owned none.

Toward the end, when the candle guttered and the eastern sky had not yet grayed but promised to, Elodie reached the entries of the autumn just past — the requisition, the seizure, the burning of surplus papers in the square, the arrest of Joss Renner on charges drawn from indexed customs files, and the flight from the storm-cracked cell. Her voice went level and careful here, the voice she had practiced for lying and found she could use for truth as well.

"You may enter all of it," she said. "Or none of it. Your choice, Mr. Vane. But if you enter it, enter it whole, because I'll swear to every word."

He wrote it. All of it. The quill did not hesitate except once — at *Joss Renner, freed from custody during the storm of the eighth of November, whereabouts unknown* — and Elodie watched the hesitation and understood it exactly. To write that line officially was to make himself answerable for it. It was the historian putting his own name in the margin.

"Whereabouts unknown," he repeated quietly, and then added, unbidden: "though it is to be hoped he remains within reach of those who can speak for his character." He looked up at her. "Character testimony carries weight in a pardon hearing. Especially when sworn by the keeper of the light and entered in the official narrative." A pause. "Especially when the keeper has, perhaps, arranged for such testimony to be ready against the day it is asked for."

"Are you asking me to arrange it, Mr. Vane?"

"I am telling you," Vane said, dipping the quill one final time, "that a pardon hearing requires a petition, two character witnesses, and an officer of the Crown willing to certify that the prisoner's value to the historical record exceeds the state's interest in his hanging. Two of those three are already in this room." He sanded the last page, closed the folio, and set his hand flat upon it — the whole weight of the night's work gathered under his palm. "The third is willing. But he will need your testimony in person, in London, before the Admiralty counsel, within the month. And he will need the girl brought safely inland before the hearing, out of reach of anyone who might find her existence inconvenient."

Elodie sat very still. Outside, the surf worked the shingle, patient as it had been for ten thousand years, indifferent to parliaments and pardons and the small furious candle-flame of human justice.

"You're offering to take us all to London," she said.

"I'm offering," said Aldous Vane, "to finish the job properly. I have entered these people into the record. It seems poor work to leave them in it undefended." He rose, stiff from the long sitting, and gathered his case, and at the door he paused with his hand on the latch. "We sail on Thursday's supply brig, if the weather holds. Mrs. Winterbourne — you had best decide who comes ashore with us, and who stays to keep the light."

The door closed behind him. In the sudden quiet, Mrs. Quill set down her mending, and Tam looked at Elodie across the table where the names lay drying, and far out on the dark water the true light turned and turned, faithful as breath.

Thursday, Elodie thought. Three days to decide who leaves Greyhaven — and who, for the first time in eleven years, must trust someone else to keep the flame.
