## 20. Departures

Wren had thought she would remember the storm most—the howl of it through the lantern glass, the way the sea came up over the harbor wall like a thing unchained. But what stayed with her, in the days after, was the quiet that followed. Greyhaven after the second storm felt like a room after a quarrel: everyone moving softly, everyone listening for something to break again.

She sat on the steps of the light tower with her father's coat across her knees, mending a tear at the elbow he would never notice and never thank her for. Below, in the square, soldiers were loading crates onto a wagon—papers, mostly, bound and boxed and stamped with the Crown's seal. The occupation was ending the way tides ended, not all at once but in long reluctant withdrawals, each wave reaching a little further up the sand before giving back.

Joss Renner walked out of the harbormaster's office with papers in his hand, and Wren knew before she read his face what they were. She had learned to read faces long before she had learned to read words.

He held the pardon out to her like it might bite him.

"Signed," he said. "Sealed. Aldous Vane's own hand on the recommendation." He laughed, a short strange sound, as if laughter were a language he'd half forgotten. "The man who indexed the papers that could've hanged me wrote the letter that unhangs me. I don't know whether to kiss him or drown him."

"Neither," Wren said. "He's coming to dinner."

* * *

He came to dinner. That was the strangest part, in the end—not the storms, not the arrests, not even the night the three of them had worked the lamp together while a transport tore itself open on the outer shoals. The strangest part was Mr. Aldous Vane, the Crown's historian, sitting at their table with his precise cuffs rolled back and his long fingers wrapped around a chipped mug of Mrs. Quill's nettle tea, eating fish stew as though he had not spent four months cataloguing their lives for the archives of the people who had tried to erase them.

He ate politely. He asked Mrs. Quill about the birthing customs of the coast with genuine hunger, and she told him, and he wrote nothing down, which Wren noticed and did not understand until later—some things, Vane seemed to have decided, belonged only in memory, where no clerk could strike them out.

Afterward he stood by the fire and addressed Joss without ceremony.

"You'll want to go inland. The excise men on the western road haven't got your new papers yet, and old grudges travel faster than post." He drew a folded map from his coat. "The widow Harkness will take you as far as Ashford. From there—" He paused. His eyes went to Wren, and there was something in them Wren had not seen before. Not pity. Something more careful than pity. Respect, perhaps, or its cousin. "From there the road is clean."

"I'm not going," Wren said.

The fire popped. Her father looked at his hands.

Vane did not look away from her. "No," he said quietly. "I don't imagine you are. Not entirely."

* * *

They argued about it for three days, which in the Renner household was a form of tenderness. Joss wanted her safe behind stone walls and a widow who owed Vane favors; Wren wanted to stay at the light with Elodie, whose hands had taught hers, whose ledger had given her a name when the parish would not. In the end it was Elodie who settled it, not with argument but with the ledger itself.

She spread it on the table—the working copy, the one the garrison believed surrendered, the one that was true enough and hollow enough—and turned to the page where Wren had first learned her letters. *Wren, daughter of Mara,* it said, in Elodie's upright hand. *Born at sea, recorded nowhere else.*

"Names need keepers," Elodie said. "I have kept yours here as long as I could. But a name kept in one place is a candle in one window. A name carried is a lantern." She closed the book and pressed her palm flat against the cover. "The original goes with you. Sewn into your coat, where the tide can't reach it and neither can any clerk with a requisition order. You'll carry every soul in this harbor, Wren. There's no safer place for them than inside someone the whole world has already overlooked."

Wren understood then that it was not exile. It was an appointment. The highest one Elodie knew how to give.

Mrs. Quill came the next morning with her basket and her needle and her forty years of unrecorded births, and together they took apart the lining of Wren's coat while Tam kept watch at the door, solemn as a churchwarden. The ledger was smaller than Wren remembered—or she was larger now, which amounted to the same thing. They stitched it flat against her back, between wool and linen, and Mrs. Quill sewed so finely that when she finished she made Wren put the coat on and turn around twice, and then hugged her hard enough to crack ribs and said into her hair, "Forty years I caught babies nobody wrote down. You're the last page of my book, girl. Don't you dare lose it."

* * *

The morning of departure came down cold and bright, the sky scrubbed blue by the season's violence, the harbor lying flat as poured pewter. Vane's carriage waited at the head of the tidal bridge—the long spine of stone that crossed the channel at low water and drowned beneath it twice a day, the town's oldest argument with the sea.

Elodie walked Wren down to the bridge itself, past the gulls arguing over the tideline, past the wreck of the transport still showing its ribs at low water like a whale the sea had given back. Neither of them spoke much. There did not seem to be words left in either of them that the other had not already read.

At the bridge's first arch Elodie stopped and took Wren's face in both her hands, the way she did when she was checking her for fever, or lies, or growing up.

"You'll write," she said. It was not a question.

"I'll write."

"And when you write, you'll tell me the weather. Always the weather. A keeper's first duty is the sky."

Wren laughed, and it broke in the middle, and Elodie gathered her in and held her while the tide ticked against the stones below and the gulls wheeled overhead crying their one gray note. Wren pressed her face into the smell of her—lamp oil and salt and wool—and memorized it the way she had memorized letters, stroke by stroke, so that no distance could make her illiterate in it.

"You gave me my name," Wren said into her shoulder.

"No." Elodie pulled back, and her eyes were wet and steady at once. "Your mother gave it. I only wrote it where it couldn't be washed out. There's a difference, and someday you'll be old enough to know how much."

Then Joss was there, awkward and gruff, folding Wren against his chest with the careful ferocity of a man holding something he had once nearly lost to a hangman's rope. He smelled of tar and the road he hadn't yet taken. "Ashford," he muttered into her hair. "Straight through. No shortcuts, no cleverness. If you're clever, I'll know."

"You always know."

"That's because I taught you all of it." He held her at arm's length and looked at her the way he looked at a chart before running a dark coast at night—memorizing, measuring, committing every bearing to heart. Then he kissed the top of her head and turned away quickly, before his face could do anything either of them would regret.

Tam had been standing at the bridge rail the whole time, still as one of the mooring posts, and now he came forward. He had nothing to say—he had never had anything to say in words—but his hands were full.

He held them out. On his scarred palms lay a gull carved from driftwood, small enough to hide in a fist, its wings swept back mid-turn, sanded smooth from months of pockets. Wren had seen him carving it by lamplight all winter, had assumed it was for himself, the way his carvings always were—his voice, made visible, since his throat refused the office.

He pushed it into her hands and folded her fingers over it. Then he tapped his chest with two fingers, and pointed at the gull, and tapped her chest.

"That means he's coming with you," Wren said, though her voice cracked on it.

Tam shook his head slowly. He pointed at the gull again, then swept his hand out toward the harbor, the light tower, the bridge, the whole gray shining world of Greyhaven—and then brought the hand back and laid it flat over Wren's heart.

"Oh," Wren said. Stupidly, wonderfully. "Oh, that's better."

He grinned then, gap-toothed, and for once it didn't matter that he had no words, because he had never needed them less. He hugged her quick and fierce and let go, and stepped back to stand beside Elodie, and the two of them together—the keeper and the boy, the lamp and its hands—made a picture Wren knew she would carry the rest of her life, filed somewhere behind her ribs next to her mother's face, which was fading, and this coat's secret weight, which never would.

Vane cleared his throat from the carriage door. "The tide," he said gently, "waits for no historian."

* * *

The crossing of the bridge took eleven minutes. Wren counted them against the stones underfoot, the way Tam had taught her, and by the fourth arch she could no longer see the faces at the far side, only figures—two still ones and one small one raising an arm, and above them all, tiny with distance, the white tower standing its eternal watch.

Halfway across, the water beneath the arches began to stir. The tide had turned. She felt it before she saw it, a shudder in the old stone, the sea sliding back in to claim the road it lent them. By the time they reached solid ground on the inland shore, the bridge behind them was going under arch by arch, the harbor closing its gray hand, and Greyhaven became what it had always truly been: an island, a lamp, a story.

Vane handed her up into the carriage with courtly care and climbed in after, settling his long frame opposite her and Joss. He produced a leather folio, opened it, and regarded her across it for a long moment.

"I have a proposition," he said. "Not for today. For later, when you're settled at the widow Harkness's and the roads are dry." He turned the folio around. On the page, in his immaculate hand, was a heading: *Testimony of Wren, daughter of Mara, born at sea.* Beneath it, nothing yet. Blank lines waiting like furrows before spring.

"The official history will carry your mother's name because I entered it," he said. "But an entry is not a life. An entry says *she existed.* It does not say *who she was*, or what songs she sang, or why a smuggler loved her, or what she said to her daughter on the last night anyone saw her." His pen hovered. His eyes, behind it, were not the predator's eyes from that first interview in the tower—Wren could see that now, could see what the winter had done to him, or perhaps what the lamp had done, working on him by slow degrees the way it worked on everything it touched. "That part, no archive can demand and no clerk can seize. I am asking for it freely. When you are ready."

Wren sat with the weight of the ledger against her spine, every name in the harbor riding between her shoulder blades, and looked at the blank page, and understood at last what Elodie had been doing all those years by lamplight—not keeping records, but keeping people, one line at a time, against the day the sea and the Crown and forgetting itself all came for them at once.

The carriage lurched forward. Behind her, the tide swallowed the last arch.

"Yes," Wren said, and took out the little carved gull, and set it on her knee facing forward, toward the inland road, toward whatever came next. "But we start with her name. And we start with the weather."

Vane's pen touched paper.

Somewhere behind them, faint over the sound of the wheels, she could almost believe she heard the lamp turn on for the evening—a small stubborn light on a gray coast, burning because a woman willed it, waiting because a woman kept it, and Wren rode away from it carrying everything it meant, stitched flat against her heart like a second spine, holding her upright all the miles into spring.
