## 1. What Can Be Carried

The rain came sideways off the harbor, the kind of rain that had opinions about you, and Zephyrine Sterling stood at the mouth of the Salt Gate checkpoint and let it.

They had learned years ago that hurrying was the first thing that killed a courier. A man who hurried looked like a man with somewhere to be. A man who hurried was a man carrying something. So Zephyrine stood under the broken eave of the gatehouse, hat brim dripping, coat already soaked through to the second layer, and watched the checkpoint the way a fisherman watches water — patiently, without appearing to watch at all.

Two soldiers at the barrier. The older one had his collar turned up and his rifle slung backward, the way men carried weapons they had stopped believing in. The younger one was new — boots still black, not yet salt-eaten — and he was the danger. New men had something to prove. New men searched bags with enthusiasm. The old ones searched with their eyes and waved you through, because everyone in Vessany Harbor was guilty of something and the war had made them tired of pretending otherwise.

Beyond the barrier, the town climbed its hill in the rain. Zephyrine counted the chimneys smoking out of habit. Fewer than last month. Fewer than the month before that. A town stopped smoking from the top down first, the way a body stopped warming from the extremities in, and Vessany Harbor had been going cold for a year now, district by district, as the republic that had promised to protect it stopped answering its letters.

The republic was still printing proclamations, of course. Zephyrine had read three of them pasted to the customs house wall on the walk down from the coach road, each one promising victory in a tone that had gone from confident to defiant to pleading over the winter, the way a drowning man's voice changes. The last one had been printed on paper so thin the rain had already blurred half the words, which felt honest in a way the words themselves were not.

*You are not afraid,* Zephyrine told themselves, which was a lie, and then, because lies needed managing the way cargo did: *You are afraid, and you are going to walk through that gate anyway, and the fear will sit in the back of the cart where it belongs and not touch the reins.*

That was the whole discipline of it. Not courage. Cargo management.

Zephyrine did the inventory again. It was a ritual, and rituals were the second thing that kept a courier alive, after slowness.

The satchel: oilcloth, scuffed, the strap mended with tarred twine so it looked like what it was — a fisherman's bag, a workingman's bag, a bag with nothing in it worth a bullet. Inside: a mended netting needle and three spools of twine. A tin of lamp oil, half empty, because a full tin invited questions and an empty one invited boredom. A loaf of black bread wrapped in cloth, stale on purpose. A letter from a cousin in the interior, genuine, boring, complaining about the price of coal — Zephyrine had read it four times and could recite it, which was the point. If a soldier asked about the letter, Zephyrine would be annoyed, would complain about the cousin, would produce a small and genuine grievance about a woman named Marta who wrote long letters and said nothing. Authenticity was a discipline too. You could not fake boredom under a rifle barrel unless the boredom was real.

The coat: this was the cargo.

The lining of the left breast had been opened and restitched by a woman in the interior whose name Zephyrine had never learned and whose face they had made a point of forgetting, because she had sewn into it a strip of oilskin the width of two fingers, and inside the oilskin was the codebook — not a book, a card, a single card of cipher groups in a hand so small it looked like a stain — and beneath the card, folded to the thickness of a matchbook, the dispatch. The dispatch was the reason for the coat. The coat was the reason for the walk. Everything else was set dressing, and Zephyrine had spent three days making the set dressing good: wearing the coat in the rain so the rain-stains were real, sleeping in it so the creases were real, spilling soup on the right cuff because a courier with a spotless coat in a starving town was a courier with a story problem.

*If they take the coat,* Zephyrine thought, doing the arithmetic they did at every checkpoint, every time, *then the dispatch is gone, and the garrison at the Cape batteries doesn't learn the relief convoy is a fiction, and they surrender, and the surrender is announced, and the men who believed the fiction are shot or worse. Cost: several hundred lives, plus the fiction itself, which is the last thing holding the coast together. If they take the satchel: cost, a tin of oil and an afternoon of Marta's complaints. If they take the hat: cost, dignity.*

Everything could be measured. That was the mercy of the work. Fear was formless; cargo had weight.

"Next."

The older soldier had said it without looking up. The younger one looked up. Of course he did.

Zephyrine moved at the pace of a man with wet boots and a long walk behind him, which was true, and stopped at the barrier, and waited, because waiting was the older soldier's job and the younger one would learn that or he wouldn't.

"Purpose in town?"

"Fish," Zephyrine said. "What's left of it."

The older soldier almost smiled. The younger one didn't. "Open the bag."

Zephyrine opened the bag. Slowly. There was a speed at which you opened a bag for a soldier that said *I have nothing to hide and also I resent you*, and Zephyrine had practiced it in front of a cracked mirror in a rooming house in the interior until it was perfect: the sigh through the nose, the fumble with the buckle that was not a fumble, the flat way the items came out one at a time and were laid on the barrier table like offerings.

The netting needle. The twine. The lamp oil. The bread.

"Bit early in the season for net work," the young soldier said.

"Bit early in the season for the republic to still exist," Zephyrine said, "but here we are."

The older soldier did laugh then, one syllable, quickly smothered, and the young one flushed and looked at his sergeant, and the sergeant looked at the rain, and in the space between those two looks Zephyrine understood the whole checkpoint, the whole town, the whole rotting apparatus of the state: nobody here believed in the barrier. They were holding a gate for a government that had stopped writing back, and they knew it, and the knowing made them either cruel or soft, and this morning, in the rain, it had made them soft.

"Coat," the young soldier said, because he had something to prove and the bag had given him nothing.

There it was. The arithmetic, live, in real time, rain running down Zephyrine's neck. *If he takes the coat—*

But the coat was not the risk. The coat was the solution. The coat had been worn into truth. The young soldier took it, turned it, felt along the seams with the flat of his hand — Zephyrine watched his fingers and did not watch his fingers, kept the face fixed in the mild boredom of a man standing in the rain — and the fingers passed over the left breast, over the restitched lining, over the oilskin and the card and the folded dispatch, and found what they were supposed to find, which was a coat that had been slept in and rained on and souped on and worn by a fisherman who was cold and wet and wanted his coat back.

The stitches were the whole art. The woman in the interior had restitched the lining with thread a half-shade off from the original, and had made the stitches slightly uneven, and had then — this was the genius of it — rubbed the seam with a damp cloth and let Zephyrine wear it dirty, so the repair read not as a repair but as a wear-point, the kind of seam a working coat develops at the breast where a man keeps his pipe or his knife. A soldier's fingers read coats the way blind men read type. They were looking for new thread. They found old thread.

"Name?"

"Sterling."

"Residence?"

"Harbor row. The blue door with the broken shutter, if it's still standing."

The young soldier wrote it down, which meant nothing, because the garrison's lists were three months out of date and the clerk who kept them had been conscripted in the autumn, but the writing down was a ritual too, and Zephyrine had learned to respect other people's rituals the way you respected a dog's — not because they made sense, but because the animal was calmer after.

"Go on, then," the sergeant said. "Before the rain finishes the job."

Zephyrine gathered the satchel's contents back into it — needle, twine, oil, bread, Marta's letter — took the coat from the young soldier's arms with a nod that was neither grateful nor insolent, and walked through the barrier into Vessany Harbor.

The fear stayed in the back of the cart. It always did, on the far side. That was the strange arithmetic of checkpoints: the fear was heaviest before the gate and lightest after, and then it began to grow again immediately, because after one gate there was always the next.

* * *

The town smelled of tar and wet stone and, underneath, faintly, of hunger — that particular thin smell a town gets when the fish are gone and the grain is requisitioned and the bakeries are making bread out of what bread used to be. Zephyrine walked the harbor road with the satchel loose at the hip and counted what had changed since the last crossing, which was a courier's second habit, after the inventory.

The chandlery on the corner was shuttered. It had been open in the autumn. The shipwright's yard had half its slips empty, and the two hulls still under construction were stopped mid-rib, the way everything in the republic had stopped mid-thing — mid-sentence, mid-harvest, mid-war — waiting for a government to finish a thought it had apparently lost the thread of months ago. A queue stood outside the requisition office, silent, orderly, the orderliness of people who had learned that disorder got you nothing and got it faster.

And on the customs house wall, new paper over old paper over older paper: the proclamations, layered like sediment, the republic's entire recent history in paste and pulp. Zephyrine stopped, as a bored fisherman would, and read the top layer.

It was not about victory. That was the change. The winter's proclamations had been about victory, shouted louder each month as the shouting worked less. This one was about order. *By decree of the Provisional Authority for the Maritime Districts, all documentation pertaining to the movement of persons shall be consolidated under military administration. All guarantees, permits, and registers issued by civil offices are subject to review.*

Zephyrine read it twice. *Subject to review.* The phrase had the specific blandness of a sentence that would kill someone, later, in a room the sentence's author would never enter. Civil offices. Registers. Persons.

The rain thinned. Somewhere up the hill a church bell struck the hour — the customs house clock, actually, the great brass clock that had kept harbor time for a hundred years, and Zephyrine realized with a small cold start that the bell was wrong. Slow. Four minutes slow, maybe five. The clock had never been slow. The clock had been the one thing in Vessany Harbor that did not lie, and now it was losing time, and no one had climbed the tower to correct it, because the man who corrected it had been a civil employee, and civil employees were being consolidated, or reviewed, or simply forgotten.

A republic died the way a clock ran down, Zephyrine thought. Not all at once. Four minutes at a time, until the hour it struck was no longer the hour at all, and everyone adjusted their lives to the lie without ever agreeing to.

They turned up toward the customs house anyway. The dispatch could wait an hour — the garrison at the Cape would not stir before dark — but there was a name on a list in that building, and Zephyrine had been carrying that name for six years in a place no soldier's fingers would ever search, and the proclamations on the wall had just made carrying it feel different.

The customs house door was open. Inside, out of the rain, a clerk was moving along a shelf of registers with a lamp, checking spines against a paper in her hand, and Zephyrine knew her — Odile Ferrand, the one clerk left in the building who still wound the clock, the one who had once, in peacetime, argued a harbor fee down to nothing for a fishing widow and then lied about it to her superiors with a straight face. Zephyrine had liked her instantly and had never told her so.

Odile looked up at the sound of boots, and her face did the thing faces did now, the quick flicker of *friend or requisition*, and settled on something tired and almost warm.

"Sterling. You're wet."

"I'm always wet."

"Everyone's always wet." She set down her lamp. "If you've come for harbor fees, the fees are now a military matter, which means they are nothing, which means they are whatever a captain says they are at the moment he says it."

"I came about the register," Zephyrine said. "The passage register. The one Ferrand keeps — the one *you* keep."

Something closed in Odile's face, not hostility, but a door being held. "Who told you there was such a register?"

"The same wind that told everyone. Two hundred names, Odile. People promised safe passage out when the coast goes. Promised by the civil authority, in writing, with seals."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the rain picked up again against the high windows, and somewhere in the tower the slow clock began to strike the quarter, each stroke a little late, a little flatter, like a heart that had started counting wrong.

Odile looked at Zephyrine for a long moment, and then at the door, and then she did the thing Zephyrine would remember afterward, in all the years after, as the exact moment the shape of everything changed: she reached up and drew the bolt across the customs house door, quietly, the way you bolt a door when the danger is already inside the town.

"Two hundred and six," she said. "And you'd better sit down, Sterling, because one of the names is yours."
