## 2. The Ledger of Names

Zephyrine did not sit down.

They stood in the middle of the customs house with the rain loud on the high windows and the bolted door at their back, and they heard themselves say, in a voice that sounded borrowed from some calmer person, "That's not possible. I'm not a passenger. I'm a courier."

"That's what they'll have written you down as, probably." Odile was already moving behind her desk, keys out, the great iron ring of them held flat against her palm so they wouldn't sing. "But paper doesn't care what you are. Paper only cares what it says." She nodded at the stool on Zephyrine's side of the counter. "Sit. Or don't. But you're going to want both hands."

The customs house at Vellane Harbor had been built by people who expected it to last longer than the country around it — stone walls two feet thick, windows set high like a ship's, a floor of black oak worn pale in the paths where four generations of clerks had walked from shelf to shelf to counter. In peacetime the smell had been ink and tar and cold stone. Now there was a fourth smell underneath, faint and wrong: damp plaster, the slow rot of a building whose roof fund had been reallocated three wars ago to something more urgent. Everything urgent. Everything always more urgent than the thing that would actually save you.

Odile stopped at the third shelf, second row, and pulled down a ledger as thick as a Bible.

It was bound in gray sailcloth, not the red morocco of official registers, and Zephyrine understood before she said anything what that meant. Gray binding meant unofficial. Meant drafted, provisional, *we'll see*. The kind of document a republic produced when it wanted to make a promise without quite committing to the shape of its own signature.

"This isn't in any catalog," Odile said. She set it on the counter between them with both hands, the way you'd set down something sleeping. "There's no stamp on it. There's no docket number. If you asked me tomorrow, in front of witnesses, whether this book existed, I would say no, and I would be telling the truth in every way the law cares about." She looked up. Her eyes were the color of the harbor in February, and just now they had that same flat, waiting quality, water over something dark. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"You're telling me it's deniable."

"I'm telling you it's *already been denied*. Twice. Once by the ministry that authorized it and once by the man who countersigned it, who has since been reassigned somewhere inland where nobody can ask him questions." She untied the cloth strap that held it shut. "Two hundred and six names. Harbor towns get evacuation guarantees when the government wants the fishing fleet kept running, because fish is protein and protein keeps a garrison alive. That's the whole bargain. You keep hauling nets, and if it comes to the worst, we take your people out by sea. Two hundred and six souls signed for passage on neutral hulls, costs borne by the republic." Her mouth did something that was not a smile. "Except the republic is currently costing itself out of existence, so."

Zephyrine sat down after all. Not because Odile told them to. Because their knees made the decision independently.

* * *

The ledger opened to a page of columns, and the columns were beautiful, which was the first terrible thing about it.

Whoever had drawn it up — Odile herself, Zephyrine realized, recognizing the hand, the small precise numerals with their distinctive crossed sevens — had done it properly. Name. Age. Occupation. Address. Dependents. A column for vessel assignment, mostly blank. A final column headed simply **STATUS**, in which every entry so far read the same word in the same careful letters: *verified*.

"It's real," Zephyrine said slowly. "This isn't a fraud to sell berths."

"It's real. That's the point of it, and it's also the problem with it. A fraud only hurts the fool who buys it. This one—" Odile tapped the STATUS column. "Two hundred and six people have arranged their lives around this book. Sold what couldn't be carried. Told their children. One family in the Low Quarter sold their boat, Sterling. Their *boat*. To a broker, for half value, cash, because they believed the republic's promise written in my hand." She straightened. "So when you came through that door tonight asking me what's worth carrying across a checkpoint, this is my answer. Not the codes sewn into your coat. Codes are replaceable; I could dictate new ones to you in an hour. This book exists twice — here, and nowhere. And the nowhere copy is the one that counts."

Zephyrine looked down the page. Names ran past like water. They read them the way you can't stop yourself reading names, each one briefly becoming a person: *Maren Kovač, netmender, 54, dependents: none. Tomas and Edda Brill, shipwrights, dependents: two.* On and on, the whole fragile population of a harbor town rendered into a list, and there was something obscene in how neatly it fit, how two hundred and six entire lives compressed into eleven pages and a gray cloth cover.

Then they turned a page and stopped breathing.

*Marchetti, Ilse. 26. Assistant keeper, Vellane Light. Dependents: Aurel Marchetti, keeper, 61.*

STATUS: *verified.*

The room went very quiet. The rain didn't help. Rain never helps; it just gives the silence something to hide behind.

"You knew," Zephyrine said.

"I put her name in myself. Third week of March." Odile's voice was gentle in a way she clearly resented having to make it. "She came down from the light on her father's cart, stood in this room dripping on my floor, and asked me — politely, Sterling, she thanked me for the *forms* — whether the guarantee covered lighthouse personnel or only harbor. As if she weren't sure she'd qualify. As if keeping the fire burning over the entrance channel might not count as enough." Odile shook her head once, a sharp motion. "I wrote her name before she finished the sentence."

Zephyrine ran a thumb along the edge of the page, not touching the ink. Fourteen months since they'd last stood in the same room. Fourteen months, and here she was anyway, in blue-black iron gall ink, age twenty-six, occupation assistant keeper, status verified. Alive on paper. Guaranteed.

In peacetime — and God, it had actually been peacetime, they had all walked around inside it without once looking up — Zephyrine had spent a week at Vellane Light waiting for a weather window that never opened, and most of that week in the lamp room arguing with Ilse Marchetti about nothing. About whether charts lied. About whether her father's dog could count. About whether it was sentimental nonsense to believe a place could owe you anything. They had climbed the gallery rail at dusk to watch the fishing fleet come home through the fairway, and Zephyrine had said something flippant — they could no longer remember what, only that it had been flippant, that flippancy had been their native language then — and Ilse had looked at them with those lighthouse-keeper's eyes that measured distance in nautical miles and patience in years, and said: *You're the kind of person who promises things to be liked. Don't promise me anything unless you mean it as arithmetic.*

So Zephyrine had meant it as arithmetic. That was the part they'd forgotten until this exact moment, standing in front of her name in a dead language of columns. They hadn't said *of course I'll protect you*, the way people said it, airily, meaning nothing. They had said: whatever comes, if it ever comes to running, I will carry you out myself, and they had even — this was the detail that undid them — worked out the tide math on the back of a chart scrap, which tide, which window, which cove. Ilse had folded the chart scrap and put it under the lamp-room clock, and said, *Good. Now it's a schedule,* and gone up to trim the wick.

Fourteen months. And the whole time, apparently, her name had been sitting in a gray-cloth ledger in the customs house, verified, while Zephyrine crossed borders with other people's secrets in their coat lining.

"She doesn't know," Zephyrine said. "About the seizure risk. About—"

"Nobody knows. That's rather the definition of a secret list."

"No." Zephyrine heard their own voice go flat and hard, and noted it distantly, like weather approaching. "I mean she doesn't know it's already failing. Odile. Look at me and tell me how bad it is. Not the official version. Yours."

Odile didn't answer right away. She went to the window instead — the high one, the one you needed the step-stool for — and stood a moment with her hands folded behind her back, listening to the rain the way a sailor listens to rigging.

"There was a requisition order drafted for this district six weeks ago," she said finally. "I know because I filed the correspondence, because filing correspondence is what I am for. It's sitting in a ministry tray somewhere waiting for a signature, and the only reason it hasn't been signed is that the man whose signature it needs can't decide yet whether Vellane gets garrisoned or burned, and he expresses his indecision by going hunting." She stepped down off the stool. "When that order is signed — not if, Sterling, when — the army will seal this town inside a day. Customs house first, because customs houses hold records, and armies have learned that records are ammunition. Every name in this book becomes, technically, documentation belonging to an enemy population. Do you understand? The moment they march in, this ledger stops being a promise and starts being evidence against every person in it."

"And the armistice lists."

"Are being drafted from exactly these kinds of records. Which means whoever holds the paper holds the amnesty. Which means—" she spread her hands, palms up, empty— "the republic made a promise, wrote it down, and then handed the writing to the men with matches."

Zephyrine looked at Ilse Marchetti's name for a long time. Verified. The word had been written in March, in a warm room, by a woman who believed in forms. It looked so permanent. Ink always looks permanent. That's its whole con.

* * *

They talked logistics for an hour. Odile was thorough: which neutral captains still honored their contracts, which cove, which tides, which of the two hundred and six had skills worth a berth on any boat at all. Zephyrine copied none of it down. Copying was for people who expected to keep their notes.

At the end of it Odile re-strapped the ledger, and Zephyrine said, "Give it to me."

"No."

"Odile—"

"If I give it to you, you carry it through a checkpoint sewn into a coat, and if they find it, they find two hundred and six enemies in one convenient bundle, plus you. No. It stays where it is, behind the third shelf, until I have a better idea, and meanwhile you—" she jabbed a finger at Zephyrine's chest, the first unclerkly gesture Zephyrine had ever seen her make— "you will finish what you came here to finish, and then you will get yourself to the light and tell that girl her name is on a list, because if this goes wrong she deserves to hear it from a friend and not from a proclamation nailed to a doorpost."

"A proclamation."

"They've started posting them in the occupied districts. Lists of persons declared hostile. Collaborators, fifth-columnists — the vocabulary improves weekly." Odile unbolted the door, slowly, and stood with her hand on the iron. Rain came in on a draft, cold and salt. "Go home, Sterling. Sleep if you can. The quarter-hour clock is lying to you anyway; everything in this town is running slightly late and slightly flat."

Outside, Vellane lay dark under the rain except for one light in all that blackness — the lighthouse, far out on the mole, turning its steady white blade across the water. Three seconds bright, four dark. Ilse's rotation. Zephyrine stood in the street and counted it, three and four, three and four, the way you'd count someone breathing.

They thought: *she's there. She's there right now, tonight, and her name is safe on a shelf, and the republic still stands, technically, in the way a house stands while it's falling.*

They were halfway to the harbor inn before they let themselves think the other thing, the thing that had been circling since Odile drew the bolt:

That in fourteen months they had never once sent word. Not a letter, not a favor, not a line on a shipping manifest. They had left the promise under a lamp-room clock and walked away and called it kept.

And tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever the ministry man finished his indecision, an army was going to walk into this town and pick up that gray cloth book, and read it, and find her name.

Zephyrine turned back toward the mole, toward the light, and had taken perhaps ten steps when the first bugle sounded from the eastern ridge — thin, doubled, answered from the south.

Not a patrol. Patrols didn't sound assembly.

They stood in the rain in the empty street, counting the light sweep past — three seconds bright, four dark — and understood that they were no longer deciding whether to warn her.

Dawn was coming up behind the ridge, gray and early, and with it, out of the mist above the harbor road, came the first column of soldiers.
