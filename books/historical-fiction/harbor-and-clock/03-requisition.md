## 3. Requisition

Odile Ferrand had worked in the customs house for nineteen years, and in all that time she had never once opened the shutters before the bell of the garrison church finished its sixth stroke. It was not superstition. It was order. A town could lose its harbor, its fish market, even its government — and God knew hers had lost all three by degrees — but as long as the customs house opened at the same hour every morning, the town could tell itself it still had a spine.

So when she came down Rue des Salines in the grey before dawn and saw soldiers already on the steps, she understood before anyone told her. The spine was being removed.

They were regular army, not militia — she could tell by the way they stood, shoulder to shoulder, bored and obedient, rifles slung with the muzzles down the way regulations required and no one ever followed. There were eight of them, and an officer in front consulting a paper under a lantern that some orderly held for him. The lantern light made his face orange and weightless, like a mask floating above his collar.

Odile stopped halfway across the quay. Behind her, the first gulls were waking, screaming at nothing. Ahead of her, two more soldiers were unrolling coils of wire across the customs house doors, threading them through the iron rings where in peacetime the fishing boats' mooring lines had been tied when the weather turned.

"You there," the officer said, without looking up. "Customs clerk?"

"Deputy registrar," Odile said. "Ferrand."

"Good. You have keys."

It was not a question, so she did not answer it. She looked past him instead, down the long face of the quay, and saw what she should have noticed from the corner: soldiers at the harbor mouth, dragging barriers across the road; a corporal nailing a paper to the fish market post; beyond the breakwater, out on the flat dark water, the low silhouette of a gunboat sitting where no gunboat had any business sitting, its running lights burning sullen red.

The town was sealed. Not threatened, not patrolled — sealed, the way you seal a jar.

"There's a requisition order," the officer said. He held the paper out now, at arm's length, as if handing her something unpleasant he'd found in the street. "Effective this hour. The building is annexed for military administration. All records are confiscated for inspection."

"All records," Odile repeated.

"All documents. Ledgers, registers, correspondence." He folded the order along its existing crease, precisely, and put it back in his coat. He had the particular neatness of a man who had done paperwork all his life and resented having to stand in the cold doing the work the paperwork described. "You may take personal effects. Nothing else leaves."

Odile had spent nineteen years learning that the fastest way through an official's sentence was to find the word doing the least work and pull on it. She was already tired, already cold, and the gunboat's lights were making her stomach hurt.

"Inspection," she said. "By whom?"

"The military administration."

"For how long?"

"Until concluded."

"And the records of open cases? Vessels in transit? Persons holding transit guarantees?"

Something moved behind the officer's eyes — not guilt, exactly. Recognition. He knew exactly which ledger she meant, because someone upstairs had written her a list, and persons holding transit guarantees would have been near the top of it, underlined.

"That's covered," he said.

"It isn't. Transit guarantees are instruments of the state. They're not documents, they're obligations—"

"They're paper," the officer said, and for one moment his voice dropped its administrative polish and became simply tired. "Madame Ferrand. Open the doors."

* * *

She opened the doors because eight men with rifles were not a negotiation. But she opened them slowly, and she went in first, and she lit the lamp over the counter with hands that were almost steady.

Inside, the customs house smelled the way it always had: ink, salt, pipe smoke from the harbormaster's old chair nobody had removed since he died, and the faint sweetness of the tallow Odile used on the drawer runners so they wouldn't swell in the damp. In nineteen years she had learned every sound the building made — the tick of the roof timbers warming, the groan of the cellar door when the tide was high. This morning it sounded like any other morning. That was the obscene part. Buildings don't know.

The soldiers came in behind her and stood in the entrance hall, uncertain, until the officer waved them forward. Then they began.

They were not vandals. That surprised her, and then she understood it shouldn't. Vandals destroy out of passion; these men destroyed out of inventory. They carried the ledgers off the shelves two at a time and stacked them on the counter by size, spines aligned, the way she herself would have stacked them. One young soldier picked up the register of wrecks — sixty years of drowned ships in a hand too beautiful for its subject — turned it over looking for something worth taking, found nothing, and set it facedown on the pile with everyone else's lives.

Odile stood at the counter with her keyring in her pocket and counted. It was what she knew how to do. Incoming registries: eleven. Outgoing: nine. The tonnage books. The bond files. The correspondence chests from the cellar, hauled up dripping rope and smelling of brine, and among them—

There.

The ledger. Green binding, half again as wide as the others, third shelf of the registrar's cabinet, where it had lived since spring. Two hundred names inside. Two hundred people who had been promised, in ink, with stamps, under seals that had seemed meaningful at the time: safe passage, when passage came.

She had shown it to Zephyrine Sterling only days ago. She could still see their face in this exact lamplight, bent over the open page, finger moving down the column of names, slowing — she had watched it slow — until it stopped on *Ilse Marchetti*, and Zephyrine had gone very quiet in a way that Odile, who had raised three brothers and buried two, recognized as the quiet of a person standing at the edge of something.

"Third shelf," the officer said behind her. "That one too."

"No," Odile said.

The room stopped. Not dramatically — the soldiers didn't freeze or level rifles. They just paused, the way men pause when a woman says *no* in a tone they've been trained to hear.

"Madame Ferrand—"

"That is not enemy documentation." Her voice came out lower than she intended, almost conversational, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "That is a registry of protected persons. Every name in it was entered under the evacuation statutes, countersigned by the ministry, stamped with the republic's own seal. If you take it, you are confiscating the republic's signature on two hundred promises. You're not seizing enemy papers. You're seizing your own."

She heard herself and marveled a little. Nineteen years of arguing with fishermen about tariffs had built, somewhere inside her, a person who could say exactly this, in exactly this voice, while eight soldiers and a tired officer watched.

The officer crossed to the counter. He put his hand on the green ledger's cover, flat, the way you'd steady an animal.

"I have a list," he said quietly. "Categories. Documentation concerning enemy persons is to be segregated and forwarded. This—" he turned it so she could see the stencil already chalked on its cover, *ENEMY CORRESPONDENCE*, in a hand that had never read a page of it, "—has been categorized. I don't do the categorizing."

"Who does?"

"Madame." He almost smiled, and it was the worst expression she'd seen all morning. "I stamp things."

He picked up the ledger. It was heavy; he shifted it in his grip, and for an absurd instant Odile wanted to step forward and show him how to hold it, spine down, both hands, the way it deserved.

And something in her — something that had been standing very still for nineteen years behind a counter, stamping, filing, recording, believing in the difference between right form and wrong form — stepped forward instead.

She put both hands on the ledger.

She was stronger than he expected. She got real purchase on it, got it half out of his grip, felt the cover bend under her fingers, and heard herself saying, over and over, words she couldn't later reconstruct: *these are people, these are names, you cannot, these are people* — and then two soldiers had her arms and the officer had the ledger back and the room resumed around her like water closing over a stone.

"Take her to the quartermaster's office," the officer said. His breathing hadn't changed. "Not the cells. The office. Lock it and put a man on the door."

"Charges, sir?" one of the soldiers asked.

"What?" He looked at her, really looked, over the ledger in his arms. "No charges. Interference with military administration. Write it down." He hesitated, and then, in a different voice, almost gentle, almost apologetic, he said the thing she would carry out of the room with her, the thing that would keep her awake in whatever room they locked her in:

"It's only paper, madame. Whatever's in it. It's only paper."

* * *

They marched her up the quay through the waking town. Shutters cracked open ahead of them and closed again. At the fountain, old Marek stood with his milk cans and did not move aside fast enough, and a soldier shoved past him and the cans went over, white spreading across the stones, and nobody shouted, which was worse than shouting. The whole town was learning, in real time, the same lesson: look at the stones, not the soldiers. Count your cans later.

At the fish market post, Odile read the proclamation as they passed, because reading proclamations was another thing nineteen years had taught her to do without slowing down. Sealed zone. Requisitioned port. All persons lacking military documentation subject to detention. And at the bottom, in the list of designated categories, a phrase she read twice, then a third time, feeling the ground tilt under her professional composure:

*...including irregular persons and enemy collaborators per attached schedule.*

Attached schedule. Somewhere in some staff officer's baggage was a list of names, and the green ledger was on its way to becoming that list's source. Every promise the republic had made, boxed up and relabeled as evidence against the very people it had promised to.

In the quartermaster's office they sat her in a chair and a very young soldier locked the door from the outside, and then, after a moment, unlocked it again and left it ajar, and stood in the corridor where he thought she couldn't see him, not quite facing away. She filed that.

The office had one window, high, barred, looking out over the harbor. From it she could see the customs house steps, the soldiers carrying the stacks out to a requisitioned dray, ledgers thumping into the bed like sacks of grain. She could see the gunboat. She could see, far down the coast, gray in the early light, the lighthouse — dark. Dark at dawn was normal. She stared at it anyway, because Ilse Marchetti was in that ledger, and Zephyrine Sterling had come asking about her with a tenderness Odile hadn't seen from anyone in months, and now both of those facts existed only in heads, in memories, in a woman sitting locked in a quartermaster's office watching the paper version of them loaded onto a cart.

Paper burns. Paper gets lost, miscategorized, forwarded, misfiled, burned again. She had seen forty years of records die of natural causes. These wouldn't even get natural causes.

Very well, she thought. Her hands had stopped shaking somewhere around the fountain, though she noticed it only now, distantly, as if reporting on someone else. Very well. If they want to treat it as paper, let them. Paper has one weakness.

It has to be read aloud.

She closed her eyes and began, silently, methodically, in the order she'd shelved it, going back through the pages she had handled most: *Abelard, Tomas. Abelard, Yvette. Achterberg, Pieter. Amado, Rosa…*

Out in the corridor, the young soldier shifted his weight. The dray creaked away toward wherever they kept things they meant to burn. And Odile Ferrand, deputy registrar, arrested without charges, began to memorize.
