## 4. The Salt Road

The marsh began where the road ended, which was the point of it.

Zephyrine had stood at the edge of the causeway for a full minute before committing — long enough that Bram Voss, ten paces ahead with the easy stride of a man walking his own hallway, turned around and looked at them.

"You can stand there until the patrols come," he said, "or you can walk. Standing's the only option that ends badly."

Zephyrine stepped off the road and into the mud.

It took them at the ankle immediately, a cold grip through the boot leather, and for one absurd moment they thought of the checkpoint two days behind them — the young corporal who had run his hands along their coat lining and felt, surely, the ridge of stitched paper against the wool, and let them pass anyway because his hands were cold and his tea was waiting. Everything they carried was measured that way now: in what it would cost if found. The codes in the lining would cost a firing squad. The forged travel pass in their breast pocket would cost prison. The memory of Odile Ferrand's face as she'd shown them the ledger — *two hundred names, look, yours could be among them* — would cost something they hadn't finished pricing yet.

They walked. The mud released each footstep with a sound like a kiss given reluctantly.

"Keep to my line," Bram said without turning. "The channels drain twice a day and fill fast. Step wrong and you go into water past your waist, and the water here isn't clean. Cut your leg in it and you'll know within a day whether you keep the leg."

"How comforting."

"I'm not paid to comfort."

"Are you paid at all?" Zephyrine asked. "I have nothing. The republic that owes me four months' wages no longer exists in any functional sense."

Bram stopped. He didn't turn all the way — just enough of a rotation of the shoulders to show a face that was weathered into something like kindness by force rather than temperament, forty or fifty years old, hard to say, with eyes the flat grey of the sky over the flats.

"Odile Ferrand sent me," he said. "That's what I'm paid. She pulled me out of a customs cell six years ago when I was caught running brandy without a flag, and she never once mentioned it afterward, which is how you know a favor is real." He started walking again. "She sent word through the fishwives that you'd need out of town before dawn, and that you were worth more than the trouble you come with."

"Worth more than—"

"She didn't specify the currency." A shrug rolled down his back. "Come on. The tide's our friend until noon and our enemy after."

* * *

They crossed the first channel on a plank bridge so old it had gone silver and soft, its boards flexing underfoot like something alive. On the far side the marsh rose into low hummocks of samphire and thrift, and the smell changed — salt and sulfur and rot, the honest stink of a place that pretended to nothing. Behind them the town sat under its morning smoke, harbor cranes motionless, and Zephyrine made themselves look at it properly: the customs house with army trucks already drawn up outside it like feeding animals, the flag on the signal tower replaced overnight, the streets empty in a way streets should never be empty.

Two hundred names in a ledger. One of them Ilse Marchetti. And soldiers carrying the ledger away into a building sealed like a tomb.

"You keep looking back," Bram said. "Looking back is how people drown in the flats. They forget their feet exist."

"A man I know is in a cell because he showed me a book."

"Odile's in a cell because she showed half this town that book. You were last in a queue of witnesses." He said it without cruelty — a correction of fact, the way you'd tell someone the tide time. "Anyway, she knew the risk when she opened the drawer. That woman has been risking her neck for other people's paperwork since before you had an opinion about anything."

"Do you know what's in the ledger?"

"No."

"Passage guarantees. Two hundred people promised evacuation to neutral water if it ever came to this. It came to this, and the army confiscated the promise and called it enemy documentation." Zephyrine heard their own voice rise and flattened it. "There's a girl on that list. Ilse Marchetti. Her father keeps the lighthouse north of town."

"The dark lighthouse."

"It's not dark by choice anymore." They walked a while. Somewhere off to the left a curlew cried, three descending notes, like a question asked twice too often. "I knew her before any of this. Before the war, before the collapse, when the republic still pretended to be a thing you could believe in. I swore I'd get her out if it ever went bad. It was an easy promise then. Promises made in peacetime are free."

"And now they're not," Bram said.

"Now they're not."

He grunted — not agreement exactly. Acknowledgment. They came down off the hummocks onto firmer ground, a shingle spit between two channels, and he finally stopped and dug in his coat and produced a flask and bread wrapped in waxed cloth. He handed over the bread first, which Zephyrine chose to read as a gesture of some kind.

"Eat," he said. "You'll want your legs after the next crossing."

Zephyrine ate standing up, watching the water in the channels slide seaward with visible purpose, baring mudflats that gleamed like hammered tin. "Can I ask you something honest, Voss?"

"You'll ask it regardless."

"When the borders moved last spring — when your side of the estuary became their side of the estuary — did you stop smuggling? Or did the routes just change?"

Bram considered the flask, drank, wiped the mouth of it on his sleeve. "Routes changed."

"That's what I thought. So borders move and men like you cross them anyway, because cargo is cargo and channels don't care whose flag is flying. But promises — everyone says promises stop at the border. That's the whole argument for every treaty ever signed. My government can't protect you past this line, therefore we owe you nothing past this line. And I keep thinking: the channels didn't stop existing. Why do the promises?"

Bram looked at them for a long moment. Something moved behind the flat grey eyes — surprise, maybe, that the courier with mud to the knee had a mind in working order.

"Because cargo doesn't testify," he said. "A crate of brandy doesn't come back in ten years and say you swore. People do." He screwed the cap onto the flask with exaggerated care. "You want the smuggler's answer? Nothing survives a border except what someone carries across it. Goods. Names. Grudges. Promises. All the same freight. The border doesn't kill any of it — it just makes it work. Makes it cost."

"So the promise survives."

"If someone carries it." He pointed the flask at them. "But hear the rest, because this is the part your type always skips. Carrying costs. You're out here in the wet with your coat full of paper because somebody's promise got expensive. Two hundred names in a ledger, and every one of them is a debt somebody wrote down and then walked away from. Now the bill's come due and the man who signed it is hiding in the hills calling himself a government-in-exile." He spat, expertly, downwind. "Promises survive borders, courier. They just don't survive cowards."

Zephyrine swallowed the last of the bread. It tasted of nothing, which was fine; it was fuel. "Then I'm carrying."

"We'll see. Carrying's a word too."

* * *

The second crossing was worse. Bram had been right about the tide's schedule being a kind of friendship, but friendship with the tide was conditional, and halfway across the wide channel the water came back early — a thin tongue of it first, finding the lowest prints, then a broad sheet rising past the ankle, past the calf, cold enough to make breath a deliberate act. Bram kept them angled upstream of the direct line, reading the water's return the way other men read print.

"Don't fight the pull," he said. "Let it push you toward the far bank. It's doing our work for us if you stop arguing."

Zephyrine argued with the water anyway, briefly lost their footing, and was hauled upright by a fist closed in their collar with a strength that didn't match Bram's lean frame.

"There," he said, releasing them. "Now you owe me a life. That's the exchange rate on this road. Everyone who crosses owes everyone else. Keeps the accounting simple."

"Is that why you're doing this? Odile's favor?"

"Partly." He waded on. His voice came back over his shoulder, half-lost in the wind off the flats. "And partly because I ran cargo through that harbor for fifteen years and watched your republic tax it honestly and stamp the papers fairly, and I never thought I'd see the day I'd be nostalgic for a customs house." A short laugh, no humor in it. "Now there's a captain sitting in it who burns what he can't use. You know what happens to a man like me when everything gets requisitioned? Smuggling needs two sides to exploit. One side, one law, no gaps — that's not smuggling weather. That's drowning weather."

So he needed the chaos. Zephyrine filed that away: allies had reasons, and reasons were load-bearing walls. Knock one down and the whole alliance came off its foundations.

On the far bank they wrung out what could be wrung and kept moving inland, up along a creek that wound through reeds tall enough to swallow them both. Bram navigated by small uglinesses — a broken fencepost, a burnt-out duck blind, a dead sheep he noted and stepped around with professional detachment — landmarks only a man avoiding roads would know.

"Where does this road end?" Zephyrine asked.

"Nowhere good. A drainage hut, then the dunes, then a choice of bad options." He glanced back. "Which brings us to you. Where are you actually going, courier? Because Odile said 'out of town,' and out of town is where her instructions end. South is the government road, and the government's gone quiet — no orders, no pay, just garrisons sitting on bridges asking questions nobody can answer. North is their lines and that captain with his fire barrel. West is open water, which requires a boat, and boats are being requisitioned faster than promises." He let that sit. "So. What's the destination?"

Zephyrine had been avoiding the question even inside their own head, and now it came out plainly, the way truths do when your boots are full of marsh water. "The lighthouse."

Bram stopped walking.

"You said yourself the north is their lines."

"I know what I said."

"Then say why."

"Because her name is in that ledger and mine might be too, and every hour I spend going anywhere else is an hour I've decided she can wait." Zephyrine heard how it sounded — the guilt sharpened into geometry, a straight line drawn from satchel to lighthouse — and hated it and meant it anyway. "I made a promise when keeping it was free. The price changed. The promise didn't. That's the whole of my philosophy, Voss, and it fits in a sentence, and I'm aware it will probably kill me."

For a moment Bram said nothing. Then he laughed — a real one this time, brief and rough, startled out of him.

"A sentence," he said. "God help me, Odile sent me a romantic." He started walking again, faster, and Zephyrine had to lengthen stride to keep up. "All right. North it is, since we're dying anyway. There's a fisherman's path along the creek that the patrols haven't found because it isn't on any map — the fishermen keep it off the maps on purpose. We can make the dunes by dusk if the tide behaves."

"*We*?"

"I'm not letting you walk into occupied country alone. You'd drown in the first creek and my life-debt would go uncollected." He said it lightly, but Zephyrine had learned in two days of collapse to hear the other thing underneath men's light voices. He'd watched the harbor seal shut this morning same as they had. He needed the gaps in the law to stay open, and right now the only gap running north went through Zephyrine's promise.

Fine. Load-bearing walls worked in both directions.

* * *

They reached the drainage hut at midafternoon: a low brick shed with a rusted sluice gate, half-sunk in the bank of the creek. Inside smelled of oilcloth and old smoke. Bram built a small fire from driftwood while Zephyrine peeled off their boots and examined feet gone white and soft as drowned paper.

While the fire burned, Bram talked — not sentimentally, but the way men talk when silence would mean thinking. He named the channels and their tempers. He named the patrol schedules by heart, corrected himself twice, and muttered that the schedules had gone to rot since the requisition, which cut both ways. And then, poking the fire, almost casually:

"You said two hundred names. How sure are you of that number?"

"Odile counted them in front of me. Twice."

"And you remember any besides the Marchetti girl?"

Zephyrine looked at him.

"Because numbers are cheap," Bram said, meeting their eyes. "Numbers are what governments trade. But a name, spoken by someone who knew the face that goes with it — that's harder to confiscate. If that ledger burns, courier, and it will burn, captains like this one always burn what frightens them — then what's left is whatever you're carrying in your head. So start carrying. Tonight, before you sleep, say me five names besides hers. Tomorrow, ten. By the time we reach the dunes you'll be a walking page, and pages get copied."

Zephyrine sat with that. Outside, the tide turned with a sound like a long exhale, and somewhere beyond the reeds a patrol engine ground along the distant coast road, paused, and moved on.

"Anselm Rey," they said at last. "Schoolteacher, near the chapel on the headland road. Wife named Marta. He signed for passage for his whole class of eleven-year-olds — eleven names after his own." Their voice held. "Odile read them out to me. I wasn't paying attention the way I should have been."

"Nobody ever is," Bram said. "That's the trick of it. Go again."

They went again. Five names, haltingly, then fragments — a cooper from the Tanner's Row, twin sisters who kept the ferry ticket office, an old harbor pilot whose name Zephyrine could only half-recall and whose face they couldn't summon at all, and the failure of it sat in their chest like a stone swallowed sideways. Two hundred people, reduced to a ledger, and the ledger in a locked room, and the room full of soldiers, and the nearest copy of the truth walking north through a marsh in the lining of a coat that held only codes.

When the fire had burned down to coals, Bram lay back with his hat over his eyes and said, to the rafters:

"One more thing you should carry. Word came through the fishwives yesterday, ahead of the tanks." A pause long enough that Zephyrine thought he'd fallen asleep. "There's a proclamation posted in every market square from here to the headland. Enemy collaborators, names and descriptions, arrest on sight." He lifted the hat brim a fraction. "I didn't read all of it. But I read one name twice, because I'd been hearing it from you since before dawn."

The coals ticked. The curlew called somewhere out on the darkening flats.

"What name?" Zephyrine asked, already knowing.

"Marchetti," Bram said. "Ilse Marchetti. Daughter of the lighthouse keeper. Collaborator, it says." He settled the hat back down. "So when we go north, courier — and we're going — understand what we're walking into. It's not a rescue anymore. It's a race."

Zephyrine sat in the failing light with their ruined feet stretched toward the embers, and began, silently, deliberately, to memorize the names.
