## 5. Enemy by Declaration

The poster was pasted to the wall of the fish auction hall sometime in the night, and by morning the rain had already begun to take it apart.

Zephyrine stopped three steps away from it, the way a person stops when they recognize their own handwriting on a stranger's door. The paper was the gray of army stock, wider than the handbills the town crier used, and someone with authority had nailed it at eye level so that no one could pretend not to see. The rain ran down the face of it in slow gray ropes, but the ink held.

PROCLAMATION No. 44, it read, in a typeface that had never once been used for anything kind. By order of the Provisional Command of the Occupied Coast, the following persons are declared enemies of the transition and are stripped of protection under military law. They may not be sheltered. They may not be traded with. They may not be spoken for.

There were eleven names. A harbor pilot who had once guided a blockade runner through the northern channel. Two brothers who owned the boatyard on the east quay. A woman whose only documented crime, so far as Zephyrine knew, was having married across the border twenty years ago, before the border meant what it meant now.

And then, fourth from the bottom, in the same flat type as all the rest:

MARCHETTI, ILSE — lightkeeper's daughter — collaborator with enemy signals activity. Status: at large.

Zephyrine read it four times. The word *collaborator* sat in its sentence like a stone in a shoe — small, wrong-shaped, impossible to stop feeling. *Signals activity.* Ilse had never signaled anything in her life except the hour and the weather and the safe edge of the shoals. She had grown up in a lamp room. She knew the grammar of light the way Odile knew the grammar of ledgers: as a language whose entire purpose was to keep people from breaking against the dark.

Someone had decided that this made her dangerous.

A fishwife brushed past Zephyrine, glanced at the poster, and did not slow down. That was the thing about proclamations, Zephyrine thought. They didn't need to be believed. They only needed to be seen, and then stepped around, and then, eventually, obeyed without being remembered as anything anyone had chosen.

*At large.* Which meant she had run. Which meant they were looking.

Zephyrine stood in the drizzle with the satchel against their ribs — lighter now, the codes delivered two days ago into a ministry office where a clerk had accepted them without counting them, which told Zephyrine everything about how much the government still valued its own words — and felt something turn over inside them with the heavy, deliberate motion of a keel coming about.

They had promised her. Four years ago, in peacetime, when peacetime had seemed like a permanent condition rather than an intermission. They had stood on the gallery of the lighthouse with the whole sea going gold below, and Ilse had laughed at some joke Zephyrine couldn't even remember now, and Zephyrine had said it plainly, because plainness was the only currency Zephyrine had ever trusted: *If it ever goes wrong, I will come for you. Whatever it costs.*

She had said, *Nothing here will ever go wrong.* She had said it like a fact about tides.

Zephyrine turned away from the poster and walked, not fast, because fast drew eyes, toward the north quay, where the fishing fleet rotted at anchor under new flags.

* * *

Bram Voss found them at the tar sheds, because Bram always found them; it was one of his less charming talents.

"Seen the wall," he said, without greeting. He leaned against the shed door, arms folded, a man built out of angles and salt, his face carrying the particular blankness he wore when he was angry and had decided not to spend it yet.

"I've seen the wall."

"They posted a second copy at the customs house. And a third up by the chapel road, which is either stupidity or a message, and I don't like either option." He picked at a splinter of tar on the doorframe. "Marchetti girl."

"Yes."

"You know where she is?"

"No." And that was the truth that had been sitting in Zephyrine's chest since dawn like swallowed ballast: *no.* The lighthouse had gone dark six nights ago. Zephyrine had noticed it from the ridge above the salt marshes on the second night and told themselves it was maintenance, or fuel shortages, or the army's blackout orders — plausible explanations, and plausibility was a drug Zephyrine had been taking in increasing doses. But the coast between here and the point had other lighthouses, and those were dark too, ordered dark, and still the Marchetti light had been different, because it hadn't gone dark when the others did. It had stayed lit three extra nights, a stubborn ordinary candle of it, and then it hadn't.

Six nights. Zephyrine had spent six nights constructing reasons not to be afraid, and every one of those reasons had been written in the same handwriting as the poster.

"The keeper," Bram said. "The old man. Anyone hear from him?"

"He doesn't use the radio anymore. Since the requisition he won't touch it — says the frequencies belong to the army now, and he'd rather be deaf than overheard." Zephyrine heard their own voice go flat, reciting facts, and hated the sound of it. "I sent word through the oyster buyers last week. Nothing came back."

"Nothing came back," Bram repeated. He let that sit. Then: "You're going up there."

"I haven't said—"

"You've been saying nothing else since you read that poster. Your whole face is saying it. You have the most legible face of any courier I've ever met, which is a professional catastrophe, by the way." He pushed off the doorframe. "So. You're going up the point, alone, into a district under martial proclamation, to find a woman whose name is currently worth a patrol's promotion to whoever brings her in. And you want me to tell you it's a bad idea so you can ignore me with a clear conscience."

"That would be convenient, yes."

"It's a bad idea." He said it evenly, without heat. "It's also the only idea you've got, and I know how that feels." He looked out at the harbor, at the gray water slapping the black hulls. "Patrols changed their pattern Tuesday. They run the coast road in pairs now, motorbikes, and there's a foot post at the old toll bridge that wasn't there before. Somebody upstairs decided the point matters."

"Or somebody downstairs decided the lighthouse matters."

"Same difference, if you're the one walking into it."

Zephyrine looked at him. Under the bluffness, under the smuggler's arithmetic, there was something else, and it took Zephyrine a moment to name it, because Bram Voss wore sympathy the way other men wore stolen coats — badly, and only indoors. It was worry. He was worried, and he was covering it with logistics.

"Why do you care?" Zephyrine asked. "About her. About any of it. You told me on the salt road that promises don't cross borders. You told me borders are just lines where promises get devalued, like currency."

Brimming silence. Rain on the shed roof. Somewhere across the harbor, soldiers were loading crates stamped with numbers onto a barge, and the crane sang its one long idiot note.

"What I told you," Bram said finally, "is that promises don't survive borders. I never said anything about people surviving them. Different question." He rubbed his jaw. "My mother kept a light too. Not official. A lamp in the window of a house on the estuary, lit for my father, every night for nine years after the sea took him. Everyone in the village thought she was mad. She said the lamp wasn't for him. It was so anyone else on that water would know a person lived there who watched." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own honesty, recovering fast. "Anyway. Lighthouse families are a stubborn breed. She'll have gone to ground somewhere clever and she'll be insufferable about it. You'll want backup getting to her, and you won't ask, so I'm offering before pride does you damage."

"Bram—"

"Don't thank me. Thanking people makes them think they're owed things." He was already moving, pulling his collar up. "Toll bridge post changes at six. If you're crossing after dark, you cross by the weir, not the bridge. And leave the satchel. Whatever you're still carrying in there — leave it. You go up that point as nobody. A nobody with empty pockets and bad boots. Understand?"

Zephyrine understood. They understood, too, that Bram had just volunteered for more than company on a walk, and that arguing about it would cost time neither of them had.

"Six nights," Zephyrine said quietly. "She's been dark six nights."

"Then you'd best stop standing in the rain talking to me."

* * *

Zephyrine left the satchel buried under the floor of the tar shed — codes gone, but still a few papers that could hang a person: a letter of introduction, half a map, Odile Ferrand's small careful note listing which names in the ledger had children. Zephyrine had carried that note for weeks without knowing why, refusing to examine it, the way one refuses to probe a bruise. Now its meaning was simple: Zephyrine had known, on some level that operated independently of hope, that names could become contraband. That a list could be a weapon pointed at everyone on it.

The poster had proven it. Proclamation No. 44 was a ledger too. Just a shorter one, and honest about its purpose.

Zephyrine walked north out of town along the cart track above the marshes, in the last gray light, wearing bad boots and an emptied coat, and tried to think the way the situation required instead of the way the heart insisted. The situation required arithmetic. Ilse was declared. Declared meant hunted. Hunted meant either caught already — and no announcement had been made of an arrest, and armies loved announcing arrests, they were the only harvest that reliably grew — or hiding, which meant she had help or luck, and luck was not a renewable resource on a coastline crawling with patrols.

If she was hiding, where? Not the lighthouse; the poster made the lighthouse the first place any sergeant would look. Not the village below it; the village had seen the posters too, and villages had learned in the last year what association cost. Somewhere with a second exit. Somewhere with water.

The old lifeboat station, Zephyrine thought, two miles past the point, sealed since the republic decommissioned it. Ilse had shown it to them years ago, laughing, calling it *our family's tomb of useless heroism,* showing off the rusted davits and the boat that had never once launched. Her father had kept the key. Ilse would have kept the key too.

It was a thin thread. It was the only thread. Zephyrine held onto it the way a drowning sailor holds a log: not because it floats well, but because letting go has a worse outcome by any measure.

The marshes went silver and black around them. Out on the flats, birds lifted and settled, lifted and settled, indifferent to proclamations. Zephyrine thought about the promise — the exact words of it, spoken on the gallery in gold light, in a world where the ledger was just a book and names were just names and the worst thing that could happen to a person was weather — and found, examining it honestly for perhaps the first time, that the guilt they had been hauling around was not actually guilt about the promise.

It was guilt about the interval. Four years of intervals. Letters answered late, visits shortened, one whole year lost to a route change and a sick relative invented as cover. Zephyrine had treated the promise the way couriers treat everything: as freight, properly stored, checked occasionally, assumed sound. And freight assumes it will arrive. Freight does not imagine itself seized, crated, burned.

Odile had understood this first. In the customs house, showing Zephyrine the ledger, running her finger down the column to the M's, she had said — casually, terribly — *"Paper is only a promise until someone decides it isn't. Then it's evidence."* Zephyrine had nodded and filed the phrase and done nothing, and eight days later the army had rolled the evidence out of the building in a captain's leather case.

Now the army had written its own page for Ilse. Shorter than Odile's entry. Crueler. *Enemy. Collaborator. At large.* Three words doing the work of a verdict, no trial attached, no trial imagined necessary.

Zephyrine stopped on the track, in the full dark now, and let the cold in, and made the decision properly, with witnesses: the marsh birds, the far-off crane note from the harbor, the dead star of the lthouse invisible beyond the point.

Not guilt. Guilt was cargo, and cargo slowed you, and Bram had taught them that much if nothing else. This was something with a sharper shape. This was a debt with a due date, and the due date was *before they find her,* and everything else — the armistice rumors, the amnesty lists, the whole rotting machinery of the transition — was secondary to that single clause.

The promise had a deadline. Deadlines could be met.

Zephyrine walked on, faster now, cutting toward the weir, keeping the drowned moon at their left shoulder. The boots were bad. The coat was empty. The name they traveled under tonight was nobody's.

Two miles past the point, the old lifeboat station crouched above the tideline like a thing pretending to be ruins. As Zephyrine came down the shingle, heart going hard and stupid against the ribs, a line of lamplight opened along the ground — a shuttered lantern, cracked an inch, extinguished instantly.

Then a voice, low and fierce and unmistakable, the voice Zephyrine had crossed a collapsing country rather than admit they'd been listening for:

"If you're a patrol, get it over with. If you're not—" a breath, a catch, anger fighting hope for the same throat— "*who taught you the path over the weir?*"
