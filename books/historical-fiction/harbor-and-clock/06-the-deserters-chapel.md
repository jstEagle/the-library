## 6. The Deserters' Chapel

Anselm Rey heard them before he saw them — two sets of feet on the shingle path, one heavy and unhurried, the other light and trying too hard to be quiet. He marked the page in his book with a strip of bark, closed it, and listened harder. Rain had stopped an hour before dusk, and the wet world carried sound the way dry sand carries footprints.

"Whistler," Bram Voss called from the treeline, using the signal word they'd agreed on in another season, when the word had meant less.

"In," Anselm said.

They came through the gap in the yew hedge where the hedge had given up years ago. Bram first, broad as a door, salt-crusted and smelling of marsh mud. Behind him — Anselm stood, and took a moment he didn't let show on his face — behind him came the courier. Zephyrine Sterling. Anselm knew the name the way you know a name from a newspaper that has since stopped printing: the republic's runner, the one who carried what couldn't be spoken over wires. Thinner than the rumors suggested. Eyes doing arithmetic on the chapel, the graves, the tarpaulins, the smoke hole, counting exits. Good. People who counted exits lived longer, and Anselm needed this one alive.

"You brought rain with you," Anselm said.

"Brought worse than that," Bram said. "Brought the customs house's problem wearing a coat."

"I'm standing right here," Zephyrine said.

"Yes," Anselm said. "That's rather the point." He gestured toward the chapel door. "Come inside where the wind can't hear us. It gossips."

* * *

The chapel had been a chapel once, in the way a wreck is still called a ship. Its roof held over the nave only where the deserters had stretched sailcloth and tarps; the tower was open to the sky like a broken tooth, and swallows had claimed the belfry arch for their own insolent architecture. The pews were gone — burned in October, the coldest week — but the stone floor kept some of the day's heat if you knew which slabs to sleep on, and Anselm did, because he had been a schoolteacher for nineteen years and knowing where warmth gathered was the same skill as knowing where attention wandered.

Twenty-three people lived here now. Anselm counted them every evening the way he'd once counted heads after recess, with the same low dread of being one short. Eleven from the republic's army, nine from the army now sealing the harbor town, and three who had never worn either uniform — a fisherman's son, a girl who'd fled the work camps, and old Teodor, who claimed to have deserted from a war so old no one could verify it, which Anselm privately considered the most honest kind of military record.

The rule of the chapel was simple and had to be repeated often: *in here, your war is luggage. You may carry it. You may not open it.*

Tonight the rule would be tested. Tonight he had a courier under his roof, and couriers were not luggage; couriers were cargo, and cargo drew fire.

He sat them at the east end, near the brazier, where the light was worst for reading and best for faces. Bram lowered himself onto a slab with the groan of a man twice his width. Zephyrine remained standing a moment longer, scanning the room again — the young deserter Karel mending a net, the girl Sefa asleep against a pillar, Teodor awake as always and pretending not to listen, which was his form of prayer.

"You'll want tea," Anselm said. "You won't want what passes for tea here, but you'll want it."

"I want information," Zephyrine said.

"And I wanted a pension and my old classroom with the map of the world that still had the old borders on it." Anselm poured three tin cups from the blackened kettle. "We trade wants for what's available. Sit."

Zephyrine sat. Up close, Anselm could see the cost written on them — the specific exhaustion of someone who has been measuring every object they own by what it would cost to be found holding it. He had seen that look on children carrying letters home from the front. It aged people in the wrong direction.

"Bram says the ledger survived," Anselm began.

"Bram says a lot of things."

"The ledger," Bram said, "survived. I watched them carry it into the customs house in a document case with a brass clasp. Odile Ferrand's ledger. Two hundred names."

"How do you know there are two hundred?"

"Because Odile told me there were two hundred, and Odile counts things twice."

Anselm turned back to Zephyrine. "And you want it back."

"I want to know if it still exists," Zephyrine said. "There's a difference. Things carried into that building don't always come out as things. I'm told there's a captain."

"Captain Erhart Voss—" Anselm stopped himself at Bram's snort. "No relation," he corrected, "though the shared name has caused both of them distress."

"None," Bram said. "It's caused me amusement and him rage, which is the correct distribution."

"Captain Voss," Anselm continued, "is not a cruel man, or not only one. He is something I find more dangerous. He is an orderly man in a disordering time. He believes the mess around him is clerical error. And so he treats it clerically. Anything in his possession that serves the requisition is filed. Anything that doesn't serve the requisition is burned. Not out of malice. Out of tidiness." He let that sit. "I had a headmaster like him. He burned forty years of school records because the new ministry wanted standardized forms. There were children in those records. Vaccination dates, family notes, who couldn't afford shoes. Burned in the yard on a Tuesday, and he was proud of the neatness of the pile."

Zephyrine's cup had stopped halfway to their mouth. "The ledger serves nothing. That's what you're telling me."

"It serves no requisition," Anselm agreed. "Which makes it kindling on a schedule. Though—" and he raised a finger, because he had learned as a teacher that bad news should always come attached to its one thread of good, or the listener drops both— "it hasn't burned yet. We know this because we know the building. Or one of us does."

He whistled softly, two notes, and from the shadow of the tower arch rose a lanky figure Anselm had recruited in November: Perrin Duval, formerly a corporal, formerly before that an apprentice locksmith, formerly before that a boy who could pick any lock in his village except, tragically, the one on his mother's pantry. Perrin had spent four months last year working repairs inside the customs house and had the floor plan of it drawn in charcoal on a piece of sailcloth, folded now into his boot like scripture.

"Perrin," Anselm said. "Tell our guest about the map room."

* * *

Perrin talked for ten minutes, and Anselm watched Zephyrine the whole time, because a plan is only half information; the other half is whether the person receiving it can hold it without breaking.

The customs house, Perrin explained, had three entrances in daylight and none worth trying at night unless you were a rat or Bram Voss. The ground floor was stores — requisitioned grain, cordage, the harbor's confiscated inventory, catalogued with a precision that Perrin clearly found obscene. The first floor held offices and the guard room. But the second floor, in the old harbor master's suite, was Captain Voss's map room: charts, dispatches, the sealed correspondence of a town that no longer had anyone to write to. And locked in the iron cabinet beneath the window facing the breakwater —

"The ledger," Perrin said. "I saw the case go in. Brass clasp, customs-house stamp, red spine. He keeps the cabinet key on his belt. Left side, with the whistle."

"He burns things where?" Zephyrine asked.

"Yard, behind the coal store. Iron basket, the kind from a public garden. Twice a week he does a burn — Tuesdays and Fridays, at dusk, when the smoke reads as ordinary against the kitchens."

Today was Wednesday. Anselm did the arithmetic aloud, because arithmetic spoken is arithmetic shared: "So we have until Friday dusk before whatever is in that cabinet meets the basket. Assuming he burns everything. Assuming he hasn't already."

"He hasn't," Bram said. "A man that tidy burns on schedule. Today was Tuesday. I checked — the smoke was kitchen fat and old rope. No paper smell."

"You can smell paper through kitchen smoke?" Zephyrine said.

"I can smell burning money," Bram said, "and names are money now. Worse. Amnesty lists are coming, everyone says so. Names on paper will buy lives within the month. If Voss is smart he knows it. If he's smart, the ledger is leverage, not litter."

Anselm set down his cup. This was the part he had been waiting for, the part he had rehearsed in his head while mending the tarpaulin, because he had been a teacher long enough to know that the hardest lessons to give are the ones where you talk someone out of hope and into resolve in the same breath.

"Then here is what this house can offer you," he said. "And here is what it cannot."

* * *

He stood, because standing was a habit of the classroom and he had stopped apologizing for it. Around the chapel, the evening ritual had begun without instruction — Karel banking the brazier, Sefa stirring, Teodor drifting closer under the pretense of checking the sailcloth seams, listening with the diligence of a man whose entire profession for eighty years had been other people's conversations.

"There are twenty-three of us," Anselm said. "Nine of us wore the enemy's uniform eleven days ago. Eleven wore ours. In this room we have made a country of one rule, and the rule holds because no one has yet tested it with anything heavier than an insult. What you're asking — taking that ledger back — will bring soldiers to this coast. Not maybe. Certainly. If we fail quietly, we fail alone. If we fail loudly, they will search these marshes, and they will find a chapel full of deserters from both armies, and they will hang us in mixed batches for efficiency."

Silence. The swallows shifted in the tower.

"So I won't pretend this is sanctuary offering you shelter," Anselm went on. "It isn't. It's a lifeboat deciding whether to take on another passenger when the sea is coming up. What I can offer is this: knowledge, a floor plan, a schedule, a locksmith, and twenty-odd people who owe no government anything and therefore have no reason left to lie. What I cannot offer is safety, certainty, or a promise that everyone under this roof agrees. Some of them think the ledger is a dead republic's scrap. Some think it's bait. One of them—" he paused, and chose honesty, because the chapel ran on it— "one of them may yet prove to be the kind who sells information for rations, because hunger makes merchants of us all, and I can't watch every mouth."

"That's a hell of a sales pitch," Bram said.

"I was a schoolteacher. We call it setting expectations."

Zephyrine had listened without moving. Now they spoke, quietly, and Anselm heard in their voice the thing he had been listening for — not the courier's clipped trade language, but something underneath, older and less armored.

"There's a name in that ledger that isn't a name to me," Zephyrine said. "Ilse Marchetti. Lighthouse keeper's daughter, south point. I made her a promise in peacetime, when promises were cheap, that I'd keep her safe. Her name's in that cabinet with a red spine, and she's been declared enemy by a poster, and her lighthouse has gone dark, and I don't know which of those facts killed her or none of them have yet. So no — I'm not asking your lifeboat to take on a passenger. I'm asking whether any of you remember being promised something by someone who then let the world get in the way."

Teodor spoke from the shadows, his voice like gravel poured slowly. "I remember."

Sefa, awake now, said nothing, but pulled her blanket around her shoulders and looked at Zephyrine with the flat direct stare of the young, which is the closest thing to a vote.

Karel put down his net. "If the amnesty lists come," he said slowly, "and names buy lives — then that ledger isn't two hundred people. It's two hundred of us. Different names. Same paper."

Anselm felt the room turn, the way a classroom turns, all at once and irreversibly, and he let it turn before he spoke the last of it.

"Then we accept," he said. "Cautiously. Conditions: no meetings in daylight, no more than four know any single part of the plan, and if it comes apart, this chapel never heard your name. Those aren't terms of distrust, Courier Sterling. They're terms of survival, and survival is the only institution still functioning on this coast." He extended his hand, thin and ink-stained and steady. "Welcome to the deserters' chapel. Mind the third slab by the brazier; it wobbles."

Zephyrine took the hand. Their grip was cold and did not let go quickly.

"One more piece of news," Anselm added, "before you sleep, because you'll sleep badly either way and better with it said. Our last traveler through the lines brought word from the town. Your clerk — Ferrand. She wasn't shot at the arrest. She's being held in the guard room of the customs house itself. Interrogations, plural. Still refusing to sign anything."

Zephyrine went very still. "She's in the same building as the ledger."

"She is," Anselm said. "And here is the strange part — the reason I tell you now and not tomorrow. The soldier who brings her her bread is a boy I taught to read. Last week he passed her an extra heel of loaf wrapped in a scrap of paper. She wrote on it in return, in psalm rhythm, the way she used to mark her customs manifests so smugglers could read them and inspectors couldn't." He reached into his coat and produced the scrap, folded small, soft as cloth from handling. "I haven't opened it. It isn't addressed to me. It's addressed to whoever comes asking about the ledger."

He held it out across the brazier's dying light.

Zephyrine took it.
