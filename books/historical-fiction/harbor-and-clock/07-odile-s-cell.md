## 7. Odile's Cell

They took her shoes at the door of the customs house and gave them back at the door of the cell, which told Odile Ferrand two things: the army intended to keep her, and the army had not yet decided what keeping her meant.

The cell had been a records annex once. She knew it the way you know your own kitchen — she had signed for the shelving brackets eleven years ago, requisition 44-C, and she could still recite the invoice number if anyone cared to ask. The shelves were gone now. A cot remained, bolted where the filing cabinets had stood, and a bucket, and one high window barred with iron that wept rust in long orange tears down the plaster.

Outside, the harbor worked on without her. She could hear it — hammers, the gulls arguing over whatever the soldiers threw from the mess tent, the deep complaint of a cargo derrick that needed greasing and would not get it, because the man who kept the grease schedule was sitting on a cot with no shoes.

Three days ago she had been the customs clerk of this town. Now she was, according to the paper they'd made her listen to being read aloud, *an enemy functionary detained under emergency provision*. Enemy functionary. She had been born four streets from this cell. Her mother was buried up the hill behind Saint-Aubin's, under a stone Odile paid to have cleaned every spring.

She sat on the cot and did not cry, because crying used water and there was only the bucket, and because she had decided — somewhere between the checkpoint and the confiscation order, somewhere in the white noise of Captain Vasseur saying *enemy documentation* while his lieutenant pried the ledger out of her hands — that she would not give them anything else. Not tears. Not the satisfaction.

They had taken the ledger. They had not taken it entirely.

* * *

The interrogations happened in the morning, after bread that tasted like damp chalk. A Lieutenant Corvec conducted them — young, precise, a voice like a man reading terms of service aloud at his own funeral. He had a folder with her name on it and very little in it, which she knew because she had processed half the paperwork herself before the seizure and knew exactly how thin a file could be when a government was collapsing faster than it could file.

"You are the custodian of record," he said the first morning.

"I was the custodian of record."

"Where is the duplicate ledger?"

"There is no duplicate ledger." This was true, and it was also the most useful kind of sentence, the kind that is honest and useless at once.

"Then every name exists only in the original. Which is now military property."

"Which is now evidence of promises your army made and does not intend to keep," Odile said, and got a backhand for it, not hard, corrective rather than angry — a teacher's slap, which was worse somehow, and made her understand something about Corvec that she would think about later, in the dark, turning it over like a coin.

He came back the next morning. And the next. He asked about shipping manifests, about coded correspondence, about Zephyrine Sterling, whose name she denied knowing with the serene confidence of a woman who had watched Sterling sign the visitors' book eleven times in her own hand. Each interrogation ended with the same question circling back: the ledger, the ledger, the two hundred names.

And each afternoon, after the questioning, the guards changed shift, and for forty minutes the corridor outside her cell was manned by exactly one soldier, a heavy boy from the interior who sat where she couldn't see him and hummed when he was bored, tunelessly, the way boys hum who have never had to be quiet for anything.

It was during those forty minutes, on the fourth day, that Odile heard them carrying boxes out of the map room.

* * *

She heard more than boxes. She heard the sergeant — a different one, gravel-voiced, the kind of man the word *sergeant* was invented for — telling someone that Captain Vasseur wanted the annex cleared by week's end. She heard the word *burn* used twice, casually, the way you say *file* or *shred*, and she heard the second speaker ask whether the amnesty lists hadn't been drafted yet, and the sergeant answer that the lists would be drafted from whatever survived, so it wasn't as if anything important was going anywhere.

Paper burns. That was the whole strategy. Not justice, not even cruelty with a face on it — just fire, applied to the parts of the record that embarrassed the men holding the matches. Names promised passage would go into a brazier, and then the amnesty commission would find no record of any promise, and two hundred people would discover, in the fullness of legal time, that they had never been guaranteed anything at all.

Odile sat on the bolted cot with her hands flat on her knees and understood that she was the last person in this town who had read every page of that ledger.

Every page. Twice. Once when the guarantees were drawn up, and once — God forgive her slowness — the night before the requisition, when something in the weather of the town had made her walk back to the office after dinner and sit with the book under the lamp like a woman rereading letters from the dead. She had run her thumb down the columns. Marchetti, Ilse, lighthouse keeper's daughter, category: enemy-declared, guarantee: full passage, witness: S. Sterling. She remembered thinking, absurdly, *what good handwriting Sterling has for a courier.* She remembered thinking the ledger was safest exactly where it was, under lock, in a building with an army marching toward it.

Fool. But a fool with a memory, and a memory is the one archive no captain can requisition.

So she began, there on the cot, to do the work.

* * *

She started with structure, because structure was her profession. Two hundred and fourteen names, though she would stake her pension on the exact number being two hundred and thirteen living plus the infant born in March whom she'd entered in the margin with a note about the father's trade. Alphabetical within category, category by district. She rebuilt the first column in her head the way she'd once rebuilt a collapsed filing system after the flood year — bottom shelf up, one drawer at a time, no skipping.

Aubert. Aubert's wife. The Aubert boy with the bad lung, listed under a special annotation because the medical board had flagged him. Then Bacqueul — no. Wait. Bacqueul came under fishermen, not merchants; the categories interleaved alphabetically but never merged. She caught herself merging them, corrected it, felt the small cold satisfaction of an error found and fixed. This was the work. Not heroism. Accuracy.

By the sixth day she had the first thirty-one names solid, and she discovered the problem that would define everything after: unaided memory frays. Thirty-one names held fine in daylight. But sleep stole them at the edges — she woke on the seventh morning unsure whether it was Perrin or Périn, whether the fisherman's widow was Marguerite or Margarethe, and uncertainty, in this particular work, was a form of losing people twice.

She needed a container. Something shaped, rhythmic, load-bearing. Something a mind could grip when its own grip slipped.

There was a psalm she'd had to learn as a girl, at Saint-Aubin's, for a confirmation she'd doubted her way through and completed anyway. Psalm One hundred and seven. The seamen's psalm — her father's choice, naturally, the lighthouse families all used it. *They that go down to the sea in ships.* It had verses enough, and a cadence, and — this was the thing she seized on, lying awake while the humming guard shifted outside — it was built of lists. Storms and deliverances, waves going up to heaven and down to hell, hungry souls and gathered multitudes. A psalm was already a ledger that had learned to sing.

That evening, when bread came, she asked the humming guard for a pencil.

"No," he said, without looking up.

"For my accounts," she said. "I'm a clerk. I keep accounts in my head or I don't sleep."

"Captain says no writing materials."

"Then I'll say my psalms instead," Odile said, "out loud, all evening, until someone comes to stop me," and the guard, who wanted nothing in this world less than to be the soldier who filed a report about a praying prisoner, said, "Quietly, then," and went back to his humming.

Quietly, then.

* * *

Here is what she built, alone, in a cell that smelled of rust and old paste, at the pace of one verse per night:

She took the psalm's rhythm — long line, short line, the turn at the end — and she laid names into it the way you lay stones into a wall's courses. *Aubert the cooper, his wife, and their sickly son* fit the meter of the storm-verses almost obscenely well. *Bacqueul, who fishes the northern ground* carried the wave-cadence. Where a name wouldn't sit naturally, she bent the phrasing around it — inserted a detail, a trade, a street, because details are mortar and bare names crumble. The cooper's son had the bad lung: *whose breath fails him in winter.* Marguerite, not Margarethe — *the widow who mends nets at the third stair,* a phrase with hooks in it, impossible to lose.

Each name got stitched to a verse number, so that even if the sequence tangled she could always re-derive the count: fourteen names in the storm section, nine in the wilderness section, and so on down through the psalm's long body to the harbor-image at the end, where the sea is stilled and the mariners are brought to their desired haven — *their desired haven,* which she now understood had been about this all along, about safe arrival, about a promise of landing written into scripture centuries before any government thought to make one and cheaper ones thought to burn it.

Some nights the work was easy and the names came like water finding a channel. Some nights her head ached and the guard hummed off-key and she lost Bacqueul three times in an hour and hated herself with a clean, professional hatred. On the ninth night, after a bad day — Corvec had raised his voice for the first time, and threatened transfer to the mainland, and she had understood with cold clarity that transfer might mean simply disappearing her — she recited the first twenty-two names forty times and dreamed in meter and woke with the list intact, and lay still on the cot feeling something she hadn't felt since the requisition:

Useful. Dangerous, even. A woman with two hundred names sewn into her skull is not property. She is contraband.

* * *

Corvec came on the eleventh day and did not ask about the ledger.

He sat down across the table — they'd given him a proper chair now, and a lamp, and she noted the upgrades the way she noted all inventories — and looked at her for a while.

"You've been reciting," he said. "Guard reports chanting."

"Guard reports should be filed in triplicate," Odile said. "See that they are."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth and was suppressed. He turned a page in the thin folder, uselessly, for cover.

"The armistice talks are progressing," he said. "There will be an amnesty process. Names will be reviewed."

"From what document?"

"That isn't—"

"Because if it's from the ledger you confiscated," Odile said, "you should know that Captain Vasseur has ordered the annex cleared for burning by week's end. I heard the sergeant say so through the wall. So when your amnesty commission sits down to review names, Lieutenant, it will be reviewing ash." She let that sit in the lamplight between them. "I mention it because you strike me as a man who would prefer to know, professionally speaking, when his own orders are being emptied of their contents."

Corvec looked at her a long moment. He was very young under the uniform; she had known that since the first backhand, which had been administered like a boy imitating something he'd seen done correctly by worse men.

"I can't discuss disposition of confiscated materials," he said finally, and stood, and gathered his folder, and at the door he paused with his back to her and said, quietly, not turning around: "Psalm one hundred and seven. My grandmother sang it. The fishermen's one."

"Yes," Odile said. "It has room in it."

He left. She sat with her heart going harder than she liked, and examined what had just happened, and concluded — cautiously, clerkishly, requiring corroboration — that the young officer had just told her, in the only language available to both of them, that he could be turned. Not today. But the crack was there, and light was coming through it, and men like Corvec were how things got in and out of buildings.

First, though, she needed the crack to widen, and she needed to get word out of this cell before the burning began. Five days, maybe six. The sergeant had said week's end on day four.

* * *

The note took her two days to compose and cost her nothing to write, because she wrote it nowhere at all. She composed it in her head, in the psalm-meter itself, so that it would survive even if the delivery failed and only she ever carried it: a message wrapped inside the storm-verses where no censor would think to look, addressed to no one and therefore deliverable to anyone — Bram Voss's people, the chapel deserters, Sterling if Sterling lived. It named the ledger's location as precisely as she knew it: map room, second floor, iron cabinet, crated pending disposal. It named the deadline. It named the psalm, so that whoever received it would know the key and know that she — Odile Ferrand, customs clerk, enemy functionary, keeper of names — was alive and intact and singing.

Delivery was the problem. Guards searched outgoing trays. Laundry was burned, not washed — the army had decided hygiene was a peacetime luxury. In the end the answer walked past her door twice a day carrying slop buckets: the humming boy.

On the thirteenth morning she timed his round, and when he leaned to slide the tray through, she said, "Boy."

"Sergeant says don't talk to—"

"When you were a child," Odile said, "did you ever learn a psalm? The sailors' one. They go down to the sea in ships."

He stopped. He had a face like unfinished bread. "My mother sang it," he said. "When the boats were late."

"It goes in sections," Odile said. "Storms, then wilderness, then the haven at the end. Fourteen names in the storm section, do you follow me — fourteen, then nine, then—" and she recited the first verse-and-a-half with the first two names folded invisibly into it, Aubert and the sickly son, and saw the boy's brow knot with the effort of a mind unused to being asked to hold anything.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because my grandmother taught it to me and mine is gone," Odile said, which was true, and because — she held his eyes — "if you ever repeat it to anyone, exactly as heard, in order, it will be worth more than you are currently being paid. More than you're currently being fed. Ask yourself who might pay for words like that, someday, and keep them safe in the meantime." She smiled at him, the smile she'd used on inspectors and tax auditors for twenty years. "Your mother would want you keeping the words safe."

He took the tray. He hesitated in the doorway, humming stopped, and then — so quietly she nearly missed it — he repeated the first half-verse back to her, Aubert and all, garbled once and self-corrected.

"That's it exactly," Odile said. "Now you have it forever. Don't lose it."

He went. His footsteps slowed at the end of the corridor, and stopped, and she heard him murmuring the verse to himself like a boy checking homework, and she lay back on the cot and closed her eyes and allowed herself, for exactly one minute, the luxury of shaking.

Then she straightened, put her hands flat on her knees, and went back to the wilderness section, where nine names were waiting to be sung into shelter — and somewhere out beyond the wall, past the derrick that needed grease and the gulls and the gray working harbor, she fixed her mind on the chapel Bram Voss had spoken of in the one conversation she'd overheard before arrest, a ruined place with deserters in it, and on the hope that words set to psalms travel the way all real contraband travels: mouth to mouth, hand to hand, until they reach someone brave enough to act on them.

Six days, perhaps five. Somewhere in this building, two hundred and fourteen names sat in a crate awaiting fire.

Odile Ferrand opened her mouth and began, softly, at the beginning.
