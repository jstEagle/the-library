## 8. The Lighthouse Dark

Ilse had counted the stairs so many times she no longer needed the rail. Ninety-seven steps from the kitchen to the lamp room, and she had climbed them in the dark every night for eleven days, because a lighthouse that could not be seen to be dark was safer than one with a candle guttering behind smoked glass. She climbed now out of habit rather than duty, her hand trailing the cold iron, her father's oilskin coat hanging off her shoulders like a tent. It smelled of him — tar, tobacco, the sweet rotten smell of the sea he'd never once complained about in thirty years of keeping this light.

On the eleventh night, someone knocked at the base door.

Three knocks. A pause. Two more.

She knew that rhythm. She had known it since she was sixteen years old, since the summer Zephyrine Sterling had come to the point with their government's papers and their government's manners and stayed three weeks longer than any government required, helping her repaint the daymark stripes, learning the names of gulls as if gulls mattered, swearing — laughing, then not laughing — that if anything ever happened, they would come back up this coast for her.

She did not move. Her whole body wanted to move; she held it still on the eighty-ninth step and listened.

The knock came again. Three, pause, two. Then a voice, muffled by oak and salt-rotted weather stripping: "The light's out. I've been watching it go dark for two nights from the marsh side."

Her heart was doing something stupid against her ribs. "Lights get repaired," she called down. "People get told to leave."

"Ilse."

"You shouldn't be here." She came down anyway, step by step, furious at herself for coming down. At the last landing she stopped, one flight above the door, where the stairwell opened into shadow and she could see without being seen. "Do you know what they're calling me now? There's a poster. My name is *on* it, Zephyrine. Printed. Official ink. Enemy collaborator. Do you understand what your republic has decided I am?"

Silence below. Then: "I know what's written about you. I've read it."

"You've read—" A laugh came out of her, ugly and small. "Then you know it's a lie."

"I know it's a lie. Open the door."

"The patrols—"

"Passed me an hour ago. They won't circle back before the tide turns. Bram counted them." A pause, and when the voice came again it was quieter, stripped of everything performative. "Ilse. Please. I didn't walk four days through salt marsh to argue through a door."

She stood there another ten seconds, hating how much she had missed the sound of that voice, hating the missing itself, because missing was a kind of forgiveness and she had promised herself she would not forgive. Then she unbarred the door.

Zephyrine looked worse than the war should have made anyone look. Rain-wet, mud to the knees, a coat too thin for the season, eyes with bruised hollows under them. They stood in the doorway holding a satchel against their chest the way other people held children or weapons, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the wind came up the point and shoved between them into the tower like an uninvited guest.

"You look terrible," Ilse said.

"You should see the marsh," Zephyrine said.

She let them in. She barred the door behind them, and then, because her hands needed something, she put the kettle on the spirit stove in the keeper's kitchen, and they sat across from each other at the table where her father had eaten breakfast every morning of her life, and the kettle ticked, and the wind worried at the tower, and Zephyrine said, "Where's Aldous?"

"Gone." The word came out flat. She had practiced it flat. "Six days ago. Four men in the night, no warrant, nothing read aloud. They took him down the coast road in a requisitioned mail cart. He wasn't even allowed his boots. He'd been keeping the light for them, Zephyrine. All these months, running the fog signal for whatever navy claimed the channel that week, neutral as a church, and they took him without his boots." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated that, hated that after eleven days of practicing grief in silence it still found a seam. "He's at the internment depot at Corvel, I think. Or beyond it. No one answers inquiries. There is no one left to answer inquiries."

Zephyrine had gone very still. It was a thing she remembered about them, that stillness — how they could stop like a clock, everything in them gathering into one held place. "Why are you here and not taken with him?"

"Because I was in the lamp room when they came, and the stairs are narrow, and I am not proud of what I did with a fire hook." She poured the water over the last of the tea — real tea, traded from a fisherman two coves down who asked no questions — and pushed the cup across the table. "They'll come back for me. They were interrupted, and interruptions offend armies. So if you've come to warn me, you're eleven days late."

"I've come to do more than warn you."

"Then you've come to lie to me, because there is nothing anyone can do. Look at yourself. You crossed lines to get here. You can't cross back with me. There's nowhere to cross *to*." She wrapped both hands around her cup. The heat hurt and she welcomed it. "So say whatever you came to say and go, before you're caught under my roof, which is no longer a roof anyone should be under."

Zephyrine set the satchel on the table between them. Carefully, like setting down something breakable.

"Do you remember," they said, "in June — the year of the regatta, when we sat on the gallery and you asked me what my work actually was?"

"I remember you lying charmingly about it."

"I'm not going to lie tonight. My work was carrying documents between this town and the capital. One of the things I carried was a ledger." Their voice stayed level but she could hear the effort of the levelness, the way you can hear a rope bearing weight. "Two hundred and six names. People this harbor government had promised passage out — evacuation ships, safe conduct, neutral ports. Fishermen, widows, a defrocked customs clerk, a schoolteacher. People whose politics or parentage or marriages made them inconvenient to whoever wins this war. The paper was their guarantee. Without it they're nobody, legally. They don't exist."

The kettle's small heat ticked. Somewhere out in the dark the sea worked at the rocks with its patient, idiot persistence.

"My name isn't in that ledger," Ilse said slowly. "It wouldn't be. I'm a lighthouse keeper's daughter. We're not anybody's inconvenience."

Zephyrine looked at her, and it was the looking that told her, before any words did. That particular refusal to look away. Pity's honest cousin.

"No," Ilse said.

"Ilse—"

"No. Say it isn't."

"It's there. Page forty-one. Marchetti, Ilse, daughter of Aldous, lighthouse. Your father went to the customs house himself, in September, and gave his information to the passage commission. He did it quietly — he didn't tell you because he thought there'd be time, thought the ships would come before the army did." A breath. "And there's more, and I need you to let me say all of it before you decide anything."

She wanted to throw the tea in their face. She wanted, absurdly, to go up the ninety-seven steps and lock herself in the lamp room among her father's clean brass instruments. She did neither. She said, "Say it, then."

"The ledger was confiscated. Eleven days ago. Dawn requisition, the whole town sealed, the customs house seized. Everything in it is now army property — classified as enemy documentation." Zephyrine's hands flattened on the table, steadying themselves or the table. "Which means the paper that proves you exist, that guarantees you a ship and a safe port, is locked in a military map room under a captain named Delacour, who burns what he cannot use. And the armistice lists — the amnesty lists, the ones that decide who gets pardoned and who gets processed when this ends — are being drafted from those same ledgers. If your name comes off the list, Ilse, you don't get arrested. You just stop being anyone the law remembers."

The wind found some gap high in the tower and made it moan. Ilse discovered she was standing, though she had no memory of rising, both palms flat on the table now, leaning toward Zephyrine as if proximity could make the words rearrange themselves into something survivable.

"He signed us up," she said. "Without telling me. He wrote me down like a piece of cargo—"

"He wrote you down like a daughter," Zephyrine said, sharp for the first time. "He wrote you down because he was old and he'd kept a light through two changes of flag and he knew exactly what armies do to people on the wrong page of a book. And then my republic — the one that promised him the passage, the one whose seal I carried in my coat lining for three years — let a captain put that book in a crate. So yes. Be angry. Be angry at him and at me and at the whole rotten arrangement. But be angry *fast*, because we have weeks, maybe less."

"*We.*" She heard herself repeat it, tasting the word. "There's no we. There hasn't been a we since you left in October and wrote me four letters in a year."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed, and her voice was shaking now, all the flat practice gone to pieces. "It isn't fair. None of this is fair, so why start measuring? You want fairness? Here's fair: I spent three years believing that if it ever got bad, you would come up this coast road. That's what you swore. On the gallery, in June, with the regatta fireworks going off over the water like it was the most beautiful year anyone had ever lived in. And instead what happens? Your government writes my name in a book, loses the book to the people shooting at it, and *you* arrive — eleven days after my father is taken — to inform me that I am legally a ghost." She was crying, furiously, wiping her face with the sleeve of the enormous oilskin coat like a child. "Did you know? When you carried it. Did you know my name was in there?"

"Yes."

"And you carried it anyway, back and forth, month after month—"

"Because carrying it was the only thing keeping it alive!" For a moment Zephyrine's composure broke entirely and she saw underneath it, saw the exhaustion and terror and the terrible stubborn tenderness she had fallen for at twenty-two, and it was almost worse than the anger. "Every trip I made, I was the book. Do you understand? Odile Ferrand trusted me with it because there was no one else. I knew your name was in it. I checked. First week, I checked, and when I found it I read that page so many times I can still recite it — Marchetti, Ilse, born at the point, occupation listed as *assistant keeper*, your father's little joke because there was no such position and he wanted one line of the record to say you mattered. I carried that page through checkpoints knowing that if they searched my coat, it wasn't just two hundred strangers dying. It was you. And I carried it anyway, because the alternative was letting it burn, and I could not—" Their voice failed, recovered. "I could not let it be your page."

The kitchen was quiet except for the wind and the sea and Ilse's own ragged breathing.

She sat back down. Her legs simply declined to hold her anymore. She looked at Zephyrine across the table — mud-caked, hollow-eyed, still holding their body around that satchel like a second ribcage — and felt the anger shift under her, the way a sandbar shifts, not disappearing but moving, becoming something else.

"They're going to come back for me," she said.

"Yes."

"And if they take me, there's no list, no ship, nothing. Just a depot somewhere next to my father."

"Probably."

"So what's the plan, Zephyrine? Tell me there's a plan and it isn't just you sitting in my kitchen getting us both shot."

Zephyrine reached into the satchel and drew out a folded paper, worn soft at the creases. They unfolded it on the table between the teacups. Smudged charcoal, hurried hand: a sketch of a building she recognized — the customs house, drawn from memory, with rooms marked and a small square labeled *map room* and arrows showing guard paths, annotated in at least two different hands.

"There's a plan," Zephyrine said. "It involves smugglers, deserters, a clerk in a cell who's memorizing the pages faster than they can burn them, and forged wine merchant's passes of frankly insulting quality." They looked up, and there at the bottom of everything, under all the fear, was the thing they had never managed to say in June with the fireworks going. "But it doesn't work unless the harbor lights go dark at the right hour. All of them. Including yours. And only one person on this coast knows those lights well enough to kill them quietly."

Ilse looked at the sketch. At her own name, unwritten but present on every line of it. At Zephyrine, who had carried her through checkpoints inside a coat lining without telling her, and who sat now in her father's kitchen asking her to commit treason against the country that had erased her.

"If we do this," she said, "and it goes wrong — I don't get to be a ghost politely. They'll make an example."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking," Zephyrine said, "because I swore something on that gallery, and I've been carrying it ever since like it weighed something, and I'm done carrying promises alone."

Outside, far down the coast road, a engine coughed — once, twice, mechanical and patient — and both of them went still as the lamp room above them waited, dark, full of brass and glass and her father's absent hands, ready to be lit again by whoever was brave enough or foolish enough to reach it first.
