## 9. Terms of the Armistice

Anselm Rey had taught arithmetic to children for nineteen years, and he had learned in that time that a number only frightened people when they finally understood it. So he did what he had always done: he wrote the sum on the chapel wall, in chalk, where everyone could see it.

*Fourteen days.*

Below it, smaller: *Two hundred names. One ledger. One building with soldiers in it.*

He stepped back and looked at his work the way he used to look at a lesson plan, and felt the same old useless tenderness, and hated himself for it. Tenderness was not going to open a locked map room.

The deserters drifted in through the morning fog — two from the coastal regiment, one from the army now holding the town, all of them moving like men who had forgotten how to walk without looking over their shoulders. They brought bread, a tin of oil, and news. The news was the worst of the three.

"Truce," said Corporal Henn, the older of the two coastals, dropping onto a pew as if his legs had been disconnected from his judgment. "They're saying six weeks. Maybe less. Delegates are already traveling."

"From whom?" Anselm asked, though he knew. He had stopped believing in sources months ago; now there were only currents, and you read them like weather.

"Both sides. The officers eat together at the Grand Hotel in Vellacourt, did you know that? Same tablecloth. Our captain dines with theirs." Henn laughed, a dry sound with nothing in it. "They shoot us all day and share a pudding at night."

Anselm wrote *six weeks* under *fourteen days*, then crossed out fourteen and left only the smaller number, because hope was a thing you rationed like salt.

* * *

Zephyrine arrived at noon, mud to the knees, with Ilse Marchetti a half-step behind them — close enough to reach for, far enough to deny. Anselm watched them come down the chapel path and thought: *there go two people carrying the same weight and refusing to split it.* He had seen married couples do this for decades. It never ended well, and it never ended, either.

"You've heard," Zephyrine said. Not a question.

"I've heard rumors. Rumors are how the war talks to us."

"It's more than rumor." Zephyrine set their satchel on the altar rail — the chapel's altar was a plank across two barrels now, and no one apologized for it — and pulled out a newspaper, three days old, smuggled from the occupied quarter. "Proclamation. Hostilities to cease upon signature of terms. Amnesty and repatriation to follow 'according to existing registers.'"

Anselm read the phrase twice. *According to existing registers.* His stomach did what it always did when bureaucracy showed its teeth: it went cold and precise.

"Existing registers," he said. "Meaning the ledgers."

"Meaning *the* ledger." Zephyrine's voice stayed level, which Anselm recognized as an effort. "Every name Odile copied into her memory, every name guaranteed passage — it sits in Captain Roche's map room, and in six weeks or less, whoever drafts the amnesty lists is going to sit down with that book and decide who exists."

"And who doesn't."

"And who doesn't." Zephyrine folded the paper along its creases, again and again, until it was a small hard square, a stone. "If your name isn't on paper, you don't exist. That's not a metaphor anymore, Anselm. That's the actual mechanism. They've made erasure a clerical procedure."

Ilse had stopped at the chapel door. She hadn't come inside. Anselm understood why without asking — the last time she'd been inside a building with strangers, her father had been taken out of one. She stood in the gray light with her arms crossed, and when she spoke, her voice carried the flat clarity of someone who had already done the arithmetic herself.

"My name is in that book twice," she said. "Once because your republic promised me passage. Once because their army declared me an enemy. Two documents, opposite conclusions, same handwriting." She looked at Zephyrine. "Which one do you think survives?"

No one answered. Anselm admired her for asking it anyway. In his classroom, the children who asked the unanswerable questions were always the ones worth teaching.

* * *

He convened them in the afternoon among the broken pews — Zephyrine, Ilse, the two Henn brothers, a defrocked customs clerk named Perrin who could forge anything except a clear conscience, and Bram Voss, who arrived late and smelling of the harbor, which meant he'd been bribing someone, which meant he knew something.

"The armistice is real," Bram said, without preamble. "My contact at the wine merchants says requisition orders have slowed. You don't strip a town bare if you're giving it back. They're winding down." He dropped a bottle on the pew — a good vintage, requisitioned and re-requisitioned until its provenance was a small history of theft. "But here's the part nobody's saying out loud. The amnesty lists aren't being drafted after the signing. They're being drafted *now*. In advance. From the seized records."

Perrin went pale. "That's not how amnesty works. Amnesty is a clean slate. You can't—"

"You can't what?" Bram smiled without warmth. "You think there's a rule? There's a captain with a ledger and a list to fill, and every name he leaves off is a person who can be quietly processed afterward. Irregular persons. Undocumented arrivals. Enemy collaborators. Pick your phrase. The paper does the killing now, Perrin. It's very modern."

Anselm listened and felt the old classroom instinct rise in him — the urge to put the problem on the board, break it into parts, make it solvable. So he stood, took the chalk, and wrote:

*What we have:* Odile's memory (partial). Ilse's half-copy (half of half). Bram's passes. Perrin's pen. Fourteen days, maybe fewer.

*What we need:* The ledger. A boat. Neutral water. Time.

*What we don't have:* marked through twice, so hard the chalk snapped.

"We stop talking about whether," he said, turning back to them. "We talk about how."

* * *

It was Ilse, later, by the driftwood fire outside, who said the thing Anselm would carry longest. He was mending a net — a useless domestic gesture, but his hands needed work or they'd start shaking — and she sat across from him feeding the fire with splinters of pew wood, which he chose not to object to.

"You were a teacher," she said.

"Nineteen years. Until I told my students the war was a lie told by men who'd never see a trench."

"And then?"

"Then I learned what happens to teachers who tell the truth. There's a reason the chapel has room for deserters. Every institution in this country expels the honest eventually. It's practically admissions policy."

She almost smiled. Then didn't. "In school," she said, "did they teach you what happens to names?"

He looked up.

"When a country falls," she said, poking the fire, "the first thing that dies is the record. Not the people — the *record*. My grandmother came from across the water, from a village that isn't on any map anymore. Not destroyed. Just *unrecorded*. Redrawn away. And when she died, there was nowhere to prove she'd been born at all." She fed the fire another splinter. "A name is a place, monsieur Rey. Burn the place and the person has to live in the weather."

Anselm set down the net. "Is that why your father kept the copy?"

"My father believed paper was a promise you make to the future." Her jaw tightened. "I used to think that was naive. Now I think he was the only adult in the room." She stood, brushing ash from her skirt. "Six weeks. Your chalk says less. So we should stop counting days and start spending them."

She walked back toward the lighthouse road, where Zephyrine waited in the fog like a question mark that had learned to stand upright. Anselm watched them meet — no embrace, just a falling into step, two people walking in the same direction, which was its own kind of vow — and he picked up his chalk stub and went back inside.

On the wall, under the numbers, he added one line. Not arithmetic this time. Something older than arithmetic, from a lesson plan he'd burned years ago:

*A name unspoken is a grave unmarked.*

Then he blew out the candle, because candles visible from the road got chapels shelled, and sat in the dark listening to the sea count something of its own against the breakwater.

Fourteen days. Fewer. Outside, a patrol passed on the coast road, boots keeping time, and Anselm found himself thinking about the young officer he'd seen at the fish market raid — the one whose eyes had wavered when they dragged the old fishwife's stall apart, the one who'd looked at Anselm's hands, bound, with something that wasn't yet shame but was circling it. Men cracked in both directions. It was worth remembering.

He slept two hours. In the third hour, footsteps on the chapel path woke him — quick ones, wrong ones — and Bram slipped through the door smelling of panic instead of harbor.

"They've moved the schedule," Bram said. "The delegates arrive early. We don't have fourteen days."

"How many?"

Bram held up his hand, fingers spread, then closed three of them.

"Two," Anselm said.

"Two. And Zephyrine needs to hear it tonight, because tomorrow—" Bram stopped. Listened. Somewhere down the coast, thin and wrong, the lighthouse turned its beam across the fog once, twice — lit, though no one had authorized it, lit like a signal, lit like a hand raised in a classroom.

"That's Ilse," Anselm said slowly. "Why is she lighting the lamp?"

Bram's face, in the dark, went the color of ash. "Because that's the signal we agreed on," he said. "For *soldiers coming*. And there's a boat putting in at the north channel right now, full of them."
