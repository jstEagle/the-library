## 10. Mapping the Customs House

The soldiers at the north channel came ashore at low tide, which was the only reason any of them went home breathing.

Bram had gotten the chapel people moving within minutes of seeing the light — a discipline he'd learned young, from a father who said you can argue with an army but never with a tide. They doused the fire, scattered into the dune grass with blankets and children and one shrieking gull of a deserter named Pilo who had to be gagged with his own scarf, and they lay in the cold sand while forty men in field gray walked the tideline with lanterns and found nothing but the chapel's cold ashes and a smell of woodsmoke they couldn't trace to anything.

By morning the patrol had marched on toward the harbor road, and the chapel had a new rule: no lights after dark, ever, for anyone, including God.

Which meant that when Bram set out two nights later to bribe a dockhand, he did it in a dark so complete he navigated by smell — tar, salt, the wet-wool stink of the marsh where it gave way to cobbles. He'd dressed down for it: patched jersey, cracked boots, a fishmonger's apron rolled under his arm in case he needed to look employed at short notice. His papers were forged well enough to survive a glance and badly enough to invite a second one, so his whole plan rested on never being glanced at twice.

He was good at not being looked at. It was, he sometimes thought, the only honest trade left to him.

* * *

The dockhand's name was Teun, and he drank at the Anchor & Chain, a tavern near the fish market where the army had set up a requisitions office and therefore where every man who worked the quays drank with the specific silence of men counting their money in their heads and deciding whether they'd live long enough to spend it.

Bram bought him a glass of the good stuff — real plum brandy, hoarded, worth more per bottle than Teun made in a week — and sat down beside him without introduction, the way old friends do.

"You're Voss," Teun said.

"I'm a wine merchant."

"You're Voss. You ran the Salt Road before any of this, and my cousin says you still run it, and my cousin also says I should be careful, because the last man who took your money ended up rich and dead." Teun turned the glass slowly on the table, watching the lamplight swim in it. "So. How much?"

"That depends on what I'm buying."

"The customs house." Teun didn't look at him. Nobody looked at anybody in the Anchor & Chain; conversations happened sideways, aimed at glasses and table edges. "Everyone knows someone wants into the customs house. The captain's made a fortress of it. Guards on the door, guards on the water side, guards on the roof smoking cigarettes like the chimney's theirs now."

"Guards are the easy part," Bram said. "I need the shape of the thing. Rotations. When they change, how many, which doors get locked and which just get closed because closing them is somebody's job and that somebody stops caring after midnight."

Teun drank. Set the glass down with the care of a man giving himself time.

"There's a map room," he said. "Second floor, harbor-facing corner. The captain had the windows painted over the first week. Painted over, in a building full of maps — like he's the only one allowed to see where anything is anymore." A bitter little laugh. "He keeps the confiscated documents in there. Papers, ledgers, letters pulled out of the mail. Every few days his adjutant carries a crate down to the yard stove, and the yard smells like burning ink for an hour, and we all stand around pretending it's normal to smell a town's paperwork going up its own chimney."

Bram kept his face still, but inside him something turned over slowly, like a body in water. *Every few days.* Crates. Burning.

"And among the things not yet burned," he said carefully, "there's a ledger. Customs ledger, blue cover, thick as a Bible. Names in it."

"How would I know names?" Teun said. But his eyes flicked, just once, toward the harbor, and Bram understood: Teun had a name too. Everyone in this town had a name in some book or other, or feared they did, or loved someone who did. That was the whole war, as far as Bram could tell — two armies arguing over whose list of human beings got to be the real one.

"The rotations," Bram said. "All of them. And the yard stove schedule."

"That last one will cost extra."

"It always does."

Teun told him. Not all at once — he doled it out between drinks, the way men give up dangerous information, in pieces small enough to swallow. Day guard: eight men, changed at six and at midnight, with the change taking the better part of twenty minutes because the sergeant counted everything twice and recounted what he miscounted. Night guard: five, and of the five, three clustered at the front door where there was lamplight and company, one walked the quay circuit in nineteen minutes if the rain was light and thirty if it wasn't, and the fifth —

The fifth was the interesting one.

"Boy called Renn," Teun said. "Sixteen, maybe. Stands the water-side post, second floor landing, inside. Never been tested, never seen action, and here's the thing about Renn—" Teun leaned in, and his breath was plum-sweet. "He asked me last week whether I thought the war would end soon. Asked me like I'd know. Like the answer mattered to him more than his pay. You understand what that means?"

"A boy who thinks about endings," Bram said, "is a boy thinking about what comes after. And boys thinking about what comes after will trade their post for a version of it."

"He'll trade it cheap, is what I mean. Or lose it. Or look the wrong way at the wrong moment." Teun shrugged. "I'm saying there's a soft spot in the wall. I'm telling you where it is. What you build against it is your sin, not mine."

Bram paid him in coin wrapped in oiled paper, and then paid him again for the stove schedule, and Teun pocketed both without counting, which was either trust or contempt, and on the whole Bram suspected the latter, and deserved it.

At the door, Teun caught his sleeve.

"Voss. The ledger with the names. If it exists —" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "My sister's name might be in it. She worked the pilot boats. When the requisition came she ran, and nobody's seen her since, and if there's paper somewhere that says she was promised passage —"

"Then she was promised," Bram said. "Paper doesn't make promises true. People do. But if the paper exists, we want it, and not for the reason you think."

"What reason, then?"

"Because someday," Bram said, "some clerk is going to sit in a clean office and decide, from a list, who gets to exist. And I intend to be holding the pen's inkwell when he reaches for it."

* * *

He came back through the marshes with the rotations written in fish-gut grease on the inside of his apron — invisible until warmed, legible over a flame, gone the moment a wet thumb crossed it. Old smuggler's trick. His father's trick, actually, and like everything his father taught him it came without instructions for living, only for surviving.

The chapel was dark as promised, but Anselm Rey had hung a wool blanket over the east doorway and behind it a single candle stub, and by that mean light the deserters were gathered: Pilo picking at his scarf, the Kessel twins sharing one blanket, old Marta who wasn't a deserter at all but had nowhere else and had appointed herself the chapel's cook and conscience. And at the center, waiting, Zephyrine Sterling.

They'd come back from the lighthouse two days ago with Ilse Marchetti, and Bram was still adjusting to the fact. He'd expected the famous promise to be a weight Zephyrine carried alone, the way they carried everything — folded small, packed tight, weighed nightly like contraband. Instead here was Ilse, sitting against the wall with her father's sea coat around her shoulders, sharpening a boning knife with the unhurried menace of someone who had decided exactly whom it was for. Here was the promise walking around on its own legs.

Zephyrine looked up when Bram knelt at the blanket's edge. "Well?"

"Warm my hands first. What I have is written in something you don't want on your fingers."

They heated a tin plate over the candle, and Bram pressed the apron's inner seam to it, and the grease letters rose brown and shining like something surfacing from deep water. Zephyrine copied the rotations onto the back of a hymnal page while Anselm read aloud over their shoulders, committing it to memory the way he'd once committed schoolroom roll calls — Anselm trusted paper least of any of them, having been a teacher and learned what governments do to textbooks.

"Eight by day, five by night," Anselm murmured. "Change takes twenty minutes. Quay circuit, nineteen minutes dry. And a boy named Renn on the water side who asks questions about endings." He looked up. "That's a lot of certainty for one brandy."

"He's a dockhand whose sister has a name in the ledger. Certainty's the cheapest thing he owns."

"Or the most expensive thing he's selling," said Ilse, without looking up from her knife.

Bram spread his hands. "Both. Everything's both, now. But I'll stake my neck on Teun, which is the only currency I've got left, so draw your own conclusions."

* * *

It was Marta who found the note.

She'd been down to the harbor at dawn with the other fishwives — requisitioned labor now, gutting the army's catch for army rations — and she came back with herring, gossip, and a strip of waxed paper sewn flat into the lining of her basket handle, which she noticed only because her hands knew the weight of baskets and this one had lied to them.

Odile Ferrand's handwriting. Small, upright, unshakable even though — Bram learned later what the interrogation had involved, and marveled that the hand didn't shake at all.

The note was four sentences long.

*I am alive. They keep me for the paperwork, which suits me.*

*The blue ledger survives. The captain locked it himself, map room, second floor, harbor corner, iron box, two keys — his ring and the adjutant's.*

*They burn crates Tuesdays and Fridays after evening ration. Friday's crate is already labeled. I saw the label. It says DOCUMENTS, OBSOLETE.*

*I have hidden pages in psalms. Psalm 119 is very accommodating. Tell no one by name. Trust the boy on the landing.*

Silence held the chapel for a long moment after Anselm finished reading it aloud.

"*Obsolete,*" Zephyrine said at last. The word came out flat, pressed thin. "Two hundred lives, filed under obsolete."

"They haven't burned it yet," Bram said.

"They will on Friday."

"Then we go Thursday night." He heard his own voice take the tone it took when he was laying a course — no weather in it, just bearing and distance. "Listen. Thursday. Day guard changes at six; we let the evening change happen and move in the twenty minutes the sergeant wastes counting twice. Rain's coming — I can taste it in the marsh, and if I'm right the quay circuit stretches past thirty minutes. Ilse cuts the harbor lights at the same hour; she knows the lamp wiring better than the army does, and a dark harbor brings every guard to the windows facing the water. Which is the corner we don't use."

"The painted windows," Anselm said. "They can't see out of the map room regardless."

"No. But they can see out everywhere else, and that's the point. We want eyes on the water, not the yard." Bram tapped the hymnal page where Zephyrine had written *RENN*. "Water side, second floor landing. One boy. Sixteen, untested, thinking about endings. Somebody talks to him. Somebody stands on the far side of that landing and gives him a version of after that's worth looking away for. One minute of looked-away is all we need. Ninety seconds and we're rich in names."

"And if he doesn't look away?" That was Ilse, finally setting down the knife. Her voice was level, but her knuckles weren't. "If the boy does his duty and shouts, and five guards come down a stairwell onto whoever's standing on his landing?"

Bram met her eyes. She had her father's eyes, he realized — the lighthouse keeper's eyes, eyes built for watching for ships that might not make it. He'd watched those waters himself for years, from the smuggling side.

"Then the person on the landing," he said, "better be the fastest liar in this chapel."

Everyone looked at him, which was fair. He was the fastest liar in the chapel and everyone knew it, including him, including whatever remained of his conscience, which had been objecting steadily since the word *Thursday*.

"Thursday," Anselm said quietly, making it a decision. "We'll need passes for the yard. Forged."

"I've got a rubber stamp off a dead quartermaster and a bottle of the right ink. By Wednesday I'll have us walking papers good enough to survive a glance." Bram stood, knees cracking, and felt the strange lightness that came over him whenever a plan stopped being talk and became a thing with a date attached. Hope, he thought. This is what hope feels like when it grows teeth: it doesn't warm you. It bites.

"One more thing." He held up Odile's note. "This goes in the stove tonight. And tomorrow I need someone to find out what the adjutant looks like — face, height, how he wears his cap. Because there are two keys to that iron box, and if the captain's key stays on the captain's belt, then somewhere between the map room and the yard stove, we're going to need to become the adjutant."

Marta snorted. "You? You haven't the shoulders for it."

"I don't need the shoulders," Bram said. "I need twenty minutes, bad lighting, and a coat."

He was almost at the blanket-hung door when Zephyrine caught his arm. Up close, in the candle stub's last light, they looked older than the war had any right to make anyone — worn smooth in some places, split raw in others, like driftwood.

"Friday's crate," Zephyrine said. "*Documents, obsolete.* If we're wrong about any of it — the hour, the rain, the boy —"

"Then we learn what the burning smells like from inside it." Bram covered their hand with his own, briefly. "But we won't be wrong about the boy. I've been sixteen and posted somewhere I didn't believe in. I remember exactly how it felt."

"How did it feel?"

"Like waiting," Bram said, "for someone to give me permission to be on the right side."

He slipped out through the blanket into the full dark. Behind him the chapel held its breath, and beyond the marsh the harbor lay black where the army's lamps burned, and somewhere in that blackness, in an iron box on the second floor, two hundred names waited to find out whether they were paper or people.

Three days to Thursday. Two, if the rain came early.

And then, faint across the water, carried on the wind the way bad news travels — a bell from the customs house, ringing at the wrong hour, ringing twice, which Bram had lived here long enough to know meant one thing:

They were moving the burning forward.
