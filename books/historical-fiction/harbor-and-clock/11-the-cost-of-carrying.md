## 11. The Cost of Carrying

Ilse had spent nine days in the lamp room learning the arithmetic of hiding.

It was simple arithmetic, and it insulted her. One keeper's daughter, one dark lighthouse, forty-one steps down a spiral stair that groaned like a ship in weather — she had counted them in the dark until the numbers wore smooth. Forty-one steps between her and the door, and at the bottom of every one of those steps, someone else was out carrying her name into danger.

She heard Zephyrine come up the stair before she saw them. Everyone in the town had learned to identify footsteps by weight and rhythm now; the whole peninsula moved like a household of thieves. Zephyrine climbed the way they did everything — evenly, without wasted motion, as if hurry were a kind of confession.

"You're back," she said, not turning from the window.

"Bram got me through the marsh channel before the fog lifted." Zephyrine set their satchel on the iron table with the care of a person setting down something that might go off. "Odile sent word out through the dockhand's wife. The ledger survived. It's crated in the captain's map room."

"Crated."

"For burning, most likely. Or shipping. With that captain it's hard to know which is worse."

Ilse turned. Nine days of lamplight-less darkness had taught her face new habits; she could feel them settle now, the stillness she'd borrowed from her father, who had stood watches through two wars without ever letting his hands learn to shake. "Then we go and get it."

"*We*," Zephyrine repeated.

"There are four people planning this and I'm not one of them. I've been counting that."

"You've been counting what?"

"The cost." She came away from the window. The lamp room was cold enough that her breath showed, and she was glad of it; it gave her something to do with her mouth besides argue. "Bram risks his passes. Odile is already in a cell. Anselm is already hunted. And you—" she stopped, redirected, because *you* was a door she couldn't stand in front of for long, "—you walk into checkpoints with codes sewn into your coat. That's five. Five people spending themselves for a lighthouse keeper's daughter who's supposed to sit in the dark and be safe."

"That was the arrangement."

"I didn't agree to any arrangement. You arranged it. Both of you." Her father too — that was the grief under it, always her father, taken three weeks ago with his hands raised and his boots unlaced because they'd come at low tide and he hadn't even had time to lace his boots. He had looked at her over the soldiers' shoulders with an expression she kept in a locked room in her chest and did not open. It hadn't been fear. It had been instruction. *You know where everything is,* he'd said with his whole face. *Keep it.*

"Your name is on a proclamation poster in four towns," Zephyrine said quietly. "Enemy collaborator. If a patrol finds you—"

"If a patrol finds me, they find me doing something. That's a different thing than being found in a lamp room like a lamp that forgot how to light."

Zephyrine almost smiled. She saw them fight it and lose by a millimeter. Nine days of sharing cold bread and colder silences in this tower had taught her the whole small country of their face. "Ilse—"

"No. Sit down. There's something you need to see, and you're going to want to be sitting."

* * *

The half-copy lived inside the counterweight shaft, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed linen, in a cavity her father had chiseled when she was eleven years old and the first war was ending and he had said, in the voice he used for lessons that mattered: *A lighthouse is a promise, Ilse. Promises need duplicates the way ships need charts.*

She brought it up wrapped in her only spare sweater and laid it on the iron table, and unwrapped it with hands that were steady until they weren't.

Forty pages. Thin paper, copied in her father's upright clerk's hand — not his usual hand, which slanted, which was lazy, which she would have known anywhere. This was the hand he used for logbooks that inspectors read. Names, in columns. Ports of transit. Dates. Stamps. Half the ledger of names, two hundred souls' worth of hope reduced to a hundred pages, and here were fifty of them.

Zephyrine didn't touch it at first. They stood over it the way people stood over graves they'd thought were empty. "Where did you get this?"

"He copied it. Last spring, when the passage guarantees were first signed." Ilse heard her own voice go flat and precise, which was what it did instead of breaking. "He went to the customs house ostensibly about oil requisitions. Odile Ferrand let him sit in the records room for an afternoon. He came home and didn't sleep for four days and I thought he was ill. He was copying. In the lamp room, between wick trimmings, in the hand he saved for documents. He never told me what it was. He just told me where the cavity was, again, like I was eleven. Like he knew."

"He knew," Zephyrine said.

"He knew the republic would rot before he died. Is that what knowing looks like? Planning your daughter's survival around paperwork?" She heard the fury arrive in her voice and let it, because fury was easier to carry than the other thing. "Half. It's half. When the soldiers came for him, there wasn't time — he'd burned the rest two weeks earlier because patrols had searched the house twice and he said—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "He said a copy that gets found kills everyone whose name is in it. So he kept half and burned half, and he told me which half, and he made me recite the ports of transit until I could do it in my sleep, and then they took him over the rocks at low tide with his boots unlaced, and the other hundred pages are ash in a stove in the keeper's cottage."

The wind moved around the lantern housing and found nothing to say either.

Zephyrine sat down slowly across the table from her. In the gray light through the storm panes their face looked older than the last time she'd studied it in peacetime, at the harbor fair, before any of it — but then, everyone's did. "Fifty pages," they said. "Names and transit routes. Ilse, do you understand what this is? The amnesty lists are being drafted from ledgers in army hands. If the map-room ledger burns or ships away, this is—"

"I know what it is."

"It's half of two hundred lives."

"And it cost my father his method, his sleep, and possibly his life, so don't you dare tell me to hide it in a wall and stay out of it." She put both palms flat on the table, on either side of the oilcloth, claiming the ground. "I'm done being cargo, Zephyrine. I've been carried since the day you promised my father you'd see me right. Carried to the marshes, carried to the chapel, carried up these stairs and told to sit tight while other people spend themselves on my behalf. I sat in this room for nine days doing sums, and every sum came out the same: I am the only person alive who knows those fifty pages by heart and knows which pages are missing and can recognize my father's abbreviations. Nobody else can authenticate a forgery if we have to forge. Nobody else knows the transit routes well enough to spot an error. You need me, and more to the point—" her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through, "—more to the point, I need to not be in this room anymore. Grief that doesn't move turns to stone. I've watched it happen. My father's hands never shook because he never once sat still."

Zephyrine was quiet a long moment. Through the storm panes, the harbor lay flat and pewter-colored, the requisitioned customs house square and smug against the quay, smoke rising from its chimneys at hours no honest office cooked a meal.

"Fifty pages," Zephyrine said finally. "And the other hundred?"

"In the stove." She swallowed. "But not entirely. He made me read them aloud. All of them. Twice each, the winter before last, on long watches. He called it keeping me awake." She laughed, and the laugh hurt going out. "I thought it was a game. A hundred names, ports, dates, stamps. I can't write them all down — I'd misremember some, transpose others, and a wrong name is worse than no name, he drilled that into me too. But I hold the missing half in here—" she touched her temple, "—well enough to check anything against it. Well enough to know what we're missing and who. Well enough that if the ledger in that building burns, not all of it will burn true."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long time. She knew this look too — the one where they recalculated, ran the numbers on a person like they ran numbers on everything they carried. Weighing what things would cost if found. She watched the sum change as they looked at her.

"You're certain," they said.

"I've been certain since the second night. I waited until you came back safe to say it, because unlike some people I plan."

That earned the smile, full measure this time, brief and real. Then it faded into something more careful. "If you come off this rock with us, there's no arrangement protecting you. No line I can draw where you're safe on one side. Everything I've done for nine months has been built around one idea — keep Ilse Marchetti outside the reach of the thing that wrote her name on a poster. If I stop doing that, and something happens to you—"

"Then it happens while I'm moving," Ilse said. "Which is the only acceptable direction for grief."

* * *

They planned through the afternoon by feel and memory, no lamp, voices low under the wind. Bram's forged passes could cover three travelers to the fish market, not four — unless one of the passes described a lighthouse keeper's widow's daughter traveling to identify confiscated family effects, which was a category the requisition bureaucracy had invented itself and therefore could not refuse. Odile's smuggled note had given guard rotations. Ilse drew the customs house interior from her father's descriptions, from the one afternoon she'd visited the records room herself at fourteen, bored, memorizing doorways out of a child's habit of mapping exits.

She was good at this. She discovered it with something like embarrassment, the way you discover a talent for swimming only after falling into deep water. Zephyrine spread the forged passes on the table and she saw at once that the third one had the revenue stamp inverted, and said so, and watched Zephyrine go very still.

"Bram missed that."

"Bram's never worked a counter. I stamped requisition forms for my father for six years. The stamp sits crown-up when the document faces the reader. Every clerk in that building will know it without knowing they know it."

Zephyrine picked up the pass and looked at her across it, and she saw the exact moment they stopped arguing with her. It was visible, like a tide turning — reluctance giving way to the colder, cleaner emotion underneath, which was respect, which was also relief. Nobody wanted to be the only competent person in a drowning room.

"We'll need the harbor lights cut the night we move," Zephyrine said. "The mole light and the pier head. Bram says the patrol boat runs on the lights; kill them for eleven minutes and the channel is blind. But the switchgear for the mole light is in the harbor office, and the pier head is gas — someone has to climb it with a hood and a match and stand there in the open."

"I know which one you're assigning me," Ilse said, "and you can save it. The pier head ladder is mine. I've climbed it since I was twelve, in weather that would peel paint. There's no one in your chapel full of deserters who has done it once."

"You haven't done it in nine months of war conditions with a patrol boat in the channel."

"No," she agreed. "And neither has anyone else. Which makes me the least risky choice, by your own arithmetic. What would it cost if found? Less, for me, than anyone. They're hunting a novelist's courier and a defrocked teacher and a smuggler with a face known on three coasts. They are not watching for the enemy collaborator's daughter to walk up a ladder they've forgotten exists, in her own village, where every stone knows her." She held their eyes. "Let me spend myself on my own name. It's the only currency I have left and I will not die rich in it."

Zephyrine closed their eyes briefly — the look of a person taking on weight and adjusting their spine for it. When they opened them, they nodded once, and it was done, and she felt the strangest sensation: lighter. Not happy. Nothing near happy. But the stone in her chest had turned back into flesh, aching and alive.

"One more thing," she said, and pulled the oilcloth bundle toward her, and began folding the fifty pages into the false bottom of the satchel Zephyrine had set on the table. "These travel with me now. Not sewn in your coat. If we're separated, the copies and the memory are in different bodies. That's the whole point of a duplicate — it fails separately or not at all. My father's rule."

Zephyrine watched her fold. "You sound like him."

"I am what's left of him," she said. "So treat me accordingly."

Below them, far down the dark tower, a sound rose through the stone — the tide turning in the base of the rock, the sea filling the old cistern channel, patient as a clock. Ilse had grown up believing lighthouses kept time for ships. She understood now it was the reverse: the water kept the time, and everything human merely kept faith against it.

Three days, Zephyrine had said the plan needed. Three days to confirm rotations, position Bram, get word to Odile's contact.

On the morning of the second day, the fish market bell began ringing wrong — fast, arrhythmic, alarm — and Zephyrine was down the stairs before Ilse had even reached the top step, and the plan, whatever it had been, was already bleeding.
