## 12. Betrayal at the Fish Market

The fish market smelled like the war had already won.

Zephyrine noticed it first as a wrongness in the air — not the usual brine and diesel and ice-melt of the morning stalls, but something scorched underneath, as if someone had burned documents in the harbor office again and the smoke had settled here to sulk. The stalls were half-stocked. The scales were rusting. A woman sold eels from a bucket and wouldn't meet anyone's eyes, which was normal now; everyone had learned the arithmetic of looking away.

Bram walked ahead with a crate of pilchards he hadn't paid for and didn't intend to sell. That was his version of a cover story: carry something heavy enough that you look like you have somewhere to be.

"You're doing the thing," Bram said without turning around.

"What thing."

"The thing where you count exits before you count people." He shifted the crate to his hip. "There's four ways out of this market. I've counted them twice. Now look at Anselm when he comes in and smile at him like he's your old schoolteacher, because he is, and because if you look at him the way you're looking at those doors, we'll both hang."

Zephyrine made their face do what it was told. It was harder than it used to be. There had been a time — they could almost date it, the last autumn of peace, Ilse laughing on the lighthouse gallery with gull-wind in her hair — when Zephyrine's face still obeyed them. Now it reported only to dread.

Anselm Rey came through the market gate at half past seven exactly, because he was a man who believed that punctuality was a form of hope. He wore a fishmonger's apron over his coat and carried a basket of chalk — actual chalk, schoolroom chalk, bought at three times its price from a boy who didn't ask why a deserter needed it. Anselm had been teaching again, in the chapel, after dark. Writing names on slate. Drilling the deserters on the harbor rotations until even the ones who couldn't read could recite them like psalms.

He stopped at the eel stall, haggled badly on purpose, and said the phrase they'd agreed on: "Too dear for Tuesday."

"Everything's too dear for Tuesday," Zephyrine answered, and fell into step beside him toward the far corner where the ice house leaned against the seawall.

The meeting point was good and it was bad. Good: the ice house had two exits, a blind spot in the patrol circuit that Bram had timed for six days, and enough noise from the crusher that low voices vanished into it. Bad: it was public. Every meeting since the requisition had been a negotiation between secrecy and numbers — you needed enough people to move the ledger, and every person was one more chance that someone hungry would do arithmetic with your life.

They had eight tonight. Zephyrine counted them under the pretense of inspecting the ice: Odile's cousin Toma, who'd smuggled her notes out of the jail by hiding them in fish bellies; two brothers off a coal barge whose names Zephyrine deliberately did not know yet; Marta, who had rowed messages across the estuary at night for eleven days running; a boy called Pip who was seventeen and lied about it badly; and the Kessel woman, Hanne, who had deserted the army's supply corps three weeks ago and brought with her a memory of warehouse schedules worth more than the rest of them combined.

And one more. The one Zephyrine couldn't stop watching, though there was no reason yet, only an itch between the shoulder blades that had kept them alive through four checkpoints and one firing squad's near miss.

His name was Corvan. He'd come to the chapel nine days ago, barefoot, having walked out of the army's forward camp with nothing but dysentery and a rifle he'd thrown in a ditch. He was quiet. He was grateful. He wept the first night during Anselm's reading of the names, and Anselm had put a hand on his shoulder and said, *You're among the living now, son.*

Zephyrine wanted to believe that gratitude was a kind of armor. They no longer could.

* * *

The plan went around the circle in pieces, each piece small enough to hold innocently.

Toma, gutting mackerel, murmured: "Guard rotation changes at eleven. The map room goes from two men to one. The one they leave is the fat one, Berenz, and Berenz takes his supper at midnight sharp."

Marta, stacking ice chips: "The boat. Fog's due Thursday. Estuary current turns at two in the morning. If we're not on the water by half past, we're rowing against the world."

Hanne, weighing nothing on the scale: "The captain dines with the port commandant Wednesday. His adjutant drinks. The adjutant signs the passes for wine deliveries, and Bram's forged papers are better than real ones now."

And Anselm, chalking a diagram on the ice house wall as if it were a blackboard and he were thirty years younger: the customs house, its doors, its blind window, the forty feet of open cobbles between the seawall gate and the side entrance where the crates of confiscated paper stood waiting for the burner.

"Forty feet," Anselm said softly. "That's our whole war. Forty feet of open ground, in fog, carrying a book that weighs as much as a child." He looked around the circle, and Zephyrine saw him do the thing he always did, the thing that made deserters trust him and made Zephyrine afraid for him in equal measure: he looked at each of them like a student, like someone worth saving, like a name. "We rehearse tomorrow night. We go Thursday, in the fog, or we don't go at all."

"And if the captain moves the ledger before Thursday?" That was Hanne.

"He won't," Anselm said. "Odile's note confirmed it's still crated in the map room. Burning is scheduled, not impulsive. Bureaucrats burn on timetables." He smiled, tired and wry. "Our enemies' greatest gift has always been their love of procedure."

It was the kind of thing he'd said a hundred times in the chapel, warm enough to laugh at, certain enough to build on. Later, Zephyrine would take it apart sentence by sentence and find the flaw, and hate themselves for how long it took.

Because Anselm had said *Thursday*. And the meeting was set for Wednesday. And the rehearsal for tomorrow night. Four facts, arranged correctly, and any one of them handed to an officer would have been worthless — but handed over together, they were the whole plan, gift-wrapped.

Someone had only to listen.

* * *

They broke apart the way water breaks apart, in ones and twos, no pattern. Zephyrine lingered to help Toma hose down the stall, which gave them a reason to stand with their back to the seawall and their eyes on the market gate.

The itch between the shoulder blades had grown teeth.

"Corvan," they said quietly.

Toma's knife paused. "What about him?"

"Where does he sleep at the chapel?"

"West wall. Under the hole in the roof, says the rain doesn't bother him. Says he slept worse in the trenches." Toma shrugged, resumed gutting. "Why?"

"Who talked to him last?"

"Everyone talks to him. That's the whole point of the place." Toma glanced up, and Zephyrine saw the suspicion arrive in his face like weather. "You think he's a crow. You think he's flying information back to the roost."

"I think everyone is a crow until proven otherwise. I think I'm a crow. I think you are."

"He cried reading the names."

"So did I," Zephyrine said. "Crying costs nothing. Rations cost something, this month. There's a word going around the dock queues — you've heard it — that the army pays in bread for names. Real names. Places. Times." They set down the hose. "I'm not saying it's him. I'm saying I want to know who spoke to him last, and I want to know it before Thursday, and I don't want anyone at the chapel asked directly, because if it's him and we start looking at him sideways, he'll know the plan's blown and we'll lose everything anyway."

Toma was silent for a moment. Then: "Anselm spoke to him last. After the reading. Two nights ago. By the stove." He hesitated. "Whatever your itch says, Zeph, Anselm vouched for him. Anselm doesn't vouch cheap."

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "He doesn't." 

That was the horror of it, they thought, hauling the crate toward Bram's waiting cart. Not that a traitor might exist — traitors were a renewable resource in a starving country — but that the traitor's best cover would be Anselm's decency. Anselm believed so hard in the possibility of people that he'd left the door unlocked for the wolf, on principle, because locking it would mean admitting the wolf had won something already.

Zephyrine found Bram by the cart and said, low: "We move the timetable. Tonight, not Thursday."

Bram didn't argue, which frightened them more than an argument would have. He just looked at the market, at the gate, at the ordinary morning traffic of a town learning to live inside a noose, and said: "Can't. Boat's not ready. Marta needs the tide tables reworked, and the passes aren't signed till the adjutant drinks on Wednesday." He rubbed his jaw. "We're nailed to the calendar, Zeph. That's the whole cruelty of it. Even our escape runs on their paperwork."

"Then we change what we can. Move the rehearsal. Change the rendezvous point. If Corvan sold us the shape of the plan, let him sell them a shape that's already wrong."

"Now you're thinking like a smuggler. Grief's done you some good." Bram flicked the reins. "Fish market day after tomorrow, though — Anselm wants a final check-in before the rehearsal, all eight of us, same corner—"

"That's insane."

"That's Anselm. He says a family that stops meeting is a family that's already scattered." Bram clicked his tongue at the horse and the cart rolled toward the seawall road. "Talk him out of it if you can. I've tried. He once kept a detention class after school during an artillery barrage because attendance is attendance."

* * *

Zephyrine spent the walk back to the marshes composing the argument they'd make to Anselm, and never got to make it.

The message came at dusk, relayed through two hands and a child, in the chapel's private grammar — a chalk mark on the third fencepost meaning *meeting stands*, a knot in a rag meaning *all well*. All well. Zephyrine stood in the salt grass with the fading light on the water and told themselves the cold in their stomach was just hunger.

They went back into town the next morning with Marta's rowing calluses remembered on their own hands, wearing a fishwife's shawl and the face that obeyed dread.

The fish market was busy. Too busy, some part of Zephyrine noted, and dismissed it, because markets were busy everywhere now, refugees and ration queues and the endless gray commerce of survival. The eel woman was there. The ice house stood open. The crusher thundered.

Anselm arrived at half past seven exactly, punctual as hope, and Zephyrine stepped out of the crowd toward him, and that was when the itch screamed.

Not the gate. The gate was fine. It was the roofline — the customs house across the square had men on its roofline, prone, and the morning sun glinted once off a rifle barrel the way sunlight glints off ice, and then Zephyrine was moving, no decision involved, the body doing its ancient work.

"Anselm—"

They grabbed his sleeve and turned him and the world turned with him, and then the market turned into a machine.

Soldiers came out of nowhere the way soldiers only come out of nowhere in ambushes: from behind the ice house, from the fish cellars, from a covered lorry that had been parked at the gate all morning being furniture. The eel woman dropped flat behind her bucket with the practiced ease of someone who had survived other mornings. The crusher kept thundering. Somewhere a whistle blew, two notes, the army's signal for *seal the square*, and Zephyrine heard the sound of boots on cobbles multiply the way rain multiplies.

"The ice house—" Anselm shouted, but the ice house was already full of soldiers, and the second exit was full of soldiers, and Zephyrine understood in one cold flash that the six days of timing, the blind spot, the rotation — all of it had been studied, measured, and sold.

Corvan. Barefoot Corvan, grateful Corvan, weeping Corvan. Sold them for rations. Sold Anselm, who had put a hand on his shoulder and called him among the living.

"Run," Anselm said, and it wasn't a plea; it was a teacher's instruction, calm, final, the tone he must have used on children during air raids. He twisted out of Zephyrine's grip with surprising strength and shoved them hard toward the seawall gap — the fourth exit, the one Bram had counted twice, the one only three people knew about because Anselm had insisted on keeping one route unspoken, even in a room of trusted faces. *A family shares,* he'd said, *but a family also keeps one door for emergencies.* 

"Come *with* me—"

"Somebody has to be seen running the wrong way," Anselm said, and smiled — actually smiled, chalk dust and blood later, Zephyrine would remember that smile — and turned, and raised his voice into the chaos like a man calling roll: "Here! I'm here! Whatever you want, it's me you want!"

He made himself loud. He made himself the target. He drew the net after him like a man pulling a curtain, and Zephyrine watched three soldiers peel off the ice house and converge on his raised hands, and every instinct Zephyrine owned screamed to follow, to fight, to fix it, and the promise — the ledger, Ilse, two hundred names, the forty feet of open ground — held them to the seawall gap like rivets hold a hull.

Anselm went down to his knees on the wet cobbles with his hands laced behind his head, correct as a schoolboy, correct as ever. A boot caught him in the ribs anyway. He didn't cry out. Of course he didn't. He was counting exits, probably, even now, even kneeling; he was making sure the last one stayed open behind Zephyrine.

Zephyrine ran.

Through the seawall gap, into the gut-run between the fish cellars where the smell of brine hid the smell of fear-sweat, past a cellar door that opened and a hand that dragged them in — Toma, gray-faced, smelling of mackerel, holding a finger to his lips while boots passed the door close enough to hear buckles. Outside, the whistle sounded again, and a voice Zephyrine didn't know shouted orders about the square, the gates, the boats, and one order rose above the rest, repeated twice, the order that told Zephyrine exactly what the betrayal had purchased:

*"Find the schoolteacher. The captain wants the schoolteacher alive."*

Alive. Because Anselm knew the plan. Because Anselm knew the names. Because a man who taught children to read could be taught, given time and methods, to read aloud in the other direction.

In the dark of the fish cellar, Zephyrine pressed their back to the cold stone and shook — not quietly, not with dignity, just shook, the body settling its accounts. Toma knelt beside them and didn't touch them, which was its own mercy.

"They'll take him to the jail," Toma whispered. "Odile's there. She'll see him come in."

"Odile's in solitary."

"She was. They moved her last week — overcrowding. She's in the common cell by the east stair." Toma's whisper cracked. "She'll see him. And she memorizes things, Zeph. Whatever he says on the way in, whatever they let him shout — she'll keep it. She keeps everything now. It's what she does instead of despairing."

Boots again, farther off. The whistle changed to three notes: search concluded, perimeter held. Somewhere above them the market was being put back together around a missing schoolteacher, eels rebucketed, ice re-stacked, the town performing its daily impersonation of a place where nothing happens.

Zephyrine closed their eyes and saw the chalk dust on Anselm's fingers, the diagram on the ice house wall, the forty feet of open ground that had been their whole war. The plan was in enemy hands now, every piece of it except the door Anselm had kept unspoken — and the timetable was nailed to the calendar, and the fog was coming Thursday whether they were ready or not, and the man who held the found family together was in a cell with a captain who wanted him alive.

"We get him out," Zephyrine said. Their voice came out flat and strange, sanded down to its frame. "With Odile. Both of them. Before Thursday."

"Zeph, the plan barely covers a *book*—"

"The plan's dead. This is a new one." They stood, knees protesting, and pulled the fishwife's shawl straight, and felt something happen in their chest that they recognized from checkpoints and coat linings and every other moment the world had demanded a price: the dread draining out and leaving behind only the arithmetic. "And I know exactly who owes us a favor with a wine delivery on Wednesday."

Above the cellar door, through a grime-fogged vent, the noon bell began to ring across the occupied town — and beneath it, faintly, the lighthouse horn sounded once, though there was no fog yet, as if the sea itself were clearing its throat for what was coming.
