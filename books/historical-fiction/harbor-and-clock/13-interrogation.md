## 13. Interrogation

They took his boots first. That was how Anselm Rey understood he was in real trouble — not the rifle butt across the mouth in the fish market, not the sack over his head, not the ride in the truck with someone else's blood on the floorboards. It was the boots. A man without boots could not run. Someone had thought about that. Someone had sat down and considered Anselm's feet.

He tried to hold onto that detail as they marched him down a corridor that smelled of wet stone and lamp oil, because details were what he had left. Twenty-six years of teaching arithmetic to children who mostly didn't want it, then two years of teaching nothing at all, of keeping a chapel roof from falling in and keeping deserters from both armies from killing each other over card games. None of it had prepared him for a chair bolted to a floor. But details kept a mind working. The corridor was eleven paces long. The third floorboard from the door creaked. The lamp in here burned greenish, which meant cheap oil, which meant supply problems, which meant the army requisitioning this town couldn't even light its own cells properly.

A man who noticed things was still a teacher. He would hold onto that as long as they let him.

The room held a table, the bolted chair, a drain in the floor he chose not to think about, and two soldiers who put him in the chair with the brisk indifference of men stacking firewood. His wrists were strapped down. Then everyone left except one person.

He was young. That was the first thing Anselm noticed about him, before the rank tabs, before the careful face. Young — twenty-four at most, with the kind of jaw that hadn't finished deciding what it wanted to be when it grew up. He carried a folder and a cup of coffee and set both down on the table like a man setting out tools for a job he'd been trained for but hadn't yet done.

"Anselm Rey," the young officer said, reading from the folder. "Schoolteacher. Harbor district."

"Retired," Anselm said. His voice came out worse than he expected, thick and split where his lip had opened against the rifle butt. "Children are very difficult to retire from, but I managed."

The officer did not smile. He pulled the other chair around and sat with his knees almost touching Anselm's, the way they taught them, close enough that politeness became pressure.

"You were taken at the fish market at six in the morning, in conversation with known smugglers, a customs clerk under arrest warrant, and an enemy national."

"That's a busy market," Anselm said. "You should see it on Saturdays."

The backhand came fast and open-palmed, more noise than force, but Anselm's head rang anyway. He turned his face back slowly, deliberately, and looked at the officer the way he used to look at boys who'd thrown inkwells — not angry, just cataloguing.

"I'll ask you plainly," the officer said. "Where is Zephyrine Sterling?"

And there it was, laid on the table between them next to the coffee: the name. So they knew. They knew Sterling existed, knew Sterling mattered, and were asking a schoolteacher because the fish market raid had netted everything except what they wanted.

Anselm had made a decision in the truck, jostling along with a sack on his head. He had decided that he was an old man with bad lungs and a bad knee, and that whatever they took from him, he would make them take slowly, stupidly, at cost. He was not brave. He had watched actual bravery in that chapel — boys of nineteen writing letters home they'd never send, Odile Ferrand walking out of her own arrest with her chin up. Next to that, he was only stubborn. But stubbornness, he had told generations of failing students, is just patience applied under adverse conditions, and patience was the one subject he had never flunked.

"Sterling," Anselm said, tasting the name. "I don't know anyone by that name."

* * *

The first day, they asked him questions for nine hours. He counted by the changing of the guard outside the door — two men, rotated every three hours, boots on stone, a rhythm you could set a watch by if your watch had been confiscated along with your boots.

The young officer came back each time with the same folder and the same coffee, and each time the questions circled the same ground: Sterling, the ledger, the chapel, names. Always names. Who sheltered in the chapel, who moved through the marshes, who forged passes, and above all, where was Sterling now.

Anselm gave him weather. He gave him the harbor district street by street, accurate enough to check and boring enough to regret checking. He described a fishmonger named Madame Tercier who, he was nearly certain, did not exist. When pressed about the chapel, he described the chapel — the real one, the ruined one, with its collapsed nave and its dry-rotted pews — and described it empty, describing its emptiness with such loving pedagogical thoroughness that he watched the young officer's pen slow down. How many men slept there? None, Anselm said. How recently? Years, he said. It isn't habitable; look at the east wall yourself, Lieutenant, there's a crack you could post a sentry inside.

The officer looked at him for a long moment after that. Something flickered behind the careful face — not suspicion exactly. Suspicion Anselm could have handled. This was closer to fatigue.

"Lieutenant," Anselm said, before he could stop himself, because thirty years of classroom habit dies hard. "May I ask your name?"

"You may not."

"It helps me keep things straight," Anselm said. "Names help. You're asking me for so many; it seems fair I should hold one of yours."

The officer closed the folder. "This isn't a negotiation."

"No," Anselm agreed. "It's a lesson. And I've been on the other side of the desk my whole life, so I know how badly it can go."

The officer stood, and for a moment Anselm thought he saw it again — that flicker, that hairline fracture in the certainty — and then it was gone behind discipline, and the lieutenant said, "We'll continue tomorrow," and left him alone with the green lamp and the drain.

Anselm worked his jaw, tested his ribs, found them bruised but whole, and allowed himself the smallest arithmetic of hope: they hadn't beaten him today. Not really. Not the way the folder promised they eventually would. Which meant they still believed he might talk. Which meant every hour he didn't was an hour Sterling and the others were somewhere else, moving.

Nine hours bought. Now count the rest.

* * *

The second day, the questions changed, and that frightened him more than any blow.

They stopped asking where Sterling was and started asking what Sterling wanted. They brought in photographs — the fish market, the chapel door, the breakwater — and spread them on the table like cards. They asked about boats. They asked about the neutral shipping lanes with a specificity that made Anselm's stomach drop, because it meant someone upstairs had already drawn the map of what Sterling would try, and the map ended at the pier.

He answered with misdirection so old-fashioned it was nearly respectable. Yes, smugglers talked about boats, all smugglers talked about boats, it was practically their trade. North channel, south channel, he'd heard both mentioned in the chapel by men whose names he swore he never learned — deserters don't introduce themselves, Lieutenant, they're not joining a club. He steered south when he guessed north. He invented a rendezvous point up the coast at a cove called Saint-Aubin-sous-Roc, which existed, as far as he knew, only in a poem he'd once taught to fourteen-year-olds.

The lieutenant wrote it all down. That was the part Anselm watched most closely — not the questions, but the pen. When Anselm lied well, the pen moved steadily. When Anselm lied poorly, the pen paused, and he learned to hear that pause the way a sailor hears a change in wind.

Near evening, the lieutenant set down his pen entirely.

"My father was a schoolmaster," he said.

Anselm kept his face still. In his experience, confessions arrived disguised as small talk, and cruelty often waited just behind tenderness, wearing its coat.

"In Vellay. Upper school. Mathematics." The lieutenant straightened the photographs into a neat stack, edges aligned. "He said teachers make the worst prisoners. Because we can't stop organizing information, even our own suffering. We catalogue it." He looked up. "Is that true?"

"It's true," Anselm said.

"He also said—" the lieutenant stopped himself, and something moved across his face, quickly, like a bird crossing a window. He gathered the folder and stood. "Tomorrow."

"Yes," Anselm said to the closing door. "We'll continue."

Then he sat in the green lamplight and shook, quietly, hands strapped to the armrests, because the boy's voice when he'd said *my father was a schoolmaster* had sounded, for half a second, like a student's voice. Like a hand raised in class. And Anselm Rey had spent thirty years learning that a crack in a wall starts smaller than anything — a flaw no wider than a hair — and that given load and time, a crack does not heal. It only decides which way it runs.

* * *

The third day, they brought in a senior officer.

He was gray and comfortable-looking, with the soft hands of a man who had spent the war signing things, and he introduced himself as Colonel Brandt and apologized for the conditions with such sincerity that Anselm nearly admired it. Coffee was brought. Real coffee. A cushioned chair, even, though Anselm stayed in the bolted one, since standing seemed unwise.

"Professor Rey—"

"Monsieur Rey. Professor implies a salary I never received."

"Monsieur Rey." Brandt smiled with genuine warmth, or a superb imitation. "Let me be direct, because I respect directness in educated men. Your friends are conducting an operation against this garrison. We do not fully understand its scope. What we do understand is that it will fail, and that everyone associated with it will be processed under Article Nine, which I will not describe to you, because you strike me as a man with sufficient imagination." He folded his hands. "You are old. You have perhaps ten years left, and I am authorized to let you spend them badly or spend them comfortably. All I require is Sterling's location and the nature of the plan."

There it was, laid out cleanly, without the boy's clumsy cruelty. Just arithmetic. Ten years against two hundred names.

Anselm thought about the chapel. About the driftwood fires, and Odile's psalms, and Bram Voss laughing with a cigarette in the rain, and Ilse Marchetti sitting with her arms around her knees listening while he explained, yet again, why the east wall would hold another winter. About Zephyrine Sterling, who had come to him six days ago with fear in their eyes and said *I made a promise, Anselm, before any of this, when promises were cheap,* and Anselm had answered the way a teacher answers: *then it's the expensive ones that matter.*

"Colonel," Anselm said, "let me be direct, since you respect it. You've offered me ten years. I'm sixty-one. My father died at sixty-three and my mother at fifty-nine, so your offer is statistically generous, and I thank you for it." He leaned forward as far as the straps allowed. "But you've made a category error, and I'd hate for an educated man to leave with it uncorrected. You believe you're holding something of value. You are holding a retired schoolteacher with a bad knee. What you actually want is out of your reach, and my silence costs you nothing except time — which, I notice from your supply oil and your requisitioned buildings, you are also short of."

Brandt's smile remained fixed for a full three seconds after Anselm finished. Then it went away, and what replaced it was not rage but something colder and more instructive: reassessment. The look of a man discovering the merchandise was defective.

"Article Nine," Brandt said mildly, and left.

The young lieutenant appeared an hour later, alone, after dark, and dismissed the guards at the door with a word. He sat down across from Anselm and did not open the folder. For a long time he said nothing, and Anselm let him, because silence is a tool and the boy needed practice holding it.

"The cove," the lieutenant finally said. "Saint-Aubin-sous-Roc."

"What about it?"

"There is no Saint-Aubin-sous-Roc." He said it flatly, without triumph. "I checked the survey charts. There's a Saint-Aubin inland, forty kilometers from water. You gave me a place that doesn't exist and I wrote it in my report." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, and for a moment he looked every hour of twenty-four and several years younger. "Do you understand what happens to an officer whose intelligence proves unreliable? Do you know what Colonel Brandt does to officers who waste his time?"

"I imagine it resembles what he does to schoolteachers," Anselm said.

The lieutenant almost laughed. It escaped before he could stop it — a single breath of it, bitter — and then he mastered himself and leaned forward, and his voice dropped to something that wasn't interrogation anymore.

"Why?" he said. "Two hundred people on some ledger. Names on paper. Why are all of you — the smuggler, the clerk, the girl at the lighthouse, you — why does any of you care enough to die for ink? I've read the amnesty directives. I've seen the exclusion lists. Those names are going to be erased regardless. Paper doesn't survive this war. Nothing survives it. So tell me, Monsieur Rey, because I genuinely cannot work it out: what is it for?"

Anselm studied him. The green lamp hummed. Outside, boots changed shift — three hours, on schedule, the whole machine ticking beautifully toward its purposeless conclusions.

"When you were a boy," Anselm said, "did your father ever lose your name?"

The lieutenant blinked. "What?"

"Your name. On a register, a roster, a class list. Did a clerk somewhere ever spell it wrong, cross it out, leave it off? Did you ever stand in a queue and discover that officially, on paper, in the place where it counted, you weren't there?"

Slowly, the lieutenant nodded. "The mobilization rolls. Forty-nine. They had me listed dead. Clerical error. It took my mother four months to correct it." He stopped. Anselm watched him arrive at the rest of the sentence on his own, watched it land — four months of being nobody, and that was in peacetime, and that was fixable, and that was one mother with one son.

"Yes," Anselm said gently. "That. Two hundred times."

The lieutenant sat back. He looked at the folder he hadn't opened, at his own neat handwriting stacked inside it, at three days of a place that didn't exist and a chapel that was empty and a fishmonger who had never been born. He was doing arithmetic. Anselm recognized the posture; he'd seen it a thousand times across a schoolroom desk, the moment the numbers stopped agreeing with each other and the student realized one of them had to be wrong, and it was never the one they hoped.

"I can't alter your report," the lieutenant said at last, quietly, to the table rather than to Anselm. "It's filed. If I amend it, they'll ask why, and then they'll ask me about you, and then—" He exhaled. "Three days. That's what your lies have purchased, Monsieur Rey. Brandt wants Sterling before the delegation arrives to review the exclusion lists, and he's ordered a full sweep of the harbor quarter for dawn, day after tomorrow. Every building. Every boat. After that, no amount of misdirection will matter, because they won't need you anymore."

"Dawn," Anselm repeated. "Day after tomorrow."

"That's the order." The lieutenant stood, buckled his folder, and paused at the door with his back turned. When he spoke again, his voice had climbed back inside its uniform, but barely. "My father also said that the worst thing a teacher can do is mark a student wrong when the student has caught an error in the answer key. He said most of them never forgive you, and they shouldn't."

He left. The lock turned. Boots receded down the eleven-pace corridor, over the creaking third board.

Anselm sat alone in the green light and permitted himself, for exactly one minute, to feel the full weight of his ribs and his split lip and his missing boots. Then he began to calculate.

Three days. Dawn sweep of the harbor quarter. The ledger crated, Sterling moving, Ilse hidden, Bram running passes — and none of them knew. No one knew. The answer key was wrong, and the error was sitting in a cell with no boots and no way to send a message except—

Except.

He closed his eyes and listened to the guard rotation: two men, three hours, boots on stone. He listened the way he had once taught children to listen for the beat in music, the steady thing underneath, the thing you could count on. Everything that could be counted, he had told them, could eventually be predicted. And everything that could be predicted, he had never quite said aloud, could eventually be used.

Dawn, day after tomorrow. Which meant he had until then to get a word out of this cell and across a town crawling with soldiers — one word, one syllable, *dawn* — to people who might not even be looking for messages from a man with no boots.

Somewhere beyond the wall, out on the black water, a foghorn sounded. Long, then short. Patient as a school bell.

Anselm Rey began, carefully, to cough.
