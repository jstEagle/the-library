## 14. The Captain's Dinner

Bram Voss had spent eleven years lying to customs officers, so a dinner party posed no great difficulty. The trick was the same in both professions: never volunteer, never hesitate, and always be slightly more boring than the question deserves.

He practiced the accent on the skiff crossing from the salt road landing — not the harbor town's flat vowels but the rounded ones from upriver, where the wine actually came from. He practiced the name too. *Aurel Dantz, purveyor to hotels that no longer existed.* He'd worn better coats in worse causes. The one he had on was genuine wool, moth-bitten at the cuff in a way that suggested honest poverty rather than desperate costume, and Odile had sewn two sample bottles into the false bottom of his trade case along with forged requisition passes stamped with a seal she'd reproduced from memory, having spent six years forging them for a living before the war made forgery redundant.

"You'll be fine," Zephyrine had said at the landing, and then, because Zephyrine could never let a kindness stand unqualified: "If they take you, we can't come for you."

"I know," Bram said. "It's why you sent me and not yourself."

That wasn't entirely true. They'd sent Bram because Bram could pass, and Zephyrine's face was on two proclamations now, and Ilse's on three. But it was also true that Bram was the only one of them who could sit across from an enemy officer and smile without it costing him anything visible. He had learned long ago that the cheapest thing a man can spend is his own contempt.

The banquet was being held in the old Harbor Exchange, its marble floor still salt-stained from the flood two winters back, its chandeliers hung with bunting in colors that belonged to a country none of the guests currently represented. A requisition banquet. The army had seized the town's fish cellars, its coal, its boats, and now it was throwing a party with what remained of the wine.

Bram joined the queue of actual tradesmen at the service entrance, holding his case and his papers, breathing through the wait the way he'd taught younger smugglers to breathe through checkpoint waits — slow exhale, empty face. The corporal checking documents barely looked at him. Officers wanted wine; nobody questioned the man who brought it.

Inside, the hall smelled of candle wax and roast something — pork, probably, requisitioned from a farm whose family was now eating nettle soup somewhere behind the lines. Bram counted exits out of habit: service door behind him, garden doors at the far end, a stair down to the cellar corridor that connected, if his memory of the dockhand's sketch held, to the rear court of the customs house next door.

*Next door.* That was the whole geometry of the evening. The captain had moved his headquarters into the requisitioned customs house and his dining table forty feet from the map room where, if Odile's note told the truth, the ledger of two hundred names sat locked in a crate.

Bram presented himself at the wine table and began doing the thing he did best: existing usefully. He decanted. He recommended. He told a quartermaster major that the '36 was past its peak and watched the major drink it anyway and declare it excellent, because men who steal countries cannot bear to be told their stolen wine is tired.

By the second hour he had learned three things worth carrying home, and then the fourth found him.

* * *

The first thing: rotations. Two lieutenants, drunk enough to talk, argued cheerfully about whose turn it was to supervise the night watch at the records office. "Map room duty," one said, with the bitterness of a man assigned to guard paper while others guarded ammunition. "Four hours, rotation at midnight and four." Bram poured for both and memorized the schedule without appearing to listen, which was a skill his mother had called rudeness and his profession called survival.

The second thing: the ledger survived. This he confirmed obliquely, by letting a clerk's assistant boast. The young man had been showing off about his access — "I've catalogued half the confiscated documentation myself, crates and crates, all marked for disposal" — and when Bram asked mildly whether disposal meant shipping or burning, the boy shrugged with the particular carelessness of someone repeating a phrase until it stopped meaning anything. "Whatever the captain orders. Most of it burns. There's one crate he keeps under key, though. Thinks I don't know which one it is."

"And which one is it?"

"Wouldn't you like to know," the boy said, pleased, and Bram laughed with him and topped up his glass and knew, with the cold settling certainty of a man reading water before a storm, exactly which crate it was.

The third thing he learned from listening to the captain himself.

Captain Erhard Vossel — no relation, thank God — was a heavy man with careful hands and the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it. He gave a short speech about order, about the necessity of administration continuing even as the republic collapsed, about how "the peace, when it comes, will reward those who kept records honestly." Polite applause. Bram applauded too, watching the man's hands, which were remarkable hands: pale, precise, the hands of a lifelong clerk promoted past clerking into the power to decide what paper lived and died.

After the speech, Bram was near the head table refilling glasses when a colonel from further up the coast leaned in and asked, quietly, the question that changed the shape of everything.

"When do you expect to sign? My orders assume the armistice holds by month's end."

Vossel sipped his wine. He did not look at the colonel; he looked at the tablecloth, smoothing an invisible wrinkle with two fingers.

"Your orders assume wrong," he said. "The signing is not a date, Colonel, it is a list. And the list is not finished."

"Not finished?"

"There are persons," Vossel said, "whose status requires resolution before any amnesty can be drafted. Irregulars. Residents of enemy origin. Collaborators of either persuasion. The armistice text provides for exclusion categories. My task is to ensure those categories are populated correctly before the ink dries." He set down the glass. "You cannot amnesty a name that has ceased to exist. It would be like pardoning a man who was never born. Legally incoherent. Emotionally, of course—" the faintest shrug— "no one objects, because there is no one to object."

The colonel laughed uncertainly. Vossel did not.

Bram stood very still with the bottle in his hand and felt the evening tilt.

He had gone into this assuming theft. That was his frame: governments hoarded, so you stole from governments. He had assumed the ledger was collateral — paperwork caught in the machine, valuable because names were valuable, recoverable if you were quick. He had budgeted for bureaucracy, for the grinding slowness of armies, for the possibility that the armistice might beat them to the rescue.

He had not budgeted for intent.

*Erasure,* he thought. *Not delay. Not sloth. Design.*

The armistice wasn't coming late. It was coming *after* — after the lists were drafted from ledgers in army hands, after the excluded categories were filled, after every name in a certain crate had been converted from person to absence. Anselm had said it weeks ago around the driftwood fire, in his schoolteacher's careful way: *if your name isn't on paper, you don't exist.* Bram had nodded along thinking it was metaphor. It was procedure. It was a work order with a deadline and a man named Vossel assigned to meet it.

Two hundred names. Ilse Marchetti among them, declared enemy by poster. Zephyrine Sterling among them, though Zephyrine didn't know it yet — or wouldn't say it, which amounted to the same weight carried differently. And the clock wasn't the signing. The clock was whatever internal date Vossel had set for resolving his categories. Burning day.

Bram poured wine for a naval attaché and smiled at the attaché's joke and heard none of it. His hands were steady. Eleven years of lying to customs officers had taught him that steadiness was not the absence of fear but the decision to postpone it.

* * *

He worked another hour because leaving early was the only mistake a good liar ever made. He let the boy clerk show him the cellar — ostensibly to discuss bulk pricing on confiscated Rhine stock, actually to walk the corridor that connected, under the street, to the customs house rear court. Twenty-two paces, low ceiling, one lamp, a door with a new lock and no window. The dockhand's sketch had shown a similar passage. Now Bram had paced it himself, drunk, essentially, in the company of his own jailer-to-be.

At midnight exactly, per the lieutenants' schedule, he saw the map room rotation cross the garden between buildings — two soldiers, lanterns, bored gait. Bored was good. Bored guards were guards who had been told nothing about what they guarded, and men told nothing rarely improvised.

He collected his case, thanked the steward, and walked out the service door with the same unhurried gait he'd entered, pausing only to accept a compliment on the '36 from a major he had personally advised against it.

Outside, the rain had started again, thin and sideways, the kind that found the gap between collar and neck and stayed there. Bram walked three streets normally, then cut down through the ropewalk, then over the tidal wall into the marsh paths where normal men did not go and hunted men were invisible. Only when the town's lights were behind him and the mud was over his shoe-tops did he let himself feel it — the cold clarity arriving all at once, like stepping off a ledge into water you'd assumed was shallow.

They weren't fighting a delay. They were fighting a plan already in motion, signed off in triplicate, administered by a careful-handed man who drank good wine and slept well.

* * *

The chapel was dark except for the banked fire. Anselm was awake — Anselm was always awake at hours like this, sitting with a book he wasn't reading, keeping vigil over sleepers the way he'd once kept vigil over classrooms. He looked up when Bram came in dripping.

"You're back."

"I'm back."

Odile emerged from the side alcove where she'd been sleeping, hair loose, eyes immediately sharp. Ilse came down from the gallery with a lantern, and behind her, Zephyrine, coat half-buttoned, awake in the instant way they had — no grogginess, just immediate total attention. Bram had seen soldiers with less useful reflexes.

"Well?" Zephyrine said.

Bram set his case down, took the stool by the fire, and decided — deliberately, the way he decided everything — to give it to them straight. No softening. Softening cost time, and time had just become the scarcest commodity on the coast.

"The ledger lives. Locked in the map room of the customs house, one crate under the captain's personal key, guarded by rotation — midnight and four o'clock, two men, bored out of their minds. There's a cellar corridor connecting the Exchange to the rear court; I walked it. Twenty-two paces. One lock on the far door. Odile, your passes will get us into the banquet side again if we need a second entry."

Odile was already nodding, already filing it. "And the bad news. You're saving it for last, so it must be large."

"It is," Bram said. "The armistice isn't a rescue. It's the executioner."

Silence. The fire popped. Ilse's lantern swung slightly and stilled.

"Vossel — the captain — told a colonel tonight, thinking I was furniture: the signing waits on the lists. The lists are drawn from the ledgers he holds. Exclusion categories. Irregulars, enemy residents, collaborators. Everyone in our book qualifies twice over. They intend to finish the categories before the treaty is signed, so that when peace comes, it comes for people who legally exist. The rest—" he spread his hands— "were never born. You can't amnesty a ghost. His words."

Zephyrine sat down slowly on the pew bench, and Bram watched them do the arithmetic he'd done on the marsh path — two hundred names, minus whatever days remained, minus a heist that hadn't happened yet against a garrison that hadn't flinched.

"He said burning day?" Odile asked. "A date?"

"No date. Which means it's soon. Men like that don't hold unfinished work open out of laziness. If he has the crate catalogued and keyed, the only thing left is the disposal order, and disposal orders travel with the paymaster's cart." Bram looked around the fire at each of them — Anselm calm, Odile computing, Ilse white-lipped and upright, Zephyrine with their hands folded tight enough to whiten the knuckles. "We planned this like thieves. Steal the book, row away, wait for the world to make sense again. The world isn't going to make sense again. Whatever we carry out of that building, we carry alone — no amnesty behind us, no law ahead of us. Just us and two hundred names and however many days Vossel hasn't gotten around to yet."

Anselm spoke into the quiet, gently, the way he delivered all the worst news. "Then we stop planning for afterward."

"Yes," Bram said.

Ilse set the lantern down. When she spoke her voice was level and hard, lighthouse steel. "Then how fast can we go?"

Bram looked at Zephyrine. Zephyrine looked back, and something passed between them that had been passing between them since the salt road — the wordless negotiation of two people who trusted each other precisely because neither lied well to the other.

"As fast as fog," Bram said. "Which, if the weather holds, is tomorrow night."

Outside, the tide turned against the breakwater with a sound like a door closing.
