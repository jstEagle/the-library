## 15. What We Owe the Dead

The fire was built of driftwood because everything else in the cove had been requisitioned, burned, or carried away by people with better sense. It hissed as it burned — the salt still in it, weeping out of old grain — and the light it threw was unsteady and orange and made everyone at its edge look like a portrait someone had started and abandoned.

They had come down after dark, single file along the gully path: Bram first, counting his own footsteps out of habit; Odile wrapped in Anselm's coat, her hands too cold to feel the rocks she gripped; Ilse with her hood up and her chin down, moving like a woman who had learned that being seen was a wound waiting to happen. Zephyrine came last. They always came last now. It was not strategy. It was that they could not bear to walk ahead of any of them.

Anselm had gone back to the chapel before sundown to keep the deserters settled and the young ones from doing anything brave. So it was five around the fire — five and the ledger.

The ledger lay between Zephyrine's boots in its oilcloth, looking less than it was. A bundle. A brick of paper and string. Two hundred lives flattened into columns and clerk's hands. It had cost them a fire, a gate, Bram's freedom, three days of Anselm's blood, and it sat there in the salt-light like any other thing the sea might wash over and not notice.

Odile said what they were all thinking, because Odile had never once in her life left a thought unwritten or unsaid.

"We should read them again tonight. All the names. While we have the book in front of us."

"Every night?" Bram asked. He was turning a flat stone in his fingers, skipping it against nothing. "You'll wear the pages soft as cloth."

"Good," Odile said. "Let them be worn soft. Let my thumb know every page by its thickness."

Ilse spoke from inside her hood, and her voice came out lower than the fire's hiss. "And if the reading is interrupted? If soldiers come down the gully while you're at page forty of two hundred? What do the hundred and sixty behind you do then?"

Nobody answered. The tide worked at the shingle below, patient, indifferent, keeping its own ledger of stones taken and stones returned.

Zephyrine looked at Ilse. In the last week she had stopped flinching at her name spoken aloud, which was progress measured in the smallest coin available. She had also begun to argue, which Zephyrine recognized, with something like terror, as recovery. A person who argues believes there is a future worth winning the argument for.

"Say it plainly," Zephyrine said. "Whatever it is you've been circling since we came down the hill."

Ilse pushed the hood back. The firelight found her face and did not soften it.

"I say the book is already lost," she said. "I say we are sitting here guarding a corpse and calling it duty. Every hour that ledger sits on this beach is an hour the captain spends hunting it, and he hunts it because he knows where it is and we only pretend we don't. You want to save two hundred names? Stop carrying paper. Carry them." She touched her own temple, one finger, hard, like pressing a bruise. "In here. Where no crate can be sealed around them and no fire eats faster than a woman who has decided to remember."

"The half-copy," Odile said quietly. "Your father's hand."

"My father's hand, half the names, and his wrists chained to a prison cart for the trouble. Yes. That's my evidence that paper protects no one." Ilse's voice cracked and she let it, let the crack show, which was its own kind of argument. "Paper got my father taken. Paper made me an enemy by printing me so. And now you hold the same paper and propose to defend it the way you'd defend a fortification — with your bodies, in a fixed position, waiting for them to come."

"It isn't paper we're defending," Zephyrine said. "It's what the paper proves. That the republic promised. Signed, stamped, promised. If we burn it or scatter it, the promise becomes rumor, and rumors can be denied."

"By whom?" Ilse turned on them, knees drawing up, the whole of her attention like a lamp turned to full wick. "By the government that no longer answers? By the army that wrote *irregular persons* into the margins? Zephyrine — who is left to lie to? The promise isn't true because ink says so. It's true because you made it. To me. On a summer night when none of this had happened yet, when the war was a thing in newspapers, you swore—" Her voice caught on the memory and she rode through it. "You didn't sign anything. There was no stamp. There was a lighthouse rail and your ridiculous coat and your word. And I have believed that word through a winter that should have killed my faith in everything human. Don't tell me promises need paper to be real. I am the living proof they don't."

The fire spat. Somewhere out on the water a buoy bell rang once, twice, and quit, as if embarrassed.

Bram set his stone down. "She's right about the risk," he said, in the tone of a man conceding territory he intended to take back later. "But she's wrong about the remedy. Memory without witnesses dies with the rememberer. Odile gets interrogated, Ilse gets shipped, and where are the names? Gone with them. The book survives its carriers. That's the whole point of books. My family ran contraband four generations; the ones who lasted weren't the clever ones or the strong ones. They were the redundant ones. Everything important existed twice."

"Three times now," Odile said. "The book. My head. Her father's half."

"And the captain knows all three exist," Ilse said. "Which brings me to the question nobody will ask, so I'll ask it." She looked directly at Zephyrine, and there it was — the thing she had been carrying up the hill and down the gully, set on the sand between them all. "Is it worth it? One promise — mine — weighed against two hundred names? Because that's the arithmetic underneath all this talk. If the ledger moves, we all move, and moving is how Bram was taken. If the ledger burns quietly, some names die on paper but the people might live. You could put me in a boat tomorrow. Neutral water. Done. Promise kept, two hundred risks unfired."

"No," Zephyrine said.

It came out faster than thought, before arithmetic could dress itself up as wisdom. Zephyrine heard their own voice and did not recognize the certainty in it, and then, listening to the silence after, understood that they had always known this answer and had simply never been asked the question so nakedly.

"No," Zephyrine said again, slower. "Because that's not how the promise was made. I didn't swear to deliver you and abandon the rest. I didn't even know the rest existed then. But the oath wasn't conditional on the count." They picked up the ledger. It was heavier than paper had any right to be, or perhaps their arms were simply tired of pretending otherwise. "Do you know what I did at checkpoints, all those months? I weighed everything. Codes sewn in my lining — what does it cost if found? A letter from a dead friend — burn it, too heavy. I got so good at weighing that I stopped noticing I was doing it. I weighed people too. Ilse Marchetti, daughter of the lighthouse, what would it cost me? And I paid it gladly, but I paid it like a *price*, like cargo, and I told myself that was love, carrying you like freight through my days."

The fire popped. Nobody moved. Even the tide seemed to lean in.

"I'm done weighing," Zephyrine said. "Here is what I actually owe, stated plainly, since we're trading plain speech tonight. I love you. Not as payment on a debt. Not as guilt wearing a kinder coat. I love you the way the light loves the water it crosses — stupidly, constantly, without asking what the crossing costs. And I love — " their voice did something rough and unexpected — "I love this. Odile reciting psalms with names hidden in them like seeds in bread. Bram forging passes badly on purpose so the forger they check first is cleared. Anselm bleeding for three days to buy us time he'll never admit he bought. I have spent a year being a courier, carrying things between powers that didn't deserve delivery. This — all of this, every reckless one of you — is the first thing I've ever carried that I chose."

Ilse was very still. Then she laughed — one short, disbelieving breath of it, half sob — and wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears for arriving without permission.

"That's a terrible argument for military strategy," she said.

"It's not a military argument."

"No," she agreed softly. "It isn't."

Bram cleared his throat with the delicacy of a man who had watched smugglers negotiate and knew when to look at the horizon. "So we're agreed, more or less. Book stays whole, moves soon, all of us with it." He stood, brushing sand from his knees. "Then hear my one condition. We don't go out the harbor mouth. They'll expect the boat from the town — it's the only deep-water exit and they know we know it. There's a channel past the old oyster beds, dries at half-tide, runs four feet at flood. Narrower than sin and twice as dark."

"You've run it?" Odile asked.

"Once. Drunk, at nineteen, with a cargo of untaxed gin and a girl whose name I've honorably forgotten." He grinned, and the grin had teeth in it, hope with teeth, the kind Zephyrine was learning to trust more than the gentle kind. "We go at the next dark flood. Three nights from now, if the fog comes with it. And it will. Smell the air. The sea's brewing something."

He looked at each of them in turn, and when his eyes reached Zephyrine they stopped, and the humor drained out of them, leaving something older and sadder.

"One more thing. Before we load anything, before passes, before fog — someone has to cut the harbor lights, and someone has to make sure the gate watch stays drunk, and neither job can be done by anyone the captain has posters for." He held up a hand before protest. "I know my value. Forger, guide, wine merchant, charmer. But I'm also the only face in this crew the patrols *don't* have memorized. Bram Voss has no poster. Bram Voss is, in the eyes of this republic, an honest tradesman of untaxed goods."

"Bram—" Odile began.

"Three nights," he said over her, already turning toward the gully path, gathering his coat. "Spend them well. Sleep, eat, learn a few more names by heart — the lady clerk insists and she's right." At the edge of the firelight he paused, silhouetted, and his voice came back lighter than his shadow looked. "And Zeph — when this is done and we're all somewhere taxes mean nothing — remind me to tell you about the girl and the gin. It's a better story than it deserves to be."

Then he was up the path and gone, and the fire hissed on without him.

* * *

They did not sleep so much as fall toward sleep in pieces, Odile first, curled against a dune grass hummock with her lips still moving through the psalm-rhythms, tending her flock of names even in the dark behind her eyes.

Zephyrine stayed awake longest. Ilse lay close, not touching, her shoulder a hand's width from theirs, which was closer than she had allowed in weeks.

"In peacetime," she said to the stars, "you used to talk about what you'd do when the war was over. You'd forgotten you talked about it, I think. A house with a bad roof you'd fix yourself. Learning to sail badly."

"I remember."

"Liar. But it's a kind lie." She rolled onto her side, facing them, and the fire had burned down to a red heart of coals that lit her from below like something rising. "Ask me what I'll do."

"What will you do?"

"Keep a lighthouse with the light working. Read names once a year whether anyone needs them read or not. And be loved by someone who stopped weighing things." Her hand crossed the hand's width of dark and found theirs and closed around it, cold fingers, fierce grip. "That last part is a requirement, not a wish. I've amended the terms."

Zephyrine held on and watched the coals breathe and thought about three nights hence: a channel narrower than sin, a fog the sea was already brewing, a gate, a drunk watch, Bram walking alone into lamplight with no poster to protect him except his own lack of one.

Out beyond the bar, the buoy bell began to ring — steadily now, urgently, as though the fog had arrived early to warn them.

Or as though something else had come into the harbor, quiet under cover of dark, and the bell was tolling for it.
