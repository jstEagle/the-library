## 16. Night of Fog

The fog came off the water at dusk like a held breath finally released, and Zephyrine Sterling stood at the mouth of the salt road and thought: *now we find out what everything I've carried is worth.*

They had dressed for it. Bram's forged passes sat in three pockets — one sewn into Zephyrine's coat lining where the diplomatic codes had ridden for months, one tucked in Ilse's boot, one folded into Odile's psalter. Each pass bore a different name, a different pretext, the same careful forgery of a quartermaster's stamp that Bram had bought from a drunk paymaster two winters ago and kept dry against the day it would matter. The ink smelled faintly of vinegar. Zephyrine had checked each stamp twice against the sample, then a third time because dread demanded ritual.

Odile went over the rotation one last time, her voice low and even, the voice of a woman who had spent weeks memorizing names inside psalm rhythms and had learned to make memory into architecture. "The gate changes at ten. Corporal Nadeau drinks; his relief does not. Between ten and the half-hour there is one man on the door, and he faces the harbor because the wind carries sound inland and he has learned to listen the wrong way. The map room window is barred but the shutters behind it are not latched — the captain likes his night air and thinks bars are enough." She closed the psalter. "After eleven they crate the overflow documents. After that I cannot promise what is still loose."

"And if something goes wrong?" Anselm Rey asked, though he knew the answer. He asked anyway, the way schoolteachers do, so the students have to say it aloud.

"Then we are smugglers caught in a requisitioned building with forged passes," Odile said, "and the war will not remember us kindly."

Bram Voss grinned, which was his way of being afraid. "I've been hanged twice in absentia already. It stops meaning anything."

* * *

They split at the fish market, where a week ago trust had bled out onto the cobbles, and Zephyrine felt the place like a bruise even through the fog. Anselm went north with Odile toward the chapel route, carrying the escape boat's signal lamp wrapped in sacking. Bram walked openly through the gate at ten minutes past ten, presented his pass with the bored insolence of a wine merchant who had been delayed by weather, and was waved through by a corporal who wanted his drink more than his duty. That much went to plan.

Zephyrine and Ilse went the long way, along the seawall, where the fog was thickest and the stones were slick with spray. Neither spoke. There had been a time — weeks ago now, a lifetime — when silence between them had been comfortable, salt-scented, easy as tide. Now every silence had to be spent carefully, budgeted like coin. Zephyrine measured their steps to match hers and thought about all the things a person can carry: codes, ledgers, guilt, love. Weight was weight. The body did not distinguish.

At the base of the lighthouse Ilse stopped. "The harbor lights," she said. "You're sure it matters?"

"Odile says the patrol boat reads the mole by the beacon's reflection. If the light dies, they slow. They'll assume the mechanism, come ashore to complain, and lose twenty minutes shouting at a keeper who isn't here."

"My father built that mechanism," Ilse said quietly. "Cutting it feels like helping them break something of his."

"Your father crated half a ledger to save two hundred lives," Zephyrine said. "He'd understand breaking his own clock."

She looked at Zephyrine then — a long look through the fog, measuring, the same look she'd given them in the lamp room weeks ago when she hadn't known whether to strike them or hold them — and whatever she found, she nodded once and went up the spiral stair.

Zephyrine waited below in the dark, counting. At forty seconds the reflected glow on the low cloud above the harbor shuddered, dimmed, and died. The lighthouse went dark for the first time in Ilse Marchetti's living memory, and the whole harbor seemed to hold itself differently — smaller, blind, listening.

Twenty minutes. Maybe twenty-five if the fog argued for them.

Zephyrine ran.

* * *

The customs house rose out of the fog like a ship aground, its windows shuttered, army lanterns burning greenish at the door. Bram was already inside — that had been the plan, Bram with his second pass and a crate of plausible wine to deliver to the officers' mess, stalling the duty clerk with talk of vintages he could not possibly possess. Zephyrine came around the east face where the captain's night air leaked through the unlatched shutters behind the bars, and found, as promised, a gap wide enough for a coat and the person inside it.

The map room smelled of tallow and wet wool. Charts of the coastline covered the long table, annotated in a hand too confident for its content — troop positions that were guesses drawn like facts. And against the far wall, beneath the boarded window—

Crates.

Six of them, plank and batten, roped for transport. Stenciled on the nearest, in the army's blunt capitals: **PAPER WASTE — INCINERATION, MORNING ORDER**.

Zephyrine's hands were on the first crate before their mind caught up, fingers finding the battens, prying at a nail. *It's fine,* they told themselves, in the bright lying voice people use in the dark. *The ledger is in another room. Odile's note said the map room. Notes can be wrong. Plans can be wrong. Everything can be wrong.*

The nail gave. The lid tilted.

Inside, packed in straw like something precious: paper. Reams of it, requisition forms, blank amnesty schedules, and on top — face down, but unmistakable even from the reverse, even by its weight, even by the way Zephyrine's body recognized it before their eyes did — a ledger bound in gray board with a customs stamp burned into the cover.

Two hundred names. Crated like waste. Timed for fire.

Zephyrine did not let themselves think. Thinking was for later. They got both arms around the ledger and lifted it out of the straw, and it was heavier than any code, heavier than the coat, heavier than guilt — and lighter than it should have been, the way important things always are, as if importance weighs nothing and only indifference has mass.

Behind them, in the doorway, a floorboard announced Bram Voss.

"You're early," he breathed. "Or the army's late. Either way—" He saw the open crate, the ledger, the stencil on its neighbor, and his grin died the death it had been avoiding all evening. "Morning order. They weren't going to wait for the armistice at all."

"No," Zephyrine said. "Neither are we. Help me carry."

* * *

Ilse reached the customs house wall at four minutes past the quarter hour, breath ragged from the stair and the run, and pressed herself into the shadow between the buttresses where the fog pooled deepest. Her job was the simplest and worst one: watch the mole, count the patrol boat's lantern when it moved, and if it turned toward shore, make a sound — any sound, gull or whistle or scream — loud enough to buy seconds she would probably not survive spending.

Through the fog she heard the patrol boat's engine idling beyond the mole, confused, its crew cursing the dark. She counted heartbeats instead of minutes. Somewhere above her, in a building full of men with rifles, Zephyrine was lifting two hundred lives out of a box marked for burning, and Ilse Marchetti stood in the cold doing nothing, and *nothing* was the hardest work she had ever done.

Her father had taught her that the light was a promise — that somewhere out on the water someone steered by what you kept burning. For months she had hated the republic that wrote her name in a ledger and lost it. But standing in the fog keeping her small vigil, she understood suddenly, completely, that promises don't belong to governments. They belong to whoever shows up in the dark to keep them.

A boot scraped on stone. She flattened herself against the wall as a sentry rounded the corner, fifteen feet away, tobacco smoke curling through the lamplight. He stopped. Looked out at the black harbor. Spat.

"The whole coast's gone blind," he muttered to nobody, and walked on.

Ilse let her breath out slowly enough to keep from shaking.

* * *

In the corridor outside the map room, Odile Ferrand walked past two soldiers with a psalter in her hands and a pass in its pages, and her heart performed arithmetic. Ten past the hour: gate changed. Quarter past: the duty clerk still arguing vintages with Bram. Twenty past: the incineration detail was not due until morning, which meant the crates were unguarded, which meant the only thing standing between the ledger and the sea was sixty feet of corridor, one staircase, and luck.

She had counted worse odds from a cell. In that cell she had memorized page after page while guards joked about fires they would light later, folding names into psalm rhythms the way you fold medicine into bread. *Though I walk through the valley — Aldous Bem, ferryman, walks with me. Yea, though I stand in the shadow — Margit Ossler and child, in the shadow of the third door.* Memory was the one archive no captain could requisition. Tonight the paper and the memory would finally agree with each other.

A door opened ahead. An officer stepped out, young, tired — the one from the interrogations, the one whose certainty had cracked a little more each day under Anselm's patient misdirection. Odile did not slow. She raised the psalter slightly, as a woman carrying devotions does, and looked at him the way the innocent look at the armed: openly, apologetically, boringly.

He looked back. His eyes went to the psalter, to her face, to the corridor behind her where — she would learn later — a door stood very slightly ajar, and through the gap, the smell of straw.

He looked at her for three full seconds.

"Late for prayers," he said, and stepped aside.

Odile walked on, and did not begin shaking until the staircase.

* * *

The window was the problem. It had been the way in; it refused to be the way out. The ledger was too wide for the bars' gap, and the fog outside held Ilse's post and the mole and the sea, and behind them in the corridor boots were beginning to move with purpose — the changing of some guard, the discovery of some small wrongness, impossible to tell.

"Rope," Bram said. "There's always rope in a customs house."

There was. Cargo line from a hook by the charts, and Zephyrine made the loop around the window bar with hands that had sewn codes into linings and could tie knots blind, and lowered the ledger down the wall into Ilse's waiting arms — watched it go, two hundred names swinging gently over thirty feet of fog, and felt the satchel-weight leave their shoulders and enter someone else's keeping.

Then the corridor door opened fully, and the young officer stood in it with a lantern, alone.

For a moment nobody in the room moved, and the fog pressed against the window, and the patrol boat's engine coughed somewhere out on the blind water. The officer looked at Zephyrine. At Bram, rope in hand, calculating odds no honest man would take. At the empty straw in the crate, and the stencil on its side, and the morning order that would light a fire under two hundred people who did not officially exist.

Zephyrine watched him weigh it. Watched the crack Anselm had opened in him widen or seal — there was no telling which, only the length of the second, which went on and on.

The officer turned the lantern down, as if the fog needed less light to be believed.

"There's a rat in the storeroom," he said loudly, to no one, and turned his back, and walked away down the corridor, and his boots said he was not hurrying.

* * *

They went over the seawall with the ledger slung in sacking between them, four people and one book moving through fog so thick the town ceased to exist beyond arm's reach. Behind them an alarm finally woke — bells, whistles, the beautiful useless noise of authority discovering itself robbed — but by then the fish market was behind them and the driftwood fire ahead, where Anselm stood with the signal lamp already lit, and the boat, and the black water that did not care what any government had written.

Zephyrine set the ledger in the hull and kept one hand flat on its cover, breathing, alive, unable yet to believe it.

"It's heavier than I imagined," Ilse said beside them, her hair wet with fog.

"That's not the ledger," Zephyrine said. "That's everyone in it."

Down the breakwater, torchlight burst from the harbor gate — too many torches, moving too fast, and among the shouts a single voice that Zephyrine knew, raised not in fear but in defiance, holding a gate that needed holding.

Bram was not in the boat.

Bram was back there, and the gate was closing, and the tide would not wait.
