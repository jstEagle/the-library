## 17. Fire in the Map Room

Lieutenant Émile Duret had been awake for thirty-one hours when the fog came in off the water like something with intent.

He stood at the window of the adjutant's office above the customs house, watching it swallow the harbor one mast at a time. The requisitioned building smelled of other men's tobacco and old paper, and somewhere below him Captain Roche-Vasseur was sleeping off a banquet that had run past midnight — a banquet Émile had been permitted to attend only long enough to pour wine and be seen standing at attention while merchants and quartermasters toasted an armistice everyone knew was being written to exclude half the town.

The ledger sat two floors down, in the map room, in a pine crate stenciled BURN — PRIORITY.

Émile had read the stencil four times now. Each time it looked less like an order and more like a confession.

* * *

They came through the coal cellar because Odile Ferrand's note had said they would come through the coal cellar, and because Bram Voss's forged passes got them past the gate sentries who were, by the third hour of fog, more interested in their brazier than their rifles.

Zephyrine went first, coat damp with coal dust, satchel empty for once — nothing carried tonight but the promise of carrying. Behind them: Ilse Marchetti, who knew the harbor's wiring better than the harbor's own engineers and had spent forty minutes in the dark cutting the right ones so that the customs house would sit in its own private night. Behind her, Anselm Rey, three days out of an interrogation room, moving on a bruised rib and a will that had calcified into something harder than bone.

Odile was not with them. Odile was still in her cell, which was exactly where she needed to be, because Odile had memorized the guard rotations the way other people memorized psalms, and every hour of this night had been built from what she'd carried out of interrogation in her head.

The cellar stairs. The ground floor corridor. The smell of lamp oil already laid, thin and patient, along the baseboards of the east wing — Bram's work, done an hour ago under the guise of a wine merchant's delivery, casks of "cognac" that were nine parts spirits and one part accelerant.

The map room door was oak and locked, but locks had never been the plan. The plan was simpler and worse: the door was going to stop mattering entirely.

* * *

Émile heard the first shout from the east wing at ten minutes past three.

He was moving before he'd decided to move, boots on the stairs, taking the corridor at a run, and the scene assembled itself around him in pieces: smoke already threading from beneath the east-wing doors, soldiers coughing in the haze, Sergeant Malraux screaming about buckets, and through the tall windows of the map room — the map room, where the crate was — a light that did not belong.

A lantern. Moving inside the map room.

He put his shoulder to the door and it gave, and for one full second Émile Duret stood in the doorway of the most important room in the occupied town and saw everything he was supposed to see.

Four figures. A crate open on the captain's table, its contents — books, ledgers, bundles of paper tied with government twine — being lifted out by hands that moved fast and without ceremony. A woman at the window doing something to the frame with a pry bar. A big man with a smuggler's shoulders holding the corridor side. And in the center, lifting a ledger with both arms like it weighed more than paper, a figure Émile recognized from a fish market raid, from a wanted circular, from three days of listening to a beaten schoolteacher refuse to say their name.

Zephyrine Sterling.

"Stop," Émile said, and his voice cracked on it, and he drew his pistol because his hand made the decision his mouth couldn't finish.

Sterling turned. Looked at him. And did not run.

That was the thing Émile would remember afterward, in the years when he let himself remember it at all. Not the fire, not the smoke, not the woman prying the window open onto a drop into the alley. The looking. Sterling held the ledger against their chest and looked at him the way you look at someone across a table, waiting for them to say the true thing instead of the rehearsed one.

"The armistice list is drafted from these pages," Sterling said. Smoke was thickening behind them; the diversionary fire in the east wing was doing its work, eating the corridor, buying minutes. "Two hundred names. You've seen the exclusion order. *Irregular persons.* You know what happens to the book."

"I know my orders," Émile said.

"Yes," Sterling said. "I'm asking if you know anything else."

From the corridor: Malraux, closer now, coughing, shouting for the lieutenant. Thirty seconds, maybe less. The fire was realer than anyone had planned — Bram's oil had caught faster than oil should, and the east wing was beginning to make the sound fire makes when it stops being a tool and becomes a participant.

Ilse Marchetti swung out the window. Anselm Rey followed, slower, favoring the rib, and Émile watched him go and thought: *he could have given me Sterling's name. He had three days and a pair of pliers and he gave me a fictional fish merchant instead.* He thought: *why.*

Bram Voss backed toward the window last, and as he passed Émile's line of sight he said, quietly, almost kindly: "Your captain signed the burn order before the armistice was even signed, Lieutenant. Ask yourself whose names are in that crate. Then ask why nobody wants them read."

Then the smuggler was gone too, dropping into the dark, and Zephyrine Sterling stood alone in the burning map room holding two hundred lives against their chest, and the pistol in Émile's hand weighed what it had always weighed and meant something it had never meant before.

"Sterling," Émile said.

"Yes."

"Run."

* * *

Later — much later, in the version of the night he would write down and the version he wouldn't — Émile could never fully reconstruct the moment itself. He knew the facts. He knew that Malraux burst into the corridor shouting *the map room, the map room*, and that Émile turned toward him, raised his voice over the fire, and said: *East wing's collapsing — the map room's lost, get everyone to the west stair, we save the building or we lose all of it.* He knew that Malraux, who trusted him, who had shared a bottle with him eleven days ago, believed him without question and hauled two soldiers back down the corridor away from the truth.

And he knew that in the four seconds between Malraux's arrival and Malraux's retreat, Émile Duret stood in a doorway full of smoke with a drawn pistol and a clear shot and chose, deliberately, consciously, with the whole of his attention — to look at the floor.

Not away. That was the distinction he clung to, the small dishonest comfort of it. Away implied cowardice. He had looked *down*. At the boards. At the grain of the wood, at the scuff of his own boot, at anything that was not the window where a figure with a ledger dropped into the fog and vanished.

Anselm Rey had sat in a chair for three days with his face swelling and his ribs cracking and had lied beautifully, generously, spending his own blood to buy strangers time — and when they'd finally dragged him back to his cell, bleeding, he had stopped at the door and looked at Émile and said, not bitterly, not pleadingly, just as a fact offered to another adult: *"You're young enough to have a choice left, Lieutenant. Most men your age in uniform don't."*

Émile had hated him for it. He understood now that he had hated him because it was true.

The floorboards. The smoke. The sound of boots hitting cobblestones in the alley and then not hitting them, swallowed by fog. Somewhere below, the harbor bell began to ring — Ilse Marchetti's work, or the fire's, no way to know — and the whole town woke into confusion, and confusion was a country that sheltered fugitives better than any border ever had.

When Malraux came back at dawn, gray-faced, reporting the east wing gutted and the map room's contents destroyed, Émile wrote the report himself. He used the word *total*. He used the phrase *no documents recoverable*. His handwriting was steady. He noticed that it was steady and found that steadiness more frightening than trembling would have been.

Captain Roche-Vasseur raged for an hour, then went quiet in a way that was worse, staring at the blackened doorway and saying, mostly to himself: "Someone knew. Someone always knows." His eyes passed over Émile and moved on. Passed over. Moved on.

Émile stood at attention and let himself be invisible, and understood that he had just crossed a line that ran through the middle of his own chest, and that there was no report he could file about it, no superior he could confess to, no order that could un-order him. He had chosen. The choice was now a fact of the world, like weather.

He thought of the schoolteacher in his cell, offering him the future like a coin. He thought of the stencil on the crate. *Burn — priority.* He thought: *they burned people the same way, once. Priority by priority. And called it administration.*

Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor, and in the lifting fog, far out past the breakwater, he could just make out a small boat running dark toward open water.

He did not raise the alarm.

* * *

The smoke followed them all the way down the cliff path, a gray tide at their backs, and by the time Zephyrine's boots hit shingle the ledger was soaked in harbor spray and hugged so hard against their ribs it would leave bruises shaped like covers and spine.

Bram was laughing — the low, disbelieving laugh of a man who has cheated something large. "It worked. God help me, it actually worked."

"It worked because a boy with a gun decided to lose," Anselm said. He was bent double on the rocks, breathing in careful fractions. "Don't mistake it for luck. Luck doesn't choose."

Ilse said nothing. She was looking back at the burning customs house, at the fire she had helped feed, and her face in its light was unreadable — grief and triumph sharing the same small space, neither willing to yield.

Zephyrine opened the ledger. Water-spotted, smoke-stained, every page accounted for. Two hundred names. Ilse's name. Their own.

"We're not done," Zephyrine said. "This is half the promise. The boat's the other half."

Behind them, up the hill, the customs house burned on, and somewhere inside it a young lieutenant was writing the word *total* in a steady hand, and the fog was coming apart over the breakwater — where, though none of them could see it yet, a gate stood unguarded except by one man who had stayed behind to hold it, and soldiers were already moving toward him through the smoke.

The boat waited at the pier. The tide was turning. There was no time left to be anything but fast.

"*Go,*" Bram said — and pushed them toward the water, and turned back toward the gate.
