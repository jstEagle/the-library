## 18. The Long Pier

The ledger was heavier than a book had any right to be. Zephyrine felt it against their chest like a second heartbeat, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped under Bram's stolen coat — *Bram's* coat, which he'd pushed onto them at the gate with a snarl and no explanation, and they understood now what the explanation had been. A coat could be searched. A man holding a gate could not run.

They ran anyway. All of them ran, and Bram did not.

"Left of the winch house," Ilse said ahead of them, her voice flat with the particular calm she found inside fear, the calm Zephyrine had learned to love like weather. "There's a gap in the breakwater rail where we used to slip through as children. Thirty steps past the crane."

"How do you know the soldiers haven't found it?"

"Because I found it first, when I was nine, and I never told anyone but you."

Thirty steps past the crane. Zephyrine counted them without meaning to, the way they had once counted everything that could be carried, everything that could be lost: one ledger, two hundred names, three people on a pier built for forty, and behind them, faint under the fog and the smoke still climbing from the customs house, the sound of the gate.

It came as wood splintering. Then voices — orders, clipped, official, the language of requisition — and then something that was none of those things. Bram's voice, carrying across the water the way it always did when he wanted to be heard by God or a customs officer, whichever came first.

"You'll want to write this down," Bram was saying, somewhere up the dark slope of the breakwater. "Two men came through here an hour ago. Armed. Headed for the salt road. You're welcome."

*He's sending them wrong.* Zephyrine's legs did what legs do when the mind is elsewhere — they slowed, they turned — and Ilse's hand closed on their sleeve with the grip of someone who had grown up hauling rope in a gale.

"He's buying us the pier," she said. "Don't waste it."

"He's giving them himself."

"Yes," Ilse said. "So run."

* * *

The pier was old and knew it. Each plank announced them. Behind, boots on stone, more than one pair, spreading out along the breakwater's spine like water finding cracks — the misdirection hadn't held, or it had held only long enough to be worse, because now there were soldiers between them and the gate and the only way off the pier was the boat, and the boat was Odile, and Odile was small, and the tide was going out with the indifference of all large slow things.

"The gap," Zephyrine said. "Can you make the mooring line fast?"

"I grew up on this light," Ilse said. "I can tie a bowline blind, drunk, and bleeding. Get the ledger to the boat. That's your whole job tonight. Not the gate. Not me. The names."

Zephyrine dropped through the gap in the rail — thirty steps past the crane, exactly where a nine-year-old had decided history would turn — and hit the lower walkway in a crouch, ankle screaming, oilcloth clutched. Below, black water. Beyond, the tramp of Odile moving on deck, a lantern hooded and unhooded twice: their signal, once, and then dark again, which meant trouble, because twice was *come*, and once-and-dark was *someone else is here.*

Of course someone else was here. The escape route was compromised; Bram had said so with his back turned and his hand already lifting, and now the proof came along the lower rocks — a patrol boat's silhouette, running dark, nosing toward the pier's seaward foot with the patience of something that had done this many times before.

They were caught between the soldiers above and the patrol below, on a walkway wide enough for one grief at a time.

*The names,* Ilse had said. Zephyrine looked at the ledger under their arm and understood, finally, completely, the arithmetic they had been refusing since the customs house: you cannot carry everyone. You can only decide, each time, who you refuse to leave. And every decision spends someone.

Above them, at the head of the pier stairs, a soldier appeared — young, rifle half-raised, the lieutenant from the burning map room, the one who had looked away. His face in the fog-light was the face of a person doing arithmetic of his own, and losing.

"Stop," he said, without force.

Zephyrine stood still. Below, the patrol boat bumped the pilings. Above, the lieutenant's breath made small clouds. Between them, twenty feet of wet planking and two hundred names.

"I was ordered to burn it," the lieutenant said, as if reporting to a superior. "I wrote *total loss*. That's what's in the record. Total loss by fire." He swallowed. "What I'm looking at is not a total loss. So either my report is false, or you don't exist."

"That's how it works now," Zephyrine said. "You invented it. I'm just using it."

Something moved in his face — not agreement, nothing so clean. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing the machine from outside for one second, feeling its teeth.

"Seaward foot," he said abruptly, quietly. "They're boarding from the seaward foot. The harbor-side ladder is unwatched for four minutes while they sweep the pier end. After that I follow my orders again, whatever they are." He turned his face away, toward the smoke. "Go. Be nobody."

Then he walked to the rail and looked out at the water like a man guarding nothing, which was, tonight, the most dangerous thing he could have done.

* * *

Four minutes.

Zephyrine went up the stairs instead of down — Ilse was above, at the mooring — and found her kneeling at the capstan, the painter already in a knot so clean it looked ceremonial. She saw their face and read it whole, the way she read weather.

"The harbor side," Zephyrine said. "Ladder. Four minutes."

"Then we climb down and we don't say goodbye on a pier, because piers are where people get taken." She tied off the last turn, then stopped, both hands on the rope, and looked at the dark slope of the breakwater where the gate was. Where Bram was. The sounds from there had changed — fewer shouts, a strange orderliness, the sound of an arrest conducted by professionals.

"Bram," she said.

"I know."

"He stayed because of the coat. Because someone had to carry the ledger and someone had to look carriable, and he decided which one he'd be before you even argued." Her voice cracked precisely once, in the middle, like ice underfoot. "That vain, stubborn —"

"Yes," Zephyrine said, because there was nothing else that was true enough.

Ilse wiped her face with the back of one roped hand, efficiently, the gesture of a working lighthouse keeper, and pointed at the oilcloth. "That goes in the boat or it goes nowhere. Two hundred people, Zephyrine. He spent himself on two hundred people. Don't make it a bad trade by drowning in him."

The four minutes became three. They went down the harbor ladder together, Ilse first, calling depth to Zephyrine the way her father must have taught her — *two fathoms, careful, barnacle on your left* — and the dinghy that Odile had kept hidden under nets took their weight with a groan of complaint. The ledger went into the bilge boards, wrapped, lashed. Zephyrine's hands did the work while the rest of them stood somewhere up on the breakwater beside a man who was, by then, very probably in irons.

Odile materialized at the tiller out of the dark like a rumor. Her wrists still bore the marks of the cell; her voice bore the marks of psalms.

"Patrol boat is sweeping the pier," she said. "We go now, quiet, along the mole, and pray the fog holds its tongue."

"And Bram?"

Odile was silent a moment. In the silence, the fog delivered, faintly, from the top of the wall, a single sound: not a cry. A laugh. Bram Voss, being taken, laughing at somebody's face.

"He'd want that noted in the minutes," Odile said. "Push off."

* * *

The crossing was short and endless. The neutral steamer lay outside the mole, riding anchor lights it had no legal reason to show, and Ilse sat in the stern facing backward the whole way, watching the town shrink — the dark lighthouse she'd been hiding in for weeks, the burning customs house dying down to embers, the breakwater where a gate hung open and useless and guarded by nothing now, because the man who'd held it was in a wagon heading inland.

At the steamer's side, a mate with a bored lantern checked them over — crates, papers, a wrapped bundle that he was told, in Odile's best customs-clerk baritone, was ship's documents damaged by smoke. He believed it, or chose to. The world was full now of people choosing to believe things.

And then there was nothing left to do but the thing Zephyrine had been dreading since a lamp room, since a promise made in peacetime by a person who did not yet know what peacetime cost.

"This is the half we said," Zephyrine said. "The boat's the other half. It's done. You're on it."

"No," Ilse said.

Zephyrine's heart performed a full catastrophe. "Ilse—"

"I'm not staying on this boat without you, and you're not coming on this boat, because I watched you count on the pier. You counted the way you always count. Ledger, names, tide." She took their face in both hands, salt-rough palms, furious tenderness. "So here is my count. Two hundred names in that wrapping. One name missing from every list anyone ever kept, because no government writes down the people who actually keep things lit. Bram is missing. Anselm is in a cell. And you are standing here about to make me choose between the water and you, which is not a choice, because I have already chosen you, you absolute clerk, and I will be waiting wherever this boat lands until you arrive or until I die of stubbornness, whichever comes first, and I warn you I am *very* well supplied with stubbornness."

The mate coughed. The gangway waited. Somewhere behind them the fog thinned enough to show, for one instant, the whole broken coastline of the republic that had signed its name to nothing it meant.

"Ilse Marchetti," Zephyrine said, "enemy by declaration, I never once declared you."

"I know," she said. "That's why it counts." She kissed them once, hard, like sealing a letter, and stepped back onto the deck of the steamer, and stood at the rail, and did not wave, because waving was for departures that might be undone.

The boat pulled away. Zephyrine sat in the stern beside Odile, the ledger between their feet, and watched the anchor lights shrink into the fog, and let themselves feel, for exactly the length of the pull to shore, the full price of the night: one gate, one man, one love put aboard a ship to safety instead of a life together, and two hundred names saved at a cost that had not finished being counted.

Then the keel touched sand, and Odile stood, and looked inland toward the chapel hills, and said the thing that turned victory into the beginning of something harder:

"The armistice lists post at dawn. If Bram's taken, they'll process him as an irregular by morning. We have one night, one rescued ledger, and a road inland." She pulled her collar up. "Get your counting face on, Sterling. We're going to go steal a prisoner."
