## 20. Neutral Water

The fog came off the water like something that had been waiting for them all along.

Ilse had grown up inside fog. She knew its moods the way other girls knew their mothers' tempers — when it would lift by noon, when it meant to stay for days, when it was only fog and when it was hiding something. This one was the patient kind. It lay against the harbor in a white shroud so thick that the pier's end vanished ten steps out, and she could not see the lighthouse she had lived in her whole life, though she could feel it at her back like a tooth gone quiet after a long ache.

She kept her hand in Zephyrine's anyway.

Not because she needed guiding. She knew these boards better than anyone alive, knew which one sang underfoot and which ones held their peace, knew that the third piling before the ladder had a rusted cleat that caught hems. She walked them all without a sound, past the sentry post where a lantern burned as a blind yellow smear, past the upturned skiff stinking of tar and old herring. She held Zephyrine's hand because once they reached the end of the pier there would be no reason left to hold it, and she wanted to spend the last of the reasons while they were still hers to spend.

Zephyrine walked beside her carrying nothing but a satchel and that terrible upright posture, the one Ilse had learned to read over these weeks of hiding and running and loving against her own judgment. When Zephyrine squared the shoulders like that, it meant the cost had already been counted. It meant the arithmetic was finished and the answer had been paid for in advance, and no argument Ilse could make now would reopen the ledger.

She hated that posture. She loved the person wearing it. Both things were true, and she had stopped trying to make only one of them true.

The steamer was out there somewhere in the murk — the *Meridian Star*, a neutral tramp with a Greek name painted over a German one and a captain who took gold in any currency, including the currency of names. Bram had arranged it from his cell, somehow, through channels Ilse did not ask about, a letter smuggled out in a laundry bundle that ended with instructions and a joke and then, at the bottom, in smaller handwriting: *Get her out. I'll get myself out too, but do her first.* Odile had copied the passage protocols from memory into the margin of a psalm page, and Anselm had blessed the whole enterprise in the flat practical way he blessed things, which sounded less like prayer and more like weather advice: *Fog holds until mid-morning. Go early. Trust the dinghy more than the tide.*

So they went early, and the dinghy waited at the ladder, and the tide was doing exactly what Anselm said it would, and everything was going right, which Ilse knew by now was the surest sign that something enormous was about to go wrong.

They heard the boots at the fish-market end of the pier when they were four steps from the ladder.

* * *

Later — on the water, in the dark, with salt drying tight on her cheeks — Ilse would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and find that memory had taken them apart and rebuilt them out of sequence, the way grief does, the way fear does. What she retained, in pieces:

Zephyrine's hand leaving hers — deliberately, gently, the way you set down something breakable before running.

The satchel passing out of Zephyrine's grip into hers. The weight of it surprising her. Papers, always papers; the whole war came down to what fit in a bag.

Zephyrine stepping backward, away from the ladder, toward the sound of the boots, and turning to say — not whispering anymore, there was no time for whispers — "Down the ladder. Straight out. Count two hundred strokes and the bell will be ringing. If you can't hear the bell, keep counting."

"The bell's been dark for a month," Ilse said. "You told me yourself."

"I know," Zephyrine said, and smiled — that crooked, infuriating smile that had undone her at nineteen across a table covered in nautical charts neither of them fully understood, and undid her now at the end of a pier at the end of a country. "Ring it anyway. In your head. I'll hear it."

And then the boots arrived, three soldiers and a corporal materializing out of the fog like the fog itself deciding to have opinions, and Ilse went down the ladder because staying meant making Zephyrine's sacrifice worthless, and going meant living, and living meant keeping the promise in both directions, and she did not know yet which of those reasons she was obeying. Perhaps all of them. Perhaps none. Her hands found the rungs. The cold took her wrists. The harbor closed over her head like a lid.

From beneath the pier, wedged in the dinghy, oars shipped, drifting with the current she was supposed to be fighting, she heard it happen above her. The challenge called. Zephyrine's voice answering, clear and bored and perfectly pitched to a traveler with forged passes — Bram's passes, good enough to fool a checkpoint, not good enough to fool whatever the corporal had heard or been told or suspected. A scuffle. Not much of one. Zephyrine had never been a fighter; Zephyrine was a carrier, a keeper, a person built entirely out of promises and coat linings, and Ilse listened to the person she loved most in the world being taken apart by four men with clubs and rifles, and she lay flat in the bottom of a boat and did not move, because moving would have drowned two people instead of saving one, and Zephyrine's last audible words from the pier, thrown back over a shoulder as they were marched away, were:

"Two hundred strokes! Count them!"

Ilse counted.

She counted through the sobbing she refused to let become noise. She counted past one hundred, where the fog thickened and the cold found her knees. She counted past one hundred and fifty, where the bell of the *Meridian Star* began, faintly, absurdly, to ring — a real bell, an actual bell, the anchor watch marking the hour in neutral water, and she laughed once, a broken animal sound, and counted the remaining fifty to the rhythm of it.

At two hundred, a rope ladder unrolled down the steamer's black flank, and a sailor with a face like a closed fist said in three languages that she should hurry, and she climbed, and the promise was kept.

That was the word for it. Kept. The way you keep a ledger. The way you keep a grave.

* * *

The captain's cabin smelled of coffee and diesel. He counted the gold Odile had sewn into Ilse's coat hem, weighed it in his palm like a man judging fruit, and grunted acceptance. Then he looked at her properly for the first time and asked the question every authority asks: papers.

"None," she said.

"Then you're cargo," he said, not unkindly. "Cargo doesn't need papers. Cargo needs to stay below until I'm twelve miles out and bored of looking at it." He poured her a coffee anyway, unprompted, and slid it across the chart table, and that small unasked-for mercy nearly undid her more than the pier had.

Through the stern port, as the anchor came up and the fog began — finally, treacherously, beautifully — to shred, she watched the town assemble itself out of the dissolving white. The customs house first, its requisitioned flag limp. Then the roofs, the fish market, the crooked street where Anselm had taught school before either army existed to him. And last, standing apart at the harbor's mouth on its black rock, the lighthouse.

Dark. Still dark. Its lamp room was a dead eye in the graying morning, and she thought of her father in whatever yard they kept him, and of the half-copy of the ledger pages hidden in the lamp housing where no soldier had thought to look because who robs a light? One hundred and seven names, folded into oilcloth, waiting for her to come back for them, and she had come back for them, and carried them out, and now they rode in her coat against her ribs like a second heartbeat while their owners stayed behind in a country that had erased them.

The town slid aft. The lighthouse slid aft. The fog closed behind it, and the sea opened ahead, gray and wide and — this was the thing that broke her, standing alone at the rail with cold coffee she couldn't drink — *empty*. No checkpoints. No proclamations. No names written anywhere declaring what she was. Just water, indifferent and clean, the neutral water she had been promised, delivered exactly as sworn.

She had wanted this her whole life, if she was honest. The horizon had always pulled at her from the lamp room gallery, and she had imagined freedom as distance, as the far shore resolving out of haze. Now she had it, and it tasted like the inside of her own cheek where she'd bitten it on the ladder, blood and salt, and she understood with a clarity that felt like injury that the horizon had never been the point. The point had been who stood beside you looking at it.

Footsteps behind her. The fist-faced sailor, back again.

"You've got someone coming," he said. "Or you haven't. Either way, we sail in twenty minutes, and I'm telling you because the captain said to tell you." He shrugged, embarrassed by the sentence's shape. "He said somebody paid for a berth and never gave a name for it. Second berth. Paid in full, months ago, by a courier with a leather satchel."

The deck tilted under her. Months ago. Before the requisition, before the ledger, before the poster with her face on it and the word ENEMY printed beneath. Back when it was peacetime, technically, back when Zephyrine Sterling crossed borders for a rotting republic and still believed the republic might hold, they had bought two berths on a neutral ship out of a harbor town neither of them could have named yet. Two berths. Paid in full.

Zephyrine had known. All this time — through the marshes, through the chapel, through the driftwood fire where they had finally spoken plainly, the plainest words Ilse had ever received in her life — Zephyrine had been carrying a ticket for a future with two seats in it.

* * *

She went below to the second berth because her legs made the decision and her heart just followed, arguing. It was a narrow coffin of varnished pine with a blanket folded at the foot in neat thirds, military-neat, and tucked into the fold was a piece of paper folded once.

She sat down before she opened it. The engines started somewhere below her, a great slow animal waking, and the hull began to thrum, and she unfolded the paper with fingers that would not stop shaking.

It was a page torn from a notebook. On it, in Zephyrine's small upright hand — a hand she had watched write codes into coat linings and copy dying men's names by lamplight — was a list. Ninety-three names. The other half of the ledger, the half that had burned in her father's haste, reconstructed from memory, from Odile's psalm-rhythms passed along in smuggled notes, from conversations around driftwood fires. Every name accounted for. At the top, in slightly larger letters, as if it were a heading rather than a plea, as if it were simply a fact to be recorded:

*These are also coming with us. All of them.*

And beneath the list, at the very bottom, alone:

*I'm not staying because the names matter more than you. Don't ever believe that. I'm staying because you taught me what a promise weighs, and I have one hundred and ninety-three of them left to carry, and I carry things. It's the only thing I've ever been good at. Come back for me when the water is safe. Or don't come back, and live so well it counts as revenge. Ring the bell anyway.
— Z.*

Ilse read it four times. On the fourth reading she did the thing she had not done at the pier, had not allowed herself below the boards, had held back through the climb and the gold-counting and the departure — she wept, loudly, gracelessly, into the folded blanket that smelled faintly of someone else's soap and someone else's iron discipline, while the harbor fell away beneath the keel and the engines drummed and the second berth, paid in full months ago by a courier with a leather satchel, rocked empty beside her.

Above, through the port, the light changed. She wiped her face and looked out and saw why: the sun had finally beaten the fog, and the whole coast stood revealed in hard honest morning — the town, the breakwater, the black rock, and on the rock the lighthouse, catching fire with dawn like something refusing to stay dead.

And then she saw it happen, and her breath stopped, and she pressed both hands to the glass.

On the gallery of the dark lighthouse, tiny with distance, a figure moved. Small against the sky. Climbing the outside stair to the lamp room — impossible, the lamp room was sealed, she had sealed it herself — unless someone had bribed a guard, unless Odile's protocols ran deeper than she knew, unless a certain carrier had spent the hours between arrest and armistice arranging one last delivery. The figure reached the gallery. Fumbled at the housing. And then the dead eye of the lighthouse blinked, stuttered, and lit.

One rotation. The beam swung out across the water and passed over the steamer, over her, a warm stripe of white falling through the port and across the empty berth and the open letter in her hands, and swept on, and came around again, and again, steady as a heartbeat, burning in broad daylight where no rule required it, saying the only thing it had ever been built to say, which was: *here. Here. You are seen. Here.*

Ninety-three names in her lap. One hundred and seven folded at her ribs. Two hundred strokes counted to a bell that hadn't been ringing, except it always had been, if you listened properly.

The coast fell behind. The beam thinned to a star, then to a rumor, then to nothing but her own memory of it, which was, she understood now, the most reliable light there was.

Ilse Marchetti folded the letter, put it inside her coat against the oilcloth packet, and went up on deck to face the gray open sea and the far shore and the long business of staying alive until she could come back.

Behind her, growing smaller and more stubborn with every swell, the light burned on.
