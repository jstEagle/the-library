## 21. The Reading of Names

Anselm Rey had taught arithmetic to children for nineteen years, and he had never once believed the numbers would matter this much.

Two hundred and fourteen names. Odile had said the number first, standing at the chalkboard he'd salvaged from the schoolhouse ruins and propped against the chapel's broken altar. Not two hundred — the newspapers had said two hundred when they printed their careful, useless articles — but two hundred and fourteen, because ledgers count everyone, even the ones officials round down for convenience.

"Fourteen extra people," Bram had said, when Odile told him. He was still favoring his left hand; the army had held him thirty-one days and had opinions about his fingers. "That's a village. That's a street."

"That's the point," Odile said.

Now the chapel was full, or as full as it had ever been in its life as a chapel. Deserters sat with dockhands. Fishwives sat beside a former customs clerk who still instinctively straightened papers that no one had asked her to straighten. Ilse Marchetti stood near the lamp-room window she had insisted on repairing herself, her father's half-copy of the ledger open on her arm — pages soft as cloth from salt, half the names written in a lighthouse keeper's patient hand.

And on the altar table, under Anselm's own eyes because he did not trust anyone else's: the ledger itself. The real one. Crated for burning eleven days ago, carried out through smoke by people who had no right to survive as long as they had, and who had survived anyway.

The armistice was nine days old. The amnesty list was eight days old. The word *irregular* was eight days old, and Anselm had spent eight days learning what a single word could do when a government hid behind it.

He tapped the chalkboard once. The room went quiet the way rooms only go quiet for former schoolteachers — completely, and slightly resentfully.

"We read them all," he said. "Every name. Out loud. Then we write them again — three copies, three hands, three places. No man with a stamp gets to decide these people were never born."

Odile raised her hand like she was still eleven years old. Anselm almost smiled.

"Yes, Mademoiselle Ferrand."

"How do we know we have them right? All of them."

It was the question he had dreaded, so he answered it first, before dread could settle in anyone. "We don't. We have your memory, which watched guards burn pages and kept them anyway. We have Ilse's half-copy, made before the war by a man who trusted paper less than fire. And we have the ledger, which is the law's own hand. Where all three agree, we are certain. Where they disagree—" he paused— "we keep both versions. A person spelled two ways is still a person."

Zephyrine Sterling sat in the last pew, and Anselm saw the way they flinched at that — *a person spelled two ways* — because one of those spellings belonged to Ilse Marchetti, and Zephyrine had spent four months learning exactly how much damage a spelling could do.

He began.

"Aalders, Pieter."

"Aalders, Pieter," the room repeated. It had been Zephyrine's idea, the repetition. A name spoken alone is a prayer, they'd said; a name spoken back is a witness.

"Bakker, Grietje."

"Bakker, Grietje."

They went slowly. Odile stood with her eyes closed for most of it, reciting from whatever architecture she had built inside herself — psalms, she had told Anselm once, in her cell, with a guard's boots coming down the corridor; she had hidden the M-to-R names inside the rhythm of lamentations, and she could still hear the rhythm now, and would hear it until she died. When she reached those names her voice changed key, and nobody asked why, because by then everybody understood what things cost.

At name sixty-one, Odile stopped.

"Cassier," she said. "Cassier, Tomas." Then, quieter: "He's not in the captain's ledger."

Anselm checked. The rescued book skipped from Cassier, Anna to Catoir, Émile. He looked up at Ilse.

Ilse turned a salt-softened page. "Cassier, Tomas," she read. "Age twelve. Passage guaranteed, priority listing, annotation in my father's hand: *boy cannot swim, seat near stern.*"

The silence in the chapel was the kind Anselm had only heard in classrooms after bad news about a child.

"He burned with the second half," Ilse said. Her voice did not break, which somehow made it worse. "My father copied everything. Everything. I used to think he was an old fool with too much ink."

"He wasn't," Anselm said.

"No," she agreed. "He wasn't."

They wrote Cassier, Tomas into all three copies. In the margin of Anselm's hand he wrote: *source — memory of O. Ferrand and half-copy of L. Marchetti, agreement complete.* Then, because he had been a schoolteacher and could not help himself: *cannot swim.*

* * *

By mid-afternoon they had passed one hundred and forty, and the light through the broken window had gone the color of old brass, and Anselm's throat hurt, and he realized somewhere around name ninety that this was not a chore at all but the opposite — that he had not felt this since his last year of teaching, that feeling of a room holding something together by saying it out loud.

Bram took over a stretch, reading in his flat smuggler's cadence, mispronouncing foreign surnames cheerfully and being corrected by Odile each time, until the corrections themselves became part of the liturgy. Zephyrine read the names of the harbor families, because Zephyrine knew every boat owner, every chandler, every pilot, and gave each name a small unwritten biography with their eyes as they spoke it — *that one sold me rope; that one lied to a patrol for me; that one is dead* — and Anselm watched them carry two hundred people across their face like weather.

At one hundred and seventy-three, the young officer arrived.

He came alone, out of uniform, wearing a civilian coat that fit him badly, and he stood in the doorway of the ruined chapel with his cap in his hands like a man attending a funeral he wasn't sure he was welcome at. His name was Verhoeven. Anselm knew it because Verhoeven had spent three days not beating answers out of him, and had finally misfiled the interrogation report in a way that bought the chapel its three days, and had looked at Anselm across the table and asked, very quietly, *"What if the orders are wrong, sir?"*

Nobody had called Anselm *sir* in a year. It had done something to him that he was still, honestly, examining.

He crossed the room and stood in front of Verhoeven. Around them, Bram's voice continued steadily — "Petrov, Anya. Petrov, Dmitri." — the covenant refusing to pause for anything, which was precisely its power.

"You shouldn't be here," Anselm said.

"Probably not." Verhoeven's hands worked at the cap. "I resigned my commission this morning. They accepted it faster than any request I ever filed, which tells you something." He looked past Anselm at the chalkboard, the ledger, the three copying tables. "Is there a list of the ones they already took? The ones arrested after the armistice, under the irregular clause. There are transports leaving from the north quay. If someone doesn't record those names too—"

Anselm looked at him for a long moment. Mourning, he had learned this week, is just love with nowhere to put itself. But there were other places, actually. There were always other places, if you built them fast enough.

"There's a pencil and paper on the third table," Anselm said. "Sit down. You're a witness now."

Verhoeven's shoulders dropped half an inch, the way a man's do when a weight is exchanged for a different, bearable one. He sat. He wrote.

"Piquet, Marguerite," Bram read.

"Piquet, Marguerite," said the room — deserters and fishwives and one ex-enemy in a bad coat — "Piquet, Marguerite."

* * *

They finished at dusk.

Name two hundred and fourteen was Voss — not Bram, who waved it off with a rough laugh, but Voss, Lena, a net-mender's daughter granted passage in the same month Bram's own sister had drowned waiting for a boat that never came. Bram read it twice. Nobody repeated it the second time. Some repetitions belong to one person only.

Then the copying began in earnest, and this was slower and stranger than the reading: three tables, three lamps, three hands moving down the columns. Odile wrote in her customs-house copperplate, each name a small act of restoration — she was re-clerking the republic, Anselm thought, rebuilding the honest version of a job that had been stolen from her. Ilse wrote in her father's hand without meaning to; Anselm saw her notice it, see the lighthouse keeper guiding her pen, and keep going with her jaw set. Zephyrine wrote the third copy in a hand that shook only twice — once at Marchetti, Ilse, and once at a name near the end that Anselm had learned not to ask about.

Verhoeven's separate page grew alongside them: the transported, the arrested, the disappeared-since-armistice. A second ledger, begun the day the first became necessary.

When it was done, Odile brought the three copies together on the altar table and stacked them, and then — Anselm would remember this gesture until he died — she squared their edges, the way she had squared ten thousand documents in the customs house, with the same exact care. As if precision itself were the vow.

"Three hands," she said. "Three places. Mine goes inland with the chapel. Ilse's goes to sea. Whose takes the third?"

"Mine stays here," Zephyrine said. "In the lighthouse. With me."

Anselm heard what was underneath the sentence, the thing Zephyrine hadn't said aloud yet in front of everyone: *the lighthouse*, singular, *mine now*. Ilse heard it too. Anselm watched her look at Zephyrine for a long moment — the whole found family politely pretending not to watch — and then watch her nod, once, the way sailors nod at weather they've decided to trust.

"The neutral steamer sails Thursday," Ilse said quietly. "There's room on it for more than me. There's room for whoever the new lists haven't gotten to yet." She looked at Zephyrine, and Anselm saw the argument already living between them, the one that had been going since the fog, since the pier, since *come with me* had been answered by *someone has to see the names through.* "Thursday," she repeated, to the room, to no one. "Two days."

"Two days," Anselm said, "to get as many of these people off this coast as the boats will hold."

He picked up the chalk. Beneath the final column of names, under two hundred and fourteen entries in his own careful teacher's script, he wrote the only heading the document needed, the one he had been composing in his head since Odile first closed her eyes in a cell and started singing:

*Read aloud, witnessed, copied. Let no authority unmake what has been spoken.*

Then he set the chalk down, and looked around the ruined chapel at the smugglers and clerks and deserters and defected lieutenants, at the lamplight and the salt air coming through the broken window where the sea could be heard doing its patient, permanent work — and he understood, with a calm that felt almost like happiness, that the republic was dead but that this, this table, this chalk, this repetition of names back and forth into the dark, was a government now. Small. Illegal. Real.

Outside, far down the coast, the old lighthouse flashed once — relit, illegal, Zephyrine's doing — and swept its beam across the water toward neutral horizons.

Two days. And somewhere beyond the harbor bar, a tramp steamer waited with its flag of convenience and its cargo of impossible hope, and Ilse Marchetti was already counting the seats.
