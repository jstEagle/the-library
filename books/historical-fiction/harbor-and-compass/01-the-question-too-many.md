## 1. The Question Too Many

The census taker arrived at my door on a Tuesday, which I have since decided was the republic's last joke at my expense. Tuesdays were the days I did nothing. I had built my life around them the way other men build their lives around church — a small weekly observance of the fact that Dominic Underhill, courier of sealed diplomatic codes, third-class, was not important enough to be busy.

She was younger than I expected. Census takers in Vessany were supposed to be gray and apologetic, women with pencil-sharpened voices who asked about your stove and your dependents and left you feeling vaguely audited. This one had ink-stained fingers and eyes that took inventory of my face before she'd taken inventory of my rooms.

"Underhill, Dominic," she read from her ledger. "Occupation: ministerial courier."

"That's right."

"And you carry documents between ministries."

"Sealed documents. I don't read them." I said it the way I always said it, with the small pride of a man describing his own virtue. It was true, and I had made a religion of its truth. A courier who reads is a courier who talks. A courier who talks is a courier who drowns in the canal with his wrists tied. The Ministry had a way of making doctrine memorable.

"Of course," she said, and smiled, and wrote something down that was longer than *of course*.

She asked about my stove. She asked about my water. She asked whether I lived alone, and I said yes, and she looked at the single chair, the single cup, the single everything of my apartment with an expression I couldn't parse — pity or arithmetic.

Then she asked about the satchel.

It was sitting by the door where it always sat, because I never let it out of arm's reach. That was the job. The codes went where I went; I was less a man than a very slow, very nervous extension of the diplomatic pouch.

"Standard issue," I said. "Ministry property."

"Mm." She turned a page. "And how many seals does it currently contain?"

I laughed. I actually laughed, because the question was so far outside the liturgy of census-taking that it had to be a mistake, some new form, some bureaucratic experiment. "That's not really a census question."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "How many?"

"Three." The word left me before I could decide whether to give it. "Three seals. Why—"

"And when were they issued to you?"

"Friday. Look, if this is about late filings, I can—"

"Do you know what happens to documents bearing three seals after delivery?" She wasn't writing anymore. Her pencil lay across the ledger like something set down deliberately, the way you set down a knife before saying something honest. "Do you know what the protocol is for three-seal correspondence once it reaches its destination?"

"I don't ask questions," I said, and heard how it sounded — not like virtue anymore. Like a confession I'd been rehearsing for eleven years.

She stood, closed her ledger, and looked at me with something almost gentle. "Mr. Underhill. There is no census this year. There hasn't been one since the war. You should know that whoever sent me wanted to know two things: whether you still had the documents, and whether you'd opened them."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I told myself that was absurd — that some woman with ink on her fingers had rattled me with a parlor trick, that the Ministry would sort it out, that Ilse would explain everything the way she always explained everything, briskly, over coffee, as though history were a scheduling conflict.

But I sat up all the same with the satchel on my knees, and somewhere around four in the morning I did the thing I had spent eleven years not doing.

The outer seal broke like a promise. Inside were the codes — the real ones, the rotation keys I'd carried a hundred times, the ones that kept the ministries talking to each other. And beneath them, folded into the same oilcloth, a second packet I had never been given, because couriers don't get told what they carry.

It wasn't codes. It was orders.

I will not transcribe them here. Not because I've forgotten a word — I haven't, I never will — but because transcription makes it bearable, and it should not be bearable. It was a directive, countersigned twice, authorizing the clearing of the fishing villages along the Sennet coast. *Clearing.* Eleven villages. The dates were already past. The method column used a word I had only ever seen in old histories, the kind of word that belongs to other countries, other centuries, to people who are not us.

There was a routing slip stapled to the back. Final destination: incineration, Records Annex B. Which meant that before it burned, someone had needed it moved — quietly, under diplomatic seal, by a man who doesn't ask questions — from one office to another. I was not carrying the order. I was carrying its erasure.

I sat on the floor of my apartment until the light came up gray through the curtains, holding papers that weighed nothing and everything, and I understood that I had been wrong about the fundamental shape of my life. I had thought I stood beside history the way you stand beside a river — near it, hearing it, never in it. But I hadn't been standing beside anything. I'd been a stone in its current for years, turning slowly, and I had called the turning *routine*.

* * *

Ilse received me at seven-forty in the ministry annex, which meant she'd been there all night, which meant she knew.

"You opened it." Not a question. She didn't even look up from her desk.

"The census taker—"

"There is no census taker." She finally raised her head, and what I saw in her face frightened me more than the orders had. Not guilt. Calculation. "Dominic. Listen to me carefully, because I'm only going to say this once. You were never meant to be at home on a Tuesday. The schedule change came through Saturday. You were supposed to be on the road."

"So the woman—"

"Was checking whether the package was still in play." She stood, straightened her jacket, and picked up her hat. "And now we both know it is, and now you know what it says, and I am genuinely sorry for that, because ignorance was the safest place you'll ever live."

"Ilse." My voice cracked on her name like a boy's. "What do I do?"

She paused at the door. For one moment — I want to believe this, I need to believe this — something human crossed her face, some flicker of the woman who had trained me, who had taught me the canal story, who had once bought me coffee when I failed my first route.

"Go home," she said. "Don't answer your door. If anyone asks, you never opened it. In a week this will be someone else's problem."

Then she was gone, and the corridor swallowed her footsteps, and I stood alone in the annex among the filing cabinets and the flags and the smell of sealing wax, holding nothing, having handed her the only question that mattered.

By noon I had learned two things. The first was that a warrant existed with my name on it — quietly, unofficially, the kind of warrant that doesn't need a judge. The second was that Ilse had filed a report naming me as the sole holder of compromised materials, unaccounted for since morning.

She hadn't gone home. She hadn't gone anywhere. She had simply stopped existing for me — vanished into the machinery she served, leaving me outside it, stateless in everything but paperwork.

I packed the orders into my coat lining, took the satchel, and walked out of my building without looking back, because looking back is how they catch you, and because there was nothing behind me anymore except a single cup and a single chair and a version of myself that had died politely at his own kitchen table sometime around four in the morning.

The city was beautiful in the autumn light. It always is, right up until it isn't.

I went looking for the salt road, because I had heard — the way everyone hears things in the capital, in fragments, over drinks — that if you needed to disappear toward the coast, toward Bral, toward ships and foreign presses and people who owed nothing to the Republic of Vessany, there was a family that moved such cargo.

I just didn't yet know what it would cost them to move me.
