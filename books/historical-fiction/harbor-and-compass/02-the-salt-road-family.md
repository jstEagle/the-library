## 2. The Salt Road Family

I had been a courier for eleven years, and in all that time I had learned exactly one skill: how to be forgettable. I dressed like a man you'd pass on a staircase without remembering. I spoke in the flat vowels of a hundred mid-tier ministries. When soldiers looked at me, their eyes slid off like rain off oiled canvas. It was, I realize now, a kind of cowardice perfected into a profession.

It stopped working on a Tuesday.

Ilse named me. That was the word they used later, when I pieced it together — *named*, as if I were a street or a ship, something that could be pointed at on a chart. She gave them my description, my route, my lodgings above the chandler's shop on Vell Street. The woman who had signed my travel warrants, who had poured me coffee in her office and asked after my mother's health with what I had taken for genuine warmth, sold me to the men who wanted the satchel burned and everyone who'd touched it buried under it.

So I ran. Not well. A man who has spent eleven years being unremarkable does not suddenly become remarkable at escape; he becomes remarkable precisely long enough to be noticed. I went out the chandler's back stair into the fish-smelling alley, and I did the one thing no trained courier should ever do: I kept the satchel. Every rational instinct said leave it, sink it in the canal, let the codes drown. But those pages were the only proof that the government of Vessany had ordered what happened at Kelv Marsh — had ordered it in triplicate, with signatures, with the tidy bureaucratic language of men who sign things — and I found I could not make my hands let go.

I have thought about why. The honest answer is that I was tired of being furniture.

* * *

Maren Oduya's account, set down years later:

The Underhill man came to us through Sef the forger, which meant he came to us half-dead and lying about it. Sef found him in the salt sheds off the old road, shivering like a wet dog, clutching a leather bag the way some men clutch a Bible. Sef brought him to me because Sef brings me everything he can't forge his way out of, and I looked at this clerk-shaped creature with his ministry manners and his minister's terror, and I said no.

I want that on the record. I said no.

Then he opened the satchel on my table between the lantern and the bread, and I read three pages, and after that there wasn't really a choice anymore, though I've never liked pretending otherwise. You don't get to know about a massacre and go back to mending sails. Knowledge is a tide. It comes in whether you've invited it or not, and after that your harbor belongs to it.

* * *

They nearly turned me away twice. First at the door of the salt shed, where a broad woman with grey threading her braids looked at me the way a butcher looks at meat she suspects has gone off. Then again, an hour later, around a table scarred by knives and candle wax, while I laid out the documents and watched four strangers decide whether I was worth dying for.

The woman was Maren Oduya, captain of a coastal trader called the *Kindly Tern*, which smuggled — I would learn — almost everything except people's dignity, which she refused to traffic in on principle. The forger was Sef, small and quick, with ink permanently staining the cuticles of both hands like a disease of the fingertips. The priest was Father Anselm, except he wasn't, having been defrocked two years prior for preaching that the Republic's census was a sin against the soul — a position I now found darkly funny, given that a census question had started all this. And the boy was called Pip, fourteen maybe, who sat apart from the argument reading tide charts by candlelight as if they were scripture, which, in his case, they may as well have been.

"They'll kill him," Anselm said, not unkindly, the way you state weather.

"They'll kill us too, then," Maren said. "Eventually. For something." She tapped the table. "I'd rather it be for something we chose."

Sef snorted. "You're romanticizing. He's a bureaucrat."

"He's carrying proof the Republic murdered four hundred people at Kelv Marsh and called it a quarantine," Maren said. "That makes him the most valuable cargo on this coast."

I should say something here about how it felt, hearing myself described as cargo. It should have stung. Instead it felt like the first honest thing anyone had said about me in eleven years. Cargo gets moved. Cargo matters to someone at the other end. I had spent my whole career believing I was the opposite of cargo — that I was the hand, not the freight — and here was a smuggler seeing through me in a single sentence: I had always been freight. The only question was whose.

"I can pay," I said, because I had nothing else to offer. "There's money. Ministry funds, it's — technically mine, until someone cancels the warrant."

Maren looked at me for a long moment, and then she laughed — not cruelly, but with a kind of weary delight, like a woman finding a coin in an old coat.

"Keep your money, clerk. We'll take payment in truth. You get those papers to Bral, to the foreign press office at the Meridian House, and we're square." She stood, and the decision was made so casually, so without ceremony, that I understood afterward this was simply how she did everything — the way tides don't announce themselves. "Pip. Show him where he sleeps."

The boy looked up from his charts, studied me with the frank appraisal of the young, and said, "You snore?"

"I don't know. No one's told me."

"Everyone snores," Pip said, with the certainty of scripture. "You'll sleep by the bilge. It drowns out most of it."

* * *

Maren again:

He slept by the bilge and he snored, as predicted. But here is the thing I wrote down that first night, because I keep a log even of things that aren't soundings: he thanked us. Four times before morning. Thanked Sef for soup, thanked Anselm for a blanket, thanked me for nothing in particular, just for standing near him. Eleven years in the ministries and nobody had ever taught the man how to receive kindness without treating it like a transaction he'd failed to record properly.

We took him anyway. Not because he was useful — though he was, more than any of us guessed — but because Anselm said grace over dinner that night and included him in it, name and all, and I watched Dominic Underhill hear his own name spoken in a room full of strangers and come apart very quietly, the way ice comes apart, all at once and invisibly.

You can't un-see that. So he was ours. I didn't ask him. Neither did anyone else. That's how families happen on the salt road — nobody proposes it, and one morning you look up and there are five bowls instead of four, and you'd gut a man who tried to take one away.

* * *

The days on the *Tern* blurred into a rhythm I came to depend on more than I admitted. Sef taught me to spot a forged seal — "look at the corners, everyone perfects the center" — while repairing documents I hadn't known could be repaired. Anselm argued theology with me at dawn, gently, relentlessly, and when I confessed I'd stopped believing in anything around my third year at the ministry, he nodded and said, "Yes. They design the work to do that." Pip corrected my knots and my pronunciation of coastal towns and once, memorably, my understanding of the moon, which apparently governs more than tides. "It governs everything that comes and goes," he said. "People too."

And Maren ran the ship, and slowly, without ever saying it, ran me as well — not with orders but with inclusion. She started handing me the second cup of coffee without being asked. She started asking my opinion of routes, then ignoring it, then half-following it. By the fourth day I realized I had stopped checking over my shoulder every ten minutes, and that the absence of fear felt so strange I hardly recognized myself.

On the fifth night, anchored in a reed marsh waiting for a fog that would carry us past the river garrison, I sat on deck with the satchel open on my knees, re-reading the massacre orders for perhaps the hundredth time. Maren settled beside me.

"You could still burn them," she said. "Sink them. Walk away at Bral and be nobody again. You were good at it, I hear."

"I was," I said. "I'm trying to be bad at it."

She smiled at the dark. "Good. Because tomorrow we hit the river checkpoint, and Sef's pass won't hold — I can feel it in the water. And when it doesn't hold, somebody on this boat is going to have to be brave, and I need to know it isn't going to be you first."

"Why not me first?"

"Because," she said, standing, gathering the lantern, "you're the cargo. And I don't lose cargo."

She left me alone with the papers and the fog coming in off the river like a held breath, and I understood that whatever the checkpoints took from us — and I already knew, the way you know weather, that they would take — the taking had already begun. It had begun the moment four strangers decided a fifth bowl belonged on the table.

In the morning, the river checkpoint rose out of the mist, and Sef put on his best forged smile, and Anselm quietly moved his prayer book from his coat pocket to somewhere easier to reach.
