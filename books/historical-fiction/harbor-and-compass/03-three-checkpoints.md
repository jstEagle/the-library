## 3. Three Checkpoints

### Dominic

I had always assumed that courage would feel like something. A burning in the chest, a steadiness of the hands, a choir of resolve. What it actually felt like, on the morning we reached the river checkpoint at Vell's Crossing, was the specific nausea of a man who has eaten bad sausage and is pretending he hasn't.

Maren Oduya had briefed us on the flatbed wagon like a general addressing troops, except her troops were a forger with ink-stained cuticles, a defrocked priest who kept blessing things out of habit, a boy who could recite tide tables for ports he'd never seen, and me — a former courier of the Republic of Vessany, which was a way of saying: the man the whole country wanted.

"The pass is good," Maren said, tapping the document Ferro had produced. Ferro being the forger. He'd aged the paper with tea and folded it against his own ribs for two days so it would carry the honest smell of a body. "It's good," she repeated, and I noticed she was telling herself as much as us.

The river checkpoint was a wooden boom across the ferry road, two soldiers, a sergeant with a clipboard, and a queue of farmers and fishwives who had the resigned patience of people for whom checkpoints were weather. We joined the line. I kept my eyes on the back of the boy Tam's head and tried to be furniture.

The sergeant worked down the queue like a man shelling peas. When he reached us, Maren handed over the pass with the easy boredom of a woman who had done this a hundred times, because she had.

He looked at it. He looked at Ferro. He looked at the pass again.

"District seal's wrong," he said.

"It's the new seal," Maren said. "Since the ministry reorganization."

"There wasn't a reorganization."

"There was," Maren said, "if you'd been reading the gazette instead of the back of it."

It was, I would reflect later, a magnificent lie delivered with the confidence of a woman who believed it, which is the only kind that works. But the sergeant was not a reader of gazettes. He was a reader of faces, and mine — I knew this without looking — had the particular transparency of a man thinking *please don't look at me*, which is the loudest thing a face can say.

He raised two fingers, and two soldiers came, and the mathematics of the situation became very simple. Seven of us. Four guns. One satchel that could hang the Republic's own ministers, if it ever reached a printing press instead of a bonfire.

That was when Father Anselm stepped down from the wagon.

He did it slowly, the way old men do, one hand on the rail, and he said, in the mild voice he used for blessing bread, "Sergeant. A word. I am the one you want."

I did not understand. None of us understood. But the sergeant turned, and Anselm produced from his sleeve a paper — a real one, I would learn later, a warrant that had been circulating for weeks, a warrant for a priest who had been reading forbidden sacraments over executed prisoners. Anselm had been carrying his own arrest warrant like a talisman, and now he spent it.

"There is a reward," he said pleasantly. "And I make a great deal of trouble if handled badly. Whereas these people" — he gestured at us with the weary fondness of a man describing his least favorite relatives — "are nobody. Farmers. You can see they're farmers. Look at the hands."

The sergeant looked at my hands, which have held nothing harder than a pen and a satchel strap, and mercifully concluded that a man with hands like mine could not possibly be dangerous.

They took Anselm into the guard hut. He turned at the door and gave us a small bow, the kind he gave at the elevation of the host, and mouthed something that was either a blessing or an insult, and with Anselm it was genuinely impossible to say.

Maren clicked her tongue at the mule. The boom lifted. We rolled across the river, and I did not look back, because looking back is how you turn a sacrifice into a debt, and Anselm had given it as a gift.

I broke the rule anyway, at the far bank. I looked. He was standing in the doorway of the hut, watching the wagon go, and he raised one hand — not waving, just *there*, the way you'd acknowledge a tide.

"He'll be moved to Bral for processing," Maren said quietly. "Bral is where we're going. We'll get him out."

She said it the way people say things they intend to make true by saying them. I have since learned this is the family religion.

### Maren

I have run contraband past every checkpoint between the capital and the sea, and I will tell you the truth about them: the soldiers are not the danger. The soldiers are tired men who want their shift to end. The danger is the boy.

Not Tam. Tam is the best of us. The danger is *having* a boy, because a boy is the one thing a checkpoint sergeant cannot resist detaining. Boys are suspicious by nature. Boys are the right age to be deserters, runners, couriers. Boys have soft faces that crack under questioning. A boy is a hole in any disguise, and every sergeant knows it, and every sergeant wants to be the one who finds what's inside.

The second checkpoint sat where the salt road climbed out of the marsh country — a stone arch, a barrier, a corporal with a list and a lantern even though it was barely past noon. Fog off the marsh had gotten into everything. My bones knew a storm was coming before the sky did.

"Names," the corporal said.

I gave the names on the manifest, and Tam — traveling as my nephew — gave his false name in a clear voice, because we'd drilled it, because Tam drilled everything the way he drilled tide charts, until the false name fit him like a coat.

But the corporal had a list, and on the list was a description. Not of Tam. Of me. *Woman, fifties, salt trader's papers, likely traveling with a male clerk, thirties.* That was Dominic. They had Ilse's testimony by then — I knew it the way you know weather — and Ilse, God rot her careful heart, had described us both.

"Your clerk," the corporal said, and looked at Dominic. "Step down."

And then, because the fog was thick and the corporal was thorough and Tam was fourteen and could not keep still: "And the boy. Both of them. Hut."

I watched them walk into that hut and I felt something I had not felt since my husband drowned — the specific helplessness of watching water close over someone you cannot reach. Dominic I could maybe talk my way out of. Tam was a different arithmetic. A boy alone in a hut with a corporal and a list, and somewhere in that boy's coat were the re-copied pages we'd sewn into his tide charts, because Tam had insisted — *I'm the one nobody searches twice, I'm a child, children are invisible* — and I had let him be right, and now he was being right inside a guard hut.

Ferro's hand found mine in the wagon bed. The man forges documents; his hands are never still; but they were still then.

"Give it a quarter hour," he murmured. "If he's not out—"

I know what he was going to say. I've thought about what he was going to say every day since, and I've decided to be grateful we never heard it.

Twenty minutes. Then the hut door opened and Tam came out first, and behind him the corporal, and the corporal was holding Tam's tide charts and looking confused, and I understood in one cold flash what had happened: the boy had handed over the charts, opened them to a random page, and begun *explaining* them. Spring tides at Bral, neaps at Vell, the lag between moon and current. Tam can do this for an hour. Tam has done it to me for an hour, on a night when I only asked a simple question.

The corporal handed the charts back with the expression of a man who has been lectured and cannot admit he learned nothing. "Get your wagon off my road," he said, and it was almost a plea.

We rolled through the arch. Tam climbed up beside me, and his hand was shaking, and he pressed it flat against his knee to stop it, the way children do when they've decided crying is for smaller children.

"You did well," I told him.

"I know," he said. And then, quieter, in the voice of a boy discovering the weight of a thing: "The corporal had a son. There was a drawing in the hut. He asked me if I had a father."

"What did you say?"

"I said I had a captain."

I looked at the fog ahead of us and did not let him see my face, because my face at that moment was not fit for a boy to see. There are things you carry. That sentence, I carry still.

### Dominic

The third checkpoint was not a checkpoint at all. It was a table.

Someone had set a folding table across the Bral harbor road, and behind it sat not a soldier but a clerk — a small, neat man with a small, neat mustache, and beside him a stack of papers that I recognized with a lurch of the stomach: wanted notices. Mine among them. My face, rendered by someone who had clearly been told I was sinister and had done his best.

Maren went to the table. I stood with the wagon, ten feet back, being furniture again, and I watched the clerk consult a ledger, and I watched his expression change, and I knew before he spoke.

"Oduya," he said. "Your priest. Anselm Vey. He's been transferred to Bral. He's in the harbor garrison lockup." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down. "And there's a woman in the capital. Ilse Marchetti. Arrested four days ago. She's offered to identify the courier in exchange for consideration."

The world went very quiet, the way it does before a wave breaks.

Ilse. Ilse, who had taught me the codes, who had poured my wine and laughed at my jokes and called me *the last honest man in the ministry* — Ilse had named me to save her own skin, and now, caught, was offering to name me again, more formally, in exchange for walking free. She could exonerate me. That was the clerk's meaning, laid out like merchandise on his folding table: come forward, confirm her testimony, surrender the documents, and be a witness instead of a fugitive. A cell with a window instead of a noose. A Republic that would fall anyway, but would fall with me inside it, on record, on their side of history.

All I had to do was stop.

Maren had not turned around. She stood at that table with her back to me, and I realized she had heard every word, and she was not looking at me, because she was giving me the one thing nobody had given me since the census taker asked his question too many: the choice, unwatched.

I thought about Ilse's face at the last ministry dinner, candlelight on her glass. I thought about Anselm's hand raised at the guard-hut door. I thought about a boy explaining the moon to a corporal so that a stranger could carry paper to a printing press.

"Tell your garrison," I said, and my voice came out steadier than the sausage feeling in my gut, "that the courier they're looking for died at Vell's Crossing. And tell them the tide charts are wrong about everything except the one thing that matters."

The clerk blinked. "Which is?"

"That the water is coming in," Maren said.

She turned, and she looked at me — a long look, the look of a woman reclassifying a passenger as crew — and then the harbor bell began to ring, fast and urgent, the alarm bell, and down at the docks I could see soldiers running toward the quay where her ship lay, and Maren said one word, low and flat, that I will carry longer than any diplomatic code I ever memorized:

"Run."
