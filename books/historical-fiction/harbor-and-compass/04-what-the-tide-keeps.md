## 4. What the Tide Keeps

I have been boarded by customs men, revenue cutters, pirates of two nationalities, and once — memorably — a very drunk admiral who insisted on inspecting my cargo hold for contraband cheese. So when the soldiers came down the pier at Bral with their rifles slung and their faces set to official, I was not frightened. I was insulted.

They sealed the docks at dawn. That was the first wrong thing. Bral's harbor does not sleep, exactly, but it dozes, and there is a courtesy among harbors that you let it. Instead we got boots on planking and a lieutenant with a clipboard reading our ship's papers like they were a love letter from someone else's wife.

"Vessany Republic vessel," he said.

"Chartered," I corrected him. "Out of nowhere in particular."

He did not laugh. Soldiers rarely do; it interferes with the uniform.

Behind me, my crew arranged themselves into their best versions of harmless. Tam, my forger, had spent the night aging documents with tea and candle smoke until they looked older than the republic itself. Father Anselm — defrocked, unrepentant, still wearing the collar because he said it made officials hesitate, which is the only miracle he claimed anymore — stood with his hands folded like a man waiting for communion. And Pip, fourteen years old and able to read tide charts the way priests read scripture, sat on a crate swinging his legs, watching everything.

And Dominic Underhill stood apart from all of us, holding the satchel like a man holding his own heart outside his chest.

That satchel. Three weeks ago it had been nothing to me but a passenger's luggage. Now it held the orders — the massacre orders, signed, stamped, meant for burning — that would hang a government if the right eyes ever saw them. The lieutenant wanted those eyes to be his.

"Open the bag," he said.

Dominic looked at me. I will be honest about what passed between us, because honesty is the only currency left in Vessany that hasn't been devalued: I considered letting him hand it over. A clean loss. We walk away, the republic falls on schedule, and none of us hang beside it.

But then I remembered the road out of the river valley, and Anselm walking back toward the checkpoint with his collar straightened and his hands raised, buying us an hour with his freedom. I remembered Pip detained at the second post, four hours in a guardhouse, coming out smaller and somehow sterner, saying only, "The tide turned without me. It'll manage." I remembered Ilse, the handler who sold her own courier down the river, and how Dominic had defended her even after — *she was afraid*, he'd said, as if fear were a license.

People pay for each other in this world. That's the whole economy. Everything else is bookkeeping.

So I stepped forward and gave the lieutenant the performance of my career: a widow-captain, half-deaf, outraged, waving bills of lading written in three languages, two of which I invented on the spot. Tam backed me up with forged stamps so beautiful I almost wept. The soldiers boarded. They turned over my hold, my bunks, my galley, and found — thanks to Tam's foresight — a cargo of salted herring so genuinely foul that one soldier gagged and another wrote *unfit* on his report with real feeling.

They took the satchel. Of course they took the satchel. Dominic surrendered it with both hands and a bow, courteous as a diplomat, and something in his face as he did it —

Well. I'll let him tell that part himself. He's better at the inside of things than I am. I deal in weather and hulls.

* * *

Here is what the lieutenant did not know, because no one aboard had told him and no one ever would:

Three nights before Bral, Dominic had come to me at the rail and asked for paper. Not much. A few sheets. He sat up with a lamp shielded under his coat and copied every page of those orders by hand — signatures, seals, marginalia, the whole rotten architecture of it — while I kept watch and pretended not to see, which is a skill captains perfect early.

Then Pip took the copies and folded them into his tide charts. Into the charts themselves, pasted flat against the backs, beneath columns of numbers no soldier on earth would bother reading, because tide tables are the most boring scripture ever written and that is precisely their holiness. The boy carried them through the third checkpoint under a sergeant's bored nose, whistling.

The original went into the satchel. The truth went into a schoolboy's homework.

When the soldiers marched off the pier with their prize, Dominic watched them go, and I watched him, and what I saw was not defeat. It was a man setting down a weight he'd mistaken for his spine.

"They think they've got it," he said quietly.

"They've got paper," I said. "Paper burns. What you've got now is in here." I tapped my own temple, then pointed at his. "And in the boy's schoolbag. You can't seize a memory, Underhill. Believe me. I've tried to forget several, and failed."

* * *

The republic fell the way old men fall — slowly, then all at once, then claiming it had meant to sit down anyway. Within a week the ministries were emptying their files into bonfires, and within two, the foreign presses were printing what our bonfires couldn't reach.

I know because I delivered it.

There is a press in Corvane, across the strait, run by an exile named Halloran who owes me money and prints whatever I tell him to clear the debt. Pip handed over his tide charts personally, standing in the ink-smelling back room while Halloran peeled the pages free, and I swear the boy watched those orders become type the way other children watch fireworks.

Two days later the story ran in four languages. *Orders Signed. Village Named. Dates Given.* The word *massacre* sat in the headline like a stone dropped in a well, and the ripples came all the way back to Vessany, where the government that had ordered the burning discovered that paper, unlike people, can come back from the dead.

* * *

Ilse escaped in the chaos. Of course she did. She was always better at leaving than anyone I've met.

She found us at the harbor on the last morning — thinner, gray-faced, carrying nothing — and stood at the bottom of my gangplank like a woman at a door she had no right to knock on. Dominic went down to her. I didn't hear what he said. I saw her flinch, and I saw him put something in her hands — papers, travel papers, Tam's finest work — and I saw her look at them, and at him, and understand that forgiveness had arrived in the form of a forged exit.

She took it. She walked toward the north road and did not look back, and neither, after a moment, did he.

"You're not angry?" Pip asked him later.

"I'm furious," Dominic said. "It passes. Anger's just grief wearing a uniform. Eventually it deserts."

The boy nodded gravely, as though this were a navigational principle, and perhaps it is.

* * *

We scattered, the way ships' companies do when a war ends under them. Tam went east to forge wedding licenses for couples the new government wouldn't recognize, which he called the most honest counterfeiting of his career. Anselm stayed in Bral to say the funerals properly — there were many, and the new authorities had no use for rites, which meant someone had to. Pip's aunt sent for him from the fishing villages, and he left us his tide charts, minus certain pages, "for the archive," he said, with the gravity of a man donating a national treasure, which is exactly what he was doing.

And I stood on my deck in an empty harbor in a country that no longer existed, and I thought: well. That's done. Solo again. The way I like it.

Except.

Except that on the third evening, as I was pretending to check rigging I had already checked, I heard footsteps on my gangplank, and there was Dominic Underhill with a sea bag over his shoulder and no nation, no ministry, no codes, no handler, and no expression of apology anywhere on his face.

"I'm told you need crew," he said.

"I'm told no such thing."

"I'll work cheap. I can read. I can copy documents. I've recently learned to lie to lieutenants, and I'm told I show promise."

"You're a diplomat," I said. "You'll hate the herring."

"The herring and I have already met. We've agreed to disagree."

Pip came up behind him out of the dark — the boy was supposed to be on a cart to his aunt's village, but boys are tides too, they turn when they turn — and grinned at me around Dominic's legs like the punchline of a joke I hadn't known I was telling.

I am a practical woman. I know what a family is. It isn't blood, and it isn't paperwork — I've seen enough of Tam's paperwork to know paperwork proves nothing. A family is whoever is standing on your deck when the storm comes, and whoever you go looking for when it passes.

"Forepeak's yours," I told Dominic. "Both of you. Pip gets the hammock, you get the floor, and if either of you touches my charts I'll maroon you somewhere with excellent views and no food."

"Aye, Captain," they said, in unison, which was the first time in my life that word has sounded like *home*.

We sailed on the morning tide, out of a dead republic, into a strait full of fog and possibility. Dominic stood at the bow with the wind pulling at his coat, and when I came up beside him he didn't look at me, just at the water ahead, and said:

"I used to think history happened to other people. Somewhere else. In rooms I'd never enter."

"And now?"

"Now I think it happens wherever somebody decides not to burn the paper." He smiled — small, tired, real. "And wherever somebody takes you in afterward."

Ahead of us the fog was lifting off the water in long silver ribbons, and behind us the smoke of the capital still stood against the sky, and between the two — between what burns and what keeps — we ran with the tide, all of us, stateless and crewed and, for the first time in any of our lives, exactly where we belonged.
