## 1. The Summons

Xanthe Zhou measured life in portable things.

It was a habit learned early and never unlearned, the way some people never lose the accent of a place they fled. A life could be weighed in what you could carry: two hands, one satchel, a set of jeweler's screwdrivers wrapped in oiled cloth, a whetstone worn to the shape of a thumb. Everything else—the bolted workbenches, the great lens-grinding lathe that had belonged to Master Oren's own master, the shelves of pattern books with their spines cracked like old knuckles—all of it was borrowed against an uncertain future, held on sufferance, subject to the whims of landlords, guilds, weather, and tide. Vellamare taught you this. The harbor took ships as casually as it took mornings; the fog came up the seawall stairs like a landlord who did not knock. Anyone sensible kept their whole existence small enough to run with.

So when Xanthe counted their possessions—and they did count them, every month or so, in the hour before dawn when the workshop smelled of oil and cold brass—they always arrived at the same total: enough to leave, never quite enough to stay.

The counting was nearly done this particular morning. The lamp burned low on the bench. Outside, the bell of the customs house rang five, muffled by fog so thick it seemed poured rather than fallen, and Xanthe sat with the inventory ledger open before them, making small marks in graphite beside items that mattered. The good loupe. The spring gauge, Master Oren's, its brass face gone amber with forty years of fingerprints. The little tin of escapement pallets, ruby-tipped, worth more than everything else in the room combined. And at the bottom of the list, in the column where value was supposed to be recorded, Xanthe wrote nothing at all beside the final item, because there was no way to price it: the unfinished deck clock that lay under its leather dust cover in the corner, begun by Master Oren eleven months ago and untouched for six of those.

Six months since he'd walked out to check the tide-gauge at the breakwater in weather no sane man would cross a street in. Six months since the harbormaster's men had brought back his coat, heavy with seawater, and nothing else. Missing, presumed drowned—Vellamare's most economical phrase, deployed so often in this district that children used it in games. Xanthe had not finished the deck clock because finishing it felt like closing a door, and Xanthe was very good at doors that stayed slightly open. It was, if one were honest, the only kind of door they knew how to make.

The workshop above the chandler's on Rope Walk was theirs now, insofar as anything was theirs. *Theirs* meant the rent was three months behind and the chandler, fat tolerant Mrs. Vessey, had stopped mentioning it but had started leaving bread on the landing, which was worse, or better, depending on how much pride one had left to spend. Theirs meant the Guild's license hung by the door with its seal going green at the edges, and the renewal fee coming due at midsummer, and no commission of consequence since autumn. Theirs meant that when Xanthe closed their eyes at night they did arithmetic: license fee, back rent, the debt to the wire-house for the last shipment of brass stock, the interest compounding quietly in the dark like mold.

The arithmetic never changed. It only waited.

Xanthe capped the inkwell, closed the ledger, and was reaching for the lamp key to put out the light when the knock came.

Not Mrs. Vessey—she tapped. Not a customer; customers came during hours advertised in the window, and this was barely past five. This knock had weight to it. Three strikes, evenly spaced, from someone accustomed to doors being opened rather than knocked upon. The sound went through the floorboards and into the bones of the building, and Xanthe sat very still for a moment, listening to the fog press against the shutters, before rising to answer.

The man on the landing wore Guild gray—a clerk's coat, good wool, hemmed short in the fashion of men who worked indoors while other men drowned. He held a sealed letter in both hands, presented flat across his palms like something sacramental, and he looked past Xanthe into the workshop with the quick inventorying glance of his profession. Behind him, on the stairs, stood a second figure in the livery of the Harbor Watch, arms folded, expression suggesting he had been promised this errand would take less time than it was taking.

"Xanthe Zhou? Journeyman, holder of a current Guild license?" The clerk pronounced *current* with a small emphasis, a coin placed precisely on the table.

"I hold a license." Xanthe heard their own voice come out flatter than intended. "Whether it's current is between me and midsummer."

Something flickered at the corner of the clerk's mouth—not quite amusement, more the professional satisfaction of a man confirming details. He extended the letter. Heavy paper. The seal of the Harbor Guild pressed deep in green wax, the chained-anchor device crisp and unbroken, and beneath the flap, when Xanthe broke it with a thumbnail that suddenly felt clumsy, the hand was copperplate, formal, expensive:

*By order of the Wardens of the Harbor Guild of Vellamare, the journeyman Zhou is summoned to attend upon the Observatory of the Outer Head at dusk this day, there to inspect and undertake the repair of the Great Regulator, whose failure impedes the business of the port. Materials to be furnished at Guild expense. Remuneration to be fixed upon completion, at the Warden's discretion.*

At the bottom, beneath the signature of a secretary whose name meant nothing, was a second line, smaller, added after the fact in different ink:

*Discretion expected. The Guild prefers quiet work done quietly.*

Xanthe read it twice. The words rearranged themselves each time, refusing to settle into sense. The Great Regulator. The master clock of Vellamare, housed in the observatory dome on the Outer Head, from which all the harbor's time descended—the auction bells, the tide tables, the shipping ledgers, the bells of every church that bothered to keep honest hours. Apprentices grew up on stories of it. Journeymen joked about it the way sailors joke about storms they hope never to meet. No independent craftsman had been inside the clock room in a generation; the Guild kept its own horologists, salaried, sworn, and famously incurious. That the Guild should summon *anyone*—let alone a half-licensed journeyman from a failing shop above a chandler's on Rope Walk—was not merely unusual. It was unheard of.

"The Regulator doesn't fail," Xanthe said. It came out before judgment could catch it. "It's the Regulator."

"It has failed," the clerk said, without inflection. "Or so it pleases the Wardens to believe. You will be at the observatory gate at dusk. Bring your tools. The Watch will escort you as far as the headland road." His eyes made one more pass over the workshop, lingering—Xanthe saw exactly where—on the unfinished deck clock under its cover, on the dust that said *untouched*, on the corner where a master's chair stood empty with a folded coat still draped over its arm because neither grief nor tidiness had won the argument. Then the clerk inclined his head a precise fraction, turned, and went down the stairs, the Watchman following, their footsteps diminishing into the fog until the customs bell rang again and seemed to close over the silence like water.

Xanthe stood on the landing a long moment, the letter in their hand, the fog beading cold on the back of their neck.

Then they went inside and did not sleep again.

* * *

Hope, Xanthe knew, was a mechanism like any other. It ran on mainspring tension, and if you wound it too tight it either snapped or tore itself out of the case and destroyed everything around it on the way. They had learned this the hard way, at nineteen, when the first great commission had come—an altimeter clock for a merchant house, everything promised, everything paid in advance—and the merchant house had collapsed into bankruptcy before delivery, taking Xanthe's advance, their reputation, and most of a year with it. Master Oren had found them that night sitting in the dark among ruined gears, and had not lectured. He had simply lit the lamp and said: *Rate it honestly. Hope included. A clock that lies to itself keeps no time at all.*

So Xanthe spent the morning rating the hope honestly, which meant naming aloud, to an empty workshop, every reason the summons might be a trap, a humiliation, or an error, and then, having named them, declining to let any single one stop the work.

A trap: possible, but for what? The Guild had nothing to gain from a journeyman of no standing. If they wanted the license revoked, revocation required no theater.

An error: clerks did not err upward. Clerks erred by forgetting you existed, which was the ordinary fate of shops on Rope Walk.

A humiliation: likeliest of all. Some warden wanted an outsider to blame. The Regulator had failed—or been failed—and when the repair went wrong, as repairs to great mechanisms sometimes did, the record would show a licensed journeyman had been summoned, instructed, paid a modest fee. The Guild's own horologists would remain unstained. Xanthe had seen this shape before, in lesser rooms. Scapegoats were portable things too. They carried everything.

And yet.

And yet the fee was *at the Warden's discretion*, which meant unfixed, which meant negotiable, which meant—for the first time in two years—that the number at the end of the arithmetic might be written by Xanthe's own hand. License fee. Back rent. Brass stock from the wire-house at proper quantity instead of scraps. And beneath all that, the thing Xanthe almost dared not set on the scale: if the Guild summoned a Rope Walk journeyman to touch the Great Regulator, then the work would be seen. Word traveled down from the observatory the way water travels downhill. Commissions followed visibility the way gulls followed ferries. One successful repair to the master clock of Vellamare, and the workshop stopped being a room where a dead man's coat waited on a chair and became a going concern again. An heirloom restored. Not sold. Not abandoned. *Continued.*

That word did things to Xanthe's chest that they preferred not to examine.

They spent the middle of the day preparing, and preparation steadied them the way it always had—the ritual of it, tools cleaned and laid in the satchel in order of use, the loupe, the gauges, the tin of pallets, spare pivots wrapped in waxed paper, the long forceps, the burnisher, the oil horn filled fresh. They took down the good coat from its hook and found it smelling of the drawer and shook it out the window into the fog. From the shelf of pattern books they pulled the slim volume of regulator theory that Master Oren had annotated in a hand like bird tracks, and hesitated, and packed it anyway. If the great clock's guts were going to be laid open to them, they wanted his marginalia along. Grief was portable too. It just weighed nothing and everything at once.

Mrs. Vessey appeared on the landing near noon with bread that had not been asked for and a face arranged into elaborate unconcern. She looked at the satchel, the coat, the letter lying open on the bench.

"Guild gray on my stairs before sunrise," she said. "In thirty years they've sent Guild gray up these stairs once, and that was to tell your master his license dues were late."

"They're sending me to the observatory."

Mrs. Vessey went still in a way that was more eloquent than anything she could have said. Down in her shop, Xanthe knew, were thirty years of harbor gossip sorted and filed like invoices, and Xanthe watched the sorting happen behind her eyes. What she said finally was not what she was thinking. It was: "Take the bread. Fog like this turns to rain by evening, and the headland road's a miserable walk wet."

She set the loaf in the satchel herself, patted it once as if it were a small animal, and left. At the top of the stairs she paused without turning. "Your master did that repair once. Years back. Before you came. Never said two words about it after, and I never asked. But he came home strange, and stayed strange a week." A beat. "Mind how you come home."

Then she was gone, and the fog breathed through the open shutter, and Xanthe decided firmly not to think about that either, and failed completely.

* * *

Dusk came early in fog, arriving not as light failing but as light surrendering. By the time Xanthe crossed the harbor square, the lamps were lit along the quay and the water was invisible beyond them, the whole sea replaced by a pale nothing that swallowed sound and returned it wrong. The Watchman was waiting at the headland turnpike as promised, and walked ahead without speech, his lantern swinging, and Xanthe followed with the satchel knocking gently at their hip, climbing the road that switchbacked up the Outer Head through gorse and black rock.

The fog thinned near the top, which it sometimes did, the observatory standing above the city's breath. And then Vellamare appeared below—whole, sudden, impossible: a thousand lamps scattered down the hillside and around the bowl of the harbor, each one doubled in the black water, the masts of the fleet etched thin as engraving, and here and there the moving sparks of lanterns being carried along the quays by people who could not know they were being watched from above. Xanthe had lived their entire life in that scatter of light and had never once seen it whole. It looked, from up here, like a mechanism viewed through glass. Every lamp a jewel. Every street a train of wheels. And somewhere down among the shining parts, small as a pinhead, a workshop on Rope Walk where the rent was three months behind.

*Portable,* Xanthe reminded the ache in their chest. *Keep it portable.* But the ache declined to be reminded of anything.

The observatory stood against the last grey-green band of sky, its dome blacker than the dark, and the Great Regulator's presence was somehow audible even here—a faint, vast, arrhythmic hesitation in the air, like a heart missing beats, though whether Xanthe truly heard it or only believed they did, there was no telling. The keeper's gate stood open. Beyond it, lamplight showed in the observatory's lower windows, and figures moved behind the frosted glass.

The Watchman stopped at the gate and did not come further. "Gate's here," he said, as if reporting a defect, and turned back down the road, his lantern shrinking away down the hill toward the city of lamps.

Xanthe stood a moment at the threshold, adjusting the satchel, and it was then—turning to take one last look at the view, the city, the harbor, the life measured in portable things spread out below like a working drawing—that they saw the steps.

Wide stone steps rose to the observatory's main door, and at their top, huddled into the angle of the doorframe where the wind could not reach, was a bundle that was not a bundle. A coat. A small shape inside the coat. Dark hair. A face turned away, pressed to the stone.

A child. Alone, at the top of the mountain road, at the door of the observatory, in the falling dark.

Xanthe climbed the steps slowly, and as they came near, the child did not turn, did not move at all except to press closer to the door, and pinned to the front of the coat, catching the lamplight from the windows, Xanthe saw a folded slip of paper bearing a single line in a clerk's copperplate hand:

*Forget her face.*
