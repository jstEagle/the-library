## 2. A Child at the Door

The summons had come with wax and weight, the Guild's seal pressed fat and crooked into it, as if whoever had stamped it had been in a hurry to be done with Xanthe before they'd even begun. Xanthe carried it the whole way up the cliff road like a talisman, then like a debt, then — by the time the observatory's dome rose out of the fog above Vellamare — like something better left unexamined.

They had left the workshop an hour past noon, locking the door twice and once more for habit. Master Oren's rule had always been that a clockmaker locks the shop not against thieves but against hope: nothing walks off in the night so reliably as the belief that things will keep. The workshop was poor now. That was the plain arithmetic of it. Three commissions behind, one apprentice gone south to a city with better harbors, and the rent coming due with the regularity of the very mechanisms Xanthe could mend but could not seem to sell. So when the Harbor Guild wrote — *the master clock at the Observatory of Vellamare has failed; you will attend; the rate must be restored* — Xanthe read it four times and heard in it, beneath the Guild's flat officiousness, the sound of a door not closing.

*You will attend.* Not a request. But work was work, and the observatory clock was the kind of work that made reputations, or buried them.

The road up was cobbled in its lower reaches and then merely worn, stone steps cut into the headland where the carts gave up, and the fog came in from the sea in long slow breaths, thickening with altitude until the harbor below became only a suggestion — masts like needles, lamplight diffused into smears of amber, the whole city dissolved into something half-remembered. Xanthe climbed with the tool roll slung across their back, the leather warm from their shoulder, and counted steps out of old habit because Master Oren had taught that a person who counts learns the true length of things, and the true length of things is rarely what people claim.

Four hundred and eleven steps from the last lamp to the observatory gate. The fog ate sound up here. Even Xanthe's own boots seemed apologetic.

And on the topmost step, folded small against the iron door of the observatory, sat a child.

Xanthe stopped. In the trade one learned to stop cleanly, without jolt, because a startled hand ruins fine work, and the discipline held now: Xanthe simply stood, breathing fog, taking inventory the way they took inventory of everything — portable things first. The girl had none. No bundle, no satchel, no coat worth stealing. Just a child of perhaps seven or eight, knees drawn up, dark hair cut short and unevenly, as if done at home with shears and impatience, sitting against the great riveted door with the patience of someone who had been told to wait and had decided that waiting was a country she would live in.

She wasn't crying. That was the first wrong thing. Children abandoned on cold steps in fog cry, or they wail, or they do the terrible silent shaking thing that is worse than crying. This girl did none of it. She watched Xanthe approach with a level, weighing look — the look of a clerk assessing a delivery — and her stillness had a practiced quality to it, a stillness that had been rehearsed.

"Hello," Xanthe said.

Nothing. Not muteness exactly, Xanthe thought later; refusal. There is a difference between a mouth that cannot open and a mouth that has been instructed to stay shut, and even through the fog and the strangeness, Xanthe recognized instruction.

"You're on my step," Xanthe said, gentler. "Well. The Guild's step. I've been summoned here, see?" They held up the letter as evidence, feeling immediately foolish, offering paperwork to a child. "I'm expected."

At the word *expected*, something crossed the girl's face — fast as a fish under ice, there and gone. Recognition, or fear, or both wearing the same coat.

There was paper pinned to her collar.

Xanthe saw it then: a fold of cheap paper, attached with a straight pin through wool, the kind of pin found in any tailor's offcuts. The girl did not resist when Xanthe knelt — no, that wasn't right either. She permitted it, with a slight lifting of her chin that was somehow more unnerving than resistance would have been, as though she had been told this part too.

The note was written in a quick commercial hand, ink blotted where the writer had hurried:

*Leave her inside the vestibule. She is no one's. Forget her face. Payment arranged.*

No signature. No seal. The paper matched, Xanthe realized with a small cold turning in the stomach, the paper of the Guild summons in their pocket — same stock, same faint gray-green weave, the stationery of an institution too large to apologize.

"Forget her face," Xanthe read aloud, and then, absurdly, looked at her face, which was the single most memorable thing about her: a thin face, sharp-chinned, with eyes the deep brown of lacquered walnut and a small white scar through one eyebrow, old and healed, the kind children get falling against table corners in houses that are not watching them carefully enough. It was, Xanthe thought, the easiest face to remember they had ever seen. Whoever had written *forget her face* had never met her, or had known her so well that forgetting was the only option left.

"I'm Xanthe," they said, tucking the note away. "I fix clocks. Big ones, mostly. There's a very big one behind this door, and if I don't go fix it, a good many smaller ones will be wrong all week, and everyone in Vellamare will blame me, which is unfair but common." They were talking too much. They knew they were talking too much. Master Oren had a theory about that — that clockmakers talk when a problem has no gears, and this was a problem with no gears whatsoever.

The girl uncurled slowly and stood. Up on her feet she was taller than she'd seemed, thin as a pendulum rod, and she reached out and took hold of a fold of Xanthe's coat — not their hand, just the coat, fabric between finger and thumb, the way one holds something one has been told not to trust. Then she pointed at the door.

"Yes," Xanthe said. "Inside. Apparently we're both expected, though nobody had the manners to tell me about you."

The lock was old and honest — a lever tumbler, three moves, no tricks — and Xanthe thought irrelevantly that it was the first pleasant thing about the day. The door swung inward onto the vestibule: a stone room, octagonal, lit by one high window gone violet with dusk, smelling of dust and machine oil and, faintly, of the sea salt that gets into everything in Vellamare and stays like a tenant who pays nothing. Beyond, through a second arch, the stair wound upward toward the clock room, and above everything, felt rather than heard, came the enormous dead silence of the master clock. Stopped clocks have a silence all their own. Running ones breathe. This building was holding its breath.

Xanthe set down the tool roll, lit the bracket lamp with hands grateful for a task, and turned back to conduct the business of depositing a child in a vestibule, per instructions, and forgetting about it.

The business would not conduct itself.

The girl stood just inside the closed door, exactly where she'd been placed, hands at her sides, watching Xanthe work with that clerkly attention. When Xanthe moved, her eyes moved. When Xanthe stopped, hers stopped. It was like being tracked by a small, patient instrument.

"There's a bench," Xanthe offered. "Someone will come for you, presumably. Someone who isn't me." *Payment arranged,* the note had said. Arranged with whom? With the Guild, which meant with Aldane — Corvus Aldane, whose name appeared on every manifest and every rumor in the harbor, magnate of the Guild, a man Xanthe had never met and had already, on general principle and dockside gossip, decided to distrust. Was it his hand on the note? Or someone writing in his shadow?

The girl didn't move toward the bench. Instead she said — her first words, quiet, hoarse from disuse, pitched low as if the walls were owed silence —

"He says you're good."

The fog seemed to press closer against the high window.

"Who does?" Xanthe asked.

She pressed her lips together. Instruction reasserted itself. She shook her head once, a small precise negation, and Xanthe understood two things at once and disliked both: that the girl knew who had sent her, and that she'd already said more than she'd been permitted to say. And the second thing, colder: *he says you're good* meant the sender knew Xanthe's work. Knew it well enough to trust a failing master clock to a struggling workshop at the bottom of the cliff. Which meant the failure itself had been anticipated. Which meant—

Which meant Xanthe was climbing into machinery that someone else had designed, and the child on the step was part of the gearing.

"I can't forget your face," Xanthe said. "It's not a professional limitation, it's—" They stopped, recalibrated, tried honesty, which was rarer and harder than calibration. "My master used to say every mechanism tells you what it's for, if you watch how it moves. You move like someone keeping a promise. I don't forget those."

The girl considered this with grave attention, and then did the thing that undid Xanthe entirely: she walked to the bench, climbed up, sat, folded her hands in her lap, and waited — properly waited, with her whole body — for Xanthe to go upstairs and do the work they had been paid to do. Trusting, or pretending to trust, or having no other options and dressing them as trust. Any of the three was unbearable.

Upstairs, then. The stairs spiraled through the clock's skeleton, past the great driving weights hanging motionless in their shafts, past the drum of the striking train, and every flight the silence got heavier, until Xanthe emerged into the clock room and the dome opened overhead like the interior of a vast skull, and there lay the master clock on its bed of masonry: the regulator, the heartbeat of Vellamare's harbor, mother of every dock bell and office dial and tide-table clock in the city below.

It was a magnificent instrument. Even stopped, even sabotaged — and Xanthe could see sabotage from across the room the way a physician sees pallor — it was magnificent, and it was going to be the death of Xanthe Zhou, though they didn't know it yet.

That discovery belonged to the next hour. What belonged to this one was the moment Xanthe came back down the stairs — three hours later, fingers cut, mind already racing through escapements and altered pallets, certain now beyond argument that the clock had been slowed deliberately, by a careful hand, for a reason that had nothing to do with timekeeping — and found the girl not on the bench.

She was asleep on the bench, curled against the tool roll Xanthe had left behind, arms around it, cheek pressed to the leather, holding the tools the way other children hold what they are afraid to lose.

Dusk had gone fully dark outside. Below in the fog, Vellamare was lighting itself lamp by lamp, and somewhere down there a payment had been *arranged* for a delivery Xanthe had not made and would not describe. Nobody had come for her. Nobody would, tonight; Xanthe understood that with sudden certainty, the way one understands a broken pivot — not by reasoning but by looking at the damage.

*Forget her face.*

Xanthe sat down heavily on the floor beside the bench, back against the stone, and looked up at the sleeping child, and felt unease harden in their chest into something with weight and edges. Responsibility, Master Oren would have called it, and warned that it, too, was not portable — that of all the things a person carries, it is the one that grows heaviest the longer it is carried, and the only one that lightens when shared.

"Damn it," Xanthe said softly, to no one, to the stopped clock, to whoever had written that note in a hurrying hand.

The girl stirred. Did not wake. Held the tools tighter.

Tomorrow, Xanthe would take her down the four hundred and eleven steps to Sefa the lamplighter, who fed every stray the harbor produced and asked no questions until asked. Tomorrow there would be ledgers and suspicions and men like Aldane to reckon with. Tonight there was only this: a child who moved like a kept promise, asleep in a tower full of deliberate ruin, and a clockmaker who had come up the mountain to save a workshop and was beginning to understand that something much larger than a workshop had been placed, folded small, on the topmost step.

Xanthe pulled off their coat and laid it over her, and began, quietly, to count the hours until dawn.
