## 3. Gears and Ghosts

The observatory door closed behind Xanthe with a sound like a held breath being released.

Inside, the great clock room rose three stories into the dark, a cathedral of brass and iron. Moonlight fell through the lantern windows in pale columns, and the master clock stood at the center of it all like an altar — or a patient on a table, which was how Xanthe preferred to think of it. Machines were honest. Machines could be mended.

The girl had not followed them inside. Or so Xanthe believed, for the first hour.

They set down their satchel of tools on the workbench beneath the clock's face and lit the two lamps they'd carried up from the harbor road, trimming the wicks low. The Guild's summons had been specific: *the master clock loses time. Repair it before the tide auctions.* No explanation of how long it had been failing, no record of prior maintenance, no name of the last clockmaker to touch it. That absence had already begun to itch at Xanthe the way a splinter itches under skin — small, persistent, wrong.

"Let's see what you've been through," they murmured to the machine, and opened the access panel below the dial.

The smell hit first. Old oil, yes, and the mineral breath of stone, but under it something sharper — the burnt-sweet scent of metal that had been forced. Xanthe had spent eleven years in Master Oren Vey's workshop learning to read machines the way priests read scripture, and the first lesson had never left them: *a mechanism tells you its history if you're willing to listen.*

This one was shouting.

Xanthe climbed the maintenance ladder to the going train, lamp hooked over one arm, and began at the escapement — the heart of any clock, the place where time was actually made, tick by measured tick. What they found there stopped their hands.

The pallet stones had been filed.

Not worn — *filed*. Wear is patient and even; it rounds corners the way water rounds stone, gradually, everywhere at once. This was different. One pallet face had been ground back by perhaps half a millimeter, just enough to let each escape wheel tooth slip past a fraction too easily, stealing a heartbeat of time with every swing of the pendulum. A few seconds an hour. Minutes in a day. Enough that within a season, the master clock would drift so far from true that every tide table in Vellamare calibrated against it would be worthless.

And the filing had been done with care. With *skill*. The marks were fine as hair, almost invisible unless you knew what wear looked like versus what this was. Whoever had done it understood escapements. Understood them well enough to wound one precisely.

Xanthe sat back on the ladder rung and let the cold of the clock room settle into their shoulders.

*Deliberate.* The word arrived quietly, without drama, the way most terrible words do. Not neglect, not age, not the salt-damp rot that claimed lesser clocks along this coast. Someone had climbed these same rungs, opened this same panel, and slowed the beating heart of Vellamare's time itself — slowly, carefully, so that no single day's error would alarm anyone.

Why?

Their mind went where it always went when a question had teeth: to the summons. *Repair it before the tide auctions.* If the clock ran slow, and the auctions ran by the clock, then the auction times themselves would drift against the real tides. And everyone knew — dockworkers knew it in their bones, boatmen knew it in their drowned boots — that a berth auctioned at the wrong moment of tide was a berth worth less. Much less. To the men who bought cheap and sold dear, a drifting clock wasn't a failure.

It was an instrument.

Xanthe's hands had gone still on the ladder. Below them, the pendulum swung on in its long patience, and somewhere in the dark of the upper works, a gear turned with a faint, arrhythmic hesitation — the sound of a lie being told at four thousand beats per day.

They climbed down and fetched their loupe and their finest files, and began, methodically, to document everything before touching anything. Master Vey's second lesson: *never mend until you understand the wound.* They sketched the filed pallet. They measured the drift against their own pocket watch, which they trusted more than they trusted most people. Eleven seconds slow per hour, accumulating. They checked the suspension spring — tampered, subtly stretched. The crutch loop — widened by a hair. Three separate interventions, three separate hands' worth of subtlety, or one hand working across many nights.

This was not vandalism. This was a project.

* * *

It was somewhere past midnight, crouched at the base of the great train with grease to the elbows, that Xanthe felt it — the particular pressure of being watched. Clockmakers develop the sense the way sailors develop sea-legs. A workshop teaches you the exact weight of silence, and this silence had someone standing in it.

Xanthe did not turn around. They kept their eyes on the wheelwork, wiped their hands slowly on the rag, and said, conversationally, to the empty-seeming dark between two pillars:

"There's bread in my satchel. And a heel of hard cheese that's better than it looks."

Nothing moved. But nothing moved *differently*, which was answer enough.

"You've been up there since I came in," Xanthe continued, threading a new pallet stone onto its arbor with deliberate calm. "Behind the counterweight housing, I think. You breathe very quietly, but the dust doesn't. It settles off you when you shift your weight." They tightened the retaining screw a quarter turn. "I'm not going to shout. Shouting carries, and I don't know yet who it would carry to."

A pause. Then, from the shadowed gallery above the strike train, small and flat and unimpressed:

"You talk to clocks."

"I talk to anything that listens." Xanthe finally looked up. The girl stood at the rail of the gallery, half-lit by the lamplight rising from below — the same child from the steps, coat still buttoned to her chin, hair escaping its braid in dark threads. Her eyes had the watchful quality of harbor cats: fed by no one, belonging to no one, missing nothing. "You should come down. It's warmer near the movement."

"I'm fine here."

"It's not a kindness thing. It's a practical thing." Xanthe held up the filed pallet stone, turning it in the light. "I need to say out loud what I've found, because saying things out loud makes them real, and right now I'd rather they weren't real alone. You're the only other person in this building who isn't supposed to be here. That makes us colleagues."

Something flickered across the girl's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might live, in a child who had learned to keep such things rationed. She descended the gallery stairs with the silent precision of someone who had practiced not being heard, and settled herself on an overturned crate at the edge of the lamplight. Close enough to see. Far enough to run.

Xanthe respected the geometry and didn't comment on it.

"Look." They held out the pallet stone. The girl didn't take it, but she looked, her gaze sharpening with an attention that had nothing childish in it. "See these marks? Fine lines, all running the same direction. That's a file. Wear looks like weather — soft, everywhere. This looks like intent. Someone made this clock lose time on purpose. Slowly. So nobody would notice for months."

"Why?"

"That," Xanthe said, "is the question I've been asking the gears for four hours, and they haven't answered. Gears are honest but they're not talkative." They set the stone down. "But I can do arithmetic. The tide auctions start in nine days. The Guild wants this clock repaired *before* the auctions — which means whoever broke it wanted it broken *for* the auctions. A slow clock shifts the sale times. Shifted sale times mean berths sold against false tides. False tides mean—" They stopped, hearing their own voice tighten. "Mean small boatmen bidding on water that isn't where the tables say it is. Drowning in debt, some of them. Maybe worse than debt."

The girl watched them with those unblinking eyes. "You talk like my father," she said suddenly, and then pressed her lips together as if the words had cost her something.

"Your father knows clockwork?"

"He writes numbers." Her voice had gone careful, each word placed like a foot on ice. "He says numbers are just another kind of machine. He says—" She stopped again. Looked away toward the dark windows, where fog pressed against the glass like something wanting in.

Xanthe let the silence sit. Pressing a frightened child was like pressing a cracked gear — you learned nothing except how fast it split. Instead they returned to the workbench, took out the bread and cheese, cut a portion with the small knife they used for cleaning pivot holes, and set it on the bench's corner nearest her crate. An offering at a neutral border.

"Eat or don't," they said. "I'm going to check the striking train next. If someone slowed the going train, I want to know whether they touched the bells too."

The girl ate the bread when she thought Xanthe wasn't looking. Xanthe was always looking. It was, they reflected, a family trait of the invisible — the ones who lived in shadows learned to count shadows.

* * *

The striking train confirmed it, and worse.

Whoever had sabotaged the going train had been thorough, but the striking train told Xanthe something the going train couldn't: a signature. Deep in the strike mechanism, where the locking plate governed how many blows the great bell received per hour, someone had installed a small auxiliary cam — a clever, ugly little addition, machined from good steel by someone with access to a lathe. It was designed to introduce a variable delay into the strike sequence. Nothing dramatic. Just enough that the bell's count would occasionally lag behind the hands, so that the clock's voice and the clock's face would disagree by a minute here, two minutes there.

Two clocks in one case. A public time and a private time.

Xanthe stood before the open striking train for a long while, the lamp trembling slightly in their grip, and admitted to themselves what the evidence was assembling into. This was not the work of a disgruntled servant. This was engineering. Paid, planned, protected engineering — the kind of work that required someone at the top to want it, someone in the middle to arrange it, and someone skilled enough to execute it and stay quiet.

And the Guild had summoned *them*. Out of every clockmaker in Vellamare, the summons had come to a struggling apprentice in a struggling workshop — the kind of craftsman who would ask no awkward questions because they couldn't afford to refuse the fee. The kind whose master had already vanished into whatever silence swallowed inconvenient partners of the Harbor Guild.

The thought arrived whole, cold, unwelcome: *they chose me because I am desperate. And because I am disposable.*

Behind them, a small voice: "You've gone white."

"I'm fine."

"You're not. Your hands are doing the thing where they pretend to work but they're just holding the tool." The girl had crept closer without a sound; she stood now at the edge of the lamplight, arms crossed, studying Xanthe with frank appraisal. "My father does that. When the numbers stop making sense and he can't say so out loud."

Xanthe looked down. Their hands were indeed holding a spanner without using it. They set it down.

"What's your name?" they asked. "Your actual one. Not the one the note gave you, which was no name at all."

A long pause. The pendulum counted out eight swings of silence.

"Isra," she said at last, so quietly the word barely survived the distance between them.

"Isra." Xanthe nodded, as though accepting terms. "Well, Isra. Here's where we stand, colleague. In about five hours the fog will lift and the observatory keeper will come up the hill, and after him, sooner or later, will come whoever arranged all this — because whoever filed these pallets will want to know what I found. I have two choices. I can repair exactly what they expect me to repair, say nothing, take my fee, and go home to a workshop that may not survive the winter. Or—"

"Or you tell the truth," Isra said.

"Or I tell the truth." Xanthe laughed, but there was no humor in it, only nerves finding an exit. "And the truth, in Vellamare, has a way of needing somewhere to sleep. Somewhere safe. Which brings me to a friend of mine — a lamplighter named Sefa, who owes me for a regulator I fixed for her mother's shrine clock, and who knows every soul on the waterfront, and who has a spare room above her lamp house that smells of oil and onions but keeps the damp out."

Isra's chin lifted a fraction. "I don't need looking after."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "You clearly don't. But you need *hiding*, and hiding is a trade like any other. Sefa is the best I know at it." They began packing their tools, movements quickening now that decision had replaced deliberation. "And I need to walk the harbor road tonight anyway, because I find I can't think inside this building anymore. It's full of ghosts, and one of them files pallets."

At the door, Xanthe paused with their hand on the latch and looked back at the great clock looming in the lamplight — beautiful, wounded, complicit. Tomorrow they would begin the true repair, and play the part the Guild expected: the grateful, incurious apprentice. But tonight, walking down through the fog with a silent child beside them, Xanthe carried something else down the hill — the certainty that the breakdown was no accident, and the growing, vertiginous suspicion that the summons itself had been no accident either.

Someone had wanted them here. The only question was whether as a craftsman —

or as a scapegoat.

Below them, the harbor lights of Vellamare burned through the fog like a scattered constellation, and on the third lamp post past the chandlery, as if summoned by thought, a familiar figure was trimming her wicks against the grey dawn.

"Sefa," Xanthe called softly. "We need to talk. And I've brought you a guest."
