## 4. The Lamplighter's Oath

Sefa Okonjo had been trimming the harbor lamps since before Xanthe Zhou could hold a file without cutting their thumb, and in all those years she had learned one rule above every other: a lamplighter's work is done in the dark, and the dark keeps its own counsel.

So when the clockmaker's apprentice came down the observatory hill at first light with a child wrapped in an oilcloth against the fog — a child who walked like something folded and refolded too many times, like a letter carried so long it had learned to fear being opened — Sefa did not ask questions on the quay where anyone might hear.

She simply snuffed her polishing rag over her hand, took the girl's cold fingers into her warm ones, and said, "Well then. Come along, little wick. You'll want oil before anything else."

The child did not speak. That was the first thing Sefa noted, in her professional way, the way she noted which lamps burned yellow and which burned blue and which had been tampered with by boys who wanted brass fittings for knives. A lamp that will not light is not necessarily broken. Sometimes it is only waiting to be trusted with a flame.

"Isra," Xanthe said quietly, as if offering the name up as evidence. "There was a note. I have it. And Sefa — there's more, but not here."

"Not here," Sefa agreed. "Never here. Here is where the fog listens." She glanced up at the observatory behind them, its dome pale as a fish's belly in the grey morning, and felt the old itch between her shoulder blades that meant someone was watching. She had felt it more often these past two years. Lamps went out in Vellamare that she had lit herself; new men in Guild-grey coats walked the seawall at hours when honest people were asleep; and the names of small boatmen stopped appearing at the chandlery's credit book the way names stop appearing when the men who owned them had gone under the water.

She led them off the quay by the back ways — down Tanner's Stair, through the ropewalk where the hemp hung like drying hair, past the smokehouse where Old Piet would see them and say nothing because Sefa had once kept his son out of the press-gangs and he remembered his debts. This was how the harbor worked, beneath the harbor: a lattice of favors older than any ledger, held together not by contracts but by the simple stubborn fact that dockworkers and lamplighters and smokehouse men needed each other, tide after tide, year after year, or nobody survived.

"Here," she said, and unlocked the low door of her house on Lamplighter's Row — the narrowest house on the narrowest street, wedged between the wick-maker's storeroom and the wall of the old almshouse, with a chimney that smoked sideways and a garden the size of a tablecloth where she grew rosemary because it kept moths from her coat. "In. Both of you. Mind the step, it bites."

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of tallow and dried lavender and forty years of coming home cold. Sefa got the stove going, set water on, and put a bowl of milk-bread in front of the girl without ceremony, the way you'd feed a stray cat you intended to keep. Isra ate the way strays do too — fast, hunched, watching the door between bites — and Sefa's heart did the thing it did whenever a child came through her door hungry, which was to grow three sizes and harden all at once.

"Eat slow," she said gently. "Nobody's taking it. Nobody's taking anything."

The girl slowed. A little.

Xanthe stood by the window with their back to the light, which Sefa understood was deliberate — they had learned it from their master, that old ghost Marlowe Zhou, who never let a customer see his face until he'd seen theirs. They laid the note on the table, unfolded flat, smoothed with both palms like a man smoothing a shroud.

Sefa read it twice. *Take care of her. Forget her face. Payment sufficient.*

"Forget her face," she repeated, and laughed, short and ugly, the laugh she saved for Guild notices nailed to lamp posts. "Payment sufficient. For what? For her? For your silence?" She looked at the child, at the sharp little chin and the eyes that counted everything, and felt something go very still inside her chest, the way the harbor goes still before weather. "Whoever wrote this thinks a person is a debt that can be settled. That's Guild arithmetic, that is."

"You know something," Xanthe said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know rumors," Sefa said. "Rumors are my trade, near enough. I light the lamps and I hear what the dark says back." She poured tea — three cups, and pushed the third toward the girl without asking whether she wanted it, because children learn wanting things is dangerous and somebody ought to teach them otherwise. Then she sat, and rubbed her hands, cracked and black-knuckled from forty years of wicks and brass, and told what she knew.

"It started, near as anyone can fix it, about six years back. The Harbor Guild had four partners in those days — Aldane, of course, Corvus Aldane with his white gloves and his beautiful voice, and beside him Voss, and the brothers Kettering, and a fourth man whose name most folk have forgot because he wasn't a Guild man born, he was a tradesman raised up. Marlowe Zhou."

Xanthe's cup stopped halfway to their mouth.

"Aye," Sefa said softly. "Your master. Don't look so surprised — half this harbor knew he had money in the berths. He fixed clocks for every boat that floats and he was owed respect for it, but respect doesn't buy pilot licenses, does it, and Marlowe wanted the deepwater license for the yard. So he bought in with Aldane. Small share. Honest share, he thought." She paused. "Voss vanished first. Five years back, winter. Went out to check his mooring buoy in flat weather and never came home. Boat found adrift, empty, kettle still warm. Drowned, said the magistrates. Drunk, said the Guild. Grieved, said nobody, because Voss had no family and the Guild sent no wreath."

"The Ketterings?" Xanthe asked. Their voice was very controlled. Sefa recognized control like that; she used it herself every time she climbed the seawall ladder in ice.

"Took ship for Merrowhaven two years after, to 'restructure their interests.' Ship arrived. They didn't. Lost overboard in heavy seas, said the captain — a captain, mind, who'd never lost so much as a crate of salt before or since, and who was piloting Guild charters within the month." Sefa turned her cup slowly on the table. "And Marlowe Zhou. Three years ago now. Walked out of his own workshop on a Tuesday evening to buy a length of brass wire, per the boy who was apprenticed to him then—"

"That was me," Xanthe said. Barely a sound.

"—and never came home either. But here's the difference, lad, lass — whichever you are these days." Sefa leaned forward. "Marlowe's body was found. Down under the seawall steps, three days after, when the spring tide dropped. And the magistrates ruled it accidental, a fall on wet stone, and the Guild sent a wreath, a fine big one, white lilies, and Aldane himself came to the funeral in his beautiful coat and wept where people could see him." Her voice dropped low. "I lit the lamps for that funeral, Xanthe. Every lamp from the workshop to the churchyard. And I watched Aldane stand at the grave looking not grieved. Looking *settled*. Like a man closing a strongbox door."

The stove ticked. Isra had gone motionless over her bowl, bread forgotten, her eyes moving between their faces with the terrible attentiveness of a child who has already learned that adults' conversations contain the maps of her survival.

"Four partners," Sefa said. "One left standing. And every one of the others drowned, and every drowning called accident, and every accident closed by magistrates who dine at Aldane's table. That's not rumor, that's arithmetic. And I haven't even come to the boats yet."

"Boats," Xanthe repeated.

"The *Kestrel*. The *Merry Adela*. The *Pride of Vellamare*, that Bram's uncle captained thirty years." Sefa's jaw tightened. "Small boatmen keep losing their berths. Tide comes wrong, they say — berth floods when it shouldn't, dries when it shouldn't, and the boat takes damage, and the damage gets assessed, and the assessment gets levied, and the levy can't be paid, and the berth passes to the Guild in settlement. Neat as clockwork. Neat as—" She stopped. Looked at Xanthe. Looked at the note on the table, at the child eating bread in her kitchen, and finished the thought anyway, because Sefa Okonjo had never in her life stepped around a truth just because it had teeth. "Neat as a master clock that runs slow. A clock everyone trusts. A clock that says the tide did what the tide didn't do."

Xanthe had gone the color of old paper.

"So you understand," Sefa said quietly, "why I'm not going to forget this girl's face. Not for payment sufficient nor payment extravagant. Because whoever left her on those steps knew what she is. She's the last piece of evidence that hasn't drowned yet."

* * *

Later, when the fog outside had thinned to lace and the street woke to its ordinary noises — carts, gulls, the wick-maker's boy whistling false — Sefa found the girl standing at the little window, watching the light climb the opposite wall.

"You don't talk much," Sefa said, settling beside her, careful not to crowd. "That's all right. Talking's optional. Milk-bread's optional too, but you made a good case for it."

Isra's mouth twitched. Almost a smile. It was like seeing a lamp catch on the third try.

"Here's how it'll be," Sefa said. "You sleep in the loft above the kitchen. There's a blanket smells of lavender and there's a crack in the shutter shaped like a whale, which I've been meaning to mend for eleven years and won't, because some things you leave. If anyone knocks, you're my niece from upcoast, come to learn the wick trade. Nieces are common as gulls here; nobody looks twice." She hesitated, then said the harder thing, because the girl deserved the harder thing said plainly. "And if it ever goes bad — if ever men in Guild-grey come down this street — there's a panel behind the flour bin opens into the almshouse cellar. Old smuggler's run, older than the Guild, older than the town charter. Bram knows it, I know it, and now you know it. That's the oath, right there. Not sworn on scripture. Sworn on escape routes."

Isra looked up at her. Then, deliberately, she reached out and touched Sefa's hand — one finger, precise as a clock hand touching the hour — and pointed to her own chest, and then to the floor, and then made a small motion like a bird landing. Staying.

"Yes," Sefa said, and her voice cracked on it, which annoyed her enormously. "Staying. As long as staying's safe, and past the point where it isn't, if need be. That's what family means in this row of houses. We're not granted to each other. We build it, night by night, lamp by lamp."

From the kitchen doorway, Xanthe spoke, and their voice had a strange, hollow steadiness, like a bell before it's struck. "Sefa. The sabotage in the mechanism. The escapement was filed — deliberately, expertly, to lose exactly enough time to skew the tide tables. And the repair order bears Aldane's private seal, and it names me by name, and it insists the clock be finished before the next tide auction."

Sefa looked from the clockmaker to the child and back, and felt the whole lattice of her quiet harbor life begin, at long last, to pull tight like a net.

"And I suppose," she said, rising and reaching for her coat, because whatever came, there were still lamps to light and a city to walk through dark, "you've got an idea. Clockmakers always have ideas. It's the gears."

"No," Xanthe said. "I've got a question. About my master's ledger. Because if Marlowe kept accounts the way he kept everything else — precisely, obsessively, twice over — then somewhere in that drowned man's papers there's a record of exactly how much Corvus Aldane paid for each of his partners. And I want to know who else has been keeping count."

It was Isra who moved. Slowly, watchfully, the stray-cat caution returning for one heartbeat — and then she crossed the little room, took Xanthe's hand, and pressed it flat against the coarse seam of her coat lining, where something stiff and sewn-in lay hidden like a second spine.

Sefa, watching, felt the flame take at last, blue then gold, certain.

"Well," she said softly. "Ain't that just like the dark. Keeps its own counsel — until somebody lights it."
