## 5. What the Ledger Weighs

Morning came to the lamplighter's garret in shades of oyster and ash, the fog pressing against the single window like something that wanted in. Xanthe had not slept. They sat on Sefa's narrow bed with Isra's coat across their knees, and they had been sitting that way for a long time.

The girl herself was asleep at last, curled in the corner by the cold stove with Sefa's spare blanket pulled to her chin, one hand open against her cheek as though she had let go of something only in the last hour of dark. In sleep she looked younger than eight. She looked like a child who had been carrying a weight sized for an adult and had finally set it down — but only because Xanthe now held it.

The coat smelled of salt and camphor and something faintly sweet underneath, old perfume or old smoke, Xanthe could not decide which was worse. It was a good coat, a clerk's coat cut down for a child, wool that had been brushed and rebrushed until its original color was anyone's guess. And it was wrong. Xanthe had noticed it the night before, when they'd hung it by the stove to dry: the way it hung, the way the left front panel swung heavier than the right, the seam that bulged just slightly, like a cheek holding a secret behind it.

A clockmaker knows weight the way a priest knows prayer. Every movement Xanthe built was a negotiation between mass and motive force; a mainspring too strong for its wheel would tear itself apart, too weak and the whole mechanism lied about the time. They had spent ten years learning to feel dishonesty through their fingertips.

The coat was lying about something.

Xanthe turned it over in the grey light. The outer seam of the left panel ran crooked — not worn-crooked, not mended-crooked, but *sewn*-crooked, recently, by someone working fast and blind with a curved needle. The thread was silk, incongruous against wool, chosen no doubt because silk slips through fabric and leaves the smallest hole. Whoever had done this had known what they were doing. Not a child's work.

Xanthe fetched their kit from the floor by the door — they never went anywhere without it, a habit from the workshop, tools were portable, tools were honest — and took out the small scissors, the ones kept sharp for trimming pallet springs. Then they paused, scissors open, and looked at the sleeping girl.

*If I cut this seam,* Xanthe thought, *there is no putting it back. Whatever is in there stops being hers and becomes ours, and then we are in it, all of us, up to our necks.*

Sefa had gone out before dawn to light the morning lamps, but Xanthe heard her words still, from the night: *Partners of the Guild don't retire, love. They just stop being seen.* And the other thing, softer, said into the steam of tea: *Your master asked questions too, once.*

Xanthe cut the thread.

The seam opened like a mouth. Inside the lining, folded flat and wrapped in oilcloth, was a packet the size of a prayer book, sewn tight and dense, heavier than paper had any right to be. Xanthe lifted it out with both hands, the way they'd lift a balance wheel, and felt the true weight of it settle into their palms.

Not paper alone. Paper and vellum and something stiff at the core — thin sheets of tin, Xanthe realized, interleaved to keep the pages from tearing, from damp, from fire perhaps. Someone had engineered this document to survive. Someone had known it might need to survive a drowning.

Xanthe unwrapped the oilcloth.

The pages inside were written in a fine, cramped hand, columns ruled with a straightedge: dates, figures, and names. Dozens of names. The ink varied in shade — months of entries, years maybe. At first it read like any merchant's book: cargo in, cargo out, sums settled. But the tide column made no sense. High water recorded at hours when the moon argued otherwise; low tides noted where the almanac promised plenty of depth. Xanthe checked three entries against memory, against the little brass almanac they kept in their head the way other people kept psalms, and every one was wrong by the same deliberate margin.

Falsified tides. Recorded, dated, signed with initials at the bottom of each page — *C.A.*

Corvus Aldane.

And threaded through the columns, in a different hand, smaller, more hurried, the payments. Berths auctioned on doctored tide tables. Boatmen fined for groundings those tables had caused. Debts entered against men who had already lost their boats, their berths, and then, in several entries, their places in the harbor's record altogether — the word *drowned* appearing beside certain sums as casually as *paid*.

Xanthe's hands had gone cold around the ledger. They forced themselves to turn the pages slowly, reading each name, because these were not entries to them anymore; these were people, and someone had to read them properly. A cooper off Salt Row. Two brothers who ran a shrimp trawler. The widow Ondine — no, not the widow, the widow's son, aged nineteen, debt of forty crowns, and beside it in the second hand: *paid in full, seawall, 9th of the Grey Moon.*

Paid in full, seawall. As though the seawall were a currency.

Then Xanthe reached the page near the end, and stopped breathing.

The name stood alone in the left-hand column, ruled off from the rest as if even the clerk who wrote it had needed to isolate it. Written in the careful first hand, not the hurried one:

**MERRIT ZHOU — partner, silent — share bought back in full, 11th of the Grey Moon, three years past. Witnessed: C.A., F.Q.**

Three years past. Three years since Xanthe's master had climbed to the top of the workshop one ordinary evening to wind the regulator and had simply never come down — not dead, never proven dead, just gone, leaving his apron on its hook and his supper going cold and an apprentice who had waited up all night with a lamp in the window because that is what you do, that is what you are taught: you keep the light burning until the master comes home.

*Silent partner.* Merrit Zhou had owned part of something and been paid to say nothing, and when he had evidently stopped saying nothing — when he had started asking his questions, as Sefa said — his share had been *bought back in full*, and the man himself had been subtracted from the world as cleanly as a bad wheel removed from a train.

Xanthe did not remember standing. They were at the window when awareness returned, knuckles white on the sill, fog grey on their face, breath fogging the glass. Below, Vellamare was waking: carts, gulls, a bell buoy clanking its idiot patience out in the Roads. The city went on telling the time of a clock that was lying, and everyone went about their business over the graves recorded in a child's coat.

Behind them, a small voice said, "You found it."

* * *

Isra had woken to silence and the smell of cut wool, and she knew before she opened her eyes.

She had known this moment would come eventually. Da had prepared her for it without ever saying so — the way he'd drilled the sewing, night after night in the room behind the counting-house, his big clumsy fingers guiding hers along the seam. *"Even stitches, Issa. Even stitches save lives."* He'd sewn the ledger in himself, that last morning, and then he'd dressed her in the coat and walked her through streets still dark, and at the observatory steps he'd crouched down and held her face in both hands the way he did when he was memorizing her.

*"Give this to whoever comes for the clock. Only them. And if anyone asks, forget their face, and make them forget yours."*

She had done everything right. She had been so quiet. She had let the clockmaker person look at her and had shown them nothing, exactly as trained.

But now the coat lay open on the bed like a dissected bird, and the clockmaker was standing at the window with their back very straight, and Isra understood with a lurch that everything Da had protected her with was gone. The secret was out of the seams.

She should have been scared. She was a little. But mostly, shamefully, wonderfully, she was *tired*. Three nights of sleeping in doorways and one night of pretending to sleep in a stranger's house, and now there was nothing left to guard. The ledger sat on the blanket in its oilcloth, and it wasn't hers anymore, and some mean little voice in her said *you failed*, and another voice, quieter, said *Da said give it to the clockmaker. You gave it. You did it right.*

"You weren't supposed to open it," she said, because someone had to state the rules even if they'd been broken.

The clockmaker turned. Younger than Isra had first thought, in the daylight — a face like a drawn diagram, all clean lines, eyes the color of the inside of a shell. "No," they agreed. "I wasn't."

"Da sewed it in. Not me. I just carried it." She sat up in the blanket, chin up, the way Da did when the Guild men came round the counting-house and he wanted them to know he wasn't afraid even when he was. "It's real. Before you ask. Every number. Da wrote the small writing. He always did the small writing because Mr. Aldane's clerks lie with big handwriting." She recited it the way she recited sums, because reciting meant not crying: "Names, tides, payments. Three kinds of lies in one book."

The clockmaker came and sat down at the foot of the bed, keeping the distance polite, the way you'd approach a strange dog. "What was your father's name?"

"Elun Vashti. He was under-clerk for berths and dues." Isra watched the name land. Grown-ups thought children didn't notice things, but she had watched her father's name make faces go careful all her life. "He didn't fall off the seawall," she added, flat, before the question could be asked wrong. "He wouldn't. He was afraid of heights. He used to get dizzy on the third stair."

That was the truest thing she knew about him, and she had saved it for this exact moment, hoarded it like a coin, and now she spent it and her eyes stung anyway.

The clockmaker didn't say *I'm sorry*, which Isra appreciated, because sorry was what people said when they were done listening. Instead they asked, "Why me? Why the clockmaker?"

"The note said whoever repairs the master clock." She had the note still — she'd hidden it in her boot sole, which Da hadn't known about, so it counted as hers. She fished it out and handed it over. *Forget her face,* it said, in her father's hand, same as always. "Da said the clock is how Aldane steals. The auctions run on the clock. If the clock is slow, the tides look wrong, and the berth prices drop, and then Mr. Aldane's people buy everything cheap and sell it dear when the clock is fixed again. Da explained it twice but I only understood it once." Her jaw tightened. "He was going to testify. That's why he copied the book. He was going to give it to the harbormaster's tribunal, but they moved too fast. So he put it in my coat instead." She looked at her own hands, small on the blanket. "He said a book couldn't drown."

The clockmaker made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite anything else. "He was right."

"He was right about most things," Isra said, with the fierce loyalty of the newly orphaned, and then, because grief is honest at the worst times, "except heights."

* * *

Xanthe sat with the ledger open between them on the bedspread, and taught a child to read her own inheritance.

They went through it page by page, because Xanthe needed to understand the mechanism of the thing — everything was a mechanism, grief included, and you survived a machine by knowing its parts — and because Isra deserved to see that it was being taken seriously, that her father's cramped, brave hand was being read the way scripture is read. The falsified tide entries ran back four years. The auction dates aligned with them precisely. The debts followed the auctions like shadow follows cart. And scattered through, in the margins, in Elun Vashti's smallest hand, were the annotations of a man building his case in secret: *witness saw loading at unmarked jetty, Grey Moon, 3rd. Manifest says sandstone; crates floated wrong for stone.*

And everywhere, sooner or later, the name.

*Merrit Zhou.* Partner, silent. Bought out three years ago, and vanished the same season. Xanthe traced the letters with a fingertip, feeling for the truth the way they'd feel for a cracked tooth in a gearwheel. Master Merrit had taken Guild money once, early, before Xanthe was apprenticed — Sefa had hinted, the master had never denied it, had only gone quiet in a particular way whenever the Harbor Guild was mentioned, a silence Xanthe had mistaken all these years for shame. Now Xanthe understood: it had not been shame. It had been fear wearing shame's clothes. He had bought his way out, kept his mouth shut, raised his apprentice, wound his clocks — and then somewhere in his last year he had started asking questions after all. Old men and ledgers. Perhaps he had wanted to die clean. Perhaps he had merely wanted to stop flinching at the harbor bells.

Either way, the entry told Xanthe two things with the impersonal certainty of mathematics. One: Corvus Aldane had killed — or had arranged the killing of — Xanthe's master. Two: Xanthe was now holding the only proof of it, and the proof was sewn into a child's coat, hidden above a lamplighter's stove, in a city where the man who did the burying inspected clock repairs personally.

"I knew him," Isra said suddenly. "Your master. He came to the counting-house once. He was tall and he smelled like oil and he brought me a little brass snail that walked down ramps." She frowned, digging for detail. "Da went white when he left. I thought your master was somebody scary. But he talked to me about the snail for ever so long."

*A brass snail that walked down ramps.* Xanthe's throat closed. Master Merrit made toys for children the way other men smoked pipes — little automata from offcuts, given away freely, never sold. There had been one on Xanthe's own bench, unfinished, waiting three years for hands steady enough to finish it. So the tall man smelling of oil had walked into a corrupt counting-house, terrified for his life, and spent his courage talking to a small girl about a snail.

"He wasn't scary," Xanthe said, when they could speak. "He was brave. Braver than he looked." They closed the ledger, gently, as though it might bruise. "Isra. This book — it will hurt him. Aldane. It could bring him down, if it reaches the right hands. But getting it there will be dangerous, for you especially. Do you understand?"

Isra looked at the clockmaker for a long moment with her father's level grey eyes.

"Da put it in a coat," she said. "Coats can't walk to the tribunal by themselves." She lifted her chin. "I'll carry it. That's my job. Nobody suspects a kid."

And there it was, Xanthe thought — resolve arriving in the smallest package he'd ever seen it wear. The grief was still there, huge, pressing down on them both like deep water; he could feel it in his own chest, a pressure behind the sternum, three years of unanswered questions suddenly answered in the worst possible ink. But grief, Xanthe realized — turning the ledger over in their hands, feeling its engineered, survivable weight — grief was only weight. And a clockmaker, of all people, knew what to do with weight.

You built around it. You found the escapement. You let it drive the train.

"We keep it sewn," Xanthe said. "New seam, my stitching — better than your father's, no offense to the dead. And we tell no one. Not yet." Their mind was already moving ahead, drafting, the way it did when a difficult repair came through the door — *and who else knows? Who else has seen? Does Aldane suspect the book survived?*

"One person knows besides us," Isra said. "Madame Vashti. My mother. Da wrote her a letter before—" She stopped, restarted. "Before. She went away to cousins in Marrowgate. Da said she'd be safe there."

Xanthe nodded slowly, and filed the name away, and did not yet know — could not yet know — how much that name would cost them all before the harbor was done with it.

Below the window, out on the grey water, the observatory bell rang nine o'clock, slow and thick with fog. Xanthe counted the strokes out of habit and heard, with new ears, the tiny drag in the interval — the sabotage, the slowed train, the lie told in bronze over the whole harbor. Nine strokes stretched to fill the time of ten.

The commission. They were supposed to be at the observatory within the week, repairing that lie, making the Guild's thievery run smooth again. Paid work that could save the workshop. The summons sat upstairs at the lodgings like a contract signed in someone else's blood.

Isra was watching them. "You have to fix the clock," she said. It wasn't a question. "Da's note said so. Whoever comes for the clock."

"Yes," Xanthe said quietly. "I have to fix the clock."

And they both sat for a moment in the fog-grey light — the grieving apprentice and the orphaned child, jointed at the hip by a book full of drowned men — while outside, far off toward the Guildhall, a carriage rolled through the wet streets, and neither of them yet knew that its passenger was asking, even now, after the progress of a certain repair, and the whereabouts of a certain girl.

*Forget her face,* the note had said.

By nightfall, Xanthe would learn, someone in Vellamare was being paid very well to remember it.
