## 6. Corvus Aldane

The fog came in off the harbor that morning as if it had been paid to.

Xanthe noticed it first in the way the light changed inside the clock room — the great dial above dimming from bone-white to a bruised pearl, so that the numerals they had polished two days earlier seemed to float free of their positions, unanchored, like things that might rearrange themselves if no one watched. They had learned to read Vellamare's weather through glass and brass. Fog meant damp in the escapement. Fog meant the pendulum would run heavy for an hour until the wood of the rod absorbed its measure of the sea and settled. Fog meant work.

Fog also, on this particular morning, meant footsteps on the observatory stair that no one had announced.

Xanthe heard them before they heard anything else: not the scuff of the Guild's maintenance men, who came up the tower two at a time and breathed like bellows by the last turn; not the light, apologetic tread of the night watchman making his rounds. These steps were slow. Measured. Spaced with the deliberate evenness of a man who had never once needed to hurry anywhere, because everywhere he went, the world was already waiting for him.

Xanthe set down the burnisher. Their hands, traitorous things, had gone cold before their mind had caught up with the reason. Some animal part of them had understood the footsteps long before the rest.

"Master Zhou."

The voice came from the doorway of the clock room, warm as a lamp behind frosted glass. "I hope I find you at a convenient hour. Though I suppose all your hours are convenient. That is rather the point of your profession, isn't it?"

Corvus Aldane filled the doorframe the way a ship fills a horizon — not large, precisely, but present in a way that made everything else recede to background. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples in a pattern too neat to be accidental, dressed in a coat of dark harbor-gray wool that must have cost more than Master Oren's entire workshop. No Guild chain of office. He never wore it, people said; he didn't need to be reminded of what he was. His hands were clasped loosely behind his back, and his face was arranged into an expression of pleasant, unhurried interest — the look of a man admiring a garden he intended, eventually, to pave.

Behind him, at a respectful distance, stood two men whom Xanthe had never seen wearing anything but the shadows of doorways. Guild men. The kind that were always spoken of in lower registers of voice.

"Magnate Aldane." Xanthe rose from the works bench and bowed — not deeply, not shallowly; they had rehearsed the depth of it in the small hours without admitting they were doing so. "This is an honor."

"It is an inspection," Aldane corrected gently, and smiled to show the correction cost him nothing. "Honors come later, when something has been earned. May I?"

He gestured toward the great clock with one hand, and Xanthe stepped aside.

Watching Aldane approach the master clock was like watching a man walk the deck of a vessel he owned down to the last rivet. He did not touch anything. He did not need to. His eyes moved over the mechanism with a fluency that startled Xanthe — not a craftsman's fluency, nothing so intimate or so loving, but the fluent contempt of someone who understood exactly how much each part weighed, and therefore exactly how much each part could be bought for. He paused at the going train. He studied the escapement for a long moment, head tilted, and Xanthe's heart performed a slow, grinding revolution, because *he knows*, some whisper insisted, *he knows what was done to it and he's checking your work the way a fence checks stolen goods—*

"You've restored the rate," Aldane said. "Remarkable. My predecessor's man declared it beyond saving. Wanted to replace half the train." He turned. "You disagreed."

"The clock didn't want replacing," Xanthe said. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "It wanted understanding. Someone slowed it deliberately — a filed pallet here, a bent pin there, weight added where weight would be least visible. It's easier to make a clock lie than most people think. Easier still to make it lie slowly, so no one notices the drift."

"A clock that lies." Aldane tasted the phrase. "And you noticed."

"My master taught me to listen for it. A sick clock has a sound. A lying clock has a different one."

Something moved behind Aldane's eyes — not warmth, nothing so useful as warmth, but the flicker of a ledger being opened somewhere far back in his skull and a line being entered. "Your master," he said. "Yes. Oren Vasilov, wasn't it. The Salt Lane workshop."

The fog outside pressed against the tall windows. The pendulum swung. Xanthe discovered that a room can hold a person upright even when every joint in them has quietly come unlatched; that terror, properly corseted, wears a very convincing resemblance to courtesy.

"You know of him," Xanthe said.

"I know of everyone in my harbor, Master Zhou. It is the single greatest burden of my office." Aldane drifted along the case, his reflection sliding across the glass door that shielded the dial work. "A fine craftsman, Vasilov. Finer than his circumstances allowed him to prove. There was a time — years ago now — when I hoped to bring him into larger work. Guild contracts. Tide tables for the deepwater berths, chronometers for the pilot fleet." He sighed, a small, genuine-seeming thing, freighted with regret. "He declined. Repeatedly. With a certain... rigidity of principle that I confess I found almost admirable. Almost."

"He preferred small work," Xanthe said. Which was true. Which was also, they understood with terrible clarity in that moment, only the surface of a much deeper truth, the way a sounding lead touches mud and reports bottom without ever describing the wreck that put it there.

"He preferred small work," Aldane agreed pleasantly. "And so his debts stayed small, and his workshop stayed poor, and his apprentice—" his gaze traveled over Xanthe with the unhurried thoroughness of a customs inspection, taking in the oil-stained cuffs, the burn scars, the hollows that six months of thin meals had carved under their cheekbones, "—his apprentice learned to measure her life in portable things. Am I wrong? I have found that craftsmen of declining workshops divide their possessions into two categories: what can be carried out a back door in one night, and what cannot. You strike me as someone who has done that arithmetic."

Xanthe said nothing. There was nothing to say that would not confirm him, and confirmation seemed, somehow, like payment.

Aldane smiled as though the silence had been an answer, and perhaps the better one. He turned back to the clock.

* * *

For a while he simply looked at it, and Xanthe used the interval to study him the way one studies a mechanism one expects to have to dismantle later: the slight forward lean of authority, the hands never idle but never busy, the way the two watchers by the door oriented themselves around him without appearing to move at all, like iron filings acknowledging a lodestone. He was charming. Everyone said he was charming; it was in every anecdote told about him in every tavern from the Fishscale to the seawall, always in the tone people used for weather that had killed someone. Charming, yes, and cold underneath it the way the harbor is cold underneath its fog — invisibly, totally, and without malice. Malice requires you to regard the other party as existing.

"The auction," Aldane said at last, conversationally, to the clock rather than to Xanthe. "Thursday next. The berth allocations for the spring tides. Do you know much about tide auctions, Master Zhou?"

"Only what anyone knows. Berths are let to the highest bidder, timed to the tide tables."

"Timed to the tide tables." He nodded, savoring it. "Everything in a port city comes down to that, in the end. Who knows the water, and who merely guesses at it. The Guild keeps the tables. The Guild keeps the clock that announces the tides to the trading floor. One bell for slack water, three for the flood, the long peal for the ebb. And when that bell rings, men bid — quickly, desperately, on the strength of what the bell has told them. A fisherman with a leaky hull and four children will stake his boat on twelve seconds of bell." Aldane turned, and his smile was a masterpiece of civic benevolence. "Which is why the clock must be perfect. You understand that, I hope. Not good. Not adequate. *Perfect.* Anything less and the whole delicate machinery of the harbor grinds its teeth. Men feel it before they understand it — the sense that the ground beneath them is moving to someone else's rhythm. It makes them restless. Restless men say intemperate things at tribunals." He examined his nails. "I dislike intemperance."

"The clock will be perfect," Xanthe said. "I'll see to it personally."

"Oh, I have no doubt." He said it lightly, easily, the way one agrees with a child's assertion of ferocity. Then, moving closer — close enough that Xanthe could smell bay rum and something colder beneath it, the smell of paper kept in strongboxes — he lowered his voice into a register of pure confidence. "Let me tell you something about perfection, since you're young enough to still believe in it. Perfection is not a quality. It is an agreement. A clock is perfect when the right people agree it is perfect. A tide table is accurate when the right people have signed it. A debt exists when we say it exists, and is forgiven — " a small lift of the shoulders — "when we say that instead. The world is not a mechanism, Master Zhou, whatever your trade may flatter you into believing. It is a ledger. And I am its bookkeeper. Men who confuse the two — men who go looking for the *truth* of a gear, independent of its usefulness — those men tend to have short, loud careers." His eyes came up, mild as milk. "Your master was such a man. I mention it only as professional history. Nothing more."

The pendulum ticked. Somewhere below, gulls quarreled over something drowned.

"Is that why you've come?" Xanthe asked. Their mouth was dry enough that the words came out sanded smooth. "To tell me professional history."

"I've come to inspect the repair. As I said." Aldane straightened his cuffs, and the intimacy folded itself away as neatly as it had unfolded, leaving no crease. "And to express the Guild's confidence. The commission is yours through Thursday and beyond — there will be maintenance contracts afterward, annual retainers, a workshop that no longer needs a back-door inventory. All of which depends, of course, on Thursday proceeding smoothly. On the bell ringing exactly as the tables require." He paused at the door, and looked back, and for one unguarded instant the charm dropped entirely, the way fog thins in a sudden current, showing beneath it not a monster but something worse: an absence, a flat calm, water deep enough to drown a fleet without ruffling. "Forget nothing, Master Zhou. But also — remember selectively. It is the rarest of skills, and the most survivable. Ask anyone who knew your master well."

"Meaning no harm by it," he added, as though resuming the earlier warmth from where he had set it down. "None at all. I'm the best friend this harbor's honest tradesmen have. Ask anyone."

He went. The footsteps descended the stair at their own unhurried pace, and the two watchers followed like punctuation, and the door closed on its oiled hinges without a sound.

* * *

Xanthe stood in the clock room for a long time after.

The fog was lifting outside; the dial brightened by degrees, and the numerals swam back into their fixed places, anchored again, obedient again. Down the length of the tower the pendulum kept its count, indifferent, faithful, the one honest thing left in a room that had just been visited by the most powerful man in Vellamare.

*Ask anyone who knew your master well.*

Oren Vasilov had been gone eleven months. Fallen from the seawall, the magistrates wrote, in the manner of men who write such phrases while looking at their boots. An accident. A tragedy. A drunk craftsman, some said, though Xanthe had never once seen him take wine. And now Corvus Aldane stood beside a sabotaged clock and spoke that name like a man laying a card face-up on the table — not a threat, never a threat, threats are for people who fear consequences — a mere historical note, delivered with courtesy, in a voice like warmed iron.

*He declined. Repeatedly. With rigidity of principle.*

So Oren had refused him. And eleven months later Oren was under the harbor stones, and the clock he had loved was being slowed by unseen hands, and the apprentice he had trained to hear a lying clock was standing in the wreckage of that lie with a commission in her pocket and a price already written against it.

Xanthe looked at their hands. Steady. They had expected them to shake, and they did not, and that frightened them more than shaking would have — the discovery that terror, fully grown, does not tremble. It plans.

There was a girl asleep in Sefa's lamplit kitchen up on Lantern Row who sewed secrets into her coat lining. There was a ledger with names in it, and Xanthe would have bet the workshop — the portable parts of it, anyway — that one of the entries in the drowned-debt columns bore the tidy abbreviation the Guild used for partners: *V.*, for Vasilov.

Thursday next. The bell would ring, and men would bid on twelve seconds of it, and boats would pass into Aldane's ledger like coins into a purse.

Xanthe picked up the burnisher. Set it down again. Picked it up.

Then they crossed to the striking train, opened the access panel, and began — slowly, methodically, with the outward patience of a craftsman and the hidden velocity of a cornered thing — to learn everything about the bell that Aldane did not know they wanted to know.

Outside, the fog burned off the harbor, and the first lamps of evening would be lit soon, and up on Lantern Row a lamplighter was climbing her ladder with a secret of her own. The clock counted on toward Thursday, each tick a coin spent, and Xanthe worked among its gears like a diver working a wreck, bringing up one truth at a time.
