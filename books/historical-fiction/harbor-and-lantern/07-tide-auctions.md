## 7. Tide Auctions

Bram Halloway had worked the Saltquay berths since he was nine years old, when his hands were small enough to fit between the planks and pull out the mussels that grew there in black clusters. Twenty-six years later his hands were the size of hams and scarred like old rope, and the mussels were gone — poisoned out by whatever the Guild ships dumped in the channel on their way back from ports that never appeared on any manifest. He still came down before dawn anyway. A man who stopped coming down to the water stopped being a harbor man, and then he was nothing at all.

The fog that morning was the thick kind, the kind that ate sound. Bram stood at the head of the Saltquay stairs with a mug of chicory going cold in his fist and watched the tide come in wrong.

He'd known it was wrong before he'd measured it. You didn't need a staff gauge to tell you the sea had turned dishonest; you felt it in your ankles first, in the way the pilings groaned, in the smell of the mud. But he measured it anyway, because measuring was proof, and proof was the only coin a boatman without a berth could spend.

Three feet short. The morning tide had topped out three feet below where the almanac said it should, and it had done the same thing yesterday, and the day before, ever since the observatory clock had begun striking its slow, dragging hours.

"Still short?" Tam Osker came up beside him, a wiry man with a face like a knuckle and a voice that carried further than it had any right to.

"Three feet."

"Same as yesterday?"

"Same as every day this week." Bram drank the chicory. It tasted like burnt bark, which is to say it tasted like morning. "You know what that means."

"I know what they'll say it means." Tam spat over the rail. "They'll say the sea's gone shallow. They'll say God's draining the harbor. They'll say anything except what it actually is."

"And what is it, then?" Bram asked, though he knew. He wanted to hear it said aloud, in the open air, where saying it made it real and dangerous instead of just true.

Tam leaned close, which for Tam meant shouting distance for anyone else. "It's the auctions," he said. "The berth auctions. They're running them off the observatory clock now, off the master clock, and the master clock's been slowed. Every hour it strikes late, the tide tables go wrong, and every wrong table makes the deep-water berths look like they're silting up. Nobody bids on a berth that's dying. So the Guild buys them cheap through its own men, and then—" he spread his hands, "—then the clock gets fixed, doesn't it. And the water comes back. And the berths are worth ten times what Aldane paid, and the men who sold are ruined, and the men who wouldn't sell get assessed for dredging fees on channels that were never blocked."

Bram said nothing for a long moment. Below them, the fog thinned just enough to show the Saltquay fleet — forty boats, maybe fifty, most of them under thirty feet, most of them mortgaged twice over, riding high on a tide that had come in lying about itself.

His own boat, the *Patient Magpie*, sat third from the ladder. His father had built her. His grandfather had laid her keel. She was worth less every day the clock ran slow, and Bram understood with a clarity that felt like a hook behind the sternum that she was meant to be worth less. That was not misfortune. That was design.

* * *

The notice went up on the Guild board at half past eight, nailed there by a clerk with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that never settled on anyone's face. Bram read it twice, because the first time he assumed he'd misunderstood.

BY ORDER OF THE HARBOR GUILD OF VELLAMARE:

A PUBLIC AUCTION of tidal berths, mooring rights, and channel easements shall be held at the Guildhall on the morning of the spring tide, commencing at the hour struck by the Observatory Master Clock, per the amended Tide Ordinance of last year.

All claims of adverse possession or customary use are hereby suspended for the duration of the auction period.

Bram read *customary use* four times. Customary use. His family had tied up at Saltquay berth eleven for ninety years. There was no paper for ninety years of tying up; there was only the doing of it, generation after generation, the worn groove in the bollard where four generations of Halloway lines had sawed at the same iron ring. Customary use. As if it were a squatter's habit. As if it were theft.

"They can't do that," said young Piet Voss, who was nineteen and still believed things couldn't be done. "There's law. There's the harbormaster's court. There's—"

"The harbormaster's court sits when the Guild says it sits," Tam said. "And the Guild says it sits after the auction. Read the ordinance. All disputes arising from berth transfers are heard in the quarter following transfer. After. Not before. You buy the horse, then you argue about whether it was stolen."

"But the tides—" Piet gestured at the water, at the staff gauge standing proud and dry above the surface like a drowned man's finger. "Everyone can see the tides are wrong. No one will bid on—"

"Guild men will bid," Bram said quietly. "Guild men will bid exactly as much as Aldane tells them to bid, and no one else will be able to afford to bid against them, because no one else can get credit, because the only bank in Vellamare that lends to boatmen is the Guild bank, and the Guild bank has called in every note it holds this month. Haven't you wondered why? Half the fleet got letters. Pay up in sixty days or surrender collateral. And what's the collateral on a fishing boat?" He looked at Piet until the boy understood. "The berth. Always the berth. The boat's just how they make you carry the berth to market."

* * *

By noon the fog had burned off into a hard, clean glare, the kind of light that showed everything and hid nothing, and the quay was crowded with men and women who had no work to do because the water was too low to load from the Saltquay stairs. Unemployed harbor folk are either quiet or loud, and by noon the Saltquay crowd had passed through quiet and come out the other side.

Bram stood on an overturned fish crate because he was the biggest thing on the quay and the crowd had simply arranged itself around him until he was on top of it.

"They've slowed the clock," he said, and his voice went out over the water the way his father's had, flat and carrying, a working voice. "I don't care who believes me and who doesn't. Measure your own tides. Write them down. Every morning, every evening, write down what the gauge says and what the almanac says and what the clock said when you looked at it. Paper, neighbors. Ink. Dates. Because in six weeks, when the auction's done and the berths are gone, the story will be that the harbor silted naturally, that the sea abandoned us, that it's nobody's fault and certainly not the fault of the man who owns the dredging company, the insurance house, the bank, and the clock."

A woman near the front — Marta Quill, who smoked herrings and had buried two husbands to the sea — called out, "And who'll listen to our papers? The magistrates eat from the Guild's hand."

"Some do." Bram held up a finger. "Not all. Old Magistrate Ferren took the salt-tax case against the Granary Consortium and won it, and Aldane hasn't bought him yet because Ferren can't be bought, only embarrassed, and embarrassment needs evidence. Evidence needs paper. So we make paper." He looked around at their faces, sunburned and suspicious and hungry. "And we don't sign anything the Guild puts in front of us. Not the new mooring schedules. Not the 'voluntary' berth registrations. Nothing. A signature is a nail, and they've brought the coffin already built."

It was good talk. It was even true talk. But walking home along the seawall road that evening, Bram admitted to himself what he would never say on a crate: paper was thin armor against a man who owned the foundry that cast the anchors, the warehouse that stored the catch, and the clock that told the whole city what time it was.

* * *

He heard about the clockmaker that same week, the way harbor news traveled, which was faster than any official post and more accurate than most scripture. An apprentice from the Ashgrove workshop had been summoned up to the observatory to mend the master clock. Summoned by the Guild itself. Paid from Guild funds.

Bram went to find the workshop on a grey afternoon when the gulls were screaming like unpaid creditors. It was tucked in Lantern Row behind the chandlery, a narrow building with a bow window full of brass and shadow, and inside it smelled of oil and metal shavings and something faintly sweet, like beeswax burned in a lamp.

The apprentice — Xanthe Zhou, the harbor gossip said, though nobody seemed sure whether that was a first name or a last or both — was small and quick-eyed, with ink on their fingers and a look of someone perpetually listening to a sound no one else could hear. Bram filled the doorway entirely and waited to be noticed.

Xanthe noticed him immediately. "If it's a watch, I'm three weeks behind. If it's a ship's chronometer, I'm five."

"It's neither." Bram ducked under the lintel. "It's a question."

"Questions are free. Answers cost."

"Then let me pay in kind." Bram set his fists on the counter, gently, because he had learned long ago that his hands frightened people who hadn't seen them haul nets. "You're repairing the observatory clock. Guild commission."

"Yes."

"You know why it broke?"

"I know why it *stopped* running true, if that's what you mean." Something moved behind Xanthe's eyes — caution, calculation, and underneath both, a current of anger that Bram recognized because he swam in it daily. "Why do you ask?"

"Because that clock is going to time the berth auctions." Bram kept his voice level. "Spring tide. Guildhall. Everything my family has floated on for ninety years gets sold to whoever Aldane points at the lot, and the sale is legal because the clock said it was time. Your clock. Your repair. Your hands on the escapement that decides when the hammer falls — both hammers, if you take my meaning."

For a long moment the workshop was silent except for the tick of a dozen small clocks, all disagreeing politely with one another.

"I take your meaning," Xanthe said at last, very quietly.

"Do you." Bram straightened. "Then understand what I came to say, clockmaker. I'm not here to threaten you. Threats are Aldane's trade, not mine. I'm here to tell you what your hands are going to do, so you can't say afterward that you didn't know. When that clock strikes the auction hour, it won't just be selling berths. It'll be selling the *Patient Magpie*, and the *Kindly Gull*, and Marta Quill's smokehouse, and Piet Voss's father's drag-net rights, and every groove worn into every bollard from Saltquay to the Weir. It'll be selling ninety years of tying up at night and knowing your boat would still be there in the morning. That's the cargo. That's what rides on your gears." He pushed off from the counter. "I'd want to know, in your place. That's all."

He was at the door when Xanthe spoke again.

"The tide tables," the clockmaker said. "The ones the auction lots are priced from. Have you seen them?"

"Seen them? I've lived them. They're wrong. Three feet wrong, every high water since the clock went sick."

"No." Xanthe turned, and in the dim light their face had gone sharp as a freshly cut gear tooth. "Wrong *how*? Wrong uniformly, or wrong in a pattern?"

Bram frowned. "What difference does it make?"

"Uniform error is a broken clock. Patterned error is a written table." Xanthe came around the counter, quick, urgent now. "Someone wrote those tables, Halloway. Someone calculated the falsified depths, matched them to the slowed clock, and signed them. A slowed clock alone proves nothing — clocks fail. But a table that agrees perfectly with a sabotaged clock, week after week, tide after tide — that's not failure. That's forgery with a schedule. Do you have copies? Almanacs? Anything with the Guild's tide office stamp?"

"Aldane's people collect the old almanacs," Bram said slowly. "Sent round criers a month ago. 'Recall of outdated editions,' they called it. Paid a copper apiece for them. I thought it was charity."

"It was arson," Xanthe said. "Paper arson." And then, almost to themselves: "Almost clever. Almost."

"Almost?"

"There's always a copy somewhere. There's always someone who keeps records." Xanthe's gaze drifted toward the back room, toward something — or someone — Bram couldn't see, and for a heartbeat the clockmaker's expression did something complicated: fear and tenderness and a decision being made all at once. Then it closed like a case lid. "Bring me your measurements. Every reading you've taken. Dated, witnessed if you can. If we're going to hang a magnate with a clock, we'll need the clock to testify too."

"*We*," Bram repeated.

Xanthe looked up at him, and there was no apology in it. "I haven't agreed to anything," they said. "But I'd rather know which side of the gear I'm standing on before it turns."

* * *

That night Bram walked the seawall with his coat collar up and the wind pushing at him like a bill collector. Below, the harbor lay dark except for the riding lights of the boats — his boat among them, swinging gently on a tide that had come in another foot short, patient and complicit and waiting, like everything else in Vellamare, for the Guild to tell it what to do.

He thought about the apprentice's face. He thought about the phrase *which side of the gear*. He thought, with a bitterness that surprised him, about his grandfather, who used to say the harbor was honest because the sea couldn't lie. The sea couldn't. But everything that stood between the sea and the men who worked it — the tables, the bells, the ledgers, the law — all of it could, and all of it did, and all of it was for sale at the spring auction.

He reached the top of the seawall stairs and stopped.

There was a light burning in the Ashgrove workshop window, far down in Lantern Row, though it was past midnight. A small light, steady, the color of lamplight through brass. Working late on the Guild's clock.

Or working late on something else.

Bram stood in the wind a while longer, watching that little flame the way sailors watch a lighthouse — not because it promised safety, but because it promised somebody was awake. Then he turned for home, and behind him the harbor breathed, and three streets away, in a workshop that smelled of oil and beeswax, a silent child was about to open her mouth for the first time since anyone had known her, and change the weight of everything.
