## 8. Isra Speaks

The lamplighter's room smelled of oil and dried lavender, and Isra had been awake in it for three days.

Not all night — she slept, in the way small animals sleep, with one ear always open — but she woke to every sound the harbor made. The groan of moored ships. The lamplighter, Sefa, coming up the stairs on feet that tried to be quiet and were not, because she was old and her knees had opinions. The clockmaker, Xanthe, pacing in the next room with a mind so loud Isra could almost hear it ticking.

She lay in the narrow bed beneath a quilt that smelled of someone else's life and counted the things she had been taught.

*Do not speak. Do not give your name. Do not let them see your face twice. A child who is forgotten is a child who lives.*

Her father's rules. Her father's voice, low and urgent, the last night she had seen him, kneeling on the cold floor of the counting room while he sewed something into the lining of her coat with a sailmaker's needle and hands that shook only a very little, because he was a clerk and clerks did not shake. *If you are stopped, you are going to your aunt in Saltrow. If anyone asks, you are nobody's daughter. If anyone is kind to you, Isra —* and here he had stopped, and pressed his forehead to hers, and his breath had smelled of ink and the cheap anise candy he always kept in his desk — *if anyone is kind to you, you may decide for yourself.*

She had not decided. Not yet. Kindness was a thing she had learned to treat like deep water: you could see the shine of it, but you did not know what was underneath until you were in over your head.

* * *

On the fourth morning, the clockmaker came to her.

Isra heard her on the stairs — that careful tread, lighter than Sefa's, a person used to moving around delicate things — and by the time the door opened she had arranged herself into her best shape: small, blank, a girl with nothing behind her eyes. She had been practicing this shape for a year. It fit her like her own skin, which was, she supposed, the terrible thing about it.

Xanthe Zhou sat down on the stool by the window, at a respectful distance, and did not speak at first. That was new. Most adults filled silence the way a leaking boat fills with water, out of panic. Xanthe just looked out at the harbor, where the fog was lifting off the water in long gray ribbons, and let the quiet sit there between them like a third person.

"I've been thinking," Xanthe said at last, "about how a clock tells you when it's lying."

Isra blinked. It was not the opening she had expected. She had expected questions — *who are you, whose child are you, who put you on those steps* — and she had answers prepared for all of them, smooth little stones she could hand over one at a time until the asker grew bored. She had no stone prepared for clocks.

"When a mechanism is tampered with," Xanthe went on, still watching the harbor, "it doesn't stop. Stopping is honest. A stopped clock is telling you plainly: *something happened to me.* But a slowed clock — a clock that's been nudged, a few seconds an hour, nothing you'd notice in a day — that clock is lying. It looks like it's telling the truth. It moves like it's telling the truth. It's only the truth itself that's bent, very slightly, in a direction someone wanted it bent." She turned her head then, and looked at Isra directly, and her eyes were dark and steady and not unkind. "I've been lied to by a very large machine this week. I find I've lost my patience for smaller ones."

Isra's hands, folded in her lap, went still.

"I'm not going to ask you who you are," Xanthe said. "You don't have to tell me. Sefa's given you a bed, and I've given Sefa my word I'll pay for the food, and neither of those things changes no matter what your name is. But I'm going to tell you something, because you've been listening at doors for four days and you deserve to hear it said to your face instead of through wood." A pause. "The man who left you on the observatory steps is the same man who sabotaged the master clock. I'm nearly certain of it. And I think — I don't know, but I think — that whatever he was running from, you're carrying it. In the coat."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the harbor bell rang the half hour, and Isra flinched before she could stop herself, because for a year the bells had been the sound of her father checking his watch and saying *not yet, not yet, we wait for the tide.*

Xanthe saw the flinch. Isra watched her see it, and saw her decide not to pounce on it, and something in Isra's chest — some small, exhausted muscle she had been clenching for twelve months — loosened by a fraction.

"You may decide for yourself," Xanthe said, standing. "That's all. Whenever you're ready. Or never." At the door she stopped, and added, not turning around: "For what it's worth, I don't think you're nobody's daughter. I think you're somebody's daughter very much, and that's why you're here."

Then she was gone, and Isra lay back on the bed and stared at the ceiling beams and cried, silently, the way she had taught herself, for a long time.

* * *

She came down to the workshop that evening.

Not to speak. Not yet. She told herself she was only following the smell of oil and hot metal, the smell that had clung to her father's sleeves on the nights he came home late, the smell that meant *work being done, secrets being kept, the world being adjusted by hand.* Sefa was out lighting lamps — Isra could see her through the window, moving down the quay with her long pole, and the harbor lights waking one by one behind her like a slow, deliberate constellation.

Xanthe was at the bench, bent over the guts of a mantel clock, tweezers in hand, and did not look up when Isra came in. That, too, was a kindness, though Isra did not have the words for it yet. She simply sat on the crate by the door, the way an apprentice might, and watched.

Xanthe worked in silence for a while. Then, without looking up: "The third wheel on that train is worn. See how the teeth are hooked? Every clock wears its own way. Like handwriting." She held it up so Isra could see, then set it down again. "My master used to say you could read a man's whole life in his escapement. Whether he was patient. Whether he hurried. Whether anyone had ever taught him to cut a tooth properly, or whether he'd had to learn alone."

*Whether anyone had ever taught him.* Isra turned the phrase over. It was an offering, she realized. Not a question. A door left open on a warm room.

"I had a teacher," she said.

Her voice came out rusty, a hinge unused. Xanthe's tweezers did not stop moving, did not even pause, and Isra understood that this too was deliberate — that if she made a great event of the speaking, Isra would bolt back up the stairs and not come down again for another week.

"He taught me figures," she went on, slowly, testing each word like a plank over water. "And tides. And how to keep a ledger so it balances to the penny. He said — he said arithmetic was the only honest language, because numbers can't be bribed." Her throat tightened. "He was wrong about that. Numbers can be bribed. He found out how."

Xanthe set down the tweezers then, at last, and turned on her stool to face her fully, and waited.

* * *

It came out of her in pieces, over that night and the next, and Isra would remember afterward that it was never one great confession but a hundred small ones, each one a stone pulled from a wall she had built around herself so carefully that she had forgotten there was anything behind it.

Her name was Isra Vey. Her father was Tammas Vey, and for eleven years he had been a tide-clerk for the Harbor Guild of Vellamare — the man who recorded the heights of the water, the times of the tides, the drafts of every vessel that came up the channel. His figures were famous. Captains trusted them. Insurance houses trusted them. *My father's hand is on every tide table in this harbor,* she said, and heard her own voice crack with pride that had nowhere left to go.

And then, a year ago, the figures had begun to lie.

"It was small at first," she said. She was sitting on the floor now, near the stove, her knees drawn up, and Xanthe had not moved from the stool but had somehow made the whole room feel like it was leaning toward her. "A hand's-breadth here. A few minutes there. High tide recorded lower than it came, low tide recorded higher. My father noticed because it was his job to notice, and he went to his superior, and his superior was very kind, and very understanding, and told him that instruments drift, and clerks drift, and that a man with a daughter and a rented house might want to think about how drift can be — corrected."

The stove ticked. Outside, the harbor breathed.

"But my father had taught me that numbers can't be bribed," Isra said, "so he checked them against the old instruments, the ones in the north tower that nobody used anymore. And they weren't drifting. They were being *moved.* The tide tables were being written before the tides happened. And then I understood — I heard my father and my mother talking low at night — that the auctions were the point. The berth auctions. The Guild sells the deep-water berths at each spring tide, and the price depends on the tide tables, on how much water a berth will have and when. If the tables say a berth will be deep on a day when it will actually run shallow —" She looked up. "Boats go aground. Cargo's ruined. The small boatmen can't pay their debts, and the Guild buys their boats and their berths for almost nothing. Corvus Aldane's men were there at every auction, buying. Every time."

"Corvus Aldane," Xanthe repeated quietly. Not a question. A name being set down in a place where it would not be lost.

"He came to the house once." Isra's voice dropped. "I served him tea. He was — nice. That's the worst part. He complimented my figures. He said I had my father's head for numbers. And he looked at my father the whole time he said it, and my father looked at his teacup, and I understood that the tea was a message, and the message was: *I know where your daughter is.*" She wrapped her arms around her knees. "After that, my father started copying everything. The true tide readings. The altered ones, side by side. The auction records, and who bought, and what they paid, and what the berths were actually worth. Payments — there were payments, from a shipping house, routed through three other names. And other names. Other clerks. Other men who had noticed and been —" She stopped.

"Silenced," Xanthe said.

"Fallen," Isra said, and the word came out flat and terrible. "That's what they call it here. *Fell from the seawall.* Drunk men fall from the seawall. Careless men fall from the seawall. My father was the most careful man in Vellamare. He checked every figure twice." She was crying now, without drama, without sound, the tears simply present on her face the way weather is present. "Three weeks ago he came home white as paper and said they knew. He'd been summoned to Aldane's office on the tide of the twenty-ninth, and men don't come back from that office the same, and he had one night. So he copied the ledger one last time — the whole thing, everything, in cipher, in the old clerk's cipher he taught me when I was six because he said every child should have one secret language — and he sewed it into my coat, and he took me out the back, past the fish market, to a boatman he trusted, a man who owed him — who said he owed him —"

She stopped. Xanthe waited. The harbor waited.

"The boatman took my father's money," Isra said, "and put me ashore at the observatory steps, and gave me a note, and told me to sit still and not speak to anyone. And that was the last night my father was alive, because two days later the crier said a tide-clerk had fallen from the seawall, drunk, and my mother —" Her breath caught. "They told me she was taken to her sister's, in Saltrow. That's what the note said. *Your mother is safe. Forget your face.* But Sefa asked the Saltrow boats, and there's no sister in Saltrow. There's no sister anywhere. And I've been telling myself for a year that the note was true, because the note had to be true, because if the note wasn't true then —"

She couldn't finish. She didn't need to. Xanthe crossed the floor and sat down beside her on the cold boards, not touching her, just near, the way you'd sit near a frightened animal or a broken mechanism — close enough to warm, far enough not to startle — and Isra found that this was exactly right, exactly what she needed, and that she had not known she needed anything until this stranger gave it to her.

* * *

Later — much later, the lamps outside burned down to embers, the stove fed twice — Isra unpicked the stitching.

She had carried the coat for a year through every weather, had slept in it, had been bathed in it nearly, had defended it with her teeth once from a dockside thief who thought a child's coat held nothing but a child's poverty. Now, in the yellow light of the workshop, with Xanthe holding the lamp steady and Sefa — woken by some lamplighter's instinct, drawn down the stairs in her shawl — standing in the doorway with her hand over her mouth, Isra took the sailmaker's needle her father had left in the seam and opened the lining along the hem.

The ledger came out in sections. Oilcloth-wrapped packets, thin as biscuits, each one sewn flat against the fabric so that a searching hand would feel nothing but a child's padding. She laid them on the bench one by one, eleven of them, a year of her father's nights.

Xanthe untied the first packet with hands that were not quite steady, and unrolled it, and read.

Isra watched her read. Watched her face do the thing faces did when they found a name they knew — the small stillness, the way the breath goes in and does not come all the way back out. She watched Xanthe turn to the second sheet, and the third, and stop.

"Who is Oren Zhou?" Isra asked, though she already knew, had known since the night she'd listened through the door, had known the way you know a thing you are not yet strong enough to hold.

Xanthe did not answer for a long moment. When she did, her voice was level in the way that ice is level — smooth, and with deep water under it.

"My master. The man who taught me to cut a tooth properly." She laid the sheet down with exaggerated care, the way you handle something that might go off. "He disappeared fourteen months ago. The workshop told everyone he'd gone to the salt marshes for his lungs. I believed it." A pause. "He was a partner. In a shipping venture. He never told me with whom."

"Corvus Aldane," Sefa said from the doorway, and her old voice was grim as a keel. "Everyone knew Oren Zhou had money in a venture. Nobody knew whose flag flew over it. And now we do."

The three of them stood in the lamplight around a dead man's cipher, and Isra felt the strangest sensation, like a tide turning inside her — the long ebb of a year of silence finally reversing, pulling her words out into open water. She had decided. Her father had said she might, if anyone was kind, and these people had been kind in the exact shape of her father: careful, exact, unwilling to let a lie stand in the numbers.

"He's going to burn the workshop," Isra said suddenly.

Xanthe looked up. "What?"

"I heard them. The night before you found me — two men on the observatory steps, they thought I was asleep, nobody thinks a sleeping child listens." Her words came faster now, tumbling. "They said the clockmaker was getting too curious, and that the shop on Lantern Row was *insured for more than it earned,* and that it would be a pity — a *pity* — if a lamp were to blow loose some night. They said it the way you'd talk about weather. And then one of them said the girl was to be left at the observatory because Aldane wanted the clockmaker *indebted* — a foundling on her doorstep, a burden, a leash —" Isra's hands had closed into fists without her noticing. "You weren't given me out of charity. You were given me out of *arithmetic.*"

The silence that followed had teeth in it.

Then Xanthe said, very quietly, "Well. He's made one error in his figures." She looked at Isra, and there was something in her face Isra had never seen there before — not pity, not fear, but a hard bright thing like metal just off the fire. "He assumed a burden can't become an ally."

Outside, far off across the black water, the observatory bell rang out the hour — and Isra, who had flinched at bells for a year, found herself counting the strokes, and thinking of her father's last lesson, and understanding at last what she was carrying and what it weighed.

*The twenty-ninth tide is coming,* she thought. *And this time, the figures are going to be true.*
