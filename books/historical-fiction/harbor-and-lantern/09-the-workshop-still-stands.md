## 9. The Workshop Still Stands

Xanthe had meant to go straight home.

That was the intention, at least, as she left Sefa's lamplit kitchen with Isra's coat — the coat with its terrible seams — folded over her arm and wrapped in brown paper like a delivery of buttons. The intention was: home, bolt the door, lay everything out on the bench under the good lamp, and think. Grief was a thing she knew how to work through. Her master had taught her that much before he taught her anything about gears. *When your hands don't know what to do,* he used to say, *clean something.* She would clean the bench. She would sort the drawers. She would let the small orderly motions of maintenance carry her through the night until the ledger's names stopped swimming and settled into rows she could read without her throat closing.

But grief, it turned out, had opinions about routes.

She realized it only when she found herself turning down Cordwainer's Stair instead of taking the harbor road — the long way, the way that passed the end of Lantern Row, where the workshop stood. She told herself it was caution. A craftsman checks her tools; a woman carrying a dead man's name in a stranger's coat checks the door of the shop where that man had worked for thirty years. It was prudence, nothing more. She walked it briskly, head down against the drizzle, and it was not until she reached the mouth of the row that she understood what her feet had known all along.

There were men outside the workshop.

Two of them stood beneath the crooked sign of the house next door — Aldane & Sons Marine Salvage, though there had never been sons, only hired backs — pretending to study a bill of lading pinned to the doorpost. A third leaned in the shadowed archway across the lane, and a fourth sat in a flat-bottomed skiff moored at the little water-stair behind the row, close enough that Xanthe could hear the knock of his oar loom against the gunwale. They wore no colors. That was the first thing she noted, the clockmaker's part of her mind already opening drawers and sorting: no Guild sashes, no magistrate's badges, no livery of any kind. Men who belong to someone wear his mark. These men wore nothing, which meant they belonged to someone who did not wish to be named.

The second thing she noted was worse.

The workshop door stood open. Not broken — open, politely, terribly open — and from inside came light and voices and the small, unmistakable sounds of things being lifted and set down on wood.

Xanthe stopped so abruptly that a porter behind her swore and shouldered past. The paper-wrapped bundle under her arm seemed suddenly to weigh as much as an anchor fluke. She thought of Isra back at Sefa's, safe, fed, asleep by now if the child slept at all. She thought of the ledger's columns. She thought — with a clarity that felt like cold water taken all at once — *they know.*

They did not simply suspect. You did not post four men and open a shop's door because of suspicion. Suspicion got you followed, questioned, robbed in an alley if you were unlucky. This was inventory. This was procedure. Somewhere in Vellamare a list existed with her master's workshop at the top of it, written in a fair hand, and Corvus Aldane had signed beneath it or very near enough.

*Go to the watch,* said the sensible voice, the voice of every law-abiding apprentice who had ever lived and died poor. *This is theft. Find a constable.*

Xanthe looked down the length of Lantern Row, at the shuttered windows and the sleeping houses and the fog coming off the harbor in slow grey ropes, and asked the sensible voice one question: which constable? The Harbor Guild paid the constables' salaries the way the tide pays the shore — constantly, inevitably, without ever meaning to give anything back. Her master had said that once, laughing, over cheap wine on the shop's front step. *In Vellamare, girl, justice is a clock. It keeps excellent time as long as you own the works.*

Her master was eleven days drowned, and his killers were going through his drawers.

Xanthe Zhou crossed the lane.

She did not run and she did not creep. She walked the way she had been taught to walk into any room containing machinery — evenly, hands visible, eyes already working. The two men by the salvage-office door saw her coming and shifted, unhurried, the unhurriedness itself a message. The nearer one was broad and weather-burnt with a nose flattened by some old argument; the other was thin, soft-handed, holding a ledger of his own, and it was to him that Xanthe addressed herself, because in any pack there is one who writes things down and he is the one who can be reasoned with.

"You're in my shop," she said.

"Evening, miss." The soft-handed man smiled without warmth, the smile of a clerk closing a window at quitting time. "Not your shop, though, is it? Estate of Master Oren Vael, deceased, debts outstanding to the Harbor Guild in the amount of—" he consulted his book with relish— "four hundred and twelve marks, eight shillings. Guild seizes assets against debt. Entirely regular."

"My master had no such debt."

"Your master had many debts." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness did more to harden Xanthe's heart than any threat could have. "Drowned men are notorious for them. Now, if you'll—"

"I'm Xanthe Zhou," she said. "Apprentice of record, indentured papers filed with the Worshipful Company of Horologists, which is a guild too, clerk, and has statutes about the seizure of a working shop while an apprentice of record stands unexpired." She had no idea whether that was true. She suspected it was not true, or was true in a way that had never once mattered. But the clerk's smile flickered — half an inch, no more — and she banked it like a coal. "So before another tool leaves that bench, you'll show me the writ. Signed, sealed, witnessed. Not a note. Not an instruction 'verbally conveyed.' A writ."

The broad one with the flattened nose took a step forward. The clerk put out two fingers without looking at him — a small gesture, habitual, the gesture of a man reining dogs — and the broad one stopped.

"There's no need for unpleasantness," the clerk said. "We're not thieves, miss. We're making an inventory. Every item's listed, valued, and stored honest. When the estate's settled, whatever remains—"

"Then you won't mind waiting outside while I check your arithmetic."

For a moment nobody moved. The rain thickened, beading on the clerk's ledger cover, and somewhere behind Xanthe the man in the skiff stopped knocking his oar, and the whole lane seemed to lean in to listen.

It was the sound from inside the shop that decided it. A drawer rolled shut. Then another. Then footsteps crossed the workroom floor — she could tell by their weight and rhythm that they were at the bench, at *the* bench, at the great scarred beech slab where her master had fitted the escapement for the customs-house clock and taught her to file brass so fine you could see your face in it — and then came a sound that went through Xanthe like a blade finding bone: the particular musical clink of calipers being dropped into a canvas sack.

Something in her chest, which had been held together since the funeral with craft and routine and the sheer mechanical necessity of getting up each morning, tore cleanly along a seam.

She walked past the clerk. He could have seized her arm; he could have whistled up his dogs; later she would understand why he didn't, would learn that orders had been given about her specifically, orders involving words like *careful* and *not yet* and *let her finish the clock* — but that night she only knew that no hand touched her as she pushed open her master's door and stepped into the light.

Inside, three men were stripping her life.

One stood at the wall of small drawers, working down the rows with a candle-lamp beside him, lifting out reamers and broaches and wheel-cutting files, calling values in a low monotone — "broach set, worn, six shillings... pin vices, three, one shilling..." — while the clerk's counterpart scratched entries into a duplicate book. Another had the long bench: her master's vise was already unscrewed from the wood (unscrewed, the bolts lying on the floor like pulled teeth) and packed, and his good loupe, his *good* loupe, the one with the cracked silver rim he'd refused for twenty years to replace, hung from the man's fist by its chain, turning slowly, catching the light. The third man stood at the far end near the stove, and it was he who had dropped the calipers into the sack, and it was he who turned when Xanthe came in, and it was his face that told her everything.

He recognized her. And he was embarrassed.

Not ashamed — embarrassment is a smaller animal than shame — but embarrassed, the way men are when caught at work they'd rather not explain to a certain kind of witness. He was young. Twenty, perhaps. He had the look of a dockhand doing a job that paid better than dockwork usually did and trying very hard not to think about it in terms more specific than marks and shillings.

"We're nearly done here," he said. "You shouldn't have come."

"You're holding his lathe," Xanthe said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Inside, something was screaming in a register only dogs and clockmakers hear. "That's a Holtzmann screw-cutting lathe. Do you know what it is?"

"It's on the list."

"It took my master nine years to pay for it. He cut the gears for the harbor lights on it. He repaired the observatory's strike train on it when your Guild's own men gave up." She crossed the floor and put her hand flat on the machine's cold iron bed, and the touch of it undid the last of her composure's veneer, though her voice held. "Get your hands off it."

"Now look—" the inventory man began, but the young one raised his own hand this time, mirroring the clerk's gesture without knowing he did it, and the room stilled.

"The writ," Xanthe said again, to all of them. "Show me."

The clerk had drifted in behind her. She heard him turning pages, unhurried. "Writ's at the Guildhall," he said. "Copy can be produced tomorrow, business hours. Seizure proceeds tonight per standard practice against perishable assets. Tools flee, miss. Everyone knows tools flee."

It was absurd. It was meant to be absurd — a wall of small humiliations, each one too petty to fight, each one daring her to make a scene that could later be described as hysterical, as obstructive, as the behavior of a grieving woman. And it would have worked on her, perhaps, if they had been touching anything else. But the young man still had his hand on the lathe, and the man by the drawers had reached the top row where the little wooden boxes lived — the ones labeled in her master's cramped upright hand, *escapements, assorted* and *pallets, finished* and, in the corner, the box marked simply *X., practice pieces*, which held six years of her own failed and beloved work, every gear she had ever ruined learning her trade — and he was reaching for it now, tucking it under one arm like a parcel of rags.

"Don't," Xanthe said.

He didn't even look up. "Everything's inventoried, miss—"

"*Don't.*"

The word cracked out of her with a force that made the man's hands actually stop, and in the ringing quiet afterward Xanthe heard her own breathing, harsh and fast, and understood that she had crossed some line and could not find her way back to the courteous side of it and did not want to. Fury came up through her like flood tide, silent and enormous, filling every channel. But it was not the hot kind. Even as it rose she could feel it organizing itself, the way chaos organizes in a well-made mechanism — every ounce of it becoming torque.

"That box," she said quietly, "contains nothing. Six shillings' worth of spoiled brass, if you value it honestly, which you don't. Put it down, take your four hundred marks of scrap iron, and get out of my master's shop."

"And if we don't?" This from the man at the bench, still swinging the loupe, still smiling. "You'll set the watch on us? Write to the Company?" He looked around at his fellows, collecting their amusement. "Girl, do you know whose list this is?"

"No," said Xanthe. "But I know whose shop this is. Oren Vael's. And I know what he kept in the drawer under the vise, because I've swept this floor since I was thirteen." She let that sit. She watched the young one's hand tighten on the lathe, uncertain. "He kept a book of every repair he ever did for the Harbor Guild. Dates, parts, signatures. Thirty years of signatures, including the countersignatures of every harbormaster and every auction clerk who ever certified his work. Including, gentlemen — and I only mention it as a matter of idle interest — the certification of the observatory master clock's rate, signed twice a year for twenty years." She had invented none of this until she began saying it; now, watching their faces, she discovered it was true, or true enough, or true in the way that mattered tonight. "A shop like this remembers everything. That's what a shop *is*. So when the estate's contested — and it will be contested, by the Company, by me, by anyone whose signature appears in that book and would rather not answer questions about why his tools are in a Guild warehouse — every one of you will be a name in somebody's testimony. I'd think about that, if I were the sort of man who thinks."

Silence. The lamp flame leaned in the draft. Rain ticked at the one dark window, patient as pendulum.

It was the young one who moved first. He took his hand off the lathe — slowly, keeping his eyes on Xanthe the way you keep your eyes on a mechanism under tension — and he said, not to her exactly but to the room, "List says lathe's damaged. Cracked bed. Not worth carting." And he wrote it in the margin of the book, and stepped back.

The others looked at the clerk. The clerk's face performed several small calculations, all of them arriving at the same total: a dead man's debts were recoverable any week; a scene tonight, with witnesses waking along Lantern Row and a named apprentice with a Company and a tongue, was worth nothing at all. "Leave the big pieces," he said at last, wearily, as if conceding to weather rather than to Xanthe. "Cart what's packed. We'll complete the schedule Thursday."

"Thursday," Xanthe repeated, "you'll bring a writ."

"Thursday," the clerk agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing to nothing, and snapped his book shut.

She stood at the door and watched them out. It took four trips to the cart at the row's end. She counted every item as it passed her, memorizing, entering each loss in the ledger behind her eyes where the other ledger now lived too — the vise gone, the good anvilette gone, three quarters of the file drawers gone, her master's dividers gone — and she noted, with a coldness that frightened her a little, that the young dockhand managed to be the one carrying the box marked *X., practice pieces*, and that as he passed her the last time he shifted it so its label faced upward, and said nothing, and looked at nothing, and was gone.

Then the lane was empty. Then the fog closed its grey ledger over the cart wheels' noise. Then it was only Xanthe, alone in the gutted workroom, with the bolt drawn behind her and the stove still warm where one of them had boiled their tea water.

* * *

She cleaned. Of course she cleaned.

She righted the stool. She swept the bolts from the floor and set them on the windowsill in a neat row, ready to be screwed home. She gathered her master's scattered chalk marks from the boards where the inventory men had scuffed them and read them as she swept — depth marks for a shelf he'd promised to build, a sketch of a fusee curve, the word *ISRA?* in his hand, which stopped her breath for a moment until she remembered: a movement he'd been designing for a mantel clock, named after nothing, a private joke of a drawing done months before any of this. Or perhaps not. Perhaps he had known even then. Dead men keep their tolerances to themselves.

At some point she lit the good lamp, and unscrewed the window shutters to check the sight-lines down both ends of the row, and ate half a heel of bread standing up without tasting it. At some point she sat at the bench — at the bare place where the vise had been, in the shallow dimples its feet had pressed into the beech over thirty years, the wood's own memory of him — and put her head in her hands and let the fury drain out through her palms into the grain, and what pooled left when it drained was not calm but direction.

They would come back Thursday. Before Thursday they would come back in worse shapes, with less paperwork; the clerk's retreat had been tactical, not moral, and somewhere Corvus Aldane was being told that the Vael woman had teeth, and deciding what to do about it. The ledger under her arm at Sefa's was proof of drownings. This shop was proof of something else — of her master's innocence, of the shape of his last years, of the fact that a man four hundred marks in fictional debt does not buy a lathe on installments and teach a foundling girl to file brass by lamplight. The Guild understood that. That was why the carts had come tonight, before she could stand in this room as its witness. They weren't seizing assets. They were erasing an exhibit.

*Then don't let them erase it,* said the hard bright thing in her, the metal-just-off-the-fire thing. *Read it before it's gone.*

She stood, took the lamp to the wall of drawers that remained, and began at the top left, the way he had taught her to begin anything: systematically, without hope of finishing, trusting the method.

Most of it was what she expected — job tickets, wax patterns, a tin of odd screws that had defeated classification for a decade. In the drawer under where the vise had been (of course; she had invented the book as a bluff, but masters leave their shops to apprentices precisely so that bluffs turn out true) she found the repair ledger, fat and cracked, thirty years of dates and signatures, and she ran her thumb down the pages until she found the observatory entries, and found what she found there, and sat back on the stool with the lamp guttering and her heart going like a detached balance wheel.

Every certification of the master clock's rate, twice yearly for twenty years — countersigned by the Guild's own timing clerk. And in the margins, in her master's small upright hand, annotations. Corrections. Little patient notes like *clerk's figure 3 sec fast per day — advised — overruled.* Twenty years of a Guild clerk certifying a clock that Oren Vael's own measurements showed was being deliberately mis-set between visits.

He had seen it. He had documented it. He had been overruled, and polite, and dead within the year.

Xanthe closed the book and held it against her chest and breathed the shop's air — oil and iron and cold tea and, faintly, still, him — and the fury came back one more time, and this time it did not drain away, because she did not put her hands on the bench. She let it stay. She let it settle into the mechanism of herself and find its gear, and felt it engage.

*Thursday,* they had said. Very well. Between now and Thursday she would copy every page of this book and hide the copies in more places than even a Guild could burn. And beyond that — because her mind, even now, could not stop being a clockmaker's mind, and was already turning over the observatory's strike train the way a tongue turns over a loose tooth, already circling the escapement, already counting the bells of the city sounding the ten o'clock hour in ragged imperfect chorus from a dozen towers — beyond that, she was beginning to suspect that a clock which can be slowed by sabotage might be made to do other things besides merely keep time.

She was blowing out the lamp when she heard the tap at the window.

Soft, twice, deliberate. Down the length of Lantern Row, hooded against the drizzle with her shuttered lantern dowsed, stood Sefa — out of her kitchen at ten o'clock at night, leaving Isra alone, which Sefa did not do for anything less than fire or blood — and even across the fog Xanthe could read the set of the lamplighter's shoulders, the way she stood square to the door instead of sidling, the way she held her pole not like a tool but like a weapon.

Xanthe opened the door.

"Isra's all right," Sefa said at once, reading her face. "Asleep on Bram's coat, Bram sitting guard, don't ask how I arranged that. It's not her." The lamplighter's breath smoked in the cold, and she glanced once up and down the empty row, and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "It's the observatory. Xanthe — Aldane's posted his own man as night keeper. He's had the clock room sealed. Nobody goes in, nobody's hours are logged, and the man's under orders—" she swallowed— "under orders that nothing is to be touched until *he* inspects the repair personally. Tomorrow, at high tide."

The fog pressed in at the lane's ends. Somewhere out on the black water a ship's bell rang twice, blind and far.

"He's not inspecting a repair," Xanthe said slowly. "He's checking his investment." And then, with the repair ledger still warm against her ribs and her master's marginal notes already rearranging themselves in her memory into a pattern, a rate of error, a *signature*: "Sefa. How long until high tide?"

"Six hours," said the lamplighter. "Give or take a quarter bell."
