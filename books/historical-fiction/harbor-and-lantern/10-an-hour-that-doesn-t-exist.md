## 10. An Hour That Doesn't Exist

Xanthe had not slept in the observatory before, and the room would not let them forget it.

The clock room sat at the crown of the tower like a heart in a glass chest, and it beat whether anyone listened or not. The great pendulum swung behind its cage of brass rods, patient as breathing. Weights hung on their cables down through the dark shaft of the tower, sunk into the building's bones, and every tick traveled along those bones and out into the stone until the whole observatory seemed to hum at the edge of hearing. Xanthe had worked on clocks all their life — had put their ear to a dozen movements small enough to hold in one palm — but they had never been *inside* a clock before. That was what this was. The master clock was not a machine that stood in a room. The room was the case, and Xanthe was a gear turning inside it.

They lit the two lamps they were permitted and set to work where they had left off: the striking train, disassembled across a bench of scarred oak, each wheel laid out in order of size like fish on a market slab. Below them Vellamare burned with lamplight, harbor and hill, and beyond the mole the black water lay flat as poured ink.

Three nights since Isra had spoken her father's name aloud. Three nights since the ledger had come apart in Xanthe's hands, page by sewn page, and shown them the shape of the trap they had been hired to arm.

*Finish the repair*, Aldane had said, with his warm gray voice. *The Guild depends upon you.*

And Xanthe had understood, somewhere between one tick and the next, that they would finish it — and finish it wrong, or rather finish it true, which would be far worse for Corvus Aldane than any sabotage.

But that was three nights ago, and tonight there was only the work, and the work had led them somewhere strange.

It began, as most dangerous things in a clockmaker's life begin, with an escapement.

Xanthe knelt at the movement frame with a loupe screwed into one eye and traced the path of the recoil escapement for perhaps the hundredth time. Escape wheel, pallets, crutch, pendulum. Simple. Honest. A mechanism whose entire purpose was refusal — the escape wheel wanted to pour its weight downward all at once, and the pallets caught it, tooth by tooth, letting it go only in measured bites, one second at a time. Every clock in creation was a torrent held back by a hand that said *not yet, not yet, not yet.*

Xanthe had always loved that about escapements. They were machines built out of withholding.

But the master clock's striking train — the separate little engine of wheels that counted the hours and hauled the hammers back and let them fall against the great bells — did not obey the going train. It answered only to a count wheel, a flat disc of brass pinned with notches around its rim, one notch per hour, twelve in all. At each hour the count wheel rotated one step, gathered the lifting piece onto the appropriate pin, and released the strike: once at one, twice at two, twelve times at midnight, and then round again, faithful as tides.

Twelve pins. Twelve hours. Vellamare had lived inside that number for two hundred years, and never once asked whether it could have been otherwise.

Xanthe stared at the count wheel, and something shifted in their chest like a weight settling into a new pivot.

*What if it struck thirteen?*

The thought arrived fully formed, the way solutions sometimes did after long labor — not built up plank by plank but simply *there*, as if it had been standing behind them the whole time, waiting to be noticed.

A thirteenth hour. An hour that appeared on no chart, no tide table, no Guild schedule, no almanac printed in any city on any coast. If the count wheel carried thirteen pins instead of twelve, then at some chosen night the master clock would strike a number that did not exist — and every bell in Vellamare that answered the master, from the cathedral down to the chapel on Pilgrim's Stair, would either fall silent or answer wrongly, and either way, the whole harbor would stop and look up.

You could not ignore a bell striking thirteen. No magistrate could rule it out of order. No clerk could fold it into a ledger and initial the margin. It was a wound in the day itself, impossible to unhear.

Xanthe sat back on their heels, heart going hard, and made themselves do the craftsman's discipline: *prove it before you love it.*

They pulled the count wheel off the bench and held it to the lamp. The rim was wide enough — barely — to take a thirteenth pin if the spacing were recalculated and the wheel re-drilled. The lifting piece would need a longer tail so the gathering palette could reach the new position. The locking fly would have to run free for one extra revolution, and the fly's vanes might need weighting so the extra strike didn't overrun. And the going train would carry on regardless — that was the beauty of it. The hands would sweep past twelve o'clock and show one o'clock as though nothing had happened, because for the *hands* nothing would have happened. Only the bells would know. Only the bells would confess.

An hour that existed in the ears alone. An hour that left no mark except in every listening person in the city.

Xanthe set the wheel down and found their hands were shaking — not with fear, or not only fear. It was the tremor that came when an idea was too big to hold still, the way a heavy weight shakes the bracket it hangs from.

Because here was the thing, and they turned it over slowly, carefully, the way you turn a blade to see which edge is yours:

Aldane needed the auction timed by this clock. The falsified tide tables, the berth auctions, the debts that drowned boatmen — all of it ran on the fiction that Vellamare's time was reliable, official, sealed. The auction clerks would open bidding at the stroke of the master clock; the tide readings had been doctored to match the clock's corrupted rate; everything Aldane had built rested on the assumption that when this clock spoke, everyone obeyed.

So let it speak. Let it speak exactly as it was meant to, honest rate restored, every second true — right up until the moment Aldane's auction opened and the clock opened its mouth and said something no one had ever heard a clock say.

A halt. Not by force. Not by breaking anything, which Aldane's men could repair or punish. But by *surprise* — the one thing his money couldn't buy back once spent. In the frozen minute after a bell strikes thirteen, a thousand people would be looking at the tower instead of their papers. And a thousand people looking up was a crowd, and a crowd was the only court that Corvus Aldane could not adjourn.

Xanthe got up, crossed to the window, and pressed their forehead briefly against the cold glass. Far below, the harbor lamps made their crooked constellation along the quays. Somewhere down there Bram and his dockworkers were keeping watch on a workshop full of tools that meant more to Xanthe than most people did. Somewhere down there Sefa was climbing her rounds with a flame cupped in her lantern like a secret. Somewhere down there Isra slept — at Sefa's lodging now, under three locks and a borrowed knife — a child who had carried a drowned man's testimony stitched into her coat seam across half a city.

They had done that. She had done that. Small people, carrying enormous weights, one measured bite at a time, like an escape wheel refusing to drop.

"All right," Xanthe said aloud, to the clock, to themselves, to nobody. "Thirteen."

The word sounded less like a number than like a door opening.

* * *

They did not sleep. Sleep could wait; ideas like this one did not keep.

By lamplight Xanthe drafted the new count wheel on paper first, as their master had taught them — Master Yorick Qiu, dead these five years in name, missing these five months in fact, his name sitting quiet and terrible on page after page of Isra's ledger in the column marked *payments received* and then, later, *payments owed*, and then, last of all, *silenced*. Xanthe had wept once, briefly and privately, over that column, and then had folded the grief away like a tool returned to its drawer, because grief without action was just rust.

*Measure twice, cut once,* the old man used to say, hands thick and gentle as a bear's, filing a wheel blank while Xanthe watched from the stool too tall for them. *The metal remembers your hurry, little wren. Every mistake you make in haste lives in the clock forever, ticking.* He'd called them little wren. He'd been the one who taught them that a clock was a promise made to strangers — that when you regulated a town clock truly, you were keeping faith with ten thousand people who would never know your name but would trust their weddings and their funerals and their ships' departures to your honesty.

Well. Here was a promise made back to him, then.

Xanthe drew the wheel at full scale, chalk-white lines on brown paper. Thirteen pins at even spacing meant twenty-seven degrees and change between them instead of thirty — awkward arithmetic, but the dividing engine at the workshop could manage it, if the workshop still stood and the engine hadn't been seized with everything else. They calculated the throw of the lifting piece, the travel of the gathering palette, the torque required for the thirteenth hammer blow against a bell already tired from twelve. The numbers came together on the page like witnesses assembling. It would work. God help them, it would actually work.

The question — the cold water thrown on the fire, and they made themselves stand in it — was *when*. A thirteenth hour struck at random was a curiosity, a rumor, a story lamplighters told. It had to fall at the exact hour the tide-auction convened, so that the halt and the testimony and the tribunal all collided in the same instant of legal chaos. Which meant Xanthe needed to know the auction's date, and the date depended on Aldane, who changed his arrangements the way other men changed shirts.

And it meant someone besides Xanthe had to know the plan. A clockmaker could be locked in a clock room — the thought arrived unbidden, with a sour taste, and they pushed it away, and it circled back, patient. If anything went wrong, if Xanthe disappeared, the thirteenth hour died with them unless it lived in someone else's understanding too. Sefa, with her network of lamps and her shameless talent for gossip disguised as weather-talk. Bram, whose dockworkers could hold a quay better than any watchman.

Telling them meant risk. Keeping silent meant fragility. Xanthe chewed the end of the chalk stick nearly to splinters and wrote, in the corner of the drawing, both answers: *Tell Sefa. Tell no one else. Not yet.*

There was another problem, and it was the ugliest one. To install the false-hour mechanism they would need hours of uninterrupted access to the striking train — drilling, fitting, adjusting, testing — and the observatory keeper's assistant, a soft-spoken young man named Pell who brought Xanthe bread and news and whose eyes lingered too long on the open movement, was in the room more and more often lately. Asking questions that were almost innocent. Noting, perhaps, the growing pile of brass on the workbench that belonged to no diagram in the maintenance book.

Xanthe looked at the door of the clock room, stout and锁ed — *locked*, they corrected themselves, stout and locked — and decided to build the thirteenth pin in two pieces, disguised. The lifting-piece extension could pass as a repair reinforcement; broken tails were common in clocks this age. The count wheel's new pin could wait until the last night, installed in minutes rather than hours, a final gesture too quick to interrupt.

Build slow. Install fast. Trust narrowly.

Their master's voice again, from somewhere: *You've the look of a man building a bomb, little wren.* And their own younger voice answering, laughing: *It's not a bomb, Master, it's a complication.* Him, chuckling: *All complications are bombs. That's why simple clocks cost less.*

Xanthe laughed out loud in the empty clock room, and the laugh cracked halfway through and became something else, and they let it, briefly, before wiping their face with a rag that smelled of oil and whiting and getting back to work.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the eastern slit of a window, and with it came Pell, knocking twice, entering with the loaf and the morning's gossip and a smile that fit him like a borrowed coat.

"Guild men at the harbor office since before light," he said, setting the bread down. "They're saying the auction's fixed for the spring tide after next. Big one. All the deep-water berths at once." He glanced toward the half-dismantled striking train and away again, quick as a pickpocket. "You'll have her finished by then?"

"The clock?" Xanthe kept their voice bored, their hands moving among the parts, sorting, covering the chalk drawing with yesterday's maintenance notes. "She'll keep better time than she has in twenty years. Whatever fool slowed her down did poor work."

"Fool," Pell agreed mildly. "Strange, though. The keeper says the slowing started the same season the new tide tables came out." He smiled his borrowed smile. "Coincidence, I'm sure. Guild doesn't like coincidences mentioned."

And he left, footsteps going down the spiral stair, and Xanthe stood very still in the ticking room with the blood draining slow and cold through them.

*The same season.* Of course. Of course, of course. The clock had not merely failed — it had been bent to serve, its rate dragged just enough to make the falsified tide tables *true by the clock's measure*, so that any boatman who checked the official tables against the official time would find perfect agreement and drown accordingly. The sabotage wasn't vandalism. It was instrumentation. Aldane hadn't broken the harbor's time. He had *nationalized* it.

Which meant the thirteenth hour was not just a stunt. It was the counter-instrument. Restore the true rate, and every forged table in the Guild's archive fell out of agreement with the master clock in front of the whole city. Strike the forbidden hour at the auction's opening, and the disagreement became undeniable, public, *audible* — a discrepancy no clerk could paper over because it rang from every tower in Vellamare.

Xanthe walked to the window. The sun was coming up properly now, gilding the harbor, lighting the masts of the little boats — Bram's people's boats — riding at the berths they would lose unless something broke. The spring tide after next. Nine days. Nine days to drill and fit and test and hide the work, nine days to tell Sefa without telling the world, nine days to keep Isra safe and the workshop unburned and their own face smooth as a dial whenever Pell came smiling through that door.

Nine days, and then an hour that didn't exist.

Fear was there — Xanthe examined it honestly, the way they examined a cracked wheel, noting the depth of the flaw. Fear of Aldane's quiet men, fear for Isra, for Sefa, for Bram, for the workshop and its seized inventory, fear of the rope they hanged counterfeiters with at Tide Gate. All real. All priced in.

And underneath the fear, rising through it like water finding a channel, something they had almost forgotten the feel of: hope. Not the cautious hope of a commission that might save the shop — that small coin was spent. This was the other kind, the dangerous kind, hope with an edge on it, hope that required things of a person. Hope as a mechanism. You built it out of pins and patience, you loaded it with weight, you set the pallets of your life against the teeth of it and said *not yet, not yet, not yet* — and then, at the appointed hour, you took the pallets away and let everything you had stored up come down at once, in one ring, on every ear in the city.

Xanthe picked up the chalk and, beneath the count-wheel drawing, added a single line to the plan, small and firm:

*When it strikes, it must strike clean. Nothing half. Thirteen, and true.*

Then they rolled the drawing, hid it inside the hollow leg of the workbench where Master Qiu had once hidden letters from a creditor, and went downstairs to find Sefa before the lamps went out for the day.

At the foot of the tower, in the shadow of the arch, a Guild rider was speaking quietly to the observatory keeper — and the keeper, nodding, was looking up at the clock room windows.

Nine days, Xanthe thought. Maybe fewer.
