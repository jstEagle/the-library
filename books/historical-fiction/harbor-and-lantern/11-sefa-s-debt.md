## 11. Sefa's Debt

The lamplighter's ladder had forty-one rungs, and Sefa Oduya knew every one of them by the sound they made. The third rung groaned like a drowned man. The seventeenth was loose in its brackets and shifted under her heel unless she came at it sideways. The fortieth whistled in a north wind, which Vellamare supplied most evenings free of charge.

Tonight she climbed them all with a knot of shame sitting in her chest like swallowed ballast.

She had been lighting lamps for thirty-one years — since she was nine years old and her father's knees gave out on the harbor stair, since before Corvus Aldane had a name worth knowing, back when the Guild magnate was just a clerk's son with ink-stained cuffs and hungry eyes. She had lit lamps through two harbor fires, one flood, three magistrates' funerals, and the long slow rot of the tide-trade. She knew the city the way only someone who walked it alone at dusk could know it: by its silences, by which doors stayed shut, by where the fog pooled thickest and what it concealed when it did.

And three nights ago, in that fog, a man named Quill Harneth had stepped out of the alley behind the chandlery and said her name.

Not *lamplighter*. Not *old woman*. Her name. Sefa Oduya, daughter of Tam, keeper of the northern circuit, sixty-two years of age and owed eleven weeks' back wages by a Guild that had stopped paying its lamplighters in the spring. He had said her name softly, the way you speak to a horse you're about to hobble, and then he had said:

*"We know about the debt on your father's lease. We know your sister's boys sleep under that roof. Every night the lamp outside the Zhou workshop goes dark, we'll knock a week off what you owe. Let it go dark often enough, and the house follows."*

She had told him nothing. That was the lie she carried up the ladder tonight, rung by rung, the lie she had told herself while her hands shook so badly she'd scorched the wick of the Fishmonger's Stair light. She had told him nothing *with her mouth*. But he had seen her face do something. He had seen the flinch, the terrible arithmetic running behind her eyes — eleven weeks of wages against Xanthe Zhou's safety, her sister's boys against a clockmaker she'd known since Xanthe was small enough to ride on their master's shoulders through the lamp-lit streets — and a man like Quill Harneth did not need words from a woman like Sefa. He needed only the flinch.

*Report what you see,* he'd said. *Or say nothing at all. Silence is also an answer.*

So now she stood at the top of the ladder beside the Saltmarket light, trimming the wick with fingers that would not steady, and looked down at the harbor road where, half a street away, a man in an oilskin coat leaned against a bollard and pretended to watch the boats. He had been there every night this week. And every night, whether she meant to or not, her feet had taken routes past the Zhou workshop's door. Past the light she was supposed to keep burning. Past the window behind which Isra — small, silent Isra, who folded her coat around the ledger like a mother folding a child — sat sewing buttons for Bram's dockworkers because Sefa had told her idle hands were unhappy hands.

Harneth's man watched the workshop. And Harneth's man watched *her*, watching the workshop, and wrote it down somewhere in his little damp book, and every entry was a week knocked off the debt, or a week added to the danger, and Sefa could no longer tell which.

The wick caught. The lamp bloomed yellow. Below, the watcher in the oilskin pushed off his bollard and strolled away toward the seawall, unhurried as a man who already had everything he needed.

Sefa stood in the small warm circle of her own light and understood, with the clarity that comes only to those who work alone in the dark, that she had already betrayed them. Not tomorrow. Not if she faltered someday. Tonight. Three nights ago. In the space of a flinch.

She closed the lamp's glass door on her trembling hands and began, for the first time in thirty-one years, to walk her route in the wrong direction — not out along the northern circuit, but inward, toward the workshop, toward the people she had been quietly selling.

* * *

The Zhou workshop smelled of oil and cedar shavings and, faintly, of the fish stew Bram had brought over in dented triumph that afternoon. It was past midnight and still every candle was lit. Xanthe Zhou sat at the long bench with a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, disassembling a striking train laid out on velvet with the obsessive neatness of a surgeon's tray. Isra slept curled in the window seat, wrapped in the infamous coat, one hand still loosely holding a needle. Bram occupied the doorway because Bram always occupied doorways — he claimed he couldn't fit in chairs, which was nonsense, he simply preferred exits.

Sefa stood in the rain-dark street a long moment before she knocked, watching them through the glass: the found family assembling itself around a stolen ledger and a broken clock, unaware that one of its lanterns had a crack down the side.

When Xanthe opened the door, they took one look at her face and said nothing. They simply stepped aside. That was the cruelty of it. They trusted her so completely that her ruin showed up plainly, the way a crack shows in good glass.

Bram straightened. "What is it?"

"I have to tell you something," Sefa said, "and then you can decide what I am."

She did not sit. She stood in the middle of the warm room, hat in her hands, rainwater dripping off her coat onto the clean floorboards, and she told them all of it. Harneth in the fog. Her name spoken soft. The debt on her father's lease — a debt she had never mentioned to any living soul, because shame compounds like interest — and her sister's boys asleep under that mortgaged roof. Eleven weeks of withheld lamplighter wages, held over her like a blade balanced on its point.

"And he didn't ask me to do anything," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "That's how they do it. He asked me to do *nothing*. To see nothing. And every night I've walked my route, I've made sure to pass this door, because passing this door is what he pays for. My silence is a report. Don't you understand? He doesn't need me to say a word. He reads me like weather. Three nights ago I flinched, and that flinch is in his book now, and everything since has been me confirming it, night after night, walking past here like a—" Her voice cracked finally, all at once, like ice going out. "Like a lamp going dark."

The stew pot ticked as it cooled. Isra had woken; Sefa saw the girl's dark eyes above the coat collar, watching, saying nothing, the way she said everything.

Xanthe set down the loupe. Very carefully. As if setting down something fragile.

"The man in the oilskin," they said quietly. "By the harbor road. He's yours?"

"He's Harneth's. Mine by purchase." Sefa laughed, and hated the sound. "Eleven weeks of wages bought him. Cheap, isn't it? Thirty-one years of lighting this city's way home, and I come cheap after all."

"Sefa." Bram crossed the room, and for one horrible moment she thought he was going to put his great hand on her shoulder, and she wasn't sure she'd survive being touched gently right then. But Bram was wiser than his size; he pulled a stool over and set it beside her instead, and sat down himself, so that for once she looked down at him. "How many nights have you passed the door?"

"Every one. Since the summons."

"And how many times have you reported anything? Spoken to Harneth again?"

"Never. I've never gone to him. I swear it on my father's—"

"We believe you," Xanthe said. "That's not the question."

It was Isra who spoke, and the whole room went still to hear it, because Isra's voice was still a rare thing, thin from disuse, careful as a child crossing ice. "He knows about the ledger?"

"No." Sefa knelt to bring herself level with the girl, kneeling on wet floorboards without caring. "I would have died in the street first. He knows I watch. He doesn't know what's here. He thinks—" and here she stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, made it the trap it was. "He thinks I'm his eyes into this house. And if I stop coming past the door, he'll know why. And if I keep coming, every visit is another thread he can follow back to you, to the girl, to the papers sewn into that coat."

Silence. Rain on the glass. The sleeping weight of the observatory clock somewhere across the water, keeping its sabotaged, lying time.

"So either way I'm the crack in the wall," Sefa finished. "Either way, you should put me out now, and bar the door, and I'll go tell Harneth I've lost interest and take whatever comes. The debt. The house. It's only bricks. My sister will curse me but my sister's cursed me before and the boys are young, they bounce—"

"Stop," Xanthe said.

It was not loud. Xanthe Zhou almost never raised their voice; they were a person who believed force belonged inside mechanisms, where it could be governed. But something in the word's construction stopped Sefa mid-sentence like a pawl dropping into a ratchet wheel.

"You walked your route in the wrong direction tonight," Xanthe said. "Toward us. In the rain, off your circuit, with your own man watching. Do you know what you did when you did that? You chose. Before you confessed, before you asked our leave, you chose us with your feet. Harneth's man saw a lamplighter break her pattern, which means by morning he'll be asking why, which means—" Xanthe's mouth curved, and it was not entirely a gentle curve. "Which means the crack in the wall is already visible no matter what we do. So the question isn't whether you've betrayed us. The question is what we build in the hole you've made."

Sefa stared. "You've lost me."

"Lamplighters," Bram said slowly, and she heard the idea arrive in his big skull like cargo hitting a dock, "walk everywhere. Every street. Every night. On schedules nobody thinks twice about, because who watches a lamplighter?" He turned to look at her, and his face had changed, the slow anger of the dispossessed reorganizing itself into something faster and brighter. "You don't report to Harneth. You report to *us*. Everything. Every watcher, every shift change, every man in an oilskin on every bollard between here and the seawall. You're not a leak in the boat, old woman. You're the lookout up the mast."

"But the debt," Sefa whispered. "The house. If they move on my sister—"

"Then they move on all of us." It was Isra. The girl had risen from the window seat, the coat hanging off her small shoulders, and her voice had steadied into something older than her years — a clerk's-daughter steadiness, a girl who had watched her father do sums for monsters and learned exactly what numbers were worth. "My father thought safety was something you could buy separately. One signature at a time. He paid and paid and it never added up to safe." She took Sefa's rough hand in both of hers. "If they touch your family, everyone in this room shows up. That's how it works. That's how it's worked since the night you took me in off your own step without asking anyone's permission. You built it. It holds you too."

Sefa Oduya had wept perhaps four times in her adult life: at her father's funeral, at her father's funeral's second, cheaper repetition when the first coffin proved to have the wrong body in it, at a wedding, and once at a particularly fine sunset she would deny to her grave. She did not weep now. But her eyes burned, and she gripped the child's hands, and she felt the shame in her chest — that swallowed ballast she'd carried up forty-one rungs and across a city in the rain — begin, at last, to change state. Shame, she understood suddenly, was only solidarity that hadn't found anywhere to put itself down.

"Well," she said gruffly, when she could trust her voice. "If I'm to be a lookout, I'll need to be seen to be useful to Harneth a few more nights. Which means I'll need something true but harmless to sell him. A movement or two. Something that keeps his man writing in his damp little book while we plan whatever it is you're planning in here, because I've lit this city long enough to know you *are* planning something, Xanthe Zhou, you get that look near clocks."

Xanthe glanced at the dismantled striking train on its velvet. Then at Isra. Then at the dark window, and beyond it the far tower of the observatory, where the master clock ticked out falsified hours over a harbor full of drowning men.

"Come here," Xanthe said. "Both of you. There's something you should know about escapements."

* * *

They talked until nearly three. Sefa learned more about anchor recoil and count-wheel detents than she had ever wished to know, and understood, by the end, less about gears than about Xanthe — about the particular ferocity of a person who mends things for a living confronting a thing broken deliberately. When at last the candles guttered, Bram went out first into the rain to walk her home, refusing all argument, occupying the street the way he occupied doorways. Isra pressed a button she had sewn — a small brass anchor — into Sefa's palm, for reasons the girl did not explain and Sefa did not ask.

Her route home took her past the harbor road one last time. The bollard where the oilskin man had stood was empty.

Empty, and chalked on the stone beside it, in a quick clerkish hand, was a single mark: a circle with one stroke through it. A tally. She had seen a thousand tallies in thirty-one years of watching walls.

One. As in: once. As in: the lamplighter passed the workshop one time tonight, off-pattern, in company.

As in: they were already counting.

Sefa stood in the rain looking at the mark until Bram's hand settled on her shoulder, and then she lifted her chin and walked on, upright, unhurried, a lamplighter with nothing left to hide and everything left to guard. Two streets on, at the corner of the Saltmarket, she paused beneath the unlit lamp she would climb to relight at dusk tomorrow, and she memorized its position exactly.

Tomorrow, when Harneth's man asked her — and he would ask — she would give him something to write down. Something true. And every true thing she sold him would be a stone in the sling the rest of them were building in secret, in a workshop that smelled of cedar, around a clock that was learning, gear by hidden gear, how to strike an hour that had never existed.

She only hoped, climbing her stairs to her sister's snoring house and her nephews' warm small breaths, that the hour came before the tally on the bollard reached the number where men like Quill Harneth stopped writing and started burning.
