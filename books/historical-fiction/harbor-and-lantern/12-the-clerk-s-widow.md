## 12. The Clerk's Widow

Isra had not been back to the Saltrow since the night her father fell from the seawall.

She had told them she didn't remember the way. That was a lie, and lying to Xanthe felt like dropping sand into the fine works of a clock — nothing seized at once, but something ground a little thinner with every turn. She remembered the way. She remembered everything: the crooked stair between the herring-salt shop and the netmender's loft where you had to turn your shoulders sideways; the drain that always smelled of low tide no matter how high the water ran; the blue door with the brass knocker shaped like a coiled rope that her father had polished every Sunday until it shone like a piece of sky caught in metal.

The blue door was gray now. Someone had scrubbed the paint off in long strokes, the way you scour a thing you want no one to recognize.

Sefa stood behind her on the stair, lantern raised. The lamplighter's flame threw their shadows up the wall ahead of them, tall and wavering, and Isra thought how strange it was that shadows were always braver than the people who made them. Bram crowded the bottom of the stairs with his great shoulders hunched under the low ceiling, and Xanthe came last, quiet as ever, carrying the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against their chest the way other people carried infants.

"You don't have to knock," Sefa said softly. "If you've changed your mind."

"I haven't changed my mind." Isra heard her own voice and was surprised by it — steady, flat, grown-up. It was the voice her mother used for counting money. "I changed my mind about pretending I don't know the way. That's different."

She knocked four times, paused, then twice more — the old pattern, the one her father had taught her when she was small enough to sit on his knee and call it a game. *When you knock like this, little bird, only friends will open.*

Nothing happened for so long that Isra began to believe the loft was empty, that her mother was another ghost added to Vellamare's long census of the drowned, and that they had climbed this stair for nothing but the pleasure of breaking her own heart thoroughly. Then, through the door, came a sound like a held breath being let out over ten months, and the latch turned.

The woman in the doorway was thin. Thin in the way driftwood is thin, weathered down to what would float. Her hair, which Isra remembered as black and heavy as wet rope, was streaked with gray and pinned up carelessly with a pencil stub. She wore a man's coat over her dress, sleeves rolled to the elbow, and there was ink on her fingers — ink on all ten fingers, fresh, as though she had been writing without stopping for days.

She looked at Sefa. She looked at Bram. She looked at Xanthe. Then she looked down at Isra, and everything in her face broke open at once, like ice going out of the harbor in springtime — sudden, groaning, unstoppable.

"Little bird," her mother whispered.

Isra had planned to say many things. She had rehearsed them in bed at Sefa's, in the dark above the lamplight shop: *You left me. You let them tell me you were dead. I carried the ledger, Mama, I carried it through the salt marshes with the dogs behind me and I never once opened it, even when it rained.* She had rehearsed the angry things because the angry things were armor, and if she wore enough armor maybe she would not dissolve on this doorstep in front of dockworkers and clockmakers and a lamplighter who smelled of lamp oil and patience.

Instead she said, "Your hair is gray," and dissolved completely.

Her mother gathered her in through the doorway and held on, and Isra discovered that her mother smelled different now — not of chalk and sealing wax and the observatory stairs, but of smoke and dried kelp and some bitter tea — but underneath the new smells her arms closed exactly the same, one across the shoulders, one cupping the back of her head, and for a while none of the rest mattered. The harbor could have flooded. Aldane's men could have marched past the window singing. Isra hung on to her mother in the doorway of the gray door and let herself be eight years old again for exactly as long as the embrace lasted, which was not nearly long enough.

Then her mother pulled back, held Isra at arm's length, and did the worst thing. She looked her over — face, hands, coat, the places a coat might hide things — with a clerk's quick inventory, and her eyes filled with terror instead of joy.

"They followed you," she said. "Of course they followed you. Come in. All of you, quickly, before the lamps are relit."

* * *

Inside, the loft had been emptied almost to the walls. One table, two chairs, a pallet in the corner, and along the far wall a sight that made Xanthe make a small involuntary sound: shelves of paper. Reams and bundles and loose drifting sheets of it, covered edge to edge in columns of numbers, tide tables and payment records and shipping manifests, all in her mother's cramped clerk's hand, all copied, Isra realized slowly, from memory — from the original ledger that had been sewn into a child's coat and sent into the dark.

"You've been copying it," Xanthe said. Their voice had the careful evenness they used around delicate mechanisms. "All this time. In case anything happened to the first copy."

"In case everything happened to the first copy." Her mother — Mara, her name was Mara, Isra had to remind herself to think of her as a person with a name and not simply as Mother returned from the dead — pulled the second chair from under the table and set it near the stove without asking anyone to sit, the gesture itself the invitation. "I was the one who taught Corvus Aldane's clerks their double-entry method. Did you know that? I taught every one of them, including my husband. I know those books the way you—" she nodded at Xanthe— "know a clockwork train. If someone alters an entry, I can hear it. A false number has a weight, a wrongness, like a coin clipped at the edge." She sat down slowly, folding her inked hands. "So yes. I copied it. Every page I could carry out of that office in my head before they noticed me missing. And now you have brought me the original, sewn into my daughter's coat like a wound dressing, and I can see from your faces that you mean to use it."

"We mean to see justice done," Bram said. His voice filled the little loft the way ballast fills a hull. "Aldane's falsified the tides. He's drowning boatmen in debt for berths priced off numbers he invented. Your husband found it. He paid for finding it."

"My husband fell," Mara said.

"The seawall is high," Sefa said quietly, "but not so high a man falls from it by accident on a calm night."

For a moment the only sound was the stove ticking and, distantly, the harbor bell counting the quarter-hour. Isra watched her mother's face during that bell-strike and saw something terrible in it: not grief, grief she had been prepared for, but calculation. Her mother was weighing something, and Isra knew with a cold drop in her stomach what it was, because she had seen the same look in her father's face the last week he was alive — the look of a person doing arithmetic with lives instead of coins.

"Yes," Mara said at last. "He was pushed. I know it. I've known it since the night the harbormaster's report called it misadventure and Aldane sent flowers to the funeral — white flowers, a whole wreath, expensive ones, the kind of expense that says *I can afford your silence*." Her voice stayed level. Only her hands moved, flattening themselves on the table as if steadying papers in a wind. "Everything in that ledger is true. Every entry. The smuggling runs out of the old salt-warehouse channel, the payments to the tide-masters, the drowned debts, the partners who vanished — Pell Harrow, the Voss brothers—" and here her eyes went to Xanthe, and Isra saw the clockmaker go very still, still the way the whole room goes still when a pendulum reaches the top of its arc— "and Master Wen Zhou, who audited the berth ledgers three years ago and asked one question too loudly at the Guild dinner, and was gone within the month, and whose workshop, I am given to understand, I am looking at right now."

Xanthe did not move for a long moment. Then they set the oilcloth bundle on the table, unwrapped it, and opened the ledger with both hands, gently, the way they touched old movements.

"Show me the entries for the spring tides," Xanthe said. "Three years back. Show me what my master found."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and Isra watched them the way she had once watched her parents work: her mother reciting figures from ten months of memory, Xanthe cross-checking them against the original ledger, Bram standing at the window because he refused to sit and Sefa sitting because she had learned, somewhere in her long life of tending flames, that watching was also a kind of work. The light through the one window went from gray to gold to gray again. Numbers crossed the table like ferries.

And Isra sat apart from all of it on the pallet, pulling threads loose from the hem of her coat — the hem that had once hidden the ledger and now hid only its absence, a long seam-line scar — and listening, and understanding, for the first time, the whole shape of the thing. Not just that Aldane was wicked. Wickedness she had understood in the dark of the salt marshes. But that her father had seen it and spoken, and died; that her mother had seen it and fled, and lost everything including her own child; that Xanthe's master had seen it and spoken, and vanished. That the ledger was not a weapon. It was a list of what seeing cost.

When the light failed, Mara lit a single candle and set it behind the stove so its glow would not show at the window, and then she did the thing Isra had been dreading all afternoon, though she had not known she dreaded it until her mother crossed the room and knelt down in front of her, taking both her hands, ink-rough palms against child-soft ones.

"Little bird. Listen to me."

"You're going to tell me to stop."

Mara flinched. It was small — a flicker, no bigger than a slipped stitch — but Isra saw it, and seeing it made her strangely calm.

"Aldane has taken the girl," Mara said to the room at large, without turning her head. "He has taken the ledger. He has given a deadline measured in tides. You are craftsmen and lamplighters and dockhands. He commands the Guild, the magistrates' purses, half the watch, and men who burn workshops and call it insurance. What is your plan? That the truth will defend itself?"

"It defended itself once already," Sefa said. "It walked out of his office in a child's coat."

"And where is that child?" Mara's voice cracked at last, and it was worse than shouting, it was the sound of a woman who had spent ten months keeping her voice perfectly level and had budgeted for no cracks. "She is sitting in front of me with salt-marsh scars on her ankles and a seam-scar in her coat and eyes like her father's, and if you carry her to a tribunal with that book, Corvus Aldane will not fall alone. He will pull the building down on everyone inside it. I have read his accounts, do you understand? I know exactly what he spends on silencing people. I have seen the line items." She turned back to Isra and gripped her hands harder. "We can go south. Tonight. There are ships out of Saltrow that don't ask names. We start over. You'll have a mother, a roof, a name that isn't ours. Isn't that worth more than—"

"Than Papa?" Isra said.

The word landed in the room like a struck bell.

"That isn't what I—"

"It's what you mean." Isra heard her voice shake and kept going anyway, because her father had kept going too, all the way to the seawall. "You want us to run so we're safe, and safe means alive, and alive means Aldane keeps selling the harbor out from under the boatmen, and more fathers fall off walls, and more children get put in coats and sent into the dark. Except next time they won't come out. I came out, Mama. The book came out. Everyone here helped me come out, and they didn't even know me." She looked around the loft — at Bram's broad back squared against the window frame, at Sefa's weathered hands folded patient in her lap, at Xanthe still bent over the ledger with candlelight sliding along the fine bones of their face. "You taught Papa the numbers. He used them. Now it's my turn to use what he gave me."

Mara bowed her head over their joined hands. For a long moment Isra thought her mother would argue again, would produce one more column of reasons, one more line item of fear. Instead she said, very quietly, "Your father said almost exactly that, the night before he died. Word for word. I begged him too, and he said, *someone has to use what I was given.*" Her thumb moved across Isra's knuckles, back and forth, back and forth, like a small pendulum finding its rate. "I have been hiding for ten months, little bird. Hiding is not the same as being safe. I had begun to forget that."

Then her head came up, and Isra saw in her mother's face the clerk return — the woman who could hear a false number across a crowded Guild hall — and it was the most frightening and most wonderful thing Isra had seen in a year.

"If we do this," Mara said, rising, turning to face all of them, "we do it as clerks do it. Not with courage alone. Courage is what amateurs bring when they haven't done the arithmetic." She swept the candle toward the shelves of copied pages. "Xanthe — you said the clock's striking train can be made to speak. Good. But a chime is only a rumor unless someone can prove what hour it tolls. Bring me Aldane's published tide tables. Any copy, any edition. If he has falsified the data, there will be discrepancies against the observatory's own historical record, and I can find them faster than his men can find this loft — provided this loft stays hidden." Her eyes settled on Sefa. "Lamplighter. You keep the lamps. You see every street twice a night. Can you move papers through this city without Guild eyes on them?"

Sefa smiled, slow as lamplight spreading. "I've been lighting the same corners of Vellamare for thirty years," she said. "There isn't a shadow in it I don't know personally."

Bram turned from the window so fast his shadow swung across the ceiling. "Boat," he said. "Say there's a boat. Say the tribunal sits at the seawall and Aldane's men hold the landward streets. My crew can hold the water. Forty years of boatmen he's tried to drown — they'd row through fire for this."

Mara was already at the shelves, pulling bundles down, sorting pages into piles with quick precise motions, and Isra watched her and understood that her mother had been waiting ten months not to be safe but to be useful, and that fear had only ever been the shape the waiting took.

It was Xanthe who stopped them all, quietly, the way Xanthe did everything. They were still bent over the ledger, and their finger rested on a line near the end of it, and their voice when it came was barely louder than the stove.

"There's an entry here I need you to confirm. Dated two nights before my master disappeared. A payment — a large one — initialled C.A., received by..." They looked up, and the candlelight showed something raw in their face. "Received by an assistant at the observatory. Someone with keys to the clock room."

Mara crossed to the table, leaned over the page, and read. When she straightened, her expression had gone cold and clean as a blade laid on ice.

"That initials stands for Corvus Aldane," she said. "And whoever received that payment was bought two nights before Wen Zhou vanished — bought, most likely, to unlock a door, or to leave one unlocked, or to swear afterward that no one strange had been seen on the observatory stairs." She met Xanthe's eyes. "Which means the person who helped make your master disappear may be standing beside that clock right now — while you build whatever it is you're building inside it."

Outside, far off across the rooftops, the harbor bell began to count the hour, and Isra counted with it under her breath, all twelve strokes, and thought: *one of them doesn't belong.*
