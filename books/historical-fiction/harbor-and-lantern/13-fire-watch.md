## 13. Fire Watch

Bram Hollis had carried barrels of salted herring up the Seawall Road since he was nine years old, and his shoulders had never once complained the way they complained now, standing in the dark outside the Zhou clockwork shop with a boat hook in his hands and rain beginning to needle down through the fog.

It was not the weight. It was the waiting.

"Third time he's passed," muttered Fen, the youngest of them, a boy of perhaps sixteen who still smelled of fish guts no matter how much lime he rubbed on his hands. He nodded toward the far end of Lantern Row, where a man in an oilskin coat had strolled by three times in the last hour, unhurried as a man taking evening air, pausing each time to look at nothing in particular across the street from the workshop's shuttered windows.

"He's counting us," said Marta Dune, who had spent thirty years splicing rope and could break a man's arm with the calm of a woman braiding hair. She sat on an overturned crate with a lantern at her feet and a marlinspike across her knees like a cat she was keeping warm. "Let him count. Let him carry the number back to whoever sent him."

"And then what?" Fen asked. "What happens when they stop sending men to look and start sending men to burn?"

Nobody answered him right away. That was answer enough.

Bram shifted the boat hook from one hand to the other and looked up at the shop. Lamplight showed faintly behind the shutters of the workroom — Xanthe Zhou was still inside, or had been an hour ago, bent over whatever small and precise devilry clockmakers did at benches at midnight. The Guild's summons had gone out days back; the Guild's men had come yesterday with their inventory books and their wax seals and their soft voices, listing Master Zhou's tools for seizure, item by item, as though a man's life's work were cargo to be weighed at the dock. And now this — the third visit that day from Aldane's insurance assessor, a thin, smiling functionary named Quill who had walked the shop's perimeter making notes about *structural deficiencies* and *fire hazards common to older buildings* and *what a shame it would be, such a loss, though of course the Guild would see the family compensated.*

Compensated. Bram knew what that word meant in Aldane's mouth. It meant the ledger pages that lived in that shop — or had lived there, before Xanthe moved them somewhere even Bram didn't know — meant the girl Isra, meant the whole unraveling thread of what Corvus Aldane had done to this harbor, all of it wrapped in oiled canvas and set alight and called an accident before the fog lifted.

"Quill came by the docks this afternoon," Bram said at last, because someone had to say it. "Talked to the harbor office. Talked to two of our own men." He watched faces turn toward him in the lamplight — Old Piet with his white beard and his bad knee; the sisters Vela and Sorrel, who co-owned a crab skiff between them; young Fen; Marta; a half-dozen others scattered down the row in doorways and shadows. "Asked them, real friendly-like, whether they'd noticed anything *unusual* about the Zhou premises. Whether the wiring looked old to them. Whether the lamp oil stored on the premises might be a danger to the neighborhood."

"Lamp oil," repeated Marta flatly.

"There's half a cask of it for the bench lamps. Always has been. Now it's a hazard." Bram spat into the gutter. "He wanted witnesses. Men who'd swear they saw smoke early, or heard cracking timbers, so nobody asks questions after. Two of ours took his coin."

Fury went around the circle quiet, the way fury does among people who cannot afford to be loud. Old Piet worked his jaw. Sorrel said something low to her sister that made Vela's hands curl into fists.

"I know which two," Bram said, before anyone could ask. "And I'll deal with them tomorrow, and it won't be the way you're hoping. But tonight doesn't matter what they promised him. Tonight matters what we do."

"And what are we doing, Bram?" Piet asked. Not mocking — genuinely asking, an old man wanting to hear a young one commit to words. "Sixteen of us and a boat hook against the Harbor Guild?"

"We're not fighting the Guild," Bram said. "We're watching a fire."

The rain thickened. Down at the harbor, the fog bells rang slow and mournful, and the tide came in over the mud flats with that long, breathing hiss that Bram had slept beside his whole life and would hear in his ears if they buried him. He thought — not for the first time since Xanthe Zhou had come down the dock steps two weeks ago with a Guild summons in hand and a lost child's coat button sewn into their pocket — that the tide kept better accounts than any magnate. It took and gave on schedule. It drowned what deserved drowning and returned what didn't. Aldane had spent years doctoring tide tables, falsifying the very water, and somehow the actual sea had let it happen, patient as stone, until a clockmaker and a silent girl started pulling threads.

"Xanthe says two nights more," Bram went on. "Two nights to finish the master clock, and then everything goes to the tribunal at the seawall — the ledger, the tables, all of it, in front of the harbormaster where Aldane can't buy the room. Two nights. So we hold the shop two nights."

"With what?" Fen gestured at the boat hook, at Marta's spike, at Piet's walking stick. "Against torches? Against Guild men with knives?"

"With being seen," said Marta. Every head turned to her, because Marta Dune rarely spoke twice in an hour and never wasted a word when she did. She rose from her crate, joints cracking like ship's rigging, and pointed the marlinspike down Lantern Row, at the lamps. Sefa's lamps. "You've all been lamplighters' children. What does Sefa always say?"

Fen, who had heard the oath recited over a hundred shared suppers, answered before he understood why: *"A lit lamp is a witness that can't be bought."*

"That's right." Marta's voice was dry as tinder herself. "Aldane needs this to be a lonely little accident, a shop that burned in the night with nobody near. So we make sure nobody in Vellamare is ever far. We keep lamps burning on this row from now till the tribunal. We keep doors open. We keep kettles hot and voices loud, and if a man comes with a torch he walks past forty eyes doing it. Fire wants darkness and privacy. We take away both."

Bram felt something loosen in his chest — not relief exactly, but the shape of it. "Sefa knows?"

"Sefa's relighting the whole district tonight," Marta said. "Every lamp on the hillside will have its wick trimmed and its glass cleaned, whether it needs it or not. She says if Aldane wants a spectacle, we'll give him one worth watching."

* * *

They settled into it the way dockworkers settle into any long shift: badly at first, then well.

Bram organized watches in pairs, two hours each, the way you'd stand a deck watch in rough weather. He put himself and Fen on first because the boy was too wound to sleep and Bram was too angry to want to. They walked the row end to end, past the cooper's yard, past the net-mender's shed, past the narrow stair that climbed toward the observatory hill where the master clock's tower stood black against the lower stars. At the corner they stopped, and Bram made Fen look a long time at the workshop — the squat stone front, the painted sign with its faded wheel-and-anchor device, the attic window where Xanthe's shadow crossed and recrossed the light.

"You remember old Master Zhou?" Bram asked.

"The one that vanished. Everyone remembers."

"I apprenticed under his neighbor, the ropemaker, when I was your age. I used to watch through the window while he worked. Little brass things, tiny as fingernails, and he'd hold them up to the light and turn them, and his face —" Bram stopped, surprised by the thickness in his own throat. "His face was like a man praying. That's what I thought, anyway, being nine and godless. Now I think maybe that's just what it looks like when someone loves their work honestly."

"And now Aldane burns it," Fen said. "For *insurance.*"

"For silence. Insurance is just the excuse he'll feed the magistrates." Bram turned the boat hook in his hands. "Here's what I need you to understand, boy, because you'll tell the others younger than you someday. I've spent my whole life letting things happen. Bad berths sold out from under honest boatmen. The Pell widow's skiff seized over a debt her husband never owed. Men I've fished alongside driven up the hill to the poorhouse while I kept my head down and my mouth shut, because a man with a family can't afford principles." He looked at the lit windows, the watched door. "I told myself the water was Aldane's and we only borrowed our living from it. But it isn't true. It's ours. We're the ones who know every fathom of it, and he's the one who lies about it on paper. Tonight is the first night in thirty years I've acted like I believe that."

Fen was quiet a moment. Then he said, "My da says the same thing about the strike year. That everyone talks about the big moments, the speeches and the riots, but really it was just men standing places they'd decided not to move from."

"Your da's a wise man," said Bram. "Stand here."

* * *

Midnight came and went with the fog rolling up off the harbor in slow gray banks that swallowed the far piers and left the nearer world close and wet and strangely intimate. The watch changed. Piet and Vela took the middle hours, then Sorrel and one of the Kell brothers. Someone's wife brought bread and hard cheese and a jug of chicory coffee that tasted like scorched rope but was hot, which mattered more. A cat adopted the doorway of the workshop itself and sat washing its face in the lamplight with an air of ownership that made even Piet laugh.

At the deep hour, near two by Bram's reckoning, the workroom light went out and Xanthe Zhou came down into the street, coat collar up, eyes shadowed with the particular exhaustion of someone who has been staring at small things too long. They carried a covered dish of cold rice someone had left them and ate standing up, leaning against their own doorframe like a sailor against a rail.

"You should sleep," Bram said.

"So should you." Xanthe's voice was rough. They looked down the row at the lamps — at Sefa's lights burning in every bracket, at the open doors, at Marta darning a net by lamplight as if she'd simply chosen an unusual place to do her mending. "I heard singing earlier. Down by the cooper's."

"That was the Kells. They sing when they're nervous."

"They're nervous?"

"They're dockworkers standing between a magnate and his fire," Bram said. "Of course they're nervous. Singing helps."

Xanthe looked at him for a moment, and something moved behind their eyes — that strange clockmaker's stillness, as though they were measuring the interval between two ticks. "Why are you doing this?" they asked. "Truly. The shop isn't yours. I'm not — I'm not one of you. I've been in Vellamare six years and I still measure my life in things I could carry out the door in one trip."

Bram considered the question seriously, because it deserved that. "Do you know what I measured my life in, most years? Tides and debts. What came in, what went out, what I owed and to whom." He nodded at the workshop behind them, at the dim shape of the bench visible through the shutter slats, the tools laid out in their worn grooves. "Then a clerk falls off a seawall and a girl turns up on your step carrying the truth sewn into her coat, and I find out I'm not the man I thought. Turns out when the moment came I couldn't walk past. None of us could. You don't get to choose your family, Xanthe, but sometimes it chooses you — a girl at a door, a lamplighter with an oath, a dock full of people Aldane cheated. You've been one of us since the night you took that child in out of the rain. You just haven't finished noticing."

Xanthe said nothing. But Bram, who had raised two children of his own and knew the difference between silence that closes a conversation and silence that holds something too heavy to lift yet, let it be. After a while Xanthe went back inside, and a few minutes later the workroom light kindled again, and the shadow resumed its patient crossing of the window.

* * *

The trouble came at half past three.

Fen saw it first — he had the sharpest eyes of any of them, honed on distant buoys — and instead of shouting, which was what Bram had told them *not* to do, he began to sing. Loudly. Badly. An old hauling shanty with a chorus built for forty throats, and inside four bars the Kell brothers had taken it up, and Marta had risen from her mending with the marlinspike in her fist, and every lamp on Lantern Row seemed to lean toward the north end of the street where three figures had stopped, mid-stride, in the fog.

They were Guild men — Bram knew the cut of their coats, the Guild's anchor badge dull on their collars. One carried a cask under his arm, and even in the bad light Bram knew the shape of a five-gallon oil drum, and knew whose cellar it had come from, and felt his blood go cold and then very hot.

The singing swelled. Doors opened along the row — not slammed, opened, deliberately, householders standing in their thresholds with candles, with fire pokers, with children peering around their legs. From the hill above, faint but unmistakable, another bell began to ring: not the fog pattern, something quicker, brighter. Sefa's signal, relayed from some higher street. The whole city seemed to be waking and looking downhill.

The three men stood in the middle of it, and Bram walked toward them alone, unhurried, boat hook grounded like a halberd.

"Evening," he said pleasantly, when he was ten feet off. "Lost?"

The one with the cask — Quill's man, the same heavy-shouldered fellow who'd taken the inventory two days before — looked at Bram, then at the lamps, the doors, the singing dockworkers gathering behind their song leader, and did the arithmetic every coward does and hates himself for. "Wrong street," he said.

"Looks like it," Bram agreed. "That's a heavy thing to be carrying the wrong way. Mind you don't drop it. Oil's dear these days." He smiled, and it was not a kind smile, and around him forty ordinary people — crabbers and coopers, net-menders and grandmothers, a boy who sang flat — stood in the fog with their lamps lit, and none of them moved, and none of them needed to.

The men left. The crowd watched them go until the fog took them, and then, quietly, the singing stopped, and everyone breathed, and someone laughed, high and shaky, and it might have been Fen.

Marta came to stand at Bram's shoulder, watching the empty street. "They'll come back smarter," she said. "Fewer men, later hour, from the roof line maybe. Or they'll not come at all and do something worse instead."

"I know."

"Aldane won't risk a second failure with the tribunal two days out. If he moves again, he'll move big."

"I know that too." Bram rolled his shoulders, feeling the old barrel-hauling ache settle in familiar as an old coat. "So we don't sleep. All of us. Till it's done."

They held the watch through what remained of the night. Toward dawn the rain quit, the fog thinned to pearl, and the sky over the harbor went the color of a fresh-scraped hull. Bram stood in the street with his hands empty — he'd given the boat hook to Piet hours ago and forgotten to miss it — and watched the light come up on Lantern Row, on the unburned shop, on the lit lamps guttering out one by one as morning made them unnecessary.

And it was then, in that gray and gentle hour, that a runner came puffing up from the harbor stairs — one of Sefa's youngest, barefoot, breathless, holding a folded scrap of paper like it might ignite in her hand.

"From the observatory," she gasped. "From Xanthe. They say — they say the assistant talked. Aldane has the girl."
