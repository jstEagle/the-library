## 14. Sabotage Reversed

The clock room at midnight was a country unto itself.

Xanthe had closed the great door against the observatory's corridors hours ago, wedged a wedge of beech under it that no passing guard could fail to notice — *the apprentice works late, the apprentice obeys* — and then stood for a long moment in the drum of silence beneath the master clock, listening to the lie of it. The pendulum swung. It had been swinging, more or less faithfully, since before Aldane's men first put their thumbs on the mechanism, and it would go on swinging after everyone in Vellamare now alive was dust. That was the comfort and the horror of clocks: they outlasted their liars.

"Right," Xanthe said aloud, to the room, to the gears, to Master Ferren's ghost which had followed them up the hill and refused to leave. "Let's find what they did to you."

They lit three lamps instead of one. Two for working light, hung on the brass hooks the old horologist had set into the stone a century ago, and one — this was superstition, and Xanthe knew it, and did it anyway — placed low on the floor near the base of the case so that anyone crouching in the dark corners where the sabotage had surely been done would have to work in the same honest brightness Xanthe worked in. If there were watchers among the shadows, let them be watched.

Then they began.

The diagnosis had been half finished for days; tonight was for certainty. Xanthe climbed the ladder into the clock's interior with the countwheel cradled above their head and went at the going train the way a physician goes at a body whose symptoms are known but whose wound is hidden. First the escapement. They examined each pallet tooth under the lamp with a loupe, running a fingertip along the steel, feeling for the almost-nothing of a filed edge. Nothing. Then the suspension — the thin spring from which the pendulum hangs, that innocent-looking ribbon of metal. A clock's rate lives or dies there, in its effective length, and a saboteur clever enough to slow a master clock without breaking it would not touch the pendulum rod itself, which any fool could measure. They would touch the spring.

Xanthe found it within the hour.

There — a second leaf, brazed over the true spring, so fine a shim of hardened brass that at a glance it looked like nothing but age, like patina, like the honest corrosion of forty years. But it was new metal under the loupe; the crystalline grain of fresh braze caught the lamplight differently than old. Someone had lengthened the pendulum's effective reach by a whisper — a whisper, repeated sixty times a minute, eight-sixty-four thousand times a day, became minutes lost, became tides mispredicted, became berth auctions held when the water was two feet shallower than the tables claimed and small boatmen signing contracts against data that had been quietly, mechanically murdered.

"They made you an accomplice," Xanthe said softly, resting a hand on the pendulum rod, warm now from their own palm. "It wasn't your fault. None of it was your fault."

The words came out stranger than intended, thick. Xanthe swallowed and got back to work.

Removing the shim without evidence was the trick. Anyone could cut it away and leave a scar that Aldane's people would read as confession. So Xanthe heated the braze joint with a spirit lamp no larger than a thimble, working by feel through a folded leather glove, coaxing the shim free at exactly the temperature that would leave no discoloration on the surrounding brass. It took ninety minutes. When the second leaf finally lifted away whole, curling slightly like a dead moth's wing, Xanthe sat back on the ladder step and shook hands that had gone rigid with concentration, and laughed once, quietly, at nothing.

*He'd have done it faster,* they thought, and the thought had no bitterness left in it anymore, only weight. *But not better.*

* * *

By the second lamp-oil change the clock was keeping true time again — Xanthe checked it against the star transit through the observatory's shuttered meridian slit, twice, because a repair done once is an opinion and a repair done twice is a fact — and the honest tick filled the room like breathing. Any other night, any other life, Xanthe would have packed the tools and slept three hours in the chair and been proud.

Instead they cleared a space on the bench, laid out a square of clean linen like a surgeon's cloth, and began building the thing they had no commission to build.

An hour that does not exist.

The idea had come to them three nights ago in the borderland between waking and sleep, the way the true ideas always did, arriving uninvited and fully formed like a bird through a window. A striking train is a machine for counting hours and announcing them: the countwheel turns, lifts levers, releases weights, and the bells speak the number. Every clock in Vellamare answered to the master's twelve hours. Twelve was law. Twelve was tide-tables and contract clauses and the Guild's whole architecture of legitimacy, all of it standing on the assumption that the master clock could only ever say what the day allowed it to say.

But the countwheel didn't know that. The countwheel knew only counts.

If a secondary cam were fitted to the striking train — a cam that could be engaged at will, overriding the normal count, driving the lift levers through a sequence no countwheel ever encoded — the clock could strike thirteen. Or strike eleven and then strike again. Or hold its tongue at the top of the hour entirely, a silence so profound in a harbor city built on bells that every ship, every clerk, every auctioneer would look up at once. An hour that doesn't exist is not just a wrong number. It is a hole in the law of time itself, and holes in law are where testimony gets heard.

Xanthe drew it first in chalk on the bench top, then in graphite on paper, then committed it to memory and burned the paper in the spirit lamp, watching the diagram curl orange and vanish. What remained lived now behind their eyes, and in their hands.

The parts had to come from nowhere. That was the discipline of it, the game, the prayer: build a forbidden mechanism out of materials whose absence no inventory would notice. Xanthe harvested a worn escape wheel from the drawer of failures — Ferren's drawer of failures, kept religiously, every ruined part saved because *a mistake is a part you haven't found the use for yet*, the old man used to say, and here he was, two years drowned in the harbor ledger's silence, still teaching. The spring stock came from offcuts. The cam blank was a broken fly-fan arbor, filed down through an hour of patient, wrist-aching work until the lobes rose under the file tip exactly where Xanthe wanted the future to bend.

Somewhere past three in the morning the trance took them.

It was familiar country, this place beyond tiredness, and Xanthe entered it the way sailors enter fog — noticing the border only afterward, from inside. The lamps burned low and gold. The file sang its small song. Time stopped being a sequence and became a material, something to be worked, thinned, shaped; Xanthe filed and fitted and tested and filed again, and each motion was perfect because each motion was the only thing in the world. There was no Aldane in the trance. No ledger, no seawall, no Isra asleep upstairs wrapped in Sefa's spare coat with her fists curled under her chin like she was holding something even in dreams — though she wasn't holding anything anymore, because the ledger lay now in three places, sewn copies, distributed, and Isra herself had said, with the terrible practicality of children, *"Now you can't lose me and keep it."*

No. In the trance there was only steel and the shape it wanted to become.

This was what love looked like when Xanthe made it. They had understood that somewhere in the last week, watching Sefa trim a wick with the same total attention, watching Bram splice a rope. Precision was the language some people spoke love in because the ordinary words broke under the weight. Every thousandth-of-an-inch Xanthe shaved from the cam's rising face was a sentence in that language, addressed to Isra, to Ferren, to the boatmen whose names they'd never know, to the harbor itself: *I will make this exact, because you deserve things that are exact, because vagueness is what men like Aldane hide inside.* The false-hour mechanism was a letter written in escapement steel, and it said: *when the moment comes, I will not miss.*

Toward dawn the assembly was complete on the linen cloth, small and ugly and perfect — a fistful of mismatched salvage arranged into a device that could crack the Guild's authority like a knuckle. Xanthe fitted trial assemblies to the striking train without fastening anything, dry-running the geometry with oiled fingers, learning the installation blind so that when the real moment came they could do it in minutes, maybe seconds, possibly in the dark, possibly with hands shaking.

The last test was the one that mattered. Xanthe disengaged the main drive, mounted the mechanism, and released it by hand, slowly, feeling the levers rise.

The hammer lifted. Held. Hung at the top of its arc for one impossible heartbeat —

— and struck. Once. A single deep note rolled through the clock room and out through the bones of the tower, soft as the mechanism was, but Xanthe heard it in their sternum anyway, felt it in their teeth. Thirteen, whispered. Thirteen, rehearsed. Somewhere below in the sleeping city, a dog barked and fell silent, and Xanthe sat very still in the lamplight with their heart going hard, half exalted and half afraid, like someone who has spoken a name they were taught never to say.

"All right," they breathed. "All right. We can do this."

* * *

They cleaned the room as the sky outside the meridian slit went the grey of old pewter. Tools back in their rolls, filings swept and folded into paper — filings tell stories to people who know how to read them, and Xanthe had learned recently to assume everyone was reading. The harvested shim from the sabotage they did not destroy. They wrapped it in waxed cloth and sewed it into the hem of their coat with sailmaker's stitches, next to the copy of Isra's ledger pages that already lived there. Evidence of the crime, and evidence of the correction, carried together, one against the Guild and one for the defense. Whatever happened, whoever searched them, the truth would be on their body.

That was when Xanthe noticed Pell.

The junior assistant had been around the observatory for a week now — hired suddenly, recommended from somewhere inside the Guild, a mild young man with ink-stained cuffs and an obliging manner, the sort of person who was always carrying something for somebody. He was supposed to have gone home at dusk. But through the gap under the clock-room door, in the grey pre-dawn light, Xanthe saw his shadow pause in the corridor. Stop. Turn toward the door. Remain there long enough for the silence to acquire a shape.

Then his boots moved away, unhurried, down the stairs toward the harbor.

Xanthe stood in the doorway a long moment, one hand flat against the clock case, feeling the mechanism's warmth through the wood like the flank of a large animal. The trance was gone entirely now, rinsed away by cold adrenaline, and in its absence the night's accounting arrived all at once: the mechanism hidden, the clock restored, the evidence stitched into cloth — everything ready, everything nearly safe — except that a mild young man with ink-stained cuffs had just listened at their door and was descending, at this exact moment, toward the one man in Vellamare who paid for information about doors.

Xanthe looked at the clock. The clock, honest now to the second, ticked steadily onward into the new day, indifferent, faithful, doomed perhaps, and Xanthe thought with sudden clarity: *I have until the next tide.*

They picked up their tool roll and ran for the stairs.
