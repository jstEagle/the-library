## 16. The Long Pendulum

They locked him in with the clock at the turn of the tide, and Xanthe thought, not for the first time, that there were worse cells. There was warmth here, of a kind — the great case breathed heat from the weight-shaft, and the oil-and-brass smell was the smell of every good day of his apprenticeship. There was light: three lamps, set where Pellam wanted them, throwing his shadow huge against the pendulum's white arc. There was even a stool, a cot, a jug of water, a covered tray that he had no appetite for.

There was a guard at the door, and another on the gallery above, and Pellam himself came and went like weather, smiling his thin smith's smile, checking the work as though Xanthe were an apprentice again and might, at any moment, file a pivot too deep out of simple spite.

Which was not, Xanthe admitted to himself in the small hours, entirely outside the realm of possibility.

*Work,* Aldane had said through Pellam, standing below the clock room's window in the blue dark like a man inspecting a horse he intended to have shot in the morning. *Finish it. Make it true. And when it is finished, you will walk out of here, and your shop will stand, and the girl will be fed — if you are silent, and quick, and do nothing clever.* He had said *the girl* the way other men said *a shipment*, and Xanthe had looked at his own hands so that his face would not do anything expensive.

So: work. The striking train lay open before him like a patient on a table, wheels lifted, arbors cleaned, and Xanthe bent over it with the file and the burnisher and let the craft close over his fear like water over a dropped coin. This was the mercy of the trade, the thing his master had known and taught without ever saying it plainly: that precision is a kind of prayer, and while you are praying you cannot also be drowning.

The clock had been made honest again — that part was done, three nights past, the sabotage unwound gear by gear, the slowed escape wheel replaced, the rate corrected against the transit stars until the master and the sky agreed within two seconds a day. What remained was the finishing: polishing, adjusting, the hundred small obeisances a fine mechanism demands before it will perform.

And what remained besides that.

Xanthe glanced up at the guard by the door — a broad man called Odo who chewed something steadily and looked bored, and boredom was the only door in this room that wasn't locked. Then up at the gallery, where the second man sat with a lantern turned low, mostly asleep. Then down, at the striking train.

Twelve bells. Twelve strokes on the hour, run by a counting wheel with twelve pins, each pin lifting the gathering pallet once. It was the simplest music in the world, and like all simple things, it could be lied to beautifully.

His master — Merren Zhou, dead four years now in everything but paperwork, vanished off the seawall the way inconvenient men were always vanishing off seawalls in Vellamare — his master had loved one piece of theory above all others and had drilled it into Xanthe with the patience of a man planting an orchard he'd never sit under. *Anyone can make a clock strike,* Merren used to say, tapping the counting wheel with his smallest screwdriver. *Any fool can count to twelve. But listen, boy — the striking train is the only part of a clock that speaks. Everything else just turns. If you ever needed a clock to say something — truly say it — you'd build the message into the strikes themselves.*

Xanthe had been eleven. He had asked when anyone would ever need a clock to say something. Merren had laughed and said, *God willing, never,* and ruffled his hair with an oily hand, and Xanthe had spent a week washing the oil out.

He understood now. Four years late, in a locked room under guard, with the tide running out toward his deadline and Isra held somewhere in the Guild's long fingers — he understood it the way you understand a bridge only when you must carry someone across it.

The counting wheel had twelve pins. But between the pins — between the twelfth stroke of the hour and the first stroke of the next — there lived the locking plate, the fan fly, the little constellation of detents and levers that decided when the train was released and how long it stayed free. A wheel added there would not change the hour struck; it would change only the *spaces*. The pauses. The silence between notes, which any musician knows is half the music.

A message in the silences. Short gap, long gap, double gap. Lamp-language. Sefa's language — the lamplighters of Vellamare spoke across whole districts in shuttered lanterns, and Sefa had taught Xanthe the harbor code one winter on the seawall, laughing, saying, *You never know when you'll need to tell the whole quay something the constables can't hear.*

*A name, a place, an hour — and the word that meant: come.*

Xanthe took a fresh brass blank from his own kit — they had left him his tools, oh yes, they wanted the work done well — and began to file.

* * *

It could not be hurried. That was the cruelty of it and, in the end, the safety of it. A hidden wheel in a striking train must be cut to tolerances finer than a hair's breadth or the whole train binds and the clock stands dumb, and a dumb clock would tell Aldane instantly that something was wrong. So Xanthe worked slowly, openly, honestly — polished the bell hammer's shoe, trued the fly, adjusted the gathering pallet to a whisper — all the legitimate labor of a craftsman finishing a masterpiece. And threaded through it, stitch by stitch the way Isra had stitched her father's ledger into her coat, the false work: the extra wheel, its arbor, its cam.

The cam was the heart of it. One revolution per day, timed so that at the third hour after midnight — the hour the auction would convene, the hour Aldane had chosen because the tide tables he'd falsified put high water exactly then — the cam's lobe would drop a single lever, and the lever would unlock the space-wheel, and the spaces between the twelve strokes would stop being spaces and start being words.

Three short, three long. *Mer-ren.* His master's name, spelled in lamp-code, spoken by a bell to whoever in Vellamare still knew how to listen. Then the pause-pattern that meant *observatory* — Sefa's shorthand, *the tall place, the eye of the city*. Then the hour: *three*. Then, spaced apart like a heartbeat, like a sewn seam, the oldest signal in the harbor code, the one fishermen's wives flashed from headlands when a boat was coming home safe:

*Come.*

Xanthe filed the cam's lobe by lamplight, testing it against a wax blank, and found his eyes stinging, and did not stop. Grief is a poor craftsman's excuse, Merren used to say. Feel it after. He would feel it after. He would feel all of it — the master who had died for a ledger's worth of truth, the child who had carried that ledger sewn against her ribs, the shop with Guild seals on its doors — after, if there was an after. Now there was only the lobe of a cam, and the angle of its rise, and the depth of its cut, which must be exact, which must be *perfect*, because somewhere across the sleeping city a woman with gray in her braids would be standing in the dark at three in the morning listening to a bell, and either she would hear a name or she would hear nothing, and everything hung on the difference.

"Talking to yourself?" Odo said from the door.

"Counting," Xanthe said. "Pins."

Odo grunted. Boredom held. Good.

Xanthe counted pins. There were twelve, and he hated every one of them, and he loved them, because they were going to betray Corvus Aldane in the most polite possible way — by doing exactly their job, striking the hours, keeping perfect time, while hiding a rebellion in the rests.

* * *

On the second night Pellam brought a visitor.

Xanthe heard the boots on the iron stair before he saw them — two sets, one familiar and shuffling, one new, deliberate, expensive. He kept his eyes on the escapement. He had learned that much in Aldane's presence: keep your hands busy and your face empty, and the powerful will talk over you as though you were furniture, and furniture hears everything.

"—striking true, you say." Aldane's voice had not changed since the observatory visit. Warm as mulled wine, cold as the bottom of the harbor. "Every hour."

"Within a hair," Pellam said. "The boy does honest work, I'll grant him that."

"Oh, honesty." A soft laugh. "The most negotiable of virtues. I've bought more of it than any other commodity in this port." Boots crossed the floor. Xanthe watched a shadow fall across the open train and kept filing. "How long?"

"Two tides. Three at the outside. Then it will run for a century."

"A century is more than I need. I need a fortnight. After the auctions consolidate the berths, the clock can do whatever clocks do." A pause, and then, closer, almost gentle: "Zhou."

Xanthe looked up. Aldane stood over the striking train with his hands clasped behind his back, magnificently tailored, utterly at ease, a man in his own house even in a stranger's clock room. His eyes went over the disassembled wheels with the flat assessment of a buyer.

"You're wondering," Aldane said, "whether I know."

Xanthe said nothing. Saying nothing was a skill; he was getting practice.

"That the girl's little coat contained more than a child. That your master's name appears in her father's book. That I have read it — every page — and burned my copy, and that the pages you imagine are hidden elsewhere are ash, wherever they are." Aldane smiled. "I'm telling you I know. Because men do foolish things when they believe they have secrets. Men with no secrets only work." He straightened. "Finish the clock. Strike my auction hour. And when the tide turns tomorrow night, you'll walk down these stairs, collect your tools, and find your shop untouched and the girl returned to — well. To whatever the harbor decides to do with orphans." He moved toward the stair, then paused, as though remembering something pleasant. "Your master begged, near the end. Did you know? Not for money. For his name. Asked me to leave it out of the record so his apprentice wouldn't inherit the shame." A shrug, silk moving on silk. "I granted it. You see? Honest men and I understand one another perfectly."

The boots went down the stair. Pellam lingered, gave Xanthe a look that might have been warning or might have been something else — Pellam's face had only ever had one setting, and Xanthe had stopped trying to read weather in stone — and followed.

The door shut. The bar fell.

Xanthe sat very still among the wheels and let the fear finish its pass through him, wave after wave, until it ebbed. *Burned my copy.* Perhaps. Aldane burned copies the way he granted names: cheaply, strategically, and never quite all. Isra's mother was alive — the widow had confirmed the ledger, confirmed the codes, confirmed the payments, before she'd gone back into hiding begging them to stop. The widow's testimony existed. Bram's dockworkers had stood fire watch on the workshop all night with hammers and hooks and the particular stubbornness of men who owned nothing but their word. The ledger's *contents* could not be burned, because they had walked out of Aldane's world on a child's legs and scattered themselves into a dozen harbor hearts.

But none of it mattered — none of it could gather, could converge, could arrive at the same hour in the same place — unless the harbor learned when and where. And Xanthe was the only line out of this room, and Xanthe was guarded, and Xanthe's hands were the only hands allowed near the one voice in Vellamare that spoke to the whole city at once, every hour, whether anyone was listening or not.

Aldane thought he had confiscated a secret. He had actually confiscated a bell tower.

Xanthe wiped his eyes on his sleeve, took up the cam, and went back to work.

* * *

The last night, they left him alone with it.

Perhaps it was confidence — the clock ran, the rate held, the auction hour approached, why waste a man's wages sitting on a stair? Perhaps it was contempt, which was only confidence wearing better clothes. Whatever it was, by midnight Odo had gone, the gallery man had gone, and Pellam had barred the outer door from the outside with instructions that Xanthe should sleep, since a trembling hand served no one.

Xanthe slept for exactly as long as it took to be sure the footsteps had faded.

Then he worked. The space-wheel went in at the quarter-bell — a wheel no bigger than his palm, riding a stud he'd soldered into an old tap hole, invisible unless you knew to look and impossible to see at all behind the fly. He cut the final depth on the cam by touch, lamplight being too conspicuous to raise fully. He assembled the train blind, by feel, in the dark, the way Merren had once made him do for a whole winter, complaining, *"Someday the lamps will fail, boy, and the clock must not."* Someday had come. The clock would not.

At some hour past two, with the tide audibly turning in the harbor below — a long sigh against the seawall, the sound of the deadline arriving — Xanthe set the last bridge, seated the last jewel, and wound the striking weight to the top of its barrel.

The pendulum swung. The escapement ticked, honest, beautiful, metronomic. Below him the observatory's lesser dials all agreed, and somewhere beneath them the whole city of Vellamare slept in agreement too, on Aldane's clocks, by Aldane's time, bound for Aldane's auction.

*Xanthe pressed his ear to the case and waited for three o'clock.*

When it came, the bell did not disappoint. Twelve strokes would come at noon; tonight was only the single stroke of three — but the space-wheel turned beneath it even now, and tomorrow at this hour, at the auction's opening, it would speak: three short, three long, *the tall place*, *three*, *come*. A name sewn into silence. A summons no magistrate could subpoena and no guard could search, traveling out over the roofs of Vellamare at the speed of sound, to every ear that knew the old code — and one ear in particular, belonging to a lamplighter who owed Xanthe nothing anymore, having already confessed her blackmail and been forgiven, and who therefore owed Xanthe everything.

Unless she didn't hear it. Unless the wind took it, or the harbor noise buried it, or Sefa simply slept, human and tired, three streets away behind shuttered windows.

That was the flaw in the whole beautiful design, and Xanthe could do nothing about it now. The message was in the metal. It would be struck whether anyone heard or not. He had built his defiance into brass where no betrayal could reach it, and the rest belonged to the city.

Xanthe sat back on the stool in the dark, hands finally still, and listened to his own heartbeat slow to match the pendulum.

Below, faint through the stone, the harbor bell rang the half-hour — and then, strangely, rang again, a stutter, a doubled stroke, wrong, urgent. Then a third time. And beneath it, rising up the seawall and through the observatory foundations, came a sound Xanthe knew from fire watches and press-gang riots and every bad night the harbor had ever produced:

Boots. Many boots. On the stair.

The bar on the outer door began to lift.
