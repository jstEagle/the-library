## 18. The Thirteenth Hour

The Exchange Hall had been built to make small men feel large. Its dome rose ninety feet into a painted sky, gilded clouds and fat cherubs, and beneath it the Harbor Guild had arranged its auction like a wedding: white bunting on the railings, lanterns trimmed and lit, clerks in fresh ink-stained cuffs seated at long tables with the berth ledgers stacked before them like loaves. Corvus Aldane stood on the dais in dove-grey wool, one hand resting on the rail as though the whole hall were a vessel he was steadying against weather.

Bram Oduya stood in the crowd with his arms crossed and his heart going like a maul.

He had dressed for it — his good coat, the one with the brass buttons his mother had sewn back on twice — because Aldane wanted the dockworkers present. Wanted them visible, standing in their dozens along the walls while their berths were sold out from under them, so they would learn how the world worked. So they would go home quiet.

Bram had counted forty-one of them before the doors even opened. Sefa's lamplighters drifting in twos and threes. Netmenders from the east quay. The oyster women, who could shout down a gale when they chose. Old Pell, who hadn't climbed a ladder in six years, standing straight as a mast near the front. And threaded through all of it, moving without hurry, the look that passed between them: *not yet. Wait.*

On the dais, a clerk struck a small bell. "Honored members of the Guild, masters of vessels, holders of berths—" The words went on, smooth as poured oil. *By order of the Harbor Guild, acting under its charter, the winter tide-auctions are opened.* Bram felt the anger rise in him like water in a lock, slow and enormous, and he let it, because Wren—because Xanthe had told them what the anger was for. It was ballast. It kept you upright in a swell.

Aldane stepped forward. He did not raise his voice; he never needed to. "Gentlemen. This morning we sell certainty." A ripple of appreciative laughter from the Guild benches. "The tides have been measured, tabulated, and certified by this Guild's own observatory. What you purchase today is not mud and pilings. It is the future, guaranteed."

*Certified by this Guild's own observatory.* Bram looked at the great clock high on the eastern wall of the hall, its face pale as a drowned moon above the bunting. Xanthe was up there. Had been up there since before dawn, locked in the clock room with two of Aldane's men on the door, finishing the work they'd been forced to finish. Somewhere behind that painted face, gears were turning that would either drown forty families or save them.

Bram's hand found the coin in his pocket — the harbor token, worn smooth, that every one of them carried now. Not yet. Wait.

"Let us begin," said Aldane, "with Berth Fourteen."

And the master clock began to strike.

* * *

Xanthe Zhou heard the first blow of the hammer from inside the case itself, felt it through their palms where they pressed flat against the great wheel, and thought, absurdly, of their master's voice: *You'll know it's right when the strike sounds like a sentence, not a cough.*

Twelve strokes. That was noon. The auction would open on twelve strokes, the clerks would read the tide tables, the falsified tables, and Berth Fourteen — old Marta's berth, where her sons had moored their skiff for thirty years — would be sold to some Guild favorite for a third of its worth, and the debt would begin to grow like coral.

Xanthe's fingers rested on the mechanism they had built through the night: a small assembly of levers and a cam cut from brass stock, hidden in the shadow of the striking train where no inspector had thought to look, because no honest clock had any reason to look there. One lever. One choice. They had imagined this moment a hundred times, and in every imagining their hands shook.

They did not shake now. That was the strange mercy of it. The work held them steady, the way it always had. Twelve years of apprenticeship lived in these hands, and every hour of it had been leading somewhere other than where Master Fen had intended — but leading, all the same.

*He's listening,* Xanthe thought. *Wherever he is, whatever happened to him, he's listening.* And if the Guild had silenced him for knowing too much, then let this be the answer. Let the clock speak for him, since he could not.

Below, faint through the floor, Aldane's voice: "—the certified low-water mark, as measured—"

Xanthe moved the lever.

The gathering wheel, which had spent the last hour winding itself tight for exactly twelve blows, released past its locking pin and caught on the new cam — and kept turning. In the dark of the case, in the smell of oil and dust and their own sweat, Xanthe watched the count-wheel rotate past XII, past the blank space where no hour lived, and settle onto the tooth they had cut themselves, filed smooth over three nights with a needle file no longer than a finger.

The hammer drew back.

Thirteen.

The sound went through the hall like a crack through ice.

* * *

Sefa was halfway up the aisle with her lighting pole disguised as a walking staff when the thirteenth stroke fell, and she stopped dead, and she laughed — one sharp bark she couldn't hold in — because she had helped build this and still it landed on her like a wave.

One stroke too many. From the master clock. The clock that *was* time in Vellamare, that every ship's chronometer was set by, that the tide tables were stamped against, that courts and contracts and funerals all answered to. It had just declared an hour that did not exist.

For three full seconds the hall made no sound at all. Then it made every sound.

"What in the deep—" A clerk dropped his pen. On the Guild benches, men were on their feet. Someone shouted that the clock had broken, someone else shouted that clocks didn't break like *that*, and a lawyer near the front — hired, Sefa knew, by the oyster women, paid for with coins scraped together over months — was already saying, loudly, in the carrying voice lawyers keep folded away for exactly such moments: "I must object! If the Guild's own master clock is in error, then *every measurement certified by it is in question*. Every table. Every lot!"

"There is no error," Aldane said, and his voice was still smooth, but Sefa had known powerful men her whole life and she heard the seam in it, the place where the silk was pulled too tight. "A mechanical fault. We will proceed—"

"On what authority?" the lawyer asked pleasantly. "Your charter requires auctions be timed by the observatory standard. The observatory standard has just struck thirteen. Which hour is it, Magnate? Noon? Or the hour after? Because I see no law governing the hour after."

Laughter — real laughter, ugly for Aldane, rolling up from the walls where the dockworkers stood. Bram caught Sefa's eye across the crowd and touched his pocket. *Now.*

And then the bells began.

It was the signal they had planned, but hearing it was another thing entirely. Out in the city, the harbor chapel's bell took up the count — thirteen strokes, deliberate, unmistakable — and then Saint Ilva's tower answered, and then the fish market bell, and the fire bell on Customs Quay, rung by lamplighters who had volunteered for the rope, and then, impossibly, the little cracked bell on the pilot boat *Kestrel* out in the roads, ringing and ringing, and every bell in Vellamare declaring the same impossible hour, until the sound came through the stone walls of the Exchange Hall and filled the dome like weather.

Sefa had spent thirty years lighting lamps, one wick at a time, and she had never once seen the whole city lit at once. She was seeing it now, in sound.

"They're answering it," breathed the young clerk beside her, forgetting himself. "They're all answering it."

"Not answering," Sefa said. "*Counting.*"

Because that was the message Xanthe had hidden in the chime pattern, the thing she had decoded off the strike-train notation three nights ago with tears running down her face: the false hour wasn't chaos. It was a summons. Thirteen strokes, and every bell that repeated it was a lamp lit, a witness standing, a name added to the roll. By nightfall there would be no corner of Vellamare that could claim it hadn't heard.

On the dais, Aldane turned to his clerks and said something quick and low, and two of them went pale and left at a run — toward the observatory, Sefa guessed, toward Xanthe. Her grip tightened on the pole. *Hold on, love. Hold on. We're coming.*

* * *

Isra had not been invited to the auction. Isra had walked in anyway, small and straight-backed in her mended coat, and stationed herself beside the harbormaster's tribunal clerk — a grey woman named Vice who owed Sefa a favor and had agreed, nervously, to let the child stand near her desk "in an observational capacity."

When the thirteenth stroke fell, Isra felt it in her teeth, and her first thought was of her father. He had loved the master clock. He had wound his pocket watch against it every morning of her life, holding it up to his ear like a shell. *If the clock ever lies to you, little bird,* he'd said once, laughing, *then the whole world gets to lie too.* She hadn't understood it then. She understood it now, standing in a hall full of shouting men while bells rang an hour into existence, and she pressed her hand flat over the pocket where the ledger should have been — should have been, because Aldane had taken it, had held it up in his study like a dead bird and said *this ends here* —

Except. Except that Xanthe had spent one long evening teaching her about copies, and duplicates, and how a careful man keeps his records in more than one place, and how a careful girl might do the same. The ledger Aldane held was real. It was also three days out of date. The true pages — recopied in Isra's own careful clerk's-child hand, verified line by line against her memory — were at that moment in a oilcloth packet sewn inside the tribunal clerk's document case, ten feet from the harbormaster's own deputy.

Her father had taught her that too, without meaning to: *always date your entries, always keep the fair copy.* She stood very still among the shouting and thought: *Papa. Listen. They're ringing your hour.*

* * *

In the clock room, the door burst open and one of Aldane's men came through it with his cudgel already drawn, and Xanthe did not turn around.

"Stop it," the man said. "Stop the strike. Now."

"It's finished," Xanthe said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them. "The train has fully discharged. You can't stop a bell that's already been rung — ask any priest." They turned then, slowly, hands visible, and looked at the two guards with something that was not quite a smile. "Besides. Strike what? The mechanism is correct now. Perfectly correct. I repaired it, as commissioned. The Guild can inspect every gear." *They will find nothing but honesty,* the thought arrived warm as brandy, *and one small tooth that proves nothing except that someone once cut it.*

"You've killed us," the second guard said hoarsely. "You've killed *us*, do you understand? He'll—"

"He'll what?" Xanthe asked. Below them, muffled by floors of stone, the city was still ringing. "Arrest the whole harbor?"

What the guards did not know — what only Xanthe knew, alone in the ticking dark — was that the thirteenth hour was only half the mechanism. The other half was simpler and crueler: the clock's new rate was now *true*. From this morning forward, the observatory standard ran honest, and every falsified tide table in the Guild's possession would drift measurably against it within a day, within an hour, in front of any competent examiner. Aldane had ordered the repair completed. It had been. Completely. That was the trap, and it had been sprung by his own signature on the commission.

Xanthe leaned against the great wheel and listened to the bells fade, quarter by quarter, like a tide going out, and allowed themselves — for exactly four seconds, they counted them — to feel it. Triumph, terror, grief, hope, all of it compressed into four seconds of leaning against warm brass while the escapement ticked below them like a patient heart.

Then boots on the stairs, many boots, and the guards straightened, and Xanthe pushed off from the wheel and folded their hands, because Corvus Aldane did not climb to the clock room for small reasons.

* * *

Below, in the hall, the auction had frozen in legal chaos, and Bram Oduya watched it freeze with the patience of a man who had waited twenty years for ice thick enough to walk on.

The lawyer was relentless. "The lots cannot proceed, Magnate. Your own charter, article nine: sales conducted under disputed time are voidable *ab initio*. Do you wish every berth sold today to be voidable? Because I promise you, the buyers will wish otherwise."

"The clock will be corrected within the hour," Aldane said.

"Corrected to *what*?" Old Pell called out from the wall, in a voice like a rusted hinge, and the hall roared.

And that was when the doors opened and Deputy Harbormaster Vice walked down the center aisle with a document case under her arm and a child's hand in hers, and the crowd parted the way water parts around a prow, and Bram felt the whole room turn — Aldane last of all, turning slowly, his composure finally, visibly cracking at the edges like paint over rot.

"Corvus Aldane," Vice said, in a voice trained to carry over storms, "by authority of the harbormaster's tribunal, convened under emergency petition, I am served to inform you that testimony regarding the certification of tide data will be taken *now*, at the seawall, publicly, and that your attendance is required — not requested."

"You have no grounds—"

"We have a ledger," said Isra.

Her voice was small and clear and it went through the hall like the thirteenth stroke had gone through it, and Bram saw Aldane look at the child — really look at her, perhaps for the first time — and Bram saw him recognize her, and Bram saw him understand, all in the space of a single breath, that the girl he had sent away with orders to forget her face had walked back into the center of his world carrying his own handwriting.

Behind him, unnoticed, two of his closest allies were quietly removing the Guild chains of office from around their necks and setting them on the bench.

Bram uncrossed his arms at last. Around him, forty-one dockworkers, a dozen lamplighters, the oyster women, the netmenders, all of them, began to move toward the doors — toward the seawall, toward the light, toward the hour that did not exist until this morning and now belonged to all of them.

"To the seawall," Bram said, and it was not a request, and it was not addressed to Aldane at all.
