## 19. Testimony at the Seawall

Isra had learned early that grown-ups spoke in two languages: the one they used with children, which was soft and full of nothing, and the one they used with each other, which had teeth in it. Her father had spoken both, but he had also spoken a third — the language of his ledgers — and it was that language she carried now, sewn into a coat that no longer fit her, clutched against her chest as the crowd surged toward the seawall like water finding its old bed.

She could not see over shoulders. She did not need to. She knew the way of crowds the way she knew the way of her father's counting room: by sound, by pressure, by the small telltale silences where something important was happening. The crowd moved the way tide moved, and she let it carry her, and Bram's huge hand found her shoulder somewhere in the middle of it and did not let go.

"To the seawall," people were saying, all up and down the street, in the tone of a thing being decided rather than discussed. "To the seawall."

Above them, from every quarter of Vellamare, the bells were still ringing.

They should have stopped by now. Isra knew clocks the way other children knew prayers; her father had kept a small regulator on his desk and wound it every night while telling her how the master clock above the harbor governed everything — auctions, tides, the opening and closing of the Guild's day. Bells rang the hours and then fell silent. These bells would not fall silent. They rang eleven, and then they rang again, and again, and between the strikes there was a pattern she almost recognized, a stutter and repeat, like a message spelled in metal.

*Thirteen,* Xanthe had said that morning, when the clock struck the hour that did not exist. *There is no thirteenth hour. Nothing in the harbor can proceed against a time that isn't on any chart.*

Isra had not understood it then. She understood it now, walking in the press of bodies toward the seawall: Xanthe had broken time itself for them. For one morning, the whole machinery of Vellamare — its contracts, its deadlines, its lawful openings — stood still, because a clockmaker had made the city's heart beat an impossible beat. And into that stillness, all these people were carrying their voices.

The harbor opened out before them all at once, the way harbors do, sudden and enormous. Fog was burning off the water in long rags. The masts stood thick as reeds. And along the seawall, where her father —

Where her father had fallen.

Isra stopped walking. Bram's hand tightened on her shoulder, gentle as a door closing.

"I know," he said, though she had said nothing. His voice was gravel poured slow. "You don't have to come closer, little clerk. You've done your part."

*Little clerk.* Not a softness-word. A teeth-word, but kind teeth, the kind that held things without breaking them. Her father had been called clerk too, and men had said it like an insult, and he had worn it like a coat of arms. Isra looked up at Bram, who had hands like mooring posts and eyes that had gone red-rimmed since dawn, and shook her head.

"My part," she said, "isn't finished."

* * *

The harbormaster's tribunal had convened on the seawall steps because there was nowhere else to convene it. Harbormaster Ondrey was an old woman with a face like driftwood and a voice that carried over gulls without ever rising; Isra had seen her twice before, once at a christening and once at an auction, and could not have said which occasion she'd seemed more suited to. Now Ondree sat at a table of rough planks that dockworkers had carried out themselves, flanked by two magistrates who kept looking over their shoulders at the crowd and at the Guild men at the edges of it, who kept looking at each other, and finding no orders there to follow.

Because Corvus Aldane's word had always been the first word of every Vellamare morning, spoken through the auction bell, and this morning the bell had struck thirteen instead, and his word had gone out of the world like air from a punctured hull.

"Order," Ondrey said, to nobody in particular, and the crowd — forty-one dockworkers, a dozen lamplighters, the oyster women in their aprons, the netmenders with tar-stained fingers — pressed close and went quiet in a way crowds rarely do, quiet the way people are quiet when they have decided, together, all at once, without saying so, that this thing is going to be heard.

Sefa came forward first.

Isra watched her go — the old lamplighter with her ladder-shoulders and her lampkey swinging at her hip, the woman who had fed her stew three nights ago without asking a single question except *are you hungry*, which was the only question that had mattered. Sefa climbed the tribunal steps and turned to face the crowd, and her voice, which Isra expected to be thin, was not thin at all.

"I've lit lamps in this harbor for thirty-one years," she said. "I've been paid in Guild coin and I've been threatened with Guild fists, and last winter I agreed to report on my friends to keep my license. That's mine to confess and I confess it here." A murmur ran through the crowd like wind through grass. Sefa lifted her chin. "But this morning the clock told me something, in its own way, and I'm going to tell you what it said, because a clock can't lie even when a lamplighter can."

And she described the chime pattern — the long-short-short, the repeated phrase buried in the striking train — and Isra felt her breath catch, because she had heard it too, had half-recognized it without knowing why, and now she understood: Xanthe had hidden words inside the bells. Words about falsified tides. Words about drowned debts. Words that ended, Sefa said, with a name.

"Master Ferren Zhou," Sefa said. "Clockmaker. Disappeared four years ago after auditing the Guild's tide tables. His apprentice finished his work this morning — and put his name inside it, where only someone listening closely would hear."

The crowd did not cheer. It was past cheering. It made a sound instead that Isra had never heard a crowd make, low and unified, the sound of a great many people breathing in at once and deciding what to do with the breath.

Then the widow came forward.

* * *

Maren Vasska walked like a woman walking to her own trial, spine straight, hands folded, dressed in the grey of official mourning that had never been given an official end. Isra's chest hurt looking at her. It was the first time she had seen her mother since the night the boatman had rowed them apart in the fog — her mother toward the smuggling caves under the point, Isra toward the observatory steps with a ledger sewn into her coat and instructions whispered against her hair: *don't look back, don't speak your name, forget your own face if you have to.*

Her mother had clearly not forgotten hers.

Maren reached the tribunal steps and did not mount them. She stood at the bottom, facing Ondrey, and her voice when it came was the voice Isra remembered from the counting room, the third language, the ledger language, precise and cold and utterly without mercy for error.

"I am Maren Vasska, widow of Clerk Edvin Vasska, who the Guild reported fell from the seawall on the ninth of Stormmonth." She paused. "He did not fall. He was thrown. I know because he told me he might be, and asked me what to do with the proof if he was, and we decided together that our daughter would carry it, because no one searches a child."

The silence along the seawall was absolute. Even the gulls seemed to bank away from it.

"For six years my husband recorded true tide readings for the Harbor Guild," Maren continued. "And for six years, Corvus Aldane published false ones. Berth auctions timed to spring tides that had already passed. Penalties levied against boatmen for mooring failures that were never failures — that were arithmetic, deliberate arithmetic, designed to drown small operators in debt and take their berths for pennies. My husband kept the true tables. He kept them because his conscience demanded it, and he hid them because his family required it. He encoded them in a cipher of his own design, which I taught to our daughter, because children learn ciphers faster than adults learn courage."

Someone in the crowd began, very softly, to say Aldane's name, and others took it up, and Ondrey struck the table with the flat of her hand.

"Testimony will be orderly or it will be struck," she said, and the name died away, reluctantly, like a wave pulling back over stones.

Aldane's remaining men shifted at the edges of the crowd. Isra counted them without meaning to — her father had taught her to count exits — nine of them, maybe ten, pushing at the back ranks where the netmenders stood, and the netmenders did not move, and behind them the dockworkers crossed their arms in a single motion, forty-one pairs of forearms like pilings driven into the earth. Nobody swung anything. Nobody had to. The crowd simply became heavy, the way water becomes heavy, the way a harbor holds a ship it has decided not to release.

"The ledger exists," Maren said. "It is here. It is complete. And there is a witness who can read it aloud in full, whose testimony no magistrate in this harbor can dismiss as forgery, because the witness was taught the cipher by the hand that invented it —"

"Mother." 

Isra heard her own voice before she had decided to use it. It came out smaller than she wanted. Everything in her wanted to stay behind Bram's legs, in the shadow, in the silence she had kept since the observatory steps. *Don't speak your name.* But names were exactly what today was for. Names were what the clock had rung out over the water all morning — Ferren Zhou, and the vanished partners, and the boatmen in the debt rolls — names dragged up out of the dark the way wreckage comes up when the tide turns.

She stepped out from behind Bram. The crowd parted for her the way it parts for something small and solemn, a funeral procession of one.

She climbed the tribunal steps alone. Up close, Harbormaster Ondrey's driftwood face was not unkind, but it was not soft either, and Isra was grateful. Softness would have undone her. This — the flat attention of an officer of the harbor, waiting — this she could stand inside of.

"State your name for the record," Ondrey said.

*Don't speak your name.* Her father's voice, and her mother's instruction, and three nights of silence in a strange workshop with kind strangers, all of it pressing down on her tongue like a hand. Isra closed her eyes. She saw the counting room. The regulator clock on the desk, ticking. Her father winding it. *A ledger is only honest if someone signs it, Isra. An unsigned truth convinces no one. Remember that, whatever else you forget.*

"Isra Vasska," she said. Her voice cracked on the second syllable and she let it. "Daughter of Edvin Vasska, clerk of the Harbor Guild. I have the true tide ledger. I sewed it into my coat myself. I can read it."

Ondrey leaned forward. "You understand, child, that testimony given here binds you. There will be questions. There may be worse than questions."

"Yes," Isra said, and found, to her surprise, that she meant it the way her father would have meant it — completely, down to the last entry, balanced.

She unpicked the seam with her fingernails. Three nights ago her fingers had shaken doing it in a stranger's workshop; now they were steady, because steadiness, she understood at last, was not a thing you had or lacked but a thing you borrowed from the people standing behind you, and there were hundreds of them, holding their breath along the seawall. The oilcloth packet came free. The ledger inside was small, dense, unglamorous — columns and columns of her father's narrow hand, numbers that had cost him everything.

She laid it on the plank table in front of the magistrates, and the sound it made was very small, and everyone acted as though it had struck the earth.

"Begin anywhere you like," Isra said. "Page one records the true high water for the ninth of Thawmonth, six years ago. The published table for that date is wrong by eleven feet. My father noted the discrepancy and initialed it, and beneath it there is a payment entry — to a berth-holder named Tam Ostrell, compensation for a mooring penalty he never incurred. Tam Ostrell lost his boat the following spring. He is standing—" she pointed, arm steady, straight into the crowd, "—right there."

Every head turned. A weathered boatman with white stubble on his cheeks stood among the dockworkers, mouth open, one hand pressed flat over his heart as though something in there had knocked loose.

"He's been in the ledger since the beginning," Isra said. "They all have. Every name. I'd like to read them."

* * *

She read.

She read for a long time. The sun climbed and burned the last fog off the water and still she read, her father's cipher unfolding under her tongue like something that had waited years to breathe — dates, true tides, false tides, payments, initials, the careful marginal notes in which Edvin Vasska had done the only thing left to a silenced man: he had written down that he knew, dated and signed, so that someday, somewhere, the knowledge could testify without him.

When she reached the entries naming Aldane directly — the private account, the disbursements to the harbor watch, the single chilling line *E.V. warns again; offer withdrawn* — the magistrates had stopped pretending to verify anything. One of them was writing. The other had his head bowed over the ledger as if in prayer, and perhaps he was; Isra did not know what magistrates did with their guilt, only that it must weigh more than a child could carry.

The Guild men at the edge of the crowd were gone. She noticed it during a pause, mid-column: the pressure at the back had simply drained away, the way menace does once it understands it is outnumbered not in strength but in witness. Somewhere behind her, Bram laughed once, short and satisfied, at whatever he saw in their retreating faces.

"Entry one hundred and twelve," Isra read. "Dated four years ago, the week my father died." Her voice caught, and she gripped the table's edge, and went on. "*Rate confirmed against master regulator. Discrepancy persists. If I do not file this, no one will. E.V.*" She looked up. "That's the last entry in his hand. After that, the ledger continues in my mother's."

Maren Vasska stepped up onto the tribunal stairs beside her daughter, and together — Isra reading, her mother confirming, their two voices braided into the third language, the ledger language, the one with teeth — they finished it. The last page was the newest, written in the caves under the point by lamplight: the current season's falsifications, the auction scheduled for this very morning, timed against tide data that was fiction from the first column to the last.

Harbormaster Ondrey let the silence sit for a long moment. Then she rose, driftwood face finally cracking into something that might, in a gentler life, have been called grief, and addressed the magistrates.

"I have signed off on auction times for six years," she said. "I signed what the Guild gave me and told myself the sea doesn't lie. Today the sea sent me a clock that rings an hour God never made, and a child with a book. I find I prefer the child's arithmetic." She turned to the crowd. "By authority of the harbormaster's tribunal and the magistracy of Vellamare, this tribunal hears the ledger of Edvin Vasska as evidence. Writs will issue against Corvus Aldane within the hour. His arrest—"

She got no further, because the crowd roared — not the ugly roar Isra had feared all week, all her short careful life, but a sound like a harbor at flood tide, vast and moving and impossible to stand against, and her mother's arm came around her shoulders, and Bram's hand descended on her head like a warm roof, and Sefa was crying openly without shame, and above them all, across the water, the master clock began to strike noon.

Twelve strokes. Twelve only. Honest as sunrise.

Isra stood on the seawall steps in the middle of the noise with tears running freely down her face and did not wipe them, because her hands were busy holding the ledger, and her father's voice was in her ears in the numbers, in the initials, in every line she had spoken aloud, and she thought: *you didn't fall. You're still standing. You're standing in every word.*

The last bell faded over the water. And into the hush that followed, Bram lowered his mouth to her ear, and his gravel voice was almost gentle:

"They're bringing him now, little clerk. Down the quay, under guard. You don't have to watch."

Isra looked out at the harbor, at the masts and the light and the seawall where her father had ended and she had, somehow, begun.

"Yes, I do," she said. "I'm the witness."

Far down the quay, past the fish stalls and the coiled rope, a knot of magistrates' officers appeared, walking slowly inward, and around them the crowd parted and closed and parted again like water, and in the center of it, coatless, chains of office gone, his beautiful grey face turned toward the seawall toward the tribunal toward the small girl holding his ruin in her arms, came Corvus Aldane.
