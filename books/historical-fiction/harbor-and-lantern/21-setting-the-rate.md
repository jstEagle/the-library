## 21. Setting the Rate

The clock room was quiet in the way Xanthe had once believed no room could be after what had happened inside it.

Three days since the tribunal at the seawall. Three days since Corvus Aldane had been walked down the harbor steps between magistrates while a thousand people watched and said almost nothing — the silence itself the loudest thing Xanthe had ever heard. Three days since Isra had stood on a crate to be seen over the crowd, holding her father's ledger above her head like a lantern, and the harbormaster's clerk had read the names aloud until his voice gave out and another took it up, and then another, until every name in that drowned book had been spoken into the open air where it could never again be unspoken.

Now the observatory was Xanthe's alone. The Guild's guards were gone; the bribed assistant had fled the city or been taken, no one would say which; the great door at the base of the tower stood unlocked for the first time in Xanthe's memory, because there was nothing left inside worth locking away from anyone.

Xanthe climbed the spiral stair with a satchel of tools and stopped at the clock room's threshold the way one stops at the door of a house where something terrible and something holy both occurred.

The master clock filled the room as it always had — three stories of brass and iron rising through the tower's hollow heart, its pendulum swinging in the long slow patience that had governed Vellamare's tides and auctions and prayers for a hundred years. But now, when Xanthe looked at it, they did not see only the mechanism. They saw the hidden train they had built inside it, night after night under Aldane's guards' indifferent eyes: the little escapement of their own devising, cams filed from scrap brass, the secret tooth that waited at the twelfth hour to throw the striking train into an hour that existed on no chart, no almanac, no honest clock anywhere in the world.

The thirteenth hour. The halt that had frozen the auction, emptied the exchange floor, brought every bell in the city ringing out of order until the whole harbor turned its face upward and asked what time it truly was.

It had worked. It had saved them all. And now it had to come out.

Xanthe set down the satchel, unwrapped the tools — the good ones, Master Oren's ones, rescued from the shop before the Guild's inventory clerks could list them like so many dead men's possessions — and rolled up their sleeves.

"You did what I asked you to do," they said aloud to the clock, because three days of solitude in a tower had made speaking aloud a habit rather than a madness, and because Master Oren had always talked to mechanisms and Xanthe had sworn, somewhere in the grief-fogged weeks after his disappearance, never to become the kind of person who did that.

Well. So much for oaths.

The clock ticked on, serene, unbothered by irony.

Xanthe climbed the maintenance ladder to the striking train and began the work of making the clock honest.

* * *

It was delicate work, more delicate than building the thing had been. Anyone can break a lock; only a fool breaks one carelessly when the door behind it must still swing true. Every component of the false-hour mechanism had been designed to interleave with the original train without altering its rate — Xanthe had insisted on that even under guard, even with Aldane's man watching them file each cam, because a clock that lied about the time was useless to everyone including liars, and because some stubborn grain in Xanthe's nature had refused, even then, to let the master clock run anything but true. Only the striking sequence had been poisoned. Only the voice, not the heartbeat.

So the removal, too, touched only the voice.

Xanthe worked by lamplight through the morning, hands moving with the unhurried certainty that overtook them sometimes — the craftsman's trance Sefa called *going under the water*, because Xanthe's eyes went distant and their breathing slowed and it was no use talking to them until they surfaced. Cam by cam, pin by pin, the secret mechanism came apart in their fingers. Each piece went into a cloth bag, labeled in pencil with its position and purpose. Not to be destroyed. Xanthe had thought about destroying it, the first night, standing at the window with the pieces spread out like the anatomy of a crime.

But the thirteenth hour belonged to Vellamare now. Bram's dockworkers talked about it the way sailors talk about a wave that should have drowned them and didn't. Children in the lower streets played a game called striking thirteen, one of them standing on a barrel ringing a pot-lid while the others froze. The story would grow in the telling; that was what stories did in harbor towns. But somewhere, someday, someone would need to know exactly how it had been done — how a clockmaker with six tools and no allies had taught an honest machine to tell one necessary lie — and Xanthe intended that the answer should survive, in full, in working drawings, deposited with the harbormaster's archive under seal, to be opened only if some future Aldane ever tried to buy the tide again.

Let the mechanism testify too, Xanthe thought. Everything testifies now. That was the whole meaning of the last month.

By early afternoon the striking train stood clean. Xanthe replaced the last original lifter, torqued the final retaining screw to the exact resistance their fingertips knew better than any number, and sat back on the ladder platform to watch the train run through a full winding-and-release cycle, over and over, eleven times, twelve times, thirteen—

Thirteen. Their breath caught before their mind caught up. Then they saw it: the count wheel turning smoothly past the position where the secret tooth had lived, carrying on to twelve strikes, releasing, resetting. Twelve. Only twelve. The count was right. There was no thirteenth notch, no hesitation, no ghost.

The clock struck noon below them — twelve measured strokes rolling out over the fog-bound roofs of Vellamare, out over the harbor, out to sea — and every stroke was true.

Xanthe stayed on the platform a long moment, listening to the last vibration die in the tower stones, and found that their eyes were wet, and could not entirely say why. Relief, partly. Grief, partly. The peculiar emptiness that follows finished work, that hollow room behind the sternum where the purpose used to live.

They wiped their face with the back of a wrist, laughed shortly at themselves, and went down the ladder to fetch the graver.

* * *

There was one more thing, and they had been carrying it for days folded small against their chest like a letter they were afraid to open.

Master Oren Zhou had been gone — disappeared, drowned, silenced, the words changed depending on who said them and how much they knew — for two years before Isra came to the observatory door. Two years of Xanthe keeping the workshop alive on repair work and pride, two years of refusing to say the word *dead* even to themselves, because saying it felt like doing Aldane's work for him. And then the ledger had surfaced, and Oren Zhou's name was in it, written in his own hand in the margin of a page recording payments he had refused and threats he had reported and, finally, a single line dated nine days before he vanished: *If anything comes of me, the boy knows clocks better than I ever did. See he is paid fairly.*

*Xanthe*, not *the boy*. Isra had shown them the line twice to be sure. Their master's cramped, exact handwriting, the same hand that had annotated every drawing in the workshop, writing Xanthe's name into a smuggler's ledger as if it were a will.

He had known. At the end, in whatever darkness he had been living in, he had known what was coming, and he had spent his last recorded act making sure the world remembered his apprentice.

Xanthe had not wept when they read it. They had gone very still, and Isra had watched them with those grave dark eyes, and then the child had reached over and closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping person, and that had been somehow worse than weeping and better too.

But grief wanted somewhere to go. Grief that had nowhere to go curdled — Xanthe had seen what it became in Bram's dockworkers who'd lost boatmates to the falsified tides, in the widow Maren whose silence had nearly cost her daughter. It became weight, and weight sank people. Xanthe had decided, sometime in the sleepless arithmetic after the tribunal, that theirs would go somewhere permanent instead. Somewhere a hundred years of clockmakers would find it.

Inside the case. Where only hands that tended the clock would ever look.

Xanthe opened the access panel at the base of the great movement — the panel Master Oren himself had taught them to open, twenty years ago, lifting them up onto a stool in this same tower, this same light, saying *clocks are honest, Xanthe. People aren't. That's why I like them.* — and looked a long time at the inner face of the case: old oak, smoke-dark, crowded with the signatures and notes of every keeper the clock had ever had. Names, dates, rates. *Regulated to +2s/day, spring 1187. J. Calloway.* *New suspension, autumn 1211.* A century of quiet devotion scratched into wood by people nobody else had ever heard of.

Near the bottom, in a space left deliberately blank by the last keeper — keepers always left blanks; it was a courtesy passed down like the gravers themselves — Xanthe steadied their hand and began to cut.

**OREN ZHOU**
**KEEPER OF THE TRUE RATE**
**HE WOULD NOT SELL THE TIDE**

Their hand shook only on the last word, and they let it, and then went back and deepened every letter evenly so the shaking showed nowhere.

Below it, smaller, after a pause in which the clock counted out several hundred honest seconds:

**REPAIRED AND SET TRUE BY XANTHE ZHOU, APPRENTICE**
**THE THIRTEENTH HOUR STRUCK HERE ONCE. IT WILL NOT STRIKE AGAIN UNLESS IT MUST.**

Xanthe blew the oak dust from the letters, considered, then added one line more — the smallest, hardest one:

**HE WAS MY MASTER AND HE DID NOT DROWN. HE IS RAISED.**

Because the ledger proved it, in the end: Oren Zhou had not died a coward's death in the harbor. He had died a man who refused, and refusal written down is not drowning. It is ballast. It holds the hull upright.

Xanthe closed the access panel gently, the way Isra had closed the ledger, and sat back on their heels on the tower floor, and this time let themselves cry properly, briefly, without shame, while the pendulum swung its patient arc beside them like a metronome counting out the measure of a life.

* * *

Sefa found them there near dusk. She came up the stair with her lamp still lit from the evening rounds — she never carried it extinguished anymore, she'd said; the whole city had learned what lamps could do when they talked to each other — and she stood in the doorway of the clock room with her grey plait over one shoulder and read the scene in one long look: the tools laid out in their fussy rows, the cloth bag of dismantled history, the fresh-cut letters just visible through the panel seam if you knew where to look.

"Done?" she asked.

"True to the second," Xanthe said. "I checked it against your lamplighting round. You're still late on Quay Street, by the way."

"I'm *unhurried* on Quay Street. Old Marek likes the company." She came in and put a hand on the case, flat-palmed, the way dockworkers touch a ship's hull. "And the other thing? The thirteenth?"

"Out. Bagged, documented, and going under seal to the harbormaster tomorrow. The clock tells the truth and nothing but."

Sefa nodded slowly. Something moved through her face — relief, and under it a strange small loss, the way one feels when a storm passes and the sky is merely sky again. "Felt like it earned the right to lie, though. Just the once."

"It wasn't lying." Xanthe stood, joints protesting, two nights of work settling into their bones. "That's the thing I finally understood at about four in the morning. It wasn't lying. The thirteenth hour isn't a false time — it's the true time Aldane's auctions were hiding. All the falsified tables, all the drowned debts — the clock striking that hour was the first honest reading anyone had taken in years. It told the truth so loudly the whole city had to stop and hear it." They looked up at the great train, ticking, serene. "Now everything around it tells the truth too, so it can go back to being quietly right. That's all honesty ever gets to be, most days. Quietly right, and kept."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then: "Isra wants to see it strike. Tomorrow. She's been asking Bram to bring her up the tower all week and Bram says it's no place for a child and Isra says neither was Aldane's counting-house, so." She shrugged. "She wins, obviously. She usually does. But she asked if you'd be the one to show her the works."

"She asked for me?"

"She asked whether 'the clockmaker' would be there, and when Bram said yes, she said good, and went back to her sums." Sefa's mouth twitched. "She's teaching herself figures off her father's ledger. Maren can't stop her and doesn't truly want to. Girl keeps accounts like she's avenging them."

Xanthe looked at the floor to hide their face doing something complicated. "Tomorrow, then. Tell her I'll be here."

"And the workshop?" Sefa asked, casual in the way that meant not casual at all. "Bram says the Guild's seizure order dissolved with Aldane's arrest, and the lease is yours now, seeing as there's no master to hold it and no one left with the appetite to contest an orphan's— a *foundling's* claim, as Bram puts it, meaning you, don't play innocent. He says the docks have been asking. Boatmen with daughters, mostly. Asking whether you'll take apprentices."

Xanthe had known the question was coming. They had felt it circling for days like a gull over a cleaning table. And they had noticed, with the part of the mind that never stops working even during grief, that they had already answered it — answered it weeks ago, in fact, every time they had chosen to stay, to dig in, to build a watch rotation or hide a ledger or cut their name into shared wood instead of packing the portable things and running, which was the oldest reflex they owned and had never once actually saved them.

"I measured my whole life once by what I could carry," Xanthe said. "Master Oren caught me doing it. I was fourteen, packing my tools into one bag like the Guild men were coming, and he asked me what I was doing and I said being ready to leave. And he picked up the shop's big bench vise — forty pounds if it was an ounce — and put it in my arms and said, *carry that, then, and you'll remember you live here.*" Xanthe turned their palm up toward the tower, the clock, the city going gold beyond the fog-line. "I've been remembering it for a month. Yes. I'll take apprentices. Dock children, wharf rats, anyone with steady hands and a reason. We'll need a bigger bench."

Sefa grinned and said nothing wise at all, which was her way of being kind, and turned to go, and paused at the door.

"One more thing," she said. "Maren sent word this afternoon. She's ready — to come to the shop, to talk about Harrow in front of people, all of it, the testimony proper, for the record. She says Isra shouldn't carry the ledger story alone anymore. Says it takes a mother's voice now, not just a daughter's." Sefa's eyes were gentle and serious. "She asks if it can happen at the workshop. Saturday, when the docks empty out. She says—" and here Sefa's voice did something it rarely did, softened all the way through, "—she says she wants to bring her husband's account book home to a room full of people who build things that keep time honestly. Whatever that means. Mothers."

Xanthe looked at the clock. The pendulum swung, and swung, and kept.

"Tell her Saturday," they said. "Tell her the door will be open. It seems the thing to do with doors now."

Below them, far down in the tower's roots, the master clock gathered itself and struck the half hour — eight strokes, measured, true, unhurried — and the sound rolled out over Vellamare like a promise being kept in public, and Xanthe stood in the light of Sefa's lamp and listened to it all the way to the end.
