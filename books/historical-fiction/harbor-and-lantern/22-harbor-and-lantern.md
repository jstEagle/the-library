## 22. Harbor and Lantern

The morning after the trial, Xanthe woke in the workshop and did not know, for three full breaths, why the light through the shutters looked different.

It took them that long to remember: nothing was coming for them. No Guild men on the corner tallying their comings and goings. No summons with a wax seal. No clock owed to a man who smiled like a knife being sheathed. The light was only light — ordinary, salt-grey, sliding across the floorboards where their master's bench still stood exactly as he had left it four years ago, down to the chalk mark where he'd rested his coffee cup and never once wiped it away.

Xanthe lay there a while, listening to Vellamare wake up. Gulls over the fish market. A cart axle complaining on the cobbles. Somewhere near the seawall, Bram's voice, already raised in argument or laughter — with him it had never been necessary to distinguish — and beneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, the master clock in the observatory tower striking seven true and unhurried strokes.

Seven o'clock. The honest hour. Xanthe had set that rate themselves, file and oil and patience, and now the harbor arranged its whole day around a sound they could trust. It was the strangest feeling they had ever had, and they lay in it the way you lie in warm water, not wanting to stand up and let it drain away.

*Carry nothing you cannot put down.* Their master used to say it when he caught Xanthe hoarding — spare gears, pretty stones, the small magpie treasures of an apprentice who had learned early that things leave, people leave, and the only sane response is to keep your hands closed. He would pry one of those hands open, gently, and drop whatever treasure it held back onto the bench. *If you can't put it down, little wren, it isn't you holding it.*

Xanthe had thought, for most of their life, that this meant poverty. Now, waking unafraid in the room where everything had nearly been taken from them, they understood it was something else entirely. It meant you could put things down because there would be a table to put them on. There would be a room. There might even be someone across the table, complaining about the state of your files.

They got up, opened the shutters, and began sweeping the workshop for the first time since the fire watch.

* * *

The shop reopened on a Tuesday, because Sefa declared Tuesdays lucky — she had lit her first lamp alone on a Tuesday, she said, and burned down nothing whatsoever — and because Isra had counted the days until Tuesday with the grim precision of a child who had learned that waiting is easier when you can measure it.

Xanthe had expected the reopening to be quiet. They should have known better. By the time the shutters came down, half the lower harbor was crowded into a space meant for six, and the smell of Bram's wife's honeyed buns fought the smell of solder to an honorable draw. Dockworkers stood among the gear cabinets with their caps in their fists, careful of their boots on the clean floor, the way big men are careful when a place matters. Lamplighters leaned in the doorway. Two of the harbormaster's clerks came, off-duty and slightly ashamed of themselves, and were fed anyway.

And the children. That was what Xanthe had not prepared for. They came in a loose flock behind Bram — dock brats, lamp-tenders' kids, a boy whose father had drowned in a debt-berth auction and who stood at the back with his chin up and his eyes doing arithmetic on every tool in the room.

"Apprentices," Bram said, with the air of a man announcing weather. "You said you wanted apprentices."

"I said I might, eventually—"

"There's nine of them." Bram counted with his whole arm. "Well. Seven. Pip counts twice because he'll run off if you don't watch him."

Isra, perched on the high stool at the master's bench — hers now, though no one had said so out loud yet; some things are claimed by sitting — looked up from the escapement model she was disassembling with terrifying confidence. "Eight," she corrected. "The girl by the door hasn't come in yet because she doesn't think she's allowed."

Xanthe crossed the shop and opened the door wider.

The girl on the step — thin, oil-haired, hands hidden in her sleeves — looked up the way a stray looks at a hand extended too fast. Xanthe knew that look. Had worn it, once, on this same dock, outside another door, before a man with chalk dust on his fingers had said, *well, are you coming in or do you plan to repair the street?*

"You're allowed," Xanthe said. "That's the entire point of the door."

She came in. She put down, very deliberately beside the stool, the single thing she owned: a bent tin lantern with a cracked chimney. Then she looked at Xanthe as if daring them to comment.

"First lesson," Xanthe told the assembled eight-and-nine, "is that everything in this room can be repaired except time wasted arguing with me. Second lesson is lunch."

The cheer rattled the window glass. From the doorway, Sefa laughed her lamplighter's laugh, low and warm as struck flint, and Xanthe understood that the sound had been the true bell opening the shop — everything after was ceremony.

* * *

The weeks that followed settled into a rhythm Xanthe could not have designed and would not have traded. Mornings were instruction — how to cut a wheel, how to listen to an escapement the way you listen to a pulse, how to hold a file so it forgives you. Afternoons were work, real paid work, because the harbor had discovered that a clockmaker who had humiliated Corvus Aldane made a fine talisman, and berths and boats and shopfronts all wanted honest time hung above their doors.

The apprentices were not natural craftsmen. They were better than natural craftsmen, because they had none of the vanity of talent — only hunger. The debt-drowned boy, whose name was Oskar, filed the same brass blank for eleven days until it ran true, and when it did he did not crow. He simply held it up to the window light, turned it, and asked quietly, "Can I do another?" Xanthe, who had wept over their own eleventh-day blank, taught him the polishing grits and said nothing, which was the highest praise either of them could afford.

Isra learned fastest and fought hardest against it. She approached mechanisms the way she approached everything — as interrogations. *Who benefits if this part fails? Who slowed this wheel? What is it hiding?* She had been her father's daughter too well; the clerk's habit of suspicion lived in her small precise hands. Some evenings Xanthe found her at the bench past dark, taking apart a movement that had done nothing wrong, and they would sit with her without speaking until she put it back together, and something in her shoulders came down a fraction each time, gear by gear, like a mechanism finding its rate.

"It still bothers me," Isra said one such evening, not looking up. "That clocks don't keep secrets. Everything else does."

"Clocks tell the biggest secret there is," Xanthe said. "That things pass. You just have to be able to bear watching them do it."

Isra considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "I can bear it," she decided, and wound the movement, and let it go.

* * *

Sefa and Bram came formally on the first of the month, which in Vellamare meant with paperwork and witnesses and an alarming quantity of food.

There was no orphanage that would take a smuggler's daughter — Aldane's fall had cleansed his crimes from respectable memory, but not from respectable prejudice — so the harbor had done what the harbor always did with things the city refused to count. It had built around them. Sefa signed as guardian; Bram signed as surety; the widow Verany, Isra's mother, signed as witness with a hand that shook and then steadied, because her daughter stood beside her in a lamplighter's coat two sizes too large, sleeves rolled with ceremony, and some steadiness is borrowed from proximity.

They had asked Isra, first, alone, whether she wanted this. Xanthe knew because Isra reported it herself, later, at the bench, with her characteristic lack of ceremony: "They asked me whom I wanted to live with, and I said both, and also the workshop, and also I want a key." She had gotten all three. The key hung at her throat on a cord, next to the smaller key of the lantern she refused to replace.

The tribunal papers named her Isra Sefa-Bram, which was legally nonsensical and emotionally exact, and the clerk who wrote it had shrugged and said the harbor had survived worse calligraphy.

Xanthe stood witness with ink on their thumb and thought about doors. About the observatory steps where a silent child had waited with orders pinned to her coat saying *forget her face*. Nobody had forgotten her face. That, in the end, had been the whole of the revolution — a city full of people declining, at every lamp and berth and threshold, to forget.

* * *

Autumn came down the coast in long silver rains, and with it the anniversary preparations — the harbor's festival of the Thirteenth Hour, invented within a season, already treated by everyone under forty as ancient tradition. There would be lamps strung from the mole to the market. There would be a minute of silence at the false hour, when the master clock would strike once — Xanthe had built the mechanism a permanent home, a single permitted anomaly, a scar kept visible so the healing stayed honest — and then the bells of every quarter would answer, and then, tradition demanded, everyone would eat too much and argue about tide tables.

Sefa bullied Xanthe into stringing the workshop's share of lanterns personally. "You made the hour," she said. "You don't get to hide from it. Craftsmen stand under their own arches."

So Xanthe stood on a ladder in the rain-shadow of the eaves, hanging lanterns with cold fingers, and looked down the wet street at their own life lit from below: the workshop windows gold and busy, apprentices' heads bent over benches; Isra in the doorway directing operations with a clipboard she had confiscated from a harbormaster's clerk; Bram arriving through the drizzle with bread the size of a buoy; Sefa moving along the street touching each lamp in passing, the old unconscious blessing of her trade, leaving small suns behind her.

Four years ago — five? — Xanthe had arrived in Vellamare with one trunk, two suits of clothes, and a letter of apprenticeship, measuring life in portable things. Everything they owned still fit in that trunk. That had not changed. What had changed was that the trunk sat under the bed gathering dust, and nobody in the harbor would have been able to say when it had stopped mattering, least of all Xanthe.

Belonging, their master had tried to teach them, was not granted — not by guilds, not by blood, not by seals on paper. It was built. Gear by gear. Lamp by lamp. Tuesday by Tuesday. You built it the way you built a clock: you chose the parts that ran true, you cut away everything that dragged, you seated each piece in its proper place, and then — this was the part no apprentice believed until they felt it — you wound it and let go, trusting the train to carry itself.

Xanthe climbed down, and Isra handed them a bun, and the rain eased, and somewhere above the rooftops the observatory clock struck the quarter with its clear unhurried voice.

"Come inside," Isra said. "You're missing the lesson. I'm teaching them the striking train."

"You're teaching them my striking train?"

"Somebody has to," said Isra, with her mother's exact bureaucratic serenity, "in case anything happens to you," and went in, and left the door open.

Xanthe stood a moment longer in the wet street, under their own lanterns, carrying nothing they could not put down.

Then they followed her in.
