## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

Mara Kavanagh had learned to weigh her life in her two hands.

She did it some mornings without meaning to — a habit born the winter her mother died, when she'd stood in this same workshop and understood, with the cold clarity of a girl of fourteen, that everything she loved could be packed into a single sea chest. Her father's house above the harbor was full of furniture and linens and the great sideboard that had come from County Cork, but none of it was hers. Hers was what she could carry: eleven tools in a roll of oiled leather, her mother's garnet ring on a chain at her throat, and one photograph in a brass frame no bigger than a playing card.

Eleven tools. One ring. One photograph. She counted them now, in the gray light coming through the workshop's single salt-fogged window, and found the arithmetic unchanged. It steadied her the way counting ticked seconds steadies a clock. Things could be measured. Measured things could be trusted.

The photograph came first, as it always did. Her mother stood at the harbor rail in the picture, squinting against the sun, one hand raised either to wave or to shade her eyes — the photographer's long exposure had blurred the gesture into something ambiguous, so that Sorcha Kavanagh seemed forever caught between greeting and grief. Tomas stood beside her, fifteen years old and grinning, his collar blown crooked by the wind. Mara herself was not in the picture. Someone had to hold the box camera, and it had been Mara, behind the lens, learning even then the lesson of her life: that she would be the one who watched, who kept, who carried.

She set the frame face-down on the workbench, the way she always did when she meant to work. A clockmaker could not look at ghosts and gears at once.

The workshop occupied the ground floor of a building that leaned, very slightly, toward the water — as everything in the harbor district leaned toward the water, as if the town itself were considering the plunge. Her bench ran beneath the window; her lamp hung from a beam stained dark with forty years of oil and pipe smoke; and along the walls stood the clocks entrusted to her repair, each one stopped at its own private hour of failure. A mantel clock at 4:17. A tall case from the customs house at noon exactly. A ship's chronometer, its gimbals gummed with salt, frozen at 11:52 of some night its owner had never explained.

Three years ago there had been a fourth clock in the room — Tomas's, a small walnut regulator he'd bought secondhand and never once wound properly. After the Sea Wren went down and the town rang its bell for the dead, Mara had taken the regulator home and wound it and kept it running for a full year before she could bear to let it run down. Letting it stop had felt like a second burial. She had not opened its case since.

Now she bent over the customs-house clock and did not think about any of that, because outside her window the harbor was already talking.

* * *

Rumor traveled through Saltcote the way damp traveled through stone — invisibly, everywhere at once, settling into joints. By seven that morning it had reached the fish market; by half past, the chandlery; by eight, when Mrs. Oduya sent her boy round with the mantel clock's missing pendulum bob wrapped in a scrap of newspaper, the newsprint itself carried the rumor in three lines set below an advertisement for liver pills:

*SICKNESS REPORTED AT THE FRENCH PORTS. QUARANTINE FEARED FOR WESTERN HARBORS.*

"Me mam says the fishing fleet'll be turned away," the boy announced, bouncing on his heels in the doorway, eager to deliver doom the way only a child can. "Me uncle says there's men dying in the streets of Brest and the ships are coming here anyway because we're the last port what'll take them."

"Your uncle says a great many things," Mara said, holding out two coppers for the errand.

"He says your da knows already. He says the harbormaster's had letters." The boy pocketed the coins and fled, delighted, back down the crooked lane.

Mara stood a moment with her hand still extended. *Your da knows already.* Of course Declan Kavanagh knew already. Her father had been harbormaster for nineteen years; he knew the weather before the weather did, knew which captains drank and which lied about their cargo, knew the harbor the way Mara knew the inside of a clock case — every wheel, every escapement, every place where something might be made to slip. If plague was coming to Saltcote, Declan would have known it in the marrow of him days ago, and said nothing, because saying it would make it true.

She returned to the customs-house clock. Its strike train was worn past adjustment; she was fitting a new lift wire when the first bell began.

Not the church bell. Not the harbor bell. The *observatory* bell — the deep-throated master clock that sat atop Kestrel Point and marked the hours for the whole coast, the clock every ship's officer trusted and every townsman set his watch by. It should have sounded ten strokes for ten o'clock.

It struck twice and stopped.

The silence afterward was enormous. In her workshop full of stopped clocks Mara had grown used to a certain stillness, but this was different. This was the sound of the whole town holding its breath. She heard a dog begin to bark somewhere near the quay. She heard Mrs. Oduya's shutters bang open across the lane. And she heard, faintly, rising from the direction of the harbor stairs, voices beginning to multiply like flies — because a town that had spent three days swallowing rumors of plague did not hear the great clock fail and think *mechanical trouble.* A town like that heard the clock fail and thought *the end is arriving on schedule.*

Mara put down her pliers.

She took up the roll of oiled leather and chose four tools, then five, then, after a moment's grim consideration, all eleven. She hung her mother's ring around her neck where it always hung. She looked at the photograph, face-down beside the vice, and after a moment she slipped it into her apron pocket instead of leaving it on the bench — an act with no practical justification whatsoever, and which she therefore declined to examine.

Then she climbed the outer stair to the rooms above, where her father was shrugging into his coat with the harried speed of a man already late for a disaster.

"You heard it," Declan said. It was not a question. Her father asked questions only when he didn't already know the answer, and that was rare enough that Mara could count those occasions in her memory the way she counted her portable possessions.

"The whole point heard it. What's being said at the quay?"

"That God has stopped His own watch over Saltcote, if you ask the Irishmen, or that the mechanism's failed, if you ask anyone with sense." He settled his hat brim low. There was gray in his beard now that she hadn't noticed last week; or perhaps there had been, and she had chosen not to notice, the way she chose not to open Tomas's regulator. "There are men at my office who want the shipping register and men at my office who want the berth lists, and by noon there will be men at my office who want both, and I have to be there before they are. Which leaves—"

"The observatory. I know. I'm going now."

He paused at the door, and for a moment the harbormaster fell away and there stood only her father, tired in a way that had nothing to do with hours. "Mind the cliff path, Mara-girl. Wind's coming around to the south."

It was such a small thing to say, such an ordinary paternal thing, that it frightened her more than the failed bell had. Her father warned her off danger the way he always had — automatically, habitually, as though warning were a reflex beyond his control. But lately, these three strange rumor-heavy weeks, she had begun to wonder whether Declan Kavanagh knew something about danger that he was keeping behind his teeth. He had been short with the customs men. He had burned a correspondence in the parlor grate and told her it was nothing, old accounts. He had started locking the drawer of his desk that had never once been locked in her twenty-two years.

"Da." She caught his sleeve. "The letters from France. The sickness. Is it truly as bad as the papers say?"

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, shuttered, gone. "Papers say what sells," he said. "Go mend your clock."

And he was down the stairs and gone into the street, coat flying, a big man making himself small with hurry.

* * *

The cliff path climbed from the northern edge of town in a series of switchbacks cut into the chalk, hedged with wind-bent gorse, until the rooftops of Saltcote fell away below and there was nothing but the sea's long gray conversation and the white tower ahead, crowned with its dome and its weathervane and, inside that dome, the heart of the coast.

Mara had climbed this path perhaps two hundred times in her life. She knew every handhold, every patch where the chalk crumbled, every place the wind came crosswise and tried to take one's hat. She had climbed it at twelve with Tomas racing her, both of them shrieking, their father's warnings left far below on the quay. She had climbed it at nineteen, alone, in the week after the burial-at-sea service, because the observatory keeper had let her sit in the clock room and listen to the chimes, and the chimes were the only sound in the world that hadn't changed when Tomas drowned.

Today she climbed slowly, and the wind did indeed come around to the south, carrying a smell she couldn't place — tar and cold salt and something else, something faintly wrong, like a note too close to true for the ear to name.

From the ridge she could see the whole harbor laid out below: the fishing fleet riding at anchor, the quay crowded despite the hour, her father's office with its flag already hoisted. Beyond it, the harbor district — Saltcote's oldest quarter, the tangle of lanes where she'd been born, where the Kavanaghs had lived since before the lighthouse, where every third doorframe bore a mark from some childhood game of hers and Tomas's. If quarantine came — and everyone now believed it would come — that quarter would be sealed first. It was always the poor quarters that were sealed first.

She thought of what could be carried out of it. Eleven tools. One ring. One photograph. Three years of her brother's absence, which weighed nothing at all and could not be left behind.

At the tower door, old Mr. Aldous the keeper was waiting, and Mara saw before he spoke a word that his hands were shaking.

"Mara." He pulled her inside, out of the wind, into the spiral stair's cold stone hush. "Thank God. Listen — before you go up, I have to say something. I've been meaning to send for you for weeks, and I let it go, God forgive me, I let it go."

"Aldous, breathe. It's a broken clock. Whatever else is wrong—"

"It isn't only a broken clock." His voice dropped, and his eyes went up the stairwell, toward the clock room, as though the mechanism could hear him. "It hasn't struck right in three years, girl. Not since the spring of '45. And today it stopped altogether, and I think—" He pressed his palm flat against the stone wall as if to steady the tower itself. "I think there are things in that clock room that were never mine to keep secret. And now the quarantine's coming, and the harbor's closing, and I don't know how much longer any of us has to say anything true."

Mara stood on the first step, her tool roll heavy against her hip, her mother's ring cold against her breastbone, and looked up into the dark spiral that led to the master clock — to the machine she had loved longest, almost longer than any person.

"What things?" she asked quietly. "What secrets, Aldous?"

But the old man only shook his head and gestured upward, and above them both, through sixty feet of stone and silence, the great clock began — unexpectedly, wrongly — to move.
