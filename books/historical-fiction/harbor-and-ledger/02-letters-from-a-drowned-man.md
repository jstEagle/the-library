## 2. Letters from a Drowned Man

The observatory smelled of cold brass and old salt, the way all high places near the sea did — as though the air itself had been wrung out of a sailor's coat. Mara Kavanagh stood in the tower's round chamber with her satchel at her feet and let her eyes adjust to the gloom before she let her hands touch anything.

The master clock dominated the room like a captive giant. It stood twice her height in a case of blackened oak, its face pale as a drowned moon, and it was silent. That silence was the wrong kind. A stopped clock was ordinary; a clock that had been *stopped carefully*, its hands frozen at eleven minutes past four as if someone had laid them down gently rather than let them fall, was a message.

"You came." The voice came from the stair — thin, winded, ancient. The keeper emerged into the lamplight, a man folded in on himself like a fist that had forgotten how to open. Mr. Aldous Vane, they called him; Mara had seen him perhaps twice in her life, always at a distance, always at dusk.

"Your letter said the escapement had failed," she said. "It said the chimes had gone irregular."

"They have." He did not come closer. He hung back by the stairwell door like a man who feared the machine might resent his presence. "Three months now. Sometimes it strikes the hour twice. Sometimes it doesn't strike at all. Sometimes—" His throat worked. "Sometimes it strikes when no hour has come."

Mara frowned. "That's not an escapement fault."

"No," said Vane. "I don't imagine it is."

She knelt and opened her satchel, laying out her tools on a square of oiled cloth: the pin-vice, the burnisher, the fine tweezers with the bent left tip she'd straightened so many times the metal remembered her fingers. Her mother's ring clicked against the satchel's clasp as she worked — she'd taken to wearing it threaded on a cord these past weeks, ever since the plague rumors began and everyone in the harbor district started speaking quietly about what could be carried and what must be left. You measured your life differently when you thought you might have to fit it inside one bag.

"Who wound it last?" she asked.

"I do. Every week. I've done it thirty-one years."

"And when did the trouble start?"

Vane's eyes went to the clock, then away, quick as a man avoiding another man's gaze on a street. "After the storm. The March storm."

*The March storm.* The same storm, though three years apart meant nothing to grief, that had given the sea back Tomas Kavanagh's boot and nothing else. Mara felt the familiar cold slide down between her shoulder blades and ignored it, the way she'd trained herself to ignore it, the way a clockmaker ignores the ticking of every other clock once she's absorbed the rhythm of her own.

She opened the case.

The doors swung wide on hinges she'd have sworn were newer than the rest — brighter metal, cleaner cut. Inside, the great movement rose in tiers, wheels within wheels, brass gone amber with age except where recent hands had polished it bright. She put her lamp close and began at the top, as her master had taught her: understand the whole before you touch the part.

The going train was sound. The striking train was sound — more than sound; it had been *modified*. Someone had fitted a secondary cam to the hour wheel, a small eccentric disc that would throw the strike train out of count at intervals no honest horologist would choose. Mara turned the disc under her fingertip and understood, with a lurch that had nothing to do with machinery, that this clock had been taught to lie. It would strike hours that hadn't arrived, or swallow hours that had. Anyone listening across the water — anyone who knew the code — would hear the observatory speak in a language only they could read.

Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself the way you notice rain through a window: it was happening to someone else. Later there would be shaking. Later, always later.

She traced the modification to its anchor point, and the anchor point to the case frame, and there — where a decorative bracket should have been screwed flush to the oak — the bracket sat proud by the width of a fingernail. A false plate. She had grown up in a workshop where her father hid nothing because he had nothing to hide, but she had also grown up in a harbor town, and harbor towns taught you what false bottoms were for.

Vane made a sound behind her. "Girl—"

"Hold the lamp," Mara said, in a voice that was not hers, or was hers from some deeper stratum, her father's voice worn like a borrowed coat. And Vane, thirty-one years keeper of this tower, took the lamp and held it.

The bracket swung aside on a pin hinge. Behind it, the oak had been hollowed — not crudely, but with care, the cavity lined with oilcloth. Inside lay a bundle of letters, tied with tarred twine, maybe twenty of them, thumbed soft at the corners from handling.

She drew them out. The topmost envelope bore no direction, only a date: *14th October 1847.*

Fourteen months ago. Fourteen months after they had buried Tomas Kavanagh's empty coffin above the tide line while their father held himself upright by sheer refusal, and the whole harbor had doffed its caps to the drowned Kavanagh boy.

Mara broke the seal because her hands had already decided, and read:

*Dear Keeper — Tell them the payment went astray again. Three months of silence bought is not silence kept. My father sends what he can spare and it is never enough for them. If they come to the house, tell him to give them the ledgers' second page and nothing after. Tell him I am alive. Tell him it changes nothing.*

*— T.*

The handwriting broke something loose in her chest that she had nailed shut at fourteen years old and reinforced at seventeen, at nineteen, every year since the sea closed over her brother. It was unmistakable. It was the hand she'd watched form itself across ten years of kitchen-table lessons — the peculiar backward loop on the *T*, the way he crossed his letters in a single slashing stroke like a man signing his own name to everything he wrote. She had teased him about it. *You write like you're stabbing the page, Tom.* He'd laughed and said that was exactly how it felt.

She read the letter again. Then the next. Then the next. The letters ran backward in time as she peeled the bundle, each one dated, each one in that slashing hand, each one addressed to the keeper of the observatory and no one else — instructions, warnings, accounts of sums delivered and sums withheld. The dead man wrote of weather in a cove she didn't know. The dead man asked whether their father still took his tea black. The dead man, in the earliest letter, dated eight months after the sea took him, had written nine words that Mara read forty times without their changing:

*I am sorry. It seemed the only door open.*

"Mara Kavanagh."

She had not heard the stairs. The tower's spiral swallowed footsteps the way it swallowed everything else, and now a stranger stood in the doorway where Vane had been — or Vane had moved; she couldn't say; the room had come adrift from sequence. The stranger was perhaps forty, weathered in the selective way of men who work outdoors but not at labor: browned at the cheekbones, soft at the middle, dressed well enough to pass in any company and plain enough to be forgotten in all of it. He carried a canvas satchel of his own and wore the easy stillness of someone accustomed to arriving where he wasn't expected.

"The letters are not yours," he said. It was not a threat. It was worse; it was bookkeeping.

Mara closed her fist around the bundle. "Whose are they?"

"You know whose." He stepped into the lamplight, and his gaze went to the clock, not to her face — appraising the mechanism, checking it like a man checking a horse he'd boarded at a strange stable. "I'm Corwin. I carry them. I've carried them two years, up the cliff path every month, and in two years no one has opened the case but the keeper." His eyes came to her at last. "Until the clock failed, and they sent a clockmaker. Of course they sent you. They always send a Kavanagh eventually."

"My brother is dead," Mara said. Her voice came out level and that frightened her more than trembling would have. "He was lost off the Murel Head in 1845. There's a stone in the churchyard with our family name cut under it."

"There is," Corwin agreed mildly. "I walked past it this morning. Very tidy stone." He set his satchel down on the keeper's table and began, unhurried, unbuckling it. "You'll want proof. Everyone wants proof first; grief argues longer than greed does. But you're holding twenty letters in a hand you'd know blindfolded, girl, dated long after his burial, and we both know what you're holding. So the question isn't whether Tomas Kavanagh writes those letters." He drew out a sealed packet, heavy, coin-shaped shadows beneath the paper, and laid it on the table between them. "The question is whether you're going to help me deliver the next one."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below them the sea worked at the cliff root, patient as arithmetic. Mara became aware that Vane had retreated to the far side of the chamber, that her knees were locked against the stone floor, that the letters in her fist had gone soft and warm with the heat of her grip.

*Tell him I am alive. Tell him it changes nothing.*

"What," she said, "is the next one?"

Corwin smiled without warmth, the smile of a man confirming a figure already entered in a ledger. "That depends," he said, "on what your father's ledgers say. And I think you'd better find out before he knows you know."

* * *

She did not remember descending the cliff path. She remembered fragments instead, the mind shedding continuity the way a struck bell sheds sound: the gorse scratching her ankles; the letters transferred from fist to satchel to the space against her ribs where her mother's ring hung; the moment on the lower switchback where she stopped and pressed her palm flat against cold rock because the horizon had tilted and her body insisted, briefly, on being sick. Grief overturned is a physical thing. Three years of mourning is a structure, and she had lived inside it the way you live inside a house — forgetting it was built at all — and now every wall had come down at once and the sky where the roof should be showed a brother alive, alive, alive, and hiding, and writing, and *sorry*.

By the time the rooftops of the harbor district rose around her, chimney smoke standing straight in the windless evening, Mara had rebuilt her face. She was good at that; she'd learned at the funeral, walking behind her father, matching her steps to his.

But her hands still shook, and there was no one on the cliff path she could tell why. Not the neighbors who nodded. Not the fishwives who crossed themselves against the plague talk. Not even — especially not — Declan Kavanagh, harbormaster, honest man, whose signature she was about to go searching for among the pages of his ledgers, praying to a God she'd half forgotten that she would not find it.

The office lights burned late, as they always did. Mara stood in the lane outside a long while, watching her father's shadow move across the papered window, bent over his books.

Then she went in.
