## 4. Corwin's Bargain

Corwin Ash had carried a great many things across a great many thresholds in his nine years as a courier — bank drafts sewn into coat linings, a dead man's ring, once, and twice a living man's confession — but he had never before carried an invitation. He turned the word over as he climbed the cliff path at dusk, testing it the way a moneylender tests a coin against his teeth. That was what this was, though the ring would sooner have called it something softer. *Terms*, they had said. *An offer of assistance*. The ring was fluent in euphemism; it was how they slept.

The observatory stood black against the last bruised light, its dome like a skull bowed over the sea. Below it, the harbor district lay already half-blind — lamps unlit, shutters nailed, the plague rumors doing their quiet work faster than any quarantine officer could. Corwin counted the lit windows out of habit. Three. A dying district kept poor watch, and poor watch was his trade's oldest friend.

He had watched Mara Kavanagh for two days now, from the vantage of a courier's patience: through the observatory's salt-fogged glass, from the bench outside the chandler's where he could see the cliff path. He knew what she looked like when she worked — her brother had that same downward intensity, that way of forgetting the world existed because something smaller and more honest had replaced it. It unsettled him to see Tomas alive in her face. Dead men were supposed to stay dead in more ways than one.

She would be up there with the master clock, elbow-deep in its brass intestines. She would have found the letters by now. He had made sure of the timing — arrived two days after she'd begun the repair, no sooner, because a grief needed time to curdle into questions before a bargain could take hold of it.

He knocked on the observatory door the way couriers knock: three measured raps, neither timid nor demanding, the knock of a man who expects doors to open.

Footsteps. A pause. The door opened a hand's width on a chain, and one dark eye appeared in the gap.

"You're not the keeper," the eye said. "He went down to the village."

"No," Corwin agreed. "I'm not." He held up the oilcloth packet where the fading light could catch it. "I believe you've been reading his correspondence. I've brought the rest of it."

The chain did not come off. But the door did not close either, which was its own kind of answer, and after a long moment she unhooked it and let him into the lamplit room where the master clock towered like a patient in a sickbed, its case opened, its gears laid out on wool cloth with surgical neatness.

Mara Kavanagh stood with her back against the clock case, arms folded, and Corwin gave her credit for that — most people in her position would have been pacing, or weeping, or reaching for something heavy. She simply watched him the way she probably watched a mechanism that ticked wrong: cataloguing everything, trusting nothing.

"You've read them," he said. It wasn't a question. "The letters in the case."

"I don't talk about other people's post."

"They're your brother's."

Something moved behind her face — fast, swallowed whole, gone. "My brother is at the bottom of the channel off Gull Point. There's a stone with his name on it behind St. Brendan's. I chose it myself." Her voice stayed level, and that cost her; he could see the cost, the fine tremor at her jaw where the levelness was being paid for. "So either you're mad, or you're cruel, or you're exactly what you appear to be, which I haven't yet decided."

"And what do I appear to be?"

"A man who delivers things." Her eyes went to the packet. "For people who pay you to."

"That's fair." Corwin set the packet on the workbench but kept two fingers on it, an old habit, a courier's small superstition that a thing wasn't delivered until the hand holding it said so. "My name is Corwin. I won't insult you by pretending I'm here against my will or under duress or any of the other stories men tell when they're ashamed of their errands. I'm here because I'm paid, and because —" he paused, surprising himself — "because I carried letters between your brother and your father for three years, and in all that time I never once met you, and I confess I wondered what sort of sister a man like Tomas Kavanagh would write home to."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to trim the wick.

"He wrote to my father," she said slowly. "Three years of letters. And my father answered them. And my father paid —" She stopped. He watched her decide how much to say to a stranger. "I found the ledger entries. Declan's own hand. Quarterly. Like rent."

"Not rent." Corwin unwound his scarf, sat uninvited on the keeper's stool, and made himself comfortable, because comfort was a language too and it said: *I am not leaving quickly, and I am not afraid of you.* "Silence. Yours, hers, theirs — the ring's word for it is *stability*, which tells you everything about how men speak when money does their talking for them. Your father has bought your brother's continued breathing for thirty-six months running. He has never missed a payment. Not once, even in the bad seasons, even the winter the pilchards failed. You may judge Declan Kavanagh however you like, Miss Kavanagh, but understand what you're judging: a man who chose his son's life over his own reputation and paid for it in installments."

"My father is the most honest man in that harbor."

"Yes," Corwin said softly. "That's what makes it work. No one audits honesty."

* * *

He laid it out for her then, because it was time, and because the ring's instructions had been precise, and because — though he would never have admitted this under oath — he wanted to see her face while she heard it.

"The men your father answers to are called the Ferrymen, which is the kind of name clever men give themselves when they want to sound older than they are. They run goods through your father's harbor: tobacco, brandy, French lace, and lately —" he watched her — "things with papers that say one port and mean another. Your brother crewed for them for two years before he understood what they were. When he tried to leave, they explained the terms. There is only ever one term, Miss Kavanagh, and it is *forever*. So Tomas did what a frightened young man with a boat and good tides could do. He went out on the ninth of March, three years ago, and a burned dory came ashore on the eleventh, and the sea was given a body it never had, and a stone behind St. Brendan's got a name carved in it."

"And the Ferrymen believed it?"

"The Ferrymen believe what they're shown. They let it be true because a dead man pays better than a hunted one — your father keeps paying, and a dead man asks no dangerous questions, and everyone's ledgers balance." Corwin spread his hands. "But balances are delicate things. And yours is about to tip."

He told her about the sickness then. Not the rumor version, the gauze-and-gossip version that moved through the harbor district in whispers — the real shape of it, the reports that crossed his desk because couriers carry everything, including news no one wants. Cases inland. Cases at the fish market street. The magistrate's office drafting the order that would nail the harbor shut within the fortnight, maybe less. No ships in. No ships out. No berth, no passage, no escape for anyone whose name was written on the wrong side of the quarantine line.

"Your father will be trapped here," he said. "His office, his ledgers, his complicity — all of it sealed inside the cordon with him, waiting for whatever the fever leaves behind. And the Ferrymen know it. A trapped man can't flee his debts. They intend to collect everything the moment the chains go on the harbor mouth."

Mara had gone very still, the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish. "You said terms. You said the ring sent terms."

"They did." He slid the oilcloth packet across the bench toward her. "Inside is the last payment schedule. One final sum, delivered by the first week of the quarantine — after that, the Ferrymen stop buying silence and start selling evidence. Your father's handwriting, your father's accounts, handed to the magistrate with a bow on it, and the family name dragged behind it all the way to the dock. Unless the payment arrives."

"And if it arrives?"

"Tomas stays breathing. The ledgers stay buried. Life goes on, such as it is."

She laughed then — one short, hard note with nothing in it of laughter. "And the second thing in the packet? Because there are two things. I can feel the weight of it."

Corwin looked at her a long moment. Outside, the sea worked at the cliffs below with its patient, indifferent violence. Somewhere in the clock's case a gear he had not yet seen her reinstall ticked faintly against its neighbors, a small insistent heartbeat in the silent room.

"The second thing is a berth," he said. "Passage on the *Merrow*, out of the outer anchorage, sailing the night before the quarantine closes. Cabin passage, legitimate papers, a name of your choosing. The Ferrymen offer it to you because they've done their arithmetic, Miss Kavanagh, and so should you: your father cannot deliver the payment alone anymore. He's watched now — watched by the harbormaster's clerks who suspect him, watched by the ring's own men who trust him less every season. He needs a courier he isn't married to and doesn't love. Someone quick, someone invisible, someone with a reason to walk unnoticed along every quay in the district." He let the pause sit. "Someone who repairs clocks, and might plausibly be making house calls."

"You want me to carry their money."

"I want you to survive. The money is the road that leads there. Deliver the payment and your brother lives, your father's name holds, and you board the *Merrow* with clean hands and a new horizon." He rose, took his hat from the peg, settled it with the unhurried ease of a man whose errand was concluded whether or not it was accepted. "I know how it looks from where you stand. Believe me, I've stood in enough kitchens watching families hear terms to know. But consider it kindly, if you can: the Ferrymen didn't have to make this offer. They could simply have waited for the quarantine and taken everything. Instead they've left a door open. Mercy wears strange clothes in this trade, but it does occasionally wear clothes."

"It isn't mercy," Mara said quietly. She hadn't touched the packet. "It's just a longer leash."

"Yes," Corwin said. "It is. Most mercy is." At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and allowed himself one honesty, since he'd carried so many lies past this threshold already. "There's one thing more you should know, and it isn't in the packet. The *Merrow* sails from the outer anchorage — but Tomas won't be aboard her. He's closer than you think, Miss Kavanagh. Closer than your father knows. And he has asked me, in his last letter, for one thing only: that whatever you decide, you decide it knowing your brother is alive, and hiding, and listening every night for the harbor bell."

Her breath left her like a struck gear slipping its tooth.

"The salt caves," he said, "below Gull Point. If you walk the low path at slack tide tomorrow, you'll find the tidepool entrance. He'll be watching for you." Corwin pulled the door open onto the wind and the dark. "Deliver the payment, refuse it, burn the packet — that's yours to weigh, and I'll carry your answer wherever it sends me. But go and see him first. A bargain should never be struck by a woman who hasn't counted everything she's bargaining away."

He stepped out and pulled the door closed behind him, and stood a moment on the observatory step with the wind pulling at his scarf, listening. Behind him, through the thick stone, he could hear it begin — the low, broken rhythm of a woman striking the dead clock's case, once, twice, and then again, as if grief were something that could be repaired by a firm hand and a smaller hammer.

Corwin turned up his collar and started down the cliff path toward the lights of the dying harbor, and did not look back.
