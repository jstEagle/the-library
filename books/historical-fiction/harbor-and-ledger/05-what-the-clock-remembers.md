## 5. What the Clock Remembers

The master clock came apart the way all honest machines did: reluctantly at first, then all at once, as if it had only been waiting for someone with the courage to ask.

Mara worked by lamplight on the observatory's upper gallery, the sea a black presence beyond the tall windows, close enough that she could smell it — salt and kelp and something faintly sour beneath, the smell of a harbor gone sick. She had spread a wool blanket across the workbench and laid her tools out in strict order: screwdrivers by size, pliers, the brass drift punch her father had given her when she was eleven, its handle worn to the shape of her grip. The letters from Tomas were folded in her bodice, over her heart, where they had lived for two days now like a second pulse.

She would not think of them. Not yet. There was a clock to mend first, and clocks were kinder than brothers. A clock told you exactly what it was, gear by gear, and if it lied, it lied for a reason you could find and hold in your hand.

The great movement rose out of its oak case like a cathedral in miniature. Brass wheel-work three feet across, the escapement a delicate ferocious heart beating seconds into the dark, and above it the chiming train, the part she had come chiefly to see. The keeper — old Halloran, stooped and silent as driftwood — had told her the chimes had gone wrong years ago. *Struck strange*, he'd said. *Off their count.* He'd said it quickly, the way a man says a thing he has said many times and does not wish to examine.

She examined it now.

The striking train was wrong. Not broken — nothing so simple — but *altered*. Mara lifted the fan that governed the strike's duration and turned it in the light. Someone had filed the count wheel's teeth, deepened certain notches and shallowed others, and pinned a small auxiliary cam to the hour shaft, a clever ugly addition brazed on by a hand more confident than careful. She knew every legitimate configuration of a four-train regulator; she had rebuilt half the tower clocks in the harbor district with her father looking over her shoulder, telling her *a clock is a promise, Mara-girl, you do not break a promise*. This was no configuration. This was surgery done for a purpose.

She counted the notches. Twelve hours, yes — but the intervals between strikes had been redistributed, some stretched, some compressed into clusters. Three quick, then a pause, then five. Two, a long silence, seven.

Her hands went still on the brass.

She had heard that pattern before. Every child of the harbor had heard it and thought nothing of it — the observatory clock had struck oddly for as long as anyone could remember, a famous eccentricity, a thing tourists remarked upon. *Listen, the old clock can't count.* Fishermen joked about it in the taverns. Her father had joked about it once, at supper, years ago, and her mother had looked at him in a way Mara had been too young to read.

Three quick, a pause, five.

A signal. A counting code any child could learn without knowing they learned it — carried on the most trusted voice in the harbor, audible for three miles over water, repeating faithfully every hour of every night. No lantern on a headland ever needed showing. No boat ever needed to signal from the offing. The clock did it for them, and the whole town listened, and the whole town heard nothing.

Mara sat down slowly on the keeper's stool because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

Her own craft. Her own guild's honest promise, bent into a smuggler's whistle. She thought of her apprenticeship papers, signed in her father's shop; of the guild oath she had sworn at fourteen with her hand on a finished movement — *that the work of my hands shall tell true*. She had thought the rot in her family began with Tomas's drowning, or with whatever her father was paying to bury. But here it was, older than all of it, brazed into brass by some clockmaker's hands years ago. The rot had been ticking above the town since before Tomas could walk.

And whoever had altered this clock had known movements the way she knew movements. Had understood count wheels and cam timing well enough to make a lie that ran for years without losing a second. It could have been any journeyman in the district.

It could have been her father.

No. Declan Kavanagh repaired clocks, he did not butcher them. But even as she thought it, another voice in her answered: *you found his handwriting in a ledger paying a dead man's keep. How well do you actually know what those hands have done?*

She worked until dawn because working was the only prayer she knew. She disassembled the going train and cleaned each wheel in benzine, re-polished the pivots, replaced the worn pallets. She would set the clock right — that at least she could do, that at least was hers. But she left the altered striking train untouched, laid out on the blanket like evidence, like an exhibit in a trial that had not yet been convened.

* * *

Halloran came up the stair at first light with tea and bread, and stopped in the doorway when he saw the pieces arranged on the blanket. He was seventy if he was a day, knuckles swollen like burls, and he had kept this observatory for longer than Mara had been alive. He set the tray down very carefully, as though the floor might resent even that small disturbance.

"You found it," he said.

"I found it." She did not rise. "Who cut the count wheel?"

The old man lowered himself onto the bench across from her, joints cracking, and for a long moment he looked not at her but at the sea, which was turning the color of pewter in the early light.

"Your father will be angry," he said. "That you found it. He always said a clever apprentice was a danger."

The words landed like a dropped weight. "So it was him."

"He didn't cut the wheel." Halloran's voice was dry as old rope. "I did. But he brought me the drawings. He stood where you're standing, twelve years ago, and explained to me what needed doing and what would happen to my granddaughter if I refused. There were men in the gallery below. I cut the notches while they watched." His swollen hands folded together. "I've cut them again every time the wear showed. Eleven times now. I know the pattern better than my own prayers."

Mara's throat was tight. "Twelve years," she managed. "The ring has used this clock for twelve years?"

"Longer. Before your father was harbormaster, even — but it was small things then, a few casks, men the town pretended not to see. Declan tried to stop it when he took the office. You'd have been a baby." Halloran's eyes came around to her at last, and there was something in them worse than guilt — recognition, an old man seeing his own long captivity in a younger face. "They don't ask twice, girl. They ask once, and after that you're theirs. Your brother learned that too, I think, though God knows I don't have the right to say it."

"You knew." It came out of her harder than she intended. "You knew about Tomas."

"Everyone who pays attention knows the harbor is dying of more than fever." He said it gently. "I didn't know your boy lived. I only knew your father's ledgers had pages missing and payments that made no sense, and that he aged ten years in one winter."

Mara stood and walked to the window because staying seated felt impossible. Below, the cliff fell away to the gray water; the harbor district lay along the shore, roofs and rigging, smoke rising thin and straight from a hundred chimneys of houses that might be empty within the month. Somewhere down there, her father was keeping books that balanced only through ruin. Somewhere in the salt caves, if Corwin had told the truth, her dead brother breathed and waited.

"Halloran," she said. "Why give me this now? Why not stay quiet, the way you've stayed quiet for twelve years? I'm nobody. An apprentice."

He pushed himself up, crossed to the corner of the gallery where the old tide logs were shelved, and worked his fingers behind the boards. What he drew out was wrapped in oilcloth and tied with tarred string — a book, thick, its cover scarred, its pages swollen with damp and age.

"Because you're leaving the striking train apart on that blanket," he said, "and any other clockmaker in this district would have reassembled it by now and asked me nothing. Because your father trusted you up here alone, and he doesn't trust anyone anymore. And because—" his voice caught on something, and he cleared it like a man clearing a blocked flue— "because I'm seventy-three, and my granddaughter is married and safe in Cork, and I am tired, Mara Kavanagh. I am tired of listening to that clock lie."

He put the book in her hands. It was heavier than it should have been.

"The true log," he said. "Every alteration, every date, every signal pattern, written in my own hand the same night I cut the wheels. Times of boats signaled out. Names where I knew names — and I knew more than they thought, because old men are invisible." He tapped the cover once, with a finger twisted by age and remorse. "It's proof. It's the kind of proof magistrates understand, because it isn't memory and it isn't testimony. It's a record kept in brass and ink for twelve years, and brass doesn't forget."

Mara held the logbook against the folded letters under her heart and felt the weight of both — the living brother's words, the dying man's record — pressing on the same ribs.

"Why me?" she asked again, quieter.

Halloran looked at the dismantled striking train, gleaming on its blanket like a confession.

"Because the clock needs putting right," he said. "And I'm too old to hold the file steady anymore. And because whoever puts it right ought to know exactly what they're unmaking — and decide, with their eyes open, whether the truth of it goes to the magistrate or into the fire." He met her eyes. "That's the choice your father couldn't make, girl. That's the choice your brother ran rather than make. And now it's yours, and I'm sorry for it, and I'd take it back myself if my hands would let me."

Below them, faint across the water, came the sound of the harbor bell, and beneath it — thinner, wrong, familiar — a bugle from the quarantine hulk. Then voices on the stair, boots on stone, two men ascending fast, and Halloran's face went white as gull-feather.

"That'll be the harbormaster's men," he said low. "Come to fetch the logbook I swore on my granddaughter's life never to let exist. Mara — the back stair, and take it with you, because if Declan's people want it hidden, then hiding it is the last useful thing these old hands can do."

Mara slid the logbook inside her coat just as the door opened.
