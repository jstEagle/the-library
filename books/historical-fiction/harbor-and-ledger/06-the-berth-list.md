## 6. The Berth List

The paper came at low tide, carried up from the harbor by a boy too young to have read what he delivered but old enough to have memorized the faces of those who would weep over it. Mara took it from him on the doorstep of the harbormaster's house, and the boy looked up at her with the flat curiosity of children who have learned that grown people break in predictable places, then turned and ran back down the hill before she could find a coin for him.

She stood in the doorway a long while. The fog had thinned overnight into something almost honest — a pale gauze over the water, through which the masts of the anchored fleet showed like stitches in a wound that would not close. Somewhere below, a bell buoy clanged with the arrhythmic patience of a thing that did not know how to count.

*By order of the Quarantine Commission of His Majesty's Port*, the document began, and Mara read the rest standing very still, because stillness was the only way she knew to keep her hands from shaking.

The harbor district was closed. Effective upon posting. All vessels barred from trade or passage save those chartered under the evacuation act. And there — in a clerk's careful copperplate, the words that turned her family into arithmetic:

*Berths allocated per household: one (1).*

One berth. The Kavanagh household: two souls. Declan Kavanagh, harbormaster. Mara Kavanagh, apprentice clockmaker.

Two names, one space. The clerk had not needed to write the arithmetic twice; it sat on the page like a stone on a grave.

Mara folded the paper once, precisely, along the crease already forming, and went inside.

* * *

Her father was in the kitchen, though *kitchen* was generous for the room now. They had eaten most of what the house contained weeks ago, selling what they could and hiding what they couldn't, and the room had contracted around its emptiness the way a dying fire contracts around its last coal. Declan sat at the table with his hands wrapped around a cup of chicory he wasn't drinking, and when Mara laid the quarantine order beside his elbow, he read it the way he read everything — left column first, top to bottom, the habit of a man who had spent thirty years finding the numbers in any sentence.

He read it three times. Mara watched his lips move on the third pass, silently shaping the word *one*.

"Well," he said at last. His voice was steady. That steadiness frightened her more than trembling would have. "Well. That's that, then."

"Father—"

"No." He raised a hand, not unkindly. "Let me be wrong about something for a moment." He set down the cup and turned it slowly on the table, aligning its handle with the table's edge, with the window frame, with some private meridian only he could see. "I have been harbormaster of this port for twenty-two years. I have signed manifests for eleven thousand ships. I have pulled drowned men out of this harbor with my own hands and written their names in my ledgers in my own neat hand so their widows would have something straight and true to look at." He looked up at her. His eyes were the same gray as the sea in November, and just now just as warm. "And at the end of all of it, the Crown grants me one berth on a leaky chartered brig and expects me to be grateful."

"They're expecting gratitude?"

"They're expecting silence," Declan said quietly. "That's what the berth is. Payment in kind. A man who has seen what I've seen, given a place on the last ship out — they don't want my thanks, Mara. They want me gone before anyone thinks to ask me questions." He smiled, and it was the worst thing she had seen in weeks, because it was gentle. "But I'll take it. God forgive me, I'll take it, and I'll be grateful, because you're coming with me and that is the only line on that page I care about."

Mara's heart stopped and started again, badly, like a mainspring catching.

"You assume—" Her voice cracked; she started over. "You assume the berth is yours. You haven't even asked whose name goes on it."

"It's mine by right. It's mine by office." He said it without pride, the way a man says water is wet. "And it's ours together, which is the same thing, because I am not putting you on that ship alone and staying here to drown politely in a quarantined district like a gentleman. Don't look at me that way. I've done the sums. There is no sum in which I leave you behind."

*There is no sum*, she thought, *in which I leave Tomas behind either. And yet here we are, father, doing arithmetic.*

Because that was the wound of it, the clean deep cut beneath everything else: she could not tell him. Three days since Corwin's bargain, five days since the letters in Tomas's hand had fallen out of the observatory clock's case into her lap like birds shot mid-flight, and she had told her father nothing. Every hour she kept the secret, the lie grew another ring, patient as wood. If she spoke now — *Tomas lives, Tomas hides in the salt caves north of the point, Tomas has been alive since the week we buried an empty coffin* — then the berth became a battlefield, and her father would do exactly what she would do: fight for the other name. Two people, each willing to die rather than take the seat. One seat.

Love made arithmetic. She understood now why mathematicians went mad.

"Whose name will you write?" she asked, because she had to ask something, and it was the safest cruel question in the room.

"Mine, obviously." He frowned at her. "Yours goes in the passenger column under dependent — no. No, that's not right either." He rubbed his jaw, and for a moment he looked so much like Tomas — the same tired tilt of the head, the same gesture — that Mara had to grip the back of the chair. "They'll want my signature on the transfer documents. Harbormaster can't evacuate himself without countersigning the abdication of the office. So: your name, my name, one line through the one that stays." He pushed the paper back across the table toward her, and his hand, for the first time, was not entirely steady. "You hold it. You're better with papers than I ever was, God help me, and my hand hasn't been true since the fever. Write us both down, Mara. Write us both down and let's not speak of it again until the boat comes."

She took the paper. Her fingers left faint damp prints on the clerk's copperplate, ghost-signatures neither of them had authorized.

"I need ink," she heard herself say. "And the good pen. If a name gets crossed out on an evacuation berth, Father, it gets crossed out properly. The magistrate's men will be checking these lists against their own. A sloppy strike-through reads as forgery."

Declan nodded slowly, and something eased in his shoulders — the terrible relief of a man who believes his daughter has agreed to save them both.

"Then make it clean," he said. "Whatever you write, make it clean."

* * *

Upstairs, in the narrow room where she had slept since childhood, Mara sat at the little desk beneath the salt-fogged window and laid out her instruments the way she laid out tools for a repair: pen, ink, blotting sand, the quarantine order unfolded flat and weighted at the corners with four brass clock weights borrowed from her kit.

The blank line waited.

*Kavanagh household — berth holder:* and after it, ruled and empty, a space the width of one man's future.

She thought about the salt caves.

She had gone at dawn yesterday, telling herself it was reconnaissance, telling herself anything at all. The tide had been going out and the caves breathed around her, exhaling the smell of kelp and cold stone, and in the third chamber — the one they had played in as children, where Tomas had taught her to whistle through cupped hands until the sound came back off the rock doubled — she had found signs of him the way you find signs of a fire: recently, unmistakably, everywhere. A driftwood pallet. A tin cup. Fish bones picked clean with a knife-blade's precision, and beside the hearth-blackened stones, folded under a flat rock, a shirt she had mended with her own hands three years ago, before the *Merrow's Bane* sailed, before the drowning, before the coffin full of ballast stones and lies.

He was alive forty minutes' walk from the house where his father drank cold chicory and rehearsed leaving. Alive, and starving by the look of the bones, and hiding from the very evacuation that might carry him free — because Corwin's ring watched the caves, and Tomas knew it, and a man who has been dead three years cannot walk onto a ship's manifest without someone remembering he exists.

Unless his name appeared on the list. Unless the harbormaster's daughter, who had altered chimes and read ledgers and learned from the best hand in the port how a document is made true, wrote the name herself.

Mara dipped the pen. Held it over the line. Watched a black drop gather at the nib, tremble, hang.

Her father's face floated up in her mind: *There is no sum in which I leave you behind.* And beneath it, older, from the funeral, his hand hard on her shoulder at the graveside while the empty coffin went down: *He was the best of us, Mara. Whatever happens, remember that. The best of us.*

He had known something even then. Or suspected. Or simply loved his son too completely to imagine the sea winning.

And Tomas — hollowed, guilty, begging her by torchlight in the cave to give the berth to Declan, *he stayed, I ran, it has to be him* — Tomas would refuse to come. She saw it already: the scene assembling itself in the salt dark, her brother's ruined gentleness, her father's immovable honor, and between them Mara with a pen in her hand, the only one of the three of them willing to commit a small precise sin to keep the people she loved breathing.

The drop of ink fell. It struck the margin, not the line — a small black period ending a sentence she hadn't meant to begin.

Mara blotted it carefully, sand and cloth, and set the pen down. Outside, the fog was closing again over the harbor, and the bell buoy counted its mindless hours, and somewhere north of the point the tide was turning toward the salt caves, filling them inch by inch with the cold arithmetic of the sea.

She had until the ship arrived. Three days, the notice said. Seventy-two hours in which to choose a name.

She looked at the blank line for a long time.

Then she blew out the lamp, folded the paper into her bodice where it lay over her heart like a second pulse, and took down her cloak — because the tide would be low again by midnight, and there was a cave north of the point where a dead man was waiting to find out whether his sister loved him enough to destroy their father.

The pen, she decided, would come with her. Some repairs were best made close to the wound.
