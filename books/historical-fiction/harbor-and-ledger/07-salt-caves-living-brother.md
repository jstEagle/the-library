## 7. Salt Caves, Living Brother

The tide was going out when Mara came down to the caves, and it seemed to her that the sea itself had conspired with the quarantine — pulling back its gray skirts from the rocks as if to say, *look what you've left uncovered.*

She carried a lantern in one hand and the berth paper folded against her breast in the other, and she had told herself the whole climb down that she did not know why she'd come. That was a lie, and she let herself keep it, because the truth was too enormous: three years of grief, and a stranger's letters, and her father's neat entries in a ledger that should have held only honest cargo. The letters had named the caves. *If it goes wrong for me, tell no one — but if you must look, look where the salt eats the stone.* Tomas had written that to Corwin two months ago. Her dead brother had written it in his looping, leftward-slanting hand, the hand she had watched form its first letters on a slate at their mother's knee.

The cave mouth opened in the cliff like a held breath. Inside, the light went green-gray and strange, filtered through seawater still trapped in the lower pools, and the sound was everywhere — drip and sigh, drip and sigh, the cavern breathing around her.

"Tomas," she said.

Her voice cracked against the rock and came back to her doubled, and for one humiliating moment she understood how easy it would be to go mad here, calling names into salt hollows.

Then, from deep in the dark, a match flared.

* * *

He was thinner than grief had painted him. She had spent three years imagining him drowned — bloated, or else serene, some sainted drowned boy with his hair drifting like weed — but the man who stood up from the shadows by the far wall was alive in all his shabby particulars. His coat was too big and patched at the elbows. His beard had gone ragged. There was a scar along his jaw she didn't know, and his eyes had the flat shine of a lamp low on oil.

"Mara." He said her name like a man testing whether ice would hold.

"You're supposed to be under the churchyard stones," she said. "Beside Mam. Father paid for the plot. I put rosemary on your marker every Sunday for two years."

"I know." His voice had rust in it. "I used to come at night. Before they moved me out here. I stood at the wall and watched you."

"You—" The word broke. She set the lantern down on a ledge with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to throw things, and there was nothing here to throw but the dark. "You stood at the wall. You watched me bury my whole life next to a box of ballast stones, and you *stood at the wall*."

"Yes."

"Don't say yes like it's simple!"

"It isn't simple." He came toward her into the lantern light, and up close she could see how badly he'd been eating, how the guilt had worn him the way the salt wore the stone — not all at once, but grain by grain. "Nothing about it was simple. Sit down, Mara. Please. You'll want to sit."

"I'll stand."

He nodded, and told her anyway.

* * *

He had been twenty-two and foolish with pride the winter the Ring first came to him. Not the word he used — he called them *the men from upcoast*, as if naming them gently might make them smaller — but Mara knew them now from Corwin's talk, from the ledgers, from the altered chimes above the town. They ran contraband through the harbor: brandy, silk, guns on the bad years, and always, always people's silence. They had needed a harbormaster's clerk who could read tides and turn blind eyes, and Tomas, ambitious and owed money on debts their father must never learn of, had said yes.

"Three voyages," he said. "That was the bargain. Three, and I was out, and no one the wiser."

"And?"

"And on the third there was a man with a knife, and a crate that cried." His face did something terrible and brief. "I got out of that trade, Mara, but I couldn't get out of the knowing. And the knowing is the thing they own. Once you know what comes through their harbor, you're theirs forever — unless you're dead. Dead men can't testify."

"So you died."

"So I died." He laughed, and it was the worst sound in the cave. "It was meant to end it. A man lost overboard in the spring squall — the Ring doesn't chase ghosts, and neither does the law. I thought Da would grieve, and hurt, and go on. I thought he'd never have to choose between me and them. I thought—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "God forgive me, Mara, I thought dying was the generous thing. I thought I was *protecting* him."

"By letting us weep over an empty coffin."

"By keeping him clean!" It tore out of him. "You don't understand what they'd have done. If Declan Kavanagh's son turned informer, they wouldn't kill the son — that's mercy, and these men don't spend mercy. They'd have taken it out of the family. Out of the *harbor*. Every ship he ever waved in safe, every berth, every man who works his docks — burned down to the pilings, and made to look like his doing. I'd seen it done in Fennwick. So I gave them a corpse instead of a traitor, and everyone got to live."

"Everyone got to lie," Mara said.

He lowered his hands. In the lantern light his eyes were wet and old.

"Yes," he said quietly. "That too."

* * *

She made herself ask the question then, though she had known the answer since the observatory, since the letters, since her father's handwriting had risen off the ledger page at her like a hand rising from water.

"When did you find out he was paying?"

Tomas turned away. For a long moment there was only the cave breathing, drip and sigh, drip and sigh.

"Fourteen months after," he said. "Corwin came to the cove at Dunnaseer where I'd been staying. I near took his head off with an oar before I knew him. And he sat down on the shingle and laughed at me, this awful soft laugh, and he said, *Your da pays quarterly, regular as rent. Has since the month after the funeral.*" His jaw worked. "Do you know what that is, Mara? To be mourned by a man who is also being blackmailed over your grave? To know your death bought nothing — that the thing you died to prevent happened anyway, quietly, quarter by quarter, in your father's own hand?"

"He thinks you're alive."

"He has to. No man pays silence for a corpse." Tomas's voice flattened. "He's never once tried to find me. Never sent a word. Do you understand what that means? He'd rather buy the ghost than face it. He pays, and he keeps his ledgers tidy, and he tells himself it's for the family — the same as I told myself drowning was for the family. We are liars, all of us Kavanaghs, and our lies are made of love, which is the worst kind, because you can't even hate them properly."

Mara thought of the office at night, the lamp burning late, her father's pen scratching. All those hours she'd admired — *there goes Declan Kavanagh, honest as iron, working for his men* — and it had been this. Quarterly. Regular as rent.

"Why now?" she asked. "Why do the letters start coming now, after three years?"

"Because the plague changed the arithmetic." He said it simply, and it chilled her more than any shout would have. "Quarantine closes the harbor, the Ring loses its route, and men like that don't fold up shop — they take everything loose and run. The last ships out of this port are worth more than we are. Corwin's masters want the ledgers, Mara — the real ones, the ones that prove Da paid them, because those pages are leverage over a harbormaster whose testimony could hang them. And when they've taken what they want, loose ends get cut." He looked at her, and the lantern flame doubled small in each eye. "I'm a loose end. Now you are too."

* * *

She told him about the berth then. She took the folded paper from her breast and held it up between them, and watched her brother — the drowned man, the ghost at the churchyard wall — read his own name's absence in the single empty line.

"One," she said. "One berth left for the Kavanagh family. The rest of the district walks or drowns, and we get one. Da assumes it's his. He's already started deciding what to carry."

Tomas did not reach for it. That was the thing she would remember afterward, in the years when she had learned to love him again without forgiving him entirely: he did not reach for it. His fingers curled back against his palms instead, as if the paper were hot.

"Burn it or give it to Da," he said.

"You're his son."

"I'm his *dead* son." Each word cost him; she could see it in the tendons of his throat. "Mara, listen to me. That man has buried a wife and a child. He's paid blackmail for three years to keep the rest of us breathing. If you put my name on that line, you're asking him to run from the law and the Ring both, with his name black, his harbor closed, and his living earned nowhere — you're asking a proud man to live as a fugitive on charity, and it will hollow him worse than grief ever did. But if you write his name, he gets out. He gets to stand before a magistrate someday with clean hands and say he kept his family safe. He gets a life. I—" his voice failed, steadied— "I stopped having a life three years ago in the spring squall. Everything since has been salt and waiting. Don't make him pay for my cowardice twice."

"It wasn't cowardice."

"It was exactly cowardice, dressed up as sacrifice, which is how cowards dress." He almost smiled. "We learned lying at the same table, little sister. Don't pretend you can't see through me."

She could. That was the poison in the reunion, she realized — not that he was alive, not even the three wasted years, but that everyone had chosen alone. Tomas had chosen to die without asking his father. Declan had chosen to pay without asking anyone. Each of them wrapping the others in lies like bandages, tight enough to strangle, and calling it protection, and none of them — not one — ever saying *let us carry it together*. Even now, standing in front of her with his ruined face and his beggar's coat, Tomas was still choosing alone: *give the berth to Da*, as if she were not also a Kavanagh, as if her name were not also written in the book of everything they'd broken.

"No," she said.

Tomas blinked. "No?"

"I'm not choosing for either of you. Not this time. I've had three days of everyone in this family deciding what's best for everyone else in secret, and it has bought us a burned ledger, a blackmailing courier, and a brother in a cave." She folded the paper and put it away. "Da sails or stays by his own hand. You sail or stay by yours. And if you both refuse the berth out of nobility at each other, so help me God, I will write my own name on it and leave you both here to argue forever."

For the first time, something crossed Tomas's face that was not guilt. It took her a moment to recognize it — wonder, maybe, or the beginning of hope — and it aged backward off him, so that for one second he looked like the boy who had taught her to skip stones.

"Mara," he began, "the Ring won't wait for family feeling. Corwin sold us out — I heard it from the caves, men coming down the coast road tonight, tonight, do you understand? They're coming for the ledgers, and for anyone who's read them—"

Above them, muffled by forty feet of cliff, the observatory clock began to strike.

Mara counted. She could not help counting; she was a clockmaker's apprentice, she counted strikes the way other people counted heartbeats.

Eight strokes. Clear, true, unaltered — the repaired mechanism singing the honest hour across the water for the first time in fifteen years.

But it was half past seven.

And below them, faint on the evening air, came the sound of oars — many oars, muffled, rowing hard against the ebb tide toward the harbor stairs.
