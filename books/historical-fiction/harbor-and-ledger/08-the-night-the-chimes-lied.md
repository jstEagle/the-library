## 8. The Night the Chimes Lied

Mara heard the lie before she understood it.

She was at her workbench, oiling a carriage watch by lamplight, when the observatory clock struck eleven — and struck wrong. Not wrong the way a broken thing is wrong, but wrong the way a forged coin is wrong: correct to a careless eye, false beneath it. The interval between the first bell and the last had been shaved. Compressed. She had spent three nights with that clock's guts in her hands, and she had put them back honest, gear by gear, so that now, for the first time in years, the chimes fell where God and Greenwich meant them to fall.

Eleven o'clock, truly. Which meant Corwin was already late, and men like Corwin were never late unless they intended never to arrive at all.

She set down the watch. Her hands were steady — clockmaker's hands, trained against tremor — but her heart was running fast and uneven as an escapement out of beat. Tomas was in the salt caves with a satchel of bread and the berth paper folded against his ribs. Her father was in his office above the emptying harbor, sitting with the ledgers he had promised — *promised* — to let her carry to the magistrate come morning.

Outside her window, the harbor district lay dark except for one strip of lamplight along the quay, and on the water, three ships rode at anchor where two should have been.

*They're here,* she thought. *Tonight.*

She took her tools — not all of them, only what fit in the satchel, because she had learned months ago to measure her life in portable things — and she ran for the harbor through streets that smelled of brimstone and salt and the sweet-rot stench of quarantine lime.

* * *

Declan Kavanagh had spent thirty years keeping books that could bear witness, and tonight they had turned on him like witnesses will.

He sat in the office with the lamp turned low and the true ledger open before him, though he no longer needed to read it. He knew every line. He knew the entries in his own neat hand going back five years — payments marked as *dredging fees*, *pilottage*, *harbor improvements* — each one a small civilized word wrapped around a coward's coin. He had told himself once, in the beginning, that he was buying time. That he was protecting Mara. That a man could pay a serpent until the serpent grew fat and lazy and forgot how to strike.

Then Tomas had drowned — or had not drowned; Declan still could not make his mind sit still long enough to hold both grief and fury in one hand — and the payments had not stopped, because the ring had learned what Declan had refused to learn about himself: that he would always pay. Paying was easier than truth. Paying kept his daughter's hands clean and his son's grave filled and his name unmarked on the customs rolls.

Until tonight, when Corwin had come up from the quay at dusk with a smile like a crack in porcelain and said, *The terms have changed, harbormaster. The ships change hands. The ledgers come to us. And you'll want to be gone before my friends arrive to collect.*

Declan understood then that he had never been a partner. He had been a ledger himself — an account kept open until it was worth closing.

He rose and went to the window. Below, lanterns moved along the quay in a pattern no lawful port would show: converging, deliberate, eight men or ten, moving toward the customs stair. And out on the black water, the ring's ships had drifted closer through the evening, patient as creditors.

He looked at the ledger. Every name in it that mattered was his own. But there were other names, too — dockworkers who'd signed receipts they couldn't read, widows paid off with hush money, young clerks whose only crime was trusting the harbormaster's neat hand. If the ring took these books, they would not burn them. They would use them. Every signature would become a hook, and the hooks would be baited with survival: testify that Declan Kavanagh acted alone, or share his gallows.

His people would hang anyway. Men like Corwin did not leave witnesses behind them; they left instruments.

Declan looked around the office slowly, the way a man looks at a room when he knows it is the last time. His father's barometer. The brass weathervane arrow Mara had made him at nine years old, engraved *HARBOR AND LEDGER* in her careful child's letters. The chair worn to the shape of him.

Then he took down the lamp and broke it across the desk, and the oil went everywhere, and the flame went with it.

* * *

Mara saw the light from four streets away.

It was not the orange bloom of a hearth fire or even the greedy glare of a warehouse blaze. It was white at its heart — lamp-oil white, the color of a fire fed deliberately — and it climbed the customs house windows like something alive climbing a ladder, and by the time she reached the quay the heat was on her face from fifty yards and the smoke was rolling black over the water.

*"No."*

She said it aloud, to no one, and pushed through the crowd gathering at the safe distance crowds always keep. Dockworkers in their nightclothes. A constable blowing his whistle at a fire that whistles could not arrest. Old Mrs. Enright from the net-house, weeping without knowing why.

And at the edge of it all, apart, unhurried, hatless in the wind of the blaze: Corwin. Watching the customs house burn with the mild satisfaction of a man watching a debt get settled.

"You," Mara said, and her voice came out flat and strange, and Corwin turned.

"Miss Kavanagh." He did not startle. That was the worst of it — he had expected her, or something like her. "You shouldn't be here."

"The ledgers." She grabbed his sleeve and felt the muscle of a man who had hauled cargo up gangplanks all his life. "Your people came for the ledgers and he burned them instead. Is that what your bargain bought? You were going to take everything anyway — the ships, the books, the—"

"The terms changed," Corwin said. Gently, almost kindly, which was somehow worse. "The harbor closes within the week. Quarantine, magistrate's men, soldiers if we're unlucky. Everything that can be seized will be seized, and every paper in this district becomes evidence. Your father understood before you did, I think. Look at him."

She looked. And there, at the foot of the customs stair, silhouetted against the fire he had made, stood her father — not fleeing, not fighting, just standing in the furnace-light with his hands hanging open at his sides, watching thirty years of careful handwriting curl into ash.

She had imagined this moment a hundred ways since finding his entries in the ledger. She had imagined confronting him, accusing him, making him say it aloud. She had never imagined simply watching him — watching the man who taught her that a mark made in ink was a promise made before God choose destruction over truth, and choose it calmly, the way he did everything. Neatly. In his own hand.

"Where are the ships' papers?" she demanded of Corwin. "If the ledgers burn, the ring has nothing to hold over anyone. You've lost your—"

"We never needed the ledgers, girl." Corwin's kindness curdled into something older. "We *are* the ledgers. Every man who took our coin remembers his own accounts perfectly well. Your father burned paper. Paper was never the problem."

Behind him, on the quay, the smugglers who had come to seize the customs house found nothing to seize but fire, and their frustration was turning loud and ugly. Someone began breaking shutters along the waterfront. Somewhere a ship's bell rang alarm — the *Merrow*, maybe, or the *Salt Rose*, the last vessels left in a harbor being eaten.

And Mara understood, standing in the falling ash, that the night was not finished with them.

"Your brother's cave is not as secret as he thinks," Corwin said quietly, close to her ear, and the world tilted on its axis. "My friends are thorough. My friends are also, at this moment, otherwise occupied. You have until dawn. Then I stop being able to be slow."

"Why?" The word tore out of her. "You gave me the letters. You carried me his messages. Why warn me?"

Corwin looked at the burning customs house, at the harbormaster's silhouette small and still against it, and for one moment something moved behind his face — not remorse, perhaps, but the memory of it, like the ghost-ring a cup leaves on a table.

"Because a man I once was died in a place like this harbor," he said, "and no one wrote to me afterward either." He straightened his collar. "Go to the caves, Miss Kavanagh. Take your brother and whatever name you mean to give him. The last berth sails at first light, whether the manifest says Kavanagh or not."

* * *

Declan felt her presence before he saw her — he had always known her footsteps, even over the roar of the fire — and he did not turn around. He could not. If he saw his daughter's face right now, he would explain himself, and explanations were the one luxury he had forfeited the moment he broke the lamp.

"You burned them," Mara said. Not a question. Her voice sounded scraped hollow, like a shell.

"There were names in those books that were never mine to give away."

"There were names in those books that were *yours*. Yours most of all." Now she came around to face him, and he had to look, God help him, he had to look at her — ash in her hair, eyes bright with a fury that had grief underneath it like ballast. "I was going to take them to the magistrate in the morning, Da. I was going to tell the truth and let you answer for it like a man, because I thought — I *thought* — that after everything, you might finally choose to stand in the daylight."

"And hang," Declan said quietly. "Is that the daylight you wanted for me, Mara? For Tomas?"

"For *us*! All of us, together, with clean hands, however it hurt—" She stopped. The fire filled the silence between them, roaring like the sea. When she spoke again her voice was lower, and worse than the fury had been. "But you couldn't. Not once in five years could you choose the truth when the lie was cheaper. So tonight you chose fire. And tomorrow I'll choose for myself."

"Mara—"

"I know where Tomas is," she said. "I've known for weeks. And I know about the berth. One name, Da. One name on the list, and I'm the one holding the pen."

She turned and walked away down the quay, into the smoke, toward the cliffs and the salt caves and whatever remained of the night. Declan stood at the foot of his burning office with the heat peeling paint from the doorframe beside him, and he understood, with the terrible clarity of a man reading his final entry, that his daughter had just measured him — weighed him like one of her little brass mechanisms, found the flaw in the works — and that she was right, and that he loved her, and that none of it would save anything now.

Above the burning harbor, thin and clear across the water, the observatory clock began to strike midnight.

Twelve bells. True intervals, honest weight, each one falling exactly where it belonged.

Declan listened to the whole of it, alone, while everything he had built went up in the dark — and then he picked up his coat and started walking, because dawn was coming, and there was one thing left in this world he could still do right.

He just had to decide what it was.
