## 10. What Can Be Carried

The launch came in against the ebb, oars dipping slow and deliberate, and Mara stood at the base of the cliff with the oilcloth bundle pressed to her ribs and did not move.

Corwin stepped ashore before the boat touched gravel, hopping the last foot with the ease of a man who had spent his life stepping between things — ships and shores, names and other men's names. His smile was already arranged when he looked up at her.

"Miss Kavanagh." He swept off a hat he wasn't wearing, an old joke of his, and she hated that she knew it was a joke, hated that three days of knowing him had been enough to learn his gestures. "You're up early."

"You're early," she said. "The berth isn't due until tomorrow."

"The berth is due when I say it's due." He glanced up the cliff face, toward the observatory where the master clock's face caught the first gray light, then back at her, and the smile thinned into something more honest, which on Corwin was somehow worse. "The magistrate's men reached the harbor district an hour ago. Your father's office is still smoldering, in case you were wondering how your night went. They'll want statements. They'll want the ledgers. And they'll want to know why the harbormaster's daughter is standing on the evacuation beach with a bundle under her arm and no family in sight."

"Then you should hurry," Mara said. "You wouldn't want to be here when they arrive. A courier with your debts to the ring — I imagine they'd have questions too."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Not fear exactly. Arithmetic. She had learned arithmetic from her father's ledgers; she could see Corwin doing his own sums now, weighing what she knew against what she could prove, the oilcloth bundle against his own neck.

"You altered them," he said slowly. "The ledgers."

"I preserved them." She let the word sit. *Preserved.* The way one preserves a specimen. "Every entry naming the ring is intact, dated, witnessed by ink and time. Every entry touching the dockworkers — the tally boys, the coopers, the widows who mended nets for your people — those are gone. In their place are entries my father might have written on his best day. You'd never know, unless you knew."

"And the magistrate?"

"Will hang four men whose names I gave him freely, and never learn that forty innocents ever touched that money." She shifted the bundle higher under her arm. "That was my price for giving them the names at all. The magistrate agreed because the magistrate wants convictions more than he wants completeness. You taught me that, Corwin. Everyone trades. The only question is what."

He laughed then, short and unlovely, and looked out at the water where the tide was dragging itself off the flats. "Your brother said you were the careful one. Tomas said — *Mara counts everything twice.*"

"Tomas talks too much. He always has." Her voice cracked on his name and she let it, deliberately, because there was no longer any use pretending otherwise. "Where is the ship?"

"Anchored past the point. We go out on the launch as soon as—" He stopped himself, but too late, and Mara felt the whole shape of the morning settle into place like a escapement finding its beat.

"As soon as my father surrenders," she finished. "You're waiting for Declan. That's the last condition, isn't it? The ring doesn't leave until the harbormaster is in irons and can't follow. That was the price of passage — not for me. For Tomas."

Corwin didn't confirm it. He didn't need to.

* * *

She found her father at the harbor steps, sitting on the lowest one, where the morning wash could reach his boots. He had dressed properly — good coat, cravat tied with the fussy precision she had mocked since she was nine years old — and his hands rested on his knees, open, empty, as though he had already given everything away and these were just the hands left over.

Behind him, two of the magistrate's men waited at a respectful distance, patient as gulls.

"Papa."

"Mara." He didn't turn. He was watching the water come in and go out, come in and go out, a man measuring something only he could see. "I watched them bring your mother home from the observatory road the day she died. Carried her down these very steps. I've been counting the tides since then. Twenty-six thousand-odd, near enough. A man counts a thing that long, he starts to think it belongs to him."

"It doesn't," Mara said.

"No." He almost smiled. "It doesn't."

She sat down beside him. The stone was cold through her skirt. Above them the harbor district lay empty in the early light, doors swinging, washing still strung between windows like abandoned flags, the whole quarter holding its breath for the quarantine that had already, in every way that mattered, fallen.

"They told me about the ledgers," Declan said quietly. "The magistrate's man. Said half the book was clean as scripture and half of it would hang the ring twice over. Said the clean half was in my hand." He finally turned to look at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly steady. "It's a fine forgery, Mara. Finest work you've ever done. For a moment this morning, reading it, I believed it myself. Believed I was the man in those pages."

"You could have been."

"No." He said it without drama, the way he corrected an error in a column. "A man is the sum of what he's actually done, love, not what someone loves him enough to write down. If I board that ship, I spend the rest of my life being the man you invented. I'd rather be the one who stands on these steps."

"The berth is yours," she said, and heard her own voice go thin. "By law it's yours. I can't—"

"You can give it to your brother." He took her hand then, and his grip was the same grip that had guided hers over a file when she was seven, that had steadied her elbow through every gear she ever cut. "I raised two children to tell the truth and pay what the truth costs. It seems I only ever managed the lesson for one of you at a time. Tomas ran from the cost. You're running toward it. And me—" he exhaled, long and slow, like a clock winding down, "—me, I'm going to walk up these steps and pay it, and I will stand in that courtroom and hear whatever you have to say, Mara, all of it, the true ledgers and the false ones both, and I will hold my head up. Because my daughter saved forty families, and the fact that she did it with my pen doesn't make it less her doing."

"There will be a trial. There might be transportation. There might be—"

"There might," he agreed. "And you'll write to me anyway, wherever they send me, and I'll read every letter, and I'll know my grandchildren — if God is kind and distant — will grow up honest." He squeezed her fingers once, hard, and released them, and stood, and brushed off his coat with the little precise motions she would carry in her hands forever. "Now go. The tide won't wait, and neither will that smiling courier. And Mara —" he paused on the second step, looking back down at her with an expression she had no name for, "—the watch you carry. My watch. When you wind it tomorrow, think of me at about this hour. I find I'd like to be thought of at this hour, of all hours."

Then he climbed the steps to the magistrate's men, and they fell in around him, and Mara sat on the cold stone with her arms around the oilcloth bundle and watched her father ascend out of her life one measured step at a time, upright, unhurried, a man keeping his final appointment precisely.

* * *

The salt caves at low tide smelled of kelp and wet stone, and Tomas came out of the dark of them blinking like something born underground.

He was thinner than the letters had let her imagine. Three years of hiding had hollowed his cheeks and stooped his shoulders, and his hands — once quick enough to rig lines in the dark — hung at his sides as if he'd forgotten their use. But when he saw her face and read the news in it, he made a sound she hadn't heard since childhood, small and broken and unmistakably her brother.

"He wouldn't take it," Tomas said. "The berth."

"He wouldn't take it."

Tomas laughed, and the laugh became something else, and she held him while he wept against her shoulder, this grown man who had died bravely on paper and could not bear to live honestly in person, or so she had thought. But standing in the cave with the tide coming back in, listening to him try to apologize in fragments — *I never wanted him to pay, I thought if I vanished they'd leave you alone, I thought dying was the cheap thing, God help me, I thought death cost nothing* — Mara understood that he had not been a coward. He had simply made the same catastrophic mistake their father had made: he had tried to carry everything alone, and called the carrying love.

"We're leaving," she said into his hair. "Both of us. One berth, two passengers, and a courier who owes me silence. Can you row?"

"I can row," Tomas whispered.

"Then say goodbye to the water, little brother. It kept you once. It doesn't get to keep you twice."

* * *

They went out on the launch at first light, Corwin silent at the tiller, watching Mara the way a man watches weather he has decided not to argue with. Tomas sat in the bow with his back straighter than it had been in three years, facing the open sea because he could not face the shore. Mara sat amidships with the bundle in her lap and her father's watch ticking against her breastbone, and she did look back. Someone in the family ought to.

The harbor fell away behind them — the black scar of the burned office, the empty docks, the cliff with its white crown of observatory. The magistrate's carriage was a dark speck on the coast road, taking Declan Kavanagh away to a truth he had finally chosen over himself.

And as the launch cleared the point, the master clock above them struck eight.

Mara closed her eyes and counted. Eight strokes, even, unhurried, true — every gear she had cleaned and reset singing in its proper place, the chimes running honest for the first time in fifteen years, rolling out across the water and the emptied harbor and the drowned district and the whole gray turning world. No signal hidden inside it. No lie wearing its voice. Just the hour, plainly told, the way her father had taught her all hours must be told eventually.

Tomas turned at the sound. "Is that—?"

"That's the clock," Mara said. "It remembers everything. Now it tells the truth about it."

Corwin muttered something that might have been a prayer and might have been a curse and steered them out past the point, and the sound faded astern, stroke by stroke, until it was only memory.

They sat in the bottom of the boat, the three of them bound for a ship and a coast and lives none of them could see yet, and Mara opened the bundle one last time to take inventory: the true ledger, testimony and mercy bound in one cover; her tools; her mother's ring; the photograph. Everything she had chosen to carry. Everything she had chosen to leave. It weighed less than she expected. It always had — that was the secret no one told you, that what can be carried is never really the weight of the things.

"What name do we use?" Tomas asked quietly, somewhere past the point, somewhere past looking back. "On the manifest. They'll ask."

Mara looked at her brother — alive, breathing, salt-streaked, hers — and then at her own hands, which had learned to write in two hands apiece, true and false, and had finally found a third way.

"Kavanagh," she said. "We keep the name. We just make it mean something new."

The watch ticked against her heart. The oars bit the water. Ahead, around the headland, the sea opened wide and silver and utterly unknown, and Mara Kavanagh — daughter of a harbormaster, mender of clocks, keeper of two truths and one lie and one long forgiveness — leaned into the morning and rowed toward it.
