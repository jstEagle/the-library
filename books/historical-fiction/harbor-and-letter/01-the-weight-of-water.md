## 1. The Weight of Water

The river taught me to count before the counting-house did.

Fourteen strokes down, hold, twenty-six up. That's the rhythm of a body pulling itself off the bottom of the Vessing with a full sack of oysters, and if you break the rhythm—if you panic or hurry or let your mind wander to what might be drifting down there in the dark with you—you spend your air on nothing and come up light-handed and light-headed, and Dockmaster Pell writes *deficient* next to your name in his book. So I counted. I had been counting for twelve years, since I was nine years old and my father's signature on a Company indenture turned me into an asset with lungs.

*Bram Thornbury, diver, term of twelve years, wages held in trust against board and gear.* Trust. The Company's word for money you never see.

That morning in early March, the ice still held the middle of the river like a lid on a pot, and we worked the open channels near the pilings where the current ran too fast to freeze. Hessel and I shared a skiff. He pulled rope; I went down. This was the arrangement, and arrangements were the only currency the harbor respected.

"Bottom's generous today," Hessel said, meaning the opposite. He meant the silt had shifted overnight and the beds would be half-buried, and every oyster I brought up would cost me twice the breath.

"Then I'll be generous back," I said, and went over the side.

* * *

The cold in March is not a temperature. It is a hand that closes on your chest the moment you break the surface and doesn't fully let go until you're dry by a stove an hour later. I have heard men describe drowning as peaceful, and I have always wanted to correct them: it is the *cold* that is peaceful, eventually, because the cold stops you feeling anything at all, including ambition.

Down among the pilings the world was brown-green and quiet as a church. My fingers found the oysters by shape—ridged, heavy, clustered like fists—and my hands worked without me while the rest of me did what it always did down there: watched, listened, remembered.

You learn things at the bottom of a river. You learn which hulls leak because you can hear them dripping three berths away. You learn that the Corvane barge *Maudlin Grace* rides two inches lower on her waterline than she did in autumn, which means someone is storing weight below decks that isn't on any manifest. You learn the sound of the harbormaster's launch, and the sound of the unmarked skiff that comes at dusk some Thursdays and ties up under the customs shed where no honest boat has business tying up.

I memorized all of it. Not on purpose, at first. It was simply that a body hanging weightless in brown water with nothing but its own heartbeat for company has nowhere to put its attention except outward, and after twelve years the habit had grown into me like a barnacle. I knew the tide tables better than Pell's clerks. I knew which watchmen drank and which merely pretended to. I knew that Quenn the tally-master had a limp that came and went depending on whether the paymaster was watching.

And I knew better than to know any of it out loud.

That was the trick of surviving Vessing, and I had learned it young, the way a gull learns the wind: be useful and unremarkable. Bring up your quota. Mend your nets clean. Laugh at jokes a half-beat late so nobody thinks you're clever enough to be trouble. A diver who notices things is a curiosity; a diver who *mentions* what he notices is a problem, and the Company solves its problems at the whipping post or worse. So I filed everything away in the dark rooms of my head and locked the doors behind me, and if anyone had asked—and no one ever asked—I'd have said I noticed the water was wet and the oysters were heavy and that was about the size of my education.

* * *

Twenty-six strokes up. Hessel hauled the sack over the gunwale and I hung onto the side a moment, gasping, the sky a flat white ceiling above me.

"Three more," he said. "Pell wants forty before noon."

"Pell can want," I said, and dove again.

The second dive was worse—the silt had indeed crept over the beds, and I worked by feel, prying oysters loose while the current tugged at my ankles like a child demanding attention. Halfway through the sack, my hand closed on something that wasn't shell. Rope. Old netting, waterlogged, tangled around a piling base. I should have left it; salvage mending wasn't my task and Pell docked wages for time misspent. But the netting was snagged on something, and hauling it free would take one pull, and one pull became five, and then I was untangling a knot that some fisherman had tied badly and abandoned years ago, and I thought, not for the first time, that the river never gives anything up cleanly. It surrenders in installments.

By the time I surfaced with the third sack, my lips had gone the color of the sky. Hessel took one look and made the call himself, which surprised me—he was not a man given to generosity before the noon bell.

"That's it. In."

"I can finish—"

"In, Bram." And then, quieter, in the voice he used maybe twice a year: "You've been down long lately. Longer than you think. I count."

Hessel counted. Well. Everyone in Vessing counted something.

* * *

We rowed in past the Company wharf, where the *Maudlin Grace* sat fat and low and lying about herself, and the stevedores moved crates stamped with the Corvane seal—a kestrel with wings spread, talons open, which everyone agreed had been chosen by a man who had never actually met a kestrel. Kestrels are small. They kill things bigger than themselves by falling on them out of a clear sky, and they do it alone. The seal got the talons right, at least.

Dockmaster Pell met us at the ladder with his book already open. He was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the general air of someone who had been weaned on arithmetic instead of milk.

"Forty-one," he said, weighing the sacks with his eyes. "Short."

"The bed's silted," Hessel said. "Half the eastern beds are buried. Ask anyone who works the channel."

"I don't ask divers. Divers exaggerate." He made his mark. "Thornbury. Harbormaster's office wants you Thursday. Gear inspection."

"Inspection," I repeated. Gear inspections happened once a year, in June, when the ice was a memory and the clerks had nothing better to do than weigh lead weights and count knives. It was March. Nothing better to do arrived in March.

"Thursday," Pell said again, in the tone of a man who enjoyed saying the same thing twice because repetition was a kind of power. He looked at me a moment longer than the conversation required, and I gave him back exactly nothing—a diver's face, empty as the underside of an overturned skiff—and climbed the ladder to the drying shed.

* * *

The drying shed smelled of tar and hemp and other men, and it was the closest thing I had to a home, being neither owned by the Company nor claimed by anyone else. I sat by the stove with my boots steaming and mended net while the others talked, which is what the others mostly did—talked, loudly, about things they only half understood, so that anyone listening would conclude they understood nothing at all. It was a whole harbor full of men practicing my trick, most of them badly.

"—says Marrow's fleet put into Graywater, three ships, flying no colors at all—"

"—no colors means piracy, that's what it means, and the tribunal will hang him from the customs gantry if they catch him, you'll see—"

"They'll never catch him. Old Kestrel Marrow could sail through the harbormaster's bedroom and out the window."

"There's a price on him now. Fifty crowns. My cousin saw the posting."

Fifty crowns. I kept my eyes on the netting and my needle moving, in-out, in-out, and somewhere behind my face a door I hadn't opened in years rattled on its hinges. Kestrel Marrow. There was a name I hadn't heard spoken aloud since I was a boy, since evenings when my father would go quiet and thoughtful over his pipe and say things like *there are debts, Bram, and there are Debts, and you'll know the difference when one calls on you.*

I was nine when the indenture papers were signed. I was ten when he died—fever, they said, though the fever came suspiciously fast on the heels of an argument he'd had with a Company man about mooring fees. And I was eleven the last time I heard the name Marrow in our kitchen, my mother saying it like a prayer she didn't quite believe in anymore: *he swore, Bram. Your father saved his ships and he swore on the water that the Thornburys would never stand alone.*

Sworn men die. Sworn men's fleets scatter, and sometimes they end up branded enemies of the Company with fifty crowns nailed to their heads. Promises are like nets; leave them in the water long enough and they rot, and whatever they were holding spills out into the dark.

I finished the net, coiled it, and said nothing. I was very good at saying nothing. Twelve years of practice.

* * *

That evening I walked the long way along the quay, because the long way passed the posting-board outside the customs shed, and I wanted to see the notice with my own eyes. Paper lies cheaper than men do, but even paper usually gets the name right.

There it was, pinned at an angle between a grain tariff and a notice about cholera upriver: *KESTREL MARROW, enemy of the Corvane Trading Company, fifty crowns, deliverable alive to the harbormaster or dead to whoever brings proof.* Below it, smaller, the legal language: *all persons found aiding, abetting, provisioning, or corresponding with the named party to be judged accessories and liable to forfeiture of goods and bond.*

Forfeiture of goods and bond. Bond. Indenture. Anyone bound to the Company who touched that name would burn their twelve years for kindling.

I read it twice, memorized the wording—it was what I did, collect things, store them, lock the doors—and turned away, and that was when I saw the clerk.

She stood in the doorway of the customs shed with a ledger under her arm, a thin woman with sharp eyes the color of harbor water, and she was not pretending to read the tariff notices. She was watching me read the other notice. When our eyes met, she didn't look away the way people were supposed to. She tilted her head, very slightly, like a bird that had heard something underground.

Odile Vash, the harbormaster's own clerk. I knew her by sight the way I knew everything on this waterfront—thoroughly, invisibly, from a distance.

I touched my cap, gave her the empty skiff-underside of my face, and walked home through the blue dusk with my collar up, telling myself the evening meant nothing. A clerk watching a dock. Notices going up. Gear inspections called out of season.

None of it meant anything. Things drifted in rivers all the time—silt, rope, rumors, names.

It was two days later, hauling in the dawn set, that my net came up heavier than any oyster bed could account for—and the river, surrendering another installment, finally put something in my hands that no amount of saying nothing could explain away.

But that is getting ahead of myself, and the river hates a rushed story. It delivers in installments. It always has.
