## 2. What the Net Brought Up

The dead man came up on the morning tide, which was considerate of him. Most things in Vessing arrive when they're good and ready, and the river takes its share of the rest.

I'd been working the oyster beds off Gullspit Shoal since before dawn, hauling in a poor catch and pretending not to mind, because minding is a luxury the indentured learn early to do without. Twelve years I've given the Corvane Trading Company, and in return they've given me a number—B-1147, stamped into a brass tag I wear at my throat like a saint's medal nobody prayed to—and a cot in a room with nine other numbers, and the right to keep breathing so long as I remain useful and dull. I have worked very hard at dull. It is the safest trade on the river.

The net went heavy just past the second bell. Heavy in the wrong way—not the drag of a full dredge but the lopsided pull of something that had opinions about being hauled. I braced against the gunwale and brought it up hand over hand, and there he was, tangled in the mesh like a fly in amber: a man in a courier's oilskins, gray as the water, his face already negotiating terms with the fish.

Now, a sensible diver hauls up a drowned man, rows to the Company dock, and reports it. There's a bounty for bodies recovered—two coppers, paid in scrip that spends like regret—and the harbor watch likes couriers especially, because couriers carry things worth recovering. The sensible thing would have taken me ten minutes.

Instead I looked over my shoulder at the watch boat idling near the mole, looked back down at the dead man, and did neither of those things.

Here is what stayed my hands: his fist. He'd been down there in the cold a day or more, and still his fingers were locked around a leather dispatch case, knuckles gone white as shell. Dead men don't grip like that unless they gripped like that alive. Whatever was in that case, he'd died deciding no one else should have it easily. And Corvane harbor watch does not recover things gently. They recover them, and then they recover the people who were nearby when they were found, and then those people are never quite seen again except at roll call, looking thinner.

I am useful and unremarkable. But even unremarkable men get asked questions, and I remembered—suddenly, completely—the winter two years back when a deckhand named Pellow fished a strongbox out of the shallows and turned it in honest as Sunday, and how Pellow got three extra months added to his indenture for "interference with Company salvage." Interference. As if the river belonged to him.

So I did what any coward with a conscience would do. I pried the case loose, tucked the body's arms back into the net, cut the mesh free of the anchor line, and let him drift toward the shipping channel where the watch boat would find him eventually and count their coppers. Then I rowed for the reed flats north of the tannery, where nobody goes unless the tallow smell doesn't bother them, which it doesn't bother me because I grew up downstream of worse.

* * *

The case was heavier than it looked. Lead-lined, which told me plenty all by itself—couriers don't armor ordinary letters. I didn't open it in the boat. I'm not sure I could say exactly why, except that some doors you want daylight behind you before you walk through.

I worked the rest of the day like nothing had happened, which is a skill I've polished to a shine. I mended nets. I counted oysters for the tally clerk and let him short me four in the barrel without blinking, because blinking invites conversation. I ate my noon bread and listened to the dock talk, which is mostly weather and wages and who got flogged for what, and underneath that, always, the low murmur about Kestrel Marrow—the privateer turned traitor whose name has been spoken in Vessing the way you'd speak of a fire, quietly and while checking the exits.

They say Marrow's fleet burned a Company warehouse in Saltreach. They say he's got a price on his head equal to forty years of my indenture. They say a lot of things the Company wants said, and I have spent twelve years memorizing everything while appearing to notice nothing, so believe me when I tell you: dock talk is a ledger too, and somebody writes in it on purpose.

By dark, the case was under the floorboard beneath my cot, wrapped in my spare shirt, and I was waiting for the dormitory to fill with the particular snores of nine exhausted men before I dared light my stub of candle.

Let me tell you about my floorboard. Everyone indentured finds one eventually—a place where the world can't reach you. Mine holds a whetstone, six coppers I didn't report, my mother's thimble, and now this. It is the whole of my estate.

The lock on the case was a courier's seal-lock, meant to show tampering. But the wax seal itself was intact, pressed deep with a sigil I didn't recognize at first—a kestrel in flight, wings back, stooping—and I sat very still for a long moment with my thumb resting on it, feeling like a man who has picked up a coin and found it warm.

I broke the seal. I want to be honest about that part: I broke it without hesitation, and I've thought since about what that says about me. Twelve years of obedience, and the first sealed thing put in my hands, I opened like a hungry man opening bread.

Inside was a single letter, written in a careful, formal hand on paper that had survived its swim better than its carrier:

*To the Thornbury name, wherever it endures.*

That was the salutation. My father's name, my mother's name, mine—all of us dead or scattered or sold, and yet the letter came looking anyway, patient as debt.

*I write from the north road with little time. The bearer carries proofs of what Corvane has done to make me an enemy in ink rather than deed. The Thornbury name stood guarantor for me in the year of the armistice—witnessed, sworn, and sealed. I ask that the debt of that season be honored now, when honoring costs something. If the name endures, let it answer.*

Below the signature—it wasn't signed Kestrel Marrow, it was signed simply *Kestrel*, which somehow made it worse—was a clause copied out in full, dated eleven years back. I read it three times. I know ledgers. I know contracts the way priests know psalms, because Corvane taught me both, and this was genuine. My father's mark was on it. His actual hand—I'd know those crooked capitals anywhere; he wrote like a man wrestling his own letters into submission.

*In consideration of shelter, provisioning, and passage rendered to the fleet of Kestrel Marrow during the interdiction, the undersigned stands guarantor, that should Marrow call upon the Thornbury name in any season of need, the debt shall be answered.*

My father promised protection. In peacetime. To a man the law now calls an enemy.

I sat there on my cot with the candle guttering and nine men snoring around me, and I felt the walls of my small, safe life rearrange themselves around this piece of paper. Because here is the arithmetic they never teach you at the whipping post: a guarantor's promise doesn't expire just because the promiser dies. And a promise of protection, kept for a branded enemy, is not called loyalty anymore. It's called treason, and treason on the Vessing docks is settled with rope, not coppers.

I should have burned it. That was the sensible thought, arriving right on schedule: feed it to the candle, go back to being B-1147, dive for oysters until my indenture runs out in— I did the math I've done ten thousand times—eleven months, nineteen days. Eleven months, nineteen days, and I'm a free man with nothing but my own name to lose, which is more than most of us ever get.

Eleven months, nineteen days. And my father, dead seven years in the marsh fever, reaching out of the ground through a stranger's drowned hand to complicate the count.

I thought about him writing that clause. He must have written it laughing, or near enough—he had that kind of humor, the kind that signs things. *Should Marrow call, the debt shall be answered.* He probably never imagined the call would come by way of a corpse in my net, arriving years late and addressed to a name that only existed anymore because I was too stubborn to let it die. Bram Thornbury, B-1147, useful and unremarkable. The last leaf on a tree somebody else burned down.

And underneath the dread—that cold, climbing dread of holding paper that could hang me—I felt something else move, slow and unwelcome, like a cramp in a leg gone numb. Recognition. This was why my father taught me to read the current and the ledger alike. Why he made me memorize tide tables and tariff schedules "for safety," he said, though we owned nothing worth protecting. A man doesn't arm his child against the world unless he suspects the world will someday come collecting.

He hadn't left me money. He hadn't left me land. He'd left me a promise, and promises are the only inheritance that gets heavier the longer you carry them.

The candle died. I didn't relight it. I lay in the dark listening to the river through the wall—the same river that had carried the courier to my net, the same river that would freeze solid within the month and turn the whole harbor into a road—and I understood, with the flat certainty of cold water in the ears, that I had maybe until ice-break. The spring tribunal comes with the thaw; everyone knows the Company uses it to settle its accounts, and a letter naming Marrow's guarantor is exactly the kind of account they settle.

Somewhere across the river, if half the rumors held, Kestrel Marrow was alive and gathering whatever enemies-of-the-state gather in winter. Between him and me lay black ice, Company riders, a harbormaster's office that would very much like to know what a drowned courier had been carrying—and eleven months, nineteen days of the only safety I have ever known.

I slid the letter back into its lead sleeve, put the sleeve under the floorboard, and rested my palm flat on the wood, the way you'd check a sleeping animal.

"Damn you, Da," I whispered, which is a prayer, where I come from.

Then I closed my eyes and began doing what I do best: noticing everything. The courier's route, north road to Vessing, means he came by the toll bridge—which means someone at the bridge house saw him last. Someone who might know whether he traveled alone. Someone whose questions, if the watch is asking their own, I'd need to hear answered first.

Tomorrow I would start pulling threads. I only hoped, as sleep took me at last, that the letter had told the whole truth about what my father's name was guaranteeing—because folded inside the lead sleeve, I'd felt the edge of a second paper I hadn't yet dared to read, and it was addressed, in my father's own crooked hand, to me.
