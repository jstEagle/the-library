## 3. Ledgers and Lies

The dead man's name was Pell Wick. I learned it the way I learned everything in Vessing: sideways, by accident, while pretending to be someone who couldn't add two numbers without moving his lips.

I'd gone to the Salt Row chandlery to buy pitch for the nets, and there was a knot of men at the door arguing about wages owed to a courier who'd drowned between here and Greyfall. "Pell Wick," one of them kept saying, as if repetition were a form of payment. "Twelve years carrying Company post, and they're saying his family gets nothing because he was traveling unregistered. Unregistered. The man had a satchel with a Corvane seal on it, didn't he? Or did the seal drown too?"

The chandler's boy looked at me, and I looked at the pitch barrels with the vacant devotion of a man who has never once had a thought worth hanging. That is a skill. My father taught me it as a child—*Bram, when the tax assessor comes, you are a boy counting fish*—and twelve years of indenture have honed it into something close to artistry. I can make my face into a room no one wants to enter.

But walking home along the quay, with pitch in one hand and the other curled around nothing, I let myself think about the letter hidden under the third floorboard of my sleeping loft, wrapped in oilcloth like something that might still bleed.

Pell Wick hadn't drowned by accident. That much I knew now, and knowing it changed the taste of the harbor air.

* * *

Here is what everyone in Vessing knows about Kestrel Marrow, which is to say here is what the Company has spent three years teaching everyone in Vessing to say: that Marrow was a fleet captain who turned pirate; that he skimmed Company cargo during the border war and then burned a warehouse full of records to hide it; that the tribunal of '41 found him guilty of theft and treason against the charter, and stripped him of writ and name; that any man who aids him aids an enemy of every honest hand on this river.

I could recite it myself. We all could. It hung in the taverns like smoke, and like smoke, nobody remembered where it first came from.

What nobody asked—and I include myself in this indictment, because I had twelve years of practice not asking—was why a man would steal from the company that owned half his cargo holds already, then burn the evidence of a theft so small it hardly warranted the matches. The arithmetic never worked. But arithmetic only matters to people who are allowed to check it, and none of us were allowed anything.

My father had done business with Kestrel Marrow. I remembered that much from before—the tall man with the grey-streaked beard who came to our house above the net-shed and drank tea with Father while I was supposed to be asleep on the stairs, listening. *A promise made in peacetime holds better than one made in war,* I'd heard Father say once, though I hadn't understood it then. I'd been nine. Promises were things adults broke at each other over supper.

Now I sat in the loft with a candle stub and the letter, reading for the fourth time the line that had ruined my sleep:

*The bearer carries proof sufficient to void the judgment of '41. The Thornbury name stands surety.*

Proof. Of what? A dead man's satchel might tell me, if I could find where the harbor watch had stashed it. And the harbor watch answered to—

To Harbormaster Cetch. Whose office I cleaned twice a week, because the Company believed even its most dangerous secrets deserved a tidy floor.

* * *

My father kept his accounts in a ledger bound in green sailcloth, and every Sunday night he'd sit with it open while the fire got low, running his thumb down columns of figures like a man telling beads. *Numbers don't lie,* he said once, and my mother laughed from the doorway and said, *No, but men who write them do,* and Father had smiled in a way I was too young to read and said, *Which is why we write ours plain.*

He died owing the Company four hundred crowns on a boat that sank with most of his honesty aboard. The debt passed to me the way debts did, and the indenture papers were drawn up within the month by a clerk named Odile Vash, whose handwriting was so precise it seemed to disapprove of me personally. Twelve years of labor for the Corvane Trading Company, term ending next spring. I signed because the alternative was debtor's prison, and prison doesn't have a river in it.

For twelve years I have been useful and unremarkable, which is the safest combination ever invented. I dive for oysters when the season allows, mend nets when it doesn't, haul crates, count crates, lose count deliberately when a foreman is watching and find it again when he isn't. And all that time—this is the part I've never told anyone, the part that felt less like rebellion than like breathing—I memorized everything. Cargo manifests glimpsed upside down. Which warehouses held tin and which held paper claiming tin. How many barrels came off the *Glad Tern* and how many the manifest swore she carried. The difference between those two numbers, multiplied across six years of shipping seasons, is a fortune. Someone has been collecting it. I assumed it went up to some Company office in the capital, clean as rain.

I never wrote any of it down. Writing is evidence. I kept it in my head, in the dark, the way you keep a stone in your shoe—proof of every road you've walked, and aching accordingly.

* * *

On Thursday I cleaned the harbormaster's office.

Cetch himself was at the customs shed, terrorizing a wool merchant, which left me alone with his desk, his cabinets, and forty minutes. I swept slowly. I am very good at sweeping slowly. The trick is to look bored rather than careful, because boredom is invisible and care invites questions.

The current-year ledger was out on the desk—not locked away, which told me something about how frightened anyone thought they needed to be. I didn't touch it. Touching is for amateurs. Instead I stood at the window beating a cushion while I let my eyes do the work, page by page, the way I'd read manifests for years.

Entry after entry, the same shape repeated: cargo received, cargo recorded, and the gap between them flowing—always flowing in one direction—to a second set of figures marked *adjusted.* Adjustments authorized under the emergency statutes of the border war. Except the war ended five years ago, and the adjustments hadn't. There were pages of them. And at the bottom of each adjustment column, small and neat and initialed, the same letters: *C.V.C.* Corvane Vessel Charter—no. I looked again. *Cetch, Vice-Consul.* The harbormaster had been signing the Company's books into fiction for at least six years.

And then, on the March page of three years back, an entry that stopped my thumb on the cushion mid-beat:

*Marrow fleet, 14 vessels, seizure adjudicated. Deficiency assessed against guarantor estate (Thornbury).*

Thornbury. My father's estate, long since absorbed, long since ash. They had entered a judgment against a dead man's name to square a ledger line that never existed—Marrow's fourteen vessels recorded as seized contraband when I knew, I *knew*, having watched them unload at Pier Four in the autumn of '38 with my own memorizing eyes, that those ships had come in full and been recorded empty. The theft wasn't Marrow's. It was committed *against* him, on paper, and when he protested it, the paper convicted him for objecting.

They branded him an enemy because he was the proof.

The cushion beat against the window frame. Somewhere below, gulls argued over fish guts, and the river lay flat and grey and indifferent, and I stood in a room I was paid two coppers an hour to clean, holding perfectly still while something inside me caught fire.

I want to describe it properly, because it was new. Fear I know like a hometown. Loneliness I know like weather. But this was heat, rising up through the chest, and behind it a clarity so sharp it frightened me more than the fear ever had: the certainty that what I was looking at was *wrong*, not by the law's shifting measures but by my father's plain-columns measure, by the standard of a man who wrote his accounts honest because honest numbers were the only inheritance he could leave. They had taken his name after he died and used it to seal a lie. They had taken Kestrel Marrow's fleet, his name, his whole life, and fed them into the gap between two columns of figures.

And Pell Wick had been carrying the proof of it, and Pell Wick was at the bottom of the river, and I was the reason the proof hadn't arrived—but I was also, now, the only place it existed.

I finished the cushion. I swept under the desk, around it, past the cabinet where older ledgers slept in dust sheets. I noticed which key hung on which hook. I left the office cleaner than any office deserves and walked out into the noon light looking like a man thinking about his dinner.

Inside, I was doing arithmetic. Not the Company's kind. Mine.

* * *

That night I took up the floorboard and unwrapped the letter and read it again by candlelight, and the words rearranged themselves around what I'd seen in Cetch's ledger. *Proof sufficient to void the judgment.* Wick hadn't been smuggling—he'd been *testifying*, carrying documents toward whoever had hired him to carry them, and the question that mattered was the one I'd been circling for three days: proof for whom?

Not for the tribunal. The tribunal was the Company wearing robes. Not for the harbormaster, who needed no proof of his own crimes. For Marrow, then—or for someone who meant to put it in Marrow's hands. Someone who believed the old guarantee still held. Someone who had addressed a letter not to a person but to *the Thornbury name*, as if names outlived their owners and could be called upon.

Father, what did you promise him?

I sat with the letter until the candle guttered, and the fire I'd felt in the harbormaster's office settled down into something steadier and worse: obligation with a body count attached. If I surrendered the letter, I erased the last copy of the truth and stayed invisible until spring, until freedom, until whenever the Company next needed a Thornbury name to balance a book. If I kept it, I was no longer a man who noticed nothing. I was a man the harbormaster would notice.

There was a third thing, though, that I only admitted at the very edge of sleep, and admitting it scared me more than either choice: I wanted them to answer for it. Not fined. Not reassigned. Answered. My father's plain columns demanded it, and I had inherited his handwriting if nothing else.

Tomorrow I would go back to that office. Tomorrow I would learn which dust sheet hid the ledger of '38, and whether the forgery ran deeper than one March page.

It was almost dawn when I heard the boots on the wharf below my loft—two pairs, unhurried, stopping directly beneath my window—and a voice I recognized as Odile Vash's saying, quietly, to whoever was with her: "Wake him. The harbormaster wants a word about the drowned courier."
