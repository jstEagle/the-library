## 4. A Promise Made in Peacetime

My father wrote letters the way other men built walls—carefully, with mortar between every stone, and never a gap you could see through. I found them where I knew they'd be, because Da was a man who believed that anything worth hiding was worth hiding in a predictable place. The tin under the third floorboard of the hearth at Thornbury House, which had not been our house for twelve years and was now a Company warehouse for salted herring, smelled of brine and mouse droppings when I pried it up on my knees in the dark.

I should explain how I got into the salt house at midnight without explaining too much, since explaining too much is how people end up in the river face-down. The Corvane Company kept two keys to every building: one hung on the harbormaster's belt, and one hung on a nail in the divers' shed that everyone pretended didn't exist because admitting it existed would mean admitting someone might want to use it. I had memorized the nail's location years ago, along with the location of everything else—the watch rotations, the tide tables scratched inside the shed door, the exact number of paces between the salt house and the water (forty-one, if you skipped). Being useful and unremarkable means you learn to carry a whole harbor inside your head and let it show nowhere on your face.

So there I was, kneeling on the floor of the house my father built, holding a tin box while herring brine soaked through my trouser knees, and inside the box were eleven letters tied with the green ribbon my mother used to wear in her hair before the fever took her.

The courier's letter sat in my coat pocket, its broken seal wrapped separately in oilcloth like something venomous. *To the Thornbury name,* it had said. I had read it four times by candlelight in my bunk at the indenture dormitory, holding the blanket up with one hand like a boy reading something shameful. Each time, the words arranged themselves the same way:

*Kestrel Marrow calls upon the sworn word of Aldous Thornbury, made in the year of the blockade. The debt stands. The protection promised is claimed.*

That was all. No explanation. No pleasantries. Just a dead man's IOU presented for payment, nine years after the man himself went into the ground and six years after Kestrel Marrow's name was dragged through the harbor mud as an enemy of the Company.

I had come to the salt house to find out whether it was real.

* * *

The first letter was dated the winter of the blockade, and my father's handwriting was younger then—looser, the letters leaning like they'd had wine. I read it by the light of a shuttered lantern, sitting on a herring barrel that creaked with my every breath.

*Marrow,* it began, which told me these were copies, or drafts, or letters never sent—letters written *to* Kestrel Marrow, not from him. My father, keeping his own counsel in ink.

*You ask what price for the use of my docks and my cellar and my silence. You ask it like a man buying cloth, and I will answer you like one. There is no price in coin. Coin is for strangers.*

*I have watched your ships run the northern ice for three years and I have never once seen you take from a Vessing boat. That is worth more than coin, though I'd thank you not to quote me saying so. Take the cellar. Take the east dock after moonset. And when this war ends—if it ever does—we will speak of peacetime terms.*

The second letter was shorter, and angrier.

*You cannot pay me in favors I do not yet need. Stop trying. A man who insists on settling accounts before the meal is finished has usually poisoned something.*

I laughed at that, alone in a dark warehouse among barrels of herring, and the laugh came out wrong, half a sob. Twelve years I had known my father as a quiet man who mended nets and paid his taxes and died owing nothing to anyone—or so the Company's assessors recorded. Here was another man entirely. A man who bargained with smugglers and found them honorable. A man with wine in his handwriting.

The fifth letter was the one that stopped me cold. My hands shook so badly I had to set the lantern down on the barrel, and the light swung shadows across the rafters like hanging men.

*If you are asking me to put the Thornbury name under yours, understand what you ask. The Company's writ says no subject of Vessing may shelter, supply, or guarantee the debts of any party the Company names an enemy. They have not named you yet. But I have watched Harbormaster Coyle's clerks copying their lists, and I know how a list becomes a noose.*

*So here is my answer, and I make it once, and I will not unsay it: the Thornbury name is yours to call. Whatever they brand you, whatever they take from me for it, my name will stand under yours as shield and surety. Not for coin. For the three winters your guns kept the raiders off my river while the Company's own guardships sat in the roads and counted their cargo fees.*

*A promise made in peacetime is decoration. This one is made in war, and wars do not forgive, and neither do I. Call on it whenever you must. I only pray you never have cause.*

Below that, in different ink, added later—I guessed from the steadier hand, years later—a single line:

*Sworn before witnesses, Aldous Thornbury, guarantor. Let any who hold this paper hold my bond.*

There were witness marks beneath. Two of them. One was a scrawl I recognized from harbor documents: **O. Vash**, in a clerk's round, careful script. The harbormaster's clerk. Odile Vash had witnessed my father's oath.

The other name was unfamiliar—*M. Ferane*—but the signature had been crossed out and rewritten twice, the way a hand does when it's trembling.

* * *

I sat on that herring barrel for a long time after that, listening to the river groan under the ice, which it does at night in a way that sounds like furniture being moved in an upstairs room by nobody.

Here is what I understood, assembling it the way I assemble everything—silently, completely, showing nothing:

Kestrel Marrow had sheltered behind my father's name during the war, or meant to. When the Company turned on him—branded him, seized his assets, posted his description at every customs house—they had done it with ledgers, and those ledgers, if the drowned courier carried what I suspected, were forged. And my father's sworn guarantee, witnessed and sealed, was the one document that could stand against it. Because you cannot call a man enemy for debts when a sworn guarantor's name sits under those debts, unless you also prove the guarantee void—and to prove it void, you must open the very ledgers that would damn you.

It was elegant. It was why they'd killed the courier rather than simply search him, probably—no, that wasn't right either. Or maybe it was. I was learning fast that the Company's arithmetic had a lot of bodies in it.

And my father had died without telling me. Six years old when the fever came through, I remembered him mostly as hands: hands mending nets, hands lifting me onto the dock pilings, hands writing things at night that I had assumed were inventory. He had made this oath and told me nothing, told *anyone* nothing, and the promise had lain under a floorboard like a seed under snow, waiting.

Waiting too long. That was the bitter root of it. The letter in my pocket called on a dead man's bond, and the law said a dead man's bond dies with him, and the Company's law especially said so. If I walked into the tribunal and produced that letter, they would laugh me out of the hall and then arrest me for possessing it.

Unless the ledgers were forged. Unless proof existed that the debt itself was a fiction, manufactured to strip Kestrel Marrow of everything he had and brand anyone who might shelter him. Then the guarantee wasn't decoration. Then it was a blade, because it forced the question: *what debt? Show us the true books.*

I thought about my father writing *I will not unsay it.* I thought about how long silence had kept me alive. Useful and unremarkable. Notice nothing, say less. Twelve years of being a ghost in plain clothes, and here was my father's ghost handing me a reason to be seen.

I am not a brave person. I want that on the record, whoever finds this account, if anyone ever does. Brave people feel fear and walk through it smiling. I intended to calculate my way through it like a diver calculating air: how long can I hold, how far is the surface, what happens to my body past the point where the body wants to give up.

But calculation requires knowing what's on the bottom, and I did not know. I had a dead courier, a live claim, eleven letters, and a witness whose name I'd seen that very week in the harbormaster's office, attached to sharp eyes that had already looked at me one time too many.

Odile Vash. She had watched me turn in the day's salvage with that particular stillness she had, the stillness of a heron over water, and asked whether the net had brought up "anything unusual." And I had said no, in the flat voice I use for lying, which is indistinguishable from the voice I use for everything else.

She had held my gaze perhaps two seconds longer than necessary.

Either she knew about the letter and was measuring me for a hook, or she knew about the letters—these letters, my father's—and was waiting to see what I'd do with my inheritance. With clerks, it is nearly impossible to tell the difference between an ally and a trap, because both spend their days pretending to be paperwork.

I retied the green ribbon. I put the tin back under the floorboard, then reconsidered and took the five most important letters out and sewed them flat against my ribs inside my shirt lining, because floorboards can be pried up by anyone and seams can only be found by someone looking for a seam. Then I pinched out the lantern and let the dark have the room.

Forty-one paces back to the water's edge, skipping. The river lay black and seamed with white, ice thick enough to bear a wagon, and out beyond the point, somewhere north in the dark, Kestrel Marrow was supposed to be holding some rocky scrap of coast with men loyal to a promise made before I could read.

Nine years late, the call had come. Too late for my father to answer it.

Which left me.

I stood at the water's edge with the letters warm against my ribs and understood, with the calm of a man watching his own net go down, that I would have to cross that river before the tribunal convened—that I would have to find Odile Vash first and learn whether her eyes meant warning or noose—and that whichever answer waited on the other side of the ice, there was no version of my life anymore where I stayed small enough to survive it.

The river cracked somewhere out in the dark, long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Spring was coming early this year. So was the tribunal. And I had perhaps three weeks to become, for the first time in twelve years, remarkable.
