## 5. The Harbormaster's Eye

*Odile*

The harbormaster's office smelled of tallow, tar, and the particular staleness of men who believe their own arithmetic. I had kept its ledgers for nine years, which meant I knew exactly how much of what was written in them was true. Roughly a third, if one was generous. I am not generous.

Corvane paid me to be precise. Nobody paid me to be honest. These were compatible employments for most of my life, right up until the courier fished out of the shallows failed to appear on any manifest, and his letter failed to appear in the recovered-effects inventory, and Harbor-Sergeant Dunmoor's report failed to mention that the dead man's fingers had been pried open before he reached the mortuary slab.

Three failures. In my experience, things fail in threes only when someone is helping them.

"Vash." Harbormaster Cregg Vane didn't look up from his brandy. "The Thornbury diver. The one who found the courier."

"Bram Thornbury. Indentured, twelve years served of fourteen. No infractions. Not one, in twelve years."

"And that doesn't strike you as odd?"

"It strikes me as either very good or very careful, sir, and in my experience those look identical from across a room."

He grunted and turned back to his cup. Vane trusted me the way a man trusts a well-made lock: entirely, and without ever wondering what it would cost him if the key changed hands.

I went back to my desk and pulled the courier file for the fourth time. Name: unlisted. Route: unlisted. Employer: *unlisted*, though every courier on the Vessing run carried something that said Corvane somewhere, stamped or stitched or tattooed behind the ear. This man carried nothing. Which meant he wasn't carrying nothing for Corvane.

Which meant somebody at this desk — not mine, but near enough — had made him into a blank.

I am not a brave woman. I want that on the record, wherever records go when ledgers burn. But I know ink the way a priest knows scripture, and I had spent three evenings comparing the Kestrel Marrow debt entries against the freight logs they were supposed to reconcile, and they did not. The sums were forged by someone with an excellent hand and no patience, because patience is what you forge when you're innocent and haste is what you forge when you're afraid.

I copied two pages anyway. Then, being neither brave nor stupid, I began looking for the person reckless enough to have already noticed.

There was exactly one candidate: a diver who had hauled a drowned man out of the river and reported it in eleven flat sentences without once mentioning what the dead man held.

* * *

*Bram*

Company summons arrived the way weather arrives in Vessing — written on everyone's face before it breaks over your own. Mine came Tuesday, delivered by a harbor runner who wouldn't meet my eye, which told me more than the paper did.

*Bram Thornbury, indentured, to present at the harbormaster's office, third bell.*

I spent the walk rehearsing. Twelve years of practice at being unremarkable, and here was the whole trick of it distilled: say less than you know, notice less than you see, and let them finish their own sentences so they never ask you to finish yours.

The clerk who received me was not what I expected. I'd imagined some half-asleep functionary; instead there was a woman with gray-shot hair pinned so precisely she could have used it as a drafting compass, and eyes that did arithmetic on me before I'd cleared the door.

"Bram Thornbury." She said it like she was checking a sum. "Odile Vash. Sit."

I sat. The chair was positioned too close to her desk, which I decided was either carelessness or theater.

"You pulled a body from the shallows eleven days ago," she said. "A courier. You'll forgive me for skipping the part where we establish that you remember."

"Yes, miss."

"You reported it within the hour. Commendable. You described the body, the tide, the net damage." She opened a folder — a real folder, with a wax tab, which was more ceremony than drowned oyster-men ever got. "You did not describe anything he was carrying."

My pulse did something I hoped my face declined to attend. "His hands were closed. I didn't pry at a dead man, miss. That's watch work."

"It is watch work." She let the silence sit a moment, weighing it. "And yet Harbor-Sergeant Dunmoor's report says the effects were inventoried at the mortuary, and the inventory lists one knife, one coin, one boot gone. No letter."

"Was there a letter?"

I regretted it before the words finished leaving me. It was the wrong question in the wrong direction — an innocent man asks *was he robbed*, not *was there a letter*. Her pen stopped moving.

"That," she said softly, "is a very interesting thing to wonder about."

The rope tightened one loop. I could feel it around my ribs now, the paranoia, snug as rigging. Every exit I mapped out of this conversation had Company seals on the far side.

"I heard talk," I said, falling back on the oldest lie in the harbor: other people. "Dockhands say the courier run was Company business gone strange. Talk reaches the water. Water reaches us."

"Mm." She studied me for a long moment, and I understood I had not fooled her even slightly — and that she had known I wouldn't, and had asked anyway, to see what shape my lying took. Some people gamble with coin. Odile Vash gambled with understanding.

Then she did something I still turn over at night. She slid a folded paper across the desk, tapped it once with two fingers, and said, "Courier routes are filed quarterly. This quarter's hasn't been entered yet. Clerks misplace things. Divers sometimes find them."

She withdrew her hand. The paper stayed.

"For the record, Thornbury, I've asked you nothing, you've told me nothing, and this interview concerned the condition of a net." A pause. "Your net was damaged in the recovery. File a claim. Damages come out of the Company's account, not yours. Take what the water owes you."

It was the most elaborate sentence anyone had ever aimed at me, and every clause of it meant the same thing: *Take it, read it, and don't come back here.*

"Thank you, miss," I said. "About the net."

"About the net," she agreed.

I took the paper. My hands were steady until the office door closed behind me, and then they weren't, and I walked down to the waterline and stood among the pilings with the folded page burning a hole through three layers of coat, trying to decide whether I'd just been handed a lantern or led into a snare.

* * *

*Odile*

He took it left-handed, which surprised me. Most people reach for dangerous things with their strong hand, as if boldness lived in the muscle. He reached with the weak one, keeping the strong hand free — for the doorframe, for balance, for whatever a man keeps his strength free *for*. Twelve years of indenture teaches a specific grammar of survival, and Thornbury spoke it fluently.

I liked him immediately, which was inconvenient.

Cregg Vane believes the diver knows nothing. Dunmoor believes the diver stole the letter and can't read it. Both are wrong in instructive ways. The diver knows *something* — the left-handed reach tells me he suspects the paper might bite — and if he can't read the ledger columns himself, he knows someone who can. Smugglers' children always do.

That evening I locked the office, checked the lock twice as I do, and walked home the long way along the ice road. The river groaned underfoot, that low shifting complaint it makes in late winter, like a large animal dreaming. Half of Vessing says the sound frightens them. I find it honest. Nothing else in this town tells you plainly when it's about to give way.

At the bend by the salt sheds, Dunmoor fell into step beside me. He does that. He thinks it's subtle.

"Clerk Vash. Long hours."

"The ledgers don't file themselves."

"No," he said. "They get filed *properly*, don't they. That's the work." He smiled at the ice. "Vane tells me you interviewed the Thornbury diver today. About a net."

"Damages claim. Routine."

"Mm. Funny thing about routine." He stopped, and I stopped, because stopping seemed safer than demonstrating otherwise. "There was a letter on that corpse, Vash. There's always a letter. And letters that don't reach their address have a way of making trouble for everyone who touched them. Everyone." His eyes went, briefly and deliberately, to my satchel. "You'd want to be sure you haven't touched anything."

"Good night, Sergeant."

He peeled away toward the watch-house. I walked on, unhurried, counting my heartbeats because it steadied the hand, and by the time I reached my door I had finished the sum I'd been carrying all day.

Dunmoor knew the letter existed. Dunmoor had searched my satchel's history, if not my satchel. And Dunmoor had just told me so with a smile, which meant the forgery at the top of this web was nervous enough to start watching the clerks.

When the powerful begin watching clerks, clerks have approximately two choices, and one of them is a coffin.

So I sat at my own table, by my own candle, and finished copying the third page. If Bram Thornbury was clever enough to survive twelve invisible years, he was clever enough to understand what I'd put under his hand. And if he wasn't —

Well. Then the rope finds him first, and I will have lost nothing but a piece of paper that never officially existed.

I blew out the candle. Somewhere out on the black river, the ice groaned again, longer this time, and I lay in the dark listening and thought: *soon*.

* * *

*Bram*

I waited three days before opening the courier-route sheet, which Aunt Sessie later called "either wisdom or cowardice wearing wisdom's coat." Three days of hauling oysters with a dead man's mystery folded against my ribs, three nights of listening to it whisper. On the third night I finally spread it under the floorboard candle, beside the sealed letter I should have burned and the ledger fragments I definitely should have.

The route sheet was genuine — I know Company paper the way I know my own scars. And there it was, in the unfiled quarter: the courier's run, his stops, and the final entry in a different hand. *Diverted. Veray Landing. Hold for H.* Veray Landing was a mudflat. Nobody held anything at Veray Landing except the harbormaster's private salvage crews.

*Hold for H.* Hold for the harbormaster.

The drowned man hadn't been lost. He'd been *routed* — sent to the one place the evidence could be caught, drowned, and forgotten. And the letter in my floorboards, the promise with my family's name on it, had been swimming in the same net the whole time.

I sat back on my heels and felt the fear change, the way water changes when it goes under the hull — same substance, different pressure. They hadn't just killed a courier. They were hunting everything he carried, and I was carrying it, and Odile Vash had handed me the map to the trap with her own two fingers and said *take what the water owes you.*

Warning or lure. I turned it over and over, and both answers fit, which is the worst kind of fit.

What I knew for certain was smaller and harder: the tribunal was weeks away, the ice was thinning by the hour, and somewhere across the black river a man named Kestrel Marrow was waiting for a call my father had sworn to answer.

Someone knocked on the shutter. Two taps, a pause, one tap — Aunt Sessie's signal, the one we hadn't used since I was small enough to hide under her cargo tarps.

I put out the candle. Whatever came next, the invisibility was over.
