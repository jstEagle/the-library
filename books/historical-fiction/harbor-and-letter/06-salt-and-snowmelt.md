## 6. Salt and Snowmelt

The river froze the way grief does: gradually, and then all at once, and then for a long time nothing moved at all.

By the first week of Frostmoon, the Vessing was a road of black glass running from the Company docks to the far marshes, thick enough that the oyster barges sat locked in their cradles of ice and the whole port walked out onto the water like it was cobbled street. Children skated where I'd nearly drowned twice. Carters took shortcuts across what had killed men I'd known. The harbor had a long memory and a short one, depending on what was convenient.

I had decided to be inconvenient.

It didn't feel like a decision. It felt like a stone in my boot that I kept meaning to shake out, and then one morning I realized I'd been walking on it for a week and it had worn through to the bone. The letter sat sewn into my mattress ticking. The courier's name—Fenwick, I'd learned, a Company man eleven years, no family recorded, which meant the Company *was* his family, which meant someone in that family had drowned him—sat in my mouth like a coin I couldn't stop biting.

I could do nothing. That was the sane choice. Do nothing, stay small, let Kestrel Marrow hang as an enemy of the Company in absentia when the tribunal convened at ice-break, and live out my indenture as an unremarkable man with an unremarkable grave waiting. My father had made a promise. Dead men's promises are like dead men's debts; everyone agrees they're real, and no one expects you to pay them.

Except I had read his hand. *Any call they make, I will answer, and my children after me.* He'd written it in the good ink, the ink he saved for contracts and christenings. My father did not use good ink lightly.

So I went looking for people who owed him favors, or thought they did, or could be reminded.

* * *

Brother Anselm kept a chapel that wasn't a chapel anymore—the Company had stripped his orders eight years back for, as the official record put it, "doctrinal irregularity," and as Anselm himself put it, "refusing to preach that the Company's ledgers were scripture." He still said mass on Drownsmoor mornings for whoever came, which was mostly widows and one dog, and he still wore the gray cassock with the collar torn out, like a man who'd been hanged and hadn't noticed yet.

He was mending a roof timber when I found him, which is how he did everything: badly, cheerfully, with nails in his mouth.

"Bram Thornbury," he said around the nails. "You've grown. Last time I saw you, you were small enough your father carried you under one arm like a loaf."

"That was a long time ago."

"Long enough to be true, not so long I'll forget it." He spat the nails into his palm and looked at me properly. Anselm had eyes like a confessor's—he saw everything and charged nothing for it, which in Vessing made him either a saint or a fool. "You have your father's way of standing in a doorway. Like you're deciding whether the room's worth entering."

"It isn't."

"And yet here you are." He set down the timber. "Come inside before your face freezes in that expression. You'll want tea. I only have the kind that tastes like bark, but it's hot, and heat is half of any comfort."

Inside, the chapel smelled of tallow and river damp. He poured from a pot that had seen better decades, and I told him. Not all of it—I kept the forged ledgers out of it, because even Anselm might be compelled by a magistrate—but the shape of it. A letter. A promise. A call come too late. A woman branded enemy who might be neither enemy nor guilty.

He listened the way he mended roofs: patiently, and with occasional sounds of structural dismay.

"You understand," he said when I finished, "that if you're caught, they won't ask whether you're right. They'll ask whether you're useful. And a diver who can find things at the bottom of a harbor is very useful, alive and obedient."

"I know."

"Do you? Because knowing it and knowing it are different animals, and only one of them bites." He turned his cup slowly. "What do you want from me?"

"The crossing. To the marshes, past the Company pickets on the north bank. Kestrel's stronghold is two days' walk beyond the salt fen. I need a guide, or a route, or a miracle."

"Mm." He drank. "The pickets rotate at dusk. The fen path floods with snowmelt before it freezes again—there's a window, three days perhaps, when the surface crust holds a man but won't hold a horse. Riders won't follow onto it."

"You know this how?"

"I was a priest on the marsh parishes for six years, boy. I buried smugglers and tax collectors both, and sometimes in the same hole." He set the cup down. "Here is my price. Not coin. When you go—and you will go, don't insult me by pretending otherwise—you carry a letter for a woman in the fen settlements. Sister Maren. She tended me when I had marsh fever and I never repaid it. Whatever else you're carrying, hers goes on top. If the Company searches you, they find her letter first, and it's nothing—herbs, prayers, gossip about a christening. It buys you a moment's confusion. Confusion has saved more lives than courage."

"That's all?"

"All?" He laughed, short and gray. "The rest is mine to give free: pray to whatever listens that the ice holds until you're across. And Bram—" he caught my sleeve, and his grip was stronger than his roof-mending suggested, "—you're planning this like a man carrying something alone. Don't. Alone, you're a package. Accompanied, you're a conspiracy. One gets opened. The other gets feared."

* * *

My aunt Sable kept a smokehouse on the Mudquay that officially cured herring and unofficially cured everything else. She was my mother's sister, which explained the temper, and had run cargo under three different flags before losing a finger to each of two of them, which explained the hands.

She didn't look up when I came in. She never did. She finished salting whatever she was salting, wiped her palms on her apron, and then turned around with the expression of a woman appraising fish she suspected had been swimming in bad water.

"Bram Thornbury," she said. "Twelve years you've worked Corvane piers and never once darkened my door for anything but eel. Now you come in winter, off-schedule, wearing your honest face, which I've always found the least trustworthy thing about you."

"Aunt Sable."

"Don't 'Aunt Sable' me. Ask what you came to ask."

So I asked. A boat—not for the crossing itself, Anselm's fen window handled that, but for the second leg, down the coast from the fen to the stronghold anchorage, because walking into Kestrel's camp overland marked me as foot traffic, and foot traffic gets searched. A boat arriving by sea arrives as news.

She listened without blinking, which from Sable was a courtesy.

"You want me," she said, "to put my hull—a hull, mind you, that I paid off two years ago, that owes nobody, that floats quiet and quiet is its entire fortune—into waters where the Company has declared every sail hostile. For you. Who I last saw stealing liquorice from this very shelf at age nine."

"I didn't steal it. You gave it to me."

"I gave it to you so I'd have something to hold over you, and you never came back to be held over. Twelve years, Bram. I'd almost forgiven it."

"Sable." I used her name bare, the way she hated and respected. "Kestrel Marrow is going to be condemned at the tribunal. On forged entries. I've seen the pages."

Something moved behind her face—quick, gone, like a fish under ice. "Seen them where?"

"In the harbor. In the water. Where the Company puts the things it doesn't want read."

Silence. Outside, the wind came off the frozen river with a sound like a file drawn across iron. Sable picked up her knife and looked at it, and then at me, and I understood I was being measured against something—against my mother, maybe, or against some old arithmetic of hers about what risks balance out.

"My brother-in-law," she said finally, "your father, rest the stubborn man—he stood surety for me once. Before your time. When the Revenue had me cold, and every skipper in the Mudquay suddenly couldn't remember my name, he stood in the magistrate's hall and pledged his house." She snorted. "His house. Which you now don't own, thanks to the Company's indenture terms. So in a sense the debt's already been collected by worse men than me."

"He never told me."

"No. He wouldn't. Standing surety loudly is vanity; doing it quietly is the whole trade." She pointed the knife at me. "Here's my price, nephew, and it's steep. When this is done—if it's done, if you're alive, if there's ever an 'after'—you come back and you work my smokehouse through one full season. Herring season, which is the worst one. My knees are done and my apprentice ran off to the navy and I am *tired*, Bram Thornbury, tired of being the only one who knows how the brine goes."

"One season."

"One season. And you bring back that boat, or what's left of it, or your debt doubles and I'll collect from your bones."

We shook on it. Her grip took my fingers like a trap takes a fox—no malice in it, just certainty.

At the door she stopped me. "One more thing. There's a clerk at the harbormaster's office. Vash. Odile Vash. She's been asking after net crews. After *you* by name, or near enough." She studied me. "Whatever you're carrying, boy, somebody upstream already knows it exists. Plan accordingly."

* * *

That night I sat in my room with the letter and the ledger pages spread on the floor, and I did something I hadn't done since I was a child: I spoke out loud to my father.

"You could have mentioned," I told him, "any of it. The pact. The debts. The fact that being invisible was only ever a hiding place, not a life."

The candle gutted. The river groaned under the frost, a long shifting complaint, the sound of something vast deciding whether to move.

Three days of fen-crust. A boat. A priest's letter on top of the packet. A season of herring owed. A clerk asking questions. It was a poor conspiracy—one defrocked holy man, one retired criminal, and a diver who'd spent twelve years learning to be overlooked. But Anselm was right about one thing: accompanied, I was no longer a package to be opened. I was something that would have to be reckoned with.

I sewed the ledger pages back into my coat lining, and Maren's letter on top of them, exactly as promised.

And under the door, where it had been slid sometime while I walked the Mudquay, lay a folded paper I hadn't noticed coming in. I bent and picked it up. Neat clerical hand. A witness list for the spring tribunal, names in order, sworn testimony scheduled—

and halfway down, in fresh ink, my own name.
