## 7. The Tribunal Summons

The summons came down from the Company house on a Tuesday, which I have always thought was fitting. Tuesdays are the day Vessing does its lying—market prices posted, tithes collected, the harbormaster's docket read aloud in that flat voice that makes even a hanging sound like bookkeeping.

I was the one who copied it out. That is the particular cruelty of my position: Odile Vash, clerk to Harbormaster Dren Corvane's office, third finger of his right hand and first pair of his eyes, was given quill and ink and instructed to reproduce the tribunal writ forty times, one for each posting board along the waterfront. *By order of the Corvane Trading Company, all persons bound by indenture shall appear before the spring tribunal to give testimony against one Kestrel Marrow, declared enemy of the Company. Refusal shall be met with penalty of the post.*

Penalty of the post. We are a port town; we like our language nautical even when it means flesh.

I copied those words forty times without my hand shaking once, which surprised me. I had expected it to shake. Instead there was a strange steadiness that comes over me when I am doing something I know to be wrong—the same steadiness, I imagine, that a forger feels, or a hangman. My letters were very beautiful. Master Dren complimented them. He said, "Vash, you have the neatest hand on this river," and I said, "Thank you, sir," and we both stood there in the warm smell of tallow and paper while somewhere below us the frozen river held its breath under three feet of black ice, holding a drowned man, holding a letter, holding whatever it was that Bram Thornbury was not telling anyone.

Because yes. I knew about Bram Thornbury.

Let me be precise about what I knew, since precision is the only virtue I have never been accused of lacking. I knew that a courier had been pulled from the river in January, dead of cold water rather than cold steel, which interested me because couriers who work for the Company in winter usually die of other things. I knew that the net-man who found him—young Thornbury, the diver, the one who has spent twelve years perfecting the art of being furniture—had gone pale as gull bone when the harbor watch asked what else the net brought up. And I knew that the watch log recorded *no articles recovered*, in my own handwriting, because I wrote what Master Dren told me to write, and Master Dren told me to write it before he had even heard the full report.

You do not pre-decide what a dead man was carrying unless you already know. And you do not already know unless you put it in the water yourself, or know who did.

So when the summons went out, and I saw the name list attached to the back page—the witnesses the tribunal intended to call—I did what I always do with documents that pass through my hands. I read them. All of them. Including the ones addressed to other people, including the ones not addressed to anyone, including, once, a letter Master Dren burned in the stove while I was standing right there, which I had already read that morning while dusting his desk, because I dust thoroughly.

The witness list ran to thirty-one names. Fishers, net-menders, dockhands, two pilots, the ferryman's widow. Every Thornbury on the rolls appeared on it, which was no surprise; the Company holds grudges the way the river holds silt, patiently and in layers. Bram Thornbury was listed fourth. What was a surprise—what sat in my chest like swallowed ballast as I read down the column—was the thirty-first name.

*Odile Vash, clerk.*

My own name. In my own copied hand, though someone above me had added it after, in Master Dren's blunt scrawl, the ink still sharp enough to smell.

They meant to call me. The clerk. The woman who writes down what she is told. They meant to stand me at the tribunal rail and ask me, presumably, whether the harbor records were true. Whether the ledgers were accurate. Whether Kestrel Marrow's debts were real debts or invented ones, whether the branding of an enemy had been done in ink or in ambition.

And here is the thing about being asked to swear the records are true: either you lie under oath in front of the whole harbor, or you tell the truth and hang your master with your own testimony. There is no third door. I looked for one. I am very good at looking for doors. There isn't one.

* * *

That evening I stayed late in the records room, ostensibly reconciling the winter toll books, actually sitting in the dark with a single lamp and thirty-one names and the growing, sickening understanding that I had spent nine years building the machine that was now going to feed on me.

I want to say something in my own defense here, and then I want to stop saying it, because I've earned neither the time nor the sympathy. Here it is: I told myself I was a clerk, not a conspirator. A pen, not a hand. Ink does not choose what it spells. I wrote the tolls, the tariffs, the indenture rolls, the punishment dockets, and I told myself each entry was a stone too small to build anything, and then one day I looked up and there was a wall, and I was inside it, and so was everyone else.

But I also want to record the truth, which is that some stones I placed knowingly. The year Marrow's fleet was struck from the registry, I entered the strike myself. I noticed the debt figures didn't reconcile—not didn't balance, mind, clerks notice unbalanced books the way sailors notice weather—but didn't *reconcile*, meaning they balanced beautifully, too beautifully, round numbers where the river's trade makes nothing round. Forged ledgers don't hide their lies in errors. They hide them in tidiness. I noticed. I said nothing. I took my wage. I bought a new coat.

There. Defense made and retracted. Onward.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the ice on the river boomed—a long, deep report like a cannon under the world, the sound it makes when the thaw starts working at it from beneath, water worrying at the underside of everything solid. It had been booming like that for a week. Everyone in Vessing pretended not to hear it. You cannot live beside a river all your life and not know what that sound means: the ice is dying, and it will not die quietly, and when it goes it will take the piers with it if it feels like it, and every judgment made on frozen ground will have to be remade on moving water.

The tribunal was set for ice-break. Of course it was. Master Dren wanted the harbor assembled, every worker standing on the quay in their Company gray, when the verdict fell. A spectacle. Loyalty performed with the whole town as witness, the enemy condemned just as the river opened and the shipping season began—as if to tell the water itself: *this is what happens to debts unpaid.*

I pulled a fair copy of the witness list from the file—it was my copy, my hand, technically Company property, but I have long ago stopped believing the Company owns anything honestly, including its property—and I folded it into quarters, and I sat there holding it like it was a coal.

What I intended to do with it, I could not have said. That is the honest truth of conscience: it doesn't arrive with a plan. It arrives with a weight. You find yourself doing things without authorization, your own hands going rogue like a crew that's stopped believing in the captain. I had spent nine years being the most reliable instrument in that building, and here I was, unreliable, holding stolen paper in a dark room, listening to the ice die.

* * *

Bram Thornbury mends nets on the south quay on Thursdays. Everyone knows this; it is part of being furniture, that you keep a schedule. I had watched them for years the way you watch anything regular—a tide table, a bell—with half attention, until January, when watching became attention.

Thursday morning I walked the south quay with a ledger under my arm, doing nothing so suspicious as looking at anyone. The fog was up, the low white kind that turns the harbor into rumor. Bram sat cross-legged among coils of line, fingers working the mesh, face doing its usual excellent impression of a person thinking about knots.

I did not stop walking. Stopping is how people get remembered. I slowed—just enough—and let the folded paper fall from under my arm onto the netting beside them, and kept on, and said, in the tone I use for reading out dockets, "Witness lists posted Monday. All names. Check yours."

Then I walked on, heart going like a drum in a parade, and did not look back, because looking back is the second way people get remembered.

I had gone perhaps thirty paces when I heard it behind me: the small, specific silence of someone who has stopped working. Not a gasp, not a word. Just the absence of the shuttle clicking through mesh. Anyone else would have missed it. I have spent nine years learning what documents sound like when they're hiding something, and it turns out people make the same noise.

I did not look back. But I smiled, privately, into the fog, and it was not a kind smile. It was the smile of a woman who has just committed her first crime in nine years of lawful wickedness and found it lighter than expected.

* * *

They came to me at dusk. Of course they did. I'd have been insulted if they hadn't.

I was locking the records room when the shadow detached itself from the stairwell—Bram Thornbury, hood up, moving with that careful weightlessness the divers have, like someone permanently prepared for the floor to become water. Up close their eyes were older than the rest of them, the way harbor eyes get.

"You left something on my nets," they said.

"I leave things everywhere. Terrible habit. Cluttered desk, cluttered quay."

"They'll know it came from inside."

"Eventually. Which gives us—" I glanced at the window, where the last light was going off the ice like a coin sinking, "—four days. Possibly five, if the thaw stalls. The tribunal convenes at ice-break."

"Why give it to me?"

"Because your name is fourth on that list," I said, "and mine is thirty-first, and I have recently discovered that I object." I turned the key in the lock, more for something to do with my hands than any need. "Thirty years I could have gone without discovering that. You should feel flattered."

"They'll call you to swear the ledgers are true."

"Yes."

"And if you refuse—"

"The post. Yes. If I lie, I damn Marrow and keep my skin. If I tell the truth, I lose the skin and possibly save the town. I've been running the arithmetic for two days and I warn you it keeps coming out wrong, which is how I know it's finally honest arithmetic."

Bram studied me for a long moment, and I let them. I have a face built for studying; it gives away less than a locked drawer. When they spoke again their voice had dropped to nearly nothing, the voice of someone who has spent twelve years learning exactly how quiet a person can be.

"The courier's letter named my family. As guarantors. For him."

I nodded slowly, as though this confirmed a suspicion, which it did, though not the one they thought. What it confirmed was larger and worse and better: the drowned man had been carrying proof, and the proof had reached the one family in Vessing with a reason to use it.

"Then you're not a witness," I said. "You're evidence. And evidence, Master Thornbury, gets destroyed—unless it moves faster than the people trying to burn it." I handed them the second thing I had carried all day, sewn flat against my ribs under my coat: three pages, copied in my own hand, from the winter ledger of two years past. Round numbers. Beautiful, tidy, lying numbers. "Copies. The originals stay where they are until I can lift them clean. Read them tonight. Burn them if you must. But decide quickly."

"Decide what?"

"Whether you're going to the tribunal," I said, "or whether you're going to beat it there."

Down on the river, the ice boomed again—longer this time, a groan that traveled the whole length of the harbor and came back changed, thinner, like a promise breaking. Bram looked toward the sound, and I watched them understand it the way I understood it: four days, maybe five, and then the black ice would go all at once, and everything done on top of it would have to answer for itself on open water.

"I'll need to cross," Bram said quietly. Almost to themselves.

"I know," I said. "I've seen the tide tables. So has whoever Master Dren sends riding the north bank." I pulled my coat tight. "Which means you'll need to move before the fog lifts tomorrow—or not at all."

The lamp in the records room behind us gutted out on its own, and neither of us moved to relight it.
