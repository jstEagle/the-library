## 8. Crossing the Black Ice

I have drowned before, in the way that people who work water do—the half-drowning, the lung-burning pull toward surface, the coughing up of river that tastes like iron and old coins. I was eleven. My father hauled me out by the collar and said, "Now you know what the river wants, so don't ever tell it your name."

I thought about that advice a great deal in the hour before dawn, standing at the reed bank with ledger pages sewn into my coat lining like contraband scripture, which I suppose is exactly what they were.

The fog had come up off the estuary around midnight, thick and white and utterly indifferent to human affairs, the kind of fog that makes the harbor lamps into smudged ghosts and swallows the north bank entirely. Aunt Sarel had called it "God's own tarpaulin" and charged me two silver marks for the observation, which she deducted from what I owed her for the boat nail and the flask of pepper-spirit. The defrocked priest, Father Ilan, had been less mercenary about it. He'd simply taken my hands and said, "Whatever you're carrying, carry it lighter than your fear," which would have been more comforting if he hadn't then asked me to remember him to Kestrel Marrow, on the grounds that they'd shared a cell during the border war and Kestrel still owed him for a horse.

Everyone in Vessing wanted something from this crossing. That was how I knew it was real.

* * *

The ice had held all winter the way old men hold grudges—visibly, creakingly, with everyone pretending not to notice the strain. By February it should have been safe as a warehouse floor. But the thaw had come early this year, sneaking up the river from the sea two weeks ahead of itself, and for three nights I'd heard it groaning in my sleep, that long animal complaint of black ice under pressure, like something enormous turning over and deciding whether to wake.

The tide tables said low water at the fourth hour. Odile's voice in my memory said: *before the fog lifts—or not at all.* And beneath that, unspoken but louder: *Master Dren knows. Whoever forged those ledgers knows you found them, or will soon.*

So I went at the third hour, in the dark part of morning when even the gulls haven't made up their minds.

The pages rode against my ribs—eleven of them, cut from the duplicate ledger with a razor thin enough to pass as an accident of binding, each one proof that Corvane had rewritten Kestrel Marrow's debts into treason and the harbormaster's hand had signed the lies. Eleven pages weighing perhaps four ounces. They felt like ballast. They felt like wings. Mostly they felt like paper, which is the terrible thing about truth: it weighs nothing until you try to carry it across a river that doesn't want you.

I stepped off the reed bank at ten past three, and the ice took my weight with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

* * *

The crossing was nine hundred feet in summer, when the ferry ran. In winter, over the wind-scoured black expanse between the pilings of the broken pier, it might as well have been nine hundred miles. There are no landmarks on ice. There is only the next step, and the next, and the fog pressing in from both banks until you exist in a gray oval about the size of a coffin, moving.

I counted steps. It's something I do—I've told no one this, but I count everything, always have. Steps, oysters, nets mended, insults endured. Numbers are a way of being in a place without being *of* it, and I had spent twelve years being nowhere at all by this method. So: forty steps. Eighty. One hundred twenty, and the groaning started.

It wasn't loud at first. It came up through the soles of my boots more than through the air, a deep structural complaint, the sound of a thing too large for its own bones. Then, somewhere off to my left—a direction I could not have sworn to, in that fog—a crack rang out sharp as a whip over a slave auction block, and I stopped counting and started listening instead, which any survivor will tell you is the correct exchange rate.

The river was breaking up early. Not tomorrow, not at tribunal week, not on anyone's schedule but its own. Tonight. Now.

And behind me, faint but unmistakable, hooves on the frozen mud of the south shore.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about terror: it is clarifying. All those years I'd spent memorizing everything while appearing to notice nothing—the tally marks, the cargo manifests, the way Master Dren's clerks inked entries twice when they were lying and once when they weren't—all of it had been preparation for exactly one moment, and the moment was this: a man named Bram Thornbury, alone in a fog on dying ice, with eleven pages of proof against his chest and riders behind him and a black split in the world opening somewhere ahead.

I ran. Not well. Nobody runs well on ice; you run the way drunks walk, all faith and no physics. The cracks were coming faster now, overlapping, a conversation happening beneath me in a language I almost understood. I aimed for where the north bank ought to be and let the fog sort out the details.

The hoofbeats reached the shore behind me and stopped. Voices, muffled. A lantern flared somewhere in the whiteness behind, a dirty orange smear, useless in the murk but looking for purchase anyway.

"Thornbury!" A voice I knew—Dren's rider, Halloway, a man who once beat a dockboy unconscious for miscounting barrels. "We know it's you! Company property comes home or Company property hangs!"

There is a version of me that has answered that call for twelve years. That version died somewhere around step six hundred, though I didn't feel him go. You never do. People leave you quietly, even the versions of yourself.

The ice gave out from under me at what I later calculated—because I calculate everything, even while drowning—was roughly step seven hundred and eighty.

* * *

Cold like that isn't temperature. It's an argument. The river seized me the way it had at eleven years old, except there was no father on the bank this time, no collar-hauling hand, and I went down into black water that was full of ice moving underneath like the backs of sleeping animals. The current took me sideways. My coat filled and dragged at me, eleven precious ounces of truth trying to tow me to the bottom, and I remember thinking, with the strange bookkeeping clarity of the nearly dead: *this is what it costs, this is the price column, this is where you sign.*

My shoulder hit something solid—an ice shelf, the underside of the sheet, smooth and terrible as cathedral glass. I clawed along it. My fingers went from pain to nothing in the space of ten feet, and I followed them anyway because following numbness forward seemed better than following the current down.

I surfaced in an air pocket against a pressure ridge, gasping, and hauled myself out through a fracture the width of a coffin lid, and lay on the black ice breathing like a landed fish while somewhere behind me Halloway's lantern swung and his voice called my name twice more and then, blessedly, retreated—the fog and the breaking ice convincing even Company men that some debts couldn't be collected tonight.

I got up. I don't remember deciding to. I got up because my father told the river not to learn my name, and the river had disobeyed him, and I intended to make that disobedience expensive.

* * *

I walked the last hundred steps soaked to the bone in air cold enough to be manufacturing new ice around me, and if you've never had your clothes freeze to your body mid-stride, I can't recommend it; every joint became a rusted hinge. The fog began to thin in the east, going from wool to gauze, and I could see the north bank at last—and fires. Three of them, ringed in the bare trees above the waterline, burning steady and defiant, the way fires burn when the people tending them have decided not to run anymore.

Kestrel Marrow's camp. The enemy of the Company. My father's debtor, in the oldest and strangest coin.

A sentry stepped out of the treeline before I was halfway up the slope—young, a woman with a boar spear and frostbite scars on her cheeks, and she looked at me the way you look at something the river coughs up.

"Name," she said.

I stood there, dripping, freezing, half-dead, eleven forged ledger pages frozen stiff against my ribs, and for the first time in twelve years I did not reach for a useful, unremarkable answer.

"Bram," I said. "Bram Thornbury."

Her spear shifted. Behind her, at the largest fire, a figure rose—broad-shouldered, gray at the temples, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had been waiting for this exact stranger without knowing it. The light caught a face like winter coastline, and eyes that measured me the way harbormasters measure ships: tonnage, cargo, seaworthiness.

Kestrel Marrow looked at me across the firelight, and said, in a voice like a hull scraping stone:

"The courier carried a letter for the Thornbury name. He never delivered it." A pause, long enough for the river to crack again behind me, loud as a verdict. "You'll be either the debt or the collector. Which?"

I opened my mouth to answer—and saw, over Kestrel's shoulder, at the edge of the firelight, a familiar figure stepping out of the trees, snow in her hair, her clerk's coat traded for oilskins.

Odile Vash had crossed the river too.
