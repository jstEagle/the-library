## 9. Enemy at the Water's Edge

*Bram*

They took my coat at the edge of camp, which I had expected, and my boots, which I had not.

"Kestrel Marrow's rules," said the woman who took them, a broad-shouldered creature with a fishhook scar through her eyebrow. "Nobody meets him warm."

"That seems theatrical," I said.

"He likes theatrical." She jerked her chin toward the fire pit at the center of the camp, where perhaps thirty people sat among lean-tos pitched on the frozen shingle of the far bank. They watched me pass with the flat appraisal of gulls deciding whether a thing was edible. Someone laughed. Someone else did not.

I had crossed a river for this. I had nearly drowned in it—my coat, my beautiful stupid coat with its sewn seams, sat in that black water somewhere between Vessing and here, along with two months of my life's careful arithmetic. I stood barefoot on the ice-slick mud in the last blue light of a February evening and told myself that trembling was only the cold.

The tent at the far end of the camp was larger than the others, patched with sailcloth of three different vintages. The scarred woman lifted the flap and shoved me through without ceremony.

Inside, a lamp burned low. A man sat behind a folding table, mending a net.

He did not look up. That was the first test, I understood later. He let me stand there dripping on his floor while he worked a shuttle through mesh, his hands moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had done ten thousand things ten thousand times. He was younger than I expected—forty, maybe less, weathered to fifty—and broader than the woodcuts suggested. The Company's wanted-notices gave him a beard like a storm front and eyes like execution. In person the beard was going gray at the corners and the eyes were simply tired.

"You're the Thornbury whelp," he said, to the net.

"I'm Bram Thornbury."

"The father's dead."

"Six years."

"Mm." He drew the shuttle through. "And you crossed my river to tell me what? That you found a letter? That you're sorry it took so long?" Now he looked up, and the woodcut artists had undersold him after all. "I've had twelve years to imagine that conversation. You're a poor substitute for it."

* * *

*Kestrel*

The boy—I couldn't tell if Bram was a boy or a girl or something that hadn't bothered to declare itself, and it didn't seem to matter to them, so I let it not matter to me—stood there shaking with cold and held their ground. That was worth something. Most people who came to my camp came to beg, or to sell me, or to die loudly. This one just waited, teeth chattering, like patience was a skill they'd practiced until they were good at it.

"Sidda take your coat?" I asked.

"And my boots."

"She's thorough." I set down the net. "Sit by the brazier. Then show me why I shouldn't have you flogged back across the ice as a Company provocation."

They sat. They put their bare feet near the coals and winced, and kept talking anyway, which I'll admit I respected.

"Twelve years ago my father sheltered your fleet in the harbor at Vessing. Fourteen hulls, through one winter and part of a second. He fed your crews off his own stores when the Company cut rations to the river towns. And in return you swore—" Bram reached into their shirt and produced a folded packet, water-stained, wax-crumbled at one corner. "You swore you'd answer any call the Thornbury name made on you. This is the call. It arrived four years late because the man carrying it drowned, and the Company fished him out of my net instead of out of history."

I didn't take it. Not yet.

"I know what I swore," I said. "I was there. What I want to know is how the Corvane Trading Company came to hold paper with my seal on it, in the hands of a courier who then conveniently drowned."

"They didn't hold it. I do. I pulled it from the river myself, eight days ago."

"Eight days."

"The ice took time to cross safely," Bram said. "And I wasn't finished gathering."

"Gathering what?"

And here the whelp smiled—a small, crooked, entirely unlovable smile—and said: "Proof that your enemy is a fiction. Proof the Company built you out of forged ink so they'd have someone to hang their losses on. Proof signed, indirectly, by Odile Vash."

That name got my attention. It should have.

* * *

*Bram*

He read the ledger pages by lamplight, twice through, and I watched his face for whatever a man's face does when he learns the shape of his own ruin. It didn't do much. That frightened me more than anger would have.

"These are copies," he said finally.

"Copies traced from the originals in the harbormaster's office. The hand is mine. I copied them over three nights using the Company's own ink, so even the discoloration matches. The entries postdate your branding by two years—they show cargo written into Corvane's books under your flag that never existed, losses invented after the fact to justify the seizure of the fleet's collateral. Your piracy was manufactured retroactively. Kestrel Marrow the enemy was invented to cover a shortfall in the third quarter."

"I know," he said.

I stopped. "You—know?"

"I've known for six years. A clerk with a guilty conscience sold me a glimpse of those same pages before she defected. Half a glimpse. Enough to confirm the shape, not enough to prove anything in a court." He laid the pages down with strange gentleness, as though they might bruise. "What I did not have was a full set, in a matching hand, carried by a Thornbury, arriving before the tribunal. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your father's letter isn't why you're alive right now. Timing is."

I considered several replies and discarded all of them, mostly because my hands were still shaking too badly to gesture with any authority.

"Then the promise—"

"The promise brought you here. Good. The evidence keeps you here." He leaned back. "Because here is my problem, Bram Thornbury. Every court on this river owes its bread to Corvane. If I walk these pages into the spring tribunal, I'm an outlaw presenting stolen documents, and the tribunal will burn them and me in the same afternoon. So the question is not whether your proof is true. It's whether truth can be made loud enough that burning it costs more than hearing it."

"And your answer?"

"My answer has always been fire." His mouth twisted. "But fire is what they want. Give them a burning ship and every town on the river believes the enemy story forever. No." He looked at the ledgers again. "They convened the tribunal in public this year. On the quay, at ice-break, before the whole harbor—the Company wants theater, a hanging with an audience. Very well. We give them an audience. And in front of that audience, we don't fight. We read."

* * *

*Kestrel*

I tested them one more time that night, because I'd promised myself I would and because trust is a muscle I've let go slack.

"Odile Vash sent you warning," I said. "A copy of the witness list. Why would the harbormaster's own clerk help you?"

"Maybe it's a trap."

"It's absolutely a trap," I agreed. "The question is whose."

Bram fed a stick to the brazier. "Then we build a plan where a trap doesn't matter. If Odile betrays us, we lose nothing but the element of surprise we never had. If she doesn't—if her conscience is real—then the Company's own witness list becomes our stage directions." A pause. "My name is on that list. I'm summoned to testify against you."

"And will you?"

"I'll testify," Bram said. "Just not in the direction they intended."

I laughed before I could stop myself—first time in months—and the sound came out rusty, like a gate in winter. Somewhere outside, Sidda was yelling at someone about firewood. The river muttered under its ice, restless, ready to break ahead of schedule the way it does some years, as if it too were impatient with tribunals.

Here is what I decided, sitting across from a half-frozen stranger holding my dead friend's letter: Aldric Thornbury had been dead six years and had still managed to keep his word. That was worth more than a full set of forged ledgers. Ledgers can be burned. A family that answers calls across that many winters cannot be bought, and cannot easily be forged.

"You'll stay in my camp," I said. "Not as a guest. Guests get traded back. As crew, provisionally, until the ice breaks."

"I've been crew to a trading company for twelve years. I find I have strong opinions about the working conditions."

"So do I. Mine involve hanging owners." I gathered the pages, squared them, and tied them in oilcloth. "We go over the testimony tonight. Every entry, every date, until you can recite it in your sleep. Because when we stand on that quay, Bram Thornbury, there will be no second reading. The river won't wait for us and neither will the rope."

Bram nodded slowly. Then, with the small crooked smile again: "One condition."

"A condition. From my rescuer, barefoot in my tent."

"When this is done—when the ledgers are read and the harbormaster falls, if he falls—you burn the indenture rolls. All of them. Every contract Corvane holds on every soul in Vessing. Not just mine."

I looked at this person my old friend had sent me, this unremarkable, useful, invisible creature who had crossed black ice with stolen truth sewn into their coat, and whose first demand was not freedom but everyone's freedom.

"Done," I said. "Now sit down. We have until the thaw, and the thaw is early this year."

Outside, far downriver, the ice groaned—an enormous, grinding sigh, like something ancient turning over in its sleep—and began, at last, to move.
