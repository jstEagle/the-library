## 10. When the Ice Breaks

The tribunal was held on the customs quay, because Corvane wanted the harbor to watch. That was their first mistake. You don't invite a crowd to something unless you want the crowd to know, and I had spent twelve years learning exactly what crowds do with knowledge once they've been handed it.

They'd built a scaffold of raw pine at the quay's edge, three chairs for the assessors and a whipping post off to the left, freshly tarred, which I took as a courtesy to my future. The river ran behind it all, black in its channels now, the ice gone gray and rotten at the edges, groaning all night for a week like an old man asked to move furniture. Everyone in Vessing spoke of the ice-break the way you speak of a relative who might die at any moment — with dread, and a little hope, and constant checking.

I stood in the pen with the other indentured witnesses, forty-some of us in Company gray, and I did what I have always done. I memorized everything while appearing to notice nothing.

The harbormaster, Grole Vash, sat center chair in his braid of office, his hands folded like a man at prayer who has never once meant it. Odile stood behind him with the ledger chest, her face arranged into that clerk's expression I knew by then: the one that is not blank but merely *withheld*. She would not look at me. That was how I knew she'd look when it mattered.

Kestrel Marrow was brought up under guard — they'd let him keep his sword, which told me someone upstairs thought this was theater, a surrender dressed as trial. He looked thinner than at the camp, harder, and he found me in the pen within ten heartbeats and gave no sign at all. Good. We had agreed: no sign.

The reading of charges began. *Enemy of the Company. Fraud upon the ledgers. Conspiracy against lawful commerce.* The assessor's voice went on like tide through a grate. Around me the crowd of stevedores and oyster-wives and net-menders shifted and murmured. Behind us the river cracked — a long report like a cannon — and half the crowd flinched and half of them laughed. The ice talking. It talks all spring, if you listen long enough. Most people just call it noise.

I had been called fourth-to-last on the witness list Odile slipped me in February, sewn where the ledger pages had been, in the same coat, in the same seam. Which meant I had hours to wait, and forty witnesses ahead of me, each one reciting their piece: they saw Kestrel's men take crates, they heard conspiracy, they signed their marks. Some of them wept. One old rigger looked at his hands the whole time he lied, as though the lie were written on them and he could read it into truth by attention.

And then Odile Vash was called, and everything I had planned balanced on the point of a clerk's conscience.

She stepped forward, gave her name and station, and swore on the Company seal. And then she said, in a voice pitched to carry no farther than the scaffold:

"I testify that certain entries in the winter ledgers were altered after the fact. I testify that the alterations were made under direction of the harbormaster. And I testify that the original pages exist, and were carried by a courier of this Company, who drowned carrying them."

The quay went quiet enough that we could hear the river clearly. It groaned. Somewhere out mid-channel, something vast shifted under the gray.

Grole Vash did not turn red or stand up shouting. That frightened me more than rage would have. He simply said, "Clerk Vash appears to be confused," and signaled the guards, and two of them took her arms.

"The originals were copied," she said, louder now, letting them take her, not resisting, making them drag a small neat woman across her own testimony. "Copied before they were destroyed. By hand. In a fair script. And the copy will be read today."

*Today.* She'd moved my cue. She'd put herself in front of it, a wall of clerk's credibility for the thing to hide behind — or she'd doomed us both, and there was no way on this quay to tell the difference. That had always been the question with Odile. Warning or trap. She had finally answered it by standing in neither place.

They called the next witness anyway, some poor coil-hauler who stammered through two sentences about crates before the assessor waved him off, and then the presiding magistrate leaned down to confer with Vash, and I understood: they would bury her accusation in procedure. Adjourn. Reopen. Make it vanish into files.

The river decided otherwise.

It came as a sound first — not the groaning, which we'd all gone deaf to, but a *crack*, high and clean as a mast snapping, from the channel mouth. Then a second. Then the whole harbor turned, helpless, the way people turn toward weather, and we watched the ice go. It didn't melt; it *failed*, plates the size of chapel floors heeling up out of black water, grinding, the sound arriving through the soles of my boots and up my spine. A year of winter breaking apart in eleven minutes. Nobody had ever seen it happen so fast. The oyster-wives were shouting. Children ran to the rail. The guards on the scaffold forgot they were guarding anything.

I walked out of the pen. Nobody stopped me. Twelve years of being unremarkable bought me thirty feet before anyone even registered motion, and by then Kestrel had stepped up onto the scaffold's low dais, uninvited, unguarded, holding up his hands like a man announcing a toast.

"Vessing!" he called. "You came to watch a man hang. Stay and hear him read instead."

And I climbed the steps with the pages inside my coat, where they had ridden across black ice in February, sewn against my heart like the letter before them, like my father's promise before that, and I took them out, and the crowd — because crowds are only waiting, always, for permission — went still.

"Corvane winter ledger, third entry, Frostmonth the ninth," I read. My voice shook and I let it; better they heard a person than a proclamation. "*Received of K. Marrow, toll and tariff paid in full, forty barrels salt-fish, six hundred weight of tallow, receipted.*" I looked up. "Now the same date. The page they filed instead." I turned the sheet. "*No record. Goods seized as contraband. Marrow declared debtor by concealment.*"

"Who authorized—" the assessor began.

"*G. Vash, harbormaster,*" I read over him. "Same hand. Same ink. But not the same ink as the rest of the book." I held the page high, facing the sun, and the crowd nearest the scaffold rose on their toes, because everyone in a port town knows what a ledger looks like, has kept one or been kept by one, knows what a fair page smells like. "Different iron in the ink. Different nib. Written twice, weeks apart, and backdated. Every entry naming Kestrel Marrow is forged. Every debt they mean to make him pay tonight was invented in this office, by that man, to take a fleet."

Silence. Then the river again — a boom like a door slamming in the world's cellar, and the pack ice drove into the quay pilings hard enough that we felt the timbers shudder, and the crowd roared back at it, at us, at the whole rotten arithmetic of the morning.

What happened after happened quickly and also, in my memory, slowly. The stevedores came forward not as a mob but as a jury, which is worse. Grole Vash stood, drew himself up, began some sentence about the sanctity of Company writ, and got no further, because a fishwife's thrown herring — I swear this on my father's grave — struck him in the ear, and the laughter that followed broke something in the morning that never healed. The guards looked at the assessors, the assessors looked at each other, and the writ of the Corvane Trading Company in the port of Vessing ended not with a battle but with a shrug, forty strong men easing the guards aside like moving furniture. They took Vash's braid of office off him the way you'd take a collar off a dog that had bitten one child too many.

Odile stood free among the crowd, straightening her coat. She caught my eye at last, and nodded once, clerk to clerk. An audit concluded.

* * *

My indenture was voided by sunset, in a document drawn up on the quay itself because no one dared make me walk to an office for it. Twelve years, four months, eleven days. The clerk — not Odile, some junior shaking so hard his nib spat — wrote *services rendered, contract dissolved, the Thornbury name cleared of all obligation*, and I watched the Thornbury name come back to me on paper after twelve years of belonging to a company, and found it weighed almost nothing. A name is only worth what you can spend it on.

Kestrel found me at the waterline, where I had gone because the whole harbor was down there watching the floes grind past, huge and luminous in the dusk, and I wanted to see my father's river running free of them.

"They'll offer you everything tomorrow," Kestrel said. "A berth. A pension. Forgiveness, which costs least and means less."

"I know."

"And?"

I looked at his ship riding at anchor past the ice-run, the *Harrow*, black-hulled, scarred, alive. Somewhere south of here my father had sheltered this man's fleet in wartime and been promised, in a letter I only read last month, that the favor would be answered whenever it was called in. It had taken twelve years and a drowned stranger to collect. Promises are strange cargo. They don't sink. They just wait in the current until somebody's net comes along.

"I spent twelve years being useful and invisible," I said. "If I stay, they'll spend the rest of my life thanking me for it, and I'll die in a warm room remembering every entry I didn't read aloud. I'd rather be nobody somewhere the water moves."

Kestrel studied me the way he'd studied the ledger pages — looking for forgery, finding none. "We sail on the morning tide," he said. "There's work for hands that notice things."

I went back that night to the garret over the net-loft that had been mine for twelve years and packed everything I owned, which fit in one satchel, and had always fit in one satchel, and I had simply never let myself count it before. On the desk I left the courier's letter, opened, its wax broken, addressed to *the Thornbury name*. Whoever came after would find it and understand, or not. Names pass through Vessing like water. That's what water is for.

At dawn the tide turned, and I walked the quay one last time through the wreckage of pine scaffolding, past the empty whipping post, past Odile, who stood at the harbor-office door with the ledger chest under her arm, beginning whatever reckoning she would now keep for the rest of her life.

"Thornbury," she said.

"Clerk."

"You'll want to keep copies," she said. "Of everything. Trust rusts faster than iron."

"It's not trust I'm keeping," I said, and touched the coat seam where the pages had ridden, and went down the stairs to the water.

The last floes were going out on the ebb, white and grinning, melting as they went. Kestrel stood at the *Harrow*'s rail. The gangplank hummed under my boots, plank by plank, and I did not look back until the ship swung with the current — and then I did, once, because I am not made of stone, only raised on it — and Vessing lay along its river in the early light, small and gray and finished with me, and I finished with it, and the harbor opened ahead like a page no one had yet written on.
