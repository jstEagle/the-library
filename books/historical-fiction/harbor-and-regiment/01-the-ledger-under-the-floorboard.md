## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The morning report came in with the rain, as it always did, and like the rain it told only part of the truth.

I stood at the admissions desk with the paper in my hand and mud to my ankles, reading the list of the dead the way you read a menu when you already know what you'll order. Private Alfred Dunn, 2nd Battalion. Not on the list. I had held his hand at four o'clock yesterday morning while he asked me — three times, because morphine makes a man loop like a gramophone needle — whether his mother would be told he'd been brave. I said yes. It was the only lie I've ever told that I'd tell again without hesitation, and even then I meant to correct it later, in ink.

So: not brave, according to the morning report. Not anything, according to the morning report. Private Alfred Dunn had been subtracted from the world by an officer's pen before death could finish the job properly.

"More tea, Sister?" asked Finn Calloway, materializing beside me with the chipped enamel pot that was our hospital's entire ceremonial treasury. Finn is twenty-two, all elbows and good intentions, with the particular talent of the very young for appearing exactly when you want to be alone.

"No thank you."

"You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're about to argue with someone who isn't here." He peered at the report upside down, which is how most of our paperwork gets read anyway. "Who's missing?"

"Nobody," I said. "Go see to the burns ward."

He went, but he looked back twice, which I noted and filed away. Finn notices things. It's his best quality and, if this war has any sense of irony left, it will someday be his worst one too.

* * *

Twelve years ago I started keeping a ledger. Not out of principle — I want that understood. Principles are what people claim afterward, once the thing they did turns out to have been admirable. No, it began out of spite, which is a much more durable foundation.

I was twenty-three, a probationer at a garrison hospital where the commandant had a habit of striking men from the rolls for infractions real and imagined, so their families would receive no pension, no letter, no proof they had ever bled for anyone. A private named Osgood — drunk on duty, technically, though the truth was he'd been drinking because his brother's name had come up in the casualty telegrams and nobody at the fort would let him go home to bury him — died of a fever in my ward. Struck off the week prior. As far as the army was concerned, Osgood had never existed, and therefore could not die, and therefore his mother in some grey northern town would wait forever for a son who had been erased rather than merely lost.

I wrote his name in the back of my nursing textbook. Then I bought a proper ledger with my own wages, and I have been buying them ever since, one after another, filling each and hiding it beneath the floorboard under my cot like a woman hoarding contraband sugar. Which, in a sense, I am. Names are the sweetest contraband there is.

There are two hundred and eleven names in the current volume. Two hundred and twelve, after tonight.

* * *

At half past nine, when the ward had gone quiet except for the breathing of forty men and the wind worrying the canvas of the roof, I knelt by my cot and pried up the board.

It comes up easier than it used to; the nails gave up years ago, and now it lifts with a fingernail's pressure, which pleases me obscurely. Beneath it: the old ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, my spare stockings, a tin of letters I cannot bring myself to reread, and darkness. The smell of earth and creosote. The smell, I sometimes think, of everything the army would prefer buried.

I brought the ledger up into my lap and opened it across my knees. By candlelight, in my careful hand — a nurse's hand, trained for prescriptions where a misread word kills — I wrote:

*Private Alfred Dunn, 2nd Btn. Died 14th October, 0340 hours. Wounds, abdomen. Asked whether his mother would be told he was brave. He was. She will be.*

Then, because the ledger deserves honesty even if the morning report does not: *Omitted from official record. Reason unstated. Reason always unstated.*

I sat with the candle guttering and thought about Mrs. Dunn, whoever she was, wherever she was. Somewhere she was setting a table or darning a sock or watching a road, waiting for news that had been decided against her by a man whose name I didn't know and whose boots were probably cleaner than mine. That's the thing about omission. It isn't violence, exactly. It's tidiness. Somebody, somewhere, found Private Dunn inconvenient and tidied him away, and the war rolled on, spotless.

Well. I keep a dirty little book under my bed, and every name in it is a smudge on somebody's clean record. Let them promote themselves until their collars strangle them. I have the receipts.

* * *

You might ask why I don't simply send the names home myself. Write to the mothers directly. I considered it, early on — sat up one whole night in my probationer's room drafting letters in my head, each one beginning *Dear Madam, I regret to inform you,* and each one ending in disaster. Because a letter can be traced. A letter has a postmark, a hand, a witness. And a nurse who writes unauthorized correspondence about omitted soldiers is not a heroine; she is a problem, and problems near the front get solved with a transfer to somewhere worse than nowhere, or with nothing so gentle.

A ledger under a floorboard hurts no one. It waits. It says: *you were here, and I saw, and someday, when the world is sane again, this will matter.* Whether someday ever comes is not my department. My department is the writing-down.

Besides — and here is the confession I make only to the underside of a floorboard — I need it more than they do. Twelve years of this war, or wars, or the long rolling catastrophe we've stopped bothering to give separate names to, and a person needs something to stand on. Mine happens to be made of paper and other people's forgotten names. When the shelling gets close enough to taste, when the men in Ward C scream in a register that lives in your teeth afterward, I think: *two hundred and twelve. Two hundred and thirteen.* Each name is a small stone, and I am building something out of them, though I couldn't tell you what. A wall, maybe. A cairn. Something that says *human beings passed through here and were counted, damn you, counted.*

* * *

Near midnight I went through the wards on my rounds, holding the candle low so the light wouldn't wake them. This is my favorite hour, if a person in my profession can be said to have favorite hours. The men sleep the way soldiers sleep — completely, desperately, like drowning men who've been given one breath's leave — and in sleep they are not privates or numbers or line items. They are just faces. Some of them boys. One of them, in the far cot, younger than my ledger is thick.

Finn was still up, sitting on a supply crate in the corridor, mending a stretcher strap with the intense concentration of a man defusing artillery.

"It's midnight," I said.

"I'm aware."

"Sleep is a medical treatment, Calloway. I prescribe it."

"In a minute." He tied off the strap, then looked up at me with that open, unguarded face of his, the one that makes officers trust him and makes me nervous. "Sister Blackwood. Can I ask you something strange?"

"You can ask. I reserve the right to answer strangely."

"Do you ever write them down? The ones that—" He stopped, picked at the strap. "The ones nobody sends word about. I found a paybook today, in the laundry bundle. Belonged to a man who died in August. Nobody's claimed it. Nobody's even asked."

My heart did something complicated, the way it does when the world brushes against the hidden thing.

"Why do you ask?"

"No reason." He shrugged, too quickly. "Just seems like somebody ought to. Remember them, I mean. Formally. Like a register." He smiled, embarrassed. "Silly idea."

"Not silly," I said, and heard my own voice come out softer than intended. "Not silly at all."

He nodded, satisfied, and went off to his bunk, and I stood in the corridor with the candle shaking slightly in my hand, feeling as though I'd just shown a stranger the inside of my locked room without opening the door.

* * *

The bombardment started at two.

I knew the rhythm of them by now the way farmers know weather. This one was distant — three miles east, maybe four, over toward the river line — close enough to set the canvas trembling and rattle the instrument trays, far enough that the men slept through most of it, or pretended to. I stood at the ward entrance and watched the horizon flicker, that obscene silent lightning, and felt the old familiar split in myself: the part counting seconds between flash and sound, the part thinking, absurdly, *the armistice talks resume Thursday.*

Because that's the cruelty of this particular season. Everyone knows it's ending. The newspapers say it, the officers say it over their brandy, even the men say it, in the joking way soldiers say things they can't afford to believe. Peace is coming. Peace is coming the way dawn comes — eventually, visibly, and not one minute before it chooses to. And in the meantime the guns keep their appointments, and men keep dying into my ledger, and the officers keep tidying.

Two hundred and twelve names. How many more before the pens are finally put down? Ten? Fifty? Will I fill this volume and start another, and another, a lifetime of kneeling at floorboards, a spinster of the margins?

Let it be so, I thought, watching the horizon burn politely at a distance. I'd rather be a librarian of the lost than a decorator of the guilty.

I snuffed the candle and went back to my cot, and lay listening to the guns, and did not sleep so much as wait for morning.

* * *

Morning arrived gray and dripping, and with it, the boy.

Though I didn't know that yet. At seven o'clock I knew only that the night had passed, that the burns ward needed dressings changed, that Finn was late to breakfast for the first time in his punctual young life, and that Sergeant Pryce was standing at the end of the ward with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she reserves for paperwork that has committed a personal offense against her.

"Hazel." Imogen Pryce has been my closest friend for six years, which means she uses my Christian name only when something is wrong, and calls me Sister Blackwood when something is very wrong indeed. "There's a— situation. At the entrance."

"What kind of situation?"

"A child," she said. "Wounded. Left at the door sometime during the night. And a note." She hesitated, which Imogen never does; hesitation on Imogen is like a crack in a load-bearing wall. "Matron wants it handled quietly. There's talk of turning him over to the provosts."

"Turned over—" I was already moving. "He's a wounded child, not a stolen rifle."

"That's what I said." She fell into step beside me, voice dropping. "Hazel. The note. You should read it yourself before anyone else decides what it means."

We crossed the yard together through the drizzle, past the ambulance bay, past the cook tent exhaling its thin gray smoke, and there at the entrance — under the lean-to where we take in the dying when the wards are full — a blanket lay on the stretcher rack, and in the blanket a boy.

Eight years old, perhaps. Dark hair plastered to his forehead. A bandage, amateur-tied, around his left arm, soaked through brown. He was awake. That was the worst of it — he was awake, and utterly silent, and looking at me with eyes that had already finished being frightened and moved on to something older.

And beside him, weighted under a stone against the wind, a folded piece of paper.

I picked it up. Unfolded it. Read four words in a slanted, decisive hand I would know anywhere — a hand I had last seen seven years ago on a note pinned to a bouquet of wildflowers, saying *you owe me a dance, Nurse* —

*Forget this face. Please.*

The guns went on muttering to the east. The boy watched me. And Captain Elias Vane — dead these five months, officially, or worse than dead, which I would learn soon enough — had just reached across a continent and a war and taken hold of my collar with four words and a wounded child.

Some promises, I have learned, do not wait politely under floorboards. They come to the door in the rain.
