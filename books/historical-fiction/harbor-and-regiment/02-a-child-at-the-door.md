## 2. A Child at the Door

There is a particular sound a field hospital makes when the shelling starts again. It is not a scream and it is not a silence. It is a sort of collective exhale, two hundred people at once deciding to be brave about it, and I have come to find it almost comforting — the way you might find a familiar ache comforting, because at least it tells you where you are.

"Inside," I said to the boy, and hauled the door open against the wind.

He didn't move at first. He looked at my hand, then at my face, then down the dark road behind me as if expecting someone to follow him out of the rain. Nobody did. The road was empty in both directions, which was its own kind of message. Somebody had brought him here under cover of an artillery barrage, set him on our step like a parcel, and driven off fast.

Somebody who wrote *please*.

"Inside," I said again, gentler this time, and crouched to his level. Rain ran off the brim of my cap and dripped onto the stones between us. "You're shattering. Look at you. You're made entirely of shivers."

He let me take his arm. That was the first thing I noticed properly, once I got past the blood and the size of him — how he let me touch him without flinching, which no child of eight does who hasn't been taught to be brave about strangers, or taught something worse. His left sleeve was soaked through dark and his boots were wrong for him, too big, stuffed at the toes with what looked like newspaper. Someone had dressed him in a hurry out of someone else's kit.

The shells were walking closer. I could hear them doing that thing they do — the whistle climbing a note, then the flat concussive thump, then the earth shrugging. Somewhere across the compound Private Osgood began singing hymn verses very fast and slightly out of order, which was his habit and, apparently, his theology.

"What's your name?" I asked, steering him through the doorway into the corridor, where the lamplight swung with every distant impact and the smell of carbolic and wet wool closed over us like water.

Nothing. Not a word. He looked up at me with grey eyes, steady as a surveyor's, and said nothing at all.

"All right," I said. "Don't know yours either, some days. We'll get along fine."

* * *

The wound was in his forearm — a long tear, not deep, but filthy, the kind that goes bad on you if you look away for a day. Shrapnel or glass; there was grit in it that glittered faintly when I held the lamp close, and I picked out four pieces with the forceps while Corporal Ennis held the boy's shoulders and told him about her sister's goat in a low, running commentary that had nothing whatever to do with anything, which is the correct way to talk to wounded people.

The boy never cried. He watched the forceps the whole time with that same surveyor's attention, as though supervising the work, and only once did his breath go ragged, and even then he caught it and smoothed it out like a bedsheet.

"He's not said one word," Ennis murmured to me. "Not a peep. Finn found him?"

"Finn found him." I tied off the last of the bandage. "Where is Finn?"

"Being sick in the yard, last I saw. He went white as a candle. Said the boy looked at him—"

She stopped, because the boy looked at her then, just briefly, and whatever was in that look made a grown corporal lose the thread of her own sentence.

"—said the boy looked at him," she finished, more quietly, "like he was counting him."

"Nonsense," I said, because it was easier than agreeing. "Children don't count people. Go see if the laundry's still standing, and bring me dry clothes for him. Small ones if we have them, which we don't."

Ennis went. The boy and I sat in the swinging lamplight, and outside the guns walked their slow circuit of the valley, and I took out the note again because I couldn't help it.

Four words and a *please*. The hand was slanted right and pressed hard into the paper, the way writing goes when a man writes standing up, or lying down, or with someone's footsteps coming. I knew it the way you know a voice through a wall. Seven years, and I could have picked it out of a sack of a thousand notes.

Elias Vane had terrible handwriting and beautiful punctuation. He crossed his sevens. He put a small careful full stop after everything, even apologies — especially apologies, I suspected, though I'd never had one from him long enough to check. And he had pulled me out of a burning aid station in the spring of '13 with his coat over my head and his voice in my ear saying, with absurd calm, *left foot, right foot, Nurse, the fire's not going to wait for either of us*, and afterward he had sent wildflowers with a note pinned to them, full stops and all, saying I owed him a dance.

I never paid that debt. There was always another convoy, another ward, another year. That is the trap of wars, if anyone asks you: not that they take things quickly, but that they teach you there will always be later.

I folded the note small and put it inside my cuff, which is where I put things I am not ready to think about, and looked at the boy.

"Who brought you here?" I asked him.

Nothing.

"Was it your father?"

At that, something happened. Not a flinch — I'd have preferred a flinch. It was smaller than that and worse: his chin came up half an inch, and his eyes went from mine to the door, to the road beyond it, and back, quick as a man checking his escape route. A soldier's arithmetic, in a face with milk teeth still in it.

"Well," I said, to the room as much as to him, "that answers several questions and raises a great many more."

* * *

The bombardment slackened around midnight, the way they do, easing off eastward like weather with somewhere else to be. By then I had done the ordinary things — the things that keep a hospital honest no matter what arrives at its door. Dry clothes, borrowed from the convalescent ward and rolled at the cuffs. Broth, most of which he ate, holding the spoon correctly, elbow off the table, training showing everywhere like thread through cloth. A cot in the linen room, which is where I put people I do not wish to be found, being intimately acquainted with the geography of not being found myself.

Sister Marshfield would want him logged. Regulations required unauthorized persons to be reported within twelve hours, and Sergeant Pryce — God preserve me from rule-keepers with good memories — would have him documented, vaccinated, and entered into triplicate before breakfast if I gave her the chance. And any record of him anywhere in this hospital was, I understood without being able to say how, a nail through his hand.

*Forget this face.* You do not write that about a child you are delivering to safety. You write it about a child someone is looking for.

I sat on the edge of the linen-room cot while he settled, and I asked questions the way you ask questions of the sleeping, expecting nothing.

"How old are you?"

Silence. But he turned his face toward me.

"Do you speak English at all, or are you simply refusing on principle? Because I respect the second one enormously."

A pause. Then, so quietly I might have invented it: "He said not to tell anyone anything."

The first words out of him, and they weren't a name. They were orders, received and kept. My chest did something complicated.

"He said that, did he. Your father."

"Not my—" He stopped himself hard, jaw tightening, and looked at the blanket. Whatever the correction was, it had been forbidden too. "He said a lady with dark hair would help me. She would have ink on her fingers."

I looked at my hands. Ink on the middle finger of the right, as always, ground in past scrubbing — the permanent shadow of a ledger I would have sworn to no one under oath existed. Seven years ago a man in a burning doorway had noticed a nurse's hands well enough to memorize them, and had counted on the memory holding.

"Elias," I said softly, testing it, watching the boy's face. "Elias sent you."

And the boy — Tomas, though I wouldn't learn that name for two more days; that night he gave me nothing — the boy reached out and gripped my sleeve with his bandaged arm, careful even in desperation not to pull the wound, and shook his head once, sharply, and glanced at the door.

Not denial. Caution. Even the name was contraband.

"All right," I said. "All right. No names tonight. Names tomorrow, maybe, behind three locked doors." I tucked the blanket the way my mother used to, which surprised me; I hadn't known my hands remembered. "Sleep. Nothing gets through that door without my permission, and I deny permission to nearly everyone."

His eyes stayed open long after his breathing steadied. Children sleep shallow when they've been taught to. I knew the habit from the other side of the age line.

* * *

In the corridor I found Finn Calloway leaning against the wall with a mug of tea he hadn't touched, looking like a man rehearsing a confession.

"He came out of nowhere," Finn said. "No cart, no horse, no engine noise. One minute the step's empty, next minute there he is, sitting up straight like he'd been posed. And the strange part—" He rubbed his neck. "The strange part is whoever left him must've carried him through the barrage. On foot. In that rain. You don't do that unless—"

"Unless leaving him is more dangerous than keeping him," I finished.

Finn looked at me. Finn is twenty-three and has the sense God gives to orderlies, which is considerably more than the sense He gives to colonels, and I watched him arrive at the conclusion I was trying not to pack my bags for.

"That note," he said. "It wasn't the first time you'd seen that handwriting."

"No."

"And whose was it?"

I thought of the ledger under the floorboard, four years of names in slanted, decisive entries of my own. I thought of full stops after apologies. I thought of a debt seven years overdue.

"A friend," I said. "Officially deceased since spring. Unofficially—" I stopped, because unofficially I had nothing yet but four words, a wounded child, and a cold ring of certainty settling around my throat like a collar. "Unofficially, I'm going to find out."

Finn nodded slowly and drank his cold tea as a kind of toast. "You know," he said, "when I found him, before you came — he grabbed my wrist. Hard, for a little fellow. And he said one thing."

My stomach dropped a full deck. "What thing?"

"*Tell the ink lady the list is in me.*"

The lamps swung. Somewhere east the guns cleared their throats and fell quiet, and in the quiet I stood in a corridor smelling of carbolic and rain, hearing a sentence that meant nothing by itself and everything in a war where I kept a secret book of men the army had decided to stop counting.

A list. In an eight-year-old boy. Carried the way you carry shrapnel — inside, and for a reason.

"Finn," I said, very evenly, because the alternative was not evenly, "go find Sergeant Pryce. Tell her nothing. Just tell her I need to speak with her at dawn, and watch her face when you say it."

"Why her face?"

"Because," I said, turning back toward the linen room, toward the small guarded breathing behind the door, "if the army has decided Captain Elias Vane never existed, then someone in uniform delivered that decision to this hospital tonight. And Imogen Pryce reads morning reports the way priests read scripture."

Behind me, the boy slept with a list in him and his father's caution around him like a coat. Ahead of me, dawn was coming up grey over the marshes, and with it the daily papers — the casualty lists, the promotions, the omissions dressed as clerical error.

Seven years ago, Elias Vane saved my life and never sent me the bill.

Tonight he had sent it. Four words, one child, and interest accruing at the rate of one war.

I went to check the floorboard.
