## 3. Twelve Years of Silences

Sleep has never come easily to me in wartime, but tonight the boy's breathing through the canvas partition is the only sound in the ward, and it is steady enough to be a lullaby for someone else. Not me. For me there is only the ledger under the floorboard and the handwriting on that scrap of paper, and twelve years of silences stacking themselves up like sandbags against a door that is finally, finally being kicked in.

I know Elias Vane's hand the way you know a voice through a wall. The *f* that never quite closes its loop. The way he crosses his *t*s like a man signing something he doesn't believe in. I have seen that handwriting on casualty lists, on requisition forms, on one letter I was never supposed to keep.

Forget this face, it said.

As if I could forget any face. As if forgetting were a thing I had ever learned to do.

* * *

The first time Elias Vane saved my life, I did not thank him. I swore at him. This is, I am told, characteristic.

It was the autumn of the second year — no, the third; the years blur like wet ink, which is perhaps why I started keeping records in the first place — and our forward aid station at Marren Cross had taken a shell directly through the roof. I remember the particular quality of that moment: not fear exactly, but an offended sort of astonishment, as though the war had reached into my operating theatre and knocked over my instruments like a clumsy farmhand. There was fire in the rafters and a corporal on my table with his abdomen open, and I was clamping and packing and shouting for morphine when the second shell came in.

I remember thinking, with great clarity: *the table will hold, the table always holds.* And then the wall did not hold, and the world turned to noise and dust and a ringing so complete it erased everything else.

When the ringing thinned, I was lying in the yard with a man's arm hooked under mine, dragging me by the collar like a sack of turnips, and I was shouting — hoarsely, uselessly — that I had patients in there, that he should put me down and go back for them, that he was a coward and a thief and stealing a nurse from her post was theft of army property.

He set me down behind the water cart, looked at me through a face grey with plaster dust, and said:

"Corporal Denner died eleven minutes ago, Sister. You were working a dead man. The other two are already out. You're the only thing left in that building worth carrying."

And then he went back in anyway, because the morphine case was still inside, and he came out with it under one arm while the roof came down behind him like a curtain.

That was Captain Elias Vane. Thirty-one years old, regimental adjutant, a staff officer with clean boots and a reputation for being precisely the kind of careerist I despised — the kind who visits field hospitals the way bishops visit parishes, briefly and for the good of their souls. Except that his boots weren't clean, I noticed later. They were polished over old scorch marks from some earlier fire he'd walked out of. A man who kept his scars under the shine.

I apologized to him three days later, stiffly, in front of the mess tent. He accepted it with such perfect courtesy that I understood immediately he would never let me live it down, and we became friends in the way that people do in war: quickly, completely, and without ever once saying so.

* * *

We were not friends the way friends are friends in peacetime. There was no time for that, and no privacy either. Ours was a friendship conducted in the margins of documents and the five-minute gaps between catastrophes. He would appear at my station with a requisition I hadn't asked for — blankets, chloroform, the good splints — and I would sign for them without asking where they'd come from, because asking would have required him to lie and me to hear it. He drank his tea the way he did everything, fast and standing up. He called me "Sister Blackwood" for two full years before he called me Hazel, and when he finally did, it was during an amputation, of all things, shouted across the table over the sound of the saw, and afterward neither of us remarked upon it, because remarking would have made it smaller.

But there was one evening. There is always one evening; that is how wars get people to agree to things.

It was during the winter lull — the third winter, the cold one, when the fighting paused more from physics than from mercy — and I had been up forty hours with a fever ward, and he found me sitting on the steps of the chapel that served as our morgue, eating a ration biscuit and crying without any expression whatsoever, the way you learn to do, tears simply falling off a face that has gone on strike.

He sat down beside me. He didn't touch me. He took half the biscuit without asking, which was somehow more intimate than touching.

"My sister," he said, after a while, "used to do that. Eat when she cried. Said grief burns calories."

"You don't have a sister."

"I don't have anything," he said. "That's rather the point of me. Which is why I've been thinking about what happens after."

"After what?"

"After all this." He gestured with the biscuit at the frozen mud, the chapel, the whole grey apparatus of the war. "Everyone's got a plan for after. Pryce wants a farm. Your Colonel wants a knighthood. I've been trying to work out what I want, and I keep coming back to just one item on the list." He looked at me then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and honest in a way I'd never seen on a staff officer, or on anyone. "If it ever goes wrong for you — truly wrong, the kind of wrong that paperwork can't fix — you send for me. No rank between us. No flag. None of it. I'll come if I have to walk."

"And if it goes wrong for you?"

"Then you come for me," he said, "and God help whichever army is standing between us."

We shook on it. Actually shook hands, sitting on the morgue steps in the freezing dark, like merchants closing a deal. It was absurd. It was the most binding contract I have ever entered into, and I have since signed receipts for several hundred corpses.

I thought about that promise maybe twice a year, the way you think about a will you've written — comfortingly, distantly, certain it concerns a future self and not the present one.

* * *

There were other evenings, of course. Memory is dishonest in its selections, and I refuse to let it make this into a romance, because it wasn't, or wasn't only, or was something better than that — rarer. Once, at a rest billet, he taught me to play cards properly and lost every hand and accused me of witchcraft with such sincerity that the chaplain nearly intervened. Once I stitched up a wound on his forearm — shrapnel, shallow, the kind men laugh about — and he watched my hands the whole time and said, quietly, "You're the steadiest person I know," and I said, "You're bleeding on my floor," and we left it there, the true thing unsaid, folded away like the letter I kept.

Because there was a letter. A year ago — no, fourteen months now — when he was transferred to the eastern command, he wrote to me. Not an official letter. A private one, four pages, and in the middle of the third page, in the middle of a paragraph about the weather, he wrote: *Something is rotten in the supply chain here, Hazel, and it isn't rats. If I stop writing, don't look for me. But if you ever hear my name spoken badly, remember whose mouth is saying it.*

I burned that letter, as one does. I burned it and remembered it, which is the trick nobody teaches you: the fire takes the paper, not the words. Every line of it is still in my head, filed alongside the names in my ledger, in the archive I carry behind my ribs where no inspection will ever find it.

And then his letters stopped. And then, this morning, a boy appeared at my door with a note in that unmistakable hand, and orders came down within hours that Captain Elias Vane was a traitor, and I understood — with the cold, total clarity that arrives perhaps three times in a life — that the promise had come due.

* * *

Here is what I know about promises made in wartime: they are easy to make and monstrous to keep, because they are made by people who do not yet know what keeping them will cost, and kept by people who know exactly.

Pryce would tell me I'm being romantic. Sergeant Imogen Pryce, my closest friend and the most honest cynicism in a khaki uniform, keeps a mental ledger too — hers is of rules, mine of names, and she would say that a promise extracted on a morgue step by a starving man holding half a biscuit is not a contract, it's a symptom. She would say that Elias Vane is, per official record, a traitor, and that traitors' children are not my responsibility, and that the road I'm considering ends in a tribunal with my name on the docket.

She would say all of that. And she would say it while standing between me and whoever came to take the boy, because that is the paradox of Imogen Pryce, and I love her for it, though I'd deny it under oath.

But Pryce isn't here tonight. Tonight it's me and the boy's breathing and the ledger under the floorboard, and the arithmetic of twelve years.

Twelve years. That's how long I've been writing down the names the reports omit. It began small — a private here, a stretcher-bearer there, men who died between one morning report and the next and simply ceased, administratively, to have existed. Someone had to hold them somewhere. Then the omissions grew strange: whole platoons thinned, incidents unexplained, men written off as desertions who I had personally buried. I stopped understanding the pattern long before I understood there was a pattern. I only knew that every name I wrote down was a small act of treason against the idea that paperwork is truth.

And now I begin to suspect — lying here in the dark, listening to a child breathe — that Elias Vane kept a ledger too. That his was longer, and closer to the source, and that the word *traitor* is what the guilty call the man who catches them counting.

Forget this face, he wrote. As if I could. As if I haven't spent twelve years doing precisely the opposite of what he asked, professionally, devotionally, one name at a time.

Tomorrow — or the day after; soon — I am going to have to decide what that promise is worth. Whether it binds me to a man, or to a boy, or to the truth those two share between them like blood. Whether no rank and no flag means anything when the rank is my own survival and the flag is the only country I have left.

The boy stirs in his sleep and murmurs something — a word, half-formed. A name, maybe. They start early, the ones who carry names.

I got up, lit the lamp low, and took out the scrap of paper again. Forget this face.

"No, Captain," I said to the empty ward, to the twelve years, to him wherever he was hiding. "I don't think I will. You knew that when you wrote it. You knew me."

Outside, the guns had gone quiet, which is never a good sign. Somewhere east of us, a hunted man was waiting to find out whether a promise made on a morgue step still held. I blew out the lamp and began, in the dark, to pack.
