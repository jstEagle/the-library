## 4. Declared Enemy

The dispatch arrived at eleven o'clock on a Tuesday, which I record here because Sergeant Imogen Pryce believes that all catastrophes should be timestamped. It gives them dignity. It also gives you something to do with your hands while the world rearranges itself — note the hour, initial the log, file the paper. There is a great comfort in procedure. It is the only religion I have ever practiced with any devotion, and like most religions it fails you completely at the moment you need it most.

I was in the adjutant's tent, reconciling the morphine returns against the requisition ledger, when Corporal Dunmore brought the signals folder in on a clipboard, as though treason were something one could clip.

"From Division," he said. "Marked immediate."

"Everything is marked immediate," I said. "The last immediate order was about boot polish."

"This one's got a red tab, Sarge."

I took the folder. Dunmore hovered, the way men hover when they hope to be told to go and also hope to be asked to stay. I dismissed him with the flat of my hand and opened the folder alone, because I had already seen the name through the window of the envelope and wanted, foolishly, a moment of privacy with it before the world got hold of it.

*Captain Elias Vane, Royal Engineers (attached), is to be considered absent without leave and suspected of defection to enemy elements. Any information regarding his whereabouts is to be reported forthwith. All personal correspondence between Captain Vane and any member of His Majesty's forces is forbidden under Section Twelve. Possession of such correspondence will be treated as evidence of complicity.*

There followed a physical description, which was absurd, because whoever had typed it had never met the man. *Height: medium.* Elias Vane was not medium at anything. *Complexion: fair.* He had a face like a coastline — weathered, irregular, mapped by somebody who'd been in a hurry. *Distinguishing marks: none.*

None. The man had a scar across his left forearm from a girder at Ypres and a habit of standing exactly between you and whatever was falling, and they had typed *none*.

I read the dispatch three times. This is what rule-keepers do: we read things again, looking for the loophole, the misfiled clause, the error that will make the terrible thing untrue. It is not optimism. It is auditing. And my audit came back clean, as audits do. Defection. Treason, pending. Forbidden correspondence. *Forthwith*, which is the army's favorite word for *faster than thinking*.

I put the folder in the out-tray, which is where bad news goes to become everyone's problem, and I sat very still for a moment and listened to the canvas of the tent tick in the wind.

Then I did the arithmetic I should not have done.

Hazel Blackwood had gone white as unbleached calico two nights ago over an eight-year-old boy, and she had lied to me about it. She was a magnificent liar in the way that only honest people are — badly, with her whole face — but she had tried, and Hazel trying to lie is its own confession. And this morning she had asked me, casually, over the porridge, whether I thought the post still ran reliably through the eastern sector. Casual as a landmine.

I am not a clever woman, but I am a thorough one, and thoroughness will get you to the same destinations as cleverness, only by a road with more signposts.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about Elias Vane before that Tuesday, so you understand the size of what fell on us.

I knew him the way you know a man who pulled your best friend out of a fire. Secondhand, but thoroughly — Hazel talked about him the way soldiers talk about home: rarely, briefly, and always as though mentioning it might jinx it. A captain who'd found her under a collapsed aid station in '16 and dug for six hours with his hands because the shoring equipment was twenty minutes further away than his fingernails. Who'd sat with her after, when she couldn't stop shaking, and told her about his son instead of asking her about the dead, because he understood that some salvages need distracting from.

A man who made a promise with her on a morgue step somewhere — I never got the whole geography of it, and I never asked, because there are rooms in my friends' houses I don't enter without knocking.

I had met him twice myself. Once at a review, where he'd stood in the rain without an umbrella and made some joke about engineers being waterproof by trade. Once here, eighteen months ago, passing through with convoy paperwork, when he'd stayed for supper and helped Finn Calloway fix the stove and left the ward cleaner than he found it, which is more than can be said for most visiting officers, including the ones with medals.

He'd looked at Hazel across that stove and I had seen — because I notice things; it is my curse and my profession — that there existed between them an entire vocabulary conducted in eyebrow. I had thought, idly, that in a kinder war, or a kinder century, something might have been made of that. I had filed the observation and moved on, because filing observations is what I do instead of feeling them promptly.

So: treason. Elias Vane. The two nouns refused to marry, and I sat in the adjutant's tent performing their forced wedding and finding the banns fraudulent.

Here is what twelve years of service had taught me about accusations of defection. They come in two flavors. The first kind follows evidence: a man crosses lines, is seen, is identified. The second kind follows inconvenience: a man knows something, says something, or is positioned inconveniently close to something someone wants buried, and then a report is manufactured about him the way munitions are manufactured — industrially, and with a signature on every part.

I had no evidence for the second flavor. What I had was a lifetime of reading military paperwork, and the paperwork on Captain Vane smelled wrong. It was too fast. Accusations of treason are usually slow, gummed up with inquiry boards and witness statements. This one had arrived pre-assembled, polished, with the description typed by someone who had clearly never laid eyes on the subject. You do not type *distinguishing marks: none* about a man unless you are describing a rumor rather than a person.

And there was a third thing, which I set down here because it matters, though I didn't know it mattered yet. The dispatch was countersigned at the bottom by the office of Colonel Ashworth — liaison staff, inspection division. Ashworth had toured our hospital in the spring and complimented the tidiness of my stores for four full minutes. I had liked him the way one likes a well-organized drawer. I would revise that opinion later, expensively. But that Tuesday all I felt was a small itch behind the sternum, the itch of a figure that doesn't add up, and I did what I always do with itches. I ignored it until it became a symptom.

* * *

At noon I carried the relevant extracts to Matron, as regulation required, and watched her post the notice on the staff board: names of persons under suspicion, prohibition of correspondence, penalties enumerated. The notice was typed in the usual grey ink, the ink of bureaucracy, which is the color of weather coming.

The hospital read it the way hospitals read everything — in shifts, between tasks, with that particular field-hospital literacy that lets a nurse scan a death list while stirring a pot. Most of them skimmed past Vane's name without reaction. He was a stranger to them. That is the mercy of large organizations: they dilute catastrophe by distributing it among people who weren't paying attention.

Hazel was not most of them.

She was changing dressings in Ward Two when the notice went up, and I watched her from the corridor with a clipboard held against my chest like a breastplate. She did not stop moving. I want that recorded: she did not stop, did not drop anything, did not make a sound. She read the notice with her hands still working — gauze, pin, turn, pin — the way a sailor reads weather while continuing to haul. But her face did something I had never seen it do in nine years of friendship. It went somewhere else entirely. Not grief. Grief I know on her; we've shared it like a ration. This was older and more specific. This was a woman watching a door close on a room she had furniture in.

Then she finished the dressing, smiled at her patient, said something that made the boy laugh, and walked past me down the corridor with her chin level and her eyes dry and her knuckles white around a basin.

"Hazel," I said.

"Sergeant." She stopped. She used my rank. In nine years, Hazel Blackwood has used my rank exactly four times, and each time it was a wall being built at speed. "Did you need the requisition figures? I'll have them by evening sick-call."

"The notice," I said.

"What notice?" She looked at me with open, mild, blue-eyed blankness, and it was the worst acting I have ever witnessed from a woman who once convinced a whole ward that the moaning in the next tent was a wind phenomenon. "Oh, that. Terrible business. Imagine knowing a traitor."

And she walked on, basin and all, and left me standing in the corridor with my clipboard and my religion of procedure, both of them suddenly weighing a great deal more than they had at breakfast.

* * *

That afternoon I did three things, in order, and I set them down honestly because this account deserves honesty even if I did not, at the time.

First, I checked the mail log. Rule-keeper, remember. If forbidden correspondence existed, it would pass through my log, and my log was clean — no letters to or from any Vane in eleven months, which surprised me, because I'd half expected to find something to be righteous about. Hazel hadn't written to him. Whatever was between them lived entirely off the books.

Second, I went to see the boy.

He was in the isolation cubicle at the end of Ward One, where Hazel had stashed him with a fiction about diphtheria contacts that had fooled nobody, since the child plainly had nothing worse than a shrapnel nick in his shoulder and a silence the size of a cathedral. He was drawing. He drew constantly, on anything: margins of forms, the backs of bandage wrappers, once — Finn confessed this to me in a horrified whisper — the inside of a supply crate lid. Bridges mostly. A bridge drawn over and over, better each time, the way children practice fear until it becomes skill.

I stood in the doorway and looked at him and he looked back with his father's coastline of a face, and I understood, with the click of a well-made latch, whose handwriting had been on that note. I had not seen the note. I didn't need to. I had spent enough years around officers' correspondence to recognize the product of a man writing fast, in the dark, against time.

The boy was the correspondence. The boy was the letter the dispatch had forbidden.

Third, I did nothing. I said nothing to Hazel, nothing to Matron, nothing to anyone. I initialed the day's log, filed Ashworth's circular copy in the security binder, locked the binder, and pocketed the key, and then I lay on my cot that night staring at the tent ridge and conducting the strangest audit of my career: not of stores, but of myself.

Because here is the position I found myself in, laid out in proper columns like a good ledger. Column one: Sergeant Imogen Pryce, twelve years' service, spotless record, a woman who believes that rules are the load-bearing walls of civilization and that the alternative is men like the ones who start wars. Column two: the rules currently instructed me to report any knowledge of contact with a suspected traitor, and I possessed such knowledge, and the knowledge was wearing a nightshirt in Ward One and drawing bridges.

The columns did not balance. For the first time in my adult life, the columns did not balance, and I discovered something about myself that I did not enjoy: when the arithmetic fails, I do not reach for the rules. I reach for Hazel Blackwood. I have been doing it since '17, when she worked forty hours without sleep and still remembered that I took my tea without sugar, and I had mistaken the habit for principle ever since.

Loyalty, I wrote to myself on the back of a stores form, in pencil, at two in the morning, is not a virtue. It is a direction. And mine points at a woman who is lying to me very badly about a child she is hiding from the same army that is hunting his father, and one of these days the direction and the regulations are going to arrive at different addresses, and I will have to choose which envelope to open.

I crossed that out. Then I wrote underneath it, smaller:

*Not yet. But soon.*

* * *

Wednesday, the follow-up circular came down: all personnel who had professional acquaintance with Captain Vane were invited — the word *invited* doing the work of a bayonet — to submit written statements regarding his character, movements, and any expressions of disloyalty they might recall.

I drafted mine at the adjutant's table while Dunmore drank his tea an arm's length away, because everything is witnessed in this army, especially virtue. I wrote four sentences. They were true, every one, which I note with some pride, because it is possible to be true and useless at the same time and that, I have decided, is a form of resistance available to clerks.

*I encountered Captain Vane on two occasions in a professional capacity. He conducted himself correctly on both. I recall no expressions of disloyalty. I recall that he repaired a stove without being asked.*

Let Ashworth's office make of the stove what they wished.

I signed it, dated it, and filed it, and then I walked the long way back through the ward corridor, past the notice board, past the grey ink and the enumerated penalties, and stopped at the isolation cubicle, where Hazel was sitting on the floor beside the boy's cot reading him something from a book with no cover, and neither of them noticed me, which is the only kind of witnessing I trust.

She was pointing at the drawings pinned above his cot — the bridges — and saying something low, and the boy was shaking his head, and then, after a moment, nodding. Two people built from omissions, conferring in a language of yes and no. My chest did something unauthorized.

"Hazel," I said, and she turned, caught out, guilty as sin, beautiful in the way honest people are beautiful when caught. "The boiler's leaking again. In the linen store."

Her face flickered — the lie not landing, confusion assembling itself — and then she saw my eyes go deliberately to the drawings, and back to her, and rest there one second longer than casual.

"Oh," she said carefully. "The boiler."

"Mm. I'll be doing inventory in the records room until twenty-two hundred," I said. "Alone. The door sticks, so if anyone knocks, I probably won't hear it over the filing."

And I walked away before either of us could ruin it with gratitude, because gratitude would have made it real, and if it became real then I would have to admit what I had done: that I had just handed a key to a woman I believed to be concealing the son of a traitor, and that I had done it in perfect accordance with no regulation whatsoever.

That night, in the records room, I did not do inventory. I took out the security binder and read Ashworth's circular a fourth time, and then a fifth, and I noticed what I should have noticed on Tuesday: the follow-up instruction requested statements about Vane's *knowledge* as well as his character. His knowledge. His access. What units he had inspected. What manifests he had signed. What he had *seen*.

You do not ask what a man has seen unless you are afraid of what he knows.

I closed the binder. Outside, the guns had started up again to the east, steady as stitching, and somewhere inside that noise was a man the paperwork insisted was a traitor and my entire trained judgment insisted was a witness — and God help me, the difference between those two words was about to cost somebody everything.

I unlocked the records cabinet, took out a blank stores form, and began, in pencil, at the top of a clean page, a private list of my own.

It had one entry so far: *Vane, E., Capt. — description inaccurate. Marks: one scar, left forearm. Habits: stands between people and falling things. Status: misfiled.*

Underneath it I ruled a line and left room. Something in me, some clerk-deep instinct older than my loyalty and truer than my obedience, already knew there would be more names.

I heard the records room door stick, and open, and close. Filing, apparently, over which no knock could be heard.

I didn't turn around. "The requisition figures," I said, "are late."

"They're in my apron pocket," said Hazel, "along with several other things I imagine you'd like to pretend you don't know about."

I moved the lamp so it lit the empty chair across from me, and I pushed the pencil toward her side of the table, and that — though neither of us said so, and neither of us ever would — was the moment Sergeant Imogen Pryce stopped keeping the rules and started keeping a different kind of ledger entirely.
