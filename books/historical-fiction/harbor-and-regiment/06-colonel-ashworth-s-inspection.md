## 6. Colonel Ashworth's Inspection

Sergeant Imogen Pryce had a system for inspections, and the system was this: assume everything will go wrong, and then be pleasantly surprised when only half of it does.

She had been up since four. The floors of Ward C had been scrubbed twice — once by her, once again by young Orderly Whitfield after she'd found a dried boot print near the stove that she did not care to explain to anyone with brass on his collar. The supply ledgers were balanced to the half-ounce. The medicine cabinet was locked, the keys accounted for, the morphine tally signed in triplicate. Every patient had clean dressings, a clean blanket, and instructions to look appropriately grateful and not at all like men who had spent three weeks marinating in mud and gangrene.

It was not that Pryce feared inspection. Pryce feared nothing so small as inspection. What she feared — though she would have bitten through her own tongue before saying it aloud — was the specific quality of *this* inspection, which had arrived without warning, without the usual week's notice from divisional headquarters, and without any of the paperwork that made an inspection an inspection rather than a fishing expedition.

Colonels did not simply appear at field hospitals. Colonels were announced, scheduled, catered to, and survived. A colonel who appeared unannounced was a colonel looking for something.

"Whitfield," she said, for the fourth time that morning, "if the colonel asks you anything, you answer 'I don't know, Sergeant Pryke handles that,' and then you look at me."

"Yes, Sarge."

"And if he asks you something I can't handle?"

Whitfield considered this. He was nineteen and had the kind of honest face that got men killed in card games. "Then I look confused."

"You're always confused. Just be more of it."

He smiled, blessedly incurious, and went back to folding bandages into the sharp rectangles Pryce demanded. She allowed herself one breath — in through the nose, out slow, the way the chaplain taught her back when she still attended services — and then went to find Hazel.

She found her in Ward B, kneeling beside the bed of Private Ostrander, who was sleeping off his second amputation with the peaceful expression of a man finally free of a very heavy boot. Hazel's hands moved with their usual unhurried competence, but there was something wrong with the picture, and it took Pryce a moment to isolate what it was.

Hazel was humming.

Hazel did not hum. Hazel worked in silence, or she worked while delivering lectures about proper wound drainage, but she did not hum, least of all on a morning when an unscheduled colonel was due to descend on them like weather.

"You know he's coming," Pryce said quietly, crouching beside her.

"Who?" Hazel asked, with the studied blankness of a woman who knew exactly who.

"The colonel. Ashworth. The one whose adjutant telephoned forty minutes ahead, which is either incompetence or theater, and I don't like either option." Pryce kept her voice low. Ostrander snored on. "You've been strange for two weeks, Blackwood. Strangler-strange. And now you're humming over a double amputee like it's somebody's birthday."

"I'm in a good mood."

"You've never been in a good mood in your life. You told me once that joy was a clerical error."

"That was before the war improved everyone's spirits." Hazel tied off the last bandage and stood, brushing her skirt flat. Her eyes, when they met Pryce's, were doing something Pryce had never seen them do: sliding away. Avoiding her. Twelve years of friendship, three of them in this cesspool of a hospital, and Hazel Blackwood could not hold her gaze for two full seconds.

"Hazel."

"Mm?"

"If there's something in this hospital that shouldn't be here—"

"There isn't."

"—or someone—"

"There *isn't*, Imogen." Said too fast. Said the way people say things when they've rehearsed them in the dark.

Pryce straightened. She was aware, distantly, of a sound outside — an engine, laboring up the rutted track toward the gate — and of her own heartbeat, which had developed opinions.

The motorcar stopped. A door slammed. Boots crunched gravel with the unhurried confidence of a man who had never once been late for anything or waited for anyone.

"Right," Pryce said. "Shoulders back. And Hazel — whatever it is you're hiding, hide it better than you're hiding it from me."

Something flickered across Hazel's face — gratitude, or guilt, or both wearing each other's coats — and then the ward door opened and the cold came in with him.

* * *

Colonel Reginald Ashworth was not a large man, which was perhaps why he made such a point of occupying rooms as if he owned them. He entered Ward B the way a magistrate enters a courtroom, removed his gloves finger by finger, and surveyed the patients with the expression of a man auditing livestock.

"Sergeant Pryce." His voice was dry paper. "Matron unavailable, I understand."

"Matron Ellery is at the divisional conference, sir. Since Tuesday. As scheduled."

"As scheduled." He said it the way another man might say *so you claim*. "Then I suppose you are my guide, Sergeant. I do prefer a hospital that runs itself properly. There are so few that do." He smiled, and Pryce noted that the smile engaged none of the muscles above his mouth. "You keep records, I take it."

"Everything is recorded, sir. Personnel, supplies, treatments, deaths. Signed, dated, cross-filed."

"Deaths," Ashworth repeated, mildly, as if tasting it. "A grim category. But necessary. A record is only useful, Sergeant, when it agrees with itself. You'd be surprised how often it doesn't." He began walking the ward, and Pryce fell in beside him, matching her step to his because falling behind felt like surrender. "Paperwork is the skeleton of an army. When bones go missing, the body forgets its shape."

"We don't lose bones here, sir."

"No?" He paused at Ostrander's bed, consulted a clipboard his adjutant handed him, and frowned at whatever was written there. "Private Ostrander. Admitted the ninth. Transferred from the 3rd Clearing Station. Yet the 3rd's morning report for the ninth lists no transfer of wounded to this sector at all." He looked up. His eyes were pale and patient, like a fisherman watching a bobber. "Curious, wouldn't you say? A man arrives at your hospital whom no unit admits to having sent. Where does he come from, Sergeant?"

Pryce heard herself answer before she'd decided to. "Ambulance routing error, sir. We logged it. The correction request went to divisional records on the tenth. If the 3rd's report hasn't been amended, that's a failure at their end, not ours. I have the carbon copy if you'd care to see it."

There was a silence of perhaps two seconds. In those two seconds, Pryce performed a small, rapid internal audit of herself and found, to her considerable alarm, that she had just invented nothing, concealed nothing, and yet had somehow stepped between this colonel and a question with the smoothness of long practice. She had covered errors before. She had never enjoyed it.

"A thorough woman," Ashworth said at last. "How rare." He moved on.

At the far end of the ward, Hazel was changing a dressing with her back to them, and Pryce watched the colonel watch her. He did not approach. He did not ask her name, though his clipboard surely held it. He simply looked — three seconds, five, seven — the way a man looks at a photograph he is comparing to a face he saw somewhere else. Then he turned to Pryce as though nothing had happened and asked whether she found the stoves adequate.

* * *

The tour continued. It continued, Pryce thought grimly, the way a customs search continues — technically orderly, fundamentally hostile.

In Ward A, Ashworth counted beds against his list and found the numbers matched, and seemed displeased about it. In the pharmacy, he unlocked the morphine cabinet himself — Pryce produced the key with theatrical readiness and watched his jaw tighten almost imperceptibly — and checked the tally against the ledger, line by line, while the adjutant yawned into his fist. In the kitchen he questioned the cook about requisitions for eleven minutes, establishing beyond doubt that they fed forty-one people and purchased food for forty-one people, and Pryce began, cautiously, to hope.

Perhaps it really was an inspection. Perhaps the war was ending and this was merely the bureaucratic death rattle of a man verifying inventory before the whole apparatus was dismantled. Perhaps—

And then, in the corridor between the surgical tent and the linen store, Ashworth stopped walking.

"Sergeant," he said pleasantly, "your personnel file."

"Sir?"

"Yours specifically. But also theirs, generally." He gestured with two fingers at the hospital around them — the walls, the tents, the mud, the whole sweating organism of it. "Nurses, orderlies, drivers. I'm told field hospitals attract strays. Men too ill to serve and too proud to leave. Civilians swept up in retreats. Children, even." He smiled his paper smile. "Unofficial persons. Persons who appear on no roster anywhere, who cannot be traced, who are — how shall I put it — administratively invisible. One finds them everywhere in wartime, tucked into kitchens and laundries. Do you have any such persons here, Sergeant Pryce?"

"No, sir."

"None?"

"Every person attached to this facility appears on the divisional roster, sir. I verified it myself this morning. Fourteen nurses, twenty-two orderlies, six drivers, one cook, one chaplain, one matron. Forty-five souls, all documented, all accounted for." She recited it flatly, precisely, and hated how the precision sounded — how a simple truthful answer could be shaped, by tone alone, into a wall.

Ashworth regarded her for a long moment. Outside, wind worried at the tent ropes; somewhere in Ward C a man cried out in his sleep and was hushed by another.

"Forty-five," the colonel echoed. "And no one has passed through. No visitors. No refugees taken in from the road. No... deliveries left at your door."

The word *deliveries* landed in Pryce's stomach like a dropped scalpel. She thought of Finn Calloway's white face two nights ago, of the bundle under the army blanket in Ward D, of the note she had not seen but had heard described in Hazel's shaken voice — *forget this face* — and of the boy himself, silent as a stone, eight years old at most, lying in the last cot where nobody official ever looked.

"No, sir," she said.

"You're certain."

"I run a tight facility, Colonel."

"So I see." He put his gloves back on, one finger at a time, unhurried, giving her time — she understood this now, fully, horribly — giving her *time* to change her answer. She didn't. "Your matron returns when?"

"Tomorrow, sir."

"Good. I'll want a complete copy of your admission registers. All of them. Back to the spring. My adjutant will collect them at eighteen hundred." He settled his cap. "You've been most helpful, Sergeant. Most hospitals have something wrong with them. Yours seems to have nothing wrong with it at all." He paused at the door, and for one moment the blandness dropped away like a sheet pulled off furniture, and Pryce saw the thing underneath, which was not stupidity and not cruelty exactly but a kind of terrible bookkeeping patience. "Nothing wrong with it *on paper*, at any rate."

Then he was gone, engine grinding, mud flying, and the hospital exhaled around her.

* * *

She found Hazel in the linen store, of all places, sitting on a crate of bandages with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a verdict.

"Well," Pryce said, shutting the door. "That was fun. Let's never do it again."

"He asked about admissions."

"He asked about everything. Registers, rosters, strays, children, *deliveries*." Pryce let that word sit in the air between them and watched Hazel flinch. "He wants every admission log since spring on his desk by eighteen hundred, and Hazel — love — we both know what's in those logs, and more to the point we both know what *isn't*."

"Imogen—"

"No. You don't get to do the thing." Pryce's voice cracked despite her best efforts, and she pressed on anyway, because twelve years entitled her to crack. "The thing where you go quiet and kind and decide things for both of us. Something happened. Something came to that door, and you took it in, and you didn't tell me. Me. The woman who has lied for you exactly zero times in our entire acquaintance until roughly ninety minutes ago, when I discovered I could apparently do it fluently and without practice, which frightened me considerably more than the colonel did."

Hazel stood. For a moment Pryce thought she would deny everything again, and Pryce braced for it, already composing the argument, already grieving the friendship's easy shape. Instead Hazel reached under the bottom shelf, pried loose a floorboard, and lifted out a worn leather ledger — and Pryce's heart, which had been braced for contraband, for weapons, for the boy himself, broke in an entirely unexpected direction when she opened it and saw what it actually was.

Names. Columns of names. Dates, regiments, causes of death, each entry in Hazel's careful hand, going back years.

"What is this?"

"People the reports forgot," Hazel said quietly. "Men who died and were never listed. Men whose names were struck. I've kept it since Ypres. Every erased person deserves remembering, Imogen. That's all it is. That's all it ever was." She hesitated, and Pryce watched her hesitate, and knew there was more. "Until recently. Recently it's also drawings. From the child."

"The boy." Pryce sat down slowly on the crate Hazel vacated, because her legs had filed a formal complaint. "The boy at the door. The one with orders to be forgotten. He's real. He's here. And you hid him from me for two weeks."

"I was protecting you."

"Don't you dare protect me. I am a sergeant in His Majesty's Army. Protection is my job, not yours." Pryce pressed her palms against her eyes until the dark went red. When she lowered them, Hazel was watching her with that awful gentle patience, and the anger drained out of her and left something colder behind. "He knows," she said. "Ashworth. The questions weren't random. *Deliveries left at your door.* He wasn't inspecting the hospital, Hazel. He was inspecting us. Measuring how much cover we'd give."

"I know."

"How long have you known?"

"Since the note. The handwriting was—" Hazel stopped. Started again. "I recognized it."

"Whose?"

Hazel looked at her, and Pryce saw in her friend's face something she had seen perhaps twice in twelve years: fear without any irony over it at all.

"Elias Vane's."

Pryce sat with that. Captain Elias Vane, whose name had crossed her desk ten days ago in a divisional circular stamped red on both sides: *declared enemy collaborator, wanted for treason, all contact forbidden.* She remembered reading it, remembering nothing, filing it, and going to lunch. Paperwork. Just paperwork.

"You're sheltering a traitor's son," she said, "with a traitor's note hidden somewhere in this room, in a hospital that a colonel is coming back for tonight at eighteen hundred, and you've told me now because—"

"Because he'll ask for the registers and I can't give them to him and I can't refuse, and because—" Hazel's composure finally slipped, just at the edge, just enough to show the girl underneath the nurse. "Because I'm going to have to move him, Imogen. Soon. Maybe within days. And I can't do that alone."

The linen store was very quiet. Somewhere outside, Whitfield laughed at something, young and oblivious, and the sound of it went through Pryce like a bayonet, because she understood suddenly and completely what Ashworth's visit had cost: there was no longer a version of this where Imogen Pryce was not involved. The moment she had smoothed over that transfer error, the moment she had said *no, sir* with a steady voice, she had signed something. Complicity was not a decision. It was a direction, and she was already walking.

She thought about the ledger in Hazel's hands. She thought about forty-five souls, all documented. She thought about the boy in Ward D who did not speak, and about the circular with Vane's face on it, and about her own reflection in the pharmacy glass that morning, upright, correct, and for the first time in her adult life, crooked inside.

"Eighteen hundred," Pryce said at last, standing, straightening her tunic with hands that were nearly steady. "He wants the registers by eighteen hundred. So at seventeen hundred, I'm going to walk into the records office, and I'm going to pull every page that mentions that boy, and I'm going to burn them in the stove, and then I'm going to write out a fresh set that has never heard of him. And I want you to understand, Hazel Blackwood, that I hate this. I hate every second of it. I have spent thirty-nine years being the person who tells the truth to authority, and you have made me into the other thing in one afternoon, and if this goes wrong — if anyone in this hospital pays for him — I will never forgive you."

Hazel's eyes were bright. "Understood."

"And when this is over," Pryce added, reaching for the door, "you're telling me everything. The note, the promise, whatever it is you're not saying about why a decorated captain is suddenly a collaborator. Everything."

"Everything," Hazel agreed.

Pryce believed her, which was the problem. She pushed open the door into the grey afternoon light and the smell of carbolic and mud, and she was halfway across the yard before she stopped dead, because Orderly Whitfield was running toward her from the gate, cap in hand, honest face gone white.

"Sarge," he panted. "Sarge — Finn. They took Finn at the transport pool. Military police. Sergeant Pryke — they're saying he stole from stores. But Sarge—" He swallowed. "Before they marched him past Ward D, he looked in the window. At the kid. And then one of the MPs wrote something down."

Behind her, from the direction of the road, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a motorcar turning around.
