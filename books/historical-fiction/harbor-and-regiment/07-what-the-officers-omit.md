## 7. What the Officers Omit

There is a particular hour in a field hospital — somewhere between the last of the evening dressings and the first of the night's screaming — when the war seems to hold its breath. The lamps are trimmed low to spare the coal. The moaning settles into a rhythm you can almost mistake for sleep. And if you are a woman with a stolen ledger and a talent for arithmetic that no one thought to train out of her, that is the hour for accounting.

I had been keeping two sets of books for twelve years. The official one lived in the adjutant's tent, written in the tidy hand of men who had never held a tourniquet. Mine lived under the floorboard beneath my cot, wrapped in oilcloth, and it was written in mine.

That night I spread them both out on my knees like a woman darning socks, which is what anyone glancing through the canvas would have assumed I was doing. On my left, the ledger. On my right, three months of supply manifests I had liberated from Finn's cart — he'd been using them to line a biscuit tin, and I told him I needed them for inventory, which was even true, in the way most useful lies are true.

The manifests listed everything. Rations by company. Ammunition expended. Casualties evacuated, by unit, by date, by receiving station. The army's great genius has always been paperwork; its great weakness is that paperwork remembers what officers prefer to forget.

I had noticed the thing weeks ago, the way you notice a loose thread on a sleeve — an irritation, nothing more. Private Aldous Wren, omitted from the morning report back in the spring, had died at a place called Kessel Farm. So had eleven other men whose names I carried, all from the same battalion, all on the same October morning, all filed under the same cause of death: *friendly fire, artillery misdirection.*

Eleven men killed by their own guns in one morning is not misdirection. It is a catastrophe, and catastrophes get inquiries, and inquiries get findings, and findings get names attached to them. I remembered reading about Kessel Farm. There had been no inquiry. There had been a commendation.

I began to make lists within the list.

*Friendly fire* appeared in my ledger fourteen times across two years. Fourteen incidents. Thirty-one names — thirty-one men whose deaths were explained so smoothly, so promptly, in the very first report, before anyone could have possibly counted their own dead properly. That was the detail that snagged me, the way a nail snags a hem. In honest confusion — and I have seen honest confusion; I once watched a battery shell a farmhouse full of our own wounded because the mapmaker had drawn the road on the wrong side of the river — in honest confusion, the reports take days. They argue. They contradict one another. Captains write furious letters to majors. The truth arrives late and ugly, dragged in by survivors.

These reports arrived early and clean. Pre-shaved, like kindling.

And every single incident, without exception, involved a unit commanded by one of five officers. Major Halloran, now Lieutenant Colonel, decorated for "steadiness under adverse conditions." Captain Merrick, now Major, cited twice. Colonel Ashworth himself, whose signature I found at the bottom of the Kessel Farm after-action report, approving the finding of friendly fire before some of the wounded had even reached a dressing station.

Before. I read the dates four times. The finding was dated the ninth of October. The last of the Kessel Farm casualties reached a clearing station on the eleventh.

You cannot count your dead and approve the arithmetic two days before they finish dying. Unless, of course, you already knew how many there were going to be. Unless the number was not a tragedy to be tallied but a figure to be reconciled — ammunition expended against positions confirmed, guns sold against graves filled.

I sat very still in the lamplight and let the thought finish itself, because it was going to whether I permitted it or not.

They had not been shelling their own men by accident. They had been selling the positions. Coordinates passed along in exchange for whatever passes between a staff officer and the enemy's quartermasters — money, probably, or safety, or simply the exquisite pleasure of men who believe themselves above the consequences they administer. And then, when the shells fell where they had arranged for them to fall, these same officers wrote the reports, approved the findings, buried the men under the word *misdirection*, and accepted medals for their grief.

Thirty-one names. I touched each one as I counted, the way I always do, thumb pressed briefly to the ink. Aldous Wren, who owed me a letter to his sister. Corporal Ives, who sang off-key. A boy called Tam whose surname I never learned because he died before the stretcher cleared the door, and I wrote down *Tam, 17 or thereabouts, red hair, called himself lucky.*

Lucky. Yes.

The lamp guttered. Outside, a transport ground past in the mud, and someone laughed — actually laughed, young and unguarded, the laugh of a man who did not yet know what his own officers kept in their ledgers.

Here is the thing about revelation that no one warns you: it does not arrive like thunder. It arrives like cold water finding the seam in your boot. One small wrongness, then another, and then suddenly you are soaked to the bone and have been for some time.

Because Vane's name was in my ledger too.

Not among the dead — Elias Vane was not dead, whatever else he was now. He was there in the old pages, the early ones, from the year of the burning aid station, entered in my younger handwriting: *Captain E. Vane, 4th Regiment, pulled six wounded from the fire at Marren Cross, commended.* I had started the ledger, in those days, as a book of debts — names the official record got wrong in either direction, overpraised or erased, and I wanted the truth of them written down somewhere, even if only for me.

But there was a second entry. Later. Two years ago, in my current hand, careful as a confession:

*Captain E. Vane, 4th Regiment, requested transfer of three men out of forward position under Maj. Halloran. Request denied. All three killed in "friendly fire" engagement, 14 March. Vane filed formal objection. Objection returned unread.*

I had recorded it at the time as an oddity, a small injustice, the kind of thing that happens in armies the way weather happens over harbors. I had not connected it to anything, because I had not yet known there was anything to connect it to.

Now the thread pulled through, and the whole garment turned inside out.

Vane had seen it first. Of course he had — he was the sort of officer who counted his men at morning and again at night and could not rest until the numbers agreed. Three of his soldiers moved into a position and then shelled by their own guns, and an objection returned unread, and then — what? He must have done what I was doing tonight. Pulled manifests. Compared dates. Watched the pattern assemble itself out of other people's grief. And then he must have made the mistake I had not yet made: he must have shown someone. Asked a question aloud. Written a letter up the chain to a man who was not merely negligent but implicated.

And the answer to that letter had been the word *traitor*, stamped across his life, his name handed to every checkpoint in the province, his son left on a hospital step with a note that said *forget this face.*

Forget this face. I had read it as a father's desperation — hide the boy, erase him, keep him safe by making him invisible. It was that. But it was also, I understood now, a message in the only cipher available to a hunted man: this is what they do. This is what erasure looks like when it is done deliberately. I am showing you the weapon by letting it pass over my child.

Or perhaps I was romanticizing a frightened man's scrawl. Both things can be true. Most true things travel in pairs.

What I could not deny was the arithmetic. Five officers. Fourteen immaculate reports. Thirty-one of my names. And a captain who asked one question too many, now officially an enemy of the army he had bled for, while the men who sold his soldiers' coordinates wore new rank and ribbons.

I closed the ledger and sat with my hands flat upon it, feeling its weight change. For twelve years it had been a memorial. Tonight it had become evidence. Those are different objects entirely. A memorial asks to be kept. Evidence asks to be used, and using it will cost something — I did not yet know how much, but I felt the price already accruing, interest compounding quietly in the dark like everything else in this war.

A sensible woman would burn it. I want that recorded: I considered it honestly, there in the lamplight, weighing the lives in this hospital — Pryce, Finn, the orderlies, the boys in the wards who trusted us to keep the shells on the far side of the canvas — against the thirty-one dead who could not be helped by anything except the truth. A sensible woman weighs those things and chooses the living.

But the living included Tomas, asleep two tents away, who had arrived at my door carrying his father's handwriting like a brand. And the dead included Aldous Wren's sister, waiting for a letter that the official record said she should never expect, because her brother had never officially existed on the day he died. And I am not, whatever Pryce says, a sensible woman. I am a woman who keeps accounts. The debtors in this case wear colonel's insignia, and I have never once forgiven a debt out of respect for the debtor's rank.

I rewrapped the manifests, slid the ledger beneath the floorboard, and pressed the board home with the heel of my hand. Then I sat on the edge of the cot and listened to the hospital breathe.

Which is when I heard footsteps outside my tent. Unhurried. Deliberate. The tread of a man who walks as though the ground were requisitioned for his personal convenience, and stops — just for a moment — directly opposite my canvas wall.

Then the boots moved on toward the command tent, and I heard Finn's voice greeting whoever it was, easy and warm, the voice of a man who had not yet learned that curiosity is a terminal condition in wartime.

I lay down and did not sleep. Under the floorboard, thirty-one names waited with the patience of the dead, and somewhere beyond the wire, in the dark between the lines, a man I owed my life to was hiding from the country he had tried to save — and I finally understood that he had sent me his son not because I was kind, but because I was the only person he knew who wrote things down.

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping, and with it Sergeant Imogen Pryce, bearing tea and the expression she reserved for paperwork she intended to win.

"You look terrible," she said, setting the mug on my knee.

"I was doing sums."

"At three in the morning."

"Important ones."

She studied me the way she studied supply requisitions — looking for the discrepancy, the line that didn't reconcile. Pryce has known me for nine years and has catalogued my faces the way I catalogue names. She opened her mouth, and I braced for the question.

It never came. Instead she said, "Colonel Ashworth's staff sent word down. He'll be inspecting the hospital Thursday. Unauthorized personnel, missing paperwork, irregularities." Her eyes flicked, just once, toward the floorboard beneath my cot — a glance so quick I might have invented it. "So whatever sums you were doing, Hazel, do them clean."

She straightened and walked out into the rain, and I drank my tea and understood that the water had found another seam — that the cold was rising faster now, and the boot was filling, and Thursday was coming for all of us whether we had finished our arithmetic or not.
