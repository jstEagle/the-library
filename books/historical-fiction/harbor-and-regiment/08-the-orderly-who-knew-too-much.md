## 8. The Orderly Who Knew Too Much

There is a particular talent required of an orderly in a field hospital, and it is not the talent people assume. It isn't stomach — I've seen surgeons with weaker constitutions than any laundress. It isn't strength, though we carry enough. It is the ability to see everything and be seen seeing nothing. You learn to look at a dying man's face the way you look at weather: taking full account, offering no opinion.

I had been practicing that talent for six years, and I like to think I'd gotten rather good at it. Good enough, at least, that when Nurse Blackwood started doing sums behind her eyes instead of on paper, I noticed and said nothing. Good enough that when she began carrying a boy's supper down to the linen store at hours when linen stores don't eat, I carried the bread myself and remarked only on the mildness of the weather.

Which brings me to Thursday, or rather to the Wednesday night before it, when I did the one thing in this war I cannot excuse by calling it duty.

* * *

The boy — Tomas, though officially he was nobody at all — drew the way other men bled. Constantly, quietly, and from somewhere deep. He'd been with us eleven days and had not spoken a word, but Sister Blackwood had salvaged half a pencil stub for him, and he filled every scrap of paper that came near his hands: a bridge, drawn over and over; a river's bend with a mill wheel missing two spokes; a man's face rendered in quick, sure strokes, always looking slightly left of you, as if at something you hadn't noticed yet.

I found them, you understand. The boy, I mean. The night of the bombardment, when the shells came over like a freight train derailing itself endlessly above us, I went to the door because someone had to check the sandbags, and there he was. Eight years old, maybe nine, small for either. Wounded in the shoulder — not badly, which was its own kind of message, because whoever left him had patched him first. And pinned to his coat, a note in a fine officer's hand that said, in four words, everything I have spent six years pretending not to hear:

*Forget this face. Please.*

The *please* was what undid me. Officers don't say please. Officers command, request formally through channels, or simply take. Whoever wrote that note had been desperate and courteous in the same breath, and I had stood there in the falling brick dust holding a child who smelled of woodsmoke and carbolic, thinking: *this is going to cost somebody something.*

I just assumed it would cost me my conscience, not my liberty.

* * *

Here is what happened Wednesday night, and I'll tell it plainly because lying about it now would insult everyone involved.

Sister Blackwood had gone to the officers' ward — some complaint about Colonel Somebody's morphine dosage — and the ward was quiet in the way wards get around ten o'clock: the quiet of men sleeping like they're being paid for it. Tomas sat on the end cot nearest the stove, drawing. And I, passing through with an armful of clean bandages I no longer needed to be carrying, stopped to look over his shoulder, as I'd done a dozen times.

But this time the drawing was new. Not the bridge. Not the mill. It was a column of figures — little marks, ranks of them, like a shepherd counting sheep — and beside each mark, a shape. Some of the shapes were stars. Some were chevrons. And one, circled twice with a child's furious pressure until the paper had nearly torn, was a colonel's crown and pip.

Tomas looked up at me. He had his mother's eyes or somebody's mother's eyes, dark and level and much too old, and he looked at me the way I imagine accountants look at men who've come to withdraw money they haven't got.

Then he held out the pencil to me.

I should have said no. I want that recorded somewhere better than Hazel Blackwood's floorboard, which has enough under it already: I should have said no. Instead I took the pencil, and I took a corner of the bandage wrapping — blank paper, technically hospital property, which is the fig leaf I've been dressing the sin in ever since — and I copied it. The marks, the shapes, the circled crown and pip. Badly, with my butcher's hands, but recognizably. Because some part of me, the part that sees everything and says nothing, had finally done its arithmetic and arrived at the sum: *this boy saw something worth killing over, and if it kills him, the proof dies with him, and somebody ought to hold a copy of it somewhere other than an eight-year-old's pocket.*

I folded the paper small. I put it in my tunic pocket, against my chest, where a man keeps the things he means to keep.

And Sergeant Pryce walked in.

* * *

Now, in fairness to Sergeant Imogen Pryce — and I have had occasion since to consider what fairness I owe her — she didn't see me copy anything. She saw me standing too near a child, holding a pencil, with my hand moving away from my chest with the guilty speed of a boy hiding a cigarette. That's all anyone saw. But Pryce is the sort of woman who could convict a man on the angle of his elbow, and she looked at me across that ward for three seconds that lasted the rest of my life, and then she looked at Tomas, and then she left without a word.

I told myself she hadn't understood. I told myself a great many things that night while I lay awake listening to the rain, including that the paper in my pocket was harmless, that copying a child's scribbles was no crime known to His Majesty's Army, and that Thursday's inspection concerned paperwork, not people. I fell asleep around two with the folded square still against my heart, because I hadn't been able to make myself put it under a floorboard. Floorboards can be pried up. Chests, apparently, can also be opened, but try telling your fear that at two in the morning.

They came at five.

* * *

Not soldiers — military police, which is worse. Soldiers shout and blunder and give you time to be frightened properly. Military police are quiet. Two of them, with a corporal doing the talking, and they had my file with them, which meant this wasn't improvised. This had been typed. Somewhere there was a form with my name on it that predated whatever I'd actually done, and that frightened me more than the rifles did, because forms require authorship.

"Orderly Finn Calloway." Not a question.

"That's me."

"You'll come with us. Theft of regimental property."

I laughed. I'm not proud of it; it just came out, one short stupid bark in the grey dawn dark of the ward, waking Private Ellis, who swears to this day I said something witty, which I did not. "Theft? Of what, Corporal? The spoons? I'll confess to the spoons."

"Morphine," he said, without expression. "Four grains, unaccounted for over the past month. Your signature appears on dispensary records."

And here is the thing — here is the thing I need somebody besides me to know — my signature *did* appear on dispensary records, because I sign for supplies every blessed day of my life, and any clerk with ten minutes and a grudge could make those records say whatever the army needed them to say. Four grains of morphine. A number so precise and so petty it could only have been invented by a man with a ledger of his own, someone who understood exactly how a theft should look on paper because he'd been manufacturing them professionally.

My eyes went to the door, past the corporal, into the yard where the rain was coming down like the sky had given up on subtlety. I was looking for Hazel. I don't know what I thought she could do — argue arithmetic with armed men? But the yard was empty except for the ambulance, grey and dripping, and Sergeant Pryce standing beneath its awning with her arms crossed and her face arranged into official nothing.

She wouldn't look at me.

Or — no. Let me be honest even here, in the last paragraph where honesty costs anything. She looked at me once, quickly, and there was something in it I've turned over a hundred times since: not triumph, not doubt. Recognition. The look of a woman watching a bill come due that she'd warned everybody about, written in a hand she knew.

They walked me out through the ward. Past the stove, still warm. Past the cots. And as I passed the end cot, Tomas looked up at me — he was awake, God help us, he'd heard everything — and he did the thing he had never done for anyone in this hospital, the thing that has kept me upright every hour since.

He opened his mouth. As if to speak. To call my name, or to confess, or to do whatever brave thing was gathering behind his teeth.

And I shook my head, one small shake, the smallest movement I've ever made, and he closed his mouth again and became a statue, and I have never loved a silent child more in my life.

* * *

They put me in a store tent behind the transport depot, which told me something: this wasn't a real arrest yet, it was a message being delivered slowly, with pauses for effect. They took my pockets — the folded paper went into an evidence envelope, and the corporal's eyebrows moved very slightly when he saw it was bandage wrapping, and moved again, more meaningfully, when he read what was drawn on it. Marks. Ranks. A circled crown and pip. He folded the envelope shut like a man closing a door on a room he doesn't want to smell.

Then I sat on a crate for four hours and did what I do best, which is watch and say nothing, and here is what I saw:

A captain I didn't recognize came to the tent flap twice, spoke to the sentry, and both times used the word "Thursday" — I caught it on the wind both times, plain as church bells. And the second time, he said something else, and I've replayed it so often I can hear the exact flatness of it:

"—and the nurse's quarters last. Start with the orderly's things."

The orderly's things. My kit, my locker, my letters from my sister in Cork. They were going to search my belongings for whatever they expected to find there, and they expected to find something, which meant they knew what was in this hospital worth finding, which meant the note pinned to a child's coat eleven days ago had not been the end of anything. It had been the beginning. Someone had sent that boy here believing a field hospital was the safest lie available, and someone else had followed the thread of him straight to us, patient as a boot filling with water.

I thought about Hazel then, really thought about her, sitting on that crate with my hands cuffed to a rail and the rain drumming the canvas like impatient fingers. Twelve years, she'd told me once, drunk on one glass of porter at Christmas, twelve years of writing down the names that officers strike out. I'd laughed and called it morbid sentimentality, and she'd looked at me with those steady grey eyes and said, "It's bookkeeping, Finn. Someday somebody will audit this war, and the accounts will need to balance."

Someday had apparently arrived early, and it was auditing us first.

Because that was the part I kept circling, there in the wet cold, the part that made the guilt sit in my chest like swallowed stone: they hadn't arrested Hazel. They'd arrested *me*. The least important person in the conspiracy, if conspiracy there was. The obvious one. The man who found the boy, who lingered near him, who moved his hand suspiciously toward his chest. Taking me was cheap. Taking me cost nothing and purchased everything — it told anyone else involved that the water was rising, that the search would begin with the orderly's things and proceed upward, and that the next name on the list would be a nurse with grey eyes and a floorboard.

Everything that happened next — the mill bridge, the marshes, the chapel, all of it, all the terrible and holy rest of it — traces back to that crate, to that hour, to the moment I understood that the price of sheltering Tomas Vane had come due, and I had paid it, and the receipt was being delivered to Hazel's door.

* * *

Near evening they brought me bread and tea, and with the tea, unexpectedly, came Sergeant Pryce.

She dismissed the sentry with a nod that he obeyed, which I noted, filed, and worried about later. Then she stood in the tent entrance with her back to the light and looked at me for a long moment, and when she spoke it was low and fast, a voice stripped of every ounce of sergeant in it.

"They're searching your locker now."

"I know."

"There's a girl in the laundry — Maren. If they ask about morphine, she'll say she saw you near the dispensary on the ninth. She thinks she's telling the truth. Someone's been careful with her memory."

"Why are you telling me this?"

For the first time since I'd known her, Imogen Pryce looked uncertain, and it aged her backward somehow, made her young, made her a girl in a uniform that didn't fit yet. "Because I covered for Hazel on Thursday," she said, "and I've been sick about it since, and today they arrested you for a theft that has your signature on records anyone could forge, and I find I am done pretending I don't know how to read handwriting."

She meant the note. The boy's note. She knew.

"The drawings," I said quietly. "Did they find—"

"They found bandage wrappers and socks," she said. "What they were looking for wasn't there. Which means it's somewhere else, and Finn—" and here she glanced at the tent flap, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—Finn, whatever Hazel has under that floorboard, whatever that child carries in his pockets, they're coming for it tomorrow. Ashworth's captain said *the nurse's quarters last.* Last implies a sequence. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

I understood. I was handcuffed to a rail in a store tent on fabricated charges, and I understood perfectly, and the worst of it — the part I carry still — is that understanding came with relief. Because if they were coming for the ledger, then Hazel would have to run, and running meant the boy would live, and my arrest would have bought something after all. Guilt and hope, in equal measure, in the same breath. That's the army for you. Everything comes bundled.

"Tell her—" I started.

"I'm not carrying messages," Pryce said sharply, and the sergeant was back in her voice, armor over the girl. "I'm telling you what a witness tells a witness. That's all this was."

She turned to go, and I said her name, and she stopped but didn't turn, her shoulders squared against whatever I was going to say, and what I said was this:

"When they court-martial me, ask for the original dispensary records. Not the copies. The originals will show my signature on days I was on leave. Ask for the ninth."

She stood very still. Then, without turning: "You're assuming there'll be a tribunal."

"I'm assuming," I said, "that Hazel Blackwood keeps accounts. And someday somebody audits this war."

She left. The rain went on. And I sat in the failing light doing the only thing left to an orderly who knows too much — watching, saying nothing, and keeping count of the footsteps outside my tent, waiting for morning, and Thursday, and whatever was about to knock at the door of a nurse's quarters, last of all.
