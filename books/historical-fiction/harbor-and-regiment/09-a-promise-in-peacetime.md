## 9. A Promise in Peacetime

The deserter came in with the evening ration cart, folded between two crates of turnips like something the quartermaster had ordered by mistake.

I found him because Private Weller tripped over him. Weller was carrying a bedpan at the time, which meant the swearing woke half of Ward C, which meant I had grounds to be there inside ten seconds, on the pretext of professional calm. The man on the floor of the corridor was so thin his uniform looked like it had been hung on a coat rack and forgotten. His boots had given up entirely — one sole flapped when he tried to stand, like a fish gasping on a dock.

"Refugee," said the driver, not meeting my eyes. "Found him on the Millhaven road. Thought he ought to see a doctor before he saw a magistrate."

There are many varieties of lie in an army, and most of them wear rank. This one wore turnip juice. The driver knew exactly what he'd found, and he'd brought it to my hospital rather than the provost's office, which told me more than any confession would have. Word travels strangely behind the lines. Apparently it had traveled as far as a turnip cart.

"He'll need feeding," I said. "Slowly. Six small meals or he'll make us wear all of them."

The driver nodded, tipped his cap, and left with a speed that suggested he wanted no part of whatever this was. Wise man. I envied him his turnips.

We got the deserter into a cot in the back room where we put the ones nobody official was looking for — we had several such cots, maintained through a system of paperwork so creative it could have hung in a gallery. He drank broth the way a dry field drinks rain: first desperately, then slowly, then with something like astonishment that it kept coming. His eyes were the trouble. Not feverish. Watching. Measuring whether we were the kind of people who asked questions or the kind who answered them.

I've learned to tell soldiers from each other by their silences. Most men who come through a hospital are silent because they're exhausted, or afraid, or have used up every word they own on the thing that wounded them. This man was silent the way a courier is silent. Silent the way a sealed envelope is silent.

He waited until the third bowl of broth. Then, while Tomas slept in the corner and Sister Anne pretended to inventory bandages at the far end of the room — she has been pretending to inventory bandages for twelve years and is very good at it — he reached into his collar and drew out a scrap of oilcloth, sewn there, I'd wager, with his own shaking hands and a sail needle.

"For the matron," he rasped. "For the woman who writes down names."

My hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the draft under the door.

"You were told that?" I said. "Told to find me?"

"Told to find a nurse who keeps a book nobody sent her for." He almost smiled; it cost him something and he paid it anyway. "Word gets about, ma'am. Even among the dead men in your ledger."

* * *

The message was four words long, if you didn't count the cipher, which I did, twice, by candlelight, with my heart going like a drummer boy who'd found the war's true purpose.

Elias Vane wrote in a hand I could draw blindfolded. Twelve years and I still knew the way he crossed his sevens, the slight lean of his letters as though they were all leaning into a wind coming from the left. Four words after decoding:

*Mill bridge. Before signing.*

And beneath them, smaller, as though he'd hesitated: *or not at all.*

I sat with that last line a long while. There are phrases that are instructions and phrases that are confessions, and this managed both. *Or not at all.* It said: this may be a trap. It said: I may already be dead and this letter is a ghost writing to you. It said, most plainly and most terribly: I will not ask twice, because asking twice would endanger you further, and Elias Vane never in his life spent another person's safety cheaply.

Outside, the guns had gone quiet, which was worse than the noise. Quiet nights meant either nothing happening or everything gathering.

Tomas slept with his fists curled under his chin, the way he'd slept since he arrived, guarding even in sleep whatever it was he couldn't say. On my desk lay his latest drawing — the bridge again, always the bridge, drawn with a child's ferocious precision: the stone arch, the broken mill wheel, the willow stump on the north bank. A bridge he knew better than I did.

And now a bridge I'd been summoned to.

I want to say I deliberated. I did not. Deliberation implies options, and from the moment I decoded those four words I had only one decision left, which was how fast.

* * *

Here is what I did in the three days the message gave me, and let the record show — since I am, after all, the only person keeping records around here — that I did it badly and completely.

I fed the deserter back to strength enough to travel, gave him civilian boots from the dead-box that no one would claim, and pointed him toward the coast without asking a single question about who sent him. Some mercies are also tactics. If he talked, he could talk about turnips.

I taught Sister Anne the fever chart fictions she'd need to keep the ward running without me, and I did it in the language nurses use for emergencies, which sounds like ordinary conversation but carries freight. She looked at me once, sharply, over a tray of instruments, and said, "You'll want the blue shawl, then, not the grey. Grey shows the mud." And went back to her counting. I have never loved anyone so efficiently.

I took Tomas out to the yard each afternoon and walked him past the ambulance, casually, the way you accustom a cat to a basket. He watched me check the tires and the water and the fuel with those grave dark eyes of his and asked nothing, because asking was the one language he'd surrendered.

And I packed. Small things, chosen in stolen minutes: morphine, dressings, the good scissors, tinned milk, a bar of chocolate I'd been saving against some future that apparently had arrived early. My mother's photograph, which I told myself I brought for luck and knew I brought because if I was going to be foolish I intended to be foolish in front of her.

That's where Pryce caught me. Wednesday night, near midnight, cot-side, a kit bag open between us like a confession laid on an altar.

She stood in the doorway of the linen room I'd commandeered for privacy, arms folded, in her dressing gown with her hair in its night braid, and she looked at the bag, and then at me, and then at the bag again, and I watched eleven years of friendship perform arithmetic behind her eyes and arrive at an answer she didn't like.

"No," she said.

"Pryce—"

"Don't." She came in and shut the door. That was the frightening part, actually. Imogen Pryce argues in doorways, loudly, so the argument can be witnessed and adjudicated. Closing the door meant this wasn't going to be an argument. It was going to be something worse. "You're leaving. With the boy. In the ambulance I signed for. During the hour I cover for you every night whether you know it or not."

"You cover for me every night because I cover for you every night, and because neither of us sleeps."

"Don't you dare make this funny."

"I'm terrified," I said. "Funny is the closest thing I have."

She sat down on the linen shelf, which she would have court-martialed anyone else for, and pressed her palms flat against her knees, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet in the particular way that meant she'd stopped being angry and started being hurt, which is far more dangerous terrain.

"The colonel was here Tuesday. Ashworth. Asking about unauthorized personnel. Standing at the end of your ward like a man pricing a house. And I lied to him, Hazel. Me. I lied to a superior officer with my record standing right there in my voice, and I didn't even know what I was lying to protect. So you're going to tell me. Right now. All of it. Because I have been watching you carry something for weeks and I have decided — tonight, just now, sitting on these wretched sheets — that I would rather know than be safe."

So I told her.

All of it. The boy at the door and the note in Elias's hand. The drawings — I showed her, because some evidence argues better than testimony. The map. The bridge. The face she recognized instantly, because everyone who'd served under him recognized it, and I watched her face change when she understood whose son had been sleeping in our ward eating our porridge while his father's name was posted on the bulletin board beside the word *traitor*.

Then the ledger. I lifted the floorboard and handed it to her and let her read, and I won't pretend my hands were steady. It is one thing to write down dead men's names alone at night. It is another to watch a rule-keeper hold the whole account of your twelve years of small treasons in her lap.

She read for a long time. The lamp guttered once and neither of us moved to fix it.

"The supply manifests," she said finally. Her finger rested on a page. "You cross-referenced the manifests. Where did you even—" She stopped. "Of course. You stole my filing key. Months ago. I thought I'd mislaid it."

"You did mislay it. Repeatedly. I merely provided continuity."

"Hazel." But her mouth twitched despite itself, and then the seriousness came back down like a shutter. "This pattern. The friendly fire reports. You believe officers did this. Sold positions."

"I believe Elias found it and they buried him under the word *traitor* to keep him from saying it aloud. I believe his son saw something. And I believe someone at division knows the boy is alive, because Finn Calloway is in a cell right now on a theft charge so flimsy it wouldn't hold soup."

"And the message."

I gave it to her. The cipher sheet, the four words, the terrible fifth line. She read it twice and I saw her do the same arithmetic I'd done — trap, ghost, no second asking — and arrive, being Pryce, one step further.

"It says mill bridge," she said slowly. "It doesn't say whose side holds the mill bridge."

"It says *before the armistice*. Whenever that comes. Days, they're saying now."

"And if it's a trap, they don't need you, Hazel. They need the book and the boy. You're packaging."

"Yes," I said. "I'd worked that out. Thank you."

She stood up. She paced the little room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a habit from a lifetime of small rooms, and I knew better than to speak while she worked. When Imogen Pryce is deciding something, interruption costs you a week.

"You remember Corben Hollow," she said at last, not looking at me.

"I remember everything about Corben Hollow."

"So do I. I was on triage. You were under the canvas with the abdominal case for nine hours. And when Major Ellison told you to move on and leave him, you refused, and he threatened you with a report, and I backed your refusal, and the man lived. Do you know why I backed you?" She turned. "Not because the man deserved to live, though he did. Because you looked at Ellison and I realized you were the sort of person who would go down refusing, and I decided that night that the army could have my obedience but you could have my loyalty, and I have never once regretted the allocation."

"Pryce—"

"Let me finish, I'm being eloquent and it won't keep." She took a breath. "If you take that boy to that bridge and it is a trap, they will kill you and erase you, and I will spend the rest of my life keeping a ledger of my own, with one name in it, and I will hate every page of my life thereafter. If you stay, and Ashworth comes for the boy, we hide him, badly, briefly, and then they take him and probably you anyway, because you will do something stupid and noble with a bedpan. Those are the choices as I count them. Both of them are appalling. Is my arithmetic correct?"

"It's correct," I said, "except for the bedpan, which undersells me."

"Hazel."

"Elias made me a promise once," I said. "Twelve years ago. That if either of us ever truly needed the other, no rank would stand in the way, and no flag either. We made it in peacetime, which is the only time promises can be made honestly, because wartime promises are just fear wearing nice clothes. He has called it in. He's called it in with four words and a dying man's handwriting, and he asked for the boy, not for me — did you notice that? *Bring the boy*, it says. He didn't even ask me to come. Which means he thinks I might die doing it, and he'd rather the boy lived than the promise." My voice cracked somewhere in there and I let it. "I am going to that bridge, Imogen, because I promised. Everything else — the ledger, Ashworth, the truth of it — that's duty, and I've carried duty so long I've forgotten its weight. But this is a promise, and promises are lighter and stronger, and I intend to find out which."

Pryce looked at me for a long moment across the lamplight and the open kit bag and eleven unregretted years.

Then she swore — fluently, comprehensively, in a manner that would have gotten her fined two days' pay in any properly run mess — went to the door, opened it, and came back carrying her own kit bag.

"Absolutely not," I said.

"Correct. It's mine, and I'm taking it, and you can't stop me, because if you try I'll report the ambulance missing and you'll be arrested at the gate." She began pulling my carefully packed bag apart and repacking it with the ruthless efficiency of a woman who had quartermastered other people's lives for a decade. "Morphine goes here. You packed it where it would get wet. Chocolate is confiscated as a command decision. Blue shawl, not grey."

"You said yourself I'm packaging. You're making yourself packaging too."

"Yes," she said, and zipped the bag, and set it down between us like a verdict. "That's what loyalty is, Hazel. It isn't agreeing. God knows I think this is madness — I think it's beautiful, doomed madness, and I would argue until dawn except I heard myself just now, telling you about Corben Hollow, and I realized the argument ended eleven years ago and I simply hadn't noticed. So. We leave at three, before the change of watch. I drive the first stretch; you look after the boy. And if it is a trap at that bridge, then at least they'll have to file a report about both of us, and I have it on excellent authority that reports around here tend to go missing."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, but she took it as she takes everything — as filed, stamped, and entered into the record.

"One condition," she said, holding up a finger. "When this is over — whichever way — you tell me everything else. Every name in that book. Every night you spent under the floorboard. I want the whole of it, because I have discovered I would rather share your secrets than protect you from them, and I am apparently becoming a criminal in my middle age and deserve to know the full scope of my crimes."

"You'll have it," I said. "Every name."

At five minutes to three, I lifted Tomas from his cot, still mostly asleep, and wrapped him in the blanket that smelled of carbolic and lavender because Sister Anne had been slipping lavender into the wash since the bombardment and denied it under direct questioning. I carried him down the dark corridor past Ward C, past the room where Finn's cot stood empty with his blanket folded square — Pryce had straightened it yesterday, and neither of us had commented, because some griefs are maintained rather than spoken.

The yard was fog and frost. The ambulance started on the second try, coughed, caught.

And then, as Pryce eased us through the gate with the headlights hooded, I looked back once at the hospital — the dark wards, the thin line of the stove smoke, the floorboard under which twelve years of erased men slept in ink — and saw, in the window of the linen room, a lamp move deliberately twice. Side to side. Side to side.

Sister Anne, pretending to inventory bandages, wishing us Godspeed in the only signal a hospital ever sends.

The road ahead ran black toward the river, toward the mill, toward four words and whatever waited at the end of them — and above the eastern hills, faint as a rumor, came the sound of the guns opening up again, which meant somewhere the armistice was still only a story, and we had, perhaps, just enough night left to reach the bridge before the world finished deciding anything.
