## 10. The Quarrel

I have served in three campaigns, two typhus outbreaks, and one regimental Christmas dinner, and I can report with authority that the worst of them was the Christmas dinner. But the quarrel with Hazel Blackwood on the fourth of November runs a close second, and unlike the dinner, it left marks.

It began, as most catastrophes do, with laundry.

I was folding bandages in the supply tent because folding is what I do — bandages, bedding, other people's moral failures into neat, manageable squares. The war had taught me that chaos is not defeated by heroism. Chaos is defeated by inventory lists kept in a steady hand. I had believed this for twenty years, and I still believe it now, which is precisely why what happened later that night makes no sense to me at all and perfect sense simultaneously. War does that to a person. It holds two contradictory truths in your chest like a pair of lungs, and you breathe with both whether you want to or not.

Hazel came in just past dusk, smelling of carbolic and river water, and I knew before she said a word. Twenty years of friendship teaches you the weather of a person's face. Hers had gone storm-dark and very still, which in Hazel means she has decided something enormous and has not yet told anyone, possibly including herself.

"You've heard," she said.

"Heard what?"

"Finn Calloway."

I set down the bandage I was folding. Folded it again. Set it down once more, because my hands needed something stupid to do while my mind caught up.

"They took him this morning," I said. "Theft. Two blankets and a tin of morphine, according to the charge sheet." I had seen the paperwork myself; it had crossed my desk because everything crossed my desk eventually, that being the entire architecture of my personality. "Finn Calloway wouldn't steal a breath if it were offered freely. He's the only man in this hospital who returns string."

"Yes," Hazel said. "Well. Someone wanted him out of the way, and they used the paperwork to do it."

And there it was, laid between us like an unexploded shell: *the paperwork.* Because we both knew — had known since the night the boy appeared at our door with his wound and his silence and his terrible little drawings — that paperwork was no longer a neutral country. Somewhere behind the lines, someone was writing lies in a tidy hand, and I had spent the whole day pretending not to recognize the handwriting of my own profession.

"Hazel," I said carefully, "what did Finn have when they arrested him?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Hazel Blackwood has the flattest honest stare in the entire Army Medical Corps, and I have watched her use it on colonels.

"You know what he had," she said. "A drawing. One of Tomas's drawings. And he had it because I asked Finn to keep an eye on the boy, and Finn, being Finn, kept too good an eye."

"Where are the drawings now?"

"Why do you ask?"

"Because I am trying to establish, for my own peace of mind, exactly how much trouble we are in, and whether it is the sort that ends with a reprimand or the sort that ends with a firing squad."

Something moved behind her eyes then — not guilt exactly. Guilt would have been easier. It was calculation, and calculation in Hazel is always worse than tears.

"The drawings are safe," she said.

"*Safe,*" I repeated. "Hazel. Safe where? Safe how? Safe under whose floorboard?"

"You know about the floorboard."

"I have known about the floorboard for eleven years, yes. I have chosen not to know about it out loud, which is a different thing entirely, and I would thank you not to conflate them." I heard my own voice rising and made an effort to press it flat, the way one presses a wound. "Twelve names, Hazel. Or thirty. Or however many you've got written down in that book like some kind of — parish register of the erased. I have looked away from it for over a decade because looking away was survivable for both of us. That was the arrangement. My silence bought yours, and together they bought us a hospital where wounded men get fed."

"That's not an arrangement, Immy. That's just cowardice with better manners."

The word landed harder than she intended it to, I think — I could see her regret it even as it left her mouth. But regret does not recall a shell, and neither does apology unfire a gun, as the war has demonstrated at considerable length.

"Cowardice," I said. "Say that again."

"No," she said, and lifted her chin. "No, I won't, because I meant it. You fold your hands and keep your ledgers straight and you tell yourself that's virtue, Imogen, but somewhere right now a man is signing orders that make dead privates vanish off the rolls, and the same pen is decorating the officers who buried them. Silence is how the guilty stay decorated. Every quiet clerk in this army is mortar in their wall."

"And every loud nurse in this army is a body under it," I snapped back. "You want to be a martyr, fine, that's your affair, martyrs are cheap this year. But you are not standing alone on your principle, are you? You're standing in a hospital with forty patients, nine orderlies, six nurses, and a child who cannot say his own name. When Ashworth comes back — and he will come back, Hazel, he lingered at your ward like a fly on meat — what do you imagine happens to all of them? To Sister Wren, who's nineteen? To old Marta in the kitchen? Do their names go in your book too, afterward? Will you write them down nicely so somebody remembers them?"

I should not have said the last part. Even as I said it I knew it was cruel, because it struck at the exact place where Hazel keeps whatever she has instead of a heart. She went white. Not flushed — white, the color of a field hospital sheet, the color of everything in my life.

"At least," she said quietly, "they'd be remembered."

"Oh, don't dress it up as remembrance. You'd be burying them, not remembering them. There's a difference and you know it. A name in a book doesn't warm anyone. It doesn't feed anyone. It's a stone, Hazel, and you're carrying a whole graveyard around under a loose floorboard, and now you've put Finn in a cell because of it, and you're going to stand there and tell me the arithmetic works?"

"The arithmetic works," she said, "if you count everyone. Which is rather the point of the book."

We stood there in the supply tent among the neat folded bandages, two women who had shared a tent, a war, a decade of small conspiracies against hunger and cold, and I understood suddenly that we were not arguing about a ledger at all. We were arguing about what loyalty is. I thought loyalty was keeping people alive. She thought loyalty was keeping faith. And neither of us knew how to be loyal to both at once, and the space between those two ideas was exactly wide enough for a friendship to fall through.

"Tomas goes," she said finally. "Soon. Within days. There's a message coming — I won't tell you more than that, so you can honestly say you don't know."

"Don't do this."

"So you can honestly say you don't know," she repeated, gentler now, which was worse. "Immy. Please. I'm trying to protect you."

"You're trying to protect me," I said, "by making me complicit through ignorance. That's not protection, that's paperwork. You're filing me under *didn't know* and hoping nobody checks the file."

"It's worked for twelve years."

"It has worked for twelve years because for twelve years the stakes were names in a book. This time the stake is a living boy with a hole in his shoulder and eyes like his father's, and if you think Ashworth's people will stop at Finn—"

I stopped. Too late. Her head came up like a hound scenting.

"Eyes like whose?"

"I didn't say anything."

"You said 'his father.' Whose father, Imogen?"

"There is a war on," I said, with tremendous dignity. "Everyone has a father. Statistically speaking."

"Hazel Blackwood," I might have added, *you are a liar and so am I*, but what I actually did was pick up my kit bag, because I could feel the argument sliding past words into the place where words break, and I am a great believer in leaving a room before it collapses on you.

"Where are you going?" she demanded.

"On duty," I said. "Which is more than I can say for you."

And I walked out. That is the sentence that has replayed in my head for days: *and I walked out.* Not stormed, exactly — I don't storm, storms are inefficient — but walked, with my spine straight and my chin level and my heart going like a drummer boy's, out of the supply tent, across the duckboards, past the ward tents glowing amber with lamplight, past the boy himself, who sat on a crate by the kitchen entrance watching me pass with those grey, grave, unmistakable eyes. He did not wave. Children always wave. He did not wave, and somehow that frightened me more than anything Hazel had said.

Because I knew. Of course I knew. I had known since the morning the official notice came through — *Captain Elias Vane, declared enemy collaborator, all correspondence forbidden* — and I had watched Hazel's face across the mess table do something it had never done in twelve years of horrors: crack. Just for a second. Just a hairline fracture, quickly mortared over with tea and routine. Rule-keepers notice things. It is our whole vocation. And I had filed that fracture away in the drawer marked *not mine to open*, and I had kept it shut with both hands ever since, and tonight the drawer had burst open and spilled its contents all over the supply tent floor.

Elias Vane pulled her out of a burning aid station in '14. Everyone knows the story, or the shape of it. What fewer people know — what almost nobody knows — is the promise they made after, in some hospital garden I never saw, that rank and flag would never stand between them if either ever called. I thought it was sentiment. People make promises in gardens all the time; the war eats them by the thousand along with the people who made them.

But promises, it turns out, do not die. They wait. They lie in drawers and under floorboards, patient as seeds, and when they come due they come due all at once, with interest.

* * *

I did not go on duty. I went and sat in the officers' latrine for twenty minutes, which is the traditional refuge of the emotionally compromised non-commissioned officer, and I conducted an argument with myself that was substantially identical to the argument I had just lost, except that I played both parts and lost anyway.

Because here is the thing. Everything I said to her was true. The ledger is a bomb. The boy is a lit fuse. Colonel Ashworth is not a fool — fools do not get to be colonels with his particular talent for asking questions that sound like courtesy. If he finds the child, he finds the ledger, and if he finds the ledger he finds Hazel, and around Hazel he will gather everyone she has ever folded a bandage beside. Sister Wren. Marta. Me. The arithmetic does not work. I ran it four times in that latrine, on the back of a laundry requisition, and it did not work any of the four times.

And yet.

Yet Finn Calloway sits in a cell tonight for returning string. Yet somewhere a pen decorated the men who sold their own soldiers' positions to the enemy artillery, and those men sleep warm, and the privates they sold sleep nowhere at all, unwritten, uncounted, unwept except in a stolen book under a floorboard. Yet a promise made in a garden twelve years ago has come due, and Hazel Blackwood — maddening, stubborn, saintly, insufferable Hazel Blackwood — is going to pay it if it kills her, because paying it is the only kind of arithmetic she has ever been able to do.

Silence is how the guilty stay decorated.

God damn her. She was right. She was right in the way that costs everything, which is the only way anyone is ever right about anything that matters.

I went back through the camp in the dark. The bombardment had started again far to the east — a low, polite grumble, like aristocrats applauding — and the flare light turned the mud silver and the wire black. I passed our quarters, the little hut I shared with Hazel and Sister Wren, and I saw Hazel's shadow moving inside, quick and purposeful. Packing. She hadn't lied about that. Within days, she'd said. Probably within hours, knowing her; Hazel packs like a woman fleeing a fire even when she's only going to the stores.

I stood outside my own quarters in the freezing dark like a burglar casing my own house, and I asked myself one final question, the question I suspect I had been walking toward all evening: *when they hang her, Imogen, do you want to be able to say you obeyed regulations?*

I did not want that. I discovered, with something like relief and something like terror, that I did not want that at all. Twenty years of rules, and underneath them all, it turned out, there had been exactly one rule the whole time: *not her.*

So I did what I do. I kept records. I went to the transport shed, where the ambulance keys hang on their board in a tidy row, each tagged in a careful hand, because chaos is defeated by inventory lists kept in a steady hand. I signed out ambulance number four for morning maintenance — fuel line, plausible, checkable, true enough. I carried the keys across the camp in my closed fist like a coal I was afraid would go out.

Hazel's cot was by the window. Her kit lay half-packed on it, shirts and bandages and — I noticed, and pretended I didn't — a child's coat, far too big for him, lined with blanket wool. Preparing him for cold roads. Preparing him for a long way.

I laid the keys on the folded shirt where she would find them, weight and shape unmistakable, and I did not leave a note, because a note is evidence and evidence gets people shot, and also because there is nothing to write. *I disagree with everything you are doing and I hope it destroys you rather than her* — no. Some loyalties must remain officially denied to remain real. She would understand the keys. Keys are a language we have spoken for twenty years: *go, then, I'll mind the door.*

Her shadow paused in its packing. I was already three steps into the dark by the time I heard the cot creak, the small intake of breath, the silence where a thank-you might have gone and where its absence said more than thanks ever could.

I walked back toward the ward tents with my spine straight and my heart hammering, officially ignorant, unofficially damned, and it was there — passing the kitchen, passing the crate where the boy sat — that I saw Hazel step out of our hut with her bag in one hand and Tomas's small hand in the other, heading not for the road but for the transport shed, moving fast.

And it was there that I heard, behind me, from the direction of the colonel's quarters, the whistle of the night orderly, and a voice calling for the duty sergeant, and a lamp flaring awake in Ashworth's window at an hour when decent colonels were long asleep.

Someone had talked. Or someone soon would. And I realized, standing in the silver flare-light between my best friend's escaping back and the enemy's waking window, that I had just chosen my side without saying a word — and that the word would be required of me by morning.
