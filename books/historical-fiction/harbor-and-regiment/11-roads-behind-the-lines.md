## 11. Roads Behind the Lines

There is an art to walking like a refugee, and I learned it in the first hour: you must not walk like someone going somewhere. You must walk like someone who has stopped believing in destinations.

It's harder than it sounds. Every instinct in the body wants to move with purpose — shoulders back, eyes on the road, the brisk stride of a woman with a task. Purpose gets you shot at checkpoints. Purpose gets you noticed by patrols who are bored and cold and looking for anything that isn't mud. So I taught myself the slouch, the shuffle, the thousand-yard stare that says *I have been walking for days and I will be walking for days more and please don't make me think about it.*

Tomas needed no instruction. He had been born into the slouch, poor mite, or had grown into it over eight years of being carried through other people's wars. He walked beside me with his small hand hooked into two fingers of my glove — not holding my hand, exactly. Holding my hand would have been trust. This was more like a contractual arrangement, reviewed hourly.

We went out through the laundry line at the back of the hospital, past the graves marked with sticks instead of stones because even wood was rationed, and into the country that the war had been chewing on for four years without swallowing.

* * *

The countryside behind the lines is a strange country. It belongs to no one and everyone and mostly to the crows. Orchards stand stripped of their branches, which went to duckboards and stretcher poles, so the trees look like men who've given up their coats in winter. Farmhouses wear their roofs half-off like hats knocked askew in a brawl. A church spire leans at an angle that would have gotten any architect court-martialed.

We moved by night, as agreed, and hid by day in whatever the shelling had left standing. The first day we sheltered in the cellar of a farmhouse whose upper floor had been removed, apparently by God's own boot heel. The second day, a culvert under the road, where the water ran black and smelled of iron. The third day we found a barn with two cows still in it, unbothered, chewing their cud through artillery like elderly ladies through a scandal. I decided then and there that if the world ended entirely, I wanted to come back as one of those cows.

I had planned our cover story on the first night, rehearsing it against the dark like lines for a play I hoped would close before opening. Refugees from the eastern villages. Mother and son. Husband dead at — I picked a battle at random, then hated myself for picking one where I'd actually held men's hands while they died, and picked another. Name: Mrs. Blackwood. It wasn't even a lie; I'd simply never bothered to marry anyone, and the name was mine regardless. The boy: Tomas. That much was true, and true things travel lightest.

He didn't correct me when I called us mother and son. He didn't confirm it either. He just looked at me with those grave brown eyes — Vane's eyes, unmistakably, the same ones that had looked at me across a burning aid station six years ago and asked, in effect, *are you going to help or stand there being shocked?* — and let the silence do its work.

Three nights of silence, that is. Three nights of me talking enough for both of us, which anyone who knows me will tell you is a considerable output. I told him about the cows. I told him about the stars, which were extravagant out here away from the lamps, thick as spilled salt. I told him about a hospital cat named Corporal Biscuit who once stole a colonel's lunch and was promoted for it, informally, by unanimous vote of the ward. He didn't smile. But his grip on my two fingers tightened a fraction at the word *cat*, and I logged it the way I log everything: filed, dated, kept.

* * *

On the fourth night the rain came, a fine cold needle-rain that made the mud grab at our boots like it was owed money. We took shelter in the shell of a miller's cottage — not the mill itself, mind; the mill was our destination and I gave it a wide berth even in the dark, circling two miles out of our way rather than walk past it twice. If Ashworth's men were watching it already, I didn't intend to teach them my face.

The cottage had one wall left and half a roof, and a hearth big enough to hold a small fire if we burned green wood and wet peat, which smokes terribly but smokes low. I risked it anyway. Cold kills more quietly than bullets but just as dead, and I've buried too many men to let a child die of damp on my watch.

Tomas sat close to the fire, knees up, chin on his knees, watching the flames with the concentration of a man defusing something. And I sat beside him, and the rain ticked on the broken roof tiles, and I thought about how strange it was that I had crossed half a province with a child whose voice I had never heard.

It was he who broke it. Of course it was. Children always break silences at the moment least convenient, which is, I suppose, why adults build them so carefully.

"He said you'd come."

I turned. He was still watching the fire, not me, as if the words belonged to the flames and he was merely lending them passage.

"Who said?" I asked, though I knew. My heart knew before my head did; it always has been quicker that way, my heart, and considerably less reliable.

"The captain." He said it carefully, the way children say titles they've been coached to use. "My father. He said, if ever a woman comes — a nurse, with ink on her fingers — you go with her. Even if she doesn't say his name. Especially if she doesn't say his name."

Ink on my fingers. I looked down at them. Six years of charting pulses and dosing morphine and writing names nobody else would write, and the stain had gone so deep into the whorls of my thumbs that I'd stopped seeing it. Vane had seen it. Vane had known that I would come before I knew it myself, and had armed his son with the knowing.

"Tomas," I said, "can you tell me what happened? To your father?"

He was quiet a long time. The rain ticked. Somewhere out in the dark a roof tile surrendered and slid off into the mud with a soft ceramic sigh.

"He woke me up at night," Tomas said at last, in the flat reciting voice of a child delivering testimony he has rehearsed until it has worn smooth as a river stone. "More than once. He said, listen now, and remember, because remembering is a job and you are old enough to work." His brow furrowed. "He wrote names on paper and burned the paper after. But first he made me say them back."

"Names," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "What kind of names?"

"Soldiers' names." He counted on his fingers, an unconscious gesture that undid me more than tears would have. "Private Aldous Merrick. Corporal Fenwick. Lieutenant Sorrel. Sergeant—" he stumbled, frowned, tried again, got it, and went on. There were eleven. Eleven names, delivered in order, in a child's clear voice, into a ruined hearth in a dead village, while the rain came down on the whole rotten war.

Eleven names. I had seventeen in my ledger that matched the pattern — men struck from reports, men written off as misadventure, men whose deaths had decorated other men's careers. And here was a boy of eight carrying eleven more, memorized the way other children memorize psalms, because his father understood that paper burns and boys, God willing, do not.

"Do you know why your father had you learn them?" I asked gently.

Tomas considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, as though childhood were a post requiring diligence. "He said names are the only thing that can't be confiscated." A pause. "He said they can shoot the messenger but the message walks on two legs if you raise it right."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — a short, cracked sound, half sob wearing a laugh's coat. Vane. Six years, and he was still exactly himself: turning mortal danger into a lesson plan, treason into homework, and handing his son the one weapon no tribunal could seize.

"You're doing the job well," I told him.

"I know." No false modesty in that household, evidently. Then, quieter, watching the fire: "Are you the one who writes them down? Papa said there was a woman who keeps a book. He said she thinks every man deserves his name back."

The rain, the fire, the small warm weight of him beside me — I felt suddenly that I was being audited by history, and that the auditor was eight years old.

"Yes," I said. "That's me. Though I'm starting to suspect your father and I have been keeping the same books from different ends."

"He said you'd say something like that." And there — there it was, finally, briefly, like sun through shell-smoke: the ghost of a smile. "He talks about you. When he thinks I'm asleep."

* * *

We slept in shifts after that, though I confess I did most of the sleeping-adjacent and none of the actual sleeping. Something had shifted between us, some load-bearing wall quietly removed. He no longer gripped my fingers like a contract. He leaned against my shoulder like a fact.

And I lay awake under the half-roof doing arithmetic that frightened me. Because if Vane had sent his son to me — had spent his last free hours coaching a child in names and routes and the identification of nurses by their ink-stained hands — then he had known how little time he had. Men who expect to survive send letters. Men who expect to be silenced send their sons.

And if Ashworth's circle had hunted Vane hard enough to drive him to that, then they would hunt a boy who could recite eleven names in his sleep. They would hunt the woman carrying him. They might already be hunting Finn Calloway, who had seen the child's face and drawn the wrong conclusions at the wrong time, God keep him.

I had spent twelve years believing that a ledger was armor — that ink, properly applied, was a kind of shield. Lying in the ruins of a stranger's house with a child's breath warm against my arm, I understood for the first time that it was also a target. Everything I had written was a list of people worth killing to keep forgotten, and I had sewn the list into my coat and strapped it over my heart like a love letter.

Well. Perhaps it was one.

* * *

On the fifth night the clouds tore open and the moon came out, full and shameless, and I cursed it roundly because moonlight is a refugee's enemy. We had perhaps three miles to go to the marsh track that would take us around to the mill bridge from the north — the approach Vane's message had specified, *before the armistice is signed, or not at all*, and the rumors drifting back along the refugee roads said the signing was close now, days maybe, hours if the politicians were feeling ambitious.

We walked fast despite the light, hugging the hedgerows, and it was Tomas who stopped first. Children stop first; they haven't yet learned to override their instincts for the sake of appearances.

Ahead, where the road bent down toward the river flats, there were fires. Not cottage fires — nothing so domestic. These were laid in a line, military-fashion, and there were figures moving between them, and a lantern swinging in a rhythm I recognized from a hundred inspections: the bored metronome of sentries walking a post.

A checkpoint. On the north road. Which meant someone had guessed our route, or was guessing at all routes, casting a net wide enough to catch anything moving toward the bridge.

I drew Tomas back into the hedge, down into the black wet bracken, and put my hand over his mouth before he could ask the question. Above us the moon poured down like spilled milk, lighting the fields silver, lighting us — I had no doubt — like a pair of actors caught mid-scene.

The nearest sentry was perhaps two hundred yards off. Young, by his gait. Probably as frightened as I was. Probably with orders to stop anyone traveling toward the mill, and questions to ask, and a rifle to ask them with.

Tomas looked up at me. In the moonlight his face was very pale and very calm, and his lips shaped words without sound, words I couldn't read but somehow understood anyway: *what do we do?*

I thought of the ledger against my ribs. I thought of eleven names in a boy's head. I thought of a promise made in peacetime by two fools who believed peace would hold.

"We walk through," I breathed, and took his hand — properly, this time, no contract about it.
