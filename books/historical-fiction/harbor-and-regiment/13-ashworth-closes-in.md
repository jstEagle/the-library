## 13. Ashworth Closes In

There is a particular pleasure in a well-kept office at the end of a long war, and I have earned mine twice over — once in the field, once in the filing room, and the second earning was by far the more dangerous.

I say that plainly because I have never had much patience with men who lie to themselves. Lying to others is a professional necessity; lying to yourself is a clerk's vice, and whatever else I am, I am not a clerk. The distinction matters. Clerks shuffle paper because they are told to. I shuffle paper because paper is where wars are actually won — not in the mud at Passchendaele or wherever the newspapers are weeping this month, but in the requisition ledgers, the casualty returns, the morning reports that decide which men existed and which did not.

Which men existed. You will forgive me if I smile at the phrase. It has become the whole of my profession these past two years, and I have come to think of it as a kind of authorship. Somewhere behind the lines there is a woman — there is nearly always a woman — who has taken it upon herself to be my unauthorized editor. She writes names back into the margins. She keeps a little book of my deletions, like a schoolmistress marking up my prose.

I did not know her name yet. That was the morning's business.

* * *

The orderly they brought me was called Calloway. Finn Calloway, twenty-three years old, six months at the Third Field Hospital, no disciplinary record worth the ink. He sat in the chair across from my desk the way soldiers sit when they haven't yet decided whether to be frightened — spine straight, hands folded, eyes doing their best to be somewhere else entirely. His left eye was swelling nicely. My sergeant had been thorough during transport. I deplore unnecessary violence; it clouds a man's thinking, usually the wrong man's.

"Mr. Calloway," I said, "thank you for coming."

"I wasn't given a choice, sir."

"No," I agreed pleasantly, "you weren't. That's rather the point of you."

He blinked. Good. A man who blinks is a man who hasn't rehearsed.

"You were arrested for theft. Let us dispense with that fiction immediately — it is a fiction, and I find fictions tiring before lunch. What you were actually doing was copying a drawing made by a child currently residing at your hospital without authorization. Do you follow me so far?"

His jaw worked. "The boy's got nobody, sir. He was left at our door half-dead. Somebody had to—"

"Somebody," I said, "had to obey orders. Orders exist precisely so that compassion doesn't have to make decisions it is unqualified to make. But we'll come back to philosophy, Mr. Calloway, only if you insist on wasting both our mornings. Here is what interests me." I laid three photographs on the desk, face down, like a card trick. "You copied one drawing. Tell me what it depicted."

"A bridge," he said. Too fast, and then, watching my face, he understood he'd answered a question I hadn't asked aloud, and went pale in the way honest men do.

"A bridge," I repeated. "And here is what puzzles me. Children draw bridges, houses, dogs, enormous suns with faces. All very charming. But this child draws the same bridge over and over — and he draws it accurately, with the mill race marked, and the ford downstream, and the shell damage rendered in the correct sequence, which means either the boy is an architectural prodigy or he has studied that bridge with an adult's attention. And underneath, in his father's hand, someone has written coordinates. Would you like to see?"

I turned the photographs over. I confess I enjoyed the small theatricality of it. One must take one's pleasures where the war provides them.

Calloway looked at the drawings for a long moment. Then he did something I had not anticipated, and which I have thought about since with a mixture of irritation and, if I am honest, respect. He looked up at me and said, "You're frightened of him."

"The boy?"

"The child. And his father. You've got a whole office and soldiers and a swollen eye for me, and you're frightened of an eight-year-old's crayon."

There is a version of me — younger, perhaps, or simply more tired — that would have laughed and told him the truth, because the truth was harmless to a man in his position and I do enjoy an audience. Instead I leaned forward and let my voice drop into the register I reserve for chaplains and auditors.

"Mr. Calloway, let me explain your situation, since no one else will. Tomorrow morning you will be charged with theft, and the charge will stick, because I say it sticks, and my signature has a way of becoming fact. You will lose your stripe, such as it is, and serve six months, and you will do it in a place where the food is worse than the hospital's, which I understand is saying something. Or — you tell me who brought the boy to your door, who has been hiding him, and what Sister Blackwood carries in the tin box under her cot, and you walk out of here this afternoon with a transfer to a rear depot where the war will never find you again."

I knew about the tin box, of course. I want that understood. I had known about it for eleven days, ever since a laundress with debts sold me the shape of a secret she hadn't fully understood herself. I did not yet know its contents, but a man who keeps a private census of the dead under his floorboard is not collecting stamps.

Calloway's face did something complicated. Guilt, mostly — guilt is the default expression of decent people in my line of sight — but underneath it, calculation, and underneath that, something harder. Loyalty, God help us. The war was ending and every man jack of them had suddenly discovered principles.

"I don't know about any tin box," he said.

"Of course you don't."

"And Sister Blackwood—" His voice cracked and he steadied it. "She pulled forty men out of a burning ward at Steenwerck. Carried two of them herself. If she's hiding a lost kid, then maybe the lost kid needed hiding, and maybe the question isn't why she hid him but why you want him found."

Silence, then. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a lorry changed gear.

I could have pressed him. I could have had the sergeant return and improve upon his eye. But torture is the recourse of officers who cannot read, and I could read Finn Calloway as easily as a morning report: he would not give her up, and breaking his other eye would gain me nothing except another omission for somebody's ledger — the thought arrived unbidden and I disliked it intensely, that I had begun to think in her terms.

"Take him back," I said to the sergeant. "Separate quarters. No visitors."

As the door closed behind them, I sat down and permitted myself the luxury of speaking aloud to an empty room.

"Frightened," I said. "Of a child's crayon."

It was, of course, precisely true, and Calloway had no idea how precisely.

* * *

Let me tell you about tribunals, since no one tells the truth about them until it is too late.

When the armistice comes — and it is coming, the rumors wash through headquarters daily now, November, December, any week — there will be a peace, and a peace requires paperwork, and the paperwork this time will include commissions of inquiry. I have seen the drafts. I helped draft one of them, which is the sort of irony history enjoys at my expense. There will be men in clean uniforms sitting behind tables in some requisitioned schoolhouse, asking careful questions in careful voices about the summer of '16 and the winter of '17, and they will have access to records, real ones, the deep files, and they will cross-reference.

I know exactly what they will find, because I put it there.

Three engagements. That is all it ever was — three engagements in nineteen months, and each of them a masterpiece of arithmetic. A battalion's position, sold through a Dutch intermediary to the enemy, followed by an "unfortunate friendly fire incident" that conveniently eliminated the junior officers who had grown curious about their orders, the signals men who had handled the traffic, the quartermaster sergeants who had noticed the discrepancies in supply manifests. Dead men file no complaints. And the officers above them — myself among them, though always at one careful remove, my name appearing nowhere near the trigger, only beside the aftermath — received commendations, promotions, and eventually seats at tables where armistice terms are drafted.

Do you begin to see it? When the tribunal asks why Captain So-and-so's company shelled its own flank, the answer in the file is *error*. Errors are survivable. What is not survivable is corroboration. A pattern of omitted names — thirty-one men across three incidents, every one of them erased from the morning reports within days of death, every erasure bearing ultimately upon my authorization — assembled in one place, by one hand, sworn and witnessed, becomes not error but evidence. It becomes rope.

Somewhere in that mud-caked hospital, in a tin box under a floorboard, sits my rope, kept thread by thread by a nurse with a conscience.

People imagine that men like me fear bullets. Bullets are restful; they end things cleanly, and I have arranged for several of them and slept well afterward. No — what men like me fear is being *remembered wrongly*. Being remembered at all. The tribunal will not hang me for killing men; killing men is what the war was for. It will hang me for the bookkeeping, for the sin of editing reality, and the terrible joke is that the weapon against me is nothing but names. Thirty-one names in a woman's handwriting, plus whatever the Vane child carries in his head.

Ah, yes. Vane.

Elias Vane was the mistake I have spent eighteen months trying to correct, and like most mistakes it began as a virtue. He was the finest intelligence officer I ever fielded — I promoted him myself, before I knew what he was capable of noticing. Signals officers notice things; it is their curse. Elias noticed that the "friendly fire" batteries always seemed to have current maps of positions that should have been secret, and being an honest man, he did the honest thing: he started keeping notes. Names again. Dates. The Dutch intermediary's routing. Enough, if it reached a tribunal, to hang my entire circle from the same beam.

We learned of his notes in the spring. We declared him a traitor — which was elegant, I grant myself that; the accusation of treason is wonderfully self-sealing, because no one examines a traitor's evidence, they only hunt the traitor — and we hunted him across four provinces while he sent his son ahead of him like a parcel of proof. Because that is what the boy is. Not a hostage, not bait. An archive. Eight years old and memorized, names and dates and the coordinates of a bridge where his father means to pass the package to whoever can carry it into history.

Forget this face, said the note left with the child. I have read it — a copy of it reached me within hours of the boy's arrival; my reach is longer than people credit. It reads, to a sentimental eye, like a father's plea. To my eye it reads like a courier's instruction: *this package must not be traced.*

* * *

By evening I had the map out, and I will admit that the day's work had a certain satisfaction to it, grim as the occasion was. I am good at this part. Give a man fragments and I will give you back a narrative.

Fragments: a laundress's rumor of a tin box. An orderly caught copying a child's map. Hospital requisition records showing two extra rations drawn nightly for six weeks — a child eats, even a silent one — signed off, curiously, by Sergeant Pryce, whose signature on those forms was irregular enough that I have set it aside for later consideration. And then, this afternoon, the crowning fragment: a motor ambulance missing from the Third Field Hospital pool, logged out at twenty-two hundred hours two nights past and returned, according to the fuel ledger, having traveled sixty-one kilometers further than any legitimate route required.

Sixty-one kilometers. I measured them on the map with a pair of dividers, walking the distance backward from the hospital along the refugee roads, the shattered lanes, the places where a woman pushing a claimed breakdown might wait out daylight. The dividers stopped at a bend in the river twelve kilometers north of Saint-Aubin.

Where there is a mill.

The old grain mill at Ferrières — blown in the retreat two winters ago, its bridge half-collapsed, the ford below it passable on foot at low water and nowhere else for nine kilometers in either direction. A child had drawn it faithfully, over and over, in crayon, with a ford marked downstream. A father in flight, hunted across four provinces, needing to move his son — his evidence — across country he could not trust, would need exactly such a crossing: remote, ruined, memorable. And a message had gone out through deserter channels — my people intercepted the echo of it, too late, a week stale — arranging a rendezvous at a mill bridge before the signing.

They were going tonight. Or tomorrow. The armistice negotiations stall and restart like a lorry with a flooded engine, and everyone's timetable hangs on rumors, but a rendezvous does not wait on parliaments. Which meant my timetable could not either.

I summoned Captain Reyes, who commands the military police detail attached to my office, and I will record here that she is the one person in my orbit whom I trust absolutely, chiefly because I have never been able to determine what she wants, and desireless people cannot be bought. She stood at ease before the map and waited.

"Captain," I said, "the armistice commission will soon require witnesses to appear before it. Certain parties may attempt to deliver testimony through irregular channels, outside proper procedure." I tapped the map. "Proper procedure exists so that testimony can be weighed, verified, contextualized. Unverified testimony, delivered raw to a commission hungry for scandal, is indistinguishable from slander. We must ensure that any persons carrying such testimony are... escorted. Processed. Their documents examined by qualified officers before they reach the commission. Do you understand me?"

"You want them stopped at a chokepoint and held pending review," she said.

"I want no review necessary," I said. "I want everything examined quietly, beforehand, by us."

Her eyes went to the map, to the river, to the broken tooth of the mill. "The bridge at Ferrières."

"The approaches," I corrected. "Both banks. The ford especially — a woman with a child won't attempt the span itself. Two men at the ford, two on each road, and a signals detachment to intercept any courier traffic in the vicinity. And Captain — the orders will read *detain and escort for document verification*. They will not read anything else. I want no enthusiasm. If there is shooting at that bridge, I want it provable that it was not ours."

"Yes, sir." A pause. She is one of the few subordinates who permits herself them. "And if the documents verify? If this woman really is only moving a refugee child?"

"Then she will be commended and released," I said, "and I will be relieved, and none of us will speak of it again."

She looked at me a moment longer than regulation allowed, and I had the uncomfortable sense — rare, in my experience, and unwelcome — of being read rather than reading. Then she saluted and went out to arrange the dispositions, and I stood alone with my map and my lamp and the ticking of the stove.

Here is what I did not say to her, because it is not the sort of thing one says: that I bear Hazel Blackwood — for by midnight I had her name at last, from a supply clerk, from a laundry chit, from six weeks of extra rations — no personal malice whatsoever. She is, by every account, the best nurse in the sector. She carried men out of fires. She has spent twelve years doing a small, mad, decent thing in a world that punished decency, and under other circumstances I should admire her enormously, and I do, in the abstract, the way one admires a fortress one is obliged to besiege.

But she holds thirty-one threads of rope with my name braided through them, and she is carrying them toward a table full of clean uniforms who are waiting to ask careful questions, and I did not survive four years of this war, and twenty years of its peacetime shadow before it, to be hanged by a woman's beautiful handwriting.

History will call it whichever way the survivors write it. I intend to be among the writers.

I turned down the lamp. Outside, the night was coming down cold and clear over the ruined country, good traveling weather, and somewhere north of Saint-Aubin a motor ambulance that had traveled sixty-one kilometers too far was, by my reckoning, already warming its engine.

"Mill bridge," I said to the empty office, and poured myself the last finger of the good brandy, the one I was saving for the signing. "Let us see whose names arrive first."
