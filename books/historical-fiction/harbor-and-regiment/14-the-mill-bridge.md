## 14. The Mill Bridge

The mill had been dead longer than the war. That was the first thing I noticed about it, standing in the blue hour before full dark with Tomas's small hand clamped in mine — that the ruin predated our catastrophe, that men had been finding ways to break things long before we'd made an industry of it.

The wheel was gone. Just the great wet semicircle of the wheel-pit remained, a black mouth in the millstone foundation, and the race that had once fed it ran shallow and silver with frost. The roof had half collapsed into the upper floor, so that beams jutted at angles like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down here to die and changed its mind halfway through.

"Bridge," Tomas said.

It was the most he'd said in two days. He pointed past the mill to where the river narrowed, and there it was: the mill bridge, or what the artillery had left of it. One arch still stood, stubborn as a mule, spanning the water on legs of dark stone. The rest was rubble tumbled into the current, which moved beneath the ice-fringed banks with a sound like someone whispering continuously and never quite finishing a sentence.

"That's where he'll come," I said. "If he comes."

Tomas looked up at me with his father's eyes, which seemed unfair of him. A child ought to have his own eyes, unburdened. But there they were — grey, watchful, older than eight years had any right to make them.

"He'll come," Tomas said. Not a question. Not even faith, exactly. Arithmetic. His father had sent word; therefore his father would come. I envied him that clean arithmetic. My own sums always came out with a remainder of dread.

We went inside through what remained of the loading door, climbing over fallen timber, and found a corner behind the base of the old grindstone mount where the wind couldn't reach us. I spread the blanket — Pryce's blanket, though she'd have denied lending it with her dying breath — and sat Tomas against the wall, and dug the bread and the heel of hard cheese from my pack.

"Eat slowly," I said. "Small bites. It has to last."

He ate the way he did everything now: carefully, completely, without waste. Somewhere between the hospital and here he had stopped being a frightened child and become a small, competent soldier of survival, and I didn't know whether to grieve that or be grateful for it. Probably both. Grief and gratitude had been sharing a bed in me for so long they'd worn grooves in the mattress.

I checked the road through a gap in the wall. Empty. The countryside lay under its skin of frost, all the hedgerows and broken fences furred white, the distant hills the colour of dishwater. Somewhere out there were Ashworth's men, if Finn's warning had been true — and Finn had never yet lied to me, only to officers, which is a different moral category entirely.

Somewhere out there, too, was Elias Vane. Alive. The deserter's message had said *alive*, and I had spent three nights turning that single word over like a coin I suspected of being counterfeit.

*The mill bridge. Before the armistice is signed, or not at all.*

Not at all. As if there were a version of events in which I would carry this child across half a frozen country and then simply go home. As if the promise were a subscription one could cancel.

*

The cold came down with the dark like a lid closing.

I had wintered in worse places — there had been a dressing station in the second year built into a flooded quarry where we operated by lamplight with our boots in six inches of water — but I had been younger then, and stupid in the renewable way of the young. Now every shiver felt like a withdrawal from an account I couldn't refill. I wrapped myself around Tomas the way you bank a fire for the night, and we watched the moon come up over the broken roof, thin as a paring knife.

He drew in the dark. He couldn't see the page and didn't care; his pencil moved by feel, and I held him against my chest and felt the small mechanical tremor of it travel through us both. When he finished, he didn't offer it to me. He folded it and put it inside his coat, against his heart, where he kept the others.

"What did you draw?" I asked.

"The bridge," he said. "So it doesn't forget itself."

I laughed before I could stop myself — a short, fogged breath of a laugh — and he tilted his head back to look at me, puzzled, not offended. Children are the last honest audience. They don't laugh politely at things that aren't funny, and they aren't embarrassed when you laugh at things that are.

"All right," I said. "So it doesn't forget itself."

That was the whole religion of it, wasn't it? That was my ledger under the floorboard, twelve years of names written in a hand I tried to keep steady because a shaking hand makes shaky testimony. So it doesn't forget itself. Somebody had to keep the accounts, since the official accountants were busy cooking the books.

Tomas slept eventually, in the way children do — all at once, mid-breath, as if sleep were a door he simply walked through without knocking. I stayed awake and listened to the river and counted the sounds of the night and sorted them: owl, wind, ice settling, wind, wind, and then —

— then a sound that was none of those. A footfall, out on the road, placed with care. Then another. Someone walking the way soldiers walk when they're trying not to be soldiers: heel to toe, weight rolled forward, each step an apology.

My heart did something undignified. I got my hand on the little pistol in my coat pocket — Pryce had pressed it on me at the last, along with the blanket and a look I was not permitted to interpret — and I woke Tomas with two fingers pressed gently to his shoulder, and put my other hand over his mouth before his eyes were fully open, and shook my head.

He understood. God help us both, he understood instantly, and lay silent as a stone while the footsteps came off the road, crossed frozen mud, and stopped.

A voice, low, pitched to carry no further than it had to. It spoke a few words in a language I didn't know, and then, in English, roughened almost past recognition:

"Hazel."

Twelve years, and one word. I had imagined this moment perhaps four hundred times, and in none of those imaginings had I managed anything dignified. Reality kept the standard. I came up out of our corner too fast, caught my hip on the grindstone mount, swore under my breath, and stumbled out into the moonlight with my hand still on the pistol and tears already freezing on my face, and there he was.

Gaunt. That was the word and it was insufficient. Elias Vane had always been a big man who carried himself smaller, economical, a man who took up exactly as much room as necessary and no more. What stood at the edge of the moonlight now had been pared down to essentials — cheekbones like architecture, a beard gone half grey, a coat that hung on him the way coats hang on scarecrows. There was a healing cut above one eyebrow. His hands, when he raised them slightly, as if to show me he was unarmed, were cracked and filthy and trembling very slightly, and I thought: those hands pulled me out of a fire, and they're trembling, and I would know them anywhere, and I do.

"You look terrible," I said. Because of course that's what I said. Twelve years, one word, and I led with an insult.

"You look," he said, and stopped, and something moved through his face like weather, "exactly the same."

"Liar. I've aged forty years and grown a permanent stoop." I crossed the distance between us — carefully, because part of me still didn't believe he was solid and I was afraid my hand would pass through him — and touched his sleeve. Wool. Frozen stiff. Real. "You're supposed to be a traitor. Traitors eat better, I'd assumed."

"I'm a very bad traitor." And then, over my shoulder, quietly: "Tomas."

The boy came out of the shadow of the doorway at a walk that broke into a run halfway, and Vane went down on his knees in the frozen mud and took his son's impact like a man taking a wave, arms around him, face buried in the boy's neck, and made a sound I will not describe because it isn't mine to give away. I looked at the sky and the broken roof and the moon and studied all of them intently, because some griefs and some joys are private even when performed in front of witnesses, and this man had paid enough in privacy.

When I looked back, Tomas was holding his father's face in both hands, examining it, auditing it for damage. Whatever report he compiled, he kept to himself. He simply nodded once, gravely, and tucked himself under Vane's arm, and the three of us stood there in the ruins like the world's smallest and most illegal family reunion.

*

We got inside, out of the wind. Vane moved badly — I saw it in the way he lowered himself against the wall, favouring his left side, and when I raised an eyebrow he said, "Bruised ribs. Old news," in the tone of a man who has said it to himself many times and stopped believing it around the third repetition.

"How long since you've eaten?" I asked.

"Hazel—"

"How long?"

"A day. Two." He accepted the bread and cheese I pushed into his hands and ate, at first, with restraint, and then with less, and I turned away to give him the dignity of pretending not to notice.

"Tomas told me you'd come," I said.

"Tomas is a good judge of character."

"He gets it from his mother."

Something flickered across his face — quick, complicated, gone. I hadn't meant it as a landmine. I hadn't meant anything except the truth, which is that I knew almost nothing about the woman, only that she had died in the third year of the war and that Vane had gone quiet for a season afterward in the way that men of his generation went quiet, which is to say entirely, and then resumed.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I didn't mean—"

"You meant exactly right." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "She's why we're here, partly. Why the boy matters. Hazel, listen to me." He leaned forward, and the moonlight through the broken roof fell across him in bars, and for a moment he looked like a man behind cell bars, which I suppose was accurate in every way but the material one. "How much do you know? Truly. Not what you've guessed. What you know."

"I know your handwriting," I said. "On the note pinned to your son. *Forget this face.* I know you wouldn't write that unless forgetting was the only protection left. I know that Finn was arrested for theft he didn't commit within a week of the boy arriving, which means someone knew he was there, which means someone was watching, which means—" I took a breath. "I know I cross-referenced my ledger against the supply manifests last month, and I found eleven men, Elias. Eleven. All listed as killed by friendly fire, all serving under officers who have since been promoted, decorated, or both. And I know Colonel Ashworth inspected my ward and looked at me the way a man looks at a lock he intends to open."

Vane closed his eyes. When he opened them, the exhaustion in them had rearranged itself into something harder.

"Eleven," he said. "You found eleven. I found nineteen."

The number landed in the silence like a stone down a well. I waited for the splash.

"Nineteen," I repeated.

"Over four years. Different sectors, different units, but the same signature underneath. Positions sold. Not to the enemy — that's the elegant part, the part that took me a year to understand. Sold to *contractors*. Supply syndicates with officers on both sides of the line on their payroll. A battalion gets moved to a position it can't hold. The enemy is told where, lightly, through intermediaries, so the attack comes and succeeds and everyone's casualties are attributed to enemy competence. Meanwhile the same syndicate sells the defenders short ammunition, short wire, short artillery support — and collects on both ends. Men die of arithmetic, Hazel. Nobody fires a shot at them directly. There's no traitor in the photograph. There's just a ledger entry that says *ammunition expended*."

"And the friendly fire reports?"

"Are how they bury the anomalies. A man who dies of enemy action in a position he should never have been assigned raises questions. A man who dies to his own side's misdirected barrage raises paperwork, condolences, and a decoration for whoever writes the apology fastest." He smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all night, worse than the gauntness. "Ashworth signs those apologies. Did you know that? Every one of them. His signature is on nineteen apologies for deaths he arranged. He thinks of it as tidiness."

Tomas had gone very still against his father's side. I realized he was listening, and understanding more than any child should, and that neither Vane nor I had the luxury of sending him away. There was nowhere to send him. There was only the truth, and the dark, and the freezing mill.

"Why does he want Tomas?" I asked. "Why does a colonel hunt a child?"

"Because of what I made the boy memorize." Vane's hand came to rest on the back of Tomas's head, an unconscious gesture, protective, ancient. "Before we were separated. Names, dates, unit numbers. I wrote nothing down after I understood what was happening — paper gets found, paper gets people killed, I'm living evidence of that. But a child's memory..." He looked down. "They searched me twice. They never once thought to search an eight-year-old. Tomas carries nineteen names in his head, Hazel. He *is* the ledger. Ashworth knows it. That's why the note said forget this face — because a forgotten child is safe, and a remembered one is a loose end, and Ashworth is a man who ties off loose ends personally."

"And the armistice?"

"Signing's stalled. You'll have heard. It's not diplomacy that's stalling it." His voice dropped. "It's Ashworth's faction buying time. Because the moment the guns stop, the tribunals start, and the tribunals will want records. Manifests. Apologies. Signatures. Everything I know, everything you've written down, everything this child remembers — it stops being paranoia and becomes evidence the day the ink dries on that treaty. They have until the signing to erase us. After that, erasing us becomes the crime itself."

I sat with that. Outside, the river whispered its unfinished sentence, and somewhere far off a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"So we run," I said. "Tonight. Across the lines, north, to the commission—"

"No." He said it gently, which made it worse. "The roads are watched. Ashworth had the bridge marked two days ago — I watched his scouts do it from the ridge. We don't run, Hazel. We wait until dawn, take the marsh path south where their cordon thins, and I get Tomas to the neutral delegation at Verrenhof. They'll hold him. They'll hold his testimony. Once a neutral party has it, killing him stops being convenient and starts being a scandal, and Ashworth fears scandal more than shells."

"And you?"

"I get him as far as the marshes." He said it flatly, factually, the way you read a casualty list. "After that, we'll see."

"We'll see," I repeated. "Elias. Look at me. Twelve years ago you pulled me out of a burning aid station and I made you a promise, and you made me one back, and I have kept mine in a floorboard for twelve years. If you think I've walked across a country with your son in my coat to watch you say *we'll see*—"

"Hazel."

"—then you don't know me at all, and furthermore you're a hypocrite, because the entire point of everything either of us has ever done is that promises don't expire—"

"*Hazel.*" He caught my wrist. His grip was still strong; whatever else the war had pared away, it had left the bones of him. "I know exactly who you are. That's why you're here and not home. That's why the boy is alive." He paused, and the hardness in his face gave way to something older and more tired. "The promise holds. I'm not releasing you from it. I'm asking you to spend it wisely. There's a difference between courage and expenditure, and I have watched too many good people confuse the two."

I wanted to argue. I had an argument loaded, chambered, safety off. But Tomas had fallen asleep again against his father's ribs — the boy's body apparently operating on the principle that a father's heartbeat was sufficient security clearance for unconsciousness — and looking at the two of them, the ruined man and the sleeping child, I understood that Vane had already done the arithmetic I refused to do, and that his answer had come out with himself as the remainder.

"When did you last sleep?" I asked instead.

"Define sleep."

"Unconsciousness lasting more than ninety minutes, unaccompanied by fear."

"Then I couldn't say. Some months."

"Then sleep now. I'll watch. I've had practice keeping watch over things people want erased."

He almost smiled. "You haven't changed at all."

"I've changed enormously. I've simply changed in directions visible only from close range." I settled back against the wall, pistol in my lap, and looked out at the moonlit gap where the door had been. "Sleep, Captain. Dawn's four hours off, and the marshes won't shorten themselves."

He slept. It took him perhaps three minutes, which told me more about the state of him than the gauntness had. Tomas breathed against his chest. The river went on whispering.

And I sat watch in the ruins of a mill older than the war, holding a gun and a grudge and a ledger's worth of names, and I thought about how relief feels exactly like dread wearing a kinder coat — because he was alive, he was here, the promise held, and none of it, none of it, was going to be enough.

I didn't know then how right I was. I only knew that when the eastern sky finally began to grey, and Vane's eyes opened at the first change of light like a man who slept with one ear on the world, and he rose silently and looked out at the marsh path and said, "We move now, before their watch changes" — I only knew that the cold in my chest had nothing to do with the weather.

We stepped out onto the frost. Behind us, the bridge stood its one remaining arch over the water, remembering itself.

Ahead, in the reeds, something moved that was not a bird.
