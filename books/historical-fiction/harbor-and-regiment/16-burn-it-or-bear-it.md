## 16. Burn It or Bear It

The chapel had lost its roof to a shell sometime in the autumn and gained, in exchange, a fine open view of the stars, which struck me as a poor trade for a house of God. The walls still stood, mostly. One window had survived with its saints intact, and their painted faces watched us through the frost with expressions I can only describe as professionally sympathetic. Saints are good at that. They have had centuries of practice watching people suffer and doing nothing about it.

Tomas slept against my side under two blankets and my coat, his breath fogging in the dark. The marsh behind us had swallowed our tracks hours ago, or so I told myself. I had told myself a great many things that day, and I had stopped believing most of them around the time the gunfire started at the bridge.

I had not looked back. That was the bargain I'd made with myself in the water: *you do not look back, because if you look back you will go back, and if you go back the boy dies too.* So I had waded through the reeds with Tomas's wrist in my fist and Elias Vane's voice in my ears — *go, Hazel, for God's sake, go* — and I had not looked back. I am told this is courage. I can report from the inside that it feels much more like a kind of cowardice with better posture.

The ledger sat in my lap.

I had carried it for twelve years. Through mud and mortar fire, through two changes of hospital, through one humiliating episode involving a flooded tent and a very surprised chaplain. It had cost me sleep and safety and, as of tonight, very nearly everything else. Finn arrested. Vane dead — or dying, or worse than dying, because I hadn't stayed to learn which, and the not-knowing sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. And now the ledger, that little book of resurrected names, was the single most dangerous object in the province.

Which presented a question of some urgency, and I was honest enough with myself to admit I'd been circling it since the marsh:

What was it *for*?

* * *

I took it out and held it in the starlight. A cheap ledger, the kind quartermasters use for boot inventories. Its cover was soft with handling, its corners rounded like a river stone. Inside, in my hand, in three different inks and one memorable entry in pencil during a shell shortage, were the names of the forgotten. Four hundred and eleven of them. Men the reports had erased. Men whose mothers had received the wrong story or no story at all.

I had always told myself it was a memorial. A private act of witness. *Every erased person deserves to be remembered* — I had said it to myself so many times it had worn smooth as a psalm.

But a memorial is a thing you build after the danger passes. What I had was a thing that created danger, and the danger had finally come due, and it had come due not on me but on everyone near me. Finn Calloway was in a cell somewhere for the crime of looking at a child's drawing. Pryce had handed me the ambulance keys with a face like a woman signing her own confession. And Elias Vane —

I made myself think it plainly, because I had spent the whole march not thinking it plainly, and plainness was owed to him. Elias Vane had held a bridge against men with rifles so that a nurse and a child could run. He had done it because of the names. Because of the pattern I'd found and he'd found, the officers selling their men's positions and decorating themselves with the profits, and the boy who had seen too much, and the book that proved it all.

The ledger had not been a memorial. It had been a fuse. I had simply been fortunate — until tonight — about how long the fuse was.

And so the thought arrived, the way such thoughts do, dressed in the clothing of mercy: *Burn it.*

* * *

There was a brazier by the ruined altar, left by whatever congregation or garrison had used the chapel last. It held old prayer books, torn up for kindling, and half a candle stub. I had a box of matches in my pocket — nurses always do; we light things for a living — and the ledger would take to flame in under a minute. Four hundred and eleven names to ash in the time it takes to boil water.

And then what?

Then Finn's arrest was for nothing. Then Pryce's stolen keys and stolen conscience were for nothing. Then Tomas's father had died on a bridge protecting a book of no value, and the officers who sold their men went on being promoted, and the armistice would come, and the tribunals would come, and there would be nothing to put in front of them but my word and a child's memory. My word, from a woman who had just fled her post with a collaborator's son. A child's memory, from a boy who hadn't spoken for the first weeks I knew him and could be dismissed as traumatized, coached, invented.

The fire would keep my nurses safe. It would keep the orderlies safe. It would keep *me* safe, and I could go back to being a nurse instead of a witness, and in twenty years I would take the ledger out of some locked drawer in my memory on quiet nights and read the names to myself, and myself would be the only congregation.

It was a good plan. It was a *kind* plan. It was the plan a tired woman makes at two in the morning in a roofless church, and I want to be clear that I understood all of that even as I struck the match.

* * *

The flame stood up on the match head, small and orange and perfectly indifferent to the moral crisis it had been summoned to adjudicate.

I looked at it. I looked at the ledger. And I thought about Private Daniel Okafor.

I had never met Daniel Okafor alive. He had come through my ward as a body, one of four from a "friendly fire" incident at a crossroads that the morning report described, in its entirety, as *regrettable*. No unit named. No officer named. The other three had gone into the ground with full honors and full paperwork. Okafor's name had been struck — a clerical error, someone said, though clerical errors are suspiciously good at finding the men with no one to complain on their behalf. I had written his name in the ledger at three in the morning while the ward slept, and I had felt, for the first time in years, that I had done one whole and uncorrupted thing.

If I burned the book, Daniel Okafor went back to being *regrettable*. All four hundred and eleven of them did. The officers kept their decorations, the reports kept their lies, and the war ended the way wars end — with the guilty shaking hands and the dead holding their silence forever, because the dead are the only ones who never break rank.

The match burned down to my fingers.

I hissed and shook it out, and in the dark that followed, a small voice beside me said:

"You were going to burn it."

Not a question. Tomas sat up in the blankets, hair wild, eyes too old for the face they lived in. Eight years old and he read firelight like a staff officer reads a map.

"I was thinking about it," I said, which was true, and cowardly in its phrasing, and he deserved better than that. "I was going to burn it. Yes."

"Why?"

"Because people die for it. Because a very good man died for it tonight, and I watched it happen, and I can't — " My voice did something I didn't authorize. I waited it out. "Because keeping it safe feels less important than keeping *you* safe. That's the truth. I'm sorry."

Tomas considered this with the gravity of a small judge. Then he said, "Papa said a thing. About burning."

"Did he."

"He said — " Tomas frowned, reaching for it, and I understood suddenly that he was not reaching for words but for his father's exact words, because this child had been raised on precision, on names memorized and lists recited, on the idea that a thing said wrong is a thing lost. "He said, *a list in your head is a hostage. A list on paper is a witness. Hostages get killed. Witnesses get heard.*"

I sat very still.

"He said it about himself," Tomas went on. "He said they couldn't kill what was in his head, but nobody would ever *know* it was there. So he wrote it down. He said writing it down was the bravest stupid thing he ever did." A pause. "He laughed when he said stupid."

Elias Vane. Twelve years since the burning aid station, since he'd carried me out over his shoulder with my blood on his collar, since we'd made a promise over bad wine in a peacetime that turned out to be a rest between wars — *if either of us ever needs the other, no rank, no flag, no anything stands in the way* — and the last thing he'd given me, through his son, was an argument against my own mercy.

"He was right," I said. "He was usually right. It was one of his most annoying qualities."

"He had a lot of annoying qualities," Tomas said, with such solemn fidelity to the truth that I almost laughed, and the almost-laugh cracked open into something else, and for a moment the two of us sat in a roofless church crying in the manner of people who have agreed not to comment on it.

* * *

When it passed, Tomas took my hand. His fingers were small and cold and certain.

"Recite," he said.

"Recite what?"

"Papa's list. I know it. He made me learn it before he wrote it down, so it would be two places." He said it the way children say enormous things, plainly, without understanding the tonnage of them. "If you burn the book, it's still in me. But if I — " he stumbled here, the first time I'd heard him stumble — "if something happens to me, it's still in the book. Papa said that's what *witness* means. It means it's in more than one person."

I looked at this child — this child who had crossed a marsh in November, who had watched his father's world burn, who had lied at a checkpoint with a steadiness that had frightened me even as it saved us — and I understood that the ledger had never been mine. I had only been keeping it in trust. It belonged to the dead, and to the boy, and to everyone who had ever been written out of a report by a man with a promotion in mind.

"Go on, then," I said.

He closed his eyes. And in a voice that wavered at the edges but never broke, Tomas recited his father's list. Names. Ranks. Dates. Places. The crossroads. The ridge. The supply depot at Marren that burned with the wrong company inside it. Some of the names I knew from my own pages; some were new, and I understood that Elias had kept finding more, right up to the end, right up to the bridge. My hand moved in the dark, finding the ledger, holding it against my chest like a heartbeat.

When he finished, the silence in the chapel was not empty. It was the particular silence of a room that has just heard testimony.

"Four hundred and eleven in my book," I said. "How many in his?"

"Two hundred and six," Tomas said. "Some are the same. Papa said the same ones twice is how you know it's true."

The same ones twice is how you know it's true. I thought of the tribunal that was coming — the armistice commission, the officers in clean uniforms who would want clean answers. My ledger alone could be dismissed as one woman's obsession. A child's memory alone could be dismissed as coaching. But two records, kept separately, by two people who had never compared notes, agreeing on name after name after name —

That was not obsession. That was corroboration. That was a case.

* * *

I took out the matches again. Tomas watched me, and I watched him watch me, and I let him see exactly what I was doing, because he had earned the right to see it.

I did not light the ledger.

I lit the candle stub instead, and set it on the brazier's edge, and by its light I opened the book to the first blank page and wrote, at the top, in my steadiest hand:

*Testimony of Hazel Blackwood, nurse, and Tomas Vane, age eight. Recorded in the chapel at [I left the place blank; I didn't know its name and wasn't going to invent one] on the night following the mill bridge. Two hundred and six names follow, as memorized by the witness, to be checked against the preceding entries.*

"Dictate them again," I said. "Slowly. I'm writing them down."

"Again?"

"Again. Papa said it himself, didn't he — a witness gets heard. Well. A witness gets heard *twice*." I dipped the pen. "And when we get in front of that commission, and some colonel with clean cuffs tells you that you're a frightened little boy who's been fed a story, you will look him in the eye and recite it a third time, and we will watch him do the arithmetic. Two lists. Two hands. One truth. He can burn a book, Tomas. He cannot burn you."

Tomas sat up straighter. Something moved through his face that I recognized, because I had seen it in his father's face across a burning aid station twelve years ago: the moment when fear turns into a decision.

"Daniel Okafor," he began. "Private. Crossroads engagement. Fourth of — "

"Fourth of September," I said, writing. "Go on."

He went on. Name by name, date by date, the boy's voice and my pen keeping time together in that broken church, while outside the frost crept across the marsh and the armistice stalled somewhere in a room full of men who did not yet know that their secret had learned to speak.

We were perhaps forty names in when I heard it — the crunch of a boot on frozen gravel outside the chapel wall, deliberate, unhurried, and far too confident to be a lost refugee.

Tomas stopped. His hand found mine in the dark.

I closed the ledger, put out the candle, and reached for the boy.
