## 17. The Armistice That Isn't

I have never in my life stolen so much as a pencil.

I want that on the record, wherever records are kept — the true ones, not the morning reports. Sergeant Imogen Pryce, twelve years' service, commendations for tidiness of all things, and until three days ago a woman who returned borrowed paperclips. My mother raised me on a simple catechism: the rules are what stand between us and the animals. I recited it to myself through every inspection, every filing audit, every order I swallowed that tasted like wet wool. The rules are what stand between us and the animals.

Here is what no one tells you about the rules: they can be rewritten by anyone with enough rank, and then they stop protecting you and start protecting him.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes at this hospital begin, with gossip — which is to say with Orderly Whitcombe, who could extract intelligence from a turnip. He came barreling into the supply tent on the morning after Hazel fled, waving a scrap of newspaper like a man whose house was on fire but who wanted everyone to admire the flames first.

"Signing's off," he said. "Stalled. Delegation can't agree on the repatriation clauses. Could be days, could be weeks."

"Could be nonsense," I said, counting bandages that did not need counting because my hands wanted occupation and my head wanted silence.

"That's what I told 'em. Armistice stalls? What's left to fight over?" He lowered his voice with the delicacy of a man dropping an anvil. "They say it's the colonels. Somebody in Ashworth's circle wants amendments."

Amendments. I put down the bandage and looked at him properly. "Which amendments?"

"How would I know? I'm an orderly, not a delegate." He grinned, delighted with the world's misery as only the truly harmless can be. But the grin faltered when he saw my face, and he left quickly, which from Whitcombe was practically an act of mercy.

I stood among the shelves and made myself think it through, because thinking is what I do instead of panicking, and because I had spent two nights doing neither.

The armistice would bring tribunals. Everyone knew it; the delegates had said so publicly, in the self-congratulatory way men announce justice they intend to apply to other people. And tribunals require testimony. And testimony requires witnesses.

And witnesses, if one were a man facing a tribunal, might be described as loose ends.

Hazel had run with a ledger full of dead men's names and a child who drew maps in his sleep. Ashworth had toured our ward in September, asking questions about unauthorized personnel with the pleasant menace of a customs officer, and he had looked at Hazel's cot a moment too long. Finn Calloway sat in the guardhouse on charges so flimsy that even the corporal who'd written them couldn't meet my eye. One loose end already tied off. A nurse and a boy still fraying.

And now, suddenly, inexplicably, the war needed a few more days. Amendments.

The signing wasn't stalling because peace was difficult. Peace was easy. It was the cutting of threads before the lights came on that took time.

* * *

I did what I always do when the ground shifts under me: I checked the duty roster. It's not superstition; it's method. A roster is a map of intentions. Whoever commands the hours commands the truth of a place, and rosters don't lie the way people do.

Ashworth's escort company had tripled. There were patrols scheduled along the eastern roads — the refugee roads, the roads that led toward the marsh country where a ruined mill stood on a bridge Hazel had once mentioned in passing, three years ago, in the idle way she mentions everything she actually means. And there, at the bottom of the schedule, written in the adjutant's careful hand: *Detachment, Blackwood, H., Matron — escort to divisional HQ upon apprehension. Discretion authorized.*

*Discretion authorized.* Four words that in this army meant whatever the bearer needed them to mean. I had seen "discretion" applied to prisoners who arrived at interrogation and departed in the other direction, if you take my meaning, and I don't mean discharged.

I read the line four times. Then I folded the roster with perfect military precision, set it back on the adjutant's desk, and walked out to the yard, where I stood in the cold and admitted to myself what I had been refusing to admit since Hazel walked out of my quarrel with her jaw set like a keel.

She was going to die. Not court-martialed — there would be no paperwork, nothing so honest. She would be a casualty of bandits, or refugees, or misadventure, and some clerk — perhaps me, God forgive me, perhaps me — would type the report in triplicate, and the rules I had served for twelve years would sign their own signature to her murder and call it regrettable.

My mother's catechism rose up in me one more time. *The rules are what stand between us and the animals.*

And then, for the first time in my life, I asked the question underneath it: *Whose rules, and between whom?*

* * *

Colonel Ashworth kept his dispatch case in his quarters, locked, naturally, and guarded, theoretically, by a sentry named Dobbs who owed his comfortable posting entirely to the fact that nobody had ever asked him to do anything demanding. I knew this because knowing where things are is half of administration, and I have always been the best administrator in this hospital, including the officers, including Ashworth himself, though he'd have had me flogged for saying so.

I told myself I was going to his quarters to request clarification of the escort orders. That is the beautiful thing about a lifetime of rule-keeping: you know exactly how the rules bend, and you can walk into any room carrying a legitimate errand like a pass in your pocket.

Dobbs was at his post, warming his hands over a brazier. "Sergeant Pryce. Evening."

"Sergeant Dobbs." We were the same rank, but he'd been promoted last and wore it apologetically. "The colonel wants the casualty returns collated before midnight. His orders. He said to collect them from his desk himself — sensitive material, he said, no runners."

That was a lie. Every word of it was a lie, delivered in the flat competent voice I have used for twelve years to make lies sound like the timetable. I felt nothing. That frightened me more than anything else that night — not trembling hands, not a hammering heart, just a clean, quiet nothing, like the pause between lightning and thunder when the strike has already landed somewhere behind you.

"He's at mess," Dobbs said.

"Then I'll be gone before he's back. Casualty returns, Dobbs. Midnight." I let the syllables fall like stamps on a form, and he stepped aside, because a sergeant with an errand is furniture, and furniture doesn't get questioned.

The dispatch case sat under the window. The lock was Army Standard Pattern 4, and I had watched the adjutant open one a hundred times without really watching, the way you absorb anything done often enough by someone careless. It took me four attempts and six minutes, each of which lasted approximately a year.

Inside: dispatches. Dozens of them, ribbon-bound, initialled. I took them to the window where the last light fell and I read.

I am not a stupid woman, whatever else may be said of me after this, and I had spent a career reading between the lines of official correspondence, which is the only place truth lives in this army. It did not take long.

There was the signal from October, two years back — the one ordering artillery registration against grid coordinates that the accompanying memorandum listed as *friendly dispositions, amend prior signal*. Ashworth's initials. There was the follow-up, congratulating the battery on the accuracy of fire that had fallen on its own infantry, and the inquiry into how such a tragedy occurred, and the finding that the tragedy occurred because of *confusion at regimental level*, and the courts-martial of two junior signals officers who had, as far as I could tell from the traffic, done precisely what they were ordered to do. Both men's names rang faint bells. I dug through the case. There they were again, months later — both reassigned, both then listed among casualties at Crossroads engagement. Omitted from the morning reports. I knew they would be in Hazel's ledger. I knew it the way you know your own reflection.

Page after page. Grid coordinates sold or shifted, battalions fed into fires that someone had arranged in advance, and every investigation concluding in exculpation decorated with promotion. Ashworth's initials marched down the margins like a column of ants at a picnic, tidy, inevitable, everywhere.

The last dispatch was recent. It concerned the armistice delegation and read, in full: *Signatures deferred pending resolution of administrative irregularities. List to follow. No movement on remaining items until confirmed clear.*

Administrative irregularities. A nurse. A child. An orderly in a cell. A list.

I stood in the colonel's quarters holding twelve years of murder bound in ribbon, and I confess I laughed. Once, briefly, horribly. Because the whole edifice — the inspections, the regulations, the morning reports, the entire gorgeous machine of correctness — had been built for exactly this moment, to keep men like Ashworth warm and dry while the names underneath burned as fuel. And I had polished that machine. I had oiled it. I had typed its reports in triplicate.

Well. If the rules protected the animals, then the rules could go to hell, and I would go with them, gladly, carrying as much evidence as fit in a nurse's kitbag.

I repacked the case. I took the dispatches — not all of them; a missing case invites a search, and a search finds more than papers. I took the eleven documents that together formed the spine of it: the signals, the amendments, the findings, the casualty lists, the deferral. Eleven sheets of paper, lighter than a field dressing, heavier than everything else I owned put together.

On my way out I stopped at Dobbs's brazier and held my hands over it as if warming them, and we exchanged the small talk of two sergeants on a cold evening, and my voice never shook once, and I understood finally what kind of person that made me, and I found I could live with it.

* * *

The hard part was leaving without appearing to leave.

You cannot simply walk out of a hospital three days after the matron vanished; absence draws eyes the way blood draws sharks. So I built my departure out of legitimate bricks. I requested transport to divisional HQ to query the ambulance requisitions — plausible, dull, mine by rights. I signed the duty book with my usual hand. I told Whitcombe to mind the stores, and he saluted me with a potato in his fist, and I very nearly embraced him, which would have caused talk.

At the motor pool, Private Larkin looked at my travel authorization, looked at me, and looked back at the paper.

"You're going *out there*?" he said. "After dark? Bandits on the east roads, they're saying."

"I've heard," I said. "Requisitions won't query themselves, Larkin."

He shrugged the shrug of a man who has decided another person's death is above his pay grade, and handed over the keys, and that was that. Twelve years of service concluded with a shrug and a set of keys. I had expected — I don't know what. Trumpets. At minimum a moment of internal ceremony.

Instead there was only the road, black and rutted, and the ambulance's feeble headlamps carving a tunnel out of the dark, and my hands at ten and two on the wheel like a learner, because it was true, in every way that mattered, I was a learner. Twelve years learning the wrong lesson, and now the remedial course, at night, in frost, alone.

The east road was bad and got worse. Twice I doused the lamps for patrol headlights coming the other way, easing into a ditch and waiting with my heart going like a drum corps while staff cars slid past toward the front — toward the mill country, I suspected, toward the bridge. Ashworth was deploying his pieces. I was racing a colonel across a chessboard the size of a province, and my queen was a nurse with a ledger, and my pawn was a child who didn't speak, and my own position, I realized somewhere around the second crossroads, was now roughly that of the men in Hazel's book: erased from the record, moving at night, alive only so long as nobody official caught up.

It should have terrified me. Mostly it did. But underneath the terror there was something else, something I can only describe as the specific relief of a corset unhooked after a decade — the strange freedom of finally being on the correct side of a thing, even the losing side, even the hunted side. Hazel had lived with that feeling for years, carrying her floorboard secret. How had she borne it? How had she smiled through inspections with a subversion under the boards?

Because it wasn't subversion. That was the answer, and it arrived somewhere in the fourth hour of driving, plain as a hymn. She wasn't fighting the rules. She was keeping the real ones — the old ones, the ones about names mattering and the dead being counted and children not being thrown away — while men like Ashworth wrote new rules to excuse themselves from the first set. I hadn't lost my faith in rules. I'd only found out where they'd been hiding.

* * *

The marshes near the mill were exactly as cheerful as they sound: black water, dead reeds, a moon the color of an unlit lamp. I left the ambulance in a copse off the cart track and walked the last mile with the dispatches inside my coat, against my chest, like a heartbeat I'd borrowed.

The chapel — Hazel had mentioned a bombed chapel in her last scrambled note, which I was not supposed to have found and read, one infraction among many now — stood maybe four hundred meters from the water, one wall standing, the rest open to the sky like a doll's house for a giant's sadistic daughter.

I was fifty meters out when I saw the light move inside. Candlelight, guttering, then snuffed. Then nothing, and the nothing had a quality I recognized: the stillness of people listening.

I did the sensible thing and announced myself, because the alternative was getting shot by allies, and I had come too far and committed too many felonies to be shot by allies.

"Sergeant Imogen Pryce," I called into the dark, my breath steaming, my hands well away from my belt. "Hospital corps. Traveling with eleven documents I stole from Colonel Ashworth's dispatch case, and if anyone's counting, I've also stolen an ambulance, a travel authorization, and my own soul, though the last was pre-owned. I'm looking for Nurse Blackwood, and I'm either the best or the worst thing that's happened to her this week."

Silence. Long enough that I started revising my eulogy.

Then, from the chapel doorway, a voice — low, hoarse, unmistakably hers, and shaking with something that was not fear.

"Only you, Imogen. Only you would surrender yourself with an itemized confession."

"Habit," I said. "Full disclosure. It's in the regulations."

A match flared. Hazel stood in the ruined doorway with a pistol in one hand and the ledger clutched against her coat with the other arm, and beside her, half-hidden in her shadow, a small boy watched me with eyes far too old for the face around them.

I walked toward them across the frozen gravel, and I noticed distantly that my hands had finally started to shake — now, of all times, when it no longer helped — and I heard myself say, in the flat competent voice I no longer had any right to:

"They've stalled the signing, Hazel. Days, maybe less. Ashworth's cut a list, and your name is at the top of it, and I have the proof of everything in my coat, and I don't — " My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. "I don't know where to go from here. I've never been anywhere the regulations don't cover."

Hazel looked at me for a long moment across the dark. Then she did the thing she has always done better than anyone I've known: she reached out and took the weight.

"Then you're in the right place," she said. "Nobody here knows either. Come inside. We're making a list of our own."

Behind her, in the candle-glow, the boy held up a piece of paper covered in careful childish letters — names, dozens of them — and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the armistice everyone was waiting for had already been signed, in lead pencil, by an eight-year-old, and that the men in the negotiating room had no idea yet what they'd been outranked by.
