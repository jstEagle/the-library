## 18. Reunion of the Forgotten

The farmhouse had been empty since the spring shelling, and it wore its abandonment the way soldiers wore new boots — stiffly, as if waiting to be claimed by someone with worse intentions. Hazel chose it for the cellar door hidden behind the fallen pantry shelving, and for the apple orchard gone wild, whose tangled rows would show any approaching figure a full minute before he arrived.

She was counting that minute now, watching through the crack in the shutters, when she heard Tomas whisper her name.

"Hazel. There's a woman in the lane."

"I see her."

"Is it the one who fights with you?"

Hazel almost laughed. *The one who fights with you.* Out of the mouths of eight-year-olds, an accurate summary of twelve years of friendship. "Yes," she said. "That's Imogen."

"Is she angry?"

"She's always angry. It's how she loves people. Stand behind me anyway."

But even as she said it, Hazel was reading Pryce's walk — that brisk, squared-off stride that could measure a parade ground by eye — and something in her chest unclenched and then hurt worse for it. Pryce had come alone. No escort, no ambulance, no Ashworth's men trailing her like gulls after a plough. She had come the way you come when you have chosen a side and decided not to announce it: quietly, carrying everything herself.

Pryce reached the gate, looked once at the house, once at the road behind her, and did not knock. She came around the side, found the cellar door exactly where Hazel would have put it, because Pryce had helped her hide contraband brandy under precisely such a door in 1917, and descended without being called.

She stood at the bottom of the steps with lantern light on her face, mud to her knees, her collar undone — Imogen Pryce, undone collar, the world had truly tilted — and said, "You look appalling."

"You followed me forty miles to say that?"

"I followed you forty miles because you took my ambulance, drove it through a checkpoint without papers, and are currently a deserter in the company of a child the Colonel wants found." She set down the satchel she carried. Her hands were shaking; Hazel noted it and generously said nothing. "I've come to tell you that you're next on his list, so that you can be properly frightened, and then I intend to shout at you."

Tomas stepped out from behind Hazel's skirts and looked up at Pryce with the frank appraisal he gave everyone, the appraisal of a boy who had learned that faces were documents to be read carefully.

"This is her," he said to Hazel. "This is the one from your stories."

Pryce blinked. "What stories?"

"The ones she tells when she thinks I'm asleep."

"I do no such thing."

"You do," Tomas said. "There's one where Sergeant Pryce reorganized the entire supply tent in a night and made three captains cry."

"It was two captains," Pryce said, with dignity, "and one major, and they'd earned it." And then Hazel watched it happen — watched her friend, her granite friend, kneel down on a dirt cellar floor in an abandoned farm to look an eight-year-old boy in the eye. "Hello, Tomas. I'm Imogen. Your father pulled this woman out of a fire once, which makes her chronically incapable of sensible behavior, and I have spent twelve years cleaning up after the pair of them. You're among friends. I know you have no particular reason to believe me."

Tomas considered this with terrible seriousness. "I believe you," he said. "You came alone. The bad men never come alone."

Pryce looked up at Hazel over the boy's head, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "Well," she said roughly. "That settles that."

* * *

They made a fire small enough to hide and ate what Pryce had brought — hard bread, tinned fish, and a jar of pears she claimed to have requisitioned and which they both knew she had stolen from the Colonel's own stores, an act of larceny so entirely out of character that Hazel wanted to frame the empty jar afterward.

It was only after Tomas fell asleep against Hazel's side, his drawing pencil still in his fist, that Pryce opened her satchel properly and took out the dispatches.

Hazel read them by lantern light while the wind worked at the shutters. Supply manifests. Casualty reports. Signatures — Ashworth's signature, neat and self-assured, beside grid coordinates Hazel knew by heart because the men who died at those coordinates were in her ledger, page after page of them, omitted from every official record, written only in her hand.

"Where did you get these?"

"His desk. He keeps the key in his boot, which tells you everything about the man." Pryce turned a pear quarter over in her fingers without eating it. "He's delaying the armistice, Hazel. Stalling the signing until his loose ends are cut. You're a loose end. Finn is another. The boy is the largest of all."

"Finn." Hazel set down the paper. "Imogen. What happened to Finn?"

"He's gone." A muscle moved in Pryce's jaw. "Two days ago the guard on him changed, and the new guard was one of Ashworth's, and by morning the cell was empty and the man assigned to watch him was unconscious in a puddle with his own bootlaces around his wrists. Nobody will say the word escaped aloud because it makes the Colonel livid, but yes. Escaped. Or—" she hesitated, and Hazel heard the hesitation and understood it, "—or taken somewhere quieter. I don't know which. I came to warn you before I knew which, and I left before the answer came, and I find I don't regret leaving."

Hazel sat with that. The lantern hissed. Tomas breathed against her ribs, deep and even, a child's sleep bought at a price neither of them spoke about.

"If they took him," Hazel said slowly, "then they'll want him quiet. But if he got out—"

"If he got out, he'll come looking for you. Finn Calloway has many faults but he has never once in six years failed to find Hazel Blackwood when she didn't want to be found. Usually at the worst possible moment." Pryce smiled despite herself. "I give him three days."

* * *

Finn Calloway took two.

Hazel woke near dawn to the soft scrape of the cellar door and a voice pitched low and cheerful in the dark — a voice she had heard a thousand times hauling stretchers, cheating at cards, talking his way past sentries — and she was moving before she was fully awake, and then Finn was inside the lantern light, thinner, bruised along one cheekbone, grinning like a man arriving late to his own funeral.

"You're supposed to be in custody," Hazel said.

"And you're supposed to be sensible, and yet here we both are." He embraced her quickly, hard, the embrace of someone checking that the other person was real. Then he saw Tomas stirring awake and crouched to the boy's level. "Hello, sprout. Remember me? I found the drawings."

"You held them upside down," Tomas said.

"I hold most things upside down. It's a gift." But his grin faded as he straightened, and Hazel saw the guilt underneath it, the guilt he'd carried since the night he'd copied the drawing and been arrested for it. "I'm sorry," he said to the boy, simply. "For getting caught with your picture. It cost us."

"They let me keep the pencil," Tomas said, holding it up. "So it cost less than it might have."

Finn stared at him, then laughed — a short, cracked laugh that turned into something else briefly, and he turned away and busied himself with his coat until he had control of his face again.

Behind him, two more figures came down the steps, and Hazel's breath went out of her. Orderlies. Whitfield, gray-bearded, who had carried men out of gas attacks with his scarf over his mouth and never once complained. And young Ada Marsh, whom the records listed as a laundress but who had stitched more torn flesh than half the surgeons Hazel knew.

"How—" Hazel began.

"There's a hole in the world," Whitfield said mildly, "and everyone who's ever been erased by it can feel the draft." He shrugged off his pack. "Finn came to us. Said you'd be needing hands. He was right, wasn't he?"

"He usually is," Pryce said darkly, "which is why I distrust him."

* * *

They gathered around the lantern after the bread was shared, seven people in a cellar under a ruined farm in the last week of a dying war, and it was Ada — shy Ada, laundress-who-was-not-a-laundress — who started it.

She was looking at the ledger. Hazel had it out of the oilcloth finally, checking the pages for damp, and Ada reached across and touched the cover the way you touch a church door.

"May I?" she said.

"It's not mine," Hazel said. "That's rather the point of it."

Ada turned to a blank page near the back. From her sleeve she drew a stub of pencil, and her hand shook, and she wrote a name. Private Daniel Osei, stretcher-bearer, killed at Harrow Field, struck from the rolls because his death certificate would have raised questions about the ammunition convoy that was never meant to be where it was.

"My brother," she said. "I've carried the name four years and never dared write it anywhere. I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought if I wrote it down, it would become evidence, and evidence gets people arrested. So I just... carried it. Like a stone."

Whitfield took the pencil next. He wrote two names, unhurried, his big careful hand steady on the paper. Men from his first year of service, 1914, boys from his own village, vanished between one morning report and the next.

"Nobody missed them but me," he said. "Their mothers asked. I couldn't answer. This isn't an answer either, but it's a place for the question to live."

Finn added names — three of them, quick and scrawled, men whose deaths he'd witnessed and whose paperwork had evaporated. Pryce held out longest. Pryce stood apart with her arms crossed, and Hazel watched her wrestle herself, and lost the wrestling, and came forward and took the pencil and wrote a single name in letters so precise they could have been typeset.

"Who?" Hazel asked quietly.

"Lieutenant James Corrigan. My first commanding officer. He reported the same pattern you found — the sold positions, the friendly fire that wasn't friendly. They posted him to a forward listening post the following week. There was no body." She closed the ledger gently, as though it could feel things. "I told myself for six years that he must have deserted. It was easier. Now I owe you an apology, Hazel Blackwood, for every time I called your book morbid."

"You never called it morbid."

"I called it morbid constantly, in my heart."

Tomas went last. He took the pencil in his small fist and wrote, slowly, letter by letter, a name Hazel didn't recognize — and then another, and another, working down the page with his tongue between his teeth, the list his father had made him memorize pouring out of him onto paper at last, safe in ink instead of locked in an eight-year-old's memory where men with guns wanted it to stay.

When he finished, he looked around at all of them. "Now it's not just Hazel's," he said. "Now it's ours."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The lantern flame stood very still, as if it too were listening.

"Yes," Hazel said, and her voice was not entirely steady. "That's exactly it."

She had spent twelve years believing she kept the ledger alone — that witness was a solitary vocation, a private religion, a weight you shouldered because no one else would. Sitting in the dirt with a deserter, a thief, a laundress, a stretcher-bearer, a rule-keeper, and a child, she understood she had never once been keeping it alone. She had only been keeping it unheard.

* * *

Near midday Whitfield climbed up to watch the lane and came back down faster than he'd gone up.

"Wagon on the road," he said. "Military. Coming slow — searching the farms as they go. Ten minutes, perhaps less."

They moved like a machine that had never drilled together and somehow knew the drill. Bread into packs. Fire drowned. Ledger into oilcloth, oilcloth into Hazel's shirt. But at the foot of the steps Tomas stopped, and pointed at the cellar wall where the lantern hung.

"The names," he whispered. "We leave the names."

"We carry them," Hazel said. "That's better. Walls burn. We don't."

And then, faintly, from the orchard above them, came a sound that froze all six adults where they stood: a whistle. Three notes, low, deliberate. Finn's head came up sharply.

"That's no patrol," he breathed. "That's a signal. That's—" He was already moving, up the steps, Hazel behind him, and together they eased open the cellar door a crack.

Standing among the wild apple trees, hatless, hands raised to show he carried nothing, was a gaunt figure in a stolen greatcoat two sizes too large — and beside him, supporting him, an arm across his shoulders, a second figure Hazel recognized from a photograph she had never expected to see standing up in the daylight.

"Tomas," Hazel said quietly, and her heart was hammering so hard she could barely keep her voice level. "Look at the orchard."

The boy pressed to the gap. He went utterly still. Then he said, in a small, wondering voice that broke in the middle:

"Papa?"
