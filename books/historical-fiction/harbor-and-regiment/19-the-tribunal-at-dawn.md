## 19. The Tribunal at Dawn

### Hazel

They gave us a schoolhouse for the tribunal, which I thought was either the commission's idea of mercy or its idea of a joke. A schoolhouse: chalk dust still in the air, a slate on the wall with somebody's half-erased arithmetic — *7 × 8 =* and then a smudge where a child had given up. Half the arithmetic of that morning would end in smudges too.

I had not slept. Neither had Pryce, who sat beside me with her stolen dispatches bundled in oilcloth against her ribs like a newborn, and neither had Tomas, who sat on my other side with his father's list folded into his fist so tightly I'd begun to worry about the paper's circulation. Finn stood at the back with the two orderlies who'd come over the fence with him, all three of them wearing the particular expression of men who have escaped custody once and are prepared to make a habit of it.

"You look terrible," Pryce murmured.

"It's four in the morning."

"You looked terrible at nine last night as well. Consistency is a virtue."

Outside, the armistice guns were supposed to fall silent at eleven. Inside, seven officers of the armistice commission sat behind a long table with the weary patience of men who had expected to spend the war's last hours signing documents and were instead being asked to hang one of their own. They did not know yet that it was one of their own. That was the whole of my strategy, such as it was: let them find out slowly.

Colonel Ashworth sat at a smaller table across the aisle from us, flanked by two captains with very clean boots. He looked rested. He looked shaved, pressed, faintly amused — a man attending a formality. When his eyes found mine, they stayed there a moment longer than courtesy allowed, and in them I saw the arithmetic he was doing: one nurse, one ledger, one boy. Small numbers. Easily divided.

I opened the ledger on my lap under the table and counted the names again for the hundredth time. Forty-one pages. Two hundred and six entries in my hand, thirty-eight in others' hands — nurses and orderlies down the years who had trusted me with what they'd seen and been told to unsee. Twelve years of omissions, carried under a floorboard like a heart beating in hiding.

The president of the commission, a Major-General with a voice like gravel in a tin cup, called us to order at dawn precisely because dawn is when tribunals are least dramatic. Grey light through grey windows. Coughs. The scratch of a clerk's pen. No trumpets anywhere.

"State your name and service," said the Major-General, looking at me the way men look at weather.

"Hazel Blackwood. Nursing Sister, Twelfth Field Hospital." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "And I am here to report two hundred and forty-four deaths that do not appear in any official record."

That got their attention. It got Ashworth's too; I felt it arrive between his shoulder blades like cold water.

### Pryce

I have spent eleven years believing that rules are the only thing standing between soldiers and savagery, which is why what I did next will occupy me for the rest of my life, however long the rest of my life turns out to be. I broke into my commanding officer's dispatch case, committed theft, forgery of transit papers, and desertion, and I did all of it in a single night, and the remarkable thing — the thing I keep turning over like a coin I suspect is foreign currency — is that it did not feel like breaking. It felt like the rules finally making sense.

Hazel spoke first, of course. Hazel always speaks first; it is her most infuriating and most necessary quality. She laid out the ledger on the evidence table with the tenderness other people reserve for infants, and she began, name by name, to read.

She read Private Alfred Dunn, omitted from the morning report of the 9th of March. She read Corporal Reyes, whose position had been "miscommunicated" to the artillery two days after he wrote a letter complaining about ammunition shortages. She read them all, or enough of them, her voice never rising, never theatrical, just level and terrible, like a tide coming in over something that had been buried on purpose.

The commission grew restless around entry twenty. One brigadier muttered about jurisdiction. Another asked whether the nurse understood she could be charged for impugning the honour of decorated officers.

"I understand it perfectly," Hazel said, "because I have understood it for twelve years. That is rather the point of the ledger. Somebody has to write down what everyone else has agreed to forget."

It was at this juncture that I rose, smoothed my tunic, and said, in the clear administrative voice I have used for eleven years to make lies sound like filing: "Major-General. If the commission will permit, I have supplementary documentation."

I placed the dispatches on the table. Ashworth's own seal. His own initials on requisitions rerouted three days before each of the incidents Hazel named. Orders moving fresh companies into sectors marked in his staff maps as *expected hostile activity*, when no such expectation existed outside the knowledge of the men who had sold those positions. I watched the brigadier who had muttered about jurisdiction pick up the topmost paper, read it, go grey, and pass it along the row like a man passing a live coal.

Ashworth did not go grey. I will grant him that. He went very still and very polite, which in my experience is what dangerous men do when they realize the floor has been removed but have not yet located the trapdoor.

"Where did you obtain these, Sergeant?" he asked me directly. His voice was reasonable. Almost warm.

"In your case, Colonel," I said. "You'll want to note that in the record. Stolen. Illegally. By me. I've written out a confession to that effect, which I'll submit after the commission has finished reading the documents themselves, because I take the view that the evidence should be entered before the person entering it is arrested. It's a matter of proper sequence. I'm rather strict about sequence."

Somewhere behind me, Finn Calloway made a noise that he converted, too late, into a cough.

### Tomas

The room smelled of ink and cold stove. There was a blackboard and I kept looking at it because on it someone had written numbers and stopped halfway and that made me think of Papa, who always finished things, even sentences other people interrupted.

Aunt Hazel — I decided this at the checkpoint and could not decide anything else afterwards — put a big book on the table and read names out of it. Names are important. Papa said so. He made me learn mine every night like a prayer: *if anything happens to me, these men were real, say them.* So when the officers stopped Aunt Hazel and said the book was only a nurse's fancy, unofficial, inadmissible, I stood up.

Everyone looked at me. My legs felt like the bridge at the mill, all broken spans.

"My papa made me learn names too," I said. My voice cracked in the middle but I kept going, because Papa never stopped halfway either. "Private Alfred Dunn. Corporal Luis Reyes. Sergeant—" and I said them, all thirty-eight, in order, the way I'd said them into my sleeve in the dark of cellars and carts, and my voice only broke twice more, and when I finished nobody said it was inadmissible. Nobody said anything at all for quite a while, except one officer near the end whose chair creaked, and then the Major-General asked me my name very gently.

"Tomas Vane," I said. "My papa is Captain Elias Vane, and he isn't a traitor, he's dead, and he died holding a bridge so she could carry the book." I pointed at Aunt Hazel. "So somebody should write that down too. Since you're the ones with pens."

* * *

### Hazel

There is a specific silence that falls in a room when an institution collides with a fact. I had heard it twice before — once over a body, once over a bedpan — and I heard it now, full-length, as the dispatches moved down the commission's table and Tomas's recitation hung in the chalky air.

Then Ashworth stood.

He did not rage. I almost wish he had; it would have been easier to hold in memory than what he actually did, which was adjust his cuffs and speak in the tone of a man correcting a clerical error.

"Gentlemen, this is theatre. A nurse keeps an unauthorized diary, a sergeant steals from her colonel, and a child — the son of a proven traitor, gentlemen, a man who sold *your* positions if anyone sold anything — recites a list he was coached to memorize. I have served this army for thirty years. My record—"

"Your record," said the Major-General flatly, "includes your signature, Colonel."

He turned the third dispatch around so the room could see it. There it was: Ashworth's own hand, authorizing the transfer of a replacement company into the sector at Harrow Bend — dated the day *before* the "friendly fire" incident there — and beneath it, initialed, the omission instruction for the casualty return. Not a signature beside a crime. A signature that *was* the crime, dressed in triplicate.

I watched Ashworth look at his own name. I have thought about that moment often since, and what strikes me still is how small it made him — not the arrest, not later, but that instant, a decorated man reading his own handwriting and finding a stranger in it. For one heartbeat something crossed his face that was almost grief, and I understood, with no sympathy whatsoever, that he had probably told himself a story for years about necessary arithmetic, acceptable losses, the greater good — the same story written on half-erased slates in every chancellery of every army on earth. Then the military police came in at the Major-General's nod, and the grief closed over, and he was small in the ordinary way after all: a man being walked out of a schoolhouse between two bigger men.

He paused at the door. Looked at me. And said, quietly, conversationally, as though remarking on the weather:

"Do you imagine it ends, Sister? Armies sell positions. Someone will always buy."

"Then someone," I said, "will always have to write down the names."

The door shut. The stove ticked. The clerk's pen scratched on, recording everything, which is, when you come to it, the entire job.

* * *

### Pryce

Afterwards — after the commission adjourned to deliberate for exactly nineteen minutes, after the verdicts were read in voices that had rehearsed sounding steady — I found Hazel in the schoolyard behind the building, sitting on a low wall with the ledger closed on her knees, watching the clock crawl toward eleven.

"They cleared him," she said, when I sat down beside her. "Elias. Posthumously. Restored rank, restored name, a letter to be sent to — well. To Tomas, I suppose, since there's no one else." She laughed, a wet, surprised sound. "Twelve years I've been writing down the names they erase. Today they erased one of mine and gave it back. I don't know where to put the feeling, Imogen. There isn't a page for it."

"There will be," I said. "You'll invent one. You always do."

We sat in the cold. Somewhere beyond the town, the great guns had gone quiet already — had gone quiet at eleven sharp, and none of us in the yard had quite managed to say it aloud yet, because saying it made it real, and real things can be lost.

"You'll be charged," Hazel said. "For the theft. For the dispatches."

"Probably. I've drafted my defence. It runs to one sentence."

"Which is?"

"'The regulations exist to protect soldiers, and when they cease to do so, the soldier's protection transfers.'" I shrugged. "I may add a footnote."

Hazel leaned her head briefly against my shoulder, which is the most demonstrative thing she has done in twelve years of friendship, and I permitted it, and we watched the clock tower together and waited for nothing in particular to happen, which after everything turned out to be the finest thing either of us had ever waited for.

Then Finn came around the corner of the schoolhouse at a run — Finn, who cannot walk anywhere at less than a lope and therefore only runs when the news is either wonderful or dreadful — skidding on the frost, hat in hand, face doing several things at once.

"Sister," he panted. "Sergeant. You'll want to hear this. There's a dispatch just come in from the north road. About the marsh party. About—" he caught his breath, and the world narrowed to the pause before he said it— "about Captain Vane's effects. They didn't find a body."

* * *

### Hazel

I stood up so fast the ledger slid, and Pryce caught it — of course she caught it; she catches everything — and Finn stood there holding his hat like a man who has thrown a stone through a window and is waiting to see whether anyone inside is awake.

"They searched the crossing three times," he went on, hurriedly, as though speed could soften it. "Three times, and the marshes twice. No officer's body among the dead. The wounded man the villagers took in at the ferry house — the one who wouldn't give a name—" Finn swallowed. "Sister, he matches the description. Every particular. And he's asking, they say. Asking after a woman with a ledger, and a boy."

The clock struck the quarter hour. The guns were silent. The whole world, for the first time in years, was silent, and inside that silence something in my chest that I had buried with full honours and a false headstone sat up and refused to stay buried.

"Tomas," I said, my voice not steady at all now, nor caring to be. "Go fetch your coat. We're going north."
