## 20. What Names Cost

The tribunal took three days, which struck me as efficient to the point of insult. Ashworth had spent two years building a machine that fed men into silence, and it came apart in an afternoon because a colonel's signature sat beside an omitted name in a nurse's ledger. All that cunning, all those careful erasures, undone by the fact that powerful men cannot resist signing their own work.

I was not in the room when they read the verdict. I didn't need to be. I heard it in the way the corridor changed — clerks who had walked past me for days without meeting my eye suddenly developed faces, and one of them even said good morning, as though we had not both spent the week pretending I was a piece of furniture with inconvenient opinions.

*Guilty on eleven counts.* Eleven counts for a man whose ledgers — his real ones, the regimental ones — had never once been out of balance.

Pryce found me on the hospital steps afterward, still wearing my grey uniform because nobody had told me yet what a woman wears when she has helped hang a colonel. She sat down beside me without asking, which is her way of embracing.

"He's asking for a priest and a lawyer," she said. "In that order. I'd have respected him more if it were reversed."

"Would you?"

"No." She looked out at the mud, which was doing its usual excellent impression of the end of the world. "But I'd have written it down somewhere."

That's Pryce. She forgives the world nothing, including herself, and she keeps accounts.

* * *

The verdicts came down like weather over the following fortnight, some of it clearing, some of it simply moving elsewhere.

Finn Calloway was exonerated on a Tuesday. The theft charges were withdrawn with a bureaucratic shrug — *clerical error, regrettable circumstances, no fault attributable* — as though the military police had arrested him by accident, the way one trips over a root. Finn took it better than I did. He stood in the ward with his release paper in his hand and said, "Well. I always wanted documentation of my character," and then went back to folding bandages, because Finn's method of surviving atrocity is to be useful at it.

Vane was cleared posthumously on a Thursday.

I want to tell you that word arrived with trumpets. It arrived instead as a carbon copy, third-generation typescript, delivered by a lieutenant so young he still saluted doorframes. *The charges against Captain Elias Vane are hereby set aside. The record will reflect that Captain Vane acted at all times in the interest of His Majesty's forces and died in service of the same.*

*Died in service of the same.* As though the marsh hadn't taken him. As though Ashworth's men hadn't put the bullets in. The record would reflect whatever fit in the margin, and Elias Vane — who had held a bridge with a pistol and a promise so that a child and a nurse could walk out through the reeds — was now officially a paragraph.

Tomas took the news the way children take most enormous things: quietly, sideways, while appearing to do something else. He was drawing at the window when I told him. He didn't stop drawing. But his hand slowed, and after a while he said, "Does the record say his full name?"

"Yes," I lied gently. "It does."

"Good." He went back to his bridge. "He hated being called Vaney."

* * *

It was perhaps ten days later, with the armistice ink drying and the whole country pretending to be joyful on schedule, that the ledger turned on me.

I had begun, foolishly, to feel vindicated. That is the danger of testimony: they take your book, they read it into the record, they shake your hand, and for one shining week you believe you were right. I sat in the commission offices two mornings running, answering a captain's questions about entries from 1917, feeling like a person whose twelve years of furtive pencil-work had finally meant something.

Then the commission's major — a tired man named Hollis with ink on his cuff and no appetite for ceremony — laid three pages in front of me and asked whether these names were mine.

They were mine. Every one in my cramped, disguised hand, recorded in ward tents and cellar aid stations across four years: Private Aldous Reeve. Corporal James Whitlock. Sergeant Daniel Mercer. Men I had sat with while they died, whose names officers had omitted, whom I had rescued into my ledger precisely because the army wanted them gone.

"What about them?" I asked.

Major Hollis tapped Reeve's name. "Private Reeve was omitted because the morning report listed him as transferred. He was, in fact, executed by firing squad — for the killing of two prisoners under his guard. The tribunal that condemned him was proper. The sentence was carried out lawfully. The omission was administrative embarrassment, not conspiracy." He moved his finger to Whitlock. "Corporal Whitlock died of wounds received during a reprisal action his own platoon initiated against civilians. And Sergeant Mercer—" He paused, and I give him credit; he did not enjoy this any more than I did. "Sergeant Mercer was stricken from the record because he deserted his post with the regiment's pay chest. Three of his men drowned pursuing him. Their families were told they fell in action."

I remember the light in that office. Grey, November, the kind of light that makes everything look already surrendered. I remember thinking, with absurd clarity: *my hands are cold.*

"You're telling me," I said, "that I've spent twelve years recording the wrong men."

"I'm telling you that you've spent twelve years recording *men*, Miss Blackwood. Some of them were wronged. Some of them were wrong." He folded his hands. "The commission isn't questioning your book. Frankly, your book is the only honest document anyone's produced in this war. We're asking whether you understand what's in it."

I understood nothing. I thanked him, gathered my coat, walked out into the street, and stood there among the armistice bunting until my legs agreed to move.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody warns you about keeping a ledger of the forgotten: forgetting is easy. Remembering is supposed to be the hard, noble part. I had built a religion on it. Every name under the floorboard was a small resurrection, a middle finger to the machinery that ground men into paperwork. I never once asked myself what I was resurrecting *to*.

Daniel Mercer. I knew Daniel Mercer. Not well — he came through my station once with a hand injury, and he was funny, generous with his cigarettes, gentle with the boyish orderlies. I liked him. Two months later he vanished from the rolls, and I wrote his name down because that is what I do, because a name omitted is a wound and I had appointed myself the suturer of wounds.

I never asked why. Asking why wasn't my job. My job was the *that* — the fact of omission, the fact of a wife in Portsmouth who would receive no pension because her husband had been subtracted rather than killed. I recorded the subtraction and felt righteous.

Now the commission tells me Mercer stole the pay of men who trusted him, and three of them died in ditches chasing him through November water, and their mothers got printed lies instead of truth, and somewhere behind all of it stands a wife in Portsmouth who is owed a pension by *somebody* but not by the story I told myself about mercy.

Was Mercer's wife owed her husband's true name? I believe she was. I believe that with everything left in me. Do I want to be the one who delivers it? God help me, no. I would rather deliver anything else in the world.

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat in the dispensary with the ledger open on my knees and went through it page by page, the way you go through a house after a death, deciding what to keep.

Four hundred and six names. I know the number the way I know my own age. Of those, the commission had flagged nineteen — nineteen men whose omissions hid not conspiracy but consequence. Nineteen men who had done things a lawful army could not print. And here is where my wry narrator voice fails me entirely, so forgive me while I put it down plainly:

Remembering them hurt worse than losing Vane.

Not more. Worse. Grief is clean; it asks only that you carry it. But this — this was finding that part of the weight I'd proudly shouldered for twelve years was not weight at all but rot, and that I had loved the carrying too much to notice.

Because that was the ugly truth underneath, the one I made myself say aloud in the empty dispensary at three in the morning, to the iodine bottles and the dark: *I had never checked because checking would have cost me the comfort.* The ledger was my church. You don't audit your own god.

And yet.

And yet — Reeve's mother had stood at a gate for years believing her son transferred alive somewhere, writing letters to regiments that had orders not to answer. Whatever Aldous Reeve did to prisoners, his mother did nothing, and the machinery that erased him erased her son's *death* from her, left her suspended between hope and grief like a fly in amber. That cruelty was real. That cruelty was worth recording. Even if the man beneath it was not worth mourning.

Could both things be true at once? That a name deserves to exist and the man attached to it deserved his fate?

I am a nurse. I should have learned this decades ago. Bodies are both wounded and diseased; you treat them anyway. Names, it turns out, are the same.

* * *

Pryce found me there at dawn, or near enough — she has an unerring instinct for wherever I'm failing to sleep. She looked at the ledger, then at my face, and sat down on the dispensary stool like a woman preparing for triage.

"Hollis talked to you," she said.

"How did you—"

"Because you look exactly how I looked when they gave me the dispatches to steal." She nodded at the book. "The bad names."

"Nineteen of them."

"Mm." She took off her glasses, cleaned them on her sleeve, put them back — her entire repertoire of emotional management in one gesture. "Are you going to burn it now?"

"That's the joke, isn't it. A month ago I'd have said nothing could make me burn it. Now—" I pressed my thumb against the cover until the leather dented. "Now I have excellent reasons and no idea which of them are cowardice."

"It wouldn't be cowardice," Pryce said, surprising me. "Wanting to burn it. After what it cost. Finn. Vane." Her voice caught slightly on his name; she covered it with efficiency. "Anyone would forgive you."

"But?"

"But you won't." She said it flatly, certainly, the way she reported the weather or the casualty figures. "Because you're you. Because if you burn it, then every erased man stays erased, including the ones who deserved better, and the machinery wins the argument it's been having with you for twelve years. Because—" she hesitated, and then committed, as she does, "—because you taught me that loyalty isn't to people's reputations. It's to what actually happened. Even when what actually happened is shameful. *Especially* then."

"When did I teach you that?"

"The night I left the ambulance keys on your cot." She stood up, straightened a bandage tin that didn't need straightening. "I decided then that if you went down, I'd go down accurately. Not nobly. Accurately. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

She left me with that, and the light coming up grey over the yard, and the ledger on my knees.

* * *

What I did next, I did slowly, and I will describe it honestly because honesty is apparently the discipline I've chosen for whatever remains of my life.

I did not burn it.

I took out a fresh notebook — plain, grey, unloved — and I began again. Not copying. Amending. Beside each of the nineteen names, in my same cramped hand, I wrote what the commission had given me: the crime, the court, the sentence. Not to excuse the erasure — the erasure was still an erasure, still a wound dealt to families who deserved truth — but to make the record whole. To let each name stand in full, the way a body stands in full, wounded *and* diseased, treated anyway.

It took four days. On the second day Tomas sat down beside me uninvited, read a few lines upside down as children do, and frowned.

"That man was bad," he said, pointing.

"Yes."

"And he's in the book with the good ones."

"Yes."

Tomas considered this with the terrible seriousness of eight-year-olds, who have not yet learned to look away from hard things. Then he said, "Papa said names aren't the same as the men. He said a name is what you owe the living." A pause. "He said that about the bad ones too. There were bad ones on his list."

Of course there were. Of course Elias — meticulous, moral, maddening Elias — had known this already. Had probably sat in some hiding place with his son memorizing names, doing exactly this arithmetic, arriving exactly where I was arriving: that witness is not endorsement, that memory is not mercy, that the debt of truth is owed regardless of the debtor's character.

A name is what you owe the living.

I wrote that on the inside cover of the new book. Underneath it, because I am still me, I added: *(and the dead can look after themselves).*

* * *

On the last evening of November, the commission released the amended findings publicly. Newspapers got hold of the friendly-fire pattern within hours; there were speeches, editorials, a bishop said something about national cleansing that made Pryce snort into her tea. Families wrote to us. That was the part no one had prepared me for — letters, dozens then hundreds, from wives and mothers and sons asking after names in my ledger. *Can you tell us where he fell. Can you tell us if he spoke. Did anyone hold his hand.*

For the nineteen, the letters cut differently. One woman wrote to ask whether her husband — Sergeant Mercer — had suffered, and whether he had been afraid, and whether he had thought of her. I sat with that letter for two evenings before I could answer it. I could tell her he was funny and generous with cigarettes and gentle with boys. I could not tell her about the pay chest and the three drowned men, though the public record soon would, whether I wrote it or not. In the end I answered what was true and kind and left the rest to the machinery, which would grind its grinding. I am not sure that was right. I am sure it was human, and I am learning — slowly, expensively — that these are not the same virtue, but neither are they enemies.

Finn found me sealing the envelope. He read the name on it, raised his eyebrows, said nothing for a long moment.

"You know," he said, "most people, offered the chance to curate history, leave out the embarrassing bits."

"I'm not most people. I'm a woman with a filing system."

"You're a woman with a conscience and a fountain pen. Terrifying combination." He picked up the ledger — the old one, the original, now formally an exhibit of the tribunal and returned to me with an archivist's receipt I intend to frame. "What will you do with it? Retire it?"

"No," I said. "It's not finished."

He looked at me.

"There will be more names," I said. "There are always more names. Armistice isn't the end of omission; it's just the end of this war's version. Somewhere tonight a clerk is striking a line and a nurse is noticing." I took the book back from him and held it. "Besides. There's one entry still incomplete. Tomas wants to write it himself, and he says he needs a harbor to write it in. He says—" and here something in my chest turned over, warm and frightening, "—he says his father always promised him the sea."

Finn smiled slowly. "Harbor town, then."

"Pryce knows a place," I said. "She's been carrying a map of it in her kit for three weeks and thinks I haven't noticed."

That night I packed the ledger last, wrapped in oilcloth, and lay awake listening to the wind come off the flats, and thought about what names cost. They cost twelve years of hiding. They cost Finn's arrest and Vane's life and Pryce's clean certainty about the world. And they cost this, too, which I had not anticipated: they cost me the luxury of remembering only what comforts me. From tonight forward, every name in my keeping comes with its whole truth attached, and I must carry all of it, the redeemed and the damned together, because a half-truth is just a prettier kind of omission.

I was almost asleep when Tomas appeared in the doorway, dragging his blanket, hair standing up like a startled hedge.

"I can't sleep," he announced. "Tell me a name. A good one."

"Elias Vane," I said. "Captain. Believed in bridges."

"Tell me another."

"Aldous Reeve. Private. His mother's name was Margaret, and she waited for him nine years."

Tomas was quiet a moment. "Was he good?"

"He was a man," I said. "We're remembering him anyway."

He thought about that, the way he thinks about everything — completely, dangerously. Then he nodded, satisfied, and padded back to bed, and I lay in the dark understanding that the boy had just accepted the whole weight of the thing in four words, faster than I had managed in twelve years, and that tomorrow we would need to talk about harbors, and trains, and beginnings — because Pryce had spread her map on the kitchen table that evening, tapped a small coastal town with her finger, and said, in the voice she uses for facts that have already decided themselves:

"This is where we're going. All of us. And Hazel — you'll want to see what the ground floor of that house by the water could become."
