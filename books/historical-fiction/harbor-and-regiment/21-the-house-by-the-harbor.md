## 21. The House by the Harbor

The house smelled of salt and fresh paint, which I decided was the correct smell for a new life. Old lives smelled like gun oil and wet wool. New ones, apparently, smelled like someone's idea of a color — the paint was a blue so pale it was barely a decision at all, and Hazel had stood in front of it in the shop arguing with herself for ten minutes before buying four tins.

"It's called Harbor Mist," said the man behind the counter.

"Then I suppose it's decided," Hazel said, and gave me a look over my head that said, *don't you dare laugh*, which of course made me laugh.

That was eight months after the armistice, and three weeks after we arrived in Grayhaven, which is the kind of town that exists mostly so ships have somewhere to be from. It had one street worth calling a street, two churches that disagreed about everything except the time, and a harbor full of boats leaning against their moorings like old men waiting for a bus that stopped running during the war.

The house sat on the low hill above all of it, three stories, gray shingles gone silver, with a sign out front that Finn painted himself: **BLACKWOOD HOUSE — CONVALESCENCE**. Underneath, in smaller letters he insisted on adding without asking anyone, *All Welcome*. Pryce wanted to add *By Arrangement*. Hazel overruled her. Finn's version stayed.

I was nine now. Nine and a half, if you counted the way I counted things, which was carefully.

* * *

You should understand something about the months after the tribunal, because nobody talks about them properly, and I have decided it is my job to talk about things nobody talks about. That is, I suppose, the family business.

We did not go straight to Grayhaven. First there were weeks of hearings — I sat outside rooms on wooden benches while adults went in to say true things to men who wrote them down and then, sometimes, did nothing with them. I learned to read faces through doors. I learned that justice makes a sound like a pen scratching, and that the scratching stops a great deal sooner than you want it to.

Hazel testified twice. The second time she read from the ledger aloud — names, ranks, dates, the small dry facts of people who had been subtracted from the world as if they'd never been added. She read for an hour and eleven minutes. Pryce timed it on her watch because Pryce times everything; it is how she loves people, in minutes and seconds. Afterward Hazel came out into the corridor with her voice worn down to gravel, and I gave her the apple I'd been saving, and she ate it all, core and stem, which is how I knew she'd needed it more than she'd admitted.

They cleared my father posthumously. That is the official phrase — *posthumously*, which means "after he was dead," which means the apology arrived at an address where no one lived. A colonel who had never met him shook my hand and told me my father served with honor, and I thanked him, because I was raised to be polite, and then I went around the corner of the courthouse and was sick behind a hedge, because politeness has its limits.

Finn was exonerated too, though the army spelled his name wrong on the discharge papers — *Calloway* with one L — and Finn framed the document anyway and hung it in the kitchen of Blackwood House, because, he says, "It's important to keep evidence that they can't even spell your name when they ruin your life. Keeps a man humble."

Pryce did not face a tribunal for stealing the colonel's dispatches. What happened instead was quieter and worse: she was offered a transfer to a garrison posting, a promotion, a clean career with clean paperwork, if she would simply go where no one had to look at her. She resigned her sergeant's stripes in a letter of exactly forty words. I know because she let me count them once, late at night, when she thought I was asleep and I was not asleep, and she read it to me by candlelight in a voice that didn't shake until the last word, which was *duty*, and even then it only shook a little.

So that was our regiment: a nurse with a ledger, a sergeant without a sergeant's stripes, an orderly with a misspelled name, and me. Two other orderlies, Marta and Okafor, had come south with us at first, but Marta had a sister in the midlands and Okafor wanted to study medicine somewhere with actual winter, and they left in stages, the way people do, promising letters and keeping the promise better than most.

And then there was the house.

* * *

Hazel found it before any of us knew she was looking. That is Hazel's method: decide silently, act decisively, inform everyone afterward, absorb complaints with the patience of a stone wall absorbing weather.

"A convalescent home," Pryce repeated, on the train down. "For whom?"

"Soldiers who don't fit anywhere else," Hazel said. "Men whose papers are wrong. Men who were erased and then un-erased and don't know how to be written back in."

"That's not a category the Ministry recognizes."

"The Ministry recognizes very little. That's rather the point."

I watched the coast come up out of the train window — gray water, white gulls wheeling like scraps of paper thrown by a giant, and the town huddled at the harbor's edge with its chimneys smoking — and I remember thinking, with the strange authority of a nine-year-old, *this will do.* I had spent my whole remembered life in places that were temporary: aid stations, cellars, the back of an ambulance, a farmhouse cellar where six people crowded around a book of names. I wanted a place with a floorboard I could lift and call mine.

There are no secrets under the floorboards here. Finn checked them all the first week, out of habit, and found only a dead mouse and a coin from a country that no longer existed. He kept both. The mouse is buried in the garden with full honors; the coin hangs on a nail above the kitchen door, for luck, or for memory, or for whatever reason Finn keeps things, which is all of them at once.

* * *

The first residents arrived in October. Three men, then five, then eleven. Some could not sleep indoors. One, a gunner named Whitlock, could not bear the sound of gulls and would not tell anyone why, and Hazel never asked, only arranged for him to work in the vegetable garden at the far end of the property where the birds were fewer, and by spring he was feeding them crumbs off the kitchen step, which is the sort of ending wars almost never give anyone, so I like to write it down when I find one.

My jobs were real jobs, I'll have you know. I carried linens. I read aloud to men whose eyes had been bandaged — I got fast at voices, doing the officers pompous and the privates stubborn, because a story is better when the private wins. I learned to make poultices under Hazel's instruction and to count a pulse under Pryce's, and each of them taught me their own way: Hazel taught me that healing is mostly attention wearing an apron. Pryce taught me that if you write the number down, the number becomes true, and truth is a thing you can hold.

I did not heal quickly, whatever the pamphlets say about children being resilient. Children are not resilient. Children are sponges, and what we soak up we wring out slowly, over years, in drawings and nightmares and sudden silences at dinner tables. For a long time I still woke at the sound of engines. For a longer time I still drew the bridge — the mill bridge, black water under it, my father standing on it in the freezing dark, holding the crossing so we could run. I drew it smaller every month. By spring the drawing fit in my pocket. By summer I stopped needing to draw it at all, because I understood something I couldn't have explained a year earlier: the bridge wasn't going anywhere. It lived in me now, permanent as a scar, and scars do not require daily maintenance.

He died on that bridge so that I could carry his list instead of him. I have tried to be worth the carrying.

* * *

The ledger lived in the kitchen now, in the drawer under the bread board, which everyone pretended was a coincidence and no one pretended very hard. It had grown fat since the cellar. Pryce had contributed names she'd copied from regimental records before she resigned — thirty-one of them, in her upright hand, each with a note in the margin like *no next of kin listed* or *medal revoked without cause*. Finn added four men from his own company, including his best friend Denny Ash, "which is a terrible name for a man who died in a fire," Finn said, "and I've decided to find it funny rather than otherwise, because otherwise is unbearable." Marta sent names by post. Okafor sent names by post. Even Whitlock, when he learned what the book was, asked shyly if there might be room for a cousin of his, lost at some unnamed front, and Hazel gave him the pen and pulled up a chair, and he wrote for a long time.

Everyone in the house knew about the ledger eventually. That was the difference between the war and this: there, the names had to be hidden under floorboards, kept in the dark, carried alone. Here they lay in a kitchen drawer among the string and the spare candles, and sometimes a resident would take it out just to look, the way you'd look at a photograph album, and no one arrested anybody.

But one entry had never been made.

I noticed it the way children notice absences — completely and wordlessly. The book held hundreds of names, strangers' names, my father's *witnesses*, the men whose positions had been sold, the companies gutted for other men's promotions. But nowhere, in any hand, was written:

**VANE, ELIAS THOMAS — CAPTAIN —**

Nothing. My father, the reason for the whole book, was the one man missing from it.

When I finally worked out why, I felt stupid for not seeing it sooner. Hazel hadn't left him out because she'd forgotten him. She'd left him out because writing him down meant admitting the book was finished with him — that he had become a name among names, an entry, a record. And she could not do it. And neither could I. And Pryce, who notices everything, had noticed, and said nothing, which is its own kind of love.

* * *

In June, on a Sunday with the harbor so calm it looked painted, Hazel took down the ledger, set it on the kitchen table, and put the pen beside it.

"I've been avoiding something," she said to me, in front of everyone, because Hazel does not believe in doing hard things privately if doing them publicly might make them lighter for somebody else. "It's rude of me, and it's been rude of me for months, and I'm sorry."

She turned the book to a blank page.

"He should be in here. Not as a case. Not as a correction. As himself." Her voice did something complicated in the middle of the sentence and recovered. "But it shouldn't be my hand that writes him. Or yours, if you're not ready. It's yours to give or withhold, Tomas. Now and always."

I looked at the page. I looked at the pen. Finn had gone very quiet, which for Finn meant he'd gone very serious. Pryce stood by the window with her arms folded, watching the harbor but seeing none of it.

Here is what I knew about my father's full name, because he made me memorize it along with everything else, on the long cold walks between safe houses, reciting names like prayers: **Elias Thomas Vane**. Captain. Fourth Regiment, though the Fourth had struck him and the strike had been struck and the paper trail now ran clean, thanks to a stolen dispatch case and a nurse with a stubborn heart. Born in a river town I have never seen. Liked bad poetry and good apples. Pulled a nineteen-year-old nurse out of a burning aid station twelve years ago and made her promise a thing that took twelve years to keep.

And I knew what I wanted the entry to say, which was not what the army's restored record said. The army said *exonerated*. The ledger should say something truer.

I picked up the pen. My hand did not shake, which surprised me. I printed, because my cursive was still a scandal:

**Elias Thomas Vane. Captain. He kept his promise.**

I thought about it. Then, underneath, in smaller letters:

**Father of Tomas. Friend of Hazel. Remembered by all of us.**

"Read it back," Hazel said, and her voice had gone to gravel again.

I read it back.

Finn blew his nose enormously. Pryce turned from the window and said, "Spelling's correct," which from Pryce was a eulogy, and Hazel laughed and cried at the same time, which I had seen before but never at such close range, and it turns out it looks mostly like ordinary crying, only with the corners of the mouth fighting back.

We did not bury the book under a floorboard. We put it back in the kitchen drawer, under the bread board, among the string and the candles, where it belongs. Where anything can happen to it. Where everyone knows it is.

That night I lifted my own floorboard — the one in my room, third from the window, chosen for reasons I decline to explain — and found nothing under it, and put nothing under it, and pressed the board back down flat. Some houses you hide things from. This one I decided to live in the open of.

* * *

Except.

I want to be honest, because honesty is the household religion, so I will end this part with the thing I have not told anyone yet, not even Hazel.

Last week a letter came to Blackwood House, forwarded twice, the envelope soft from travel. It was addressed to *the matron, the convalescent home by the harbor, Grayhaven* — no name, as if the sender hoped vagueness might slip past whoever decides which letters get opened.

I brought it in from the box because bringing in the post is my job, and I set it on the table with the others, and it sat there through supper looking heavier than paper ought to look. Afterward Hazel opened it, read it once, and went to stand at the window for a long time with her back to the room.

"What is it?" Pryce asked, in the voice she uses when she already suspects.

Hazel handed her the single page without turning around. Pryce read it. Then she read it again. Then she looked at me — not at Hazel, at me — with an expression I had never seen on her face before, and I am fluent in Pryce's face.

"Well," she said. "Tomas. It seems someone remembers *your* father too. Someone who served under him. Someone who says—" and here she stopped, and folded the letter, and finished in a different voice entirely, "—someone who says Elias Vane told them, before the end, where he had hidden something. And they're asking whether we might all come and see."

Tomorrow, I think, we are going to need the ambulance keys again.
