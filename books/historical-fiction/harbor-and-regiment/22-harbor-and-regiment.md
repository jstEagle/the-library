## 22. Harbor and Regiment

The harbor does not care that it is the anniversary of the armistice. That is, I think, why I have come to love it.

Tide comes in, tide goes out, and it has never once asked anyone's rank or regiment. It takes the fishing boats out in the morning and gives them back in the evening, minus whatever the sea has decided to keep, and no one files a morning report about it. The gulls argue over herring guts with more honesty than any tribunal I ever stood before. If there is a God of harbors — and after the war I am prepared to believe in stranger things — He has never struck a single name from His books.

It was Tomas who suggested we read the ledger here.

"Not in the house," he'd said, a month ago, sitting on our front steps with his legs swinging through the railing like a small clockwork mechanism wound slightly too fast, as he always is. "In town. Where people can hear."

"People don't want to hear names," I told him. "People want to hear bands."

"Then let them have both," said Tomas, who at ten years old has already learned to outflank me in argument the way his father once did, and I surrendered as I always do, and pretended afterward that I hadn't wanted to be defeated.

So it is that on this bright, cold morning one year to the day since the pens scratched and the cannons went quiet, I am standing on the fish-market steps at the head of the harbor with a ledger bound in oiled canvas held against my chest like something between a newborn and an accusation, while three streets up, in the square, the regimental band is tuning itself into existence.

* * *

I should explain how the ledger sounds now, because it has changed.

For twelve years of war it was my voice alone in it. I wrote at night by candle-stub, kneeling on ward floors, whispering the names under my breath as I inked them so they would exist in two places at once — paper and air. Private Alfred Dunn, Third Company, omitted from the morning report of the fourteenth. Corporal Reyes, who died of his wounds two days before a promotion that would have meant his mother drew a pension. The boy at Passel who had lied about his age and whose officers preferred not to admit they'd believed him.

One voice. One keeper. That was the whole architecture of the thing: a secret is only strong if it is alone.

And then, in that farmhouse cellar in the last weeks, everything changed. Finn added the orderlies he'd watched buried without record. Pryce, my Imogen, rule-keeper to her bones, took the pen in her hand as if it were hot from a fire and wrote four names — men from her first posting whom she had reported missing properly, officially, and been told to stop asking about. Her hand shook. She hated every second of it. She has since told me, several times and with increasing firmness, that she only did it because she was cold, and I have since stopped arguing, because we both know better.

Even the other two orderlies who came to us that winter — Marta and Osei — bent over those pages. And then Tomas, small and grave in the lamplight, recited his father's list from memory and made me write each name down in Elias Vane's own order, because, he said, *the order matters, Papa memorized it in order.*

A ledger kept by one person is a secret. A ledger kept by six is a testimony. A ledger kept by six and read aloud in public once a year is — I don't know what it is yet. We are finding out. Perhaps it is the beginning of the thing the tribunals were supposed to give us and only partly did: not justice exactly, but company.

* * *

The band strikes up at ten. I can hear them from the steps — brass warming, a drum testing its own pulse. They are playing the regimental march, the one Ashworth used to hum during inspections while boys with fresh amputations tried not to be heard crying behind him. I notice the thought arrive and let it pass through me without stopping for tea. That is what the last year has taught me to do. Not forgiveness. Passage.

Ashworth himself is two years into a cell in the capital, writing appeals in beautiful copperplate handwriting. His signature appeared beside enough omitted names to hang him twice over; instead they gave him prison and process, which is what civilization substitutes when it has run out of stomach for rope. Some days I am satisfied by this. Other days I remember that the officers who sold their positions mostly retired on pensions, and I take the ledger down from its shelf and read a few pages aloud to an empty room, just to keep the scales honest. Pryce says this is morbid. Pryce says many things. Pryce also keeps the ledger's duplicate copy locked in her footlocker, and checks it weekly for damp, so I feel entitled to disregard her editorial opinions entirely.

Finn is exonerated, restored, promoted — orderly to charge nurse's assistant, which is a real rank with a real stripe that he polishes obsessively and pretends not to care about. Marta runs our kitchen at the convalescent home the way a quartermaster runs an army, which is to say: ruthlessly, generously, and with total contempt for anyone who wastes bread. Osei drives the ambulance now. He drove it through last winter's floods to fetch a fisherman with a crushed hand, and the man lived, and Osei said afterward, quietly, "That's one for the good column," and I have thought about that sentence more times than I can count.

And the house itself — the House by the Harbor, Tomas named it, because he was nine and could get away with sentiment — fills and empties like the tide. Men come to us half-broken and leave whole, or leave less broken, or stay. There is a veteran called Whitlock who has slept in our east room for eight months and repainted all the shutters and will not discuss leaving, and nobody makes him. This, too, is a kind of ledger. Every household is. You keep the names you keep.

* * *

At quarter past ten, the crowd begins.

I would like to report that the whole town turned out. I would prefer to report that. But honesty is the entire point of the exercise, so: perhaps forty people gather near the market steps, most of them ours — Pryce in her best uniform coat with the new sergeant-major's insignia she earned in the reorganization and still cannot quite believe fits her; Finn holding a folding chair for old Mrs. Aldous from the cannery, whose son is in the ledger, page nine; Marta with her arms crossed and her face arranged into skepticism, which is how she expresses support. A handful of others drift close out of curiosity. Two fishermen remove their caps. A woman with a pram stops and stays.

Forty souls. The band draws a few hundred up in the square.

I am not wounded by this arithmetic. I want to say that plainly, because a younger Hazel — the Hazel who knelt on ward floors twelve years ago — would have been wounded, would have seen forty listeners beside three hundred band-lovers as proof the silences would win. But I have learned what the younger Hazel didn't know: you do not read names to win. You read them because reading is what the dead are owed, and what the living require, and both of those facts hold regardless of attendance.

Besides. The band is playing, isn't it? The institution and the individuals, side by side at last, whether the institution knows it or not.

Pryce stands beside me on the step. "Nervous?" she asks.

"I've been shot at," I say. "This is worse."

"You've done this every month for a year."

"And been nervous every month. Consistency is a virtue."

She almost smiles. From Pryce this is a decoration, and I wear it accordingly.

Tomas goes up the steps ahead of me. He insisted on carrying the ledger's ribbon-marker, and he wears his father's old watch, far too big for his wrist, strapped on with two extra holes punched in the leather. He stands at the top, turns to face the small crowd, and waits with a composure that breaks my heart in the specific place reserved for sons of brave men.

He nods to me. I open the book.

* * *

I will not transcribe the reading. It takes nearly two hours, and prose should not attempt what the wind and the gulls and forty quiet people made of it. But I will tell you what it contains, this year, because the ledger grew again this spring.

It contains seven hundred and eleven names. Eleven were added since the tribunal — sent to us by letter, mostly, from nurses and clerks and mothers across three provinces who heard about "Blackwood's book" and wrote to us the way drowning people once wrote to lighthouses. *My brother was left off the rolls of the Second Engineers.* *My husband's company was disbanded before his death could be recorded.* Each letter gets an answer, each name gets verified as best we can, and each gets a page. The ledger has become less a weapon than a harbor itself, I suppose. Things come in wrecked; we log them; they rest.

It contains, near the end, in fresh ink, the full name restored: Captain Elias Vane, Eleventh Rifles, cleared posthumously by order of the Armistice Commission, father of Tomas. Tomas reads that page himself each time. He does not cry at it anymore. He reads it the way you'd set a table for someone expected home — matter-of-fact, faithful. I think this is what healing looks like when it is done honestly: not forgetting, not dramatic grief, but setting the table anyway.

It contains, and this is the part that costs me, four names of men the tribunals proved to be guilty of their own atrocities — men whose omission from official records was not an injustice to them but an embarrassment to their officers, and who therefore landed in my book through no virtue of their own. For a long while I considered striking them. Pryce argued for it, actually — the only argument we have ever had where she took the harder line and I the softer, and we have not spoken of the oddness of that. But I kept them. Because my promise was never that the ledger would contain only the innocent. My promise was that no one would be erased. What the dead did with their lives is between them and whatever harbor receives us all; what I owe them is only that they happened, and that no clerk's pen un-happened them. Truth is not a reward dispensed to the deserving. It is a debt paid to everyone.

Mrs. Aldous weeps quietly through page nine, and Finn holds her chair steady, and the band, three streets away, swings from the march into something slower and sweeter, some folk tune arranged for brass, and for a moment the music and the names lie down together like soldiers and civilians in a barn, unequal but willing.

* * *

Near the end, something happens that I did not plan.

The woman with the pram steps forward while I pause for breath, and says, shyly, terribly: "There's a name. My uncle. He drowned in the training accident at Cape Ross, and they never — the navy said it was classified—"

And I look at her, and I look at the ledger, and I understand in one clean stroke what Tomas saw a month ago on the front steps.

"We'll need his full name," I say. "And his unit, if you know it. And anything you can tell me about him. What he looked like. What he loved. A ledger entry should be more than a rank, if we can manage it."

She talks, and I write, right there on the steps, balancing the book on my knee, while the band plays and the tide turns and Mrs. Aldous dabs her eyes and Tomas watches me with his father's level gray gaze. Name number seven hundred and twelve, entered in public, witnessed by forty strangers, requested by family. Not erased. Not secret. Just — remembered, the ordinary way, the way towns used to do it before wars got efficient about dying.

Afterward Pryce helps me down the steps and says, low, "You realize this means next year there'll be eighty people. Then two hundred. You've started something you can't stop."

"Good," I say. "I've spent my whole life stopping things. It's restful to start one."

* * *

We walk home along the seawall in the late light, all six of us and Whitlock besides, and the band plays on behind us in the square until the tunes blur into the sound of the water, which is, I suspect, how it should always be — the regiment and the harbor keeping each other honest.

Tomas walks between me and Pryce, holding one hand of each, swinging slightly, the watch sliding up and down his thin arm. Halfway along the wall he stops.

"Hazel. When I'm grown. Can I keep the book?"

I stop walking. Pryce, bless her flinty heart, does not intervene; she looks out at the water and lets the moment belong to the two of us.

"The book isn't mine to give," I say carefully. "It belongs to everyone in it."

"I know," Tomas says, with the mild impatience of the young for the slow. "That's why it should be me. I'm *in* it. Papa's in it. I know all the names already — the old ones, Papa's list. And—" he hesitates, and for a second he is eight years old again, standing at a hospital door with orders hanging over him to be forgotten — "nobody ever struck my name from anywhere. So I'd know exactly what I was keeping."

The tide breathes. Somewhere behind us the band finishes its last number and the applause scatters across the rooftops like gulls.

"Yes," I say. "When you're grown. And until then, we keep it together. That's the arrangement. Shared custody of silences."

He considers this, nods once, business concluded, and tugs us onward toward the lit windows of the house.

And I close the ledger — I closed it back on the steps, after the last name, and I carry it closed under my arm now, and I will carry it closed tomorrow — and I want to set down here, at the end of this account, what closing it means, because I suspect someone someday will find these pages and wonder.

Closing it does not mean the silences are over. Wars are not finished by signatures; they leak for generations, and somewhere tonight some clerk is omitting somebody, and somewhere a child is being told to forget a face. The book will never truly be full. I have made my peace with that, which is not the same as liking it.

No. Closing it means this: the names are no longer carried alone. Seven hundred and twelve people remembered by six keepers and growing; a boy who will inherit the keeping; a friend who guards the duplicate; a household that sets tables for the absent. A silence shared is not a silence anymore. It is a story, and stories, unlike secrets, survive being passed from hand to hand.

The lights of the house come up ahead of us. Marta is cooking something that smells like the opposite of rationing. Whitlock has lit the lamps. The harbor works its ancient arithmetic below — taking, giving back, keeping its own silent books — and the band plays one encore to nobody in particular, and I go inside, and I set the ledger on its shelf between the medical texts where it lives, spine out, unhidden, waiting for whoever opens it next.

It occurs to me, as I take down my coat, that I have not written in my own name in that book. Hazel Blackwood, kept the records, no omission, no erasure, nothing struck. There would be no reason to.

But standing in the doorway with the smell of salt and supper and lamp oil around me, listening to my strange, assembled, improbable family argue about potatoes in the kitchen, I find myself thinking that maybe there should be one final entry after all — not because I was ever forgotten, but because belonging, I have learned, is not something you wait to be granted.

I take the book down one more time.
