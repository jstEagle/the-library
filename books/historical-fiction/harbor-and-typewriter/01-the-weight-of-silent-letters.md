## 1. The Weight of Silent Letters

The type cases woke before I did, the way they always did. I came down the stairs at half past four with a lamp in one hand and my father's old apron over my arm, and the shop greeted me in its own language—the smell of ink and oil, the faint metallic breath of ten thousand lead letters sleeping in their compartments, each one face-up, each one waiting to be chosen or left behind.

I had learned the alphabet twice in my life. The first time was at my mother's knee, chalk on slate, the ordinary letters of ordinary words. The second time was here, at seven years old, standing on a stool so I could reach the upper cases, and my father had taught me that a compositor learns letters backward and mirrored, that a printer reads the world in reverse. *You will spend your life, Astrid,* he said, *holding words up to a mirror before anyone else can see them true.* He said it like a benediction. He did not say it like a warning, though it became one.

I lit the lamp over the sorting bench and began.

Sorting type is called "distributing," and it is the least glamorous labor in the trade and the only one I have ever found truly peaceful. You take the jumbled letters from yesterday's forms—letters that gave their shapes to words and were then broken apart again—and you return each one to its proper cell. A-aa goes to the upper-left. B-b below it. The em-quads in their wide compartment at the bottom, where they lie like paving stones. My fingers knew the map without consulting my eyes, so my eyes were free to look at the window, at the gray coming up over the rooftops of Vellamare, at the harbor cranes just beginning to show themselves as black strokes against a paler black.

Seven years since the Provisional Committee had taken the city. Seven years since my father's name had been read aloud from a list in Customs Square and then, three weeks later, erased from every document in Vellamare—his trade license, his tax rolls, the inscription on his own shopfront, which men with chisels came to scrape away while I watched from an upstairs window. *Reyes* had become a word the city was not permitted to remember. The sign above my door now read HALDAN & CO., STATIONERS, because Haldan had never existed either; Haldan was a name I had invented out of whole cloth and paid a clerk to enter into the register, a ghost given papers so that a real man's daughter could go on selling broadsheets.

That was the arithmetic of survival in Vellamare: you subtracted yourself carefully, piece by piece, and filled the space with something dull enough to be ignored.

I heard Mira on the stairs before I saw her—she still moved like a girl her age, all elbows and hurry, though she tried hard to learn the shop's silence. Sixteen now. She had been eleven when her mother died of the fever and I had taken her in mostly because she could carry water and count change, and partly because the shop had felt too quiet with only me breathing in it. She came into the lamplight with her hair braided badly and her collar askew, and she stopped when she saw me at the case.

"You're doing the small letters," she said. "You always leave the small letters for me."

"I'm doing the letters that matter." I held up a six-pointed minuscule between thumb and forefinger, a little sliver of lead no bigger than a nail paring. "This one printed half of yesterday's proclamation. Every *i* in 'compliance.' There were forty-one of them."

"Forty-one *i*'s and not one honest word." Mira dropped onto the stool at the stone sink and began washing her hands in cold water, wincing. She'd inherited her mother's bluntness along with her hands, and there were days I flinched at both. "Astrid. Why do we print it if it's all lies?"

"Mira."

"I know. I know." She recited it flatly, the catechism I had drilled into her since her first week: *We print what is approved. We do not read what we print. We do not keep copies. We do not speak of the difference between what is printed and what is true.* "It's just—" She wrung out her rag. "Yesterday's proclamation said the grain ships arrived full."

"And did they?"

"No. Petrus the factor told my friend Anouk they came in a third light and half of it weevil-eaten, and the Committee's man made him sign a paper saying otherwise anyway." She looked up at me, and in the lamplight her face was very young and very angry, and I recognized the expression because I had worn it once myself, at her age, before I had learned to wear other things over it. "So we set the type that says the ships arrived full. We make forty-one *i*'s in a row like beads on a string, all of them saying nothing."

I put the letter back in its cell. It made a small sound, lead against wood, like a seed dropping into soil.

"The alternative," I said, "is that we don't set the type, and someone else does, and we are on a list."

"We might already be on a list."

"Yes," I said. "But at the bottom of it. There is an art to being at the bottom of a list, Mira, and it has kept us alive, and one day perhaps you'll thank me for teaching it." I did not entirely believe the last part, but I said it anyway, because some sentences you print even when your own heart refuses the proof.

She didn't answer. Outside, the bell on the harbor gate rang the half hour, and somewhere down Salt Lane a fish cart rattled over cobbles, and the ordinary morning of an occupied city assembled itself around us piece by piece, like type in a form.

* * *

Inspector Doss arrived at nine o'clock sharp, which was his habit, and habits are the most legible text in any city. I heard him before I saw him too: the particular cadence of his boots, two quick and one dragging—he had an old injury he would not discuss—and the small clearing of his throat outside the door that was, I suspected, meant as courtesy and came out as menace.

Mira straightened her collar. I tied my father's apron. And then I opened the door with the expression I kept for inspections, which is to say no expression at all, polished smooth, a page with nothing set upon it.

"Madam Haldan." Doss touched the brim of his cap. He was a narrow man, gray as newsprint, with the pale, squinting eyes of someone who had spent years reading things closely and had come to dislike what reading showed him. "Committee business. Routine."

"Of course, Inspector. Will you take tea?"

He always refused, and I always asked, and this exchange—like the half-hour bell, like the drag in his step—was part of the liturgy. He stepped past me into the shop and stood a moment in the middle of the floor, turning slowly, the way a man turns who is memorizing a room. I let him. There was nothing to memorize that he had not memorized a hundred times before: the two presses, the imposing stone, the cases ranked along the wall, the drying lines hung with yesterday's proclamations fluttering like laundry. A shop that printed only what it was told to print is the most transparent building in the world. Its transparency is its license.

What Doss did not know—or I prayed nightly he did not know, and by day behaved as if he could not—was that transparency can be manufactured, the way a false bottom is manufactured, the way a name like Haldan is manufactured.

"This morning's work?" he asked, nodding at the press.

"The harbor schedule," I said. "Tide tables, cargo declarations. And the Committee's notice about the Victory Festival preparations." I had it ready for him, a proof sheet pulled an hour early, the ink still smelling faintly of pitch and lampblack. He took it, angled it toward the window, and read it through. I watched him read. That is another thing my father taught me: *watch the censor read, and you will learn more from his eyebrows than from any edict.*

His eyebrows did not move. Approved content, competently set. He laid the sheet down on the stone.

"The Committee wishes me to remind you," he said, "that with the Festival approaching, output may increase. Historical materials. Commemorative materials."

"We're honored to serve."

"There may also be—" and here he paused, and the pause was unusual, and I felt it land on the shop floor like a misprint on a clean page, "—revisions. Of certain older accounts. The Uprising especially. A definitive edition is being prepared."

"Revisions," I repeated, keeping my voice level, a flat surface. Seven years ago I had watched the Harbor Uprising from the upper windows of this shop. I had watched fishermen and dockers and fishwives march on the Custom House under the old regime's guns, and I had watched them win, and the city had sung for three days, and my father had printed the victory bulletins free of charge until the soldiers came for him—not the old regime's soldiers, but the new ones, the Committee's, whose first quarrel was not with tyrants but with witnesses. I had seen what happened at the north quay afterward, in the confusion the histories now declined to mention. I had seen it with these eyes and I had spent seven years arranging my entire soul around the act of not having seen it.

"A definitive edition," I said, "will be an honor to print."

Doss looked at me a moment longer than the sentence required. Then, abruptly, he turned to Mira, who was sorting type with the studied invisibility of a girl who had learned her lessons well. "Your apprentice. How long?"

"Five years, Inspector."

"She sets a clean page?"

"She sets a cleaner page than I do."

He grunted, which was the closest Doss ever came to praise, and moved on. He walked the length of the type wall, trailing one gray finger along the case edges the way a customs officer trails a finger for dust. At the far end, near the corner case—the one holding the ornamental borders and the display faces we almost never used, because ornament was suspect and display was vanity—he slowed.

My heartbeat did not speed up. This is a discipline, too. In seven years of inspections I had trained my pulse like a muscle, and it obeyed me now, walking its steady walk while Doss's finger came to rest on the corner case's lid.

"Locked," he observed.

"Ornaments and foreign sorts," I said. "Some of it came with the shop from the previous owner. I've never had occasion to use it. Lead attracts damp; I keep it closed."

"Mm." He lifted the lid.

I want to be exact about those seconds, because exactness is the whole of my trade. He lifted the lid of the corner case and looked down into it, and inside that case lay—so far as Inspector Doss could possibly know—a quantity of decorative borders, fleurons, a few Greek sorts, and a wooden box with a false bottom, and beneath the false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, the first third of something that would have hanged us all. Doss looked for the length of one slow breath. Two. His finger touched the edge of the wooden box without lifting it.

Then he closed the lid.

"Damp," he said, as if confirming my story for me. "Yes, lead attracts it. Keep it locked." He settled his cap, moved toward the door, paused on the threshold with his hand on the frame—that same dragging pause, two beats and a limp. "Madam Haldan. The definitive edition of the Uprising account. When it comes to your shop. You will find it... instructive."

"I'm sure we will, Inspector."

"It corrects certain errors," he said, to the doorframe rather than to me. "Errors that many people in this city have carried a long time. It will be a relief, I think. For everyone." And then he was gone, his boots marking their strange rhythm down Salt Lane, fading into the noise of the fish market waking up.

* * *

For a long moment neither Mira nor I moved. The press ticked as its iron cooled. On the drying lines, the festival notices stirred in a draft from the open door, forty-one *i*'s swaying together like a field of grain.

"He looked right at the corner case," Mira whispered at last. "He opened it."

"He opens it every month."

"He—" She stared at me. "Every month? You let him open it every month?"

"I let him think he's found our one secret, yes." I crossed to the corner case and ran my own hand along its lid, feeling the grain of the wood my father's hands had worn smooth. "A man like Doss needs to find something, Mira. If he finds nothing, he starts looking harder, or worse, he starts wondering why he shouldn't. So we give him the box with the false bottom, and inside the false bottom we keep—" I lifted the wooden box out and opened it, showing her the contents: three confiscated pamphlets from the old regime, a deck of cards with seditious caricatures, a letter in a dead smuggler's handwriting. Contraband, all of it, genuine contraband, chosen years ago to be interesting enough to satisfy and damning enough to explain an arrest should one ever come. A decoy hold. Ballast. "—we keep exactly what a careful inspector expects a careless printer to hide."

Mira looked from the box to my face. "And the real—"

"Is not in this shop," I said, which was true, and also not true, and the distance between those two things was the narrow plank I had walked every day for seven years. "Sweep the stones. We have tide tables to pull by noon."

She went to her work, but slower than usual, and I could feel her looking at me sideways across the shop, recalculating something—me, probably, or the shop, or the shape of the life she had assumed was simple and was discovering, at sixteen, was built over a cellar she had never seen.

I returned to distributing type. The letters clicked into their cells under my fingers, each one going home, and I thought about what Doss had said at the door. *Corrections.* A definitive edition. Revisions of the Uprising, of all things—the one account in Vellamare that mattered, the one story whose true version lived nowhere but in the heads of people like me, aging year by year, unprinted, unprintable, dissolving a little more each winter like ink left in the rain.

There is a loneliness in this trade that I have never managed to explain to anyone who doesn't keep silent for a living. It is not the loneliness of the room—I had Mira, I had the shop's familiar noises, I had Bram arriving at midmorning with his pipe and his grumbles. It is the loneliness of being a mirror that shows everything backward. I held every true word of my life upside down in the type stick and never once got to read it forward. I knew the number of *i*'s in every official lie. I could set "the grain ships arrived full" faster than I could set my own father's name.

By noon the tide tables were pulled and hanging to dry, and Bram had come and gone to fetch paper, and the sun had crossed the yard beyond the window, and I had nearly persuaded myself that the morning was ordinary—that Doss's pause at the door had been only age, that *definitive edition* was only the Committee's vanity, that the plank under my feet was as wide as it had ever been.

Then Mira came in from sweeping the step and stood in the doorway, unnaturally still, her broom forgotten in her hands.

"Astrid," she said. Her voice had gone thin. "There's a child on the doorstep."
