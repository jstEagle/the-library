## 2. A Child at the Door

The knock came before the light did.

Astrid Reyes had been awake for an hour already, as she was most mornings, sitting in the dark of the composing room with a cup of chicory going cold beside her, listening to Vellamare breathe. The harbor had its own grammar of sounds: the groan of hulls against pilings, the winch-creak of the early boats, the gulls arguing over whatever the tide had failed to take. She knew them the way she knew her type cases—by touch, by weight, by which ones were missing. So when a sound arrived that didn't belong, her body understood it before her mind did.

Not a knock, exactly. A thump. Soft. Once.

She set down the cup and did not move for a full minute. Seven years of living under the Provisional Committee had taught her that the first instinct—go to the door, see who it is—was the instinct that got people erased. The second instinct, the one she had drilled into herself like a form set backwards in the chase, was to wait. Count. Listen.

The gulls went on arguing. The harbor kept its rhythm. Nothing else moved on Printer's Row.

She rose anyway. That was the thing about instincts; you could bury them under procedure, but they didn't die. They waited in the dark like letters in a locked case, patient, knowing their turn would come.

* * *

The pre-dawn was the color of wet ash. Astrid opened the shop door only as far as the chain allowed and looked out through the gap at the step where, six hours earlier, she had swept away the Committee's leaflets—the ones announcing the Victory Festival, the ones with the new history already typeset in bold, cheerful letters: *HARMONY RESTORED TO THE HARBOR DISTRICT.*

There was no one in the street. There was a bundle on her step.

Her mind performed its familiar triage in half a second: package, contraband, trap. Packages were left with the night porter at the corner, never on steps—steps were for people who couldn't afford to be seen delivering anything. Contraband meant Odile Ferro, but Odile never left goods unclaimed; she'd sooner sleep in the bilge with her ink barrels than trust a doorstep. Which left trap.

Then the bundle moved, and made a small sound, and every category in her head collapsed at once.

A child. A boy, perhaps five or six years old, asleep—or pretending to be asleep—with his knees drawn up to his chest, wearing a coat too big for him that smelled of tar and cold wool. His hair was dark and fine and stuck to his forehead with sweat. One hand clutched something against his chest, fingers curled white around it even in sleep, the way children hold things when someone has told them *don't let go*.

Astrid stood in the doorway with the chain still fastened and felt seven years of careful silence begin, very quietly, to crack.

Because she knew that face.

Sleeping, tear-streaked, thinner than any child's face ought to be—but she knew it. She had seen it across a crowded square three years ago, and once before that in this very shop, when the woman had come to order two hundred copies of a shipping manifest and had stayed to argue gently, reasonably, devastatingly, about kerning. Elena Voss. Archivist of the Second District. A woman who spoke about records the way Astrid's father used to speak about type—as though they were alive, as though they could be wounded.

Elena Voss had been purged fourteen months ago. Astrid knew because everyone knew, and because knowing was the test: the Committee announced erasures quietly, one line in the daily bulletin, and then watched to see who flinched. *Citizen E. Voss, formerly of the Archive, is to be regarded as having rendered no service to the state.* No service. Fourteen years in the Archive, cataloguing the harbor's births and deaths and shipwrecks, and she had rendered no service. People had repeated the line in the market without inflection, and Astrid had printed it, and both acts had felt identical: like setting a body in type and pulling the lever.

And now Elena Voss's face was asleep on her step, clutching something, breathing in the small ragged rhythm of a child who has learned to cry silently.

"No," Astrid said aloud, to the boy, to the street, to herself. It came out as barely a breath.

She looked left down Printer's Row. Empty. Right, toward the harbor. A lamplighter making his rounds two blocks off, his flame a smudge in the grey. Above her, the shuttered windows of the chandler's widow, who missed nothing and reported most of it.

Every rule she had built her life on arranged itself in her mind with terrible clarity. *You do not open the door. You do not look too long. You go back inside, you wait until the porter's cart comes through at six, and by then the street will have done what streets do with abandoned things.* The Committee did not need informers everywhere. It had made the whole city into one, each citizen the eyes of the next, everyone watching everyone else watch. Survival was a craft, same as printing. You survived by omission. You survived by what you declined to see.

The boy shivered in his sleep. His hand tightened around whatever he held.

Astrid thought of her father—not the way the Committee had let her remember him, the corrected version, the loyal printer who died of fever—but the real way, the forbidden way: Marcus Reyes standing at his case at midnight, inking a page he would never sign, saying, *Astrid, the press doesn't care what's true. That's why the hands have to.*

She unlatched the chain.

Later—and there would be a later, if she was lucky, if she was quick—she would not be able to say whether it was courage. It hadn't felt like courage. It had felt like a seam giving way, like a forme that had been locked too tight finally splitting along its weakest line. Seven years of silence, and the crack ran straight through the middle of her, and what came out through it wasn't bravery. It was just her father's voice, and a child's breath fogging in the cold, and the unbearable arithmetic of a face she recognized being told, by everyone, to stop existing.

She knelt and gathered him up. He weighed almost nothing. Tar-smelling coat, bird-boned shoulders, a heartbeat going fast as a press run beneath her palm.

He woke, and his eyes opened, and they were Elena Voss's eyes exactly—dark, level, older than six years had any right to make them—and Astrid watched the fear arrive in them and braced for the scream that would bring the whole row to its shutters.

It didn't come. He looked at her, and looked past her at the dark doorway of the print shop, and said, in a whisper hoarse from disuse:

"You're supposed to forget my face."

* * *

Inside, she sat him by the stove in the composing room and fed him bread softened in warm milk, and he ate the way children eat when they've been hungry long enough to learn not to show it: steadily, without pleasure, watching her the whole time.

His name, he said, was Tomas. Just Tomas. When she asked about his mother, his jaw set in a way that no six-year-old's jaw should set, and he said, "I'm not supposed to say." When she asked who brought him, he shook his head. When she asked who told him to come here—to *this* door, of all doors in Vellamare—he looked down at his fist, still curled around the thing he'd been holding, and didn't answer at all.

It was the note that answered for him. She found it tucked inside his coat while checking whether the garment was damp enough to give him a fever—a folded scrap of cheap paper, written in a hurried hand that had tried to disguise itself and mostly failed, the letters formed by someone accustomed to official documents trying very hard not to be official:

*Forget the face. Not the name. Keep the name somewhere safe. — A friend of the Archive*

Astrid read it four times. Then she fed it to the stove and watched the words curl and go black, and felt the heat of the flames on her face and understood that she had just committed her first crime in seven years, and that it had taken less than ten minutes, and that some traitorous chamber of her heart had found it *easy*.

"Are you the printer?" Tomas asked.

"I'm the printer."

"My mother said printers keep things." He said it carefully, like a phrase memorized. "Even when other people throw them away."

Astrid's hands went still on the bread knife. Outside, the sky over the harbor was turning from ash to a thin, guilty gold, and soon the row would wake, and the porter's cart would rattle past, and the day's approved work would arrive in its sealed envelope from the Committee, and she would stand at her case and set lies in beautiful, flawless type, and pull the lever, and be invisible, and be safe.

On her worktable lay the galley proofs of yesterday's bulletin. *Harmony Restored to the Harbor District.* And beneath the headline, in the third column, the item she had set without letting her eyes linger: *Citizen T., minor, disposition resolved per directive 44.*

Disposition resolved. She had set those words herself, eleven days ago, in twelve-point Caslon, and had not let herself wonder what they meant, because wondering was the first step off the cliff, and she had spent seven years learning not to look down.

*Tomas,* the note had said. *Keep the name somewhere safe.*

She looked at the boy—at Elena Voss's dark, level eyes looking back at her, waiting, trusting her already, God help them both—and Astrid Reyes felt the last of her careful silence crumble like a badly cast sort under pressure.

"Mira will be here within the hour," she heard herself say. "Mira is my apprentice. She'll want to know your name."

"You're not supposed to ask my name," Tomas said. But he was leaning toward her across the table, and his fist had finally loosened, and in his palm lay a small brass object, green with age: a printer's apostrophe, a piece of punctuation from a case that had been melted down years ago, that Astrid's father had carried in his pocket every day of his life.

Someone had given this child her father's mark, and pointed him at her door, and told him *forget the face.*

Astrid closed her hand around the apostrophe, and the bell above the shop door began, far too early, to ring.
