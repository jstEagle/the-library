## 3. The Committee's New History

The bell had barely finished its second ring when Astrid understood that the morning would not be about the boy at all.

She had eleven seconds—she counted them, a habit from her father, who used to say you could measure any man's intentions by how long he took to cross a shop floor—and in those eleven seconds she did three things with the ease of long practice. She stepped sideways so her body stood between the door and the stairwell. She slid Tomas's apostrophe into the drawer of galleys where she kept the broken type no one was meant to look at. And she said, in the voice she reserved for officials, warm and empty as an unswept room, "Good morning. Reyes and Son, licensed printers."

"Reyes and Daughter," said the man at the door, without smiling.

He was young—younger than she expected, with a clerk's soft hands and a sash in the harbor-blue of the Provisional Committee, and behind him two soldiers who looked bored in the way of men who had not yet been asked to do anything terrible. He carried a leather folio under one arm the way a priest carries a reliquary, or a tax man carries a ledger; it took Astrid a moment to decide which.

"You're Halloran," he said, reading from a card. "Provisional Committee, Bureau of Public Record. You printed the grain notices last month. Clean work." He glanced past her shoulder into the shop, and his eyes moved over the presses, the type cases, the hanging sheets of approved proclamations drying on their lines—the way a man's eyes move over a room he has already been told everything about. "Is there somewhere we can talk?"

Behind her, upstairs, a floorboard settled. Six years old, Astrid thought. Six years old and hiding above my head because I let him through the door.

"The front office," she said.

* * *

The office still smelled of her father's pipe tobacco, though he had been dead seven years and the pipe itself had gone to the melt with everything else bearing the Reyes name. She kept the smell deliberately—a saucer of his old leaf on the mantel, refreshed twice a year. It was sentimental, and sentiment was dangerous, but it also gave her something to do with her hands when men like Halloran sat down across from her and began to speak of things that were not true.

Halloran opened the folio. Inside were printed proofs, still smelling faintly of some other, better press across town—the Committee kept its own machines now, big ones, donated by merchants who had learned which way the wind blew. He laid them out on the desk like a hand of cards.

"The Bureau is commissioning a commemorative broadsheet," he said. "The third anniversary of the Uprising approaches, and the people require a unified account. Your shop has the reputation. We'd like it done here, by hand, the old way. It gives the thing—" he hesitated, searching for the word, "—gravity."

Astrid looked at the topmost proof. The headline was set in the heavy display face every printer in Vellamare knew: *THE HARBOR RISING: TREASON ANSWERED WITH MERCY.*

She read it the way she read all documents now, in two layers at once—the layer of ink and the layer underneath, where the silences lived. She had gotten very fast at both.

"There will be text beneath," Halloran went on, tapping the proofs. "An account of the events of the ninth of March. The mob incited by foreign agents. The regrettable but necessary response of the citizen militia. The mercy shown afterward." He produced a second sheet, handwritten, dense with numbered paragraphs. "This is the approved narrative. Every clause. You'll set it exactly."

*The ninth of March.* Astrid had been twenty-four the ninth of March. She had stood on the fish-market steps with a sack of flour over her shoulder, four streets up from the water, and heard the first volley come off the harbor like the sky tearing. She had seen the fishermen's wives run toward the guns instead of away from them, which no one who had not seen it would ever believe. She had spent that night washing blood out of a stranger's coat in her father's back room while her father burned letters in the stove and told her, over and over, *you saw nothing, Astrid. You were home. You saw nothing.*

The approved narrative ran to forty-one clauses. She scanned it with a compositor's eye, noting the em-dashes already placed for her, the italics pre-marked, and stopped cold at clause nineteen.

*"The toll of the ninth,"* she read aloud, because Halloran seemed to expect her to read aloud, *"was twelve souls, most of them armed insurgents, and order was restored before dusk."*

"That's correct," Halloran said.

Twelve. She had helped wash blood from a coat that belonged to no one who came back to claim it, and she could have filled a coffin from what soaked the market steps alone. Mira's own aunt—one of the fishwives who traded at the harbor stalls—had not come home that night, and no list anywhere in Vellamare contained her name, because lists were written by the living and the living had been instructed.

"Twelve souls," Astrid repeated. Her voice was perfectly level. Seven years of practice, and this was what the practice was for.

"It's a reconciliation figure," Halloran said, and there was something almost gentle in his tone, the tone of a man explaining arithmetic to a child. "The old number caused distress. Distress is not useful anymore. We're building a harbor people can live in, Señora Reyes. A shared account. You of all people should understand—" and here he smiled for the first time, briefly, showing her he knew exactly whose shop this was, "—the cost of printing the wrong thing."

Her father had died with a summons on his desk and his name scraped off the trade registry. She had inherited the shop by signing a document promising she would print only what was approved, and she had kept that promise for seven years, and she had told herself every morning for seven years that keeping it was survival, and survival was not cowardice, and cowardice was not complicity. She had the sentences worn smooth as river stones. She recited them silently now, behind her face, while her mouth said:

"When do you need it?"

* * *

Halloran left the proofs and the narrative and a deposit in good silver coin, and the soldiers left with him, and the bell rang, and then Astrid stood in the middle of her shop for a long moment with the folio under her arm, feeling the way she imagined a man feels when the ground ice cracks under him—before the plunge, in the strange quiet, when he understands the winter is over whether he is ready for spring or not.

She put Tomas's apostrophe back in her pocket. Then she went to find Bram.

* * *

Bram Ostergaard worked at the stone composing table by the north window, where the light was best and had been best for fifty years, and he was sorting type the way he always sorted it—by feel, mostly, his eyes half-shut, his fingers reading the nicks and serifs like a blind man reads faces. He was seventy-one years old and had set type under three regimes, and his hands had a tremor now that vanished entirely whenever he touched metal, as if the tremor were afraid of it too.

He didn't look up when she came in. He knew her step.

"Well?" he said. "How bad?"

Astrid laid the proofs and the forty-one clauses on the table beside his galley. Bram set down his sorting stick with the care of a man laying down a knife before a meal, wiped his palms on his apron, and read.

She watched him the way she had watched him read a hundred official documents over seven years, waiting for the small muscle in his jaw. It was their private telegraph, hers and Bram's: the jaw-muscle meant *I see the lie*, and it fired at nearly everything they printed these days—the revised census, the corrected casualty figures, the new map of the harbor districts that had quietly unmade two whole streets. But today Bram read all forty-one clauses and the muscle never fired at all. Instead his face went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes, and when he reached clause nineteen his thumb pressed flat against the paper as though he could hold the number twelve down onto the page and keep it from breeding.

"Twelve," he said.

"Twelve."

"And order restored before dusk."

"So it says."

Bram nodded slowly. Then he picked up his sorting stick again, which Astrid took for assent—it was how they had always done it, the old man setting the lie into metal with those hands that did not tremble, because refusing was death and death helped no one, least of all the dead—and began to sort type for the headline.

He got as far as choosing the font size. Then he stopped.

"Astrid." His voice was low, pitched under the range of the open stairwell. Upstairs, faintly, Tomas was moving around—she could hear the small domestic sounds of a child exploring a strange house, a chair dragged an inch, a question asked of no one. "Clause nineteen. I want to be sure I have it right. Read it to me once more."

"*The toll of the ninth was twelve souls—*"

"No." Bram set the sorting stick down again, and this time he set it down the way you put something away for good. "Not that line. Not the number."

Astrid felt the floor tilt slightly under her, though of course it did nothing of the kind. In seven years, through the census and the maps and the corrected lists, through everything, Bram Ostergaard had never refused a line. He had a saying, older than the revolution, older than the regime before the revolution: *type doesn't believe anything. Type just stands where you put it.* He had taught it to her her first week in the shop, when she was nine years old and crying over something she'd read in one of her father's papers, and she had built her whole life inside that saying like a snail builds its shell.

"Bram—"

"I know." He held up one hand—not argument, just patience, an old man's terrible patience. "I know what refusal costs. I've paid it before, in the other direction, do you understand? Under the Admiralty, I set the lists. The ones with names on them. Names of men I drank with." His eyes stayed on the galley, on the beautiful orderly rows of metal, each letter identical to its fellows, none of them guilty of anything. "I told myself what you tell yourself. Type doesn't believe anything. The hands are clean; it's only the ink that lies." He turned and looked at her then, and his eyes were wet and absolutely calm. "But this isn't a list, Astrid. A list pretends the dead are enemies. This says the dead were never born. Twelve souls. My God, girl, there were more than twelve children on the market steps."

Upstairs, the floor creaked. Both of them looked at the ceiling, and in that look Astrid saw the whole shape of the trap close around them: the child above, the lie below, and between them the forty-one clauses in good silver-pointed type, waiting to be made permanent.

"If you refuse," she said quietly, "they take the license. Then the shop. Then they ask why, and there's no answer to why that ends well. You know this. You taught me this."

"I know it." Bram drew a long breath through his nose. "And maybe I'd swallow it anyway, for the shop, for the girl upstairs, for you. Maybe I'd set it and hate myself in the usual proportion." He shook his head. "But it isn't only the number, Astrid. Look at the rest of it. *Foreign agents.* *Regrettable necessity.* They're not revising the record anymore. They're replacing it, root and branch, and they want it done in a hand-printed shop because a broadsheet from our press carries weight that their machine-press filth doesn't. Our hands would be the seal of authenticity on the lie." He tapped the deposit coin Halloran had left, spun it, caught it flat. "They don't just want us silent. They want us speaking for them. There's a difference, and I'm too old to pretend I can't hear it."

Astrid stood in the north light and felt the sick recognition move through her, slow and total, the way floodwater moves through a cellar—finding every crack, filling every corner she had kept dry for seven years. She had survived the old regime by being small. She had survived the revolution by being smaller. She had told herself the Committee was rough-edged, necessary, that new orders break eggs, that history was a press that inevitably took fingers. And now the Committee had walked into her father's shop with soft hands and a gentle voice and commissioned her to print the massacre out of existence, politely, in advance, with a deposit against delivery—and she had asked, *when do you need it?*

When do you need the erasure of everyone I saw die? Is Tuesday convenient?

"Mira's aunt," she said. Her voice came out strange, distant, someone else's. "Sera. She sold mussels at the third stall. She had a laugh like a cart on cobblestones. She's in no account anywhere. Not even as a number. Twelve souls, and she isn't one of them, and neither is anyone I actually saw, and that means—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "That means somewhere there's a real count, Bram. Someone knows the real number. Someone wrote it down before they wrote this down. And in six months, if this sheet goes out in ten thousand copies, the person who wrote the true count will be the only liar left in Vellamare."

Bram was watching her with an expression she had never seen on him before. It took her a moment to name it. Hope, she realized. Terrible, cautious hope, like a man watching a fire catch in a wet season, unwilling to trust it.

"Your father," Bram said, "had a saying about counts."

"My father had a saying about everything."

"He had more than sayings." Bram rose, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and stood looking at the wall of type cases, hundreds of shallow drawers of pigeonholes, each labeled in faded chalk in her father's handwriting—*Brevier, Long Primer, Small Picas, Nonpareil*—the whole alphabet of sizes and faces, the visible inventory of the shop, complete and honest and inspected twice yearly by the Bureau. He looked at that wall for a long time. Then he looked at her.

"Astrid," he said, "the night before the Admiralty men came for your father—seven years ago, the week before he died—he came to me at closing and asked me to help him carry something down to the cellar. Two crates. Heavy as sin. He made me swear on the shop itself that if anything happened to him, I'd tell you about them only when you were ready to hear it. And I have waited seven years, girl, because I judged you safe, and safe men don't need to know where the gunpowder is." He laid his hand flat on the forty-one clauses, covering clause nineteen, hiding the number twelve with his palm. "I don't think you're safe anymore."

From the top of the stairwell came a small sound—Tomas, at the landing, watching them through the balusters with enormous dark eyes, Elena Voss's eyes, listening to everything.

"What's in the crates?" Astrid asked.

Bram smiled, and it was the saddest and oldest smile she had ever seen on him.

"Type," he said. "Letters they melted out of the public record and your father stole back, letter by letter, for thirty years. Enough of them, child, to print the truth."
