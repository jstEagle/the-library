## 4. What Bram Remembers

The molds were heavier than memory.

Bram Vosk had carried a great many heavy things in his sixty-one years—cases of type up four flights of stairs when the lift in the old Guilder Street shop broke and stayed broken; a press stone on his shoulder the winter the Committee requisitioned the warehouse; a coffin, once, with three other men who did not know whose name to say over it. But nothing had ever sat in his hands quite like the wooden box his old master pressed into them on a night of rain and candle smoke, thirty years gone now, and said: *Carry this out the back. Don't look inside. Don't come back for me.*

He had not looked inside. That was the discipline of those years—you learned to carry things without looking at them, because looking was how they caught you. A man could be hanged for what his eyes did, let alone his hands.

Now he sat in the back room of the Reyes print shop with a cup of chicory going cold at his elbow, and he looked. He looked because Astrid was standing across from him with her arms folded and her jaw set in a way that made her father's ghost stand up inside her, and because she had asked him a question he had spent seven years pretending not to hear.

*My father hid something, didn't he. You knew.*

* * *

Bram had been twenty-nine when the Grey Wardens came to power, old enough to remember the before and young enough to be frightened by it in a way that aged him early. The old regime—the one the histories called the Corvane Regime now, though at the time it had simply been *the government*, the way a fist is simply a hand until it closes—had not begun with burning books. Burning was loud, and loud things made martyrs, and martyrs made trouble. No. Corvane's people had understood something subtler, something that took Bram a decade to articulate even to himself:

They didn't burn the record. They *replaced* it.

A book did not need to disappear if every copy of it could be quietly corrected. A man did not need to die if his name could be made to mean nothing—a merchant one year, an unlicensed trader the next, then merely *a trader*, then no entry at all, the ledger line left blank as if the paper itself had never been asked to hold him. The Wardens ran it like a tide. Each revision came in small, each one plausible, each one accompanied by a printed notice so official and so boring that reading it felt like agreeing to it. And every printer in Vellamare set the corrections, because the alternative was set out in the public square in letters of rope and timber.

Bram had set ten thousand such corrections. He could still feel some of them under his fingers—the particular weight of a name being lifted out of a line of type, the little vacuum it left behind, the way the surrounding words seemed to lean inward, ashamed. Compositors talked about type as if it were alive, and in those years it had felt less alive than haunted. Every galley was a graveyard with the graves filled in.

There had been printers who refused. He could name them—not aloud, never aloud, but in the dark archive behind his eyes where he kept everything: Old Sef Marlowe of the Anchor Press, who printed one last broadsheet calling the Wardens liars and was found two days later floating past the customs house with his hands tied in printer's knots, which everyone understood to be a message addressed specifically to the trade. The Delacour sisters, whose shop burned with them in it, officially a lamp accident, unofficially a lesson. Young Piet Aris, barely nineteen, who had only refused once, over a single name, and whose family was given the choice between his imprisonment and their silence, and chose silence, and had to live with it, and some of them did not.

Bram had not refused. He had set the corrections. He had told himself he was keeping the shop open, keeping his apprentices fed, keeping the *machinery* of truth alive somewhere beneath the lies, like a seed bank under snow. Some nights he had almost believed it. Other nights he had stood at the case until dawn, unable to make his hands move, because his hands knew what his mouth would not say: that a seed bank does no good if you never plant it, and he had never planted anything, not once, not in all the grey years.

And then there was Aldous Reyes.

* * *

Aldous had been different, and Bram had known it within a month of taking the position at the Harborlight Press, because Aldous committed the one crime no Warden could easily punish: he remembered *out loud*, but only in private, and only precisely.

Other men raged about the revisions in taverns, in the vague furious language of drink, and were arrested for vagueness. Aldous kept a notebook—Bram saw it exactly once, brown leather, string-tied—and in it he recorded discrepancies the way a surveyor records faults in a bridge. *Notice 114 says the grain fleet arrived on the ninth. I stood on the pier on the eleventh and watched it come in. Two days eaten.* That was all. Not outrage. Arithmetic. He treated the regime's lies as errors to be logged, and there was something in that calm that frightened Bram more than any broadsheet, because rage burns out and arithmetic compounds.

"You're going to get us all killed," Bram told him once, in the third year, after Aldous had spent an evening cheerfully demonstrating that the Committee's casualty figures for the Dockworkers' Action could not possibly be true because the city's coffin-makers' guild records showed triple the declared output.

Aldous had looked up from his type case with that mild, infuriating smile. "Bram. What do you think type is?"

"Metal."

"It's memory with handles." He held up a sort—an *e*, worn smooth at the shoulders from decades of use. "Someone cut this face before either of us was born. Every hand that's set it since has been carrying a message forward. The Wardens can revise paper. They can't revise what's buried in a mold, because they don't know it exists." He turned the letter in the lamplight. "Paper is what they let survive. Metal is what we choose to keep."

Bram had thought it was poetry then. He was a practical man and poetry had always struck him as a way of dressing up a thing you couldn't afford to do. It was only much later—only tonight, sitting in Aldous's daughter's shop with a cold cup of chicory and a box of forbidden molds—that he understood Aldous had not been speaking poetry at all. He had been leaving instructions.

* * *

The night itself. Bram returned to it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

It was the autumn of the Purge of the Clerks, the second-to-last act of the old regime's paranoia, when the Wardens turned on their own record-keepers, having realized—correctly—that men who keep accounts can eventually present them. Archivists, registrars, tax clerks, court scribes: taken away in batches, their offices sealed, their files carted to the Customhouse yard and pulped in vats of lye while soldiers watched to make sure no one pocketed so much as a scrap.

Elena Voss had been one of them. Bram remembered her because she had come to the Harborlight Press twice, officially to collect copies of municipal notices, and both times Aldous had walked her out through the alley door himself, and both times the two of them had stood talking in the rain a moment too long, heads bent together, and Bram had pretended to be very busy with the stone.

On the night of the molds, Elena had come a third time. Bram had been alone, locking up, and had heard the knock—three quick, three slow, three quick—and opened the door on a woman with rain running off her chin and terror arranged carefully on top of resolve, the way a compositor arranges a line that must justify at any cost.

"Aldous," she said. "Is he—"

"He's coming. He sent me ahead." She stepped inside, dripping. "Bram Vosk. You're the one who sets the corrections."

"Yes."

"Do you hate it?"

He should have lied. He found he could not. "Every day of my life."

She nodded as if he'd passed an examination. When Aldous arrived twenty minutes later, soaked and breathing hard, the two of them went into the back room and shut the door, and Bram—because he was already complicit in his own heart, whatever the law said—stood watch at the front window with the lights doused.

Through the door he heard fragments. Aldous's voice low and fast: *"...the full register, names crossed off and when and by whose order... she copied it, Bram, she copied it by hand over four years..."* And Elena, once, sharp with grief or fury or both: *"They'll pulp mine tomorrow. Everything I am will be soup by Friday. Unless it's somewhere they aren't looking."*

Then quieter. Then the sound of a drawer opening, and wood on wood, and Aldous saying something Bram did not catch except the last word of it, which was *"grandchildren"*—though neither Aldous nor Elena had children yet, and Bram had puzzled over that word for years afterward, turning it like a coin, wondering if it had been *godchildren*, or a name, or whether Aldous had simply been speaking about the future in the only currency he trusted.

Near midnight, Aldous opened the back-room door and put the box in Bram's arms. It was long and shallow, wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than its size suggested. His master's face in the candlelight was the strangest thing Bram had ever seen on it: not fear. Certainty. The look of a man who has finished a job.

"The cellar under the Ferro warehouse on Salt Lane floods twice a year," Aldous said. "Nobody stores anything valuable there, which is exactly why we're storing something invaluable there. Third course of bricks above the waterline, north wall, there's a loose one behind the lime barrel. You'll know it when you touch it." He had gripped Bram's shoulder then, hard, fingers digging through the coat. "Don't tell me where you put it. Don't tell anyone. If they take me, you grieve, you drink too much for a season, and you go on setting their filthy corrections, and you wait."

"Wait for what?"

"For someone who asks the right question." Aldous smiled then, tired and crooked. "I don't know who. Could be my daughter. Could be a stranger. Could be nobody, and then the metal sleeps, and that's a grief too—but a survivable one. Go on, now. Out the back. Don't look inside. Don't come back for me."

"I can't leave you—"

"You can. You must. That's the whole design, Bram. One of us stays legible so the other can stay hidden." He pushed Bram gently toward the door. "That's how memory survives a purge. Not by winning. By *dispersing*."

Bram had gone out the back into the rain with the box against his chest, and he had not looked back, because looking back was how they caught you, and because if he had looked back and seen the lanterns already coming down Harborlight Row he would have dropped the box and run to die beside a man who had ordered him not to.

They took Aldous Reyes eleven days later. The charge was falsification of municipal records, which was the regime's blackest joke, since falsifying records was the one crime the regime itself committed professionally and without pause. There was no trial worth the name. There was a notice, printed and posted, and Bram had been ordered to reprint it for the harbor district, and he had done it, standing at his case with tears he did not wipe away falling onto the furniture, setting the lie that erased his friend in his friend's own type.

That was the heirloom. That was what got passed down: not the box, not the molds, but the *setting of the lie*. Grief in Vellamare was not handed down as a locket or a story. It was handed down as a task you performed against the person you mourned, and performed well, and survived.

* * *

"—and you waited," Astrid said.

Bram realized he had been speaking aloud, all of it or most of it, into the cold chicory and the lamplit room. His voice had gone rough as unsized paper. Across from him, Astrid had not moved, but something in her stillness had changed quality—from the stillness of a woman guarding herself to the stillness of a woman aiming.

"Seven years," she said. "You've been in this shop seven years, and you never said a word."

"You never asked."

"I didn't know there was a question to ask."

"No." Bram set down the cup. His hands, he noticed, were steady, which surprised him; he had expected them to shake for this. "That was the design, girl. Your father built it so that the truth would sleep until someone woke it *on purpose*. Not by accident. Not by torture. On purpose. Because—" and here he stopped, because the next part was the part he had carried longest, the part that was really about himself.

"Because forgetting is a choice," Astrid said quietly.

Bram looked at her. In the lamplight she had Aldous's exact expression—the mild, infuriating certainty—and he laughed, a short cracked sound that was half grief finally finding its exit.

"There it is," he said. "Yes. That's the whole of it. The Wardens, the Committee, Corvane before them—they can revise paper till the end of the world. They cannot revise a mold buried in a flooded cellar, because a mold doesn't argue. It just *waits*. Forgetting isn't something that happens to a city, Astrid. It's something a city agrees to, one corrected page at a time. And agreement—" he tapped the table, once, hard, "—can be withdrawn."

Astrid unfolded her arms. She looked, for a moment, very young and very much her father's daughter, and Bram thought: *there's the question. There's the question he waited thirty years for someone to ask, and she hasn't even asked it yet, but she's about to.*

"Where," Astrid said.

Not *what*. Not *how many*. Not *is it safe*. Where.

Bram rose, joints cracking like type being distributed, and went to the corner where the shop's oldest furniture stood under a dust sheet—the stone Aldous had bought secondhand, the one Bram had carried on his shoulder the winter the warehouse fell. He pulled the sheet back and laid his palm flat on the cold iron bed.

"Salt Lane," he said. "Under the Ferro warehouse. Third course above the waterline, north wall, behind the lime barrel." He met her eyes. "You'll know it when you touch it."

Astrid was already reaching for the lamp and her coat, and outside, far off toward the harbor, a whistle blew—once, twice, the Committee's night patrol changing shift—and Bram Vosk, who had spent thirty years choosing silence, followed his dead friend's daughter toward the door, and for the first time since the rain on Harborlight Row, he was not afraid of what he was about to look at.
