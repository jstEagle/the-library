## 5. The Smuggler's Tide

The tide came in at Vellamare the way lies came to the Committee: slow, gray, and smelling of everything it had swallowed.

Odile Ferro stood on the deck of the *Saltwife* with her boots braced against the roll of the swell and watched the dawn decide whether it wanted to be a good one or a bad one. The sky over the breakwater was the color of old pewter, shot through with one thin seam of copper where the sun hadn't yet committed itself. Behind her, the harbor spread out in its morning ritual — fishing boats coughing out past the mole, gulls screaming their eternal grievances, the cranes of the customs yard standing like gallows that had learned to be useful again.

Seven years since the Harbor Revolution, and the cranes still looked like gallows to her. She figured they always would.

"High tide's in an hour," said Pell, her first mate, materializing at her elbow the way he did, all elbows and salt-cracked knuckles and a face like a dropped pie. "Tide table says—"

"I can read a tide table, Pell."

"You asked me to remind you."

"I asked you to remind me about the *ink*, not to narrate the ocean at me."

Pell shut up, which was his best quality. Odile turned back to the water and let the wind work at her collar. Below decks, under forty bolts of honest linen and sixty jars of pickled eel that no sane person would ever buy, sat her real cargo: eleven casks of printing ink, black as sin and twice as expensive, smuggled up the coast from a widow in Corvane who made it the old way, with lampblack and linseed and a patience that had gone out of fashion everywhere else in the world.

Ink was good business. Ink was the best business Odile had found in twenty years of finding businesses, most of them briefly legal. Because ink was the one thing every regime needed and none of them could make enough of. The Provisional Committee printed proclamations the way other governments minted coin. The printers of Vellamare burned through pigment like furnaces. And when the Committee's allotments ran short — which they always did, because the Committee was better at history than arithmetic — the printers came looking for a woman with a boat and no questions.

Odile Ferro sold questions to no one. That was the whole of her philosophy, carved into her somewhere around the age of nineteen, the year she'd learned what happened to people who answered questions honestly. She moved cargo. Paper, ink, occasionally a crate of something that clinked. Never people. People talked, people panicked, people left evidence. People got you hanged.

*Paper, not people.* She'd said it so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone.

She checked the tide again, out of habit, and went below to count her casks before the buyer arrived.

* * *

The Reyes print shop sat at the top of Chandler's Stair, wedged between a chandler's (naturally) and a shuttered apothecary whose windows still bore the ghost-letters of its old name. Odile had been hauling ink up those stairs for six years now, and she knew the shop the way she knew her own hold: by smell. Oil and paper dust and the particular metallic tang of type metal, underlaid always with that faint sweetness of sizing paste. A smell she'd have denied loving, if anyone had ever been fool enough to ask.

Astrid Reyes opened the door before Odile finished knocking, which was also habit. Astrid was a woman who heard everything coming.

"You're early," Astrid said.

"Tide's early." Odile hefted the first cask off her shoulder and set it down inside the door with a thump that rattled the type cases. "Eleven casks. The widow's prices went up — something about the Committee requisitioning her lampblack supply, which I'm sure is a coincidence and nothing like theft."

"The Committee doesn't steal," Astrid said dryly, signing the tally slip without reading it. "It redistributes."

"It steals."

"It redistributes." But there was the ghost of a smile, there and gone, the kind of smile you'd miss if you weren't watching for it. Odile was always watching for it. Not for any reason. Just professional habit. Watching was free; asking cost money.

The shop was dim, the way it always was this time of morning, the shutters half-drawn against inspection eyes. Composing sticks lay where hands had left them. A galley of fresh-set type waited under its wooden cover like a sleeping animal. And over by the far wall, the old man Bram sat at his stone sorting type with the slow precision of a clock that had decided time owed him something.

Bram didn't look up. Bram never looked up when Odile came, but he always said one thing, always the same thing, pitched just loud enough to carry:

"Morning, Ferro. Mind the third stair. It's loose."

"Morning, Bram." Six years, and the man had never once said anything else to her. She'd come to find it oddly comforting. In a city full of people performing loyalty to a revolution that had eaten its own children, a loose stair was honest.

There was someone else in the shop.

Odile noticed him the way she noticed everything — sideways, without turning her head, cataloguing before acknowledging. Small. Sitting on a stool near the wash-up sink, very still, the way small things are still when they've learned stillness keeps them safe. Dark hair cut badly, by someone in a hurry. A coat too big for him, sleeves rolled three times.

A child.

Her stomach did something complicated and unwelcome.

"Astrid." She kept her voice level, kept her eyes on the tally book. "Your cousin visiting?"

"No."

The word landed flat and final as a type case dropped from a height. Odile looked up then, properly, and found Astrid watching her with an expression Odile had never seen on that careful face before — something raw underneath the composure, like a bruise showing through powder.

"He's staying," Astrid said. "That's all anyone needs to know right now."

"That's not how knowing works." Odile set down the tally book slowly. "You know how knowing works better than anyone in this city. Things get known, Astrid. Things get *reported*. There's a boy in your print shop who isn't your cousin, and you want me to haul my boat out of this conversation and pretend the harbor's empty."

"I want you to finish unloading your casks and take your payment and go."

"Fine." Odile picked up the second cask. Carried it in. Set it down. Picked up the third. Her hands were steady; her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that had developed opinions. On the fourth trip she stopped, cask on her shoulder, and looked at the boy straight on.

He was watching her. Big dark eyes, wary as a gull near a net. He had a smudge of ink on his left cheekbone, the kind you got from touching a forme you'd been told not to touch. His hands were folded in his lap like a little clerk's. And there was something in his face —

Something in his face.

Odile had spent twenty years reading faces at fifty yards in bad light. It was the trade. You read the harbormaster's face to know if the search would be thorough. You read the buyer's face to know if the price would hold. You read the informer's face to know if you were already dead.

And the face of this child, six years old maybe, sitting very still on a stool in a forbidden-smelling shop, wore an expression Odile recognized from mirrors and from memory and from one terrible night twenty years ago when she'd been small and still and trying to be invisible in a room where being seen meant disappearing.

She knew that face because she had worn that face.

She set the cask down harder than she meant to.

"How old is he?" she asked, and her voice came out wrong, gravel where there should have been iron.

"Six," Astrid said quietly. "Maybe seven. He doesn't talk much yet."

"Whose is he?"

"Odile."

"*Whose is he?*"

The silence stretched. Across the room, Bram's hands had gone still over the type case — the first time in six years Odile had seen the old man stop working while she was in the room. Even he was listening.

"You know Elena Voss," Astrid said finally. Very quietly. "The archivist."

Everyone knew Elena Voss. Or everyone had known her, two years ago, before the name came off the Committee rolls and the house on Archive Row came down to its foundations and the official record began to say that no such person had ever held any office at all. Odile remembered the woman vaguely — sharp-faced, sharp-tongued, had once caught Odile's cousin smuggling documents out of the old registry and let him go with a warning and a look that had haunted him for a decade. One of the ones who'd believed the Revolution meant *records for everyone*, back when believing things was still survivable.

Then the purges had come for the archivists, because revolutions always eat their librarians first. They know where everything is buried.

"This is her son," Astrid said.

Odile looked at the boy. Tomas, apparently. Tomas Voss, six years old, sitting in a print shop with ink on his cheek, wearing the face of a child who had already learned that the safest thing in the world was to be nobody.

*Forget the face,* the note had said, Astrid told her later. As if a face were a ledger entry. As if forgetting were a thing you could order done, like freight.

"Well," Odile said. She wiped her palms on her trousers. "Well, hell."

"That's more or less what I said," said Bram, from across the room, without looking up.

* * *

They argued about it in the back room among the paper stock, voices low, because the walls of Vellamare had ears even when the ears belonged to no one living.

"I move cargo," Odile said. "Paper. Ink. Once a consignment of very illegal saffron, but we don't discuss the saffron. I do not move *people*. People breathe wrong. People cry at checkpoints. People have names, and names get asked after."

"So does paper," Astrid said. "You told me yourself the Committee inspects shipments now. Random searches at the mole."

"Paper doesn't have a mother on the erased list. Paper doesn't have a face that—" Odile stopped. Started again. "Look. If they catch him on my boat, it's not a fine. It's not confiscation. Smuggling a listed child off this coast is sedition of the first order. They hang you for it. They hang you *slowly*, and they make the harbor watch, and I have to work this harbor for another thirty years."

"I'm not asking you to take him tonight."

"You're asking me something. You don't keep a boy hidden in a print shop out of charity. Charity runs out. You're building toward something."

Astrid was quiet for a moment. Through the wall, faintly, they could hear Bram talking to the boy — actually talking, words Odile couldn't make out, in the low rumble of a man reading aloud. Teaching letters, maybe. Old compositor, teaching a six-year-old his alphabet in a city where alphabets were becoming contraband.

"I'm going to print," Astrid said at last. "The true account. What happened at the harbor seven years ago. What happened to the archivists. All of it. Before the Committee seals the Archive and makes lying legal forever."

Odile stared at her. "Do you hear yourself? Do you know what they do to printers who—"

"I know exactly what they do. I've spent seven years doing the opposite, and I can tell you precisely what it costs, because I pay it every day. It costs remembering what you saw and writing down what you're told instead. It costs looking at your own hands and wondering whose they are." Astrid's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about her; nothing ever rose, it just deepened, like water finding its level. "I'm not asking you to agree. I'm asking whether, when the time comes, your boat can be at a certain dock on a certain night, and whether some crates of 'paper' might happen to be aboard."

"And the boy?"

"The boy stays here as long as he can. After that—" Astrid's jaw tightened. "After that, I haven't thought that far. I'm thinking about ink and paper and how many nights it takes to set three thousand pamphlets by hand. One problem at a time."

"One problem at a time is how you end up hanged," Odile said. "You solve the last problem first. The last problem is always the boat."

* * *

She stayed longer than she meant to. She told herself it was because the tide table favored a late departure, which was even true, which was the best kind of lie — the kind with a fact wrapped around it like insulation.

She drank tea in the back room that tasted of tannin and dust. She watched Bram teach the boy to sort type — not really sort, just *handle*, learning the difference between a lowercase *e* and a lowercase *c* by touch, the way you'd learn coins. The boy's small fingers moved over the little leaden letters with a reverence that made something ache behind Odile's sternum.

"Why does the *e* have a line?" the boy asked.

"So it knows which way is home," Bram said.

"That's not a real answer."

"Best kind there is."

Odile finished her tea and hated herself, mildly and persistently, for the entire hour.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't stop running. Twenty years in the trade, and she'd survived by one rule: *paper, not people.* And the rule had worked, and the rule had kept her alive, and the rule had a flaw in it that she'd never examined because examining rules is how you find out they're load-bearing. The flaw was this: paper was never just paper. Every cask of ink she'd ever run up Chandler's Stair had become something — a proclamation, a permit, a lie with the Committee's seal on it. And somewhere in six years of deliveries, some of her ink had become Astrid Reyes's quiet, obedient, Committee-approved pages too. She'd been carrying the poison and the antidote in the same hull and calling herself neutral.

Neutral. On a coast where they were erasing children's mothers out of the historical record. Neutral, with a boy sorting type ten feet away who would grow up — if he grew up — into a man whose own name was a crime.

"There's a widow in Corvane," Odile said abruptly, into the middle of Bram's lesson. Both of them looked at her. "Makes the ink. Good woman. Lost her sons in the first year — one to the old regime, one to the new. She makes ink and she asks no questions, and I have never once brought her trouble." She stood, joints cracking like rigging in a blow. "If — *if* — this ever becomes a thing I'm involved in, which it hasn't, the ink comes from her. No one else. Half the black market pigment on this coast is cut with tar and it'll foul a press inside a month. If you're going to commit treason, commit it with decent materials."

Astrid's face did something complicated. "Odile—"

"Don't thank me. Thanking me makes it real, and it isn't real, and I have a tide to catch." She shrugged her coat back on, shouldered the empty cask-slings, and paused at the door of the back room. The boy was watching her again, those dark Voss eyes, and she found she couldn't quite leave under that gaze without saying something, though God knew what.

"Mind the third stair," she said. "It's loose."

The boy nodded gravely, as if entrusted with state secrets.

* * *

The *Saltwife* took the tide out past the mole with the evening coming down purple over the water. Pell handled the tiller and Odile stood at the rail doing sums she didn't want to do.

Three thousand pamphlets, Astrid had guessed. At a thousand sheets a night, working flat out, with paper Odile would have to source and ink Odile would have to run and distribution that would need hands in every fish market and tavern and chapel porch from here to the headlands—

"Penny for them," Pell said.

"They're not worth a penny."

"Everything I've ever seen you think about was worth at least a penny."

Odile watched the lights of Vellamare shrink astern. Somewhere up on Chandler's Stair, a light was burning late in a print shop window, and an old man was teaching a dead woman's son his letters, and a careful woman was setting type for a lie she'd have to tell tomorrow and a truth she'd begun telling tonight.

"Pell," she said. "How much paper can we carry without it looking strange?"

He didn't ask why. He never asked why; it was his second-best quality. "Depends on the manifest. Linen shipment hides four bales easy. Eel jars hide another two if we pack the hollow ones."

"Start planning for ten."

"Ten bales of paper." Pell chewed on it. "That's a lot of paper, Captain."

"It's going to be a lot of something."

Behind them the harbor bell rang the evening watch, and Odile Ferro, who moved paper and not people, who had survived twenty years by never letting the cargo have a face, stood at the rail of her own ship and admitted to the empty water what she'd known since the moment she'd seen ink smudged on a small cheekbone:

The cargo had a face now. Six years old. Dark eyes. A line of black under the left one, like a mark of ownership.

And she was already, God help her, part of the crew.
