## 6. Faces in the Molds

The cellar door had always been a lie.

Astrid had walked past it ten thousand times in her life—through the print shop's back corridor, past the rack of drying posters, down two steps to a door painted the same gray as the wall, with a padlock that hadn't opened since before Tomas was born. Her father used to say the cellar flooded every winter and wasn't worth the trouble of draining. She'd believed him when she was seven. She'd stopped believing him at nineteen, when she found salt crystals blooming on the inside of that doorframe during the driest summer Vellamare had ever recorded. Cellars that flood don't grow salt flowers in a drought.

She'd never asked. That was the arrangement she and her father had lived by: he kept his silences, she kept hers, and between them the shop ran like a press—each of them feeding paper to the other without ever reading what was written underneath.

He'd been dead six years now. The door still kept his silence for him.

Until tonight.

Tomas stood beside her holding the oil lamp, both hands wrapped around its base because she'd told him that if he dropped it she would have to explain to Odile why the ship's only stowaway smelled of lamp oil and smoke. He'd taken this responsibility with the gravity of a boy being handed a sword. Now he looked up at the gray door with its stubborn padlock and asked the question adults had learned not to ask.

"What's behind there?"

"I don't know," Astrid said, and then, because the last three days had been cracking her open layer by layer like an egg against a bowl's rim, she amended it: "That's not true. I know what my father wanted me to think is behind there. Water and rot."

"And really?"

"Really, I think my father lied to me for thirty years." She knelt, worked the key Bram had given her that morning—the old compositor pressing it into her palm with a look that said *he meant you to have this when you were ready, and neither of us ever agreed on what ready means*—and felt the lock resist, then give, then swing open with a sound like a held breath released.

Cold air came out. Not damp. Dry, mineral, the smell of metal and old cloth and something faintly sweet she couldn't name until she did: linseed oil, decades old, soaked into rags folded somewhere in the dark.

"Careful on the stairs," she said. "Hold the light high."

The cellar stairs were stone, worn concave in their centers by feet that had come down here long before her father owned the building. That detail caught in her chest. Whoever had built this place had built it to be used, and used often, and hidden well enough that even a landlord's memory of it could be trusted to die.

At the bottom, the lamp found the room.

Astrid had imagined crates. Maybe barrels. Perhaps a smuggler's hoard—her father had known Odile Ferro longer than Astrid had been alive, and men who keep second cellars usually keep second ledgers too.

What the lamplight found instead was a library of faces.

Racks lined the walls, floor to ceiling, and on the racks sat type molds—hundreds of them, thousands, arranged in ordered rows like a choir waiting for a conductor. Brass matrices in oiled paper sleeves. Copper molds stacked in shallow drawers labeled in her father's small, backward-leaning hand. And along the far wall, under a dust sheet that had protected it so perfectly that the sheet itself seemed part of the machinery, stood a casting bench with a hand mold mounted in it, and above the bench, hung on nails, a full set of punches.

Not the shop alphabet. Astrid knew the shop alphabet the way she knew her own hands; she had sorted its letters since she was tall enough to reach the case. This was another one entirely. Heavier serifs. A narrower body. Designed, she realized as she lifted a punch and turned it to the flame, for small sizes—for pamphlets, for octavos, for pages meant to be printed fast, folded fast, and passed from hand to hand in markets where soldiers stood watching.

Her father had cut—or commissioned—a complete second alphabet. An alphabet that existed nowhere in any Committee registry, because it had never been set, never been printed, never been seen. It was a voice he had built and then never used.

Or never used until now.

"Astrid." Tomas tugged her sleeve. "What are they?"

"Letters," she said. Her voice came out strange, thick. She cleared her throat. "Little molds. You pour metal into them and they make the shapes of words."

"All of them?"

"All of them. Every letter. Twice." She ran her finger along a drawer label: *B — 10pt.* Then the next: *C — 10pt.* Then, in a separate row, drawers marked with a small stamped star she didn't recognize: accented forms, ligatures, characters she had never once needed in seven years of printing Committee proclamations. Characters for languages the Provisional Committee had removed from the schools. Characters for names the Committee had removed from everything else.

Tomas wandered the room the way children wander, which is to say fearlessly, touching everything, unbothered by the weight of things. He pulled open a drawer, peered in at the rows of brass, and pushed it shut again with the care of a boy imitating care rather than feeling it. Astrid let him. Some part of her mind was doing cold arithmetic—the molds weighed hundreds of pounds, they would need to be cleaned and sorted, the casting bench needed a new spring, they would need metal, they would need time—and another part of her was standing very still, looking at her father's handwriting on all these labels, and grieving something she couldn't pronounce.

He had prepared for this. Whatever *this* was. He had spent years of his life cutting and storing letters for a truth he might never be allowed to print, and he had died without telling her, and she understood suddenly that this was not secrecy. This was inheritance, delivered the only way he trusted: silently, so that she could honestly say she had never known.

You cannot betray what you don't know. Her father had protected her with his silence for exactly as long as his silence could hold.

It had stopped holding three days ago, when a boy appeared on her doorstep with ink smudged on his cheekbone and a note saying *forget the face.*

"Astrid?" Tomas had climbed onto the stool by the casting bench. His chin barely cleared the top. "Can I ask you something?"

"You can always ask me something."

"Why is my mama's face on the list?"

The lamp hissed. Astrid kept her hands moving, folding back the dust sheet's edge, buying her heart a moment.

"Who told you her face is on a list?"

"The lady who took me to the door." Tomas traced a circle on the bench with his fingertip. "She said your papa's shop was safe but I had to be careful because Mama's face is on a list, and now my face is on the list too, under her. Like a family. We're together on the list." He said this last part with unmistakable pride, and Astrid felt it go through her like a nail.

Children find the shape of love in anything. Even in a register of the erased.

"Your mother," Astrid said carefully, "was a keeper of records. Do you know what that is?"

"She read papers. She had a big desk. She smelled like dust and lemons."

"That's right. She read papers, and she remembered what was in them, and she wrote it down so other people could know it later." Astrid turned one of the punches over in her fingers—an *E*, she saw, in that unknown narrow alphabet. Elena's initial. She put it down. "Some people in the city don't want anyone to know what was in those papers. So they made lists of everyone who helped her remember. And they're wrong to do it, Tomas. It's cruel and it's wrong. But it's not because of anything you did, or anything she did that was bad. She did the opposite of bad."

"But why her face? Why not just her name?"

And there it was—the question, the whole question, the question the Committee had answered with seven years of proclamations pasted to walls and seven years of Astrid's own careful silence. Why not just the name. Because names alone can be mourned privately. Because a face on a broadsheet, a face described in an official account, a face *printed*, becomes a thing every citizen must perform not-recognizing in public, forever. Because erasure isn't complete until the face goes too. Because the Committee understood what Astrid's father had understood, from opposite ends:

Faces are what type is for.

"Because," Astrid said, "they think if no one is allowed to see her face, then after a while people will believe she was never real. They want forgetting to look like it happened by itself."

Tomas considered this with the terrible seriousness of six years old.

"That won't work," he said finally. "I remember her face."

"Yes," Astrid said. Her eyes were burning and she did not blink, because blinking would end something she needed to finish. "Yes, you do."

"Will you forget it?"

"No." She heard her own voice go flat and certain, a compositor's voice, a voice that locked a form and knew it would print true. "No, Tomas. And I'll tell you something better than that. Look at me."

He looked. Dark eyes, Elena Voss's jaw, the small line of worry between the brows that even grief hadn't smoothed yet.

"See all these?" She gestured at the racks, the drawers, the thousand brass faces of the alphabet. "My father kept these secret so that someday somebody could print the things that aren't allowed to be printed. Every person they put on a list—every face they tell the city to forget—we can put back. In metal. In ink. On paper. Metal doesn't get frightened, and ink doesn't take orders, and paper gets passed from hand to hand faster than soldiers can chase it." She was surprised to hear the words coming out of her own mouth, surprised at how little she had planned them, how they arrived fully formed, as if her father had left more than molds in this cellar—as if he'd left a speech, waiting six years for her lungs.

Tomas looked around the cellar with new eyes. "You can print my mama?"

"I can print her name in full. Elena Marta Voss, archivist of the Harbor Records. I can print what she did and why it mattered. And—" Astrid reached out and, very gently, touched the ink-smudged cheekbone, where days ago Odile's thumb had found the same mark, "—I can write down your face. Describe it so truly that anyone who reads it will know it forever. Dark eyes. A line of black under the left one, like a mark of ownership. Six years old in the year the harbor burned."

"Like a picture made of words," Tomas whispered.

"The best kind. Pictures burn. Words get copied."

He slid off the stool and threw his arms around her waist, and Astrid stood in her father's secret cellar with her hand on the back of a listed child's head and understood that the shop upstairs had changed its nature sometime in the last three days without her noticing. It had been a refuge. It was an arsenal now. Every drawer in this room was ammunition. Every punch on that wall was a weapon that fired only truth, and the war had already started whether she declared it or not.

She thought of Mira upstairs sorting type by lamplight, pretending not to watch the cellar door. Of Bram, who had carried this key for six years like a stone in his shoe. Of Odile Ferro calculating bales of paper. Of Kastel, whose visits had a pattern she hadn't yet decoded.

*Start planning for ten,* Odile had said. Ten bales. A lot of something.

Now Astrid knew what the something was.

* * *

They came up the stairs into the shop's back corridor and nearly walked into Bram, who was standing at the drying rack holding a composing stick he had ostensibly been cleaning for the past hour and had actually been holding like a talisman while he listened for them.

"Well?" he said.

"He cut a whole second alphabet." Astrid's voice shook slightly and she let it. "Punches, matrices, the casting bench, everything. There's enough metal down there to print for twenty years."

Bram closed his eyes. He exhaled through his nose, long and slow, and Astrid watched seventy years of vigilance loosen in the old man's shoulders by a single degree—not gone, never gone, but eased, the way a debt eases when the debtor dies honorably.

"He always said," Bram murmured, "that a printer who owns only one alphabet has only one thing to say." He opened his eyes and looked past her, down the stairwell, toward the racks in the dark. "Did you find the star-marked drawers?"

"The accents. The forbidden character sets."

"Not just for languages, girl. Look again tomorrow. Some of those star drawers hold more than type." He would say nothing else about it, she could see; the old man's mouth had closed like a forme being locked. Instead he held out the composing stick he'd been clutching, handle first, an offering, a transfer of office.

"So," Bram said. "What do we set first?"

From atop the cellar stairs, small and clear, came a six-year-old voice that had no business answering first and answered anyway:

"My mama."

Astrid looked at Bram. Bram looked at Astrid. Somewhere out in the city a patrol drummed past, and neither of them moved toward the shutters or dimmed the lamp, and Astrid reached for the stick.

"We start tonight," she said. "Elena Marta Voss. Ten point. Get me the good ink."

She was halfway to the case when the street bell began to ring—once, twice, a third time—and through the front window she saw torchlight moving up the lane toward the shop, and Censor Ivo Kastel standing beneath it, alone, hatless, waiting to be let in.
