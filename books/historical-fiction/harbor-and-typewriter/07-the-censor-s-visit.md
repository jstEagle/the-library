## 7. The Censor's Visit

The notice came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy too young to understand what he carried: a cream-colored card, heavy stock, the Committee's seal pressed in ink so dark it looked like a wound. *Inspection of premises. Censor Second-Class I. Kastel. Thursday, ninth bell.*

Astrid read it twice at the front counter, then folded it into quarters and tucked it beneath the ledger, and for a long moment she simply stood there listening to the sound of her own blood.

Kastel.

She had known the name before she knew what a censor was. Ivo Kastel had eaten at their table. He had brought her candied ginger wrapped in wax paper when she was eight, had sat with her father in the back room until the candles guttered, arguing about typography and justice in a voice that was warm even when it was wrong. That was before. Before the old regime fell and the new one rose wearing the same boots. Before men like Kastel learned that friendship was a liability to be audited.

Now his name sat on cream cardstock on her counter like a knife laid flat.

She carried it back to the composing room, where Bram stood at the stone with a setting stick in his hands, moving with the slow economy of a man who had spent fifty years making letters behave. Mira was sweeping—badly, the way she swept when her mind was elsewhere—and from the open door of the storeroom Astrid could hear Tomas narrating something to himself in the small, serious voice of a child playing alone. He had been with them eleven days. Eleven days of teaching him to fold proofs and stay quiet and answer to *nephew* if anyone asked. Eleven days of Astrid waking at every sound before dawn, listening for soldiers instead of gulls.

"Who is it?" Bram asked without looking up. He could read her footsteps by now, apparently, the way he read type.

"Kastel."

The setting stick stopped. Bram raised his head, and his eyes—pale as weak tea—held hers for a moment longer than comfort allowed. "Ivo Kastel," he said, as if confirming it to himself. "He's risen far."

"He's come to tear my shop apart looking for a boy who doesn't exist."

"Or he's come to look at you." Bram set the stick down carefully, aligning it with the edge of the stone. "Your father used to say you can't know a man by his office. Only by what he does with his hands when no one's grading him."

"My father also said that about Kastel, and where is my father? A name in a ledger. An entry marked *processed*." She heard the bitterness come out sharper than she'd intended, heard Mira's broom stop in the next room. "Forgive me. I don't sleep."

"No one asks you to forgive yourself either," Bram said mildly. "But think, girl. If they knew what's under this roof, we would not receive a card. We would receive the door kicked in at fourth bell, and no polite Thursday appointment. Whatever else Kastel wants—" he tapped the stone once, a compositor's habit, punctuation made physical— "he is giving us two days to decide what he finds."

That was Bram's gift, Astrid thought later. He could take terror and set it in order the way he set type, break it into manageable pieces, space it so it could be read. Decide what he finds. As if a censor's inspection were a page to be proofed.

She spent Wednesday preparing the shop the way an actress prepares a stage. The storeroom where Tomas slept was restocked with paper stock and damaged molds, plausible clutter. His small blanket, his wooden horse, his cup with the chipped rim—these went into the false-bottomed drawer of her father's old desk, which went itself into the cellar behind the stack of damp pulp. Tomas practiced being invisible with the grave diligence of a child who had already been abandoned once for being visible. She hated herself for teaching it to him. She taught it anyway.

Molds were harder. Her father's hidden cache—the second alphabet, the forbidden faces, everything she and Tomas had unearthed three weeks ago—could not be moved quickly, so it was not moved at all. It stayed behind its brick facade in the cellar, and Astrid lay awake Wednesday night staring at the ceiling, doing arithmetic of risk. If Kastel found the molds, they were all dead. If Kastel found nothing, perhaps they were only mostly dead. There was no version of Thursday in which she felt safe, and she finally understood, in the small hours, that safety had left her repertoire entirely eleven days ago, the moment she'd picked a shivering boy up off her step and carried him inside. From now on there were only degrees of danger, and choices made inside them.

At eighth bell on Thursday she opened the shop herself. The bell above the door gave its small honest ring.

Ivo Kastel had grown old badly and then well—there was silver at his temples now, and lines around his mouth that might have been carved by frowning or by suppressing it, and his gray coat was immaculate in the way of a man who dressed carefully precisely because nothing else in his life could be controlled. He stood in the doorway a moment, letting his eyes adjust, and Astrid watched him see the shop: the press, the cases, the smell of oil and ink that she had stopped noticing years ago but which visitors always remarked on, as if walking into a bottle of black wine.

"Astrid Reyes." He said her name the way one confirms a signature. Then, softer, almost against his will: "You have your father's counters. He kept the whole wall facing the light. I always told him light was wasted on woodgrain."

"Signor Kastel." She did not offer her hand. Protocol said the censor extended courtesies; the citizen received them. "You'll want to see the license first."

"I have the license memorized." But he took the frame down from the wall anyway, held it, looked not at the document but at the photograph of her father that hung beside it—a younger Aldo Reyes, arms crossed, half-smiling in the way of a man photographed mid-argument. Kastel held the frame a long time. When he put it back, he aligned it more carefully than it had been hanging before.

"Walk with me," he said.

* * *

An inspection, Astrid discovered, was a performance with two performers and an audience of neither. Kastel moved through the shop with the unhurried thoroughness of a man doing everything correctly: he examined the press, ran a finger along the forme to check for residue of unapproved text, asked to see the jobbing ledger, compared entries against the permits stamped in duplicate. He checked the type cases for characters out of place—he knew how to do this, she realized; he slid each case partway out and looked at the distribution of sorts the way a customs officer looks at ballast. Once, finding a capital Q lying loose in the wrong compartment, he picked it up, turned it in the light, and set it back exactly where it belonged, which was somehow worse than confiscating it.

Bram shadowed them at a respectful distance, answering questions about paper stock in the flattest voice he owned. Mira stood at the counter with her hands folded, the picture of a dull apprentice, though Astrid could see her pulse beating in her throat.

And through all of it, Astrid waited for the question. The real one. It had to come. Every route through the shop led eventually to the storeroom, and the storeroom door stood open at the end of the corridor like a held breath, and somewhere below them, behind brick, three hundred forbidden letterfaces waited in the dark like a buried congregation.

Kastel paused at the corridor's mouth. He looked down its length—at the storeroom door, at the stairs beyond it descending toward the cellar—and he was silent for so long that Astrid heard the harbor clock strike the quarter hour, and then a gull, and then her own heart.

"You have water damage," he said. "In the cellar?"

"Old tide problem. My grandfather built poorly and my father never fixed it. There's pulp down there soft as bread."

"Mm." He did not move toward the stairs. He turned instead and looked at her, and for a moment the official mask slipped—not into warmth, exactly, but into something older and more tired, the face of a man who had once argued about typography until two in the morning because he believed the arguments mattered. "Aldo Reyes printed ten thousand leaflets against the Marquis's grain tax in the winter of '31," he said. "Every one set by hand, on this press or one like it. They hanged three men for distributing them. Your father never gave up a single name." He paused. "Do you know why I remember that?"

"Because you were there," Astrid said carefully, "when it mattered."

"Because I helped him carry them." The words came out level, uninflected, impossible to read. "Boxes of them. Down Fishgut Alley, at night, in the rain. I was nineteen and full of certainties, and your father was thirty-five and full of doubts, and between us we moved a great deal of paper." Something crossed his face then, brief as a shadow of a bird. "Certainties age worse than doubts. I've found. Doubts at least keep you asking questions."

"Is that what you're here to do, Signor Kastel? Ask questions?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Behind him, at the end of the corridor, the storeroom door stood open, and from deep within the shop came a sound that stopped Astrid's breath: a small voice, singing. Tomas, in the back washroom where she had told him to wait, singing under his breath the counting rhyme Mira had taught him, the one about the fisherman's five sons, the tune threading out into the corridor like smoke through a keyhole.

*...the fourth son drowned, the fifth son fled, and the mother was left with an empty bed...*

Kastel's head turned, very slightly, toward the sound.

He said nothing.

He listened. Astrid watched him listen—watched his face do nothing at all, which was the most eloquent thing his face had done all morning. The rhyme finished. Somewhere a shutter creaked. In the silence after, Astrid became aware that her hands, at her sides, had closed into fists so tight her nails were drawing blood from her palms, and that Bram had gone very still by the paper racks, and that Mira had stopped pretending to work entirely.

"The Committee's new history," Kastel said at last, turning back to her, and his voice was pure procedure again, dry as a filing cabinet. "The commemorative edition. You'll have the approved copy by Friday next. Two thousand copies, quarto, the usual stock. There are—" he consulted a leather folio he had produced from somewhere, as though the gesture were routine— "certain passages concerning the harbor district that will require particular care in setting. Names omitted. Dates adjusted. You understand the requirements."

"I understand the requirements," Astrid said.

"Do you." He closed the folio. He looked past her shoulder at the photograph of her father, and then, briefly, at the corridor, at the storeroom door, at all the square feet of silence he was choosing not to fill. "Aldo Reyes once told me that a printer is the only tradesman who sells other people's certainty by the sheet. He said it proudly." Kastel put on his gray gloves, finger by finger. "I've thought about that sentence for twenty years. I still don't know whether he meant it as a boast or a warning."

"Which do you think?"

"I think," said Ivo Kastel, "that a good printer knows how to leave white space." He settled his coat, and the official returned to his shoulders like a yoke. "The inspection is concluded. No irregularities noted. I'll write it up tonight—clean premises, compliant operator, nothing further to report." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "The boy has his mother's voice. Elena Voss sang that same rhyme, at the archivists' winter feast, the year before the purges. I danced the figure with her. Badly."

The bell above the door rang him out into the street.

For a long moment nobody in the shop moved. Then Astrid unlocked her fists, one finger at a time, and found her palm marked with four red crescents, and walked—steadily, because Mira was watching, because Bram was watching, because Tomas was peeking around the washroom doorframe with wide brown eyes—to the window, where she watched the censor's gray figure cross the square and disappear down Harbor Road.

"Bram," she said quietly. "What does it mean, when a man reports nothing?"

Bram came to stand beside her. Out in the street, the ordinary morning resumed: carts, gulls, fishwives with their baskets, the revolution going about its business.

"It means," Bram said slowly, "that either we are very safe, or very hunted, and he hasn't decided which yet." He was silent a moment. "Or it means he's already decided, and what he writes tonight is the first page of something. Question is whether it's our file—or his own confession."

Astrid touched the glass where Kastel had stood. Behind her, Tomas padded forward and took hold of her skirt hem, and asked, in the smallest voice, whether the gray man had come to take him away.

"No," she said, and lifted him onto her hip, heavier now than eleven days ago, fed and washed and beginning to believe in meals. "No one is taking anyone."

But that night she could not sleep, and near dawn she went down to the cellar and stood before the brick facade with a lamp, listening to the building settle, wondering what kind of man inspects a room, hears a hunted child singing one corridor away, and chooses to write *nothing further to report.*

A week later, the approved copy of the commemorative history arrived for typesetting, and Astrid read the first page standing at her own counter, and had to sit down.

Because there, in the account of the harbor district—among the omissions, among the adjusted dates—was a single line she had not expected, buried in the middle of a paragraph like a sort hidden in the wrong case:

*No persons of consequence were harmed.*

She read it three times. It was not in the Committee's style. It was too precise, too pointed, too much like a man leaving a mark where only careful readers would ever find it. And in the margin, in pencil so faint it might have been accidental, someone had written four words in a hand she recognized from twenty-year-old letters her father had kept in a drawer:

*Aldo Reyes was right.*
