## 8. Testimony of the Fishwives

The errand was a lie wrapped in an errand.

"Take this invoice to Ferro's warehouse," Astrid had said that morning, pressing the folded paper into Mira's hands with the particular care she used for things that mattered more than their words. "And take your time coming back. The harbor air is good for you."

Mira was fifteen and had lived in the print shop since she was eleven, since the fever year took her mother and left her standing in the doorway of the Reyes shop with nothing but her name and her mother's shawl. She knew every rhythm of that place: the clatter of Bram's composing stick, the sour-metal smell of Odile's contraband ink, the way Astrid went silent—not the comfortable silence of work but the flattened silence of fear—whenever soldiers passed the window. And lately there had been Tomas, six years old and quiet as a held breath, sleeping in the loft above the type room, his small presence changing the gravity of the whole house.

So when Astrid said "take your time," Mira heard what was underneath it.

She walked down through the terraced streets toward the harbor, the invoice—genuine, as far as she knew, though it hardly mattered—folded against her ribs. The morning smelled of brine and tar and the fish-market's iron sweetness. Gulls wheeled screaming over the tiled roofs of Vellamare, and below them the city opened out into the great crescent of the harbor, where the fishing boats rocked at their moorings like patient animals and the Committee's new flag hung limp from the customs tower in the windless morning.

Mira had been eleven when the Harbor Uprising happened. Old enough to remember, too young to have been trusted with anything true about it. What she remembered was the official version, because it was the only version printed anywhere anymore: that on the last night of the old regime's occupation, the fishermen of Vellamare rose up to join the Provisional Committee's forces, and together they drove the occupiers from the seawall, and the harbor was freed, and everyone rejoiced. There was a mural of it now on the customs tower—fishermen with raised torches, soldiers of the Committee arriving like rescuers, doves rising from the rigging.

But she also remembered her mother, before the fever, going quiet at dinner whenever a neighbor named Salome came by, and the two women talking low in the kitchen with the shutters closed, and once, only once, her mother weeping without explanation while Salome held her hand and said, over and over, *they were already surrendering, they were already in the water.*

Mira had never asked what that meant. She had been a child, and then her mother died, and then there was no one left to ask.

Except there was. That was the thing she was beginning to understand, walking down the harbor road with an invoice against her ribs. The people who saw it were still here. They gutted fish every morning ten steps from the seawall where it happened. They mended nets under the mural that lied about them. Nobody had told them to forget, because nobody needed to—the Committee simply never asked, and never printed, and after seven years the silence had set around the truth like plaster around a broken limb.

*Unless someone asks.*

She did not know yet whose voice had put those words in her head. Perhaps Astrid's, from some late night in the type room. Perhaps her own.

* * *

Odile Ferro's warehouse sat at the eastern edge of the fish market, a long stone building with a loading door wide enough for a cart and a smell of pitch, rope, and something sharper underneath that Mira had learned not to name aloud. A boy of maybe twelve lounged at the door and let her in without question—he knew her, or knew the shop's mark on the invoice—and inside, between crates stamped with legitimate cargo manifests, Odile Ferro herself stood at a trestle table decanting ink from unmarked stoneware jugs into smaller vessels, her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her forearms black to the wrist.

"You're the apprentice," Odile said, without looking up. "Mira. The one with the steady hands."

"Astrid sent an invoice."

"Leave it on the crate." Odile capped a jug, wiped her hands on a rag that only spread the black around, and finally looked at her. She had a face like driftwood—weathered, hard-grained, difficult to read—and eyes that read everything. "You're supposed to leave now. That's how errands work."

"I know," Mira said. Her heart was going hard enough that she felt sure the woman could hear it. "I wanted to ask you something first."

"Ask."

"The fishwives." Mira made herself say it plainly, the way Astrid set type: one letter at a time, no rushing, no retreat. "The ones who sell at the south stalls. The ones who were here that night. Does anyone ever talk to them about it?"

Something moved behind Odile's eyes—too fast to name, gone before it finished arriving. "Talk to them about what?"

"You know what."

For a long moment the warehouse was very quiet. Somewhere above them, gulls argued over the roof beams. Odile picked up another jug, considered it, set it down again.

"Seven years," she said, "I've run cargo through this port under three flags, and I've learned that the questions you don't ask are the ones that keep you breathing. So I'm going to tell you the same thing I'd tell any fool child who wandered in here asking dangerous things." She leaned across the table, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "The south stalls. Go at the end of market, when the crowds thin. Ask for Salome Brenn. Tell her nothing about me or anyone else—tell her you're the printer's girl and you want to hear about the water. If she talks to you, she talks to you. If she doesn't, you walk away and you never come back and you never say a word of this to a living soul."

"Why would you help me if you think it's foolish?"

Odile straightened. For a heartbeat something other than driftwood showed in her face—something tired, and old, and buried deep. "Because nobody asked me either," she said. "Now get out of my warehouse before I remember I have a reputation."

* * *

The south stalls at the end of market were a country of leavings: silver scales scattered on the cobbles like spilled coins, puddles of melted ice darkening the stones, the day's unsold catch iced down in wooden boxes while the vendors' wives counted coin and gossiped and kept one eye on the customs tower out of habit older than the flag on it.

Salome Brenn was easy to find because she was impossible to overlook—a broad woman in her fifties with forearms like anchor cable, gray hair bound in a red kerchief, presiding over the largest stall and the loudest voice. Mira approached the way she'd been taught to approach anything frightening: slowly, hands visible, eyes down but not servile.

"You're the Reyes girl," Salome said before Mira got two sentences out. Not a question. "I've seen you carrying type trays for old Ishikawa's daughter. You've got the ink stain on your left cuff that never comes out."

Mira looked down at her cuff. The stain was, in fact, permanent; Astrid had the same one.

"I came to ask—" She stopped. Rehearsed speeches dissolved like sugar in seawater. "I want to hear about the water. About that night. What really happened at the seawall."

Around the stall, the other wives went still. Not dramatically—an ordinary stillness, the kind of pause that could be mistaken for nothing at all—but Mira felt it settle over the group like a net drawn tight. One woman began folding cloth with sudden concentration. Another looked out at the harbor and did not turn back.

Salome studied her for a long moment. Then she jerked her chin toward the end of the pier, where a breakwater ran out into the bay, and said, "Walk with me. Buying nothing today?" When Mira shook her head, Salome tossed a wrapped parcel of smoked herring into her arms anyway. "Then carry that, and keep your ears open."

They walked out along the breakwater. The morning sun lay flat and bright on the bay. Behind them the market resumed its noise; ahead of them the water slapped the stones with a patience that had outlasted three regimes.

"You were a baby," Salome said. "Eleven years?"

"Yes."

"Mm." She stopped at the breakwater's end, where a rusted mooring ring was bolted into the stone, and set her hand on it as if greeting an old acquaintance. "You see this ring? On the night of the uprising, there were forty men chained along this wall. Occupier prisoners, most of them—deserters, debtors, boys taken off ships. And when the uprising came and the Committee's people stormed the harbor, the fishermen went out first, as the story says. That part is true. My husband went out first. His boat is probably still at the bottom where they sank her."

Mira held the herring against her chest and said nothing, because Salome's voice had changed—it had gone somewhere else, seven years elsewhere—and interruptions would have shattered it like ice.

"But here is what the mural leaves off," Salome went on. "By midnight the fighting was done. The occupiers had surrendered. Forty men chained on this wall, unarmed, and the Committee officer in charge—you'll not find his name printed anywhere, though he sits on the Provisional Committee now with a medal and a pension—that man decided the chains weren't enough. He said loose prisoners were a contagion. He ordered them into the water." Her hand tightened on the mooring ring until the knuckles whitened. "Chained. In pairs. At low tide, so they'd stand in the mud as it came in. Some of them were singing, at the end. Fishermen know songs for drowning; it's an old trade. And my husband's brother was among them, and he had surrendered, and he had given up his knife like a good soldier, and none of it mattered."

The gulls screamed. The water went on slapping the stones, indifferent as arithmetic.

"Sixty-one of us watched from the stalls," Salome said. "We couldn't stop it. We weren't armed, and half of us had children clinging to our skirts. But we saw it. Every one of us saw it, and we remember it, and we have said nothing for seven years because saying it meant dying for it." She turned then, and her eyes were dry and terribly level. "Now you tell me, printer's girl. Who sent you? Because if this is some Committee test, you've just ended sixty-one lives, mine included, and you'll carry that the rest of yours."

It would have been so easy to lie. It would even have been safe, in the way that all lies were safe right up until they weren't. Mira felt the lie assembled and ready in her mouth—*no one, I'm sorry, I misunderstood, I'll go*—and she looked at it, and understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that everything else in her life would be decided by this one sentence.

"I can't tell you who sent me," she said. "Because telling you would put you in danger, and I won't do that. But I'll tell you this much. There's a press. There's type that isn't the Committee's type. And there are people—one or two, or maybe more than I know—who think the sixty-two names belong somewhere besides the bottom of the bay. I'm not authorized. I'm not anybody. I'm the girl with the ink stain. But I'm asking, because nobody has ever asked, and somebody should."

The silence after that stretched long. Out on the bay a late fishing boat puttered toward the mouth of the harbor, trailing its seam of white water.

Then, behind them, one of the stall wives called out—softly, but across the whole distance of the breakwater: "Salome. The girl should hear about the bell."

Salome closed her eyes briefly, as if receiving a blow she'd been expecting for years. When she opened them, something in her face had shifted—the wariness giving way, grudgingly, to something that looked almost like relief.

"Come back tomorrow," she said. "End of market again. Bring paper and take it down proper, the way printers do. I'll bring the others who'll speak. Some won't—you don't push them, you hear? A witness is a gift, not a quarry. But there's sixty-one of us left, girl, and we've been holding these stories in our mouths for seven years like stones." She took the herring parcel back, weighed it in her hand, and pressed it into Mira's arms again, firmly, as if sealing a bargain. "Somebody finally came with a bag big enough to carry them. Don't be late."

* * *

Mira walked home through streets that looked different now—or rather, that looked the same and meant different things. The mural on the customs tower with its triumphant doves. The Committee notice-board, freshly pasted, announcing the Victory Festival preparations. The ordinary faces of ordinary people buying bread and hauling crates, each of them, possibly, carrying a stone behind their teeth.

She had thought testimony was something grand—something that happened in marble halls, given by important people about important events. Now her arms smelled of smoked herring and her head rang with sixty-one names she didn't yet know, waiting at the south stalls like unsold catch, needing only someone to come with ice and a scale and honest weights.

*Somebody should ask.* She had said it herself, and meant it, and the meaning had changed her shape somehow, the way setting type letter by letter turned loose metal into a fixed and legible line.

At the shop door she paused, hand on the latch, rehearsing how she would tell it—Astrid first, then Bram, then the others—and it occurred to her, with a small fierce joy, that she was no longer only the apprentice who carried trays. She was becoming a collector of the true.

Then she noticed the man across the street.

He stood in the shadow of the cooper's awning, doing an excellent impression of a man waiting for someone: unhurried, well-dressed, glancing at a pocket watch as though the afternoon were merely inconvenient. Everything about him was unremarkable except one thing. He had been at the market. She had seen him near the south stalls an hour ago, and had thought nothing of it, because why would she?

Now her skin went cold in the warm afternoon. *Don't run,* she told herself, the way Odile would say it. *Running is a confession.*

Mira unlatched the door, stepped inside the shop, and pulled it shut behind her with a steady hand—while through the window's corner of glass she watched the man fold away his watch, cross the street, and stop directly in front of the Reyes print shop, reading the sign above the door as though committing it to memory.
