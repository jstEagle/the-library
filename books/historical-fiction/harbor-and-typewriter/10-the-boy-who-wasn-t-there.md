## 10. The Boy Who Wasn't There

Tomas had learned the shop the way other children learned the streets of their neighborhoods: by touch, by smell, by the small betrayals of floorboards that creaked under a certain weight and not another.

He knew that the third stair down from Mira's loft sang a high thin note if you stepped on its left edge, so he stepped on the right. He knew that the door to the back room stuck in damp weather and that you could open it without sound if you lifted the handle while you pushed, a trick Astrid had shown him once with a raised finger and a look that meant *remember this, don't ask why*. He knew where the ink lived — in squat clay pots on the lowest shelf, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling like something deep and dark, like the inside of the harbor at night. He was not allowed to touch the ink. He touched it anyway, once, just the pad of one finger, and then spent an hour scrubbing the black crescent from his skin before anyone saw it, because he understood already, at six years old, that some things left marks and marks were dangerous.

The shop was called Reyes & Son, though there was no son anymore, and no Reyes either except Astrid, who said the sign stayed because repainting it would cost money they didn't have, which Tomas suspected was only half the truth. The other half hung in the air of the place like the smell of ink: *the past is cheaper to keep than to change.*

His mother had been Elena Voss. He knew that the way he knew his own hands — not as a fact he thought about but as a fact that simply was, attached to him. She had smelled like paper too. That was the first thing he'd noticed about the print shop, the night Astrid opened the door and the warm paper-and-ink breath of it rolled out over the step: *it smells like Mama.* He hadn't said it out loud. Some things you kept even from yourself for a while, the way you kept a sweet under your tongue.

* * *

In the mornings, when the light came through the front windows in long dusty bars and the grown-ups were busy, Tomas explored.

The type cases were his favorite. They lined the wall behind the composing stone like a city built sideways — dozens of shallow wooden drawers, each one divided into little compartments, each compartment full of metal letters standing on their heads. Astrid let him look as long as he didn't touch the good cases, the ones with worn brass corners. "Those were my father's," she'd said. "They're tired. They've worked hard." Tomas understood tired things. He felt tired in a way six-year-olds weren't supposed to feel tired, a tiredness that lived behind his eyes and woke up with him every morning.

But there was a case near the window, a plain one, that nobody minded him using. Astrid had shown him how to hold a letter between finger and thumb, how the type faced backward — *mirror-writing*, she called it — and how you set the letters in a stick, snug as teeth, until they made a word.

"Start with your name," she'd said, three days after he arrived, and her voice had done something strange on the word *name*, gone soft and careful, like she was setting the word itself in type.

So he did. T-O-M-A-S. Five letters. It took him most of an hour the first time, tongue between his teeth, because the S kept wanting to go the wrong way round and the A looked like nothing at all upside down, just a little roof with a bar in it, until suddenly it wasn't.

When he finished, Astrid came and stood beside him and looked at the word in the stick for a long time. Longer than five letters deserved, he thought. Her thumb hovered over the T without touching it.

"That's you," she said. "That whole row of metal. That's you, standing up."

"Can they take it out?" he asked. It seemed like an important question. Everything could be taken out; he had learned that much about the world.

Astrid was quiet a moment. Then she took the stick gently from his hands, locked the line with a wedge of wood so the letters couldn't shift, and set it on the high shelf above the composing stone, where everyone in the shop would see it every day.

"They can melt it," she said. "That's all they can do. Melt it down into a blank. But someone who knows the alphabet can always make it again." She tapped his chest, lightly, right over the sternum. "You're the alphabet now. Understand? As long as you know your own name, they haven't melted anything."

Tomas didn't fully understand. But he nodded, because grown-ups gave you instructions like that sometimes — heavy ones, wrapped in small words — and you carried them whether or not they fit.

* * *

The letters had personalities. That was what he loved about them, what he never told anyone because they'd think it was babyish.

E was everywhere, common as gulls, turning up in every word like it owned the harbor. X was rare and dramatic; there was exactly one compartment of Xs in the whole case and Tomas checked it every morning to make sure none had escaped. Q was lonely — it only ever appeared with U, holding its hand, and Tomas thought that must be tiring, being a letter that couldn't go anywhere alone. He knew about going places alone.

M was his mother's letter. E-V-O-S-S — no, V-O-S-S, Voss, four letters, he'd sounded it out with Bram one evening by the stove, the old man pronouncing each syllable slow as dripping wax. But M was for Mama, and also for Mira, and when he found the M compartment he liked to run his fingertip along the tops of the letters, feeling their three little legs, like a table sturdy enough to hold a whole family.

Bram caught him doing it once. The old compositor had hands like tree roots and eyes that had seen too many decades to be surprised by anything, but something crossed his face when he saw Tomas touching the Ms — something old and folded away, grief maybe, or the memory of grief.

"Careful with those," Bram said, but his voice wasn't cross. "Metal remembers fingerprints. Every letter in this shop has been held by somebody who wanted to say something badly enough to risk saying it."

"What did they want to say?"

Bram lowered himself onto the stool by the case, joints cracking like kindling. "Everything people aren't allowed to say. That's most of everything, boy. You'll learn." He picked up an M, turned it in the lamplight. "Your mother knew letters too. Better than me, and I've set type fifty years. She used to come in here, before — well. Before." He put the M back in its bed, face-down among its brothers. "She'd have liked seeing you here. Among the letters. She'd have said—" His voice snagged on something. "She'd have said you were made of the same stuff."

Tomas looked down at his hands. They were small and smudged gray at the edges, ink that wouldn't ever quite wash off anymore.

"Am I?" he asked.

"You're asking the wrong question," Bram said, and got up stiffly to see to the press, which was the way old men ended conversations they couldn't finish.

* * *

It happened on a Thursday, though Thursdays meant nothing to Tomas except that Odile came on Thursdays, bringing ink and salt-bread and curses in two languages, and the shop felt fuller when she was in it, louder, safer, the way a room feels safer with more lamps lit.

He hadn't meant to listen. That was the thing he'd tell himself later, the thing he'd need to believe: he hadn't meant to. He had gone up to the loft to fetch the carved wooden horse Mira had won for him at the fish market, and the loft sat above the back room, and the back room's ceiling was thin, and voices rose the way smoke rises, finding every gap.

Astrid's voice first, low and flat, the voice she used when she was working hard at being calm. "—the fishwives are asking questions now. Mira says three of them have been stopped on the quay. Questioned. If they trace any of it back here—"

"They won't trace it to him," Odile said. Tomas knew her voice the way he knew thunder — big, rough, impossible to mistake. "They'll trace it to the fishwives, and the fishwives will swallow their tongues, because fishwives always do. The boy's a ghost. No papers, no name on any list since his mother's was struck. He doesn't exist. That's the one gift the bastards gave him."

"He exists," Astrid said. "That's rather the point of all of this, isn't it?"

A silence. Then Bram, slow as ever, each word set down carefully like type in a stick: "There's a middle question we're all dancing around. So I'll set it plainly, the way I'd set anything else, and we can read it together." A pause, the creak of the stool. "Would the boy be safer somewhere else?"

Tomas went very still on the loft floor, the wooden horse pressed against his chest, its carved mane digging into his collarbone.

"I wondered when someone would say it," Odile said. "I've been thinking it for a week. There are boats, Astrid. Boats going north, boats going anywhere. Families pay passage. A boy alone with a woman who claims she's his aunt — it's done every day. He goes north to Corvessa, to some inland town where nobody's ever heard of Vellamare, and he grows up fishing or farming or whatever inland boys grow up doing, and nobody ever comes to a print shop door asking questions about him."

"And that's safety?" Astrid again. Her voice had changed. There was something underneath the calm now, something with edges.

"It's survival," Odile said. "I deal in survival. It's the only honest trade left in this harbor."

"He's six years old."

"He's six years old and he's the reason soldiers might burn this shop down around us. Don't look at me like that. You know I love the boy. That's precisely why I'm saying it. Love makes bad cargo-masters. Weigh the hold honestly, Astrid. What's in it? One child. Against: you, me, Bram, Mira, the molds, the press, the testimony, everything your father died hiding and everything we've bled to gather. One child against all of it. Any smuggler worth salt would make the sum."

Another silence, longer. In it Tomas could hear the shop sounds — the tick of the cooling press, wind worrying the shutters, his own heart, which had gotten loud, embarrassingly loud, loud enough that surely they could hear it through the floor.

Then Mira's voice, and it startled him, because Mira was seventeen and usually spoke to adults the way you speak to weather — present, acknowledged, unimportant. Not tonight.

"You're talking about sending him away like he's a crate of ink."

"Mira—" Astrid began.

"No. Listen. I run the errands. I hear what people say when they don't know I'm listening, which is apparently a skill this family values." Something in her voice wobbled and steadied. "Do you know what the fishwives say about the purged ones? About the forgotten? Their children knock on doors in the winter and nobody opens. Nobody teaches them letters. Nobody asks their names, because asking is dangerous and not-asking is easy. There's a girl on Anchor Lane, eleven years old, who hasn't spoken since her father was taken. Eleven. She's forgetting words. Real words, ordinary words, just falling out of her head because there's no one to say them to." Mira's breath caught. "That's what 'somewhere safe' looks like. That's what you're proposing. A room where nobody says his name until he loses it."

"He'd be alive," Odile said, but quieter now, the thunder gone to rain.

"Alive," Mira repeated. "My grandmother was alive for nine years after the last purge. Alive in a chair by a window, alive until she wasn't, and in nine years she never once said my grandfather's name out loud because it was on a list and she was afraid the walls were listening. Is that the future? A chair by a window and a name you're not allowed to use?"

Nobody answered. Tomas realized his cheeks were wet. He wiped them with the heel of his hand, careful not to make a sound, and hugged the horse tighter.

Bram spoke last, and when he did, his voice had the weight of the whole type case behind it, every letter, every compartment.

"I hid in silence for thirty years," the old man said. "Thirty years I set whatever they told me to set and told myself the metal didn't know the difference. And then a man put his life's work in my keeping — molds, letters, a whole second alphabet — and asked me one thing: *don't let them make us blank.* And I kept it. I kept it in the dark under the floor like a coward keeps a promise, telling myself someday, someday. And then a woman leaves a boy on our step with a note that says forget the face, and I look at that boy, and I see his mother's jaw, and I understand that someday was never coming unless somebody walked toward it." A pause. "We are not sending him away. We are not making him blank. If the sum is one child against everything else, then the sum is wrong, because everything else is worthless if it buys that."

The floorboards creaked — Astrid moving, maybe, or just the house settling under the weight of what had been said.

"He stays," Astrid said finally, and her voice cracked clean through the middle of it, calm and edged and breaking all at once. "He stays, and he learns his letters, and when the record goes out — whenever it goes out — his mother's name goes in it. Elena Voss. Archivist. Murdered by the Committee she served. Set in type so deep no one can ever strike it again." Another pause, shorter. "And if they come for us for it, then they come for all of us, and at least we'll all be in the same sentence."

Tomas lay down on the loft floor, very slowly, and put his cheek against the cool boards, and listened to the quiet that followed — not an empty quiet but a full one, the quiet of a thing decided.

* * *

Afterward — he didn't know how long after; minutes or an hour, time went strange when your heart was pounding — he heard footsteps on the stairs, and Mira's head appeared over the loft edge, hair escaping its braid, eyes red-rimmed.

She saw him lying there with the horse and the wet face, and he watched her understand, watched it move across her expression like weather: *he heard. He heard all of it.*

Mira didn't say *how much did you hear*. She climbed up the rest of the way and sat down next to him, close, shoulder to shoulder, and looked out the little loft window at the harbor lights coming on across the water.

"They argued about you," she said quietly. It wasn't a question, so he didn't answer it. "Grown-ups argue about the people they love the hardest. It's a defect. Nobody's found the cure."

"Odile wanted to send me away," Tomas said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted.

"Odile wanted you alive. It's not the same thing, but it comes from the same place, if you squint." Mira bumped her shoulder against his. "And then she lost the argument. Do you know why she lost it?"

He shook his head.

"Because of you." Mira turned and looked at him straight on, the way adults almost never did, as if he were a page worth reading. "Because you sit at that case every morning and you set your name in metal, and you check on the Xs like they're chickens that might wander off, and you asked Bram what the letters wanted to say. You did that. You made yourself impossible to send away without anybody lifting a finger. That's the strongest magic there is, Tomas. Being worth staying for."

Downstairs, faintly, he heard Astrid at the composing stone, the small precise clicks of type being gathered, letter by letter, into lines. He knew that sound better than any lullaby now. Somebody down there was building sentences out of little metal people, and somewhere in those sentences, soon, there would be four letters — V, O, S, S — and before them three more, and his mother would stand up in metal the way he stood up in the stick on the high shelf, whole and named and unable to be melted by anyone who didn't first get past the whole stubborn armed household of them.

"Mira," he said.

"Hm?"

"If they ever come to make me blank—" He stopped. The words were too big; he had to carry them in pieces. "Don't let them. Promise. Even if I'm not — even if I'm somewhere else. Don't let them."

Mira was quiet a moment. Then she took his hand, the one with the permanent gray smudge along the edge of it, ink that would never wash out, and squeezed it hard enough to hurt a little, which is how he knew she meant it.

"Nobody," she said, "is melting anything."

Below them, the street door rattled — the particular double-knock, soft and coded, that meant a visitor who didn't want to be seen as one. Mira went still. From downstairs, Astrid's footsteps crossed to the door, paused, and then the bolt slid back, and a voice Tomas half-recognized — dry, careful, a voice that belonged to the Censor who had stood too long in the shop and looked too long at everything — said words that drifted up through the floorboards like cold air:

"Astrid. We need to talk. Not here — but tonight, and alone. I have something your father would have wanted. And I think you already know I shouldn't still have it."
