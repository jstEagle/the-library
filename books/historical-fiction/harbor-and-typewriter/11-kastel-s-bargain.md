## 11. Kastel's Bargain

The meeting place was Kastel's choosing: the disused rope-walk behind the salt warehouses, a long brick corridor open to the sky, where the harbor wind combed through and carried off the sound of voices before it could settle anywhere. Astrid understood the logic of it. No walls meant no ears in them. Even so, she took a roundabout route through the fish-market's closing chaos, doubled back twice along the canal, and arrived eleven minutes late to find Ivo Kastel already waiting, standing at the far end of the rope-walk with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring architecture.

He did not turn when she came in. That, too, she recognized as deliberate—a censor's habit of letting people approach him rather than the reverse. She stopped three paces away and said nothing. The wind filled the silence with the smell of tar and cold brine.

"Your father used to walk here," Kastel said at last, still facing the far wall where the old hemp fibers still clung in pale streaks to the brick. "After your mother died. He said the walking helped him think. Long straight lines for long straight thoughts." He turned then, slowly, and Astrid saw that the careful dryness in his face had cracked somewhere along its middle; he looked older than he had in her shop, older than he had any right to look in the six days since. "I've thought about this meeting for twenty years, Astrid. In one version or another."

"In what version did you bring soldiers?"

"In none of them." Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, more the scar tissue where a smile might once have been. "If I wanted you arrested, I've had six days and a hundred excuses. Your boy makes noise when he plays. Your compositor sings flat when he thinks no one is listening. Your apprentice has been to the fish-market four times this week on errands that don't exist."

Astrid kept her breathing level, though her pulse had begun a slow heavy drumming in her throat. *He knows. He has known.* "Then why am I here?"

"Because I have been a censor for nineteen years," Kastel said, "and in nineteen years I have never once been asked to preserve anything. Only to subtract." He reached inside his coat—an old gray thing, shabby at the cuffs, nothing like the formal dress he'd worn for his inspections—and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord. It was small. Smaller than it had any right to be, for the way it made the air between them change weight. "Do you know what happens to a man who spends his career deleting? He begins to keep things. First out of procedure—evidence copies, sealed under regulation fourteen. Then out of doubt. Then out of something he doesn't have a name for until it's too late and the keeping is the only honest act left in his life."

He held the packet out. Astrid did not take it.

"What is it?"

"A page," Kastel said. "One page, from an archive I have built for two decades in the false bottom of a bureau drawer, in a house where my wife believes I collect tax ledgers. Testimony of the Salt Gate evictions, from the woman who ran the lighthouse there—the old regime's purge, before yours purged theirs. My father signed those orders, Astrid. Did you know that? The Reyes family remembers their dead. I remember mine differently."

* * *

The wind shifted, rattling a loose tin sheet somewhere above them, and in that small metallic clatter Astrid heard her father's voice again—*paper is patient, Astrid, paper waits better than people do*—and hated herself for the comfort of it. She looked at the packet without touching it.

"You inspected my shop," she said. "Twice. You stood in my workroom with Tomas's shoes drying by the stove and you said nothing. Why? What are you buying, coming to me now?"

"Nothing. That is precisely the point, and precisely why you don't believe me." Kastel let the packet rest on his open palm between them, an offering and an accusation in equal measure. "A man in my position cannot buy innocence. But he can spend what he has. I am offering you testimony—the true account of the Salt Gate evictions, written in my own hand, signed with my own name. And I am asking one thing in exchange, which is not silence, not protection, and not your boy."

"Then ask it."

"Print me." His voice did not waver, but she saw his hand tighten fractionally around the oilcloth. "Set my confession in type alongside the rest of whatever it is Bram hums about and your fishwives whisper into your press. I signed removal orders. Forty-one families, Salt Gate, the winter of the frost. I was young and correct and ambitious, and every one of those names went through my hands before they went into the fire. If your counter-record exists—if it truly exists and I haven't invented it out of guilt and insomnia—then it is incomplete without me. A history that only holds the innocent is just another flattering mirror."

* * *

She walked away from him then—not leaving, only walking, because her legs demanded motion or they'd betray her—and stood beneath the broken section of roof where a thin column of gray daylight fell through the gap like a pillar in some ruined temple. Behind her, Kastel waited with the patience of a man who had waited nineteen years already.

Everything in her wanted to refuse him. That was the clean instinct, the safe one: the censor was the enemy, the censor had always been the enemy, and the revolution had taught Vellamare that enemies changed uniforms but never faces. To print his words would be to set the hand that had terrorized her city into the same pages as Elena Voss's name, the fishwives' weeping, Bram's decades of quiet grief. It felt like contamination.

But that was exactly the lie, wasn't it. She thought of her father's molds hidden in the cellar, of the second alphabet waiting in the dark—that history survived by being plural, by holding contradictions in the same forme without melting either one. Her whole life she had printed only what the Committee approved, and the approval had been the poison, not the ink. If she started deciding whose truth deserved type, she would become the Committee with a smaller budget and better handwriting.

"You understand what you're asking," she said, turning back. "If Kastel's name appears in our record and the record is discovered, you hang. If it isn't discovered, your own Committee erases you anyway the moment they trace it. You're not making a bargain, Censor. You're choosing how you lose."

"Yes," Kastel said simply. And then, with the first human warmth she had ever heard in him: "My wife will be provided for. That part is arranged."

"That part was arranged before you knocked on my door tonight."

"Before I ever knocked on anyone's door," he agreed. "That is the nature of the arrangement. One does the unforgivable thing gradually, in triplicate, and then one evening one finds oneself standing in a rope-walk offering a dead lighthouse-keeper's testimony to a printer's daughter, wondering how the ledger got so unbalanced." He held the packet out again, steady now. "Take it. Print it or burn it—but take it out of my house tonight, because tomorrow I inspect your shop again, officially, and I would rather search your premises for contraband than stand in your workroom knowing what sleeps in my bureau drawer."

* * *

She crossed the distance and took the packet. It weighed almost nothing. That was the terrible thing about paper—it carried everything and weighed nothing, and men spent their whole lives learning otherwise too late.

Inside the oilcloth she could feel the crispness of good rag paper, hoarded, the kind that hadn't been available in Vellamare for years. Written in a fine upright hand. Signed. Dated. *Ivo Kastel, son of Aldous Kastel, sets down the following as truth.*

"Why trust me?" she asked. "Truly. Give me the real answer, not the one that sounds well in a rope-walk."

Kastel considered her for a long moment. Out beyond the warehouse roofs, a ship's bell rang the second watch, thin and clear across the water.

"Because seven days ago I watched a woman hide a child in plain sight, under my own eyes, with nothing but stillness and dishwater," he said. "And because I have read every document this Committee has produced since the Harbor Uprising—I have had to read them, that is my punishment and my profession—and I can tell you with certainty that not one of them contains a single sentence anyone has ever risked anything to write. Risk is the whole test, Astrid. It is the only authentication that matters. Your father knew it. Your fishwives know it. Even your smuggler knows it." He buttoned his coat against the wind, and the censor's mask slid back over his features, smooth and official, though his eyes stayed human a moment longer. "Six weeks until the Archive seals. After that, everything unregistered becomes sedition—including this conversation. Tell Bram to sing flat more quietly. And tell your apprentice the fishwives miss her when she stays away too long."

He walked toward the street end of the rope-walk without another word, and Astrid stood alone under the broken roof with the packet in both hands, listening to his footsteps fade into the ordinary night noises of the harbor—a man going home to a house where his wife believed he collected tax ledgers.

She unwrapped one corner of the oilcloth just enough to see the first line, set down in that fine upright hand:

*I removed forty-one families from the record. Let the record now remove nothing, beginning with me.*

Astrid retied the cord, pressed the packet flat against her chest beneath her coat, and started for home through the darkening streets—already composing in her head, as her father had taught her, the order of type, the forme, the exact weight of a page that would make an enemy into a witness.

At the mouth of the lane she paused. Down by the water, lanterns were moving in formation along the quay—too many of them, moving too quietly, in the pattern she had seen only once before, seven years ago, on the night of a purge.

They were heading toward the fish-market.
