## 12. The Historian's Deadline

The proclamation arrived the way all Committee proclamations arrived: on paper so fine it felt like an insult, carried by a runner who would not meet anyone's eyes.

Astrid was sorting type when the bell above the shop door rang—three short strikes, the official cadence—and she set down the composing stick with the deliberate slowness of a woman who had learned that sudden movements invited questions. The runner was a boy of perhaps sixteen, in a Committee sash too large for him, holding out a folded broadsheet stamped with the Provisional seal: the harbor chain and broken oar, inked in the deep ceremonial blue reserved for matters of state.

"For immediate posting," he said. "Sign here."

She signed. Her hand did not shake. Seven years of practice had taught her hands better than that.

The runner left. Astrid waited until his footsteps faded into the fish-market noise before she unfolded the page, and even then she read it standing exactly where she was, in the middle of the shop, because Bram had taught her long ago that a printer's face was a public document and must be composed as carefully as any galley.

*By order of the Provisional Committee of Vellamare,* the proclamation began, *the Official Archive of the Harbor Revolution shall be sealed at the Victory Festival, six weeks hence, in the presence of the Committee and the people. Upon sealing, the Archive shall constitute the sole and complete historical record of the Revolution and its causes. All accounts, documents, testimonies, or printed matter contradicting the sealed Archive shall thereafter be deemed sedition against the people, punishable under Statute Nine.*

There was more—the usual flourishes about truth and unity, the usual signatures—but Astrid had stopped reading. The words *six weeks* had opened in her chest like a trapdoor.

She thought of the stack in the cellar. Three months of work: Kastel's pages, copied in Bram's cramped hand. The fishwives' testimony, still arriving in Mira's pockets, folded around herring wrappers and smuggled under loaves of bread. Her own account of the harbor massacre, written at night in a hand she barely recognized as hers, because she had not written anything true in seven years and the muscle had atrophied. Perhaps forty pages. A seed, nothing more.

And now the state had named a date after which every word of it would be a crime—not merely forbidden, which it already was, but *sedition*, carrying with it the dock, the transport ships, the names struck from the rolls. She knew how Statute Nine worked. She had printed its notices for years.

"Astrid." Bram's voice, low, from the doorway to the back room. He had seen the runner's sash through the window; he always saw everything first. "What is it?"

She held out the proclamation without turning. She heard him read it—heard the small wet sound of his breathing, which was how Bram wept, if he ever wept at all. He had been setting type for fifty-three years and his lungs carried the dust of every job he'd ever done.

"Six weeks," he said at last.

"Six weeks."

Behind them, in the back room where Tomas was supposed to be copying his letters under Mira's supervision, there was a sudden, telling silence. Children's silences were never empty. Astrid folded the proclamation, walked back, and found Tomas sitting very straight at the little table, his pencil hovering over the practice sheet, staring at them both with Elena Voss's gray eyes and the terrible attentiveness of a child who had already learned that adults' voices told him when the ground was about to shift.

"What's sedition?" he asked.

"It's a word grown-ups use," Mira said quickly, too quickly, and put her hand over his practice sheet as if she could cover words themselves.

"It means," Astrid said, because she had decided weeks ago that she would not lie to this child, not even in the small lubricating ways adults lied to children, "that they are going to make it illegal to remember."

Tomas considered this with the gravity of a judge. "You're not allowed to remember Mama?"

The shop went very quiet. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed over the fish-market scales, and the tide slapped the pilings three streets away—a sound that traveled through Vellamare's bones on days when the wind came off the water.

"No," Astrid said. "They are not allowed to stop us."

It was the first time she had said it aloud. Saying it made it real, and real things had weight, and the weight settled onto her shoulders with the familiar heft of a case of type being lifted into its frame.

* * *

That evening she laid it all out on the proofing table in the cellar, the way a captain might chart a voyage: every page they had, every resource, every liability. Odile sat on a barrel of contraband ink, sharpening a knife she had no need to sharpen. Bram stood at the table's edge with his hands clasped behind his back like a man at a funeral. Mira perched on the stairs, officially banished to bed an hour ago and officially ignoring it.

"So," Astrid said. "Six weeks. Let us do the arithmetic no one else will do."

She pulled Kastel's copied pages toward her. "His testimony, plus the fishwives—we have eleven accounts now, Mira, is that right?"

"Twelve. Widow Sarei finally talked. It took two visits." There was a flush of fierce pride on the girl's face that Astrid did not have the heart to temper, though she wanted to. Every new account was another name the Committee might trace.

"Twelve accounts. Call it sixty pages once set properly, with margins generous enough for old eyes. My father's molds give us the full alphabet, but—" she touched the wooden box, the false bottom, the second alphabet that had slept beneath the floor for seven years, "—we have one press. One. The Committee's own print works runs four presses day and night and it took them three weeks to produce the last commemorative run."

Odile stopped sharpening. "How many copies are we talking about? For it to matter. Not a keepsake. I mean *matter*."

Astrid had done this calculation twice already, once at midnight and once at dawn, and both times the number had stared back at her like something drowned.

"The fishwives alone can put a pamphlet into two hundred hands between the market and the mole. Add the dock crews Odile knows, the temple readers, the students—word travels faster than print, if the seed is planted right. But for it to survive the sealing—for there to be copies hidden in enough cellars and hulls and prayer books that no seizure can kill it—I want three thousand."

Bram made a soft sound, half laugh, half groan. "Three thousand. At our press, two pages at a time, allowing for drying—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Astrid had finished it herself: fourteen hours a night of pulling the bar, if the press held, if the type held, if their bodies held, and it still meant printing every single night for five weeks with nothing left over for error, for secrecy, for the inevitable disaster.

"That assumes we don't sleep," Odile said.

"It assumes we don't sleep much," Astrid corrected, "and that we start tonight."

"And the ink?" Bram asked quietly, which was the question that mattered, because honest ink was rationed to licensed shops by the sheet, and the shop's allotment would cover maybe four hundred pages before the discrepancy became a confession. "Three thousand pamphlets is a mountain of ink, girl. Where does a mountain come from?"

All eyes went to Odile. She examined her knife blade with theatrical interest, then sheathed it with a sigh that cost her something visible.

"I know a man in Salt Row who stretches lampblack with linseed and calls it ink and charges like it's gold," she said. "He doesn't ask what it's for because he doesn't want to know, which is the safest kind of customer relationship. It'll be thin. It'll smudge. Half your copies will look like they were printed in a rainstorm."

"They'll be readable," Astrid said.

"They'll be *barely* readable." Odile's voice cracked upward, and for a moment the gruffness fell away and Astrid saw underneath it: the woman who ran contraband ink was doing sums of her own, and her sums ended in Statute Nine. "Three thousand pamphlets of sedition moved through my route, Astrid. That's not a cargo anymore. That's a *fleet*. One informer—one—and it isn't the pamphlets they take, it's every soul on this staircase."

No one answered her, because there was nothing to answer with. The silence stretched, and in it Astrid heard the sea, and the creak of the house above them, and Tomas upstairs turning in a bed that was not his, dreaming whatever children dreamed when their mothers had been erased.

Then Mira spoke from the stairs, and her voice had a steadiness Astrid hadn't heard in it before.

"Widow Sarei said something on my second visit. She said the Committee thinks forgetting is a river—it carries everyone whether they consent or not. But she said memory is more like a net. You throw it wide enough, enough people hold an edge, and nothing sinks completely." The girl looked at Astrid directly. "Three thousand isn't too many. Three thousand is just a net with a lot of edges."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Odile swore softly, elaborately, in the dialect of the outer harbors, and pushed off the barrel.

"Fine. Fine. I'll see the ink man tomorrow. But understand me—all of you—" she pointed the rolled ledger she'd brought like a schoolmistress's rod, "—if this goes wrong, we were never a family. We were strangers caught in the same flood. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Astrid said, and hated the word even as she said it, because it was the old language of survival, the language of omission, and she had begun, dangerously, to believe in a different grammar.

* * *

She climbed to the shop near midnight, when the others had gone, and stood a long time before the locked case where the proclamation had been posted in the front window, as required. In the glass she could see the street, and beyond the street, faint in the distance, the harbor lanterns, and beyond them, invisible, the great stone Archive where the official history slept in its vaults, waiting to be sealed shut like a tomb.

Her father had built this shop the year she was born. She tried to remember his face and found, with a lurch, that she had to reach for it—that seven years of practicing forgetting had worn grooves in her own memory, and the man who had taught her to kern letters and read proofs upside-down was becoming, in her mind, a description rather than a presence. *Tall. Ink-stained cuffs. Laughed through his nose.* That was all the Committee had left her, and worst of all, they had used her own hands to do it.

She thought of Tomas asking *you're not allowed to remember Mama?*

Six weeks.

She unlocked the case, took the proclamation down, and instead of filing it as required, she carried it to the proofing press—the old one, the small hand-press her father had kept for testing jobs—and she set a portion of it in type. Just the key lines. *Sealed at the Victory Festival. All contrary accounts deemed sedition.*

She printed one copy. Then she broke the type apart, scattered the letters back into their cases, and folded the proof into the false-bottomed box beside her father's forbidden alphabet, next to the testimony of twelve fishwives and one censor.

Let the Committee announce its tomb. Tombs could be robbed. She had a press, a stolen alphabet, a smuggler, a child, an apprentice who quoted widows, and six weeks.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked—Bram, sleepless, or Mira, listening, or Tomas turning again in his dreams. Astrid blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, doing one final sum, the only one that mattered:

Not *can we print three thousand?*

But *who will we lose printing them?*

The answer arrived unbidden, in Bram's voice, in Odile's, in her own—and out in the harbor, through the dark, came the low moan of a ship's horn signaling the night tide, and Astrid knew with cold certainty that the next vessel to leave Vellamare would carry either the record or its keepers, and possibly not both.
