## 13. Night Shift

The first night, we printed nothing. We only prepared.

Astrid pulled down the false back of the composing stone—her father's old trick, a panel that looked like rot but was sound oak—and lifted out the molds one by one, laying them on felt like a surgeon laying out instruments. I had not seen them in thirty years. Old Reyes had shown me the hiding place once, drunk on plum brandy and grief for a country that hadn't yet learned what it would become, and said: *If they come for the words, Bram, the words must be able to hide faster than we can.*

He'd cast a second alphabet. Full founts, every size, matrices polished and oiled and wrapped in waxed cloth against the damp. Some letters still bore his thumbprints in the wax, pressed there the night he sealed them away. Astrid unwrapped each bundle and did not speak, and I watched her hold one mold up to the lamp, turning it, looking for her father's hands in the curve of an *a*.

Mira sat cross-legged on the floor with the fishwives' testimony spread around her in a fan of cheap paper—notes taken in pencil, in shorthand she'd invented herself so no informer could read over her shoulder. Odile lounged against the press with a cup of something she called tea and I called paint thinner, translating Mira's scratches into lines a compositor could set. And in the back room, asleep on a pallet between sacks of rag stock, Tomas breathed the small, even breaths of a child who did not yet know what the machines were making.

"Say it plain," Odile said. "What are we printing first?"

"The harbor," Astrid said. "The true account. Then Kastel's pages. Then the fishwives." She set the mold down. "Then everything else we can carry."

I cleared my throat. It took me a moment to find my voice, because the last time I had stood before this stone with forbidden type in my hands, I had been a young man, and I had put it all back in the dark and said nothing for three decades.

"Then we work nights," I said. "And we work like men who are already caught. No haste. Haste makes errors, and errors make evidence."

Odile raised her cup to me. "Listen to the old man. He's been waiting his whole life to say that."

He had. God forgive him—he had.

* * *

By the third night we had a rhythm, and the rhythm was the thing that saved us, more than any plan.

Odile came at moonset, slipping through the alley door with ink under her nails that wasn't ours—harbor ink, tar-black, cut with whale oil, the kind that dries grey if you don't know how to burn it into paper properly. She'd smuggled four hundred sheets of rag stock past the Customs House sewn into a bale of salt hay. "Paper, not people," she kept saying, as if reminding herself of a rule she'd already broken. "I move paper, you understand me. Paper doesn't breathe on you when the soldiers board."

But Tomas breathed. Tomas slept in the back room and woke some nights crying for a mother whose name had been struck from the rolls, and Odile was always the one who went to him, though she pretended otherwise. I heard her once, through the wall, in the low voice she used for no one else: *Your mother indexed the whole eastern archive, little man. Every ship, every cargo, every lie the old regime told about the grain shortage. She wrote it all down so nobody could say it didn't happen. That's a kind of brave most people can't spell.* And then, quieter: *We're going to finish her sentence.*

Kastel came on the fifth night, hood up, letting himself in with the key Astrid's father had given him twenty years ago—a key he had never returned and never reported. He brought his testimony in his own hand, forty pages folded small against his chest, and he handed it over like a man surrendering a weapon he should have surrendered long ago.

"I ordered suppressions," he read aloud that night, because he insisted the words be spoken once before they were set. He stood by the press with his back very straight, the way censors stand, and his voice cracked only twice. "I burned eleven crates of correspondence from the northern villages. I signed the erasure order for Elena Voss. I knew her. I knew her boy was alive somewhere, and I said nothing." He looked up. "Set it exactly as I've written it. Do not soften me. If I am to hang for a censor, let me hang for an honest one."

Nobody spoke. Astrid took the pages and began sorting type, and that was answer enough.

It fell to me to set his words. I want to confess that plainly: my hands, these ruined knuckles, these fingers that have placed ten million letters without complaint—they set the confession of a man who helped bury my friends. And I found, as the night went on and the forme filled, that I felt no hatred. Only the strange clean ache of a job done truly. Perhaps that is what forgiveness is. Not warmth. Just accuracy.

* * *

The true account of the harbor was the hardest, because I had seen it.

Seven years ago. The Uprising's first morning, before it had a name, before the Committee had a flag. I was forty years younger and stupid enough to be near the water at dawn. I saw the fishing boats refuse the requisition order—I saw old Marta Hollen stand up in her skiff with a lantern held high like a saint in a window painting—and I saw the Provisional Committee's gunners, our gunners, the revolution's own gunners, fire on unarmed boats in a crowded harbor while their officer read a proclamation about liberty from the quay.

Thirty-one dead. The official history says nine, and calls it a regrettable response to armed insurrection. The official history says Marta Hollen died a traitor. I saw her die a lighthouse.

For thirty-one nights I had carried that morning alone, the way I'd carried the purges, the way I'd carried old Reyes's molds and said nothing until Astrid dug them out herself. Silence is a habit like any other, and mine had gone thirty years without breaking. But now, setting the harbor account line by line—the boats, the lantern, the names of the dead gathered from Mira's fishwives—I felt something shift in my chest like a forme being locked, the quoins tightened, everything loose made fast.

Names. That was the labor of it. The official histories never bother with names; names are expensive, names are specific, a named dead man has a face and a mother and a grave someone might visit. We spent entire nights on names. Mira went back down to the fish market three more times, and came back with a list that grew from nineteen to twenty-six to thirty-one, each name checked twice, each widow's permission asked aloud: *May we print him? May we print her?*

"Yes," they said. Every one of them said yes. Some of them wept. One old woman gripped Mira's wrist and said, "Tell them he was afraid. Don't make him brave in the telling. He was afraid and he stayed anyway—that's the truth of him"—and Mira wrote it down exactly, and I set it exactly, and I believe to my dying day that no line I ever composed mattered more.

* * *

There is a sound a handpress makes when everything is right. The platen kisses the paper, the screw takes the strain, and then the pull—a deep, felt thud, less heard than experienced, a sound in the ribs more than the ears. Thud. Release. Thud. Release.

By the second week we had three hundred pages of the harbor account standing in formes, and the shop at night sounded like a heart. Odile fed the press. Astrid composed. I imposed and locked up and pulled the proofing impressions, and Mira—Mira washed balls and stacked paper and memorized everything, because that child misses nothing, and one day she will run a shop bigger than this one and she will not remember learning it, because it will have soaked into her the way the trade soaks into all of us, through the skin, with the ink.

We worked from moonset to the grey hour, then cleaned every surface, buried the formes in the coal cellar under a false floor, swept the stone with wet sawdust to take the ink smell out of the air, and opened the shutters as if we were merely tired printers, which by then we were.

I am seventy-one years old. I have not slept a full night since the twelfth of Harvestmonth and I do not care. There is a communion in night work that day people never know—the way talk comes easy at three in the morning, the way Astrid laughed for the first time in seven years over Odile's impression of a Customs inspector, the way Kastel sat in the corner sharpening our worn knives because a censor's hands wanted honest work, the way Tomas learned his letters off the scattered type and spelled his own name on the floor in ten-point and demanded we all admire it, and we did, we all stopped and admired it, six adults and a press bowing to a child's spelling.

Some nights Astrid worked with tears running silently down her face and her hands never stopping. Setting her father's type, I think, was a conversation with him. *Look, Papa. Look what you saved. Look who's using it.* I never said this to her. Some things you leave unspoken precisely because they are understood; a compositor knows which spaces need filling and which are meant to be blank.

One night near the end—it may have been the seventeenth, the fog was thick and the lamp gutted strangely—I locked the last forme of the harbor account and stood back, and Astrid pulled a proof, and we read it together under the light. Thirty-one names. Marta Hollen's lantern. The fishwife's line about fear.

"Well, Bram?" Odile said. "Is it true?"

"It's true," I said. My voice failed me on the second word and I had to start again. "It's truer than anything I ever set in forty years."

I thought of all the silence. The purges I watched from doorways. The neighbors' names I let slide out of my memory because remembering was dangerous and forgetting was free—so cheap, so terribly cheap, you could forget a whole man for the price of a downward glance. I had told myself for decades that survival was neutral, that keeping my hands working and my mouth shut harmed no one. Standing at that stone, smelling the fresh ink, hearing Tomas snore faintly through the wall, I finally let the excuse go. It had gotten heavy. I had carried it long enough.

I forgave myself there, badly and incompletely, the way old men forgive—not absolution but a decision to stop prosecuting. The record would not undo my silence. But it would end it. That was worth something. That had to be worth something.

* * *

On the nineteenth night, Odile did not come at moonset.

She came at midnight instead, early and wrong, and she did not come through the alley door laughing at her own jokes. She came pale, and she did not touch the tea Astrid poured her.

"There's a man on the Customs rolls," she said. "Transferred in last week. Vellamare-born, which is rare in that office, and he walked the quay tonight looking at ships like a man pricing them." She turned her cup without drinking. "And the Harbor Office posted new berth assignments for festival week. My *Salt Lark* is flagged for inspection, dawn after the Victory Festival. Same dawn the Archive seals."

Nobody spoke. Through the wall I could hear Tomas breathing in his sleep, quick and small, a heartbeat in the dark of the house.

"Festival week," Astrid said at last. Her voice was steady, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "That's five days."

"Five days to print what's left and get it aboard," Odile said. "And maybe five days is a gift. Maybe he was just pricing ships." She looked up, and there was no lie in her face, only the flat arithmetic of a woman who had survived the sea by counting honestly. "But I've been boarded before, and I know how the water feels the night before a storm. This feels like that."

Astrid nodded slowly, and I saw her doing sums behind her eyes—sheets remaining, hours remaining, hands available—and I saw the moment the sum came out impossible and she decided to attempt it anyway.

"Then we don't sleep again until it's done," she said. "All of it. Every page."

I picked up my composing stick.

My old bones sang against it. I let them sing.
