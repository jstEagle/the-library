## 14. Mira Is Followed

The fishwife's name was Sera Domen, and she smelled of brine and pipe smoke, and she told the truth the way she threw a net—wide, fast, and without apology.

"They came at low tide," she said, hands moving as if the memory were something she could haul in bodily. "Gray coats, forty of them, maybe more. The fishermen were pulling up their boats because of the storm warning. Nobody was protesting. *Nobody.* Old Petri was eighty-one and half-blind. He was carrying his nets." Her voice didn't crack; it hardened. "Your Committee says it was an armed uprising. I say I watched men with buckets and rope get shot on the stones, and I say the harbor ran red for two days and they brought in sand from the east quarry to cover it."

Mira wrote it all down in shorthand of her own devising—letters reversed, letters invented, words replaced by symbols that meant nothing to anyone but her. Astrid had taught her the real shorthand first, months ago, back when this was still about copying invoices cleanly. But Astrid had also taught her that any code can be broken by a patient man, so Mira had built a second one underneath, private, hers alone. If someone took the notebook from her, they would find what looked like a grocer's ledger: quantities of herring, prices per crate, weather notes.

The fishwives' testimony lived in the grocer's ledger now. Six women had spoken to her over three weeks—Sera Domen, the widow Anka, the two Vess sisters who fished out of the north slip, old Maren who everyone thought was mad but who missed nothing, and a young one called Petra who had lost her husband and would not give her husband's name even now, because names got people erased. Mira had learned to ask questions that sounded like commerce. How many boats were out that morning? What did the tide do? What did they do with the sand? A woman could answer those things to a girl buying herring without either of them committing a crime worth dying for.

Except it *was* a crime worth dying for, now. Mira understood that. She carried it in the notebook against her ribs like a warm stone.

"You're the printer's girl," Sera said, watching her fold the ledger away. Not a question.

"I'm getting herring for my mistress."

"Your mistress prints." Sera's eyes were shrewd, unbothered. "You think we don't know why you come around asking about tides and sand? We've been waiting three years for somebody to ask, girl. Three years. My only worry is that you're not careful enough to live long enough to print what we gave you."

Mira straightened her shoulders. "I'm careful."

"Everyone is careful," Sera said, "until the day they don't have to be. That's usually how they catch us." She pressed two smoked fish into Mira's hands—payment, or blessing, or both—and turned back to her nets. "Take the harbor road back. It's longer but there's more eyes on it. Never let them take you somewhere quiet."

* * *

Mira was four streets from the fish market when she noticed the man in the oilcloth coat.

She noticed him the way Bram had taught her to notice—not by looking, never by looking directly, but by reading reflections. He was in the dark window of the chandler's shop behind her, second pane, walking when she walked, stopping when she stopped to look at a display of rope she had no interest in.

Her heart did not leap; it sank, slow and cold, the way an anchor goes down. This was worse than fear somehow, this heavy certainty. She made herself count. Breathe in while counting to four, out while counting to six—the way Odile had drilled it into her one night on the wharf, laughing, saying *panic is just breath done badly.*

One man. Oilcloth coat. Gray hat, wrong for the rain, which meant he wasn't dressed for weather; he was dressed to blend with other men dressed wrong. He held a folded newspaper he never read. When she'd stopped at the rope display, he'd stopped at a distance of roughly thirty paces, and thirty paces was too far for idle following and exactly right for professional following.

*They know about the fishwives,* she thought, and then, harder, colder: *No. If they knew, I'd already be taken. They know only that a girl asks questions. They don't know what she carries.*

That distinction was everything. It was also, possibly, nothing. She couldn't know which. So she did what Sera had told her: she kept to streets with eyes.

The harbor road at midday was thick with porters and fish carts and sailors on shore leave. Mira joined its current deliberately, letting the crowd close behind her, and used the trick Bram had shown her—pass a stall, pause, circle back through the gap between stalls, and watch. If your follower crossed your path twice, he was committed. If he lost you once and didn't panic, he was good.

The man in the oilcloth coat crossed her path twice and did not panic.

*Good, then.* Good was bad. Good meant trained. Good meant the Provisional Committee's surveillance service, which meant this wasn't a thief or a lecher or a press-gang; this was the state, paying attention to a sixteen-year-old girl with a grocer's ledger full of erased people.

Her hands wanted to shake. She made them hold the herring instead, calmly, like a girl running errands.

Think, she ordered herself. Think the way Astrid thinks, backward from the outcome you need. The outcome she needed was simple and terrible: the notebook must reach the print shop or be destroyed, and preferably the first, and under no circumstances could she lead anyone to the shop. Not near it. Not within sight of its street. She realized with a lurch that she had been planning to go home directly, that she had walked this route a dozen times without thinking, that habit itself had been the danger all along.

She turned away from Reyes Street entirely.

The plan assembled itself in her mind piece by piece, the way she'd seen Astrid assemble a page—here a word, there a word, until the whole thing stood. There was a place, an alley behind the cooperage where barrels went to rot, where she and Odile had established a dead-drop months ago for ink deliveries that couldn't be seen passing through the front door. She could leave the ledger there, weighted under the third broken barrel, and Odile would collect it on her evening rounds. The notebook would travel home without her. Then she could spend the rest of the afternoon being visibly, boringly innocent—a girl buying thread, dawdling past the pastry window, going home late and scolded—while the paper went another way entirely.

But the drop was six streets east, and to get there she'd have to cross the Canal District, where the streets thinned and the eyes thinned with them, and the man behind her would see exactly where she went if he followed her into anywhere quiet.

*Sera's voice:* never let them take you somewhere quiet.

So she wouldn't go quietly. She'd go loudly. She'd go surrounded.

There was a funeral procession expected through the Merchant Quarter that afternoon—she'd heard the church bells rehearsing that morning, and funerals in Vellamare drew crowds the way blood drew gulls. If she could reach the procession, she could vanish into mourning neighbors, double back west, loop south, and approach the cooperage from the waterfront side at the hour when the fish carts jammed the lanes three deep.

It was not a perfect plan. It had maybe seven places where it could die. But standing still was the eighth place, so Mira bought a spool of thread she didn't need, smiled at the haberdasher, and stepped out into the street with her heart beating hard and her face arranged into the pleasant vacancy of a girl with no thoughts at all.

* * *

The funeral was for a shipping magnate nobody loved, which meant the crowds were larger out of spectacle than grief, and better for it. Hundreds packed the square before the Cathedral of Tides, black-shawled women, merchants in their dull best, children weaving between legs with pennies for the sweet sellers. Bells crashed overhead. Somewhere a choir was rehearsing a dirge that sounded, through stone walls, like the sea arguing with itself.

Mira moved into the crowd's edge and began to work.

First: the reflection check, done in the polished brass plate of a memorial fountain without turning her head. There—oilcloth coat, fifty paces, pushing gently through the crowd, unhurried. He had not lost her. He was very good.

Second: shed the obvious. She unwound her headscarf and stuffed it in her pocket—her scarf was distinctive, blue with a torn hem, and anyone describing her would describe the scarf. She pulled her hair loose so it fell differently, changed her walk from quick to ambling, and bought a sugared chestnut cone from a brazier cart so that her hands were occupied and her smell changed from fish smoke to caramel. Small things. Bram said pursuit lived and died on small things: a pursuer was looking for a pattern, and every pattern you broke made his picture of you blur.

Third: the crowd itself. She let the current take her toward the cathedral steps, then ducked sideways through a knot of mourners, emerged, checked the brass plate. Still there. Slower now—he was fighting the crowd's grain. Good. Crowds punish followers more than they punish fugitives, if the fugitive doesn't run.

She threaded deeper. Under the cathedral's flank there was a cloister passage, open to the public on festival days, cutting from the square to the lane behind. Today, with bells and crowds, it would be open. She timed her move to a swell of the choir, slipped down the steps, cut left, and was through the arch before the dirge ended—and in the passage's dim stone throat she allowed herself, finally, to run.

Not far. Twenty paces to the far arch, out into the back lane, then instantly slow again, chestnut cone raised, biting casually, a girl enjoying a treat. Behind her the passage stayed empty for one heartbeat, two, five—

Then the oilcloth coat emerged, and stopped, and stood in the archway doing something much worse than running.

He looked both ways. Calmly. Methodically. Left, right, left again. Reading the lane the way Astrid read a page of type.

And then he did not turn toward her at all. He turned west. Toward the harbor road. Toward the direction she had *come* from.

Toward the fish market.

Mira stood frozen with caramel on her fingers while her mind screamed the implication at her. He wasn't following her anymore. He had marked her, catalogued her, and let her go—he'd decided the girl was a dead end, or a tail to be picked up later, or—

Or he hadn't been following her at all.

*He was going where I'd been.* To the market. To the stalls. To Sera Domen and her nets and her loud, fearless mouth.

The chestnut cone dropped from her hand. She didn't notice. Fear arrived all at once now, no longer cold but white-hot, because this was the thing she hadn't planned for: she had thought of herself as the thing being protected, the cargo, the risk to others. It had never fully landed until this moment that the women who talked to her were the ones exposed. She was the disease vector. Every testimony she collected put a witness on a list.

Three years Sera had waited for someone to ask. Mira would not be the reason the asking killed her.

She ran—for real this time, openly, no craft about it—cutting back through the cloister, across the square's churn, down toward the harbor road with her lungs burning. The crowd fought her. Porters, carts, a wall of black shawls. She went under and around them, apologizing without meaning it, thinking only: *the market, the market, warn her, get her off the stalls, get the names out of the air—*

She burst onto the harbor road and the salt wind hit her and she scanned wildly for the oilcloth coat and did not see it, which frightened her more than seeing it would have. In the market square beyond, the stalls stood in their usual rows, gulls screaming above the awnings, ordinary life proceeding in its ordinary roar.

Sera Domen was at her usual place, nets spread, pipe lit.

Mira reached her, gasping, and grabbed her arm, and had absolutely nothing prepared to say. All her clever codes and routes and reflections collapsed into one whispered sentence:

"He's coming. A man in an oilcloth coat. You have to go, now, out the north end, don't go home, go to your sister's—"

Sera looked at her for a long second. Then the old woman did the most astonishing thing Mira had ever seen: she yawned. She stretched, scratched under her arm, began gathering her nets with the leisurely insolence of a woman who had suddenly remembered an appointment. "North end, sister's house," she murmured. "Which coat?"

"Oilcloth. Gray hat. Newspaper he doesn't read."

"Mm." Sera's hands worked steadily. "Girl. Look at the north entrance. Tell me if he's alone."

Mira forced herself to look, to read reflections in the wet stone and the polished brass scales of the fishmongers' row. And there—at the mouth of the square, pausing, surveying—the gray hat. And behind him, at twenty paces, a second man. No newspaper. Hands folded. Standing like a soldier standing at ease.

"Two," Mira breathed. "Two of them."

"Then it's a sweep, not a tail." Sera said it the way she'd report the price of cod. She began rolling her nets with brisk efficiency and pitched her voice for Mira alone. "Listen fast. The Vess sisters dock at the north slip—they'll be on the water by now, safe. Widow Anka's lame; she can't run; send word to her through the oyster girl, brown shawl, trustworthy. Maren's mad enough that they'll get nothing from her if they take her, and they know it, they won't bother. It's me and Petra they'll want." Her eyes flicked up, sharp and calm. "Petra sells at the third stall. You see her?"

Mira saw her—young widow, dark hair, laughing at something a customer said, unaware.

"Walk past her stall," Sera said. "Drop your handkerchief beside her basket. When she picks it up, tell her: *tide's wrong for fishing.* She'll understand. Then you go home, girl, and you go the long way, and you don't come back to this market for a month. A month, you hear me? Whatever you hear happens here, you stay away."

"But your testimony—" The words tore out of Mira before she could weigh them. "If they take you—"

"Then they take an old woman who sold fish," Sera said. "And they find nothing, because I've said nothing to anyone but a girl buying herring, and girls' notebooks are grocer's ledgers." Her eyes went, briefly, unbearably, to Mira's pocket. "Which you will carry home tonight if it costs you your arms. That page is worth ten of me. Now *go.*"

Mira went. She walked—forced herself to walk—past the third stall, and let the handkerchief fall, and leaned close while the young widow gathered it in, and whispered the tide into her ear, and watched the color drain from Petra's face and the laughter assemble itself back over it like a shutter closing. Two women, one sentence, an entire network folding itself away in the space of ten seconds.

At the north entrance of the market, Mira paused at a barrel of mackerel and used its brass scale for one last reflection.

The gray-hatted man had reached Sera's stall. He was holding up something—a paper, a printed notice, a face. Sera was shaking her head slowly, pointing vaguely south, the picture of a helpful idiot. The second man had drifted toward Petra's stall, unhurried, hands still folded.

They would get nothing today. The fishwives were scattered, silent, forewarned. The testimony was safe against Mira's ribs, wrapped in herring-prices and weather notes.

She walked out of the market at the south end, the long way home, past the pastry window where she dawdled, past the haberdasher where she bought a second spool of thread she didn't need, a girl with no thoughts at all. Only when she turned onto Reyes Street, at dusk, with the shop's lamplit window glowing ahead of her and the smell of ink and oil seeping out under the door like a welcome—only then did her legs finally begin to shake, great shuddering tremors she couldn't stop, and she stood a moment in the cold letting them pass, because Astrid should not see her like this, not yet, not first.

Inside, the family was gathered around the worktable: Astrid with type dust on her fingers, Bram bent over the composing stone, Odile sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, Tomas asleep in the chair by the stove with a wooden letter A clutched in his fist.

They looked up. Astrid saw it immediately, of course. She always did. Her eyes went from Mira's face to the pocket where the ledger rode and back again, and her voice came out low and careful.

"Mira. What happened?"

Mira set the ledger on the worktable with both hands, steadying it, steadying herself. Then she told them about the man in the oilcloth coat, and the second man with the folded hands, and the sweep of the fish market—and finished, breathless:

"They weren't following me. They were retracing me. Which means someone told them a girl has been asking questions in the harbor quarter—which means they're already looking at the fishwives, and if they're looking at the fishwives, it won't be long before they start wondering who sent her." She swallowed. "Astrid—it won't be long before they wonder about the shop."

The room went very still. The stove ticked. Tomas slept on, small and oblivious, the letter A loose in his curled fingers.

Odile was the first to move. She laid the knife down flat on the table, gently, like setting a card she'd been keeping hidden.

"Well," she said, "then we'd best find out who talked."
