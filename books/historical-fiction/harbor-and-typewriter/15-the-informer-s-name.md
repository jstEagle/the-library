## 15. The Informer's Name

Odile Ferro had a rule about names: she never learned one she couldn't afford to bury. In twenty years of running contraband up and down the Vellamare coast—ink before the revolution, gunpowder during it, ink again after, because the more things changed the more they needed printing—the only ledger she trusted was the one in her head, written in a cipher of nicknames and half-truths. The man who brought her saffron from the southern ports was *the Perfume*. The harbormaster's clerk was *the Quill*. The woman who sold her rope was simply *Rope*, because Odile had never bothered to ask for anything else.

But there was one name she had carried for years, against all her own rules, worn smooth like a coin rubbed between thumb and forefinger: *Sable*.

Real name: Dario Senn. A smuggler out of the Salt Docks, lean as a whipstock, quick with a smile, quicker with an angle. They had run cargo together in the years before the uprising—two hulls, one route, splitting the risk the way sailors split a watch. She had taught him the blind cove under the Gullspit cliffs. He had taught her which customs men could be bought with wine and which needed coin. For two winters they had been as close to partners as Odile Ferro ever let anyone be.

Then the revolution came, and Sable did what Sable always did. He found the angle.

She hadn't thought about him in months. Now, walking the Salt Docks in the gray hour before dawn with her collar up and a knife at her hip that she hoped very much not to use, she thought of nothing else.

It had taken her four days to trace it. Four days since she'd burned her ledgers in the grate behind the print shop, feeding the pages to the flames page by page while Astrid Reyes watched from the doorway with soot on her cheek and asked no questions, which was why Odile liked the woman more than she had planned to. Four days of working backward through the network like a man following a wet footprint across a dry deck: the informer had known where her ink caches were stowed; therefore he had known her drop points; therefore he had talked to someone inside the chain; therefore—

Therefore Sable. Of course it was Sable. It had the shape of him all over it. The Committee's patrol boats had started sweeping the eastern channel three weeks ago, exactly where her route bent closest to shore. Her runner Pell had been stopped and questioned—not searched, just questioned, politely, by officers who knew his face and his boat's name and the exact weight of his usual cargo. That kind of precision wasn't diligence. That kind of precision was purchased.

And last night, the confirmation. One of her cove contacts—a fisherman who ferried sealed crates without ever asking what made them heavy—had told her a stranger had been asking after the Gullspit caves. Describing them. Describing *her*. Tall woman, gray streak in her hair, scarred knuckles, laughs like a hatch being slammed. The description was good. Too good. Only two people alive knew she laughed at anything.

Odile stood at the rail of the abandoned ferry slip and looked down into the black water, and understood with perfect clarity what she was going to have to do, and hated herself for understanding it so quickly.

* * *

The Salt Wake tavern opened when the fishing bells rang and closed when they rang again, twelve hours later, and in between it served a liquor the owner distilled from whatever the harbor gave him, which explained why drinking it felt like swallowing the harbor. Odile took her usual table in the back corner, where the wall met the window and no one could come at her from behind, and waited.

Sable came at the third bell, because Sable was a creature of habit even now, even with the city seething around them like a kicked hive. He still wore his coat too big for him, sleeves rolled twice, still had that smile sitting on his face like a hat he'd forgotten to take off. He spotted her and his stride hitched—half a step, barely a stutter, but Odile had spent twenty years reading hulls for hairline cracks and she read it plainly.

*He knows I know.* Or at least he suspected. Good. Suspicion kept men honest faster than knives did.

"Ferro." He slid into the seat across from her uninvited, as if the years between them were a hallway and not a chasm. "They said you'd been seen on the docks. I said, no, not Ferro. She's smarter than that."

"Someone's been saying a lot of things about me lately," Odile said. "To people who get paid by the word."

The smile stayed. His eyes didn't. "Haven't seen you since the Festival preparations started. Busy season?"

"Busy enough. You?"

"Surviving." He turned his cup right way round but didn't drink. "Which is the whole trade these days, isn't it? Before the revolution we were criminals. After, we're patriots. Either way somebody wants us hanged. I just try to stay on the side doing the hanging."

There it was. Not a confession—he'd never be careless enough for that—but close enough that the air between them went tight. Odile let the silence sit. Silence was cargo too; you could move more truth in it than in words.

"You've been talking to Committee men," she said at last. Flat. Not a question, because questions invited answers, and she didn't want to watch him construct one.

Sable picked up his cup and drank this time. Set it down slow. "I've been talking to whoever keeps me breathing," he said. "Same as you. Same as everyone in this city since the archbishop's head came off the old mint. You think the Provisional Committee is different from the Crown? Different flag, same gallows. The only thing that's changed is the list of names on it."

"And my name's on their new list. With my routes attached. Drawn in your handwriting."

"Prove that," he said softly.

"I don't need to prove it. I need to decide what to do about it." She leaned forward, arms on the table, and let him see everything in her face, because sometimes the most honest weapon was letting a man know exactly how much you wanted to use the other ones. "Here's how I see it, Sable. Two roads. First road: I go to my contact in the Committee—yes, I have one, yes, that surprises you, which tells me you haven't learned as much as you sold them—and I give them Dario Senn of the Salt Docks. Informer. Traitor to the harbor code. They'll take him apart in the old fortress in ways I won't describe at a table where people eat. And the price is that to make the accusation stick, I show them how I know—which means showing them my routes, my drops, my caves, every plank of everything I've built for twenty years. So road one, I kill you and kill myself doing it."

The tavern noise went on around them—dice, argument, the bell of the till—but at their table there was nothing.

"Second road?" Sable said.

"Second road, you go to your Committee friends tonight and you tell them you misidentified the route. Wrong boat, wrong woman. You feed them something else instead—anything, some Crown loyalist fiction, they're starving for those—and then you take whatever ship leaves this harbor first and you don't come back. Ever. Because if I ever see your face on this coast again, road one stops being a threat and becomes an appointment."

He was quiet a long moment. Then he laughed, low, without any humor in it, and for a second he sounded like the man she'd shared a fire with in the blind cove, ten years and one revolution ago. "You always did keep the accounts clean, Ferro. That's why I never robbed you. Robbing you would've been easier than facing the audit." He turned the cup again. "You want to know why I did it?"

"No."

"They offered me a license. A *legal* license. Legitimate cargo, protected routes, a stamp on my manifest. Do you understand what that means for a man like us? Twenty years looking over my shoulder, and they offer to make me respectable. All it cost was telling a few clerks where Ferro's boats bend around the point." He shrugged, and the shrug was the saddest thing Odile had seen all year. "That's the revolution, Ferro. It didn't open the gates. It just handed out keys to whoever helped lock them."

"Save the philosophy. I've heard better from drowned men." Odile stood, dropped a coin on the table for the look of the thing. "Tonight. The misidentification. Then gone. You have until the evening tide, and then I stop choosing between roads and start taking both."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll be at your door before their soldiers are. And unlike them, I know all your doors."

* * *

She walked the long way back along the seawall, because she needed the walk and because the seawall was the one place in Vellamare where nobody could stand behind a wall and listen. The morning fog was lifting off the water in rags. Out in the roads, the fishing fleet was coming in, and beyond it, gray in the haze, rode the Committee's new patrol cutters—fast, well-armed, painted in the revolution's colors, crewed by men who six years ago had been hauling nets or breaking rocks and now carried muskets and lists.

Lists. Everything came down to lists now. Names on lists, cargo on lists, faces on lists. She thought of the boy at the print shop—Tomas, small and solemn, who held a type mold in his fist like a talisman and asked questions in a whisper, as if the walls had ears even here. *Forget the face*, the note had said. The Committee wrote forgetfulness the way other governments wrote laws.

And here she was, Odile Ferro, who had spent her whole life keeping two sets of books and trusting neither, discovering that somewhere along the line she'd started keeping a third: the one Bram called the record, the true account, the thing they were setting in type in the back of the Reyes shop while the city slept. Fishwives' testimony. A censor's confession. The real story of the harbor massacre, in letters that wouldn't burn.

She had told Astrid she moved paper, not people. She was starting to suspect the paper had opinions about that.

Because that was the arithmetic that wouldn't leave her alone, out there on the seawall with the gulls screaming their heads off about something: if she gave Sable to the Committee, she might buy safety for the route. And the route was the only artery the record had. Three thousand pamphlets weren't going to walk onto a ship by themselves. If the patrols swept her channel, if her caches were seized, if Pell was stopped with a crate of the true history under his oilskins—then everything the print shop bled for would rot in a Committee warehouse, stamped *sedition*, and the Archive would seal, and Elena Voss and the fishwives and everyone the harbor lost would go into the dark for good.

So maybe road one wasn't suicide after all. Maybe it was worse than that. Maybe it was betrayal with a profit margin.

Odile stopped at the seawall's end and stared at the water, and did the thing she least wanted to do, which was be honest with herself.

She wanted to give him up. That was the ugly truth of it, the thing she'd been circling since the tavern. Not for the route—for revenge. Sable had sold her, sold *her*, Odile Ferro, whose only crime in this world had been staying alive, and there was a hot little furnace in her chest that had been burning since the fisherman described her laugh to a stranger. Handing him to the Committee would feel like justice. It would taste like it too, for about an hour.

And that was exactly how it happened, wasn't it? That was the whole rotten machine in miniature. Somebody wronged you, and you got the chance to be righteous with someone else's blood, and you took it, and then you needed another chance, and pretty soon you were wearing a uniform or carrying a stamp and calling your vengeance a license. She'd watched it happen to half the harbor. The fishermen who became informers because the old harbormasters had cheated them. The dockers who joined the militia because the press gangs had taken their brothers. Every one of them had a grievance with a receipt.

Every one of them had been sure, right up to the moment they weren't, that they were settling accounts rather than opening new ones.

If she gave Sable to the Committee, she'd be doing the revolution's work for free. And the revolution, as far as Odile Ferro could tell, was a customer who never paid its debts—it just put your name on a list.

*No,* she decided, watching a cutter carve its wake across the channel. *Not free. Not ever.*

Let him run. Let him live out his days jumping at shadows in some foreign port, flinching at gray-haired women with scarred knuckles. That was a sentence too, and a longer one than the fortress would hand down, and it cost the route nothing. Cost her pride a great deal, but pride was the cheapest thing she owned and always had been.

She turned back toward the print shop, and her mood lifted a degree, the way it always did when she chose the harder mercy over the sweeter cruelty. Twenty years of smuggling had taught her one law above all others: the sea doesn't care who you were loyal to, only whether your hull holds. Hers held. Barely, leaking a little, patched with compromises—but it held.

She was halfway up Harbor Street, rehearsing what she'd tell Astrid—*problem handled, don't ask, buy me a drink someday*—when she saw the crowd.

It was gathered outside the Customs House, quiet in the wrong way, the way crowds go quiet when they're watching something done to someone. Odile shouldered in along the edge, ready with a curse for anyone who objected, and stopped.

Two militiamen were nailing a broadsheet to the Customs House post. Freshly printed. Official stock, Committee watermark. Odile read it upside down from six feet away, because twenty years of reading manifests at a glance dies hard, and when she finished reading it the seawall and the sea and the whole gray morning seemed to tilt beneath her feet.

It was a notice of seizure. An inventory of confiscated materials. Ink, presses, type molds—*type molds*, someone had given them the molds—and a list of persons sought for questioning in the matter of seditious publication.

Three names. Bram Hollis, compositor. Mira Reyes, apprentice.

Astrid Reyes, printer.

And below them, in smaller type, as if the Committee itself wasn't sure yet whether the boy existed: *one male child, age approximately six, name unknown.*

Odile read it twice. Then she turned and ran for the print shop, past the crowd, past the cutters in the channel, past every careful plan she'd made in the last hour, because the tide tables and the tavern bargains and the long moral arithmetic of Dario Senn had all just been rendered moot by a single sheet of official paper.

Whatever she was carrying out of Vellamare, it would have to be people after all.

The shop, when she reached it, stood with its door hanging open and its windows dark, and the smell of ink was thick in the street—and from inside came the sound of someone moving, quietly, methodically, like a person who had very little time and knew exactly what to take.
