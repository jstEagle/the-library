## 16. A Raid, Delayed

The knock came at half past eleven, three quick raps and then a fourth, delayed—the rhythm Ivo Kastel had used on my father's door for years, back when his visits meant wine and arguments about typography rather than terror.

I froze with the shutter half-latched. Below me, in the dark of the shop, Mira looked up from the composing stone, her hands still black to the wrist. Bram set down his burnisher without a sound. And Tomas—Tomas went still in the way he had learned too young, folding himself into the shadow behind the paper racks as if stillness were a coat he could put on.

I opened the door a finger's width. Kastel stood in the rain with his collar up, alone, no lantern, no clerk, no soldiers at his back. That absence frightened me more than an escort would have. Censors traveled with witnesses; it was how they kept their hands clean.

"Let me in, Astrid." His voice was low and scraped raw. "There isn't time to be careful."

* * *

He stood dripping in the middle of the shop floor, hat in hand, and did not look at the press, or the type cabinets, or the corner where a six-year-old boy was breathing so quietly that I could not hear him over the rain. He looked only at me.

"They come tomorrow," he said. "Before dawn. Not an inspection—a seizure. Writ signed by Verren himself. The shop will be stripped, the press broken, the molds confiscated."

"Molds." My mouth went dry. "They've never mentioned molds before."

"They haven't needed to. Someone told them your father kept a second alphabet. Someone who has seen inside this room." Kastel's eyes moved, finally, briefly, across the shelves, the hidden panel behind the drying rack that he had surely noticed on some earlier visit and never once remarked upon—and I understood then that every inspection for seven years had been a man walking through this shop with his eyes open and his mouth shut, keeping his own private inventory of what he chose not to see.

"Who informed?"

"I don't know. It doesn't matter. What matters is the hour." He pulled a folded paper from inside his coat—damp at the edges, official at the corners. "Seizure is scheduled for the fifth bell. The watch changes at the fourth. There will be a gap of perhaps ten minutes when the harbor lane goes unpatrolled. If you have anything in this building you cannot lose, Astrid Reyes, it leaves tonight, or it burns tomorrow with everything else."

Bram rose from his stool, slow as a ship coming about. He had not spoken in front of Kastel since the purges; now his voice came out like gravel settling. "Why tell us? You've had seven years to bury us, Ivo. Why dig us out instead?"

Kastel held the old compositor's gaze without flinching. "Because I am tired, Bram Helder, of being the man who writes down what should never have happened. Tomorrow they take this shop either way. But what you do between now and the fifth bell—" He put the writ on the counter, face down, as if he could not bear its signature facing the room. "That is between you and whatever you believe survives us."

He turned to go. At the door he stopped, and for one moment his composure cracked along a seam I hadn't known was there. "Your father once told me that a print shop is a promise made to people who haven't been born yet. I thought it was sentiment." He settled his hat. "It's the only true thing anyone ever said to me."

Then he was gone into the rain, and the latch clicked behind him, and we had five hours.

* * *

We worked like people possessed, because we were.

Odile arrived within the hour—Mira had rung the fish-market signal twice, which meant *come now, bring nothing you love*—and took one look at our faces and started giving orders as though she'd been waiting all week for permission to commandeer someone else's life.

"Paper first. Paper floats and paper lies flat. Molds after—wrap them in wool, they'll ride quiet in the false bottom under the salt barrels. Press stays."

"The press does *not* stay," I said.

"Astrid." She gripped my shoulders, rain and river water still on her coat, her voice gentler than I had ever heard it. "A press is two hundred pounds of iron and a signature in the guild rolls. You cannot move it in ten minutes through a changing watch, and even if you could, moving it tells them exactly what you were doing here. Leave them the shell. Save the heart."

She was right, and I hated her for it. The press had been my father's before it was mine; its bed still carried the shallow groove where his forearm rested during long runs, a wear-mark shaped like devotion. Leaving it was leaving him a second time. But my father had left me more than iron. He had left me letters.

Bram brought up the molds from behind the drying rack while Odile and I packed. There were more of them than I had ever counted in daylight—four crates of lead and brass, my father's second alphabet complete and gleaming with the grease of decades, plus the deeper cache beneath: pamphlets from the old regime's fall, testimonies he'd printed and could never distribute, a broadside with a woodcut of Elena Voss's face on it, young and furious and holding a book aloft like a torch.

Tomas came out of his shadow when he saw that one. He touched the paper face with one finger, very lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal.

"That's Mama," he said. "Before."

"Yes," I said, and knelt beside him. "And after tonight, nobody takes her away again. Not her face, not her name, not you."

He looked at me with those archivist's eyes, cataloguing, believing me the way children believe—absolutely, dangerously. I wrapped the broadside in oilcloth myself and did not let it out of my hands.

* * *

The plan was simple, and simplicity was its only virtue: two trips, the first to Odile's boat with the molds and papers, the second—if the watch change held—with Tomas himself, hidden in the salt-barrel cart under forty pounds of herring brine-soaked canvas. Mira would walk the route ahead as lookout. Bram would stay in the shop, an old man asleep above his work, unremarkable, unthreatening. If the soldiers found nothing but a press and a tired printer, the writ would be satisfied and no one would look further.

"And you?" Bram asked me quietly, while the women loaded the cart. "Where do you plan to be?"

"Here," I said. "When they come through that door, someone with the name on the lease has to be standing in it. Otherwise they declare the shop abandoned and start pulling up floorboards on suspicion alone."

"That's not courage, girl. That's arithmetic."

"In Vellamare, Bram, it's lately become the same thing."

He almost smiled. Then he took both my hands in his ink-stained ones, and his face did something I had never seen it do—he let it be afraid in front of me. "Seven years I watched you build this silence, plank by plank, like a coffin for yourself. Tonight I get to watch you tear it open. Whatever happens at dawn—" His voice caught. "Your father would have set your name in twenty-four point and printed it on the sky."

"Twenty-four point," I said, "is wasteful. He'd have used eighteen."

We laughed, badly, the way people laugh at funerals and before battles, and then the fourth bell rang across the harbor and there was no more time for anything but motion.

* * *

The first trip went clean. Odile and Mira moved the cart down the harbor lane through a drizzle that hid everything and everyone; I watched from the upstairs window and counted their shadows past the customs house and gone. Twenty minutes later Odile returned alone, breathing hard, salt spray to her knees, and gave me the nod that meant the molds lay safe below her deck.

Then the second trip, the one that mattered.

Tomas climbed into the barrel cart without a word. We had drilled it twice—breathe slow, count to a thousand, don't cough no matter what the herring smells like—but at the last moment his hand shot out and grabbed my sleeve.

"You'll come after," he said. Not a question. A demand, delivered with Elena Voss's exact jaw set at the angle of absolute certainty.

"After," I said. "Count to a thousand. Twice, if you need to."

Mira took the leads. Odile took the shafts. And I stood in the doorway of my father's shop and watched the cart turn the corner and disappear, and felt the strangest sensation—as if I had just sent my own heart out into the rain under a tarpaulin, and the world was now free to do its worst to everything that remained.

* * *

They came early. Of course they came early. Revolutions keep their own time.

It was not the fifth bell but the quarter-hour before when the boots arrived—not the promised gap but the full patrol, twelve soldiers and a lieutenant with the writ already unfolded, and no ten minutes of darkness anywhere in the schedule for anything to slip through. They came around the corner of Fishhook Lane in column, lanterns hooded, and I was still standing in the doorway like a woman turned to salt.

"Reyes Print Shop," the lieutenant read, though he was standing in front of it. "By authority of the Provisional Committee. Open your premises."

"It's open," I said. "It has always been open. That's rather the point of a shop."

I paid for the flippancy immediately—one soldier took my arm harder than necessary, and another drove his boot-butt into the doorframe hard enough to crack the wood my father had planed himself. Violation has a sound, I learned that night. It is splintering pine and iron dragged across stone and men's voices raised not in anger but in boredom, the boredom of men breaking things because a signature told them to.

They worked methodically, worse than vandals. Type cases overturned in cascades of letterfall—forty years of my father's patience scattering across the floorboards in a tide of e's and t's and small forgotten punctuation. The press: pried at, tested, marked with chalk for the breakers who would come at light. The drying rack torn from the wall, revealing the empty cavity behind it, and the lieutenant stared into that emptiness for a long moment while my pulse tried to climb out through my throat.

"There was a damp problem," I said. "Bricks were removed for airflow."

He looked at me. I looked back. Seven years of inspections, seven years of practiced silences, and every hour of them came down to this: whether my face was better built than his suspicions.

"Airflow," he repeated, without inflection, and wrote something in his book, and moved on.

They found Bram's cot. They found the ledger of approved work, and leafed through it with contempt. They found nothing else, because there was nothing else left to find—only an old man descending the stairs in his nightshirt, blinking, indignant, perfectly ordinary, complaining in the voice of the wronged elderly everywhere about the hour and the mess and the state of the nation.

They arrested him anyway.

"Not for anything specific," the lieutenant said, almost apologetically, as the soldiers bound Bram's wrists with cord. "For questioning. Names come with seizures; it's procedure. Someone always knows something."

Bram passed me at the door. His hands were tied and his hair was down and he looked, for the first time since I'd known him, exactly as old as he was. And he winked at me.

Winked. As if to say: *the boy is counting, the molds are under the salt, the alphabet survives, you did the arithmetic right.* As if arrest were merely the price of a job finished well.

I stood in the wreckage of the only home I had left and did not cry until the boots receded down Fishhook Lane, and then I cried exactly four tears, standing among my father's scattered letters, because there was no time for more.

* * *

Dawn found me at Odile's boat, soaked through, kneeling on the deck planks while Tomas emerged from under the tarpaulin smelling of herring and triumph, having counted to a thousand four times. Mira sat against the mast with her chin up and her eyes red-rimmed, having walked the boy the last stretch herself through streets she was still, officially, too young to walk alone. The molds lay safe in the false bottom. The broadside with Elena Voss's face lay wrapped against my chest, under my coat, over my heart.

The shop was gone. In every way that mattered, the shop had been gone since before the soldiers reached it—we had hollowed it ourselves, and what they broke was only the shell. But knowing this did not stop the grief. Some part of me had believed, these seven years, that silence was a wall thick enough to live inside. Now I stood outside it in the gray morning light and understood that walls are for houses, and houses are for people who have chosen to forget, and I had stopped choosing that somewhere around the night a child was left on my doorstep with instructions to erase him.

"They took Bram," I said to Odile. She was coiling rope, not looking at me, working the problem the way she worked everything—with her hands.

"He knew the risk. He stayed on purpose. That wasn't sacrifice, Astrid, that was strategy—an old man with no name on any list is worth less to them than a boy or a woman with a press. He made himself the least valuable hostage in Vellamare." Her knuckles whitened on the rope. "Doesn't mean they won't hang him anyway. Means they'll take longer deciding to."

"Questioning," I said. "Procedure. They'll hold him through the festival, maybe longer. Which gives us—" I calculated, and the number came out cold and small. "Five weeks. Maybe less. Before the Archive seals and everything becomes sedition, and before they finish deciding what an old compositor knows."

Odile stopped coiling. Mira lifted her head. Tomas, sitting cross-legged on the hatch cover with a handful of loose type he'd been arranging into crooked words, looked up at the word *festival* the way children look at fireworks—which, given what was planned for the festival, was its own kind of wound.

"So we have five weeks," Odile said slowly, "to print three thousand pamphlets on no press at all."

I put my hand into my coat and drew out the oilcloth package and unwrapped it there on the wet deck, letting the harbor wind take one corner of the page—Elena Voss, young and furious, her book held high, my father's ink still true after fifteen years.

"No," I said. "Not on no press at all. On *any* press. Vellamare is full of presses—guild shops, chapel basements, the Academy's own bindery. Every one of them sleeps behind doors exactly like mine was, full of people exactly like I was, waiting for someone to prove it can be done." I looked around at the three faces I had somehow gathered into a family without meaning to—a smuggler, an apprentice, a boy the Committee had ordered me to forget. "Kastel gave us five hours' warning tonight. Next time we ask him for more. And next time, we don't hide. We print."

Below us, under the salt barrels, four crates of letters rode steady against the swell—my father's second alphabet, patient as seed, waiting for ground.

Somewhere across the water, in a cell whose location I did not yet know, an old man was paying for our head start. I intended to make the bill worth it.
