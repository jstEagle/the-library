## 17. The Festival Approaches

Six days. That was what the broadsheet said, pasted to the lamppost outside the fish market in fresh ink that still smelled of the Committee's presses: *By decree of the Provisional Committee, the Grand Archive of the Harbor Revolution shall be sealed on the night of the Victory Festival. All records not deposited therein by that hour shall be deemed null, and their possession deemed sedition.*

Astrid read it twice, then bought a mackerel she didn't want, because a woman who stood too long before an official notice looked like a woman with something to hide. The fishmonger wrapped it without comment. Everyone in Vellamare had learned to buy things they didn't want.

She carried it home through streets strung already with festival lanterns—red and gold, the Committee's colors, going up like blisters on the eaves of houses whose owners had not been asked. Children ran beneath them trailing streamers. A man on a ladder hammered a banner across Cooper's Row: *SIX DAYS TO GLORY*. Astrid walked under it with her eyes forward and her heart counting backward.

The shop was full when she arrived. That alone would have told her something was happening; the Reyes print shop had spent seven years learning to be empty at all the right hours. But tonight every lamp burned behind the shutters, and Odile Ferro sat on the composing stone with her boots off, and Bram stood at the frame with his hands flat on the case as if steadying himself against weather, and Mira was at the door with the bolt drawn before Astrid's key had left the lock.

"They posted the date," Mira said.

"I saw."

Kastel was there too. He sat in the far corner where the light from the lamp didn't quite reach, out of his censor's gray, wearing a coat so plain it looked like disguise—which, Astrid supposed, for a man like him, it was. He had come through the cellar again. He was getting good at coming through cellars. She found she no longer flinched at the sight of him, and that frightened her more than his uniform ever had.

Tomas was asleep on the cot behind the storeroom curtain, one arm flung over his eyes, a wooden sort—an upside-down *k*—clutched in his fist the way other children held soldiers. Astrid looked at him longer than she meant to. Six days. After six days, anything said aloud in this room could hang them all.

She set down the mackerel. "Then we decide tonight," she said. "All of it. Everything we do, we choose now, together, and after tonight nobody can say they weren't told what it would cost."

Odile grinned, but it was her harbor grin, the one that kept her face from showing anything else. "You make it sound like a funeral."

"It might be," Astrid said. "For some of us. So let's have it honestly."

* * *

They gathered around the stone, and Astrid unrolled the plan the way her father had taught her to unroll a proof—slowly, so the ink wouldn't smear.

Three thousand pamphlets were printed. They lay in the false floor beneath the press, wrapped in oilcloth: the true account of the harbor massacre, the fishwives' testimonies in their own words, Kastel's pages, Elena Voss's name and the names of two hundred others the Committee had erased, each with the date of their deletion and, where anyone living could supply it, the manner of their death. It weighed, Astrid thought, about as much as a child. It might buy a life's worth of trouble. It might outweigh everything else in Vellamare.

"Three roads out," she said. "The ship. The fishwives. And the Archive itself. Each one needs someone willing to lose something. I'll start." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "If this fails, I'm finished. The shop, the license, the name. I've printed approved matter for seven years; when the pamphlets surface, the first question the Committee asks is which presses ran them. There are only so many. Mine will be at the top of the list. I'll be arrested, or erased—declared forgotten, my record pulled, my name struck." She heard herself say it and was surprised to find no terror in it, only a strange flat calm, like the sea before wind. "I've made peace with that. What I haven't made peace with—" she glanced at the curtain, at the small sleeping shape behind it "—is him being taken because of me. So whatever else we settle tonight, we settle who takes Tomas if it goes wrong."

Silence, except for the harbor bell far off, counting the hour in its slow bronze voice.

Odile broke it. She stretched, cracked her knuckles, and spoke to the ceiling as if the plan were written up among the beams. "My part you know. *Salt Meridian*, berth fourteen, sails on the morning tide after the festival—grain run north, or so her papers say. Three thousand pamphlets go into the grain crates, double-bottomed, sealed with wax so the damp doesn't take them. Harbor inspection at the quay is one bored sergeant and a dog that's afraid of water." She stopped grinning. Then, with visible effort, she went on. "Here's what you don't know. If they find the crates, it's not a fine anymore. The Committee hanged a runner in Saltquay last month for moving two hundred copies of something worse than nothing. Piracy, they called it. They'll call it that for me too. I've been doing this eleven years, and I've never once put myself on the wrong side of a rope for paper." She turned her head and looked at Astrid, and her eyes in the lamplight were the exact color of the harbor in November. "But I watched you set Elena Voss's name letter by letter, and I thought: eleven years I've moved everything worth money and never once moved anything worth anything. So yes. The ship is yours. My neck goes with it. I want that said plainly, so all of you carry it too."

Nobody carried it lightly. Astrid saw Bram bow his head over the type case; saw Mira's jaw tighten.

"The fishwives are mine," Mira said.

"Mira—" Astrid began.

"You said everyone chooses. I'm choosing." Mira stood, and Astrid saw with a jolt that she was no longer standing like an apprentice; she'd been standing like a woman for weeks now, and Astrid had simply refused to notice. "Forty-one women along the waterfront took testimony from me, and every one of them asked when it would be read. Not if. When. On festival night, while everyone's watching the fireworks, they'll walk the lanes with satchels—copies we strike tomorrow, thirty sets—and they'll leave them in doorways, in fish barrels, under the altar cloth at Saint Brigid-of-the-Deeps if old Maren gets her way. By morning the whole city will have held the truth in its hands before the Committee can say it doesn't exist." She lifted her chin. "What I stand to lose is—" and here, for the first time, her voice wavered "—my mother finding out. If I'm caught, they don't hang apprentices. They question them, and they question their households, and my mother will learn what I've been doing at night, and she'll know she raised a daughter who chose this, and either way—" she stopped, breathed. "Either way I stop being safe. I've decided safe isn't the thing I want anymore."

Astrid reached across the stone and took her hand, and Mira let her, which she hadn't done since she was twelve.

* * *

It was Kastel who spoke next, and he did not rise. He stayed in his corner, hands folded, a man delivering his own sentence.

"The Archive seals at the ninth hour of the festival night," he said. "The vault is closed by the Keeper of Records with three keys—his, the Committee chairman's, and mine. Censor's key. It has hung around my neck for four years, and in four years I have opened that vault perhaps sixty times, and each time I thought: here is where I should have done something. Here, and here, and here." His thumb moved unconsciously to his collarbone, where the chain lay hidden. "On festival night, during the fireworks, I am required to make a final inventory before sealing. I will be alone in the vault for approximately forty minutes. Your record has a duplicate—one we struck from the same molds. It goes into the vault, shelved among the official histories, catalogued under a number no one will think to question, because I will write the number myself. In fifty years, when some archivist opens that vault looking for glory, they will find glory sitting beside its own corpse, and they will not be able to tell which is which without reading both."

"And if they catch you," Bram said quietly. It wasn't a question.

Kastel smiled, and it was terrible to see, because it was a real smile. "Then I am tried for sedition by the revolution I helped censor, and I am hanged, or erased, or both, and the last thing history knows of Ivo Kastel is that he was the man who guarded the lie. Which is more than fair. It is, in fact, generous." He looked around the room. "You should understand what you're taking from me. Not my life—I'd have traded that cheaply any night these four years. My place in it. Whatever a man is owed by the people he wronged. I'm spending all of it on forty minutes and a shelf number."

No one thanked him. Astrid understood, somehow, that thanking him would have been the cruelest thing in the room—that what he needed was exactly this: to sit among people who would not absolve him, and pay anyway.

* * *

Bram spoke last, and longest, though he used the fewest words.

He came to the stone and laid both hands flat, the way he did when he wanted the type to listen. Seventy years of setting letters had worn his hands into something like the tools themselves—knuckles like quoins, fingertips stained permanently dark.

"I buried a wife in the old regime's year of the white banners," he said. "They took her brother for printing songs. I said nothing. I set their decrees for nine months after, and slept, and ate, and said nothing." He looked at Astrid. "The night your father hid the molds, he asked me to help carry them down the stairs, and I remember thinking: this is a crime, and I am an old man, and if they take me my wife's grave will have no one to tend it. And I picked up the crate anyway. And it was the only thing I did for twenty years, that one night, and I have counted it heavy as a life." He straightened. "So here is my confession, since we're having them. What I stand to lose is the chance to keep being a coward comfortably. At my age, arrest is just dying with extra steps. I don't fear it. What I fear—" his voice caught, and he mastered it the way he mastered a sticking form, with pressure and patience "—is the shop falling silent again. Is you children carrying what I carried. That's all. Set me wherever the weight is heaviest. I've carried forms heavier than me since before your father was born."

Astrid found she could not speak. She nodded instead, and Bram nodded back, and something passed between them that had no name but had the weight of type, of metal cast in a father's molds, of inheritance.

* * *

They worked the details until the candles guttered: the order of loading, the signals—a red lantern at the quay meaning route compromised, a whistle from the fish lane meaning Mira was clear—the hours, the fallbacks, the places each of them would go if the night broke apart. Odile drew the harbor in spilled ink on the back of a spoiled proof and stabbed at it with a finger until everyone knew berth fourteen blind. Twice Tomas woke and came to the table half-asleep, and twice the talk shifted seamlessly to grain prices and festival bread rations until he curled up again between Odile's coat and the warm stone, and the true conversation resumed above his head like weather.

It was nearly four in the morning when Astrid called the end of it. She looked around the stone—at the smuggler, the apprentice, the censor, the old man, the sleeping boy—and understood that she was looking at the only archive that mattered, the living one, the one no vault could seal: five people who now knew everything about each other, bound by shared knowledge the way pages are bound by thread, and just as tearable, and just as lasting.

"One more thing," she said. "Whatever happens on festival night—if the ship is taken, if the fishwives are stopped, if the vault stays shut—we will have done it. Not the Committee. Us. They can erase our names from every register in Vellamare and they cannot unprint what we set. Remember that, whichever of us is left. We were not silent."

Bram blew out the first candle. Odile the second. In the dark, Astrid heard Kastel rise, heard the cellar door ease open, heard him pause on the stair.

"Astrid," he said softly. "There's a rider to the sealing decree posted at dawn. Final inspections of all licensed presses begin tomorrow morning."

The candle smoke curled in the black air.

"Whose presses?" she asked, though she already knew.

"Yours is second on the list," said the censor. "They come at noon."
