## 19. The Night of the Festival

The first firework went up at half past nine, and the whole sky over Vellamare turned to falling gold.

From the shadowed mouth of Pier Six, Astrid watched the light break apart above the harbor and come down in embers over the black water. The Victory Festival had swallowed the city whole. Lanterns strung from mast to mast. Drums from the fish-market square. Somewhere a band played the Committee's anthem badly, all the brass players either drunk or conscripted or gone, and nobody left who knew the difference.

"Three thousand," Odile said beside her, not looking at the fireworks but at the quay. "You're certain?"

"Bram counted them twice." Astrid's voice came out flat and strange. All night it had felt like someone else's voice, someone standing slightly behind her shoulder, watching her hands move. "Three thousand pamphlets, bundled in oilcloth, forty bundles to the crate. Six crates."

"Six crates of sedition." Odile spat over the edge of the pier, ceremonial, an old habit. "And every soldier in Vellamare is between us and the water."

That was true, and they had counted on it being true. That was the whole terrible arithmetic of the night: the festival drew the garrison to the squares and the promenades, drew them where the crowds were thickest, because the Committee feared its own celebration more than it admitted. The piers would be thin. The piers were always thin on festival night. But thin was not empty, and the *Meridian's* hold could not be filled by ghosts.

"Mira?" Astrid asked.

"On the seawall with the fishwives' boys, ready to run the last crates if we need legs." Odile's jaw tightened. "I told her—"

"I know what you told her."

"You told her nothing. That's your mistake, not mine."

Astrid did not answer. There had been no time for mistakes to be sorted from choices; everything since Kastel's warning two nights ago had been one long held breath. The molds hidden again behind the false wall, this time wrapped in waxed canvas against damp. The press disassembled, its heaviest parts sunk in the cistern under the shop. The type—the forbidden alphabet, her father's second alphabet—packed into a single box small enough for one person to carry, because some things you did not sink into water, you carried them out yourself or you died carrying them.

The box sat now at Astrid's feet, and she was aware of it the way she was aware of her own heartbeat.

"He's late," she said.

"Ferro is never late." Odile paused. "Your boy, though. Your boy is early."

She meant Tomas, and she meant it as comfort, and it worked the way a knife works. Tomas was not here—of course he was not here, they had argued about it until dawn and she had won by refusing to speak anymore—but his absence was louder than any presence. He was across town in Widow Sarelle's back room with Kastel's sister, eating bread and honey, asking every ten minutes whether it was morning yet. She had kissed his hair and lied to him that everything would be ordinary when he woke.

*Forget the face.* The note had said that, seven years of silence ago. Tonight she was loading three thousand faces onto a smuggler's ship so that no one could ever say it again.

* * *

The *Meridian* came in low and dark under the far mole, running without lights, and Odile's whistle split the night three times—a gull's cry, then two more. Men appeared along the pier as if the wood itself had opened: Odile's crew, six of them, moving crates from the handcart with the smooth speed of men who had unloaded worse things than paper in worse places than home.

Astrid took the second crate herself. It was heavier than she expected—not the weight of paper but the weight of what was in it. Fishwives' names. Kastel's cramped confession, written in a censor's hand that shook only once, at the bottom, where he had signed it with his full name for the first time in twenty years. Bram's account of the purges, dictated in his flat compositor's voice while Astrid set the type and Mira read it back and all three of them pretended not to hear Bram's breath catch at the line about her father.

Her father's name was on the third page. Her father's face was described in words, so that even a stranger could know him.

She was halfway up the gangplank with the crate when the drums stopped.

It was nothing, at first. The band in the square faltered, picked up, faltered again. Then the lanterns along the promenade began going out—not blown by wind, in rows, in sequence—and the crowd noise changed texture, the way water changes before something moves beneath it.

Odile froze at the foot of the gangplank, head cocked, listening the way other people look. Then she swore, quietly and comprehensively, in three languages.

"That's not a raid formation," she said. "That's a sweep. They're clearing the waterfront. All of it."

"They don't know about tonight. They can't—"

"It doesn't matter what they know." Odile's voice had gone very calm, which Astrid had learned was the most frightening register the smuggler owned. "Festival night, sealed Archive tomorrow, half the garrison drunk and the other half ambitious. Someone decided to sweep the piers because it would look like diligence. We have maybe four hundred counts before they reach the mole."

Four hundred counts. Four crates still on the handcart. Astrid's mind ran the sums the way it ran lines of type, cold and fast, and arrived nowhere good.

"We push," Odile said. "We load fast, we cut lines, we go with the tide whatever's aboard. If I sail short-handed and half-laden, so be it. The record goes out tonight or it never goes."

Astrid set down her crate in the hold, climbed back into the lantern-dark, and started carrying.

* * *

They loaded like people possessed. The crew formed a chain from cart to gangplank to hatch, and Astrid stood in the middle of it passing crates hand to hand, feeling each one leave her fingers with a sensation close to grief and close to relief and identical to neither. Behind her, Vellamare burned fireworks over its own forgetting. Ahead, the black water waited to carry the truth somewhere she would never see.

Two hundred counts. She counted in her head without deciding to; the shop had trained her too well.

The soldiers came at two hundred and sixty.

Not many at first—a patrol, eight men, torches bobbing along the promenade road toward the mole. Odile saw them and did not raise her voice; she simply began untying the forward line with her teeth and one hand while directing the chain with the other. *Faster,* her gestures said. *Quieter. Faster.*

But the crates were heavy and the gangplank narrow, and Bram—seventy-one years old, with hands that had set type for fifty years and a back that had bent over cases almost as long—was slower than the rest, and the patrol reached the head of Pier Six while he was still on the planks with the fifth crate in his arms.

"Halt." A young voice, uncertain, amplified by authority. "In the name of the Provisional Committee, halt."

Everything after that happened very slowly and very fast at once. Astrid remembered it later as a series of still images, the way she remembered the Harbor Uprising: the torchlight on wet wood. Bram turning, unhurried, the crate balanced against his chest like a sleeping child. The lieutenant's face beneath his helmet, young enough that he must have been a child during the uprising, must have believed whatever he'd been told about it. Odile's hand drifting toward her belt and stopping, because knives against rifles on a lit pier was arithmetic even she couldn't make work.

Bram set the crate down on the planks. Gently, the way he set type. Then he straightened to his full height, which was not much, and looked at the soldiers the way he looked at crooked columns of letters.

"Good evening," he said. "You'll want to see my papers."

He had papers. Of course he had papers—Odile had forged them weeks ago, a shipping clerk's permit, a cargo manifest for ballast paper bound for Corvane. It might even have worked. But the sweep was not looking for reasons to believe people; the sweep was looking for bodies to fill its report, and a seventy-year-old man alone on a closed pier at midnight was exactly the shape of body reports wanted. The lieutenant circled him. One of the soldiers kicked the crate open with his boot.

Oilcloth. Bundles. Print.

Astrid stood thirty feet away in the shadow of the *Meridian's* rail, and she understood, in one white-hot instant, exactly what she was looking at. Not the crate—that crate was real, that crate was theirs, that loss was already total. She was looking at the arithmetic. The soldiers had found a man, and paper, and a story that fit together neatly: a festival-night smuggling arrest, a printer's runner caught with contraband, case closed, report filed, the waterfront swept, diligence done. They would take Bram. They would stop there. They would not walk to the end of the pier, because why would they? The night had already given them what the night was for.

Unless someone gave them a reason.

Bram turned his head, just slightly—only enough for her to see the line of his jaw, the same angle it made over the composing stick every evening for seven years—and he did not look afraid. He looked tired, and finished, and free. His eyes found the place where she stood and moved past it deliberately, the way a compositor's eye skips a word he has already checked.

*Go,* that glance said, in the language they had shared across a workbench longer than she had known Tomas's name. *The forme is locked. Don't break it for me.*

"Name," the lieutenant was saying, notebook out. "Full name, and your employer."

"Bramwell Ostergaard," Bram said, loud enough to carry down the pier, loud enough to be written down, loud enough to survive whatever came next. "Compositor. Forty years at the trade. And I'll tell you something else for free, son, since you're taking names tonight anyway—"

Astrid's whole body was already moving toward the gangplank. She did not decide it; her feet decided, her hands, the part of her that had buried a father and refused to bury anyone else. Odile caught her arm at the rail, and Astrid turned on her with something feral in her throat—

—and Bram, seeing it, seeing both of them, did the cruelest and kindest thing she would ever witness. He smiled. And he raised his voice higher, giving himself entirely to the soldiers' attention, pulling every eye and every torch to the far end of the pier.

"—tell your Committee that paper floats better than lies!"

The rifle butt came down. The lieutenant shouted orders. And Astrid stood at the rail of the *Meridian* with Odile's iron grip on her arm and the tide pulling at the hull, and she made the choice that had no clean edges, the choice she would carry the way she carried everything else, silently, in type, forever.

She stepped back from the gangplank.

Odile shoved it off the side. The *Meridian*'s engine woke below the waterline like something ancient and angry, and the pier slid away, and Bram grew smaller under the torches, upright between two soldiers, being marched toward a fate she could not follow him into. Astrid pressed both hands against the rail until the iron bit. She did not cry out. Criers got caught. That was the first rule of survival, seven years old and still true, and she hated it with everything in her.

The crate he'd been carrying was still on the pier. Five crates out of six.

Five thousand—no, twenty-five hundred pamphlets. Half the record. The fishwives' testimony, intact. Kastel's confession, intact. Her father's name, described in words so a stranger could know his face—intact, aboard, in the hold, in the dark, going out with the tide.

And Bramwell Ostergaard, who had taught her that a compositor's silence was a kind of speech, going the other way.

* * *

Vellamare burned behind them all the way out of the harbor. Fireworks, only fireworks, but from the water it looked like the city was on fire, and perhaps in every way that mattered it was. The final shells burst gold and red above the mole as the *Meridian* slipped past the outer buoys, and the Committee's celebration rained harmless embers onto the sea.

Odile put the ship on its heading and then came to stand beside Astrid at the stern rail, and for a long time neither of them spoke. The harbor lights shrank. The drumming faded into the sound of water.

"He knew," Astrid said finally. Her voice was wrecked; she heard it and didn't care. "He planned it. From the moment he picked up the crate. He counted the soldiers and did the sum faster than I did."

"He did what any of us would do." Odile's voice was rougher than usual. "What fewer of us would, honestly."

"He's seventy-one. They'll take him to the Committee cells. They'll ask him who else—" Astrid stopped. The list of things Bram knew was the list of everyone she loved, and she could not say it out loud with the record in the hold depending on her silence.

"They won't get it out of him," Odile said. "Men who spend their lives setting other people's words keep the best ones for themselves."

Astrid stared at the shrinking city. Tomorrow the Archive would seal. Tomorrow, historians in Committee colors would lock the doors on Vellamare's memory, and inside those doors—she thought of Kastel, somewhere in that building tonight, with his pages and his terrible courage—something else would be waiting for whoever opened them next. And beyond the horizon, five crates were sailing east through the dark, into harbors she would never stand in, into hands she would never shake.

It was not enough. It was also everything. Both were true, and she had to hold both, the way she held the composing stick: firmly, precisely, knowing the letter would print backwards so the world could read it forwards.

"The boy will want to know where you are," Odile said quietly. "When we send word. What do I tell him happened tonight?"

Astrid watched the last firework die over the mole, over the city that was about to forget her, and found—to her surprise—that the answer was already composed, set, and waiting, the way the best lines always were.

"Tell him his grandmother's stories are safe," she said. "Tell him the man who printed them is braver than anyone in the songs. And tell him—" Her voice failed for one breath, and then held. "Tell him I'm coming home. Whatever name I have to come home under."

Behind them, in the direction of the Committee cells, the city kept celebrating. Ahead, the sea ran black and open to the edge of the world.

Astrid Reyes turned her back on Vellamare, and did not look again.
