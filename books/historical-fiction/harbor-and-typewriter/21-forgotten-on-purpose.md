## 21. Forgotten on Purpose

The paper came three days after the Festival, folded once and slipped under the shop door like a bill for services rendered.

*Notice of Erasure. The individual known as Astrid Reyes, printer, having no record of residence, trade, or family in the municipal rolls, is declared nonexistent. All persons are instructed to disregard her.*

Astrid read it standing in the gray light of dawn, Tomas asleep against her shoulder where he had migrated in the night, and she laughed — a single dry sound, quickly swallowed — because it was the most beautiful forgery she had ever seen, and she had written it herself.

Kastel had warned her it would come to this. *They cannot arrest a woman who does not exist,* he had said, the last night, his voice low across the press bed. *But they can make you into nothing, and nothing can leave the city freely.* He had been right about everything else: the raid's timing, the soldiers' routes, the way the Committee would rather amend reality than admit a pamphlet had slipped its net. Three thousand copies of the true harbor account were somewhere on the open water by now, in fishwives' market baskets, in the Archive vault itself, sealed inside the official record like a splinter working toward the heart. And Astrid Reyes was, as of this morning, officially nobody.

She set the notice down on the composing stone, next to the type she would not be sorting today.

"Mira," she said quietly. "Wake up. We go tonight."

* * *

They spent the day dismantling themselves.

Not the shop — the shop had been dismantled for weeks, presses sold, molds distributed into hiding places only Astrid knew, the smell of ink scrubbed from the floorboards until even she half-believed no press had ever stood there. What they dismantled was smaller and harder: a life. Mira burned their letters in the stove, feeding them page by page so the flame wouldn't flare in the grate and draw eyes. Astrid packed what could pass for a widow's belongings — two dresses, a mended coat, Tomas's wooden horse, the one possession she allowed him to keep because some things should survive erasure. She left her father's portrait face-down in a drawer. Taking it would be sentiment; leaving it was testimony of a kind, a man who had existed here once, whose absence someone might someday question.

Tomas sat on the stairs through all of it, watching with the terrible patience children learn when they have been moved before. He was seven now by Astrid's private reckoning, though no document in Vellamare said so; Elena Voss's son existed nowhere except in a woman's memory and, soon, in a line of type across the sea.

"Are we being forgotten?" he asked in the afternoon, when Mira went out to buy bread and the shop was very quiet.

Astrid knelt so they were eye to eye. "We're forgetting ourselves first," she said. "There's a difference. When someone forgets you, it's done *to* you. When you do it yourself, you get to decide what you keep."

"What do we keep?"

"You," she said, touching his chest. "Me. Mira. Everything that happened at the harbor, even if no one here ever says it again." She hesitated, then gave him the whole truth, because she had decided she was finished with omissions where this child was concerned. "And your name. Your real one. Not the one we use on the street. Keep it inside yourself like a seed. One day you'll plant it somewhere they can't reach."

He considered this with his mother's grave, exact attention, and nodded once. Then, because he was also six-going-on-seven and had eaten almost nothing all day: "Can we keep bread?"

"We can keep bread."

* * *

Odile came at dusk, smelling of tar and the particular smoke of ledgers long since burned.

"They've doubled the watch at the east gates," she said, without greeting, dropping onto the bench like a woman who had been carrying the bench's weight all day. "But nobody watches the fish-quay after dark. The stench does the guarding for them." She looked around the stripped shop, and something crossed her face — Odile Ferro did not permit herself expressions often, but grief got past her guard sometimes, arriving dressed as irritation. "Twelve years I've run cargo out of this harbor. You'd think leaving it would feel like any other tide."

"Doesn't it?"

"No." She stood, briskly, before the feeling could set. "One boat, small, unremarkable, flying grain-trade colors. It puts in at the fish-quay at the second bell. From there north along the coast, then overland. I have people in Correnne who owe me more than I care to count." Her eyes found Astrid's, sharp and level. "I'm not coming with you."

"I know."

"Someone has to stay who still exists. Someone with a face the Committee recognizes and a route they haven't figured out how to close. If the record needs anything — paper, ink, a fast hull — it comes through me or not at all." She said it lightly, but her hand, resting on the doorframe, was white-knuckled. "Bram is in the Stonecellar. There's no word on—"

"I know that too."

Odile held her gaze a moment longer. Whatever passed between them, neither of them named it. Then the smuggler pulled something from her coat and put it on the composing stone: a small tin of printing ink, professional grade, nearly full. "For wherever you land," she said gruffly. "You'll need it. People like us don't stop. We just relocate."

When she was gone, Astrid stood holding the tin and understood that it was the truest farewell anyone had ever given her.

* * *

They left at the second bell, in the dead hours when even the patrols drank.

Mira carried the bag with the bread and Tomas's horse. Astrid carried Tomas, because the boy had fallen asleep sitting up and because she wanted, one last time, to feel the weight of what she was protecting — literal, breathing, heavier every year. They went down through the lanes behind the chandlers' row, past shuttered windows and a cat with extraordinary opinions, and the city that had erased her went on sleeping around her without incident, which was its own strange wound. Seven years of careful silence, of approved texts and unasked questions, and in the end walking away required no drama at all. The gate guards at the fish-quay were meant to see grain merchants, widows, nobodies. They saw exactly that. Invisibility, Astrid thought, stepping over the quay's wet stones, was not a cage after all. It was a tool, and tonight, for the first time, she held the handle of it herself.

Then Mira stopped.

"Astrid." Her voice was very small. "The lane. If we cut through Printer's Row, it's faster to the north road. But that means—"

Astrid followed the girl's pointing hand and saw it: the narrow lane, the crooked signpost, and at the end of it, dark against the lesser dark, the old Reyes print shop. Her father's shop. The place where she had learned letters by lamplight and the weight of a mold by heart, where Bram had set his first line sixty years ago, where her father had hidden an alphabet in the walls while the previous regime's censors walked the streets above. The windows were boarded now. On the door, someone official had pasted the Committee's seal and beneath it, in the tidy bureaucratic hand she had spent years setting herself, the word she had chosen for her own epitaph:

*CLOSED — PREMISES NONEXISTENT.*

"We don't have to," Mira said gently. "We can go around."

Astrid stood at the mouth of the lane with her son asleep on her shoulder and let herself look at it — really look, for the first time since the raid, since Bram's hands had been forced behind his back while he shouted the harbor's true death-count at the soldiers like a man distributing pamphlets by voice alone. She thought about her father's hands on the press. About the note under her own door seven years ago: *forget the face.* About all the faces she had obeyed, and the one face she had refused to.

"No," she said. "We go through."

The lane was thirty paces long and took a lifetime. At the door she stopped again, shifted Tomas to her other hip, and pressed her palm flat against the wood — the same wood, she realized, that had been there the night she was born in the room above, her father's ink-stained hands the first thing she never remembered. The seal crackled faintly under her fingers.

"Goodbye," she whispered. Not to the building. To the version of herself that had believed survival was the same thing as living, that silence was safety, that a life built on omission was a life. "Thank you for keeping us. I'll take it from here."

Mira, beside her, was crying without sound, the way she had learned during the raid. Astrid put her free arm around the girl's shoulders and they stood together for one breath, two — a family of the erased paying their respects to a house that officially had never stood.

Then Astrid turned her back on it, deliberately, the way you turn your back on a shore, and walked toward the boat.

* * *

The crossing was cold and the boat smelled of herring, and it was, all three of them agreed later, perfect.

Tomas woke as the harbor opened around them, the city shrinking into a smear of festival lanterns still burning late along the seawall. He watched Vellamare recede with an expression Astrid couldn't read until he spoke.

"It looks smaller than it did," he said.

"Everything does, from far enough away."

"Is Bram smaller too? From far away?"

Astrid's throat closed. She gathered him against her side and looked back at the lights — somewhere behind one of them, in the Stonecellar, an old compositor was sitting in the dark with sixty years of silence behind him and one act of noise in front of him, and she could not reach him, could not save him, could only carry forward the thing he had gone quiet to protect.

"No," she said finally. "Some things don't get smaller. That's what distance is for. It shows you which ones."

Mira sat at the bow with her knees drawn up, seventeen years old and already older than Astrid had been at twenty-five, watching the horizon like a woman reading a proof sheet for errors. "There's a light," she said after a while. "Far up the coast. A town?"

"Correnne first," Astrid said. "Then north. Somewhere with a harbor and a market and people who've never heard of the Provisional Committee." She felt the tin of ink in her coat pocket, solid as a promise. "Somewhere with a room big enough for a press."

Mira turned, and in the pre-dawn gray Astrid saw her smile — the first real one since the raid, wobbly at the edges but unmistakable. "You're going to start again. Even now."

"*Especially* now." Astrid said it and heard, with surprise, that it was true. For seven years she had printed what was permitted and forgotten what was commanded, and she had told herself it was survival, and it had been, and it had also been dying slowly in installments. Now she was officially dead — erased, nonexistent, disregarded by order of the Committee — and had never felt more alive. Mourning and liberation, it turned out, could share a boat. "I'm going to need an apprentice who can outrun constables. Know anyone?"

"One or two," Mira said.

Tomas, drowsing against Astrid's ribs, murmured something. She bent close. "What was that, love?"

His eyes opened a slit, stubborn even half-asleep. "My name," he said. "Keep it safe till we get there."

"Every letter," Astrid promised. "I already know how I'll set it. Large type. First line of the first page. So the whole town reads it before breakfast."

Ahead of them the coast unrolled, dark and patient, and somewhere along it a small harbor town slept without knowing what was coming: a widow, a girl, a boy, and a tin of ink. Behind them Vellamare's lanterns guttered one by one as the festival ended and the victors' historians sharpened their pens over an archive already poisoned from within. Astrid did not look back again.

She had spent her whole life being careful about what she remembered.

Now she intended to be reckless.
