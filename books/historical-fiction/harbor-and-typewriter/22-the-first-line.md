## 22. The First Line

The town was called Greywater, though the water wasn't grey at all—it was the green of old bottle glass, and in the early morning light it threw shifting coins of color onto the ceilings of the houses that leaned over the harbor. It was smaller than Vellamare by half, poorer by two-thirds, and it had never once appeared on a list of places worth remembering. This was precisely why Astrid had chosen it.

She had been in Greywater for eleven months before she hung a sign.

The shop sat at the end of Rope Lane, where the cobblestones gave up and became packed mud that smelled of tar and fish scales. Its previous tenant had been a rope-maker whose craft had outlived his lungs, and the room still held his ghost: hooks in the ceiling beams where hanks of hemp had once cured, a smell of fiber worked so deep into the plaster that no amount of scrubbing would lift it. Astrid didn't scrub anyway. She liked the idea that the walls remembered someone's work. Most walls she had known were made to forget.

Mira painted the sign herself, on a board Odile's sailors had salvaged from a wrecked coastal trader: **REYES**, in letters that curled slightly at the R because Mira had refused to use a stencil. "A hand should be visible in it," Mira said, when Astrid pointed out the flaw. "Otherwise we're just doing what they did—pretending machines make things instead of people."

Astrid looked at the crooked R for a long time. Then she hung it above the door and let the sea wind take its measure.

Tomas was seven now, or eight—the difference mattered less than it once had, because in Greywater nobody asked children to prove their ages against ledgers. He had lost the flinch. That was how Astrid thought of it: for the first months he had flinched at boots on stairs, at knocking, at anyone in a uniform coat, even the fishmonger's oilskin apron if it was dark enough. Now he walked into rooms as if he expected to still be in them an hour later. He slept without a candle. He had opinions about everything, loudly held, especially about the correct way to distribute type in the case—which was to say, incorrectly, with a six-year-old's faith in speed over order, and Astrid corrected him less than she should have because every mistake he made was proof that he felt safe enough to be careless.

Carelessness was a luxury she had spent seven years unable to afford. She was learning to afford it too, slowly, the way you learn a new language after forty: with effort, with relapse, with days when the old fluency came back unbidden.

On the morning the shop opened, she rose before the gulls and let herself in alone.

* * *

The press had come north in pieces, wrapped in oiled cloth inside barrels of salt herring, which Odile swore was the best disguise in maritime history—"Nobody inspects herring, Astrid. Nobody has ever inspected herring." The frame was Vellamare oak, her father's design, cut by Bram's hands twenty years before either Astrid or the revolution existed. Bram had not seen it assembled again until last week, and when the screw turned true for the first time he had put both palms flat on the bed of the press like a man checking whether a horse's heart still beat, and said nothing at all, and gone out to the harbor to sit alone on the pilings, which was how Bram prayed, if indeed Bram prayed, a question Astrid had never resolved and had learned not to need to.

Now she unlocked the type cabinet.

Her father's molds rode across the sea in a false bottom beneath the herring barrels, and they had arrived perfect—not a chip, not a worn serif, the second alphabet complete. Somewhere in Vellamare there was a Committee record listing these molds as destroyed, burned in a raid that had happened only in ink, and somewhere in Vellamare there was also, if Kastel's plan had held, a sealed vault containing a pamphlet set from these very same letters. Astrid tried, some mornings, to imagine the vault: the official Archive with its brass doors and its historians who sealed history like jam, and inside it, filed among the sanctioned truths, one document that told the harbor massacre as the fishwives had told it, one page of a censor's confession, one account of a boy left on a doorstep with instructions to forget him. She did not know if it had worked. She would probably never know. Kastel had walked into the Archive on festival night and had not come out, and the news that reached Greywater came filtered through sailors' gossip, which reported that there had been arrests, that a compositor had died in custody—"old fellow, stubborn as an anvil," the sailor said, and Astrid had thanked him for his trouble and gone upstairs and wept into Tomas's hair while he slept—and that the pamphlets had spread along the coast like spilled lamp oil finding a seam. Three ports had rioted quietly, which was to say, with petitions. One port had renamed its harbor square. In the capital, the Provisional Committee issued a statement denying everything, which was how Astrid knew the statement was false. She had printed enough denials in her life to read them like weather.

So the vault was sealed and the truth was inside it, or the vault was emptied and the truth was loose in it somewhere, or Kastel had been caught before he reached the shelves and the pages burned. She could not know. What she knew was this: the record had sailed. Odile had sworn it on her ship, and Odile's oaths were the only currency Astrid had ever seen hold its value.

And what she knew, standing now in the cold dawn shop with the cabinet open and the molds gleaming, was that knowing was no longer enough. Witness that stayed in one harbor was just memory, and memory died with its keepers. Her father had understood this—that was the whole lesson of the false bottom, the whole inheritance. Letters are how the dead keep talking. So open the shop. Set the type. Begin.

* * *

The first page took all morning.

Not because the text was long. Because she chose to compose it slowly, deliberately, the way her father had set the first page of Reyes & Sons thirty years ago—with each letter selected by hand, examined, seated. Mira came in mid-morning and stood watching without speaking; Mira had learned that particular silence from Astrid, and deployed it better. Tomas sat on a crate with his chin on his fists.

The text was short. It was, in fact, almost nothing—a broadsheet, one side, headed with the shop's name and the words *First Impression*. Below that, a single opening statement, and below that, room left deliberately blank, because Astrid intended the first thing her press ever published to be unfinished. A first line, not a last word.

She began with the name.

**TOMAS VOSS-REYES** — she had argued with herself for weeks about the surname. Voss, so that Elena Voss's line continued visibly through him, unhidden, unashamed. Reyes, because he had chosen it himself, last spring, standing in this very room, when Mira had asked him—in the casual, merciless way children ask—if he had a family name, and he had looked at Astrid for a long moment and then said, "Reyes. I'm a Reyes." Nobody had corrected him. Nobody had needed to.

Then the description. She set it line by line, feeling each letter seat itself in the composing stick with a small satisfying click, and she spoke it aloud as she worked, because Tomas had asked her to, because he wanted to hear his own face described in the voice she used for important things:

Dark hair, thick, with a double crown that would never lie flat. Eyes brown, deep-set, quick—one slightly narrower than the other, most visible when he laughed. A scar over the left eyebrow, from falling off Odile's ship's ladder the summer he turned seven. Freckles across the nose that arrived every June and left every September, faithful as tide tables. Gap between the front teeth, recently earned, proudly displayed. Left-handed. Walks on the balls of his feet. Hums when he concentrates.

"Read it back," Astrid said.

Tomas took the proof sheet in both hands, careful of the wet ink the way she'd taught him, and read his own existence aloud: *"Tomas Voss-Reyes. Dark hair... eyes brown... a scar over the left eyebrow..."* His voice caught at the freckles—he hadn't known they'd made it into the permanent record—and he looked up, and the narrow eye crinkled, and Astrid saw his face do exactly the thing the type said it did.

"That's so no one can tell you to forget it," she said. "Not a Committee. Not a historian. Not anyone. You're in metal now. Metal is very hard to argue with."

"What if they melt it?" Tomas asked, because he was a child raised among censors and he asked the questions adults flinched from.

"Then we recast it," Astrid said. "That's the whole trick, Tomas. You can burn paper. You can confiscate a press. But a mold is an idea shaped into metal, and ideas can be remade anywhere, by anyone who learns the shape. My father hid these for seven years under a floor full of soldiers. Do you know why?"

He shook his head.

"Because he believed that someday someone would want the truth printed again, and he wanted to be ready even if he wasn't there to see it." She wiped the ink from her fingers. "He was right. That's you. You're the someday."

Tomas considered this with the enormous gravity he brought to anything involving himself, then said, "Can I set the next letter? Mira says I'm ready. She says it grudgingly, but she says it."

"She says it grudgingly," Astrid confirmed, "which means it's true." And she handed him the stick, and watched his small left hands choose a lowercase *e*, examine it against the light like a jeweler, and seat it—slightly crooked, unmistakably human, exactly right.

* * *

They opened the doors at noon.

Greywater being Greywater, half the town had already heard about the press through the fish-market telegraph, and the other half came out of pure curiosity, having exhausted the town's other attractions years ago. They came in their oilskins and their Sunday coats, rope-makers and net-menders, the harbormaster's widow, two priests of denominations that rarely shared a doorway and stood now at opposite ends of the shop pretending the other wasn't there. They read the broadsheet tacked to the wall. They read the name, the description, the declaration beneath it:

*This press prints what is true, verified by witnesses and signed by them. It will print corrections when it is wrong. It accepts no committee's account in place of any person's memory. Every child born in this harbor may be registered here, free of charge, in type that cannot be ordered forgotten.*

Below it, the blank space, and beneath the blank space, a line of instruction:

*If something has been taken from your record—your name, your kin, your street, your dead—tell us. We will set it. The first hundred names are printed at the press's cost.*

An old net-mender named Sorrel was the first to step forward. Astrid had seen her at the market for months, a woman built like driftwood, and now Sorrel stood in front of the broadsheet for a long time, reading the blank space the way other people read scripture. Then she said, quietly, to no one in particular: "My brother fished out of Vellamare. Before the purges. There's nothing left anywhere that says he lived. Not a stone. Not a line." She looked up at Astrid, and her eyes were dry in the way of people who finished crying decades ago. "Would you set him?"

"His full name," Astrid said, already reaching for the composing stick, "and everything you remember of his face."

And Sorrel began to speak—haltingly at first, then faster, decades of enforced silence coming off her like rust under a wire brush—and Mira took dictation, and Tomas fetched type, and by evening the first page of the Greywater register held two names where yesterday it had held one, and the queue at the door went out into the mud of Rope Lane and around the corner toward the water.

* * *

Odile arrived after dark, as was her custom and possibly her religion.

She came in the back way, smelling of herring and salt and the particular smoke of whatever she'd burned this month, and stood in the doorway watching the shop work—Astrid at the case, Mira at the table transcribing, Bram in the corner chair he now pretended was his, Tomas asleep on the same crate he'd sat on that morning, his mouth open, the day's proofs rising and falling on his chest with his breath.

"You look terrible," Odile said to Bram. "You look wonderful," she said to Astrid. "You look employed," she said to Mira, who stuck her tongue out without looking up. Then she crossed the room, took the newest proof sheet from the pile, and read it by candlelight, moving her lips slightly, which she would have denied under torture.

"The distribution went well?" Astrid asked, though she already knew; Odile came north twice a season now, ostensibly for legitimate cargo, actually to collect the register's copies for the coastal run.

"It went," Odile said, which from Odile meant it had nearly killed everyone involved and would be retold with increasing heroism at every port until it became legend. She set down the sheet. "There's word from the capital. Actual word, not sailor gossip—I bought it off a customs man who owes me his life, so it's good."

Astrid kept setting type. "Say it."

"The Archive's been opened. Officially. Some new committee—you know how committees breed—but this one had to open the vault because half the coast had already read what was inside it, and you can't claim to hold the national memory when a fishing crew in Saltreach owns three copies of the thing you're hiding. So they opened the doors and called it 'transparency,' which is what powerful people call surrender." She paused. "Your censor's papers were in there. Filed proper. Catalogued. With his name on them."

Astrid's hands went still over the case.

"Ivo Kastel," Odile read, from a folded scrap she'd pulled from her coat. "'Testimony of Ivo Kastel, Censor Second Class, deposited this date by his own hand.' Signed and dated, Astrid. The man catalogued himself into the permanent record the same night he probably—" She stopped. "It's in there. Whatever happens, whatever they do to his name otherwise, that page survives. Vault's public now. He got what he wanted."

Astrid nodded. She found she could not speak for a moment, and was grateful that in this shop, silence was understood as work rather than absence. Then she took the scrap from Odile's hand, read it twice, and folded it with the care she gave to type, and put it in the drawer where she kept the things that must never be lost: her father's last letter, the fishwives' original testimony, a curl of Tomas's hair from the year of the ladder.

"He'll be remembered accurately," she said at last. "It's more than he ever gave anyone else. It's exactly what he asked for."

Bram spoke from his corner—rare enough that everyone turned. "Censors and printers," the old man said, "we're the same trade, in the end. Both of us decide what the future gets to know. He just decided late." He lifted his cup an inch. "Late is still counted."

* * *

Near midnight, when the others had gone and the shop smelled of ink and cooling wax, Astrid stood at the case one last time.

Tomorrow's work waited: Sorrel's brother, then the harbormaster's widow's drowned sons, then a request from the lighthouse keeper that had made Mira laugh and cry at once—the man wanted recorded every ship lost off Greywater since the old king's time, all ninety-one of them, crews and cargo and cause, because, he said, his grandfather had watched two of them go down and talked about nothing else for sixty years and no one had ever written it down and now the man was afraid of dying the way the ships had died, unwitnessed. Ninety-one ships. Weeks of type. Maybe months.

Astrid found she was looking forward to it the way she had once looked forward to nothing at all.

She blew out the lamps except one, and stood a moment in the doorway, looking down Rope Lane at the water, black and patient, holding the lights of the anchored boats like a tray of candles. Somewhere south of here was Vellamare, where she was officially no one—her name struck, her shop reassigned, her face on the forgotten lists beside the faces she had once been forced to print away. She had expected the erasure to feel like drowning. It felt instead like taking off a coat she'd worn so long she'd mistaken it for skin. They could strike her name because her name had never been the point. The point was running on a press in a town too small to bother with, and it was filed in a public vault in the capital, and it was memorized by fishwives from here to Saltreach, and it was asleep on a crate ten feet behind her, breathing, in type.

Behind her, floorboards creaked. Tomas, sleep-heavy, dragging his blanket, appeared in the doorway and wedged himself under her arm.

"Couldn't sleep," he said. "I kept thinking about my page. Is it really permanent? Even if everything changes?"

"Especially then," Astrid said. "That's what permanence is for. Things change, and the record stays, and the staying is how we prove we were here."

He thought about that, leaning against her, watching the harbor lights waver.

"When I'm big," he said finally, "I'm going to set type too. And the first thing I print is going to be you. Your whole face, described. So it's fair."

Astrid laughed—an unrehearsed sound, still strange in her own ears—and kissed the top of the double-crowned head.

"Then you'd better start practicing," she said. "I have a lot of freckles."

They stood together in the doorway a while longer, printer and apprentice and son and the whole found lineage of them, while below the town the green water moved and kept its counsel, and inside the shop the type waited in its case, patient as metal, ready for morning—for the next name, and the next, and the long unwitnessed procession of them, all asking only what everyone asks: *say that I was here.* And the press, her father's press, Bram's press, hers, answered the only way it knew how, in the language that outlasted every committee ever convened:

It would.
