## 1. The Weight of Water

The scales at the Meridian Trading Company weighed three things: pearl shell, copra, and people. The first two were measured in hundredweights on the great brass balance by the loading door. The third was measured more quietly—Hazel Underhill had been weighed at six years old and found worth exactly the cost of her mother's funeral debt, and no one had re-weighed her since.

She had decided long ago that this was a mercy.

On the morning in question—the morning before everything, though she didn't know it yet—Hazel stood at the sorting bench with a knife in one hand and half a shell of *Pinctada maxima* in the other, prying barnacles off with the patience of a woman who had learned that patience was cheaper than trouble. Around her, the warehouse breathed its usual breaths: salt, tar, coconut oil, the sweet rot of shell discarded too long in the heaps behind the north wall. Sunlight came down through the high louvres in dusty blades and lay across the floor like something valuable that nobody had bothered to steal.

"Underhill." Brandt's voice, from somewhere near the office.

Hazel did not look up. This was important. Looking up was interest, and interest was a door, and doors let things through from both directions.

"Underhill," again, closer now, boots on the boards.

"Yes, Mister Brandt." Softly, eyes down, hands still moving. A girl who kept working was a girl with no time for anything else. A girl with no time for anything else was a girl nobody needed to think about.

"The Kestrel lot's short. Forty pounds light."

"I weighed it twice, sir. Thursday's catch came in wet; it'll dry down to weight by Friday."

A pause. She could feel him deciding whether she was contradicting him or helping him. It was a delicate thing, being right without appearing to insist on it.

"Hm," said Brandt, which meant he would check, discover she was correct, and never acknowledge it. He moved off toward the copra shed, and Hazel let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding, slow through her nose so her shoulders wouldn't rise.

That was the whole art, really. Shoulders level. Eyes down. Voice like furniture being moved in another room. Twelve years of practice since the day the company clerk had entered her into the indenture ledger in a hand as neat as stitching—*H. Underhill, ward, term 14 yrs, domestic & warehouse*—and she had understood, even at six, standing in her dead mother's too-large sandals, that the world was divided into people history happened to and people it happened elsewhere to, and that she had been assigned, by debt and by temperament, to the second kind.

History, Hazel had observed, took place in capital cities, aboard warships, in offices where men with good boots signed things. It did not take place at a sorting bench in Harbor Reach, among women with cracked knuckles and men missing fingers, weighing what divers died to bring up. History had no use for them, and they returned the courtesy. It was, on the whole, an equitable arrangement.

* * *

At noon the shell-sorts broke for meal, and Hazel carried her rice and salt fish to her place on the seaward steps, which was hers the way the sorting bench was hers—by being unremarkable enough that claiming it would never occur to anyone else.

The harbor lay flat and glittering under the trade wind, all its ugliness rinsed away for the moment. Dugouts bobbed at their moorings. Out past the reef line, the company's pearling luggers sat dark on the water like patient birds, and beyond them the sea went from green to that deep blue her mother had called *the true color*.

Her mother had been a diver. That sentence contained nearly everything worth knowing about Hazel, though almost nobody left alive knew it.

Maren Underhill had come off the boats twenty years breathing-hold diving, chest like a barrel, hands that could find a shell in the murk by touch alone. She had taught Hazel to hold her breath in the shallows off Diver's Beach when Hazel was four, kneeling in the wash with Hazel's small face between her palms.

"Breathe out first," she'd say. "People think you hold your breath going down. You don't. You let go of your air, then you go. Everything hard is easier if you've already let go."

Then she would press her forehead to Hazel's, count, and come up laughing, hair streaming, saying Hazel had lasted a whole wave longer than yesterday and would soon out-dive her entirely, which was a lie, because Maren Underhill could stay under as long as a seal and everyone knew it, including the sea, which seemed to have taken it personally.

The sea took things personally. Hazel had believed this as a child and had never quite managed to stop. When the water rose calm and glassy some mornings, she thought it looked smug.

Her mother drowned on a Tuesday, in a season when there should have been no drowning at all, and the company had paid for the funeral because the company paid for funerals the way an orchard pays for fallen fruit—it was simply what became of them. The clerk wrote *accidental, apnea, shell-seeking* in the death book, and Hazel, aged six, was written into another book entirely, and both entries were made in the same neat stitching-hand, and neither book asked her how she felt about any of it.

She had learned something on that Tuesday, though it took her years to say it plainly even to herself: grief is heavy, but it floats. Everything that goes down comes back up eventually, changed, unrecognizable, wearing new names. Her mother had gone down as Maren Underhill, diver, widow, woman who sang off-key to annoy the priests, and had come up as a line in a ledger. Hazel had gone down as a daughter and come up as an indenture number. The sea kept nothing; the ledgers kept everything; and between the two of them, a person could be erased very tidily.

So Hazel had chosen the only sensible course. Be light. Be the sort of person whose name, if spoken at all, was spoken as *Underhill* and forgotten before the echo finished. Let history carry on elsewhere, among the people who mattered, and leave her to her shells and her scales and her spot on the seaward steps.

It worked. That was the terrible, wonderful thing about it. It worked every single day.

* * *

The afternoon brought the Kestrel's catch down from the drying racks, and Hazel weighed it, and it had dried down exactly to weight, and she did not look at Brandt when he passed, and he did not look at her, and this small mutual invisibility was as satisfying as a well-set bone.

By late afternoon the heat had gone soft and the warehouse smelled almost kindly of itself. Hazel worked the last of the lot: scrubbing shell, sorting by nacre and size, setting aside the pieces with the deep rainbow bellies that fetched prices in cities she would never see. Paris, maybe. London. Somewhere a lady would wear her mother's world on a bodice and never know there was such a place as Harbor Reach, or such a woman as Maren Underhill, or such a rule as *breathe out first.*

Hazel found she didn't mind. Mostly. There was a comfort in being overlooked that people who were noticed could never understand. To be unseen was to be safe. Nobody interrogated a shadow. Nobody shipped a shadow abroad in sealed crates, or sold it, or struck it from a roll. The invisible could stand in a doorway during a storm and watch the lightning with both eyes open.

Mostly she didn't mind.

There were moments—a few a year, perhaps a few a month lately, which she chose not to examine—when the comfort thinned like breath held too long, and she felt the pressure of all the unsaid things stacking up behind her ribs. Moments when she caught herself counting names instead of shell. Her mother. Old Tam, who dove until his ears bled and then dove some more. The Sef brothers. Half the beach at Diver's Town had once been able to call her by her first name, and now nobody did, and she had arranged it that way herself, plank by plank, year by year, like building her own coffin lid and sleeping comfortably under it.

*Breathe out first,* her mother said in her head, at moments like these. *Then go down.*

But go down where? There was nowhere to go. There was only the warehouse and the tide and tomorrow's weights, and the great safe gray sameness of a life that history had agreed to ignore.

Hazel set the last shell on the pile and brushed the dust from her hands.

The light was failing gold over the water when the storm came—not the afternoon thunder that grumbled off the hills and went away, but a real one, dragging its black skirt across the reef, bending the palms flat. The dockworkers scrambled to batten hatches. Rain hit the tin roof like applause from a very large, unfriendly audience.

Brandt shouted for doors. Hazel hauled the seaward door shut against the squall, turned back toward her bench—

—and heard, underneath the rain and the wind and the flapping tin, thin as a needle through sailcloth, a sound she had not heard in twelve years and would have known anywhere in the world, asleep or drowned or dead.

Somebody's baby was crying on the company step.
