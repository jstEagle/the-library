## 2. Left at the Door

The storm came in off the harbor on a Tuesday, which Hazel thought was typical of storms—they had no respect for the schedule of decent people. Tuesdays were weighing days at the Meridian warehouse, and weighing days meant Brandt in a good mood until noon and a foul one after, depending entirely on whether the shell came in heavy or light.

It came in light that week. The divers were staying ashore, all sensible of them, because even the bravest of them did not argue with a sea that had already taken people they knew. So there was little to weigh, which meant Brandt prowled the long floor of the warehouse like a cat denied its mouse, and everyone overworked at looking busy.

Hazel was busy in the honest way: she was counting shell into the company crates, each nacreous disc catching the lamplight, and thinking of nothing at all. This was her particular talent. Other people could sing, or mend nets, or hold their liquor. Hazel could empty her face of expression so thoroughly that she became, effectively, furniture. It had taken years of practice. Her mother could have done it too, once—though her mother's version had been different. Her mother's version had been holding her breath underwater for three minutes while her heart hammered like someone beating on a door.

*You can survive anything,* her mother used to say, *if you can make yourself small enough inside it.*

Her mother had been wrong about that, in the end. But Hazel kept the advice anyway, the way you keep a coat that no longer fits but belonged to someone you loved.

The rain arrived first as a rumor—a hiss along the tin roof, then a roar—and the wind found every gap in the warehouse walls and made low whistling complaints through them, like old men at a meeting. The lamps guttered. Somewhere out in the harbor, boats knocked against their moorings with a sound like impatient knuckles.

Hazel was stacking the last crate when she heard the knock that wasn't the boats.

Three soft raps at the side door—the delivery door, the one nobody used after dark. She stood still, listening. Wind. Rain. Then, threading through both, thin as wire: crying.

Not a cat. Not wind in a pipe. A baby.

She looked down the length of the warehouse. Brandt had retreated to his office with his ledger and his lamp and his bad mood. The other laborers had gone home ahead of the storm, sensibly. Nobody else had heard it, or if they'd heard it, they'd decided—as Hazel was at that very moment trying to decide—that it was not their business.

History happened elsewhere, she reminded herself. To people who mattered. A baby crying at a warehouse door in a storm was somebody else's history, and the wisest thing she could do was let it stay outside where it belonged.

She went to the door anyway.

Later she would tell herself this was practical—that whoever left it would come back, that Brandt would blame her for the noise, that it was better to know what she was dealing with. All true things. None of them the true thing. The true thing was simply that the crying got inside her chest and pulled, the way a hook pulls, and her feet went before her philosophy could stop them.

She unbarred the door a hand's width and the wind shoved it wider, flinging rain across her apron. On the step, in the lee of the doorframe where the water hadn't reached, sat a bundle wrapped in a grey blanket, soaked at one corner, wailing at the sky with an astonishing lack of gratitude for being alive.

There was a note pinned to it.

Hazel brought the whole arrangement inside—one arm under the bundle, the note pinched in her fingers—and shouldered the door shut against the gale. In the lamplight the bundle resolved itself: a baby, weeks old at most, red-faced and furious, with a thatch of dark wet-curling hair and fists like small knots. And the note, written in a clerk's careful hand, the kind of hand that had never once been punished for what it wrote:

***Forget the face.***

No signature. No instructions beyond that single line, as if forgetting were a task one could be assigned, like counting shell or sweeping the floor. As if faces were inventory to be written off.

"Well," Hazel said to the baby, "that's a rude way to arrive anywhere."

The baby stopped crying, briefly, to consider this. Then resumed, louder, having reflected on the matter and found it insufficient.

Hazel looked toward Brandt's office. She had perhaps a quarter hour before he emerged to lock up, and everything about her survival depended on how those minutes went. She should put the bundle back on the step, she knew. Let the mission find it. Let the church bell-ringer, the watchman, anyone with a larger share of visibility than hers discover it. That was the arithmetic of invisibility: never touch anything that touches you back.

Instead she sat down on an overturned crate, tucked the blanket close, and held the child the way her mother had taught her to hold a breath—steadily, without waste, ready to release at the first sign of trouble.

The baby quieted against her shoulder. It smelled of rain and milk and something faintly salty, like skin after the sea.

* * *

Brandt appeared at half past nine with his coat buttoned crooked and his lamp held high, and Hazel was back at her crates by then, hands moving, face emptied, the bundle hidden behind a stack of shell chests with the practiced ease of a woman who had spent her whole life making herself furniture.

"Storm's blown the divers ashore," Brandt said, by way of greeting. He never greeted; he announced. "Weigh what we have and go home early. I want the floor swept before—" He stopped. His head tilted, a dog hearing a distant whistle. "What is that noise?"

"What noise, sir?"

"That." He listened. "Like a cat."

"Wind in the pipes, sir," said Hazel, who was an excellent liar in the narrow sense: she lied only to preserve the world exactly as it was, never to change it. "Comes up something dreadful in weather."

Brandt frowned at the ceiling, at the pipes, at the general injustice of storms. And then, because God has a sense of humor and aims it at the deserving and undeserving alike, the baby chose that moment to hiccup—a small, explosive sound from behind the shell chests, unmistakable as a cannon.

Brandt crossed the floor in four strides.

He pulled the bundle out and held it at arm's length, the way one holds a dead fish or a legal summons. The baby, sensing perhaps that its future was being weighed, went eerily silent and stared up at him.

"Left it on the step," Brandt read from the note, lips moving. "Forget the face." He grunted. "Some diver's brat, most likely. Half of 'em can't count to the children they've got." He thrust the bundle back toward Hazel, then thought better of it and didn't, keeping his hands free of it entirely. "Mission orphanage takes them. Take it there in the morning. First light, before the cart comes. And Underhill—" his eyes narrowed, and here was the man Hazel had spent eleven years avoiding, "—forget the face. That's an order, not a sentiment."

"Yes, sir," said Hazel, in the voice of a woman who forgot things professionally.

He dropped the note on the nearest crate, took his lamp, and left through the front, into the rain.

Hazel stood a long moment in the lamplight, holding the future at arm's length.

* * *

The warehouse at night was a country unto itself: the creak of timber settling, rain drumming the roof like applause from a vast indifferent audience, the smell of salt and shell and lamp oil. Hazel sat with the baby in the lee of the shell chests, a corner she'd occupied for years during long nights, and unwrapped the blanket enough to see what she was dealing with.

Ten fingers. Ten toes. An expression of profound grievance, temporarily suspended. A healthy set of lungs, already demonstrated.

And then, tucked into the fold of the blanket near the small fist, she found the amulet.

She knew it before she fully saw it—the weight of it, the shape, the way it lay in cloth. Her hands remembered before her mind caught up. Carved shell, no bigger than a thumb joint, strung on braided cord, scarred deliberately with fine cross-hatching so it would grip the water instead of flashing in the sun. Divers' amulets were scored like that when they were blessed, and re-scored for luck, and re-scored again when their owners survived something that should have drowned them. Her mother had worn one until the day the sea kept her. Hazel had watched it go into the ground with her because the company wouldn't release the body's effects to a child, and she had been eleven, and she had learned then that grief was a thing you did quietly, off the books.

This one was heavily scarred—scored and scored again, dozens of crossings, the record of a diver who had come up from deep water many more times than luck could honestly account for.

Hazel sat very still. The rain applauded. The baby slept against her collarbone with the boneless totality of the very young.

*Forget the face*, said the note. And she understood now why the order existed, and it turned her cold in a way the storm couldn't touch. Because a note like that meant someone above wanted this child unwritten, and the company only unwrote things connected to its own arithmetic—to the ledgers, to the rolls, to the names that went into the columns and came out changed. Her mother's name had gone into a column once. What had come out was *deceased, non-chargeable*, a phrase invented by men who had never held their breath for anything.

The wise move—the move of eleven years' standing—was to leave the amulet with the child. Deliver both to the mission. Forget the face, per instructions, and remain furniture.

Hazel worked the cord over her own head and tucked the amulet beneath her shirt, flat against her sternum, where it lay small and hard and warm as a swallowed coal. She was not sure afterward whether she had decided to do it or simply discovered she had. Some refusals happen below the level of philosophy, in the place where a girl once watched her mother's luck go into the ground.

"Forget the face," she told the sleeping baby softly. "All right. I'll forget the face."

She would remember everything else.

In the morning, she would carry the child to the mission as ordered, walk past the clerks' room where Ivo sat mending papers with his careful guilty hands, and ask him—casually, oh, so casually—how a person might go about finding out who a child belonged to.

She fell asleep against the shell chests with the rain still applauding, and did not dream, and woke once to find the baby's fist closed around her finger, holding on with the whole of its small furious strength.
