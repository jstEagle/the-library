## 3. Ivo of the Second Drawer

The clerk's office smelled of ink and dried cuttlefish, which was an odd combination until you learned that Ivo used the cuttlebone to blot his pages and the ink to write on them, and that he considered both activities roughly equal in dignity.

Hazel had been delivering shell tallies to the office since she was eleven years old, back when she'd had to stand on a crate to reach the counter. In all those years she had never once seen Ivo's desk in disarray. It was not tidy the way Brandt demanded tidiness—terrified, defensive tidiness—but tidy the way a spider's web is tidy: everything placed exactly where it served him. Ledgers squared to the desk edge. Pens laid parallel like sleeping eels. And beneath it all, a second drawer.

Everyone knew about the second drawer. Nobody mentioned it.

She set the tally book on his counter and waited while he checked her figures against his own, running one long finger down the column with the concentration of a man defusing something.

"You're short four hundredweight," he said.

"I'm not."

"Your handwriting says you're not." He tapped the page. "Your handwriting also says you were tired when you wrote it, hungry when you added it, and angry about something you haven't told me. But the arithmetic is sound. Sign there."

She signed. He blotted the entry with the cuttlebone and slid the tally into a folder labeled in his small, upright hand: *Meridian Trading Co. — Shell Receipts — 1898*. Then he looked up at her over the top of his spectacles, which he wore low on his nose for reading and high on his forehead for judging, and currently they were somewhere in the middle, which meant curiosity.

"You've got a question," he said.

"I've got a tally delivered."

"You've got a question wearing a tally as a coat." He leaned back in his chair, which complained. Ivo was a thin man built out of angles and patience, with the kind of face that made strangers ask him for directions even in towns where he'd never been. "Ask it or don't. But don't stand there smelling of it."

Hazel looked at the door. She looked at the window, through which she could see the yard, and Brandt crossing the yard, and Brandt's shadow crossing the yard behind him like a second, worse man.

"How," she said, keeping her voice at the level of talk about weather, "would a person find out who a child belongs to?"

* * *

Ivo did not answer immediately. He took off his spectacles, folded them, unfolded them, put them back on, and then stood up and closed the shutters—not all the way, just enough to turn the two of them into a rumor instead of a sight.

Then he sat down again and said, quietly and with real feeling: "Are you out of your mind?"

"It's a general question."

"There are no general questions. There are only questions somebody will eventually be asked about." He pulled open the first drawer—the public one—took out a blank manifest, and began making marks on it so that anyone glancing through the shutters would see a clerk doing clerk-work. His hands were steady. His voice wasn't, quite. "Questions are how indentured people disappear, Hazel. You know that better than most. A girl asks about a birth record, and six months later there's a transfer paper, and a ship, and a signature that looks suspiciously like hers but isn't, and nobody remembers her well enough to say otherwise because she made herself forgettable on purpose. Sound like anyone?"

"That's very dramatic."

"That's Tuesday. That happened on a Tuesday." He stopped writing. "Whose child?"

"Nobody's. An orphan. From the storm."

"Hazel."

"A baby," she said, and heard her own voice crack on the word, which annoyed her enormously. "Left on the step Sunday night. Brandt wants it at the mission by tomorrow. There was a note. *Forget the face.*"

Ivo sat very still. Somewhere outside, a winch groaned; gulls argued over something dead; the harbor did its usual business of being loud without saying anything. Inside the shuttered office, Ivo looked at her for a long moment, and then, moving slowly, deliberately—like a man showing her exactly what he was doing—he reached down past the first drawer and unlocked the second one.

* * *

She had imagined, over the years, all manner of things in that drawer. Company secrets. Money. Love letters. Once, vividly, a pistol. What was actually there was a thin packet of papers tied with string, a child's shoe, far too small to be anything but kept rather than worn, and a sheet of good rag paper covered in practice signatures—all of them the same name, written over and over in gradually improving hands, the way a person teaches themselves to be someone else.

Ivo untied the string and spread three documents on the desk between them.

"Baptismal certificate," he said. "Ship's passage ticket. Letter of guardianship. All genuine-looking, which is to say, forged, but forged properly, by someone who knows what a priest's hand looks like after a long day and what kind of seal wax the mission buys cheap." He tapped the baptismal paper. "This is what finding out who a child belongs to actually looks like. Not records. Records can be drowned, transferred, burned. This—" he touched the shoe, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping thing "—this is what can't be erased. Something someone kept."

"You're going to tell me to forget the whole business," Hazel said. "Aren't you. You're winding up to it. I can see your face doing it."

"My face," said Ivo with some dignity, "is considering the matter from all sides, which is more than can be said for yours." He gathered the papers up, retied the string, and paused with the bundle in his hands. "Twenty-two years ago there was a boy on this island who could sign any man's name better than the man himself. The company found that out and bought his sentence off him and turned it into employment. Very generous. Indefinite employment." He tucked the packet back in the drawer. "He learned that the safest place in the world is inside a lie everyone already believes. And then last night a woman walks in asking me how babies get born onto ledgers, and he finds his hands shaking, and he has to admit the safest place was never safe at all. It was just quiet."

"The baby's mother was a diver," Hazel said. "I think. There was an amulet. Like my mother's."

Ivo went to the window and looked out through the slats of the shutter for a while. Down in the yard, the divers were coming up from the sheds, brown and laughing, carrying their masks like hats.

"Do you know what I write," he said finally, not turning around, "when the company needs a diver's death made clean? I write *transferred*. Port Darwin, usually. Sometimes Thursday Island. Somewhere with a name far enough away that no one checks. It's kinder than *drowned*, and cheaper than *lost*, and it keeps the insurance men happy and the rolls balanced." His reflection in the darkened glass watched her. "I have written that word perhaps forty times. I told myself each one was paperwork. Paperwork isn't anything. Paperwork is weather."

"And now?"

"Now I find myself thinking that weather leaves traces. Salt in the wood. Rust in the hinges." He came back to the desk and stood over it, and for the first time since she'd known him, Ivo looked less like a clerk and more like what he was underneath: a man who had spent twenty-two years signing other people's names and had never once been asked to sign his own. "If you want to find who a child belongs to, you start where the lies are thickest, because that's where the truth was worth burying. The dive rolls. The transfer entries. Especially anything dated near the wreck of the *Cormorant*."

"The *Cormorant* sank before I was born."

"Mm. And yet," Ivo said, pulling the dive rolls toward him across the desk, "here we both are, awake at an indecent hour of the working day, about to do something spectacularly unwise." He uncapped the ink. "Bring me anything else that came with the child. The blanket, if it's marked. The note itself—I want to see the hand. And Hazel—" he looked up, and his voice dropped back to its careful, ordinary register, the voice of a man inside a lie everyone believed, though it didn't fit him quite the same way anymore, "—if anyone asks, you came here to deliver a tally. Four hundredweight discrepancy, since resolved. Nothing else ever passed between us but shell figures and complaints about the weather."

"Complaints about the weather," Hazel agreed.

At the door she paused. "Why are you helping me? You don't even like questions."

Ivo picked up his pen, squared it with the others, and gave her the smallest smile she had ever been permitted to see.

"Because whoever left that baby wrote *forget the face*," he said, "and then chose a note short enough to burn and a night dark enough to drown in, and still couldn't keep the amulet out of the blanket. Somebody wanted the face remembered badly enough to risk everything getting it to our door. The least we can do," he said, dipping the pen, turning back to the rolls, "is be worth the risk."

Hazel went out into the yard with the sun on her shoulders and her heart hammering like a loose plank, thinking: *the dive rolls. The transfers. The Cormorant.*

Behind her, through the shutter slats, Ivo drew the *Cormorant*'s crew roll toward him—and stopped, finger pinned to a line of entries, all dated the same week, all reading the same impossible word:

*Transferred.*
