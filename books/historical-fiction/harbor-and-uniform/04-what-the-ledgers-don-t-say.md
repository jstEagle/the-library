## 4. What the Ledgers Don't Say

Hazel had learned to read a room the way her mother had read water: by what moved beneath the surface. So when she carried the ledgers into Ivo's corner of the counting room that Tuesday—three volumes of shell weights and one small bundle of laundry she was not supposed to be carrying—she watched him the way she'd watch a tide pool before stepping in.

Ivo Renner sat at his desk like a man who had been folded there. He was thin in the way of paper itself, all edges and creases, with ink permanently shadowing the first knuckle of his right hand. He wrote clean hands for other people—bills of lading, transfer certificates, the tidy fictions that let the Meridian Trading Company move cargo and men across the sea without troubling anyone's conscience—and he did it with the serene face of someone who had never once wondered where the men went.

Everyone at the warehouse believed Ivo wondered nothing. Hazel had spent eleven years believing it too.

"The shell ledgers need copying," she said, setting them down. "Brandt wants them by Friday."

"Brandt always wants things by Friday," Ivo said, not looking up. "It gives the week a shape."

"And this." She set the bundle down more carefully. Inside it, wrapped in a scrap of flour sacking, lay the amulet.

That got his attention. Not much of it—a flicker of the eyes, the pen pausing mid-stroke—but Hazel had been trained from birth to notice flickers. Her mother used to say the sea tells you everything if you watch the small movements, because the big ones come too late to matter.

"You found a rock," Ivo said.

"I found an amulet. On a baby nobody's supposed to remember."

The pen finished its stroke. Then it stopped entirely, which for Ivo was the equivalent of standing up and shouting.

"I don't know anything about any baby," he said.

"No," Hazel agreed pleasantly. "Nobody does. That's rather the point."

* * *

Ivo kept his secrets the way other men kept money: close, counted, and never spent all at once. He had a second drawer—the real kind, bottom left, beneath the false bottom of a drawer full of blotting paper—and inside it lived a life that did not belong to Meridian. A christening record in his mother's hand. A photograph of a house in a country whose name he had not spoken aloud in nine years. Papers made clean for himself, once, by a forger better than he would ever become, and which had gotten him here, to this desk, in this harbor town, writing clean papers for people who could have him hanged for the crime of being exactly as good as they needed him to be.

So he understood questions. He understood that questions were how indentured people disappeared—not dramatically, not with chains and shouting, but quietly, the way a clerk strikes through an error. One day your bunk is empty and your rations are reassigned and Brandt says, *Who?* with such genuine puzzlement that you begin to doubt you were ever here at all.

Which is why, when the girl with the unremarkable face set an amulet on his desk and said *nobody does*, Ivo should have said nothing, copied nothing, asked nothing.

He looked at the amulet instead.

It was bone or shell, worn smooth, drilled through with a hole for cord. And carved into it—a mark he knew. He knew it the way a man knows a signature he has forged: intimately, guiltily, and often. Three lines crossing a crescent. The diver's mark. It appeared on crew rolls, on hazard-pay chits, on the little stamped tokens the company issued so divers could draw against their wages at the company store, thereby ensuring the company got the wages back.

"This came off a child?" he said.

"A baby. Left on the step during the storm. There was a note." Hazel recited it flatly, the way one recites scripture or a sentence of judgment: "*Forget the face.*"

Ivo turned the amulet over twice. "And you didn't forget it."

"I'm very bad at forgetting," Hazel said. "It's my only flaw."

He almost smiled. That was the trouble with Hazel Underhill—she had a dryness about her that snuck up on a man, and a stillness that made you want to fill the silence just to hear something. Dangerous qualities in a person asking dangerous questions.

"If you're going to ask me what I think you're going to ask me," Ivo said, "the answer is no."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were about to ask me to look in the rolls."

"I was about to ask," Hazel said, "whether you've ever noticed how many divers get *transferred*."

The word sat between them. Transferred. It was a good word, a clean word, one of Ivo's own products. Men transferred to the northern station, to the southern station, to opportunities abroad. The company transferred men the way the sea took them—regularly, without explanation, and never to anywhere anyone had been.

"That," Ivo said slowly, "is not a question I can afford to answer."

"I know," said Hazel. "That's why I brought the ledgers. You'll be alone with them until Friday anyway. I wouldn't want you to be bored."

She collected the flour-sack bundle, tucked the amulet back inside, and left. It was, Ivo reflected afterward, exactly like being mugged by someone very polite.

* * *

Hazel spent Wednesday pretending to count shell.

This was easier than it sounded, because everyone pretended to count shell; the numbers in the shell ledgers bore roughly the same relationship to actual shell as a portrait bears to breathing. She weighed, she recorded, she watched the loft stairs where Ivo worked, and she thought about her mother's hands, which had been scarred in patterns the amulet-maker would have recognized.

Her mother had died in the water when Hazel was seven, and the company ledger said—she had checked, years ago, out of a child's stubborn grief—that she had been *discharged*. Discharged, like a debt paid, like a cannon fired. Hazel had hated the word for a long time before she understood it was simply the smallest lie available, the lie of least effort. The company did not hate its dead. It merely declined to keep them.

Now there was a baby with a diver's mark sleeping in Mrs. Ferro's kitchen, and a note ordering the face forgotten, and Hazel found that the old philosophy—*history happens elsewhere, to people who matter*—had developed a crack in it, and through the crack the cold was getting in.

Thursday evening, after the lamps were lit and Brandt had gone home to whatever Brandts do in the dark, Ivo appeared beside her at the weighing bench. He did not sit. He stood the way a man stands when the floor has recently betrayed him.

"You wanted me bored," he said.

"Did it work?"

"I pulled the Cormorant rolls." His voice was low and perfectly level, which is how Hazel knew it was costing him. "The diving crew. Eighteen months ago, the wreck. You remember the night?"

"Everyone remembers the night. The lighthouse was dark."

"The lighthouse was dark," Ivo agreed, "because the lamp oil contract had been allowed to lapse, because the company had decided a working light wasn't worth the expense, because—" He stopped. Breathed. "Because nobody expected anyone to ask why."

Hazel set down her weight. "How many were lost?"

"Eleven. Eleven divers, out past the shoals, when the weather turned and no light marked the channel." He laid a sheet of paper on the bench between them, copied in his own immaculate hand. "Here is what the roll says happened to eleven men."

She read. *Transferred. Transferred. Transferred.* Ten entries, each with a date, each with a destination station that Hazel was fairly certain did not exist, written in Ivo's predecessor's hand—or possibly in Ivo's own, though she looked at him and decided not to ask, because some mercies are also weapons.

"Ten," she said. "There were eleven."

"The eleventh is the interesting one." Ivo tapped the last line. "Diver listed as transferred the same night. Name of Sefu Adeyemi. And his token number—" he produced the amulet from his pocket, held it beside the page, "—matches the mark on your rock."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from under the door, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere out in the harbor, water slapped against pilings, patient as arithmetic.

"They drowned," Hazel said at last. "All of them. They drowned and the company wrote *transferred* because a drowned man's family gets compensation and a transferred man's family gets a forwarding address that doesn't exist."

"Yes."

"And the baby—"

"Is either Sefu Adeyemi's child or his grandchild, and either way belongs to a man the company says is alive and working in a place that isn't on any map." Ivo took the paper back, and his hand, Hazel noticed, was not quite steady. "Do you understand what this means? It isn't one lie. It's a system. Every death for years—every diver lost, every wage unpaid, every widow turned away from the office door because her husband is officially *transferred*—it's all in those rolls, all of it neat, all of it signed."

"By whom?"

"By whoever signs the transfers." Ivo's jaw tightened. "There's an authorization column. It's initialled. B.M."

"Brandt."

"Brandt," Ivo confirmed. "Initialled on ten separate falsifications, dated the night of the wreck, plus forty-one more going back six years. Forty-one men, Hazel. Forty-one families told their men went north, or south, or abroad, while the company saved itself the price of a lamp."

Hazel looked down at the amulet in her palm. Three lines crossing a crescent. Her mother had worn one just like it, and when Hazel was small she had asked what the mark meant, and her mother had said, *It means I belong to the water and the water knows it,* and had laughed, and Hazel had laughed too, because she was five and did not yet know that belonging could be a debt collected all at once.

"We have to put it on the record," she said.

Ivo stared at her. "On the record. Do you hear yourself? We are two indentured nobodies in a warehouse full of men who disappear people for asking about the *weather*. There is no record. That's the entire point of the record—it records nothing."

"There's a magistrate coming," Hazel said. "Mrs. Ferro heard the dock talk. Next week, on the company ship."

"A colonial magistrate doesn't care about our ledgers."

"He cares about his own," Hazel said. "And Ivo—" she closed her fingers around the amulet, "—he doesn't know yet that he cares. That's what we're for."

For a long moment Ivo Renner, forger of clean papers, keeper of second drawers, looked at the girl with the unremarkable face, and something in him that had been folded for nine years began, cautiously, to unfold.

"You understand," he said, "that if we do this, we stop being invisible. Completely. Permanently. There's no drawer deep enough to hide us after."

Hazel thought of her mother's name, absent from every list but the one in her own memory. Thought of a baby who would grow up told her father had gone away. Thought of forty-one forwarding addresses that led nowhere.

"I know," she said. "I've been invisible for eleven years. I'd like to try being somebody."

Ivo exhaled, long and slow, like a man going under.

"Then we'd best work fast," he said. "Because I checked the shipping schedule this morning, and the crates of old files bound for disposal abroad—they're already sealed."

"When do they sail?"

"Sunday. With the morning tide."
