## 5. Brandt's Kindness

Hazel had a private theory about Brandt's moods, developed over eleven years of careful observation from the safest possible distance: that he kept them, like the ledgers, in locked drawers, and opened whichever one suited the day's business.

On Wednesday morning he opened the wrong one.

She was at the scales, weighing a morning's shell—nine hundredweight and change, the pearl oysters thinning year by year the way the fish had thinned before them—when his shadow fell across the bench. She knew it was Brandt because the other men stepped back without seeming to step back, a small tide going out around her.

"Underhill."

"Sir."

"You've been with us how long?"

Here it was, then. Eleven years of being unremarkable, and the reckoning arrived anyway, as she'd always known it must. She set down the scale weight with steady hands and prepared to be noticed for the first and possibly last time.

"Eleven years, sir. Since I was eight."

"Eight." He said it as though checking her figure against some internal ledger. "You were entered into the rolls by your mother's indenture transfer. Meridian holds your papers until you're twenty-one." A pause, during which Hazel did not breathe. "Three more years."

"Yes, sir."

He looked out over the floor—the shell bins, the casking crew, old Petersen arguing with a hoist rope—and when he spoke again his voice had changed register entirely, dropped into something almost gentle, which frightened Hazel far more than shouting would have.

"I've been watching you, Underhill. You don't gossip. You don't shirk. You don't ask questions." He turned back to her. "That last one is rarer than you'd think. Most people can't help themselves."

"No, sir," said Hazel, who had spent the past week doing nothing else.

"Effective Monday, you're moved to the counting room. Shell tally and reconciliation. Your ration goes to second class—meat twice a week, tea, sugar. There's an allowance of two shillings a month besides." He produced a slip of paper and laid it on the bench between them, square and official, like a card dealt face-up. "Sign there."

* * *

Mrs. Ferro put a whole spoonful of sugar in Hazel's tea that evening, which was her way of celebrating, and sat the baby on her knee where he could watch the stove with the round-eyed gravity of a man auditing accounts.

"Second-class rations," Mrs. Ferro repeated, turning the words over like coins to test them. "Meat twice a week. In eleven years."

"In eleven years," Hazel agreed.

"And what does Mr. Brandt want in return?"

"That's what I keep turning over." Hazel wrapped both hands around the tin mug. The kitchen was warm and smelled of onions and lamp oil; outside, the storm of two nights past had left everything rinsed and dripping. "It doesn't fit. Kindness from Brandt is like rain from a clear sky—you start looking for the flood."

"Or," said Mrs. Ferro, bouncing the baby slightly, "it's exactly what it looks like, and the man wants his counting done honest for once."

"It's not that."

"How do you know?"

"Because of what he said at the end. Almost as an afterthought—he was halfway out the door. He said—" Hazel closed her eyes; the words came back with their own weather attached. "He said the magistrate's ship arrives Tuesday next, and I should have the reconciliation books ready for inspection, and there'd be sealed crates going out with her, disposal abroad, and none of my business, but a girl who could count could go far in this company. That was the word he used. *Far.*"

Mrs. Ferro had gone still. The baby, deprived of motion, made a small complaint and was absently resumed.

"Disposal abroad," she said.

"The same crates Ivo told us about. The same ship." Hazel opened her eyes. "He handed me better rations and a warning in the same breath, and he thinks I can't tell which was the gift."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, in the loft where Ivo now slept under a roof of company timber and company suspicion, a floorboard settled.

"He knows something," Mrs. Ferro said at last. "Maybe not everything. Maybe he only knows the magistrate's coming and the books want looking pretty before she does. But a man like that doesn't promote a girl by accident. Not you." She looked at Hazel with an expression that was nearly fierce. "He promoted you because someone told him you'd been asking after things. Or because someone told him you hadn't, and he wanted to know why not."

Hazel thought about this all through supper, and again while she helped dry the pots, and once more lying awake in her cot behind the shell store, listening to the harbor water slap the pilings in the dark.

Her mother had taught her to hold her breath in the shallows off Diver's Beach, before the Company took the beach and everything else. *Don't fight the water,* she'd said, hand pressed flat on little Hazel's sternum. *Be heavy. Be nothing. The water carries what doesn't argue with it.* Hazel had built her whole life inside that instruction. Be heavy. Be nothing. Be carried.

And here was Brandt, offering her air.

* * *

The counting room, when Hazel reported to it Monday, proved to be a narrow room off the warehouse office with one window, one desk, and a view of the yard gate—which meant she now saw everyone who came and went, and was seen by them, and could no longer cross her own workplace without existing. It was disorientating, like being asked to breathe underwater.

The work itself was simple enough: match the shell tallies against the weigh-bills, reconcile the differences, initial the pages. Within a day she understood why the position had stood empty since spring. The books didn't reconcile. They reconciled beautifully, in fact—too beautifully, every discrepancy smoothed away in a round, confident hand she recognized as Ivo's, entries balanced so perfectly they rang like struck glass. Someone had been adjusting the counts to cover the shortfall, month after month, and whoever held this desk was expected to initial the adjustments and say nothing.

Hazel initialed and said nothing, and thought: *this is the leash. Not the meat, not the tea. This desk. Now my name sits under theirs, page after page, and if ever the company needs someone small to feed to an inquiry, here she is, already signed.*

It was, she had to admit, elegant. She almost admired it.

Tuesday afternoon a runner brought her a note in Brandt's blunt hand: *Magistrate's party confirmed for Tuesday next. H.M.S. Perdita, arrival on morning tide. All reconciliation complete and countersigned by Friday. — B.*

Beneath it, in different ink, added later and heavily blotted: *Crates 1–6 manifest as shell samples. Do not open. Do not tally.*

Six crates. Ivo had said four.

* * *

She found Ivo at the evening meal, wedged into his usual corner of the mess bench with his tray arranged precisely at right angles to the table edge. She sat down across from him without asking, which between the two of them constituted a shout.

"Six crates," she said, very low. "Not four."

Ivo's spoon paused midway. "The manifest I saw listed four. Old files, disposal abroad."

"Now it says six. Shell samples." She watched his face do the arithmetic. "And he's got me countersigning adjusted tallies by Friday. My initials under every smoothed page in the room."

Ivo set the spoon down. For a moment he looked older, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "That's not kindness, Hazel. That's ballast. He's tying you to the hull so whatever sinks it takes you down too."

"I know."

"Do you?" He glanced up, and there was something raw in it. "Because a person can know a thing's a leash and still be glad of the collar. Warm food does things to a body. Two shillings a month does things to a mind. I've seen good people sign terrible pages for less than meat twice a week."

Hazel turned her tin of tea slowly on the tabletop. Around them the mess clattered on, forty conversations, none of them listening—or so the invisible are permitted to believe.

"When my mother drowned," she said, "the company gave me her ration for three days. As a kindness. Then they transferred her contract value against my indenture fees and told me it was generous." She met his eyes. "I know exactly what Brandt's kindness weighs. I've carried it since I was eight. But knowing doesn't make the meat smell worse or the bed any colder, and that's what frightens me. Not that I'd mistake the leash. That some cold morning I might be grateful for it."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. Then, softly, with the ghost of his careful clerk's smile: "Then we'd best make sure the collar comes off before winter."

* * *

She stopped by the kitchen on her way back, as had become her habit—a habit she examined nightly for signs of dangerous comfort and found, nightly, incurable.

The baby was thriving. That was the plain truth of it, and it undid her a little every time. His cheeks had filled out; his fists had discovered their purpose; he had begun making pronouncements—*eh. Eh. EH*—in the firm tone of a man who will be heard, and Mrs. Ferro answered each one gravely, as parliament answers the throne.

"Look at him," Mrs. Ferro said, lifting him. "Strong as kelp. Whatever current he came in on, he's anchored now."

He grabbed a fistful of her apron string and held it with the whole of his small fury, and Hazel felt the crack in her practiced indifference widen another degree—not a hairline anymore but a fault line running straight through the geology of her eleven careful years.

"Mrs. Ferro," she said. "How many days until Tuesday?"

The cook didn't pretend not to follow. "Seven. Tide turns just past dawn."

"Seven days to copy forty-one dead men out of a ledger, get them aboard a magistrate's ship, and keep a baby fed and hidden while Brandt watches me do it." Hazel heard her own voice and was surprised to find it level. "I used to think being nobody was safe."

"And now?"

Hazel reached out; the baby transferred his grip from apron string to her finger, entirely without ceremony, as though it had always been his.

"Now I think nobody was only ever a place to hide the somebody. And Brandt made a mistake, promoting her." She let the small hand hold on. "He gave me a desk with a window. He gave me the yard gate in plain sight."

From the doorway behind her, a voice she had not heard approach—Ivo, still in his coat, breathing hard from the stairs:

"Hazel. The loft. You need to see this now."

There was blood, she would remember afterward, absolutely none of it his own, on his cuff.
