## 6. Mrs. Ferro's Kitchen

There were three things Rosa Ferro had learned in twenty years of cooking for the Meridian Trading Company, and none of them were recipes.

The first was that a kitchen is the only room in any warehouse where nobody counts who comes in, because everybody assumes the cook has already counted them and found them wanting.

The second was that grief keeps better than salted fish if you wrap it right.

The third was that when an important man says *forget*, what he means is: *I have forgotten already, and I would prefer not to be reminded.*

Rosa had been forgetting things professionally since before Hazel Underhill was born. She forgot the overseer's taste for underdone mutton, so it always came out overdone and he always complained and she always apologized with her eyes down and her hands full of bread. She forgot which clerk had knocked over the pepper oil, though she knew perfectly well it was Ivo, because Ivo turned pink the way other men turned pale. Forgetting, done correctly, was a kind of arithmetic. You subtracted what people didn't want seen, and you kept the difference somewhere safe.

Hers was kept in the flour tin behind the third shelf — not the tin anyone would look in, because everyone knew Rosa counted her flour like a dragon counts gold, and no thief alive would believe she'd leave anything valuable among it. Which was exactly the sort of reasoning that had kept her alive this long, and exactly the sort of reasoning she suspected Hazel Underhill practiced too, every time the girl made herself small in a doorway.

Rosa had been watching Hazel make herself small for eleven years. It was, in Rosa's professional opinion, the most effortful invisibility on the waterfront. Some people faded. Hazel *worked* at it, like scrubbing a floor.

So when the girl came to the kitchen door on the fourth night after the storm — baby bundled against her shoulder, looking left and right with all the subtlety of a cat burglar in a bell tower — Rosa simply stepped aside and said, "Well, come in before the whole harbor sees you hovering."

* * *

The baby — Tomas, they'd been calling him, for no reason either could defend except that he seemed like a Tomas — was asleep in the basket behind the stove where Rosa kept the rising dough, because nothing settles a child like the smell of warm bread, and nothing ruins a rising loaf like being moved about by strangers. He slept with one fist curled near his jaw, solemn as a magistrate.

"He ate," Rosa said. "Twice. He shouts like a gull but he swallows like a stevedore."

Hazel sat down slowly, the way people sit when they've been standing at attention for years and have forgotten chairs are for sitting in. She looked at the baby. She looked at the stove. She looked at Rosa with that flat, careful expression she wore like an apron, and said, "Mrs. Ferro. Why are you doing this?"

"Doing what? Feeding a child in my own kitchen?"

"You don't know him. You don't know me, not really. If Brandt finds out—" Hazel stopped, recalculated, started again from safer ground, the way she did. "You could lose your position."

"Girl." Rosa wiped her hands on her apron and considered how much truth a person could be handed before it got heavy, and decided Hazel Underhill had been carrying heavy things alone long enough that a little more weight might actually balance her. "Sit still. There's something you should see, and then we'll discuss whose position is whose."

She pulled the stool to the third shelf, reached past the great flour tin, and brought out a smaller box — cedarwood, lid worn smooth as driftwood, older than the warehouse itself by Rosa's guess. She set it on the table between them.

"What is it?"

"An archive," Rosa said, enjoying the word. She'd heard the clerks use it and had been waiting years for an occasion. "Though Brandt would call it rubbish, which is why he'll never find it. Open it."

Inside, on a bed of old flannel, lay four objects. A knuckle of coral, white as bone. A brass button stamped with a ship's anchor. A child's wooden bird, its paint gone but its wings still carved mid-flight. And a strip of woven cord, sea-green, frayed at both ends, with a single shell threaded through it.

Hazel went very still. Then her hand went out — not to the amulet she'd hidden from Brandt's notice four nights ago, the diver's scarred token tucked in a stranger's blanket — but to the cord. She lifted it like it might break, or bite.

"This is a diving cord."

"It is."

"Sea-green. That's the— my mother wore one like this. The harbor divers wear them. Wore them." Her voice had gone careful again, flattened, but her thumb was moving over the shell in small circles, entirely without her permission. Rosa watched the two halves of the girl disagree and said nothing yet.

"Twenty-two years I've cooked here," Rosa said. "In that time, three children have been left at doors of this company. Not always this door. Once the counting-house step, once the mission gate with instructions passed along. Each time, someone important tells whoever found them: forget the face. And each time—" she tapped the box "—I forget."

Hazel looked up. "Four things in there. Three children."

"The coral was mine first," Rosa said. "From my husband. But it seemed rude to let it sit apart from better company." She took it out, turned it in her fingers. Coral, water-worn. He'd been a fisherman; the sea had taken him too, in its ordinary indifferent way, no ledgers required. "The button belonged to a man off the *Cormorant*. His woman left the child at the mission and drowned herself within the year, or so they said, though I noticed the rolls said transferred, and I notice things. The wooden bird came with a little girl left on the counting-house step fourteen years back. And the cord—"

"Whose?" Hazel asked. Too fast. Then, quieter, bracing herself: "Whose cord?"

"A diver named Ester Vaile. Left a son, winter of 'eighty-nine. They told me he'd be taken to relatives up the coast." Rosa met her eyes. "He wasn't. He went to a family ashore in the next port, paid through an account I wasn't meant to see the name of, and I've never been able to learn which family. But I kept her cord, because Ester Vaile once saved my husband from a squall and I do not forget my debts, whatever else I'm told to forget."

Hazel had stopped breathing. Rosa had raised sons; she knew the look of a person holding their breath not for sport or discipline but because breathing might let something out.

"Ester Vaile," Hazel repeated.

"You knew her?"

"She—" The girl set the cord down very carefully, as though setting down something explosive, and pressed both hands flat on the table. "My mother dove with her. Before me. She used to say Ester Vaile could hold her breath longer than anyone in the harbor, and that she laughed underwater, which shouldn't be possible." A pause. "Mrs. Ferro, my mother was a diver. She drowned when I was seven. The company took me as payment against her debts and I've been invisible ever since, on purpose, because visible people disappear."

"And now you're keeping a baby whose amulet says his father was one of ours."

"One of the transferred," Hazel said, and the word came out with an edge on it, newly sharpened. "That's what the rolls say. Transferred. On the night the lighthouse went dark. Ivo found it. Eleven men, Mrs. Ferro, signed away as if they'd boarded a ship instead of sinking under one, and nobody has asked where they transferred *to* in nine years."

"Nobody who mattered," Rosa corrected gently. "Nobody who was allowed to ask." She closed the box, then — deliberately, so the girl could see — pushed it across the table until it sat in front of Hazel. "You've been thinking you were the only person in this building who remembers things she isn't supposed to."

The expression that crossed Hazel Underhill's face then was worth every year of overcooked mutton. It was not gratitude, quite, and not relief, and not even surprise. It was the look of a woman who has spent her life believing she was walking a tightrope alone over a black drop, and looks down and discovers a net — and cannot decide whether to laugh or be furious that no one mentioned it.

"There were others," Hazel said. "Other children. Other tokens."

"There will be others after Tomas, unless something changes. That's the arithmetic of this place. Important men produce inconvenient babies, and cooks produce archives." Rosa stood, because sentiment sits better in a working kitchen when your hands are moving, and began shaping tomorrow's loaves though it was nearly midnight and she had no intention whatsoever of sleeping. "You can't keep a child on invisibility, Hazel. Invisibility is for one. It doesn't stretch."

"I know." Said low, to the table. "I've been trying to work out how many people it would take."

"How many for what?" Rosa asked, though she suspected, having heard from the dock boys that Hazel and the pink clerk Ivo had lately developed an interest in old rolls, and knowing in her bones — the way a cook knows a stew is about to catch — that trouble was coming to a boil somewhere above them.

Hazel looked up, and for the first time in eleven years she did not look small. She looked like her mother, which was to say: like someone deciding.

"There's a ship coming," she said. "Next week. The magistrate's ship. Brandt's already talking about sealed crates for disposal abroad, and Ivo thinks the true ledgers go with them — the real death rolls, the evidence, burned somewhere no inquiry can reach. And there's a visiting colonial magistrate who takes testimony, if testimony can reach him before the tide does." She swallowed. "It can't reach him. Not by the rules. So it has to reach him some other way, and I don't have a way, I have a baby and a forged-papers clerk and a box of shells."

Rosa kneaded the dough. Turned it. Pressed it flat and folded it over, which is also how one builds a conspiracy.

"You have a kitchen," she said. "Kitchens, girl, are where everything happens. Nobody watches a kitchen. Sailors come through it, divers come through it, the magistrate's own steward will eat in it if he has any sense, because the hotel food in this town could kill a healthy goat." She dusted her hands and looked at Hazel straight. "And you have the divers. Your mother's people. Half the boats on this quay belong to folk who lost someone the night the lighthouse was dark, and every one of them has been told for nine years to forget. Do you imagine they've forgotten?"

Hazel's breath came out at last, shaky. "They might want to forget."

"They might want to *forget*," Rosa agreed cheerfully, "right after somebody finally asks them to remember. Go home, sleep if you can, and come back tomorrow. Bring the clerk. Anyone who keeps a second drawer of his own history is family as far as I'm concerned, whether he knows it yet or not."

Hazel gathered the basket, hesitated at the door with the wind pulling at her hem, and said, "Mrs. Ferro. Why tell me now? Why show me the box?"

"Because you asked me why I was doing this," Rosa said, "and it occurred to me I've been waiting twenty years for somebody to ask that question out loud instead of just wondering it quietly into their soup." She waved her off toward the dark. "Now go. And Hazel — mind the north corner by the salt shed on your way out. Brandt's taken to smoking there after midnight, and he sees nothing, but I'd rather you weren't the thing that teaches him otherwise."

The door closed. Rosa stood a moment in the warmth, listening to small feet hurry over cobbles, then lifted the cedar box, opened it, and added to it — carefully, with ceremony — the diver's scarred amulet Hazel had left in her keeping, beside the coral and the button and the bird and Ester Vaile's green cord.

Five tokens. Four children. One lighthouse, dark nine years, that she suspected was about to be lit again in a manner of speaking.

She put the box back behind the flour and went to wake properly, because in the morning she had letters to write in her careful cook's hand to certain boat-owning widows, and bread to rise, and — if the steward of a visiting magistrate had any sense at all — an invitation to extend.
