## 7. The Magistrate's Clerk

The magistrate's ship came in on the Tuesday tide, white-sided and smug, flying a pennant so clean it looked as though it had never met weather. Hazel watched it from the loading dock with a crate of shell in her arms and the particular stillness she had practiced for eleven years—the stillness of furniture, of fence posts, of things that see everything and are seen by no one.

*The Meridian Trading Company extends every courtesy to His Honor's office.* That was what the notice pinned to the warehouse board said. Hazel read it twice and understood what Brandt's kindness had been for. The crates marked *for disposal abroad* would sail with the magistrate's own ship. Nothing hid a thing like putting it in the shadow of the law itself.

She found Ivo in the records room, pretending to ink a bill of lading.

"The clerk," she said quietly. "The magistrate brought a clerk. Mr. Quill, the dock boys are calling him. He's lodging at the Anchor."

Ivo set down his pen with exaggerated care, the way he did when his hands wanted to shake and his pride wouldn't allow it. "Hazel. Let us consider, briefly, all the reasons this is a terrible idea."

"We've considered them."

"I don't think we have. I keep a list." He glanced at the door, then at her, and something in his face gave way—something that had been giving way for two weeks now, since the night he'd shown her the Cormorant rolls and the word *transferred* written where *drowned* should have been. "All right. But we do it carefully. Carefully is our whole religion, you and I. We were raised in its church."

"Which pew do you sit in?"

"The second drawer," said Ivo, and almost smiled.

* * *

From Ivo's point of view—and he had one, however rarely anyone asked after it—the plan had exactly one good quality: it was happening to him instead of merely near him. For years he had forged clean papers for the company, making theft look like trade and erasure look like paperwork, and each document had felt like a small stone swallowed. There is only so much paper a man can be before he wants to be a person again.

So on Thursday evening, in his second-best coat, with the copied pages sewn into the lining where a honest man might keep his heart, Ivo presented himself at the Anchor Inn and asked to see Mr. Quill.

The clerk received him in a small parlor that smelled of lamp oil and mutton. Quill was younger than Ivo expected—thirty perhaps, with ink-stained cuffs and the apologetic posture of a man who spent his life being mistaken for furniture by his betters. Ivo recognized the species immediately. He saw it in his mirror.

"Mr. Quill. Ivo Sarn, clerk to the Meridian Trading Company."

"Another one," said Quill mildly. "Sit down, Mr. Sarn. Would you take tea? It's terrible, but it's hot."

Ivo sat. "You've had other visitors?"

"A fisherman with views on harbor fees. A woman about a disputed goat." Quill poured the tea without spilling, which in Ivo's experience marked a man of real competence. "And now a company clerk. Goats and fees I can help with. What is it you have?"

For a moment the whole scheme balanced on the rim of a cheap teacup. Then Ivo said, "Irregularities in the death rolls. Eleven men of the Cormorant listed as transferred who never arrived anywhere. And an infant left at the warehouse door wearing an amulet belonging to one of them." He heard his own voice steady and was faintly proud of it. "His Honor's ship sails on the morning tide. So does a crate of documents the company intends to burn abroad."

Quill listened. That was the remarkable part—he actually listened, taking no notes, asking quiet questions in the right places: *the lighthouse? the insurance filings? the date of the note?* When Ivo finished, the clerk sat back and regarded him for a long moment.

"You understand," Quill said at last, "that if I report this conversation to Mr. Brandt, your position becomes untenable. Possibly fatal. Harbor towns are careless with clerks."

"I've considered it," said Ivo. "I keep a list."

Something moved behind Quill's tired eyes—not warmth exactly, but recognition. "Then come back tomorrow at eight," he said. "Bring whatever you can carry without being searched. His Honor reviews petitions at seven, and he reads faster than any man alive. If what you've told me is true, Mr. Sarn—" he paused, and set down his cup very precisely— "then it is precisely the sort of thing my office exists for. Good evening."

Walking back through the dark streets, Ivo allowed himself, for the first time in fifteen years, to imagine being a person on the record instead of a hand behind one. It was a dangerous feeling. He should have remembered that dangerous feelings are how the sea takes divers—not suddenly, but by making them forget to hold their breath.

* * *

Hazel knew something was wrong before she knew what it was. That was the diver's inheritance: you feel the change in pressure before the water tells you why.

It was Friday morning, and the warehouse was too quiet. Not empty-quiet—watched-quiet. Brandt stood at the foot of the stairs to the loft with two men she didn't know, company men from up the coast, and he wasn't shouting. That alone was enough to stop her heart. Brandt shouting meant you were safe; shouting was weather. It was the calm days you drowned on.

"Hazel Underhill." He said her name the way he weighed shell: noting the number, assessing the price. "You'll continue with the Kowloon manifest. Alone."

*Ivo,* she thought, with a coldness that felt almost like clarity. *They have Ivo.*

She worked the manifest with hands that did not shake, because shaking cost breath and breath was survival, while above her the loft floor creaked once, twice, and went silent. At noon Mrs. Ferro passed close with the bread trolley and murmured without moving her lips, in the flat professional voice of a woman discussing flour:

"They took him at dawn. Questioning. Irregularities in the rolls." A pause, the knife hovering over a loaf. "The baby's fed. Don't go near the kitchen."

That was all. It was enough. Hazel weighed shell until her arms ached and understood, with the patience of the drowning, exactly what had happened.

They had made themselves visible, and the machinery had turned.

* * *

They let her bring Ivo his supper, which was either mercy or a message—Ivo suspected both, in roughly equal measure. The loft room they'd put him in had a proper lock and a window painted over from the outside, and Brandt himself unlocked it, which was the message part.

"Company business, Miss Underhill," Brandt said pleasantly. "Nothing for warehouse staff. Ten minutes." And then, softer, with the gentleness that was worse than any blow: "Whatever he's been telling you—questions are how indentured people disappear. You know that better than most."

She did know it. She'd known it since she was seven years old and learned that her mother's name had gone into a ledger under a column labeled *loss, adjusted.*

Inside, Ivo sat on the cot looking less like a prisoner than like a man whose own handwriting had betrayed him. His collar was torn. One eye was going beautifully purple, and he touched it with something almost like admiration.

"They were very thorough," he said. "Not about me. About the *rolls.* They wanted to know who else had seen them. They asked whether I worked alone." He looked at her then, and his voice dropped to nearly nothing. "Hazel. I didn't tell them anything. But I want to say something, and I need you to hear it properly."

"You reported me," she said. It came out steadier than she felt. "Quill. He listened so politely. He took no notes, he asked all the right questions—he asked exactly the questions a man would ask if he needed to carry a full report to Brandt by morning."

"Yes," said Ivo. "He did ask well, didn't he. Too well." He leaned forward, wincing. "Or well enough that a competent clerk could not possibly have done otherwise. Hazel—a magistrate's clerk who hears 'eleven dead men erased from the rolls' and does *nothing* would be failing his office. Reporting up the chain is his function. The question is which chain." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I know how it looks. Believe me, I have lain on this cot rehearsing how it looks from every angle like a coin I can't stop biting. But listen to me. If Brandt believed I'd already given everything to the magistrate, I'd be in the harbor by now, not sitting here eating acceptable stew. They're holding me because they're not certain yet. They're waiting to see if anything reaches Quill's ship. Which means—"

"Which means nothing has reached Quill's ship," Hazel said. "Because everything we have is sewn into your coat. Which they took."

A silence. Somewhere below, the warehouse bell rang the hour, and beneath it, faint and indifferent, the harbor breathed against the pilings.

"They didn't search me at the Anchor," Ivo said slowly. "Quill wouldn't have allowed it, if he's what I hope he is. But Brandt's men—" He closed his eyes. "My coat is hanging in the records room closet, third hook, unless someone with a knife and good seamstress's eyes has found the lining. And even if it's untouched, I cannot deliver it. I am, at present, irregularity-in-chief."

Hazel stood. Through the painted-over window the last light was dying off the harbor, and out on the black water she could just make out the magistrate's white ship riding at anchor, patient as a promise.

The tide turned at dawn. The bell would ring, the crates would go aboard, and everything her mother had been, everything eleven men had been, would sail out of the world in a box marked *disposal.*

"How long," she said, "does it take them to decide you're innocent?"

"Days," said Ivo. "If they're generous."

"Then we don't have days." She was already moving to the door, already becoming in her mind the unremarkable woman with the supper tray, invisible, unwatched, unimportant—the most dangerous thing in the harbor. "Ivo. Tomorrow, when the tide turns, you're going to hear a commotion in the loft stairwell, and I need you to be surprised."

"That won't be difficult," said Ivo. "Hazel—what exactly are you going to do?"

What she was going to do, she did not say, because she was not entirely sure, and because saying things aloud made them real, and real things could be entered into ledgers. She only knew that somewhere below, in Mrs. Ferro's kitchen, a baby slept under a blanket with a diver's amulet tucked in its folds, and twenty years of small kept tokens waited in a flour tin, and a community of divers who had never once been watched because no one had ever thought them worth watching were, even now, being counted.

The tide would turn at dawn.

So, Hazel decided, would everything else.
