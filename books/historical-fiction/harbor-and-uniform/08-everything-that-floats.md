## 8. Everything That Floats

There is a particular quality to a night when you have decided to do something unforgivable to a powerful man: the lamps burn brighter, the tea tastes sharper, and every ordinary sound—the tick of the warehouse clock, the groan of the tide against the pilings—sounds like an accomplice.

I sat in Mrs. Ferro's kitchen with a sheet of paper before me, dividing my impossible night into columns, because Ivo had taught me that any catastrophe can be survived if you make it arithmetic first.

Column one: copy the true ledgers. Two hundred pages of the Cormorant's real rolls, the ones Brandt kept locked in his office because even he understood that paper burns better abroad than at home. Column two: free Ivo from the loft, where Brandt had shut him with a padlock and a plate of supper, which was somehow more insulting than chains. Column three: get everything—copies, testimony, the baby's amulet, the baby herself—aboard the magistrate's ship before the morning tide turned at 6:14. I knew it was 6:14 because Ivo had told me twice, and Ivo never tells you anything once unless he means for you to keep it.

"That's three impossibilities," said Mrs. Ferro, reading over my shoulder while kneading bread with forearms like mooring lines. "You've made them look very tidy, but they're still impossible."

"I know."

"Good. Impossible things go better with bread." She cut me a heel of the loaf, still steaming, and sat down across from me with the sigh of a woman who had been waiting twenty years for someone to bring trouble into her kitchen properly. "Tell me about the loft."

* * *

The loft above the counting room could be reached two ways: the stair past Brandt's office, which had eyes on it, and the roof hatch, which nobody watched because nobody remembered it existed. It had been built for hoisting sailcloth in the company's ambitious first decade, and then forgotten by everyone except the pigeons and, apparently, me.

I had gone up through it at dusk, flat against the shingles while the harbor lamps came on below, and lowered myself into the dark until Ivo's voice found me out of the blackness like a hand.

"You're going to get yourself voided," he whispered—which among indentured people is the darkest word there is, worse than beaten, worse than sold.

"Already happened," I whispered back. "I'm just catching up to myself."

He laughed, very quietly, and then pressed his face to the gap between hatch and frame so we were close enough to conspire. In the lamplight leaking under the door I could see him: rumpled, unshaven, looking less like a prisoner than a clerk who had finally been given honest work. "Brandt thinks I'll crack overnight," he said. "He's wrong, but not because I'm brave. Because I've spent nine years forging documents. I know exactly what happens to men who confess. I write their confessions."

"The key?"

"Hangs on his belt. But Hazel—" and here his voice dropped into the register he used for figures that would not balance, "—the padlock is nothing. The problem is the ship. The *Meridian Star* loads sealed crates tonight for 'disposal abroad.' Your ledgers are already crated. By dawn they'll be legal cargo, and once she clears territorial waters, no magistrate on earth can touch them."

"Then we don't touch the ledgers aboard. We take the copies here."

"There are no copies here."

"There will be by morning," I said, and felt something turn over in my chest—not fear exactly. Fear I knew the way I knew weather. This was the other thing, the thing my mother must have felt stepping off the boat ladder into dark water. "Two hundred pages. I can read fast and write faster. You trained me to check the shell tallies; I can copy a roll in my sleep."

"In the dark, with Brandt circling, in six hours."

"Yes."

He was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone of a man surrendering to his own handwriting: "The false-back drawer in his desk. Third ledger from the left. The true rolls are interleaved with shipping manifests—he hides truth inside tedium, it's the safest place in the building. And Hazel. If this goes wrong, you say you acted alone. You tell them I refused to help. Swear it."

"I swear nothing," I said, and went back down the way I'd come, unseen, unheard, unremarkable. Twenty years of being nobody, and it turned out nobody had been learning the building the whole time. Nobody knew which floorboards announced visitors. Nobody knew that Brandt took his brandy on the quay at eleven and his lamp to bed at midnight. No one had ever thought to watch the invisible, because watching us would have meant admitting we existed.

Invisibility, it turns out, is not the same as absence. It is presence, perfected.

* * *

Mrs. Ferro's kitchen became a war office by ten o'clock, though it retained the smell of bread, which I maintain improved our strategy.

She fed the baby—my arms ached from not holding her, so Ferro held her, tucked in a shawl against one hip while she stirred a pot with the other hand—and listened to the whole plan without interrupting except once, to say "the tide waits for no magistrate," which was fair.

Then she put down the spoon and looked at me the way she looked at a sauce that was almost right.

"You'll need boats," she said. "And you'll need divers."

"Divers?"

"What did I say, pearl merchants?" She shifted the baby, who had grabbed a fistful of shawl with the uncompromising grip of someone who has decided ownership is a matter of will. "Child, your mother's people are still on that dock. The Tanuseri families, the free divers, the widows of the *Cormorant*. They've been diving for Meridian twenty years and burying their own dead without papers for just as long. You think they don't know? You think they haven't counted their missing?" She set the baby in the bread basket—lined with floursack linen, and no, I was not going to argue with a woman of her authority—and untied her apron. "They've been waiting for somebody with a reason to be angry and access to the warehouse. That's you."

"It could ruin them."

"They're already ruined, dear. Now they can be ruined *usefully*."

* * *

The dockside at midnight was all lantern-smoke and hushed water, and I had not stood among the divers since I was small enough to be passed from arm to arm at the fire pits. They recognized me anyway. Of course they did—I had my mother's jaw and her way of standing still, and these were people whose trade was reading the surface of things for what moved beneath.

Old Sela Tanusi came forward first, gray-braided, hands scarred white in the pattern of decades of pressure. She looked at me a long time.

"Nara's girl," she said. Not a question. My mother's name, spoken aloud on the dock, hit me somewhere below the ribs and stayed there.

"I need boats," I said, because if I started anywhere else I would not start at all. "And alibis. And muscle, maybe, if the loft hatch sticks. Before the tide turns."

Sela didn't ask what for. That is the thing about people who have been robbed quietly for twenty years—they don't need the details, only the direction.

"How many boats can the magistrate's launch signal for?" she asked instead.

"One. Maybe two alongside before anyone official notices."

"Then we send one boat and forty swimmers' worth of witnesses on the quay." Her teeth showed white in the lantern light. "A woman carrying a baby toward a magistrate's ship at dawn, followed by every diver in the harbor—Brandt can't stop that without making the scene himself, and scenes are the one cargo he can't afford. We'll be your noise, girl. You be the quiet part."

An younger diver named Ossric, broad as a bollard, cracked his knuckles and volunteered for the hatch. A widow named Prem offered to stand on the customs steps from four o'clock onward having a very slow, very public argument about fish prices, directly in Brandt's line of sight between the warehouse and the quay. "I've been invisible on those steps thirty years," she said, with dignity. "It's time it did some work."

By two in the morning I had boats, alibis, muscle, witnesses, and a plan that had grown from three columns to a whole page of arithmetic. It was still impossible. But it was impossible *loudly*, now, and there is a kind of safety in that too—a thing too many people moving to fail quietly.

* * *

I copied the ledgers from half past midnight, by shielded candle in the shell-sorting room, with the true rolls spread before me like the opened ribs of a lie. Transfer dates. Signatures in Brandt's tidy hand. Thirty-one men of the *Cormorant*, entered as *transferred* on the night the lighthouse stood dark, their wages stopped the following week as though drowning were a kind of resignation.

I wrote names. I wrote each name twice—once into my copies, and once onto a single separate sheet, in letters large enough to read across a room. Sela Tanusi. Nara Underhill. And thirty others, ending with the name stitched into the amulet hidden against my breastbone: Kel Voss, father of a child too young to know she'd been forgotten on purpose.

Somewhere around page ninety, Mrs. Ferro appeared with tea and the news that the baby had slept, eaten, and bitten her, in that order, and was therefore thriving.

"She should have a name by the inquiry," Ferro said. "Magistrates dislike unnamed children. Makes them ask questions."

"Then she'll have one." I blew on the ink. "Kel's daughter. We'll give her his name to carry and ours to live in."

Ferro looked at me over the teacups, and for the first time since I'd known her, she looked briefly, enormously pleased—as though a sauce had finally come right.

At four o'clock I doused the candle. One hundred and ninety-three pages copied. Eighteen sheets of testimony signed by divers who had come up the back stair one and two at a time, pressing thumbprints into the margin beside their marks. Everything wrapped in oilcloth, everything weighted, everything ready.

At half past four, Prem took her fish to the customs steps. At five, Sela's people began gathering on the quay in ones and twos, mending nets that did not need mending. At five-thirty, the *Meridian Star*'s bell rang the first watch, and her crates—my mother's history, thirty-one lives folded into tedium—went up the gangplank in the rain-dark.

And at a quarter to six, standing in the shadow of the warehouse with the oilcloth bundle against my chest and the baby against my heart, I watched Brandt's lamp come down the quay toward me, and heard behind me, soft as breath, the loft hatch open overhead.

Ossric's whisper drifted down through the dark, mild as milk:

"He's coming. And Miss—your clerk says to tell you the tide's at 6:14, and he's *never* wrong about arithmetic."
