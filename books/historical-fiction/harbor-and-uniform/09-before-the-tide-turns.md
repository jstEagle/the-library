## 9. Before the Tide Turns

The lamp in the counting room had a bad wick. It guttered every fourth breath of flame, and each time it did, Hazel's heart stopped with it.

She sat at Ivo's own desk—his chair still held the shape of him, which was a strange and terrible comfort—and copied the Cormorant rolls onto the backs of shipping manifests. The true pages lay open before her: eleven names written in a clerk's careful hand, and beside each one, in Brandt's blunter ink, the word *transferred*, and dates that all fell within the same black week when the lighthouse had been dark and the sea had taken what the company would not admit to taking.

Eleven names. Eleven men who had gone into the water and come out as freight.

Hazel wrote them again and again, her letters small and even, because a shaking hand made poor evidence. *Tomas Reyes. Anselm Voss. Dario Lim.* She said each name silently as she wrote it, the way her mother had once taught her to count breaths underwater—one for the surface, one for courage, one more because you could.

Outside, boots on gravel. Slow. Circling.

Brandt did not sleep on nights before a sailing; he prowled. He had done it for years, she realized now—not from diligence but from appetite, walking his warehouse like a man counting coins he hadn't yet stolen. Tonight he was checking crates. Tomorrow at dawn, the *Providence* would carry those crates to Singapore, where paper burned easily and questions drowned quietly.

Hazel slid the true ledger beneath the manifests, pinched out the lamp, and stood very still behind the door as the footsteps passed. Through the gap she saw the glow of his lantern swing across the loft ladder—and stop.

He was looking up at the loft. At the hatch.

At the place where Ivo was locked.

"Tomorrow," Brandt said to no one, in the satisfied voice of a man addressing his own cleverness. "Tomorrow it's all someone else's problem."

His lantern moved on. Hazel counted to two hundred, the way you hold your breath, then relit the lamp and kept writing.

* * *

The loft hatch opened downward, which meant Hazel had to stand on a crate and push up against its weight while keeping absolutely silent—a problem she solved by pushing in increments, one finger-width per breath, like raising a stone off a diver's chest.

Ivo's face appeared in the gap, gray in the moonlight, one eye swollen from whatever questioning looked like when it wasn't polite.

"You're late," he whispered.

"I'm exactly on time," Hazel whispered back. "You're early."

"Is that humor? Now?" He hauled himself through the hatch and dropped to the floor with a gracelessness that made them both freeze. Somewhere below, a rat expressed an opinion and nothing else. "They took my second drawer, Hazel. Everything. My mother's papers."

"We'll get them back." She pressed the copied pages into his shirt, wrapped in oilcloth. "These go aboard first. You go first too—Mrs. Ferro's cousin has a skiff at the north pilings. He'll take you out to the *Providence* under the pretext of delivering fresh water."

"And you?"

"I'm the last cargo," Hazel said. "I have something Brandt will want to see."

"That is the single worst plan I've ever heard, and I forge documents for a living."

"It's the only plan that works. If we both vanish tonight, he burns everything by breakfast and calls it rats. But if I walk down to that gangplank carrying his ledgers—" she patted the satchel at her hip, heavy with the true Cormorant book and Mrs. Ferro's twenty years of tokens, each wrapped in cloth and labeled in the cook's round hand— "he has to come take them. In the open. In front of witnesses."

"What witnesses?"

"The magistrate's clerk," Hazel said. "Who, it turns out, has been waiting three days for us to be brave enough to talk to him properly. Ivo—he never reported us. He reported *us being reported*. He let Brandt think he'd won so Brandt would move the ledgers instead of destroying them here."

Ivo stared at her. Then he laughed, a small cracked sound, and pressed his hand briefly to her shoulder—the closest thing to an embrace either of them had risked in years.

"When this is over," he said, "I'm going to forge myself a whole new life. Clean papers. A name nobody's afraid of."

"Pick a good one," Hazel said. "Something unremarkable."

* * *

Mrs. Ferro met her at the kitchen door with the baby already bundled against the wind, and with flour on her hands and tears she refused to acknowledge.

"He'll want feeding before the tide," she said briskly, handing the child over. "There's bread in the basket and there's a knife under it, and if you use the knife before the bread I will haunt you personally."

"Yes, Mrs. Ferro."

"And Hazel." The old woman gripped her wrist, flour and all. Her voice dropped to the register she used for things that mattered. "Twenty years I've kept tokens in a tin. Little bits of people the company threw away. I always told myself someday someone would ask me for them." She looked at the satchel. "Someday is tonight. Don't you dare be quiet about it."

"I've never been good at quiet," Hazel said, which was a lie so enormous they both smiled at it.

The divers were waiting at the north pilings—her mother's people, or what remained of them, six men and women with rope-scarred hands and lamps hooded in canvas. Old Sef, who had taught Hazel to swim by throwing her off a barge, pressed a boat hook into her grip.

"Tide turns at the bell," he said. "After that, the current runs against you till noon. So whatever you're doing on that quay, girl—do it loud, and do it fast."

"Loud," Hazel repeated. All her life she had practiced the opposite. It felt like being asked to breathe water.

But Sef grinned, and it was her mother's grin, and suddenly it wasn't terrifying. It was inheritance.

* * *

The quay at four in the morning was all shadow and salt-smell, the *Providence* riding high and impatient at her moorings, deckhands moving crates under a lantern's sway. The ship's bell hung dark and patient above the gangplank, waiting for the hour.

Hazel walked toward it carrying a baby, a satchel of the dead men's names, and a heartbeat she could hear in her own ears like surf.

Brandt materialized from between the crates the way he always did—suddenly, entirely, blocking the world.

"Well," he said softly. "The ghost walks."

"Good morning, Mr. Brandt."

"You have company property in that bag."

"I have *evidence* in this bag." Her voice came out steadier than she'd budgeted for. "And you have eleven dead divers in your ledgers, Mr. Brandt, listed as transfers. And a lighthouse you darkened. And a note in your own hand ordering a face forgotten."

Around them, deckhands had stopped. Lanterns turned. This was the thing no one had ever understood about invisibility, Hazel thought, her terror transmuting mid-breath into something with edges: the invisible see *everything*, and no one ever thinks to stop their mouths.

Brandt stepped closer, low and venomous. "Give me the bag, girl, and I'll forget your face instead. Kindly. Like I've always been kind."

"You promoted me," Hazel said. "Three days ago. Better rations. Because I'd started asking questions. That's not kindness, Mr. Brandt. That's a leash. And I am done wearing it."

She lifted her chin toward the quarterdeck, where a figure in a clerk's sober coat had appeared at the rail—the magistrate's man, flanked by two marines whose presence was very much not an accident.

"On the record, then," Hazel said, and her voice rang across the water, across the decks, across twenty years of silence. "Tomas Reyes. Anselm Voss. Dario Lim. Mateo Cruz. Elias Brandt-no-relation, called Eel. Joaquim Ferreira. Samuel Oduya. Peter Halloran. The twins, Rafael and Rafaella Santos. And my mother—Maren Underhill, pearl-diver, who taught me to hold my breath, and who the Meridian Trading Company wrote out of the world and called it paperwork."

Each name landed like a stone dropped into deep water, ripples going out and out. Deckhands removed their caps. Someone on the *Providence* said *amen*, quietly, in Portuguese.

Brandt lunged for the satchel.

He got exactly one strap before the marines came down the gangplank, and then it was over almost embarrassingly fast—a man built for intimidating clerks discovering that the world contained other kinds of men. He was still shouting about property and slander as they turned him around, and Hazel stood holding the baby, breathing hard, watching the leash snap.

The clerk reached the foot of the gangplank and offered his arm with grave formality.

"Miss Underhill. The magistrate requests the pleasure of your testimony. Also," he added, glancing at the bundle in her other arm, "the ship's cook makes an excellent posset, and the cabin is warm."

Above them, the ship's bell began to ring the tide.

Hazel looked back once—at the quay, at Sef and the divers with their hooded lamps, at Mrs. Ferro's distant kitchen window glowing gold, at Ivo's skiff already pulling for the ship with a man aboard who was, for the first time in his life, traveling under his own true name.

Then she stepped aboard, and the current took them all toward morning.

* * *

The baby slept through the weigh-anchor, through the bar, through the open sea, waking only as the sun came up gilded on the water. Hazel fed her on the deck while the clerk took down her statement in a hand nearly as careful as Ivo's, and when the child finished she held her up to see the horizon—the same horizon her mother had read like scripture, weather and fortune written in light.

"There's land ahead," Hazel told her. "And on that land, some people are going to write down everything that happened. Every name. Even ours."

Especially theirs.

Because history, she was beginning to understand, didn't happen elsewhere after all. It happened wherever somebody finally spoke—and the tide, mercifully, had turned.
