## 1. The Letter in the Clock

The river had been singing under the ice for three days, a low groan that Beatrix could feel in the soles of her boots, and she had decided not to think about it. There was no time to think about it. There was only the atlas.

She worked by candlelight at Aldous Wren's long bench, the one scarred by forty years of burins and compass points, her breath ghosting silver over the copper plate. The workshop smelled of tallow and vinegar and the iron tang of etching acid, and beneath it all the cold — a cold that had gotten into the plaster walls and refused to leave, that made the ink stiffen in the well if she let it sit longer than a minute. She worked with a candle at each elbow and a warming stone under the inkwell, and she worked the way a drowning woman swims: because stopping was unthinkable.

Twelve plates finished. Four to go.

The atlas had been Wren's life for six years — *The Rivers and Roads of the Northern Counties, Being a True and Particular Survey* — and for the last two it had been hers as much as his. She had inked his lines, cut his lettering, ground his pigments. He had taught her that a map was a promise: that the world could be known, that knowledge could be held in two hands. *A mapmaker who lies*, he liked to say, *is worse than a murderer, because he kills the stranger who trusted him.* She had laughed at that once. She was not laughing now, with him eleven days in the churchyard and the frost coming through the floorboards like a debt collector.

The ninth of January he had gone out to check the flood-gauge stakes at Miller's Bend and had not come back. The coroner said the ice. Everyone said the ice. The ice was convenient; the ice asked no questions.

Beatrix set her burin down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. Grief, she was learning, was not a wave. It was a tide that went out and came back when you had finally knelt down to work.

She reached for the candle snuffer and, in reaching, knocked her elbow against the mantel clock.

It was an ugly thing, Wren's clock — walnut, squat, its face yellowed, its hands stopped at some hour nobody remembered setting. It had stood on the workshop mantel since before she arrived as a girl of fourteen with ink already on her fingers. Wren had wound it every Sunday with a small brass key he kept on his person, and he had never once let her do it, not in nine years. She had thought it was a quirk. Masters were full of quirks.

The clock tipped. She caught it, heart jumping — and felt something shift inside it that was not the weight of works and weights. Something slid, with the dry whisper of paper.

She set the clock down on the bench. She told herself she would only look, only satisfy the small cold curiosity, and then go back to the plate.

The back panel was held by four screws. Wren's tool chest was open at her elbow, as it had been every day since his death, and the smallest screwdriver fit as if the panel had been waiting. Inside, past the brass works, wedged against the case like a swallow's nest in a chimney, was a packet: oilcloth, wax-sealed, and beneath the wax a second seal she did not recognize — a black stamp of a bird with folded wings.

Her hands were suddenly very steady, in the way of hands that have decided to be afraid later.

The wax cracked. Inside was a single sheet, folded in quarters, written in a hand she knew better than her own — Wren's hand, but wrong: hurried, the downstrokes torn through, the ink blotted where he had pressed too hard and kept pressing.

*If you are reading this, I am dead, and it was not the ice.*

She read it twice. Then a third time, standing, because sitting had become impossible.

*The atlas must never be finished. Burn the final four plates. Do not engrave what I have drawn. I believed I was making a survey of rivers and I have made instead an account of sins. The Corvane family will pay any price and commit any crime to keep these waters unmapped, for my plates chart what they have spent thirty years and more lives than I can count keeping dark. If the atlas is completed, they will come for it, and for whoever holds it. Trust no officer of the guild; the guild's purse has Corvane fingers in it. Trust the river, which keeps what men cannot. I am sorry. I have made you the keeper of a thing I should have drowned at Miller's Bend myself.*

It was signed with his initials and a date: the first of January. Twelve days before he died.

Beatrix stood in the cold workshop with the letter in her hands and felt the words arrange themselves into a shape she refused to look at directly. *Paranoia*, she thought. *He was old, and proud, and he had begun to see enemies in his own survey lines.* Wren had been exacting, solitary, suspicious of the guild's new officers — she had heard him mutter about them for years. Old men wrote warnings. Old men died on thin ice. The two facts sat side by side and did not have to touch.

She folded the letter along its creases, put it in her bodice against her heart, and went back to the plate. That was the discipline he had taught her: the work first, always the work first, because the work was the only thing that could not lie.

She engraved for two hours before she could not bear it anymore.

The survey notes were kept in the flat drawer under the window — Wren's field books, forty years of them, his observations in pencil, fair-copied in ink. She had been transcribing the final volume's figures onto the last four plates: soundings of the river channels, the depths at Miller's Bend, the old weirs. She pulled the volume out and opened it to the pages she had been working from.

The figures were wrong.

Not misread — she knew his hand, knew his *4* from his *9*, knew the way his *7* crossed. The figures had been altered. Someone had gone through the final volume with a pen that was not Wren's — a paler ink, a heavier nib — and changed the soundings. Changed them all in the same direction: shallower. Every depth along the northern channel, the one the atlas was meant to chart for the first time in a hundred years, had been reduced. Made useless. Made *safe*.

She sat down on the stool because her legs had informed her they were finished.

Wren had fair-copied that volume himself. She had watched him do it, in this room, in October. She remembered the scratch of his nib. The alterations had come after — after the first of January, after the letter, after—

After the ice.

The river groaned beneath the floor, long and low, like something turning over in its sleep.

Beatrix looked at the four unfinished plates laid out on the bench, and for the first time she actually looked at them. Not as work. As *what they were*. The northern channel, drawn true. The old weirs at Corvane's Reach — she had always thought the name a coincidence, the way one thinks a scar is a birthmark. The landing stages marked *disused* in Wren's hand, with small penciled annotations she had taken for antiquarian fussiness: *stone too new. Iron rings, salt-rusted, replaced yearly. Traffic at night, lanterns hooded.*

A survey of rivers. An account of sins.

She got up, took the candle, and went to the drawer where Wren kept his journal — the private one, the one he had never forbidden her to read, which had been its own kind of permission. It was not in the drawer. It was not in the chest. She searched the bench, the press, the paper cabinet, the cold grate where nothing had burned but tallow ends.

The journal was gone. The survey volume lay open on the bench behind her, its pale false figures shining in the candlelight, and the river sang its one long note under the ice, and Beatrix Thornbury stood alone in her dead master's workshop holding a candle against the dark and understood, at last, that the ice had not been the thing that killed him — and that whoever had altered those figures knew exactly what the atlas contained, and would be back to make certain it was never finished.

* * *

She did not sleep. She banked the fire, moved the letter from her bodice to the hollow under the floorboard where Wren had hidden his best vellum, and sat at the bench with the four plates before her and the altered survey open beside them.

Burn them, the letter said. She thought of the guild officers at the funeral, their black coats and their soft condolences, and how the youngest of them had asked, twice, when the atlas would be ready for the guild's inspection. She thought of the Corvane arms over the customs house, the folded-winged bird — the same bird pressed into the wax of a letter hidden inside a clock.

*They will come for it, and for whoever holds it.*

Outside, the ice groaned. Inside, Beatrix picked up her burin, set its point against the copper of the fifteenth plate — the northern channel, the true soundings, the hooded lanterns of Corvane's Reach — and began to cut.

The line went in clean and deep. It was the first line of the rest of her life, and she already knew it would cost her everything.
