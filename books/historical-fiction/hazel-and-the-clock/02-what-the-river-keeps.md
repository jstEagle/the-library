## 2. What the River Keeps

The letter had three instructions, and Beatrix read them until the candle guttered and she had to trim the wick with her thumbnail.

*Follow the river to where it bends twice. Count what is not drawn. Trust no man who offers you gold before he offers you his name.*

She set the paper down on Wren's drafting table, beside the half-finished plate of the estuary, and told herself what she'd been telling herself since midnight: her master had grown strange in his last year. Suspicious. He'd double-locked the door against imagined thieves. He'd started burning his correspondence. Old men who lived alone with their instruments sometimes went crooked in the head, like a saw left out in the damp.

Except that Aldous Wren had not lived alone. He had lived with her, six days in seven, for four years. And he had never once been wrong about a measurement.

She pulled his survey notes toward her — the field books from the autumn surveys, the ones she was transcribing into the atlas's final plates. The handwriting was his, unmistakable: the cramped, forward-leaning script of a man perpetually racing his own patience. But something itched at her as she compared the notes to the letter's first instruction.

*Where the river bends twice.*

The estuary plate showed the bend at Marlow's Reach and the long sweep past Corvane Wharf. Two bends. She counted what was *not* drawn, and found nothing, because there was nothing to find — the wharf was rendered in clean commercial detail, warehouses and cranes and the tide gauge, exactly as any respectable atlas would show it.

Exactly as any respectable atlas would show it.

Beatrix sat very still. Then she opened the drawer where Wren kept his field instruments and took out the loupe, the heavy brass one he used for checking engraver's cuts, and held it over the wharf section of the copper plate itself.

There. Faint as breath on glass — so faint she understood why she'd missed it, why anyone would miss it — were lines beneath lines. A second channel, scribed shallow and then burnished nearly flat, running behind the warehouses where no charted channel should be. It passed through what the surface drawing called solid ground. It connected the wharf's inner basin to a stretch of marsh that every other map in the kingdom marked as impassable mud.

Wren hadn't tampered with his notes. He had hidden the truth inside them, the way he'd hidden the letter inside the clock — because he had known, in the end, that anything visible could be stolen or burned or used to hang him.

Her hands were cold. She noticed this distantly, the way you notice weather.

*Count what is not drawn.* She turned back to the field books and began reading them properly, not as transcription fodder but as testimony. And there it was, page after page, in the margins she'd taken for grumbling: soundings recorded at hours when no honest surveyor worked. Depth measurements of the uncharted channel, taken by moonlight. Tide tables annotated with times of year when the marsh passage ran deep enough for a loaded hull. And once, in the margin of October the fourteenth, in letters pressed so hard the nib had torn the paper:

*They moved them at slack water. Eleven souls. I counted the lanterns.*

Eleven souls.

Beatrix closed the book. The workshop was silent except for the tick of the mantel clock, which she had not yet dared to wind again, and the small sounds of the river under the ice, which had begun lately to make noises like something turning over in its sleep.

She thought about the night Wren died. The fall from the survey boat at Marlow's Reach; the coroner's verdict of misadventure; the guild's tidy condolences. She thought about how careful a man he was on water — how he'd taught her that a surveyor's first duty was to come home, because dead men finish no maps.

He had counted eleven lanterns. And then he had died in the exact place where the two channels met.

* * *

She did not sleep. Instead she climbed to the loft where Wren kept his old journals, forty years of them in oilcloth bundles, and she carried down the volume from 1731 — the year, according to the guild registry, that Corvane Shipping had built its wharf.

The candlelight made the ink look fresh, as if the words were still wet, still warm from his hand.

*12th June. Corvane's new warehouse has no road to it worth the name and no need of one. I surveyed their approaches for the commission and found they have dug a private cut through the marsh, deep enough at spring tides for a brig of eighty tons. They paid me well to map it and better to forget it.*

*19th June. Asked Halloran at the guild whether such a cut ought to appear in the public charts. He laughed at me. Said the Corvanes are generous friends to the guild and I should be generous back. I said a map that lies is not a map. He stopped laughing.*

*3rd July. A man came to the shop. Did not give his name. Offered me a yearly sum larger than my best commission to "retire" the survey. I declined. He thanked me courteously and looked at my hands the whole time, as though pricing them.*

*30th July. Found someone has been in the shop. Nothing taken. Everything touched.*

*14th August. I am not a brave man. I have decided to be a patient one. If I cannot publish the truth, I will keep it alive until there is a world that can bear it. The atlas will carry the channel — cut fine, buried deep, waiting. Let some future hand find it. Perhaps an apprentice. Perhaps a flood.*

Beatrix sat in the cold loft with the journal open on her knees and wept without noise, the way he had taught her to work — precisely, without waste. Not for the danger. For the thirty years of him in these pages, the patient stubborn decency of a man who chose to bury truth rather than surrender it, and who had waited all that time for a hand to find it, and whose hand she now was.

When the tears were done she felt strangely lighter, and colder, and quite certain of two things: Aldous Wren had been murdered. And she was going to finish his atlas.

* * *

The knock came at midmorning, which meant the visitor had crossed town at the hour when respectable people were at church, and had chosen the one day she might plausibly be too exhausted to think clearly.

He was silver-haired and beautifully gloved, and he stood in the doorway of the workshop breathing in the smell of ink and copper shavings with the appreciation of a connoisseur.

"Mistress Thornbury." He offered a bow calibrated to the inch. "My name is Quill. I represent certain parties with an interest in this establishment."

"What parties?"

"The kind," he said pleasantly, "who prefer to remain interested rather than identified." He drifted along the racks of plates, fingertips laced behind his back, touching nothing. "A tragedy, Master Wren's accident. The guild has been remiss in its duties to you, I think. An apprentice, alone, with debts and a lease coming due. It is a great deal of weight for one young woman."

"I manage."

"I'm sure. But managing is exhausting." He turned and smiled at her, and his eyes were pleasant and entirely empty, like the eyes of the man in the 1731 journal who had priced Wren's hands. "My principals will take the workshop off your shoulders. Building, contents, plates, papers — everything, settled today, at three times its value. You could be gone by nightfall. Somewhere the winters are kinder."

Beatrix kept her face still, the way Wren had taught her to hold a line: steady pressure, no tremor. Behind Quill, on the wall, hung the estuary proof — the innocent version, the lie with the truth sleeping underneath it.

"Three times its value," she repeated.

"Cash. This afternoon."

"And if I decline?"

Quill's smile did not change at all, which was itself an answer. "Then the offer remains open. My principals are patient people. Though I would counsel you, Mistress Thornbury, that rivers in this district have a way of rising, and property near the water can be — perishable." He set a card on the drafting table, weighted it with a coin, and bowed again. "Until the ice breaks, then."

The door closed behind him. Beatrix stood listening to his footsteps fade toward the wharf, and then she picked up the coin he'd left and turned it over. It was gold. A sovereign, new-minted, and stamped on its face, small and crisp as a signature: the shipping arms of House Corvane.

So. Not paranoia. Not grief talking. The name in the letter, the channel under the wharf, the eleven lanterns, the courteous man pricing her hands — it all locked together like the teeth of a gear.

They had killed him for a map. They thought the map had died with him.

Beatrix put the sovereign in the drawer with the letter, walked to the window, and watched the river ice groan and shift in the distance, white all the way to the sea, holding its burden only until spring.

Then she rolled up her sleeves, lit every lamp in the workshop, and reached for the burin.

If the Corvanes wanted the atlas unfinished, she had perhaps six weeks before the ice broke — and she would need help, because no apprentice alive could cut forty miles of hidden channel alone. She knew exactly one engraver the guild had thrown away, and exactly where to find him drowning his reputation in the Anchor and Chain.

She blew out the candles she didn't need, left one burning over the buried channel, and went out into the cold to fetch Tomas Vane.
