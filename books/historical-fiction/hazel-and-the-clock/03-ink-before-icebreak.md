## 3. Ink Before Icebreak

The Anchor and Chain smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular despair of men who had once been somebody. Beatrix had never been inside a tavern before. She stood in the doorway with her hood dripping snowmelt and let her eyes adjust until she found him at the far end of the room, hunched over a tankard like it owed him money.

Tomas Vane had been the guild's finest engraver once. She remembered the stories Wren told: how Vane could cut a hair's breadth of coastline so fine it looked drawn rather than carved, how he'd been expelled for falsifying a chart — or so the guild claimed — though Wren had always muttered that Vane had merely refused to falsify one *for* them.

"Tomas Vane," she said, sitting down across from him without invitation.

He looked up slowly. He was younger than his reputation suggested — mid-thirties, maybe, with ink stains gone permanent in the creases of his knuckles and eyes the color of river fog.

"You're Wren's girl." His voice was rough from drink or disuse. "They say you're still rattling around that workshop like a ghost that won't accept it's dead."

"They say a lot of things." She set the sealed letter on the table between them. "I need an engraver."

"I don't engrave anymore."

"You're drinking instead. I've seen what drinking accomplishes. It accomplishes less than engraving."

Someone down the bar laughed. Tomas didn't. He looked at the letter, then at her face, and something behind the fog shifted.

"What is it?"

"A murder weapon," she said. "Or it will be, if I finish it. The Corvanes want the atlas buried. They killed Aldous Wren to bury it. And I'm going to finish it anyway and print it under both our names, and I need someone who can cut forty miles of hidden channel into copper before the ice breaks."

Tomas stared at her for a long moment. Then he picked up the letter, read it twice, and set it down very carefully, as if it might go off.

"You understand what you're asking," he said quietly. "The Corvanes don't just ruin people, girl. They unmake them. I know. I was unmade by lesser men than they."

"Then you have nothing left to lose," said Beatrix. "I do. And I'm spending it anyway."

* * *

He came to the workshop the next morning, sober and sullen, carrying his tools in a leather roll worn soft as cloth. For three days he barely spoke, studying Wren's survey notes, the tampered soundings, the buried channel that wound through the marshes like a snake through grass. On the fourth day he sat down at the engraving bench, tested the burin against his thumb, and began to cut.

Beatrix watched him work and understood, for the first time, why Wren had mourned him. The burins moved in Tomas's hands like they were remembering something. Coastlines bloomed from the copper. The hidden channel emerged stroke by stroke — every depth sounding, every tide gate, every warehouse landing where contraband and people had passed through Corvane hands in the dark.

"We cut duplicates," Tomas said on the sixth night, not looking up from the plate. "Same work twice. One set hides, one set prints. If they burn the workshop, they get ash and we get the atlas anyway."

"Where would we hide them?"

"The guild vaults would be best." He said it flatly, knowing the answer before he asked.

"They won't take them," Beatrix said. "I'm not guild anymore. Not really. An apprentice without a master is nobody. The charter dies with Aldous unless I complete the atlas and petition for admission on the strength of the work itself."

Tomas set down his burin. "Then we hide them somewhere else. The crypt at Saint Aldhelm's. A cellar. Anywhere."

"There's a better place." She thought of the sluice tunnels beneath the workshop, the old flood channels Wren had maintained against the river's temper. "Underground, dry, and known only to mapmakers. Aldous showed me the first week I arrived. He said, *if ever the work must survive the building, put it below.*"

"He said that?" Something moved across Tomas's face — grief, perhaps, arriving late. "Then he knew. All along, he knew what was coming for him."

They worked by day and hid plates by night, wrapping copper in oilcloth and lowering it into the stone dark of the sluice tunnel, where the river muttered overhead like a sleeping animal. Beatrix slept four hours a night. She ate standing up. Her hands cracked from cold acid and she wrapped them in strips of linen and kept cutting, because the atlas was the only testimony Aldous Wren would ever give, and she would not let it die with him.

* * *

The first threat came nailed to the door: a dead crow, its feathers scorched, arranged above a single printed line — *Finish nothing.*

The second came as a man in a fine coat who waited at the corner for two hours in the rain, simply to be seen waiting, so that she would know she was watched.

The third came as fire.

It was small, as fires went — a bundle of oiled rags thrust through the coal hatch, smoldering in the storeroom among the blank copper sheets. Beatrix woke at the smell and drowned it with the wash bucket, coughing, hands blistered anew, while smoke curled up through the floorboards toward the finished plates.

Tomas arrived at dawn to find her sweeping char out the door, gray-faced and shaking but upright.

"That's not a warning anymore," he said. "That's a schedule. They'll come back with more than rags next time, Beatrix. They'll come back with men."

"Then we work faster."

"No." He took her by the shoulders, and his grip was gentle and desperate at once. "Listen to me. I've been unmade before, remember. I know how this ends. We take the plates tonight — all of them — and we go. North, over the border, anywhere the Corvane name doesn't reach. You can print there. Live there. Be alive there. That's worth more than a name on a title page."

"And Aldous?" Her voice came out low and strange. "Is he worth less? He's in that channel, Tomas. Every sounding he took, every mile he mapped in the dark at risk of exactly what found him anyway. If we run, the atlas dies, and he died for ash."

"If you stay, *you* die for ash."

"Maybe." She pulled free of his hands, gently. "But here is the thing about being legally nothing, Tomas. Nothing cannot be threatened with ruin. Nothing has no apprenticeship to revoke, no license to strip, no name to blacken. The Corvanes can only take from me the things I refuse to give them. And I refuse."

He looked at her for a long time — at the blistered hands, the ink-stained apron, the soot still ghosting her jaw. Whatever he saw, it settled something in him. He sighed like a man accepting a sentence, picked up his burin, and walked back to the bench.

"Fine," he said. "Fine. But if we die, I'm haunting you specifically."

* * *

The guild's letter arrived two days later, delivered by a boy who wouldn't come past the doorstep.

Beatrix read it standing in the doorway, letting the cold wind flatten it against her hands. *The Guild of Cartographers and Engravers, having reviewed the circumstances of the late Master Wren's death and finding no registered successor of suitable standing, hereby dissolves the Wren apprenticeship and declares its works and premises subject to guild assessment.* Subject to assessment. Meaning: theirs to claim, hers to be removed from, legally and completely, at their leisure.

"They didn't even wait," she said. "Aldous isn't cold yet."

"The Corvanes bought them weeks ago, I'd wager." Tomas read over her shoulder and spat into the gutter. "So now you're nobody in the eyes of the law. Anything you print is counterfeit. Anyone who helps you shares your crime."

"Then thank you for sharing it." She folded the letter into quarters, then eighths, then small enough to feed to the stove, and did exactly that. "We have perhaps three weeks before the ice breaks. The final plate is half done. When it's finished, we print twelve copies in one night, distribute nine to safe hands, and let the truth loose before anyone can stop it."

"Twelve copies in one night requires a press, paper, ink, and a miracle."

"There's a press in the cellar my master never told the guild about. Paper in the attic. Ink we have. As for the miracle—" She smiled, and it felt unfamiliar on her face, sharp-edged, like a new-cut plate. "I've been saving one."

Through the window, down beyond the rooftops, the river lay pale and patient in the winter light, its ice thickening by the hour, holding back everything it intended to give back.

And on the far bank, small with distance but unmistakable, riders in fine dark coats were gathered at the water's edge, looking across at the workshop district — measuring, she thought, the width of the river, the depth of their patience, and how far a fire might jump when the wind came off the breaking ice.
