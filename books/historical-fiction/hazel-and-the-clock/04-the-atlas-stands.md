## 4. The Atlas Stands

The river spoke first.

I had been listening to it for three nights—the groan of ice shifting in its sleep, the low knock of water testing the underside like a prisoner tapping a wall. On the fourth night it stopped speaking altogether, and that silence was worse. That was the silence of something gathering itself.

Tomas heard it too. He set down his burin and stood with his head cocked, a man listening to a door he hoped would never open.

"Tonight," he said.

"Maybe tomorrow."

"Beatrix." He crossed the workshop to the window, where frost ferned the glass and the lamplight caught the gray in his stubble. Below, Water Lane lay black and empty, the snow already dirty where the carts had churned it. "The ice goes tonight. And if I were a Corvane man, I'd want my fire to ride the flood—let the river carry the blame."

He was right. I had known he was right since morning, when the boy from the pie shop—a child of perhaps ten, paid in nothing but fear—had run past twice, staring up at our windows. Since the guild's final letter arrived, notarized and smelling of sealing wax: *the apprenticeship of Beatrix Thornbury is dissolved, all instruments and plates held by her to be surrendered to the Guildhall within seven days.* They had made me legally no one. A woman with no master, no guild, no name in any register worth burning. If I died in a fire on Water Lane, there would be no inquiry. There would be scarcely an obituary.

So we had spent the day preparing for two things at once: finishing, and surviving.

The last plate—Plate XVII, the one Wren had hidden inside the atlas like a knife sewn into a coat lining—had gone to the press at dusk. Tomas pulled twelve copies before the ink was dry enough to smudge. Twelve copies of the smugglers' route: the coves, the false channels, the drowned stairs where the Corvanes had moved their contraband and their people for twenty years, each mark drawn in Wren's own hand and annotated in mine. Twelve copies, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the false bottoms of two sea chests. The copper plates themselves went into a third chest, packed in wool.

And beneath the floorboards of the workshop, under the very spot where Aldous Wren had bled out on a night the coroner called *a fall upon the stair*, we left the thing they wanted burned: a single finished proof of Plate XVII, weighted under the anvil, dusted with lampblack so it would catch.

Let them come for that. Let them be certain.

* * *

They came at half past eleven.

I knew before Tomas said anything, because the dog in the tannery yard three doors down began to scream—not bark, scream—and then went silent in a way that turned my stomach. Then footsteps. Not the scattered stagger of drunk men coming from the Anchor. Boots. Many boots, moving in step, which is the sound soldiers make, or thieves who have drilled.

We put out the lamps. Through the shutter slats I counted them: eight men, two carrying lanterns shuttered dark, one with a keg on his shoulders. At their head walked a slight figure in a good coat, and when he paused beneath our sign—*WREN, MAP- & CHART-MAKER*, the gilt flaking—I recognized the set of his shoulders. Mr. Quill, who had come in autumn with a generous offer and a smile like a drawer opening. He had raised the offer twice since. Tonight he had stopped negotiating.

"The back," Tomas breathed. "They'll post a man at the back."

"Let him."

The front door was locked; it did not remain locked long. The crack of the frame splintering rolled through the workshop like a shot, and then they were inside, lanterns blooming, and Quill's voice, pleasant as ever: "Mistress Thornbury? We've come about the atlas."

We were not there. We were up the outside stair in the freezing dark, on the walkway above the sluice house—the squat stone annex that controlled the flood gates serving the lower district's drainage cuts. Wren had held the lease on it forty years; it was written into papers Quill had never thought to ask about, because mapmakers' paperwork bores the men who buy maps. Every channel, every cut, every culvert in this district existed because Wren had surveyed it, and the sluice mechanism answered to a crank and a pin, and the pin was rusted but oiled, because for three nights Tomas and I had taken turns oiling it.

Below us, through the floor grate, I watched lanternlight swarm my life. Heard them find the proof under the anvil. Heard Quill say, softly, satisfied, "There. Burn it all. The fire will do the rest—the river's breaking, by morning this whole lane will be mud and ash and nobody will look twice."

The keg went against the stairwell. Oil spread across the floorboards I had scrubbed every Saturday for six years. A lantern came down in a man's hand, almost gentle, the way you'd lower a candle onto a grave.

I put both hands on the crank.

"Beatrix." Tomas's voice was very quiet. "The cuts will take the whole low district. The tannery. The pie shop. Everything below the high street drowns tonight that wouldn't have until dawn."

"Everything below the high street burns tonight instead," I said. "Water or fire. Choose."

He put his hands over mine. "I didn't say don't."

Between us we threw the pin.

The crank turned hard, then all at easy, and somewhere beneath the stones something vast let go its breath. We heard the flood gates rise. We heard the river arrive.

It did not roar, as I had always imagined floods roaring. It came *in*, a wide black muscle sliding up the drainage cuts all at once, and the first thing it took was the oil. The spreading fire died with a hiss like a snake stepped on. The second thing it took was the keg, which floated up off the floor and struck the doorframe and burst, and the fumes went up blue and wicked across the water and caught anyway—but on the water, moving, carried away down the lane in a sheet of flame that rode the flood like a lit sail, away from the workshop and out toward the basin.

The men screamed. Men who had drilled in step now scrambled in every direction at once, and the river, which does not care whose wages it is on, took the legs from under two of them and slid them down the cut. Quill stood on the rising doorstep with his fine coat hemmed in black water, and I looked down at him through the grate, and he looked up—he could not help looking up at the sound of us—and I called down to him through the din:

"You should have read the lease, Mr. Quill! You should have read *everything!*"

I do not know if he heard me. The river chose that moment to take the lower stair, and after that there was only water and shouting and the strange bright sailing of the fire, and Tomas hauling me back from the grate by the collar as a swell came up through the boards.

* * *

The high street held. That was the arithmetic I lived with afterward, awake at three in the morning in the loft of the printing house of one Ambrose Fell, who owed Aldous Wren a debt he named without being asked: everything below the high street was lost, and everyone on the high street was saved, and whether the flood would have reached the eaves by dawn without my gates is a question I have stopped asking myself, because I know the answer, and the answer is *no*.

Twelve copies survived. So did the plates, dry in their wool. And on the second morning after the flood, while the district still steamed and the Corvane men were being pulled from the mud—one alive, one not—we nailed the notice to the Guildhall door ourselves, because the Guildhall had revoked me and therefore had no power to stop me.

*THE ATLAS OF THE RIVER AND HER ROADS. Surveyed by Aldous Wren. Completed and published by Beatrix Thornbury, his apprentice. Containing seventeen plates, including the true chart of the eastern channels. Price: one shilling. To be sold at the sign of the Wren, and nowhere else.*

Fell printed fifty more that week. Then five hundred.

The atlas sold because the flood had made the river the only subject in England. Every magistrate, every merchant, every insurance clerk with a ledger full of drowned warehouses needed charts, and ours were the only ones drawn by a man who had known every stone in the current. And in every copy, Plate XVII sat waiting like a landmine under tissue paper: the cove behind Corvane House. The false channel to the cellars. The drowned stair. The route, marked in dates and tide-tables in Wren's cramped hand, that matched—to the very week—the ledgers a customs officer happened to buy in the third week of January.

The Corvanes had spent twenty years being invisible along that river. I made them legible.

By spring the eldest brother had fled to the continent ahead of a writ. By summer, Quill, fished half-drowned off his own doorstep, had traded testimony for his neck, and what he testified filled four hundred pages of transcript that read, in places, like my atlas with dialogue.

* * *

The guild sent a delegation in March. Polite men, this time, with a petition rather than a summons: the mastership of Wren's vacant chair was open, the charter allowed for special admission in cases of "exceptional service to the art," and would Mistress Thornbury consider—

"No," I said, and enjoyed the way the word landed.

I kept the guild's letters. I keep all correspondence now; it files well.

The workshop dried slowly. The lower stair was gone; we built a new one, wider. Tomas re-cut the damaged plates himself, and his initials now sit beside mine in the corner margin of every chart we print, small and unhidden, because we are done with hiding. In June we hung a new sign, and I stood in the street to watch the men raise it, my arms crossed against the cold that even June keeps here by the water.

*WREN & THORNBURY, MAP- & CHART-MAKERS.*

Not quite the old sign. Better than the old sign.

On midsummer's eve I went up to the flat roof with the last light, where Wren used to take his evening pipe, and looked east along the river to where the drowned district was rebuilding itself plank by plank. Somewhere under that new timber lay the old floorboards and the scorch shadow where the anvil had stood, and somewhere under the river lay whatever else the Corvanes had sunk over twenty years to keep it invisible.

Because that was the thing about Plate XVII. However many times I proofed it, however carefully I compared it against Wren's survey notes, one stretch of the eastern channel refused to resolve—a mile of marsh and tidal flats where his depth-soundings simply stopped, mid-line, as if the pen had been lifted by someone else's hand. The customs transcripts mentioned it once, in Quill's deposition, in a single sentence he clearly regretted: *the route ran further than the chart showed.*

Further than the chart showed. Further than even Wren had been permitted to finish.

Tonight the tide was going out, and far off across the flats I could see the mud beginning to bare itself, dark and glistening, giving up for one hour whatever it had spent a year keeping. And just at the edge of the ebb, where the chart ended and the blank paper began, something pale broke the surface of the mud—a straight line of it, regular as a drafted rule, running out toward deep water.

As if someone, long ago, had laid a road across the marsh.

And meant to drown it again.
