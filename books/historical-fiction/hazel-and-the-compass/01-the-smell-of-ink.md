## 1. The Smell of Ink

The shop woke before Petra did, the way it always had, the way it still did even now that her father was eleven weeks in the churchyard and could no longer be the one to wake it. She heard it in the dark before dawn: the small settling sounds of a house built around its trade. The cooling press ticking as its iron contracted. The damp morning air seeping through the shutters and finding the paper stock, which drank at it greedily, swelling by a hair's breadth she alone would notice when she pulled a sheet for inspection. The type cases on their long table, holding their thousands of little leaden citizens in perfect order, waiting.

She rose, dressed in the dark, and went down the narrow stair with her hand on the wall because she knew every seam of the plaster by touch. In the workshop she lit the lamp over the composing stone, and the flame caught the smell of the place all at once — ink and oil, the vinegar tang of the etching bath, the sweetish rot of old paste, and under everything the particular metallic breath of lead type, which her father had once told her was the smell of the future. *Every idea that wants to outlive its speaker must put on this coat,* he used to say, holding a sort up to the light between finger and thumb. *Lead is how a thought learns to live without you.*

He had been dead since the first week of October, taken by the fever that had come up the river valley with the autumn fogs, and Petra had learned in those eleven weeks that grief does not stop the work; it only changes what the work means. Before, the shop had been his voice and hers answered it. Now it was hers alone, and every morning when she opened the shutters and let the grey Rhineland light fall across the composing stone, she performed a small act of faith: that the letters would still be there, that her hands would still know them, that the town of Elsterbrück would still need its playbills and its price lists and its funeral notices, and that no one — not the guild, not the magistrate, not the three cousins who believed a print shop ought to belong to a man — would think to take it from her.

So far, no one had. But she kept her head down anyway. That was the discipline her father had drilled into her harder than any craft lesson: *Petra, the printer who is noticed is the printer who is closed.* He had said it lightly, usually while wiping ink from his fingers, but she had come to understand it as theology.

She began, as she began every morning, with the type itself.

There were men in Elsterbrück who thought a woman could set a page but could not cut a letter, and Petra had long ago stopped arguing with them, because the type argued better than she ever could. Her punches hung on the board above the bench in their ordered rows — a full alphabet of steel, each one cut by her own hand over six years of evenings, each one bearing the Voss face's particular character: a slightly shortened ascender, a serif like a small firm footstep. To make a single punch she would take a bar of soft steel and file it square, then cut the letter backward into its face with gravers finer than needles, checking it against smoke proofs until the impression was true, then hardening it in the fire and quenching it, and driving it into copper to strike the matrix, and casting from the matrix in the mould her father had built, and if any stage failed she began again without complaint, because complaint was also a kind of noise, and noise drew notice.

This morning she was cutting a new lowercase *g*, because the old one had developed a flaw — a hairline crack near the shoulder that showed in every impression as a ghost-thread, faint as a spider's silk, and she had found it in Tuesday's run of hymnal covers and had spent an hour with the loupe finding its source. She liked this work best of all: the hour before the town woke, the lamp turned low, the graver whispering through steel. It required a patience so complete that it emptied her mind of everything else, including, for a little while, the fresh-turned earth in the churchyard and the way Anton had wept at the graveside and then stopped weeping all at once, as though a door had been shut inside him.

She was fitting the new punch to its handle when she heard him on the stairs.

Anton came down the way he came down everywhere, at speed, already talking. He was twenty-three to her twenty-six, tall where she was compact, with their father's dark hair and none of their father's caution. He wore his good coat to breakfast now, which he had never done before October, and Petra understood why without asking: he was going somewhere after, somewhere with other young men, somewhere with talk in it.

"You're cutting type at five in the morning," he said, kissing the top of her head. "You'll ruin your eyes."

"I ruined them years ago. It's done."

"Look at this." He dropped onto the stool across the bench from her and unfolded a newspaper he'd brought in with him, its edges already soft from being passed hand to hand. "Baden has a free press law now. Do you understand what that means? A man can print what he thinks and stand in the street and say so. Cologne papers are saying it too. Even here people are talking about a constitution — actual representatives, actual rights, not whatever the chancery decides is a favor—"

"Anton." She held up the punch to the lamp, turning it. "Hold your hands flat, I want to check something."

He gave her his hands, palms down, obedient as a boy, and she looked at them absently — the ink in the creases that never washed fully out, the same stains she carried — and let him keep talking, because she knew that listening with half an ear was safer than arguing with both.

"—and if Baden can do it, why not us? We're not different people. Papa always said the guild was rotten from the inside, he said it himself, you remember—"

"Papa said a great many things at home," Petra said mildly, "and printed very few of them."

"That's exactly my point! Why should he have to? Why should anyone?" He pulled the newspaper back and smoothed it on the bench between them, and she saw the column he'd marked with a thumbnail: reports from the south, crowds, speeches, words like *freedom* and *unification* set in type so bold it seemed to shout off the page. "There's going to be a change, Petra. Maybe this spring, maybe sooner. Everyone feels it. It's like weather."

"Then it will rain or it won't," she said, "and either way the press still needs cleaning and the Kessler order is due Thursday." She stood and went to the kettle. "Widow Kessler wants two hundred copies of the memorial card for her husband's anniversary. Did you set the frame?"

"It's set."

"Did you check it against the copy?"

A pause. "Mostly."

"*Anton.*"

"It's fine, Petra. It's a memorial card, not a treaty." But he got up and went to the stone, and she heard him begin to check the frame piece by piece, and she permitted herself the smallest satisfaction. Their father had left her the shop in more than name; he had left her the habit of exactness, and exactness, she suspected, was the only inheritance that would keep either of them alive.

They ate breakfast at the workbench because the kitchen table had become, since October, a place neither of them wished to sit. Bread and dripping, coffee made from the cheap roasted grain that was all the war taxes left anyone, and the newspaper open between them like a third person at the meal.

"The bridge meeting is Saturday," Anton said, into his cup.

Petra poured the coffee slowly. "What bridge meeting?"

"There isn't one officially. There's just — people. People who will happen to be standing on the bridge Saturday evening." He grinned at her, and for a moment he was nine years old again, showing her a stolen apple. "You could come."

"No."

"You don't even know what I'd say."

"I know what happens to people who are seen," she said. "Papa taught me that too."

"Papa taught you fear."

Petra set down the pot with care, because her hands wanted to do otherwise. "Papa taught me arithmetic," she said. "One press, two of us, no father, and a guild full of men who would close us tomorrow if they had a reason. You want to give them a reason. I want to cut this *g* and pay the paper merchant and keep the roof on. That's not fear, Anton. That's counting."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she saw that he loved her and pitied her in the same breath, and that both were unbearable.

"It's coming whether we count it or not," he said quietly. "You can't set type fast enough to hold back a year like this one."

* * *

The day's work proceeded the way days should: steadily, invisibly. The Kessler memorial cards came off the press clean, two hundred of them, the black border true, the widow's husband's name rendered in Petra's own cut letters, which felt like a small strange intimacy — to hold a dead man's name in her hands, arranged and locked and inked, and to give it back to his widow forty times an hour. Herr Brandt came by at midday from the chancery to collect the tax notices, and stood in the doorway a moment longer than necessary, a thin, stooped man with ink on his cuffs from his own desk, and said, "Your father's accounts were always the tidiest in the district, Fräulein Voss. The chancery notices that."

"Does it," Petra said, and he flushed and left, and she wondered afterward why he had blushed, and decided it was none of her business.

In the afternoon she cast the new *g*, thirty sorts, and set them in the case, and ran a trial line — *the quick brown fox* and then, because it was nearly evening and she was tired and sentimental in a way she permitted herself only alone, a line from her father's favorite psalm, set backward in the stick, mirror-wise, the way all type lived: *Thy word is a lamp unto my feet.*

It printed true. Not a ghost-thread anywhere.

* * *

Dinner was soup and the last of the rye, and Anton came home smelling of cold air and other people's tobacco, and the argument resumed where it had left off, because for Anton an argument was a room he lived in.

"They're requisitioning grain from the valley farms," he said. "You know that? The garrison at Koblenz takes what it wants and pays in scrip. And the road tolls went up again. And yesterday a man was flogged in Wittlich for singing — singing, Petra — a song they didn't like."

"Then sing quietly," Petra said.

"You're impossible." He pushed the soup away, then pulled it back. "Something is going to break. I'm telling you as a fact, the way you'd tell me the press needs a new screw. Everything is stretched too tight — the harvest, the taxes, the censorship, all of it. When it breaks, I intend to be standing on the right side of it."

"And I intend to be standing in this shop," Petra said, "with a roof over it."

"You sound like him." He said it cruelly, she thought, or maybe only tiredly. "Head down. Ink on your hands. As long as the forms lock up and the bills get paid, nothing else exists."

"Yes," Petra said. "That is exactly what I sound like." She gathered the bowls. Under the table, unseen, her thumb moved along the edge of her apron where a fleck of dried ink sat, and she pressed it as if testing a seal. "And it has kept us fed through the worst autumn this house has ever had. So I'll thank you not to spend our safety buying yourself a feeling of importance."

The silence after that was long. When she looked up, Anton was staring at the window, at the dark square beyond it where the gaslight had begun to come on, and his anger had gone out of him and left something else, something younger and more frightened underneath.

"I'm not doing it to feel important," he said. "I'm doing it because someone has to write down what's happening. Someone has to keep the names."

"Keep what names?"

But he only shook his head and stood, and kissed her hair again, and went up to bed, and the stairs creaked under him in the pattern she had known all her life.

Petra sat alone at the cleared table with the lamp, and after a while she took out the ledger of the shop's accounts — the ordinary one, orders and debts, her father's neat columns continuing under her own hand — and worked through the week's figures until the numbers were smooth and reconciled and the world was a thing that balanced.

Above her, she heard Anton moving in his room. Not sleeping. Walking. Stop. Walking.

She told herself it was nothing. A young man restless in a restless season. Tomorrow there would be paper to buy and the Wittlich order to set, and the *g* was printing beautifully, and routine was a wall, and walls held.

She blew out the lamp and sat a moment in the dark, in the smell of ink that had raised her, and admitted to herself, in the privacy of it, the one thing she would not say aloud: that when Anton had said *someone has to keep the names*, her heart had turned over once, hard, like a form shifting in the chase — and that she had spent the rest of the evening pretending she hadn't felt it.
