## 2. Names in the Ledger

The guild ledger lived in the back room of the Voss press, in the second drawer of the oak cabinet that had come up the river from Coblenz before her father was born. It was not a secret book — everyone in the town knew the guild kept its register, just as everyone knew where the register slept — but it was not an open book either, and in the eleven weeks since her father's death Petra had opened it exactly twice: once to confirm that the Voss name stood properly entered among the masters of the trade, and once to check a date for the Wittlich order's imprint.

The third time she opened it, on a gray Thursday with rain ticking at the shutters like a compositor tapping sorts into his hand, she was looking for paper prices.

That was what she told herself, at least. She had gone to the drawer for the account book of 1846, because Herr Wittlich wanted his invoices matched against the old rates and would not be argued down, and the account book lay beneath the ledger, and to reach it she had to lift the ledger out and set it on the table, and there it was, lying closed under her hands, its spine cracked in the familiar places, its corners worn round as river stones. It smelled of dust and tallow and, faintly, of ink — everything in this room smelled faintly of ink; she had long ago stopped noticing, the way one stops hearing a bell that rings all day.

She took out the account book. She found the rates. She wrote them in pencil on a scrap of proof paper, and then, instead of returning the ledger to the drawer, she sat down at the worktable with it still under her hand, and did not move for a while.

The rain went on ticking.

She told herself it was coincidence, what Anton had said last night. *Someone has to keep the names.* A boy's phrase, borrowed from a pamphlet or a tavern conversation, tossed across the dinner table the way he tossed everything now — half challenge, half plea. It had nothing to do with this book. This book held apprenticeship dates and journeyman registrations and the guild fines for late dues. It was an instrument of commerce, not of conscience. Her father had kept it neatly and without passion, the way he kept everything, and if a man could look at this ledger and see revolution in it, that said more about the man than the book.

She opened it anyway.

* * *

The early pages were her father's handwriting at its most patient: the small, even letters of a man who had learned his script at the same bench where he learned his kerning. Entries from the twenties marched down the pages in tidy procession — names, dates, fees, the occasional marginal note about a disputed apprenticeship or a widow's exemption. Here was old Master Reuland, dead these six years. Here was her father himself, registered the year he married, his name written by another's hand and then never again touched, as though the act of being recorded once had made further recording unnecessary.

Petra turned the pages slowly. She was aware of a feeling she could not immediately name, something between reverence and trespass, and she decided it was simply the strangeness of reading a book her father had handled daily and never once shown her. He had shown her everything else. He had taught her to cut a *g* so true it would print ten thousand impressions without fattening, to judge dampness of paper by pressing it to her cheek, to read a galley of type upside down and backward faster than most men read it forward. But he had never put this ledger in her hands, and she had never asked, because some part of her had understood — the way children understand such things without evidence — that the guild's book was the town's book, and the town's business was not theirs to go poking in.

Except that it was theirs now. That was what the lawyer had said, standing awkwardly among the presses three days after the funeral: the guild registration passed to the surviving member of the firm, and the records were hers to maintain. *Yours to maintain.* The phrase came back to her now with a weight it had not carried in the lawyer's mouth.

The entries grew later. Her father's hand aged by degrees — not worse, exactly, but drier, the letters narrowing as though the man were writing within an ever-smaller space. And then, near the end of the volume of 1846, Petra began to notice the marks.

At first she took them for smudges. Small crosses set beside certain names, penciled lightly in a margin, the graphite barely legible in the lamplight. Then she saw they were too regular for smudges, and too deliberate. Beside each cross, in a different hand entirely — a thin, cramped, official-looking script, the kind produced by men who wrote for a living and hated every stroke — ran a notation. Dates. Places. Abbreviations she had to puzzle over.

*Konskr.* She knew that one. Conscription roll.

She turned back a page. More crosses. Another page. The crosses clustered, thinned, clustered again, like mold spreading unevenly through a ream of stored paper. And always the cramped annotations, following the crossed names like shadows.

She found her father's marginalia mixed among them — short notes in his upright hand, responding to the other script as though the two writers were conducting a correspondence across the surface of the page. Once, beside a journeyman's name and the annotation *Konskr. — 2nd call*, her father had written: *Paid fine. Exempted by sight defect. Certified.* And beneath, in the cramped official hand, grudgingly: *Certif. rec'd.*

Her heart was beating oddly. She noticed it the way she noticed a misregister on a proof — a slight doubling of the world, everything printing twice, fractionally out of true.

She turned to the newer leaves. 1847. The year of the bad harvest, the year the pamphlets began coming up the river wrapped in grain sacks, the year Anton started staying out. Her father's writing here was tired but still precise. And the crosses were thicker now. Nearly every young man registered in the guild's rolls — every apprentice over sixteen, every journeyman, every new master under thirty-five — carried the mark.

And beside the marks, in the thin official script, other words began to appear. Not only *Konskr.* anymore. New abbreviations, and after them, in several cases, actual sentences, compressed to their bureaucratic essence:

*Sedit. libell. dist. — fined.*
*Present at Bernkastel meeting. Observed.*
*Debt of dissent — see annex.*

*Debt of dissent.* Petra read the phrase three times. It sounded almost respectable, the way officials made things sound when they wished to punish them politely. A debt. As though opinion were currency, and speech an advance drawn against a man's body, repayable in years of service or cells of stone.

She was no longer pretending to look for paper prices. The account book lay forgotten beside her elbow. The rain had settled into a steady drumming, and the lamp hissed softly, and Petra Voss sat alone in the back room turning the pages of a book she had inherited, learning it the way she had once learned her letters — slowly, thoroughly, with a rising sickness in her stomach that she examined almost clinically, as if it belonged to someone else.

Because she knew how to find a name in a list. Anyone who had ever pulled a form of type knew how to search a column: let the eye fall slack, let the pattern pass beneath it, wait for the irregularity to declare itself. She had found errors in thousand-piece formes this way since she was nine years old. She applied the skill now without wanting to, helplessly, the way one flinches from a blow already seen coming.

Voss, Anton. Registered 1844. Apprentice to the master P. Voss — no relation noted, because none needed noting, because everyone knew, because the town was small and the guild was smaller. Fee paid. Dues current.

And in the margin, in the thin cramped script, a cross.

And beside the cross: *Konskr. — eligible next call. Bro. of master (deceased). Sedit. libell. dist.? — unverified. Debt of dissent — pending.*

*Pending.* The word sat there in its official patience, patient as a loaded gun laid on a table. Unverified. Pending. Somewhere in the chancery annex a clerk — she thought of Brandt, the gray little customs-adjacent functionary who came in twice a year for stamped forms and always knocked as though afraid of disturbing something — somewhere a file existed, or would soon exist, with her brother's name on it, growing fatter the way files grew, fed by informers' notes and tavern gossip and whatever Anton said too loudly within earshot of the wrong ears.

*Bro. of master (deceased).* So even their father's death was in there, converted to data, folded into the arithmetic of liability. A dead father meant no master's protection. An orphaned apprentice was a conscriptable apprentice. She could see the logic of it with terrible clarity, the way she could see the logic of a well-built press — every part serving every other part, the whole mechanism designed for one purpose, running smoothly toward one output.

She looked at her brother's name a long time.

Anton, who had been born in the room above this one. Anton, whose first job in the shop had been sweeping dustboards, and who had graduated to distributing type and complained so magnificently about the monotony that their father had laughed — one of the few times Petra could remember him laughing — and said, *Boy, when you learn that the alphabet is the whole world arranged in convenient order, you'll stop complaining.* Anton, who printed his first pamphlet secretly on the shop's own press two winters ago, and thought she didn't know, and had left the forme locked up overnight where she'd found it, and had read it by candlelight standing in this very room: eight furious pages about press laws and censorship, badly argued in places, beautifully typeset throughout, because whatever else he was, he was their father's son.

*Sedit. libell. dist.? — unverified.*

They knew. Or they suspected, which for practical purposes was the same thing. Someone had talked, or someone had guessed, or the press itself — the smell of it, the hours of it, the light burning late above the alley — had testified on its own. A printing shop raised suspicions the way a slaughterhouse raised flies.

And she had said nothing to him. Last night, while he paced overhead like a man rehearsing a speech, she had sat below with her lamp and her *g* and told herself that routine was a wall and walls held. She had known — not the particulars, not the word *pending*, but the shape of the danger, the way one knows a storm by the color of the sky — and she had chosen the wall over the warning. Chosen it because warning him meant admitting she had been watching him. Because warning him meant joining him, and joining him meant standing where the light fell, and Petra Voss had spent twenty-six years learning that the safe place in any pressroom is behind the machine, not in front of it.

Diligence. That was what she would have called it, if anyone had asked. Keeping the books. Minding the shop. A woman attending to her proper inheritance.

The lie sat in her chest, heavy and smooth as a well-polished slug of type.

* * *

She copied the entry.

She did not fully understand why she was doing it until she was already doing it — her hand moving with the automatic competence of long training, transferring Anton's line item onto a scrap of clean proof paper in pencil, small and exact: the name, the registration date, the cross, the annotations, word for word including the question mark. Then, because she was apparently beyond stopping herself, she copied the two entries above it as well — Josef Reuland the younger, flagged and annotated; Mathias Kessler, flagged, with the additional note *widow's son — see 1839 annex* — and then a fourth name whose cross and notation had caught her eye going down, and a fifth. She worked quickly, quietly, absurdly, like a thief stealing from herself. When she finished she had five lines of pencil on proof paper, and she folded the scrap small and slid it inside her sleeve, where it lay against her wrist warm as a coal.

Then she sat and listened to the house.

Rain. The gutter dripping at the corner. The settling creak of old timbers. From the front of the shop, the street sounds of a market morning muffled to nothing by the weather — a cart wheel, a dog, a voice calling something she couldn't make out. And above her, the floorboards of Anton's room, silent now. He had gone out — she had heard him leave before dawn, had pretended to sleep through it, cataloguing the sound of his boots on the stairs the way she catalogued everything, storing it, filing it, keeping the names.

*Someone has to keep the names.*

He had said it across the supper table with his chin up and his ears red, daring her to argue, and she had refused to listen, and she had understood even then that refusing to listen was itself a kind of answer, and not the innocent one she pretended.

She looked at the ledger, open to the page with her brother's life reduced to four lines of official shorthand, and she felt something settle in her that she recognized from the pressroom: the moment when a difficult forme stops fighting the lockup and suddenly, all at once, sits square. A decision taking hold. Though what the decision was, precisely — warn him, watch him, follow him, forge the registry, burn the page and swear she never saw it — she could not yet have said. All she knew was that she had crossed some internal line between the woman who maintained the records and the woman who read them, and that there was no composing stick in her father's shop that could un-set that line once locked.

She closed the ledger gently, as if it might feel her hands shaking, and returned it to the drawer, and set the account book on top of it in its accustomed place, and squared both to the edge of the cabinet the way her father had taught her before she could reach the handles.

Then she washed her face in the yard pump, tied on her apron, and went out to the shop floor to set the Wittlich order, and worked for two hours in perfect mechanical silence, pulling proofs at intervals, checking margins, correcting, locking the chase — and all the while the scrap of paper burned cold against her wrist, and all the while her mind kept returning, traitorously, helplessly, to the phrase in the cramped official hand:

*Debt of dissent — pending.*

Pending things became due. That was what pending meant. The rain eased toward noon, and the light through the south window strengthened, and she heard Anton come in the side door whistling something seditious and tuneless, and she called out to him that there was bread on the board, in a voice she kept deliberately flat — and then bent closer over her composing stick so he would not see her face, and asked no questions, and gave no warnings, and said nothing at all.

Three days later, the drums began at the bridge.
