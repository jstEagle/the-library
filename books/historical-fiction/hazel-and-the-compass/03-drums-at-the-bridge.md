## 3. Drums at the Bridge

The drums began before dawn.

Anton Voss heard them through the floorboards of his bedroom above the print shop, a low irregular knocking that he mistook at first for the press — for his sister already at work below, pulling proofs in the dark the way their father used to. But the rhythm was wrong. The press spoke in even measures, patient as a heartbeat. This was something arrhythmic and human, many hands on many skins, and it was coming from the direction of the river.

He dressed without lighting a lamp. In the glass of the window the town lay blue and silver under the last of the night, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold, and beyond the roofs he could see the bridge — the old stone bridge with its arches like the ribs of some drowned animal, the only crossing for twenty miles, the throat through which everything entered or left their lives: grain, news, soldiers, brides, debts. He had looked at that bridge ten thousand times and never once thought about who owned it.

This morning there were people on it.

Not travelers. Not carters waiting for the tollgate to open. People standing shoulder to shoulder across the full width of it, from abutment to abutment, and more coming down both river roads, hundreds of them, thousands perhaps, carrying lanterns that made the fog over the water glow like something burning beneath the surface.

Anton pressed his forehead to the cold pane. He thought of what he had said at dinner three nights ago — he could not remember the exact words now, only Petra's face while he said them, the way she had kept her eyes on her plate and her hands folded in her lap as though politics were a smell she wished would pass. *They cannot arrest us all,* he had told her. *That is the whole arithmetic of it. They have always known it, and they have always counted on us not knowing it.* She had said nothing. Their father's chair at the head of the table had been empty eleven weeks then, and the silence where his voice should have been had made every bold sentence feel slightly obscene.

Now the arithmetic was walking onto the bridge.

* * *

By seven o'clock the square in front of the Rathaus held more people than the parish registers said the whole town contained. Farmers in their Sunday coats although it was Thursday; journeymen with their tools still slung on their backs because they had walked out of the workshops mid-task; women with shawls over their nightclothes; boys perched on the pump and the horse troughs and the shoulders of men who pretended not to notice. The air smelled of wet wool, torch pitch, and bread — someone had brought loaves and was breaking them apart and handing out pieces like a communion nobody had organized.

Anton stood near the fountain with his collar up, telling himself he had come only to look. He was a printer's son and a compositor by trade; looking was what he did. He read the crowd the way he read a page, and what he read made his chest hurt.

There was no leader. That was the first thing. He searched for one the way you search a proof sheet for the error, expecting structure, hierarchy, a name at the top — and found instead a thousand small decisions happening simultaneously. A tanner was arguing with a priest, not angrily but with the urgent intimacy of two men discovering they agreed. A woman had climbed onto the steps of the chancery annex and was reading aloud from a newspaper printed in Baden, her voice cracking on the word *freedom of the press*, and the crowd repeated the phrase after her in a low rolling murmur, passing it back through itself like water finding its level. Somewhere a fife played something that might once have been a church hymn.

*They cannot arrest us all.* His own words came back to him and felt, this morning, less like rhetoric than like a door he had left unlocked and which the world had quietly walked through.

"Voss." A hand on his arm. It was Kessler — no. Not Kessler; Kessler was dead these four years, buried with his proof sheets, God rest him. It was young Metzger from the bindery, ink worked so deep into his knuckles that no pumice would ever lift it, his face shining with cold and excitement. "You can set type. We need — they're saying we need a broadside. Something people can hold. Words, real words, not just shouting. Can you do it?"

Every instinct Anton owned told him to say no. His father had survived thirty years in this town by printing almanacs and hymnals and the guild's own notices, by keeping his opinions between the lines where no censor could read them. *Keep your head down* — that had been the catechism of the house, and Petra had learned it better than he had, had learned it so well she could recite it silently with her whole body.

"Yes," Anton said. "I can do it."

The word left him and did not come back.

* * *

They commandeered the bindery because it had a hand press and a back door. By nine there were six men crowded around the stone with Anton, and the argument was not whether to print but what. Metzger wanted fire. An older man, a cooper named Brenner whose forearms were like casks, wanted dignity — demands, enumerated, reasonable, addressed to the authorities as if the authorities were still an audience rather than a target. A student home from Heidelberg, pale and eloquent, quoted things in French.

Anton listened, and then he did what his father had taught him without ever meaning to: he thought about the reader.

"Not a manifesto," he said. "A list."

They looked at him.

"A manifesto is a speech. Nobody carries a speech in his pocket. But a list — ten demands, numbered, set large enough to read by torchlight — a man can nail that to a door. A woman can copy it out and send it to her cousin downriver. It doesn't argue. It *states*. And when they come —" he heard himself say *when*, and something in the room went very quiet and very bright — "when they come, and they ask who wrote it, everyone can truthfully say: the town wrote it. There is no author. There is only type."

Brenner laughed, a short bark. "Spoken like a man whose trade is making himself invisible."

"Spoken," Anton said, "like a man whose trade is making things legible."

They settled on twelve points. Freedom of the press. Trial by jury. A citizens' guard in place of the garrison. Abolition of the remaining feudal dues. Each point short enough to set in one line. Anton composed it himself at the case, his fingers moving through the type with the blind fluency of twenty years, and he was aware — distantly, the way you are aware of weather — that each letter he lifted was a small act of treason, that the em quads and the lowercase e's were identical to the ones that had set his father's hymnals, and that the difference between a hymnal and a revolution was nothing but arrangement.

The press began to pull at half past ten. The sound filled the bindery, that familiar iron respiration he had slept above his entire life, and it sounded different today. It sounded like drums.

* * *

At noon the crowd took the bridge.

It happened almost gently. The tollkeeper — old Faust, who had collected the crossing fee for thirty years and whose ledger of tolls was nearly as famous as the guild's — simply walked out of his gatehouse, set his keys on the toll table where anyone could see them, and joined the crowd. Without the tollgate closed, without the customs officers to check papers, the bridge stopped being the state's property and became, in the space of an hour, just a road. People stood on it and wept. Boys ran its length shouting that the Rhine belonged to no one and therefore to everyone. Someone hoisted a black-red-gold banner sewn overnight from a petticoat and a dyed tablecloth, and the sight of those colors over the water hit the crowd like wine.

Anton stood on the bridge with a stack of broadsides under his arm and gave them away until his arms were empty. People took the sheets from him with both hands, the way you take bread, the way you take a relic. An old woman asked him to read it aloud to her because her eyes were bad, and he read all twelve points into her ear over the noise of the river, and when he finished she kissed his cheek and said, "My husband struck the last blow against the dues in 'thirty. They took our cow for it. Tell me it wasn't for nothing."

"It wasn't for nothing," Anton said, and found that he believed it, and that believing it frightened him more than any doubt ever had.

From the middle of the span he could see up the hill to the print shop, to the window of the workroom where a figure stood motionless behind the glass. Even at this distance he knew the posture — the straight back, the folded arms, the absolute stillness that was his sister's way of holding the world at arm's length. He raised his hand. She did not raise hers. She only watched, and watching her watch him, Anton felt the first cold thread work its way down through the exhilaration, thin as wire: *she sees what I cannot see from inside the crowd.*

He turned back to the bridge and the banners and the singing, and he made himself be happy again. There would be time later to wonder what Petra had seen.

* * *

What she had seen, though he would not learn it until evening, was the dust on the far road.

* * *

The army answered before sunset.

It was almost respectful, the speed of it. The garrison at Koblenz had evidently been counting the same arithmetic Anton had counted, and had arrived at a different sum. Two companies of infantry came down the east road in column, bayonets fixed, drums beating the quickstep, and behind them came the wagons — and it was the wagons that told the town what kind of day this would be. Not a show of force. A requisition. Armies do not bring wagons to parades.

The crowd on the bridge did not scatter, but it changed. Anton watched it change, watched the festival drain out of it the way color drains from a face. The farmers melted backward toward their carts. The women drew children close. What remained in the front ranks was mostly the young and the committed, and among them Anton stood, holding nothing now, his empty hands suddenly very visible to him.

An officer rode forward alone, a captain with a face like a closed ledger, and read the proclamation in a voice trained to carry without effort:

*By order of the Royal District Administration, the town is placed under martial law. All public assemblies are dissolved. The bridge is closed to traffic. All presses are forbidden to publish without license. All documents, records, and papers deemed seditious are subject to seizure as evidence. Resistance will be met with force.*

*All documents, records, and papers.*

Anton heard the phrase and felt it land somewhere below his stomach. He thought of the guild ledger in the chancery — the great vellum-bound book in which every master of the printers' and tradesmen's guild had been entered for sixty years, every apprenticeship, every fine, every debt, every censure. Every name. His father's name in it, entered in 1819 in a hand like copperplate. His own name, entered at his binding. And beside certain names — he knew this because his father had shown him once, bitterly, after too much wine — the small marginal marks that meant *noted*: noted for the conscription lists, noted for the old debts of dissent, noted, noted, noted. Half the town's men carried those marks. Anton carried one himself. He had seen it.

*Subject to seizure as evidence.*

"Evidence," said Brenner beside him, very quietly. "Evidence for whom?"

No one answered. Down the street, the first soldiers reached the square and began, with terrible courtesy, to clear it.

* * *

Anton ran the last hundred yards to the shop because running was faster than thinking. Through the workroom window he saw lamplight, and then Petra opening the door before he knocked, which meant she had been watching the street, which meant she had been watching for him.

"You're filthy," she said. "And you smell of ink that isn't ours."

"There were broadsides. Twelve demands. I set them myself." He heard how it sounded — half confession, half boast — and hated both halves. "Petra, listen. The proclamation. They're seizing papers. Records. Anything they call seditious."

"I know." Her voice was flat and careful, the voice she used when she was keeping something level that badly wanted to spill. "Herr Brandt came by an hour ago. He's the one who has to sign the confiscation receipt tomorrow morning. For the guild ledger." She looked at him then, straight on, and Anton saw that his sister — who refused to listen to politics at dinner, who kept her head down, who had said nothing, nothing, all these years — had already understood the thing he was only now arriving at.

"They'll take the names," he said. "Every man marked in the margins. It's a list, Petra. We handed them a list of ourselves, sixty years long, bound in vellum."

"I know," she said again.

And downstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, Anton could hear the army's drums settling into the town like a pulse that did not belong to it — steady now, regular, patient as a press — and he understood that whatever they had seized on the bridge today, the true requisition had not yet begun. Tomorrow morning, at the chancery, a shaking clerk would sign a receipt, and the book with all their names in it would pass out of the town's keeping forever.

Unless.

He caught himself forming the word and stopped, because Petra was still looking at him, and in her look he read the question she would never ask aloud: *what do you intend to do?*

Outside, the lamplighter made his rounds under the eyes of sentries, and the fog came up off the river, and somewhere in the chancery annex the guild ledger sat in the dark with sixty years of names in it, waiting for morning.
