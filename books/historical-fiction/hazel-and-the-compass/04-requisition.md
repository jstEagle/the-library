## 4. Requisition

Petra woke before the drums.

She lay in the narrow bed above the shop and listened to the sound arrive from across the river — not a roll but a slow, deliberate beating, like a hand knocking at a door that would not be answered quickly. It came in groups of six, then stopped, then began again. She had heard drums before, at festivals, at her father's funeral when the guild had sent its honor party with muffled sticks. These were not festival drums. These were drums that intended to be counted.

Below her, Anton was already awake. She could tell by the creak of the third stair from the top, the one he always skipped going down and never coming back up. By the time she had dressed and wound her hair under her cap, the smell of coffee — or something pretending to be coffee, chicory stretched past honesty these last months — was rising through the floorboards.

He stood at the shop window with his coat already on, which meant he had somewhere to be, which meant she did not want to know where.

"Don't," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"Your coat has." She poured herself a cup. Her hands were steady, and she made a small private study of that steadiness, the way she studied everything: how it held, where its seams ran, what it cost. "The bridge?"

"The bridge." He turned, and for a moment he looked so much like their father that Petra had to set the cup down. The same heavy brow, the same way of standing square to whatever he believed in, as if belief were a thing you could shoulder like a crate. "They're holding it. Students, boatmen, some of the weavers from over the water. They're saying no garrison crosses without terms."

"And the army is saying what?"

Anton's jaw worked. "The army is saying very little this morning. That's what frightens me about it."

"Good," Petra said. "Something frightens you. Then perhaps you'll stay indoors."

"Petra—"

"There are names in that ledger, Anton." It came out before she could weigh it, and she watched it land on him — the small flinch around his eyes, the half-second where he understood she knew something she had never told him. She had carried the knowledge for eleven days now, folded into her like a bad fold in a sheet of paper, and she had told herself every day that silence was kindness, that telling him would only put fear into him before there was any need for fear. Now she saw that her silence had its own weight, and that he could feel it even if he could not read it.

"What names?" he asked quietly.

"Old ones," she said. "Father's debts. Guild quarrels from before I could walk. Nothing that concerns this morning."

It was not entirely a lie, and it was not remotely true, and Anton looked at her for a long moment as if he might press the page flat and read the whole impression. Then the drums beat again, closer now, six strokes and a pause, six strokes and a pause, and whatever he might have said went out of him and was replaced by the street.

"I'll be back by noon," he said.

"By ten."

"By noon." And he was gone, and Petra stood alone in the shop with two cups of chicory cooling and the type cases silent around her like a congregation that had heard the sermon many times before.

* * *

By eight o'clock the square was full of soldiers, and by nine it was full of order.

Petra watched it happen from the shop door, which she opened because an open door was less interesting than a closed one, or so she hoped. Across the square the bakery awning threw its shadow over a knot of women who had come out with baskets and then simply stayed, baskets hanging forgotten from their arms, watching. The fountain stood dry — it had been dry since autumn — and around it the companies formed with a precision that was itself a kind of speech. Nobody shouted. That was the terrible thing. In all the stories of occupations that men told in taverns, there was shouting, there was smashing, there was noise. What actually arrived was quieter than market day. A lieutenant walked the line once, adjusted a man's chin strap with two fingers, and the whole square became a machine with nobody inside it.

The requisition notices went up first. Two soldiers with a bucket of paste and a bale of printed sheets — badly printed, Petra noted against her will, crooked margins, a forme that had been locked up by someone who cared nothing for his trade. *BY ORDER OF THE MILITARY GOVERNOR.* The words marched down the sheet in capitals, and beneath them, in a smaller and more apologetic face, the clauses: billeting, curfew, seizure of arms, seizure of correspondence, seizure of documents pertaining to sedition. The word *sedition* did most of the work. Everything else was scaffolding around it.

Then came the cart, and behind the cart came Herr Brandt.

She had known Brandt all her life in the way one knows a fixture — the chancery clerk who stamped her father's annual licenses, who attended every guild dinner and drank exactly two glasses of wine and laughed at exactly the right jokes. A thin man, gray before his time, with ink permanently shadowing the side of his right hand. Today he walked behind a sergeant the way a man walks behind his own coffin, and he carried the chancery keys on a ring he kept touching and releasing, touching and releasing, like worry beads.

They broke into the guildhall at half past nine. There was no need to break anything — Brandt unlocked the door himself, and the sergeant stood aside with a courtesy that somehow made it worse — but afterward, when the story went around the town, everyone would say they broke in, because *they entered with permission* was a sentence no one could bear to say.

From the shop door, forty paces away, Petra watched them carry out the ledger.

It was not a dramatic object. That was the horror of it. Sixty years of the printers' guild in a bound volume the size of a Bible, quarter-bound in calf, the spine cracked and re-sewn twice, the corners worn round by hands. Her father's hand was in it — his entries, his corrections, his careful marginal notes in that upright hand she knew better than any scripture. And after his entries, the entries of the man before him, and before him, all the way back, every apprentice taken in, every journeyman made free, every fine levied, every dispute recorded, every name.

Every name.

Two soldiers carried it on the board used for moving beer kegs, because someone had thought about weight, and set it in the cart between two chests already filled — she could see correspondence spilling from one, a bundle of pamphlets, the town's paper life gathered up like grain for tithe. A lieutenant checked something on a list. A second lieutenant checked the first lieutenant. Somewhere a drum beat its six strokes and paused.

And Herr Brandt came down the guildhall steps with a single sheet of paper in his hand and a pencil stub, and he stood at the cart and signed.

She was close enough to see his hand shake. Not trembling exactly — shaking, the way a man shakes who has been cold for a long time and cannot get warm. He wrote his name in the receipt book the sergeant held, and the sergeant tore off the carbon-backed duplicate and handed it to him, and Brandt took it and looked at it as though he had been handed a receipt for something other than paper. For a moment he stood there, an official document in his fist proving that everything done this morning had been done properly, and Petra saw his eyes go up, and travel across the square, and stop — just briefly, just long enough — on the woman standing in the doorway of the Voss print shop.

She did not look away. Some instinct older than caution kept her face arranged into ordinary curiosity, the face of a tradeswoman watching the authorities do what authorities did. But she met his eyes, and she saw what was in them, and it frightened her more than the soldiers had: not guilt, exactly, though there was guilt enough. It was appeal. It was the look of a man standing on a riverbank who has just signed the manifest for a cargo he knows will sink, appealing to the one witness who understands what the cargo weighs.

Then the sergeant said something, and Brandt folded the duplicate into his coat, and the cart rolled.

* * *

The rest of the morning she spent working, because working was the only prayer she had ever learned.

She had a jobbing run to set — fifty invoices for the tallow merchant, dull as gravel and therefore perfect. She stood at the stone and set type the way her father had taught her, one line at a time, never looking ahead further than the current word, because a compositor who looks ahead drops letters. Her fingers moved without her. That was the mercy of the craft; it let the mind hide inside the hands.

But the mind would not stay hidden.

*Documents pertaining to sedition.* She turned the phrase over while her fingers built the invoice, line by line, and examined it from every side as she examined a suspect letter. What pertained? Who decided? The ledger contained names — that much was certain, she had seen the flagging marks herself, the little red-inked annotations beside certain entries, made in a hand she did not recognize, sometime within the past year. Beside Anton's entry, among others. Beside the names of men who had signed the reform petition in '46. Beside men who owed old fines for pamphlets printed without license. A list inside a record, a net knotted quietly inside a rope.

Now the net was in a military chest in the chancery annex, catalogued as evidence, and evidence — she set the word *tallow*, pulled the proofing lever — evidence was a thing that existed until it was inconvenient, and then it was a thing that had never existed.

She thought about the red ink. Someone had sat with that ledger open for hours, going through sixty years, marking. That was clerical work. Patient work. Work done by lamplight, probably, in winter, by a man paid by the day. She wondered if he had any idea what he was making. Probably not. Most destruction was done by people doing a job.

"Frau Voss."

She started so badly she nearly dropped the composing stick. A boy stood in the doorway — the Kessler boy, the widow's grandson, twelve years old and employed by half the town to carry messages precisely because nobody suspected a child with dirty boots. He held out no note. He simply stood there, breathing hard, and said:

"Widow Kessler says to tell you the annex smoke starts tomorrow. And she says—" the boy screwed up his face, reciting, "—she says *tell the girl with the good hands that things kept in books are kept in nothing.*"

He waited, obedient, for a reply. Petra looked at him. Behind him the square went on being orderly, the sentries stamping their feet against the damp, the notices drying on the wall with their crooked margins.

"Tell her," Petra said slowly, "that I don't know yet what she means. But tell her I'm going to find out."

The boy nodded and was gone, and Petra stood alone among her type cases, and the invoices waited, and the drum across the square beat its patient six-and-pause, six-and-pause, counting out something she could not yet hear the end of.

She went back to the stone. One line at a time. Never look further ahead than the current word.

But her father's effects were still upstairs, unsorted since the funeral, packed into the cedar chest and the writing desk that she had not been able to bring herself to open. Eleven days of grief disguised as busyness, and now the busyness had run out, and the desk was there, and the chest was there, and Widow Kessler's message hung in the air of the shop like the smell of ink, which never quite leaves a room where printing has been done.

Things kept in books are kept in nothing.

After the tallow merchant's invoices were locked in the chase and the forme put by, Petra banked the fire, turned the sign to *closed*, and climbed the stairs to her father's desk.

The drawer stuck, as it had always stuck. She pulled it the way he had taught her to pull stuck things — firmly, without anger — and it gave, and inside lay the ordinary archaeology of a dead man: spare nibs, a tin of stamps, receipts, a length of ribbon, a small notebook of job calculations.

And beneath the notebook, wrapped in a rag that had once been part of a good shirt, something heavy and brass.

She lifted it out and turned it to the window light.

It was a compass. Not the drawing kind she used daily for arcs and circles, though it resembled one — hinged, pointed, precise — but heavier, denser than drafting brass should be, and engraved along one flat arm with a name she did not recognize, in letters worn nearly smooth by handling:

**E. HALDER**

And tucked into the fold of the rag, folded twice, a scrap of paper in her father's upright hand, the hand from the ledger, the hand she knew better than any scripture. Four lines only, and three of them were a cipher of some kind — figures and short dashes that meant nothing she could read.

The fourth line was plain, and she read it three times, and each time it changed the room.

*What must be found is under the third stone, in the cellar.*

Outside, the drums stopped. The sudden quiet was worse.

Petra sat down slowly on her father's stool, in her father's room, with her father's compass in her hand, and understood that the requisition had not ended in the square this morning. It had only begun — and that whatever the soldiers had taken from the guildhall, they had not taken everything, and her father, dead eleven days, had known it before he died.
