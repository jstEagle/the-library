## 5. The Brass Compass

The house had not yet learned to be quiet. For thirty-one days Petra Voss had woken at the hour her father used to cough — that wet, deliberate sound that had marked the mornings of her whole life, first as a wall between sleep and waking, then, in the last winter, as a countdown — and for thirty-one days the silence where the cough should have been had fallen on her like a plank. She rose anyway. The body kept its own ledger, and it did not accept erasures.

She lit the lamp in the workshop before she lit the stove, because ink came first and hunger second; her father had taught her that ordering, and she saw no reason to reform it now that he could not object. The shop smelled as it always had: linseed oil and lampblack, the vinegar bite of the etching bath, the faint sweetness of the paste pot gone sour in the corner jar. Underneath all of it, so constant she only noticed it when she left town, was the smell of lead — cold, mineral, patient. Her mother had once said the shop smelled like a church. Her father had said yes, and that type was the only sacrament he believed in. Petra had been eleven and had pretended to find this funny.

The morning's work waited in the composing stick where she had left it the night before: a funeral notice for the widow Brandt's sister, six lines, black border. Death notices were half the shop's trade now. In a town where the guild rolls were thinning and the chancery annex had begun sending polite letters about *outstanding obligations*, people still died, and their families still wanted the world told in orderly type. It was honest work. It paid little. It kept her hands moving.

She set the notice quickly, hardly thinking, her fingers walking the cases the way other fingers walked a rosary. Upper left, lowercase e, worn nearly smooth from decades of German prose. The em quads her father had cast himself, slightly proud-faced, identifiable by the tiny nick in the shoulder that no customer ever saw but that Petra could find blindfolded. She had cut her first letter at nine years old — a capital P, naturally, which her father had pronounced *acceptable* in the tone he reserved for miracles. By fourteen she was cutting punches better than his apprentice, which was why he stopped hiring apprentices. This fact had been presented to her as thrift. Only after his death, sorting through invoices, had she understood it as something else: a man keeping his daughter's skill a secret, even from the market. Even, perhaps, from the authorities, who took note of towns with too many clever hands.

She did not let herself think such thoughts during daylight. Daylight was for work.

The sun came up gray over the roofline across the alley, and by the time it did, the notice was locked in the chase, the press was oiled, and the ordinary machinery of her grief had ground through another rotation. Anton slept upstairs still, or pretended to. Since the bridge, since the drums, he came home late and rose late and spoke at dinner in a voice pitched just below argument, as though rehearsing speeches in his sleep. She had taken to washing the dishes slowly those evenings, ears lowered like an animal's, catching fragments she told herself she did not want: *delegates*, *the Frankfort petition*, *if they call the men of the guild together*. She told herself it was wind. She told herself a great many things.

But this chapter of the day belonged to neither work nor Anton. Once a week — she had done it four times now, always Thursdays, always alone — she opened her father's sea chest and went through his effects properly, the way an executor should, instead of the way she actually wanted to, which was slowly, greedily, one small object at a time, rationing him.

* * *

The chest sat at the foot of his bed under the sloped ceiling of the attic room he had kept even after there was no one to keep it separate from. It was cedar over oak, iron-bound, older than she was; he had bought it, family legend held, off a bargeman in Koblenz who claimed it had crossed the Atlantic twice. The lock had long since stopped mattering. She knelt on the floorboards, breathed the smell of cedar and old wool, and began.

Clothes first. His good coat, the shoulders still holding his shape, which she pressed briefly against her chest before folding — a weakness she permitted herself exactly once per visit. His working jackets, patched at the elbows where he leaned into the stone. Linen. More linen. A stocking with a hole she remembered darning for him and he had apparently never worn again, or worn out again, it was impossible to know.

Then the layer beneath, where he kept the things without obvious use. A bundle of letters from a cousin in Mainz, tied in the original string. His guild certificate, the wax seal intact. A wooden box of gravers in sizes she already owned duplicates of — he had simply never thrown anything away, and neither, now, would she. A clay pipe, unsmoked since her mother's death. A folded broadsheet so old the paper had gone amber, its woodcut of a liberty tree faded almost to rumor. She smoothed it flat on her knee and read the date twice. 1819. The year of the decrees, the year printers had needed to be brave or quiet, and her father, aged twenty-six, had kept this. Folded carefully. Hidden under stockings.

She put it aside on the floorboards, deliberately, next to the coat. There was a growing pile of such items, and she did not examine what she intended to do with them.

Under the broadsheet, in the chest's far corner, wrapped in a square of oilcloth tied with waxed thread, was something with weight.

She knew the shape before she unwrapped it — her hands were a craftsman's hands and shapes spoke to them first. Round, heavy for its size, metal. Her first thought, absurdly, was that he had bought a watch at last, at the end, some extravagance for the grave. She worked the thread loose with her thumbnail and peeled back the cloth.

It was a compass. Brass, cased, the size of a pocket watch, its lid engraved with a wreath of leaves so worn they were mostly suggestion. It was heavier than any instrument she had ever held, and when she thumbed the catch and the lid swung open on a hinge still smooth with forty years of oil, she understood part of why: the needle assembly was housed in a ring of what looked like silver, and the case walls were doubled. Someone had built this thing to survive something.

The needle itself was wrong.

She turned it in the lamplight. It did not point north — it pointed steadily, stubbornly, toward the attic's north wall, which meant nothing, unless the house happened to be aligned on some meridian known only to itself. She rotated the case. The needle stayed fixed relative to the case, swinging gently as the brass moved, settling always to the same mark on the dial. Not north. Not magnetic. She tilted it, tapped the crystal, held it near the iron hinge of the chest. Nothing. The needle regarded her with the serene indifference of a thing that answers to a different question.

And then she turned it to the light and saw the engraving on the inner lid, and her hands went still.

Two lines, script style, cut with a fine hand — a hand she recognized, absurdly, because she had spent her life among fine hands, and this one had the same confident entry stroke her father used on his best initials. But the name was nothing to her:

**Elias Renner — keep it moving**

Beneath the name, smaller, almost hidden in the wreath's lower curve, a single word: **cellar**.

She sat back on her heels. The attic was very quiet. Downstairs a shutter ticked in the wind coming off the river; somewhere below that, Anton's floorboard groaned as he moved around his room, or didn't move around his room, or lay staring at the ceiling composing whatever it was he composed now instead of sleeping.

*Elias Renner.* She turned the name over the way she'd turn a piece of type to check for a flaw. It was not a family name. It was not the name of anyone in the guild register she had memorized by necessity, and she had an archivist's memory for names — she kept the whole parish book in her head the way her father kept the location of every sort in every case. A stranger, then. A stranger engraved inside her father's compass — a compass that did not point north, hidden in oilcloth at the bottom of a sea chest, wrapped like something being smuggled rather than something being stored.

*Keep it moving.*

The phrase sat strangely. It was not an inscription of affection — no *from a friend*, no dates, no *in memory*. It was an instruction. Objects given in friendship got sentiments. Objects passed along in haste got orders.

And *cellar* — the cellar was not a mystery. The cellar was where the paper stock lived, damp and mouse-gnawed at the far end, where her father had stored his ink cakes in tin boxes and where, every autumn, the whole family had gone down to shift barrels and curse the stone steps. She had swept it last month for the funeral, standing in the cold down there longer than she needed to, because it had been the last place his hands had regularly touched besides the shop.

She tried to remember the cellar honestly, systematically, the way she read a forme for flaws. Left side, paper racks. Right side, the brick plinth where the second press had stood until her father sold it in the lean years — the iron bolt holes still in the bricks, she had dusted around them a thousand times. Back wall, the coal chute, bricked up after the chute collapsed. A drain. Two shelves. A nail where the lantern hung.

Nothing that answered to the word *cellar*, because everything in a cellar is the cellar.

Unless the word was not a description but a direction. She looked again at the dead needle, still pointing faithfully at its own private mark on the dial, and thought: *it doesn't tell you where you are. It tells you where something else is.* An instrument that pointed at a place instead of a pole. Which meant the mark on the dial — a small stamped triangle she had taken for a maker's flourish — might correspond to a bearing. Might correspond to a spot in a low stone room her father had entered several thousand times while she imagined she knew every inch of it.

Her father. Her careful, cautious father, who had counseled Anton at every dinner for years to mind his business, print his notices, leave politics to men with land and lawyers — that father had owned a smuggled compass with a stranger's name in it and the word *cellar* engraved like a password. Had kept a banned broadsheet from 1819 folded under his stockings for thirty years. Had stopped hiring apprentices so that no one would see how well his daughter could cut a punch.

Petra knelt on the attic floor with the compass open in her palm and felt the strange double motion of discovering that a person you mourned had been larger than the person you knew — grief widening, retroactively, to admit a man she had never met, who had lived in her father's body and behind her father's caution and had never once, in nineteen years of dinners, said a word.

She was not sure whether she wanted to weep or laugh. She did neither. She closed the lid, rewrapped the oilcloth, and put the compass in her apron pocket, where its weight pulled the fabric crooked and where she decided immediately it would live.

* * *

She made herself do the day's work first. She printed the widow Brandt's six lines — fifty impressions, clean, the black border kissing true on every sheet — and delivered them, and haggled over the bill with the widow Brandt's son-in-law, and won, and walked home through streets that were quieter than they had been two weeks ago, before the requisition order, before the soldiers started appearing at the bridge in pairs with their collars up. Quietness, she was learning, was not peace. Quietness was a held breath. She kept her eyes forward and the compass's weight against her hip and told no one, not even in the privacy of her own skull, what she intended to do tonight.

Anton was out. Of course he was out. He had eaten the supper she left covered on the hob and vanished with his collar darkened by the ink smudge he always missed at his right ear — her father's habit too, that smudge, and seeing it on Anton's collar had lately begun to hurt in a way she refused to analyze.

So at nine o'clock, with the shop shutters barred and the lamp trimmed low, Petra took the lantern and the key ring and unlocked the cellar door off the kitchen passage, and stood a moment in the draft rising from the stairs, listening to the house hold its breath around her.

The cellar received her with its familiar smells — wet stone, paper sizing gone musty, the ghost of lampblack in the tins. She hung the lantern on its nail and made herself walk the room slowly, once, like a stranger. Paper racks. Plinth. Coal chute, bricked. Drain. Shelves. Nail.

Then she took out the compass, opened it, and let the dead needle choose.

It settled instantly, unbothered by stone or lantern, pointing along the back wall toward the bricked-up coal chute — and as she brought the instrument closer, following it the way you follow a dog that has caught a scent, she saw that the needle was not merely pointing. At close range, within a hand's width, it tugged, insistently, toward a specific brick. Low, third course from the floor, second from the corner. A brick like any other brick, mortared like any other brick, except that when she knelt and put her palm flat against it, the mortar around its edges crumbled at a touch, dry and loose as bread, mortared recently and carelessly over an older joint.

Her heart was beating in her ears. She worked her fingernail into the seam, then a knife from her apron, and the brick rocked free into her hands with a sound like a closed mouth opening.

Behind it, a cavity in the old rubble, lined with more oilcloth. Inside: a packet of letters bound in string, a folded plan drawn in ink on linen paper, and a small canvas bag that clinked.

She did not open them there. Some instinct older than curiosity told her to carry the whole bundle upstairs, into the light, into the shop among the type cases where her father's presence lived most densely. She laid the bundle on the stone floor of the workshop, untied the string with fingers that would not quite stay steady, and unfolded the linen plan first.

It was a map. Routes, drawn in her father's hand — she knew his hand the way she knew her own face — running east out of the Rhineland, town by town, each stop marked with a printer's symbol she recognized from the trade: a fist holding a quill, a hand-press, a cap of liberty. The line of marks ran like stitches all the way across the map to its eastern edge, where, in letters small and clear, was written the word **Frankfurt**, and beneath Frankfurt, three words that removed the ground from under her feet:

***the free presses***

The letters were dated. The newest was dated last spring — weeks before he died. Her father had been running papers, or people, or both, along this line while telling Anton at dinner to mind his business.

Petra sat in the lamplight among the type cases, the map open on her knees, the compass warm in her pocket, and above her head Anton's footsteps crossed the ceiling, going out, going toward the bridge, going toward whatever was coming for all of them — and she understood, with the calm of a woman reading someone else's death notice, that whatever her father had buried in the cellar, she was now the one responsible for getting it moving.
