## 6. The Widow Kessler

Marta Kessler had learned to measure her days in small domestic acts that could not be construed as anything but grief. She swept the step each morning, though no one came. She polished the brass knocker her husband had bought off a peddler from Mainz, though the peddler's price had been robbery and Johann had paid it laughing. She kept the shutters of the front room open exactly the width of two fingers, so that light entered without inviting inspection. These were not habits of mourning, precisely. They were habits of survival dressed in mourning's clothes, and she had worn them now for three years, since the morning they brought Johann home with his coat torn at the shoulder and a lie on the coroner's paper — *fell from his own roof while repairing slates* — that she had signed without reading because reading it would have meant contesting it, and contesting it would have meant following him.

So when Petra Voss appeared at her gate on a Thursday evening in the last week of September, 1848, standing in the lane with her father's shawl pulled close and something wrapped in oilcloth held against her chest the way one holds an infant or a wound, Marta knew before a word was spoken what the girl had found, and she felt the old fear rise in her throat like water.

"You'd better come in," Marta said, "before Frau Eberhardt sees you standing there like a signpost."

* * *

The back room of the Kessler house still smelled of Johann. Not of him exactly — of his pipe, of the tallow he'd preferred to wax because wax was for men who had nothing to hide, of the particular dust that gathered in the seams of a man who spent his evenings bent over papers by candlelight. Marta had never aired the room. Air was a kind of forgetting, and she did not intend to forget. She lit two candles, then thought better of it and lit only one. A single flame said *I am being frugal in my widowhood*. Two flames said *someone is visiting*.

Petra sat on the edge of the chair as if it might be taken from her. She was twenty-three, perhaps twenty-four, and she had her father's hands — broad-palmed, ink-stained at the cuticle no matter how she scrubbed — and her mother's habit of holding her chin level when she was afraid. Marta had watched old Voss work through the open door of his shop for twenty years. She had watched the daughter grow up among the type cases the way other children grew up among orchards, learning the letters before she learned the psalms. There were worse educations. There were also more dangerous ones.

"Show me," Marta said.

Petra unwrapped the oilcloth with the deliberation of someone who has already rehearsed this moment and found no good version of it. Inside lay the compass.

It was smaller than most people expected. Brass gone soft-green at the hinges, the lid worn to bare metal where a thumb had worried it across years of pockets and palms. The needle sat behind glass, trembling slightly as the table took Petra's weight, settling at last toward the north window. Engraved inside the lid, in a hand too fine to have belonged to any engraver's trade — a printer's hand, a compositor's hand — was a name:

**E. HARTMANN — FRANKFURT AM MAIN — ER DRUCKT NOCH**

He prints still.

"Where did you find it?" Marta asked, though she knew the answer to this as well.

"In my father's things. In the false bottom of his tool chest, under the gravers." Petra's voice was steady the way a frozen river is steady. "There was a note with it. Just four words pointing to the cellar. I went down and found — a loose stone, and under the stone a space, and in the space—" She stopped. Her thumb moved over the compass lid the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "Nothing. The space was empty except for this. As if something had been taken out already. Or as if the space itself was the message."

"And so you came to me."

"I didn't know where else. You knew my father. People say you knew everyone he—" Petra caught herself, recalculated, went on more carefully. "Frau Kessler. My father is dead five months and I am finding things in his shop that do not belong to a type founder. A compass engraved with a Frankfurt name. A hiding place built into his own cellar floor. He was a careful man about everything else — he counted his quills, he locked his ink — and yet he left this where anyone clearing out his effects might find it. Unless it wasn't left for anyone. Unless it was left for someone specific." She looked up. "Was it left for me?"

Marta rose and went to the window, more for the gesture than the view. The lane was empty. Somewhere down by the bridge a drum was beating, the army's evening signal, and the sound arrived at the house flattened and dull, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"Sit properly, child," she said. "This will take longer than your knees will want."

* * *

"Elias Hartmann," Marta began, sitting opposite Petra with her own hands folded, because folded hands could not shake visibly. "You will not find the name in any guild book here, though half the town owed him money and the other half owed him ideas. He was a compositor by trade and a pamphleteer by temperament, which in the year 'thirty-three was very nearly a diagnosis. He printed what others only said after the third glass. Calls for a constitution. For unification. For the abolition of the censorship that kept men like your father poor and men like Hartmann hunted. He was good at it. Too good. His sheets turned up in Wiesbaden, in Cologne, in Berlin itself, and the authorities here were embarrassed into acting by their superiors, who had been embarrassed into acting by theirs."

She paused. The candle guttered, righted itself.

"There was a night in March of 'thirty-three. Cold enough that the sentries at the bridge kept to their brazier. Hartmann was to go out with a grain barge — hidden in the cargo, no papers on him, nothing but this compass, because he'd told us the needle was set wrong, deliberately wrong, that it pointed not north but along a bearing that matched the courier route east, and that a man who could read it could find the safe houses even if every other guide failed him." Marta smiled, briefly, at the memory of how she had laughed at that once. "Whether that was true or whether it was a story he told himself to make the leaving bearable, I couldn't say. What I can say is that he gave it into your father's keeping that night, along with a list, and that your father hid both, and that Hartmann crossed the bridge at midnight dressed as a flour merchant and was in Frankfurt by Sunday, alive, and printing still. That last part is on the lid, girl. It isn't decoration. It was a promise we made to ourselves."

"The list," Petra said quietly. "What list?"

"A list of names. Men marked in 'thirty-one and 'thirty-two — fines, surveillance, debts of dissent called in early, the whole apparatus. Your brother's name would be on it today if it were being written today, because Anton argues politics over dinner the way Hartmann argued them in print, only louder." Marta saw the girl flinch and did not soften it; softening was a cruelty dressed as kindness. "Your father kept that list hidden for fifteen years. Updated it. He was meticulous. When the new guild ledger was begun after the amnesty, he copied the old marks forward where they mattered, quietly, in the margins where only a compositor would think to look — because he believed, and I believed with him, that the day would come when those names would need proving, and that proof lives in records, and records live in the hands of people who understand what a record is."

The drum by the bridge beat again, and stopped.

"My husband," Marta went on, and her voice did what voices do, thinned at the center, "was not a printer. He was a cooper by trade — barrels, tubs, the honest smell of oak all his life. But he married me, and I had worked for Hartmann as a folder and stitcher when I was a girl, and so Johann learned what a proof sheet was and what it cost. In 'forty-five, when the crackdown came again — smaller than 'thirty-three, pettier, the kind that takes a man here and a press there to remind everyone of the arithmetic — Johann helped hide the proof sheets of a suppressed edition. Not in our house. Under the floor of his workshop, between the barrel staves, where no soldier thinks to look because soldiers do not imagine that coopers keep libraries."

She unfolded her hands. Folded them again.

"They came in February of 'forty-six. Four of them, and an officer, and they searched for six hours and found nothing, because Johann had moved everything two days prior to the loft above the tannery, which burned — you'll remember the fire, everyone remembers the fire — three weeks later, taking the proofs with it, and Johann climbed the tannery roof in the rain to make certain the fire reached nothing else, and fell, and the officer who had searched our house wrote *fell while repairing slates*, and I signed it."

The silence afterward was complete. Even the house seemed to hold its boards still.

"So you see, child, when you ask whether the compass was left for you, you are asking a question I cannot answer, because your father never told me whom he trusted and whom he merely tolerated, and I never asked, because asking is how networks die. What I can tell you is what I know: that compass points along a courier line that runs east to Frankfurt, that the line was alive in 'thirty-three and may be alive now, in this year of all years, when half of Germany believes the world is being remade and the other half is sharpening instruments to remake it differently. And that your father spent his whole quiet life keeping names safe from fires." She looked at the compass on the table, its needle patient as a penitent. "And now the army sits in your square and holds the guild ledger as evidence, and smoke went up from the chancery annex yesterday, and you are standing at the beginning of the same road Johann walked, and I would spare you it if sparing were possible. It is not. So I will ask you instead the only question that matters, and I want you to take a full minute before you answer, because there is no answering it twice."

Marta leaned forward. In the candlelight her face was all planes and shadow, and Petra saw — really saw, for the first time — that the widow was not old at all, only used up in places, the way a well-used tool is worn smooth.

"What do you intend to do?"

* * *

Petra did not answer in a minute. She answered in perhaps forty seconds, which Marta would later decide was either reckless or honest, depending on the ending, which neither of them could yet see.

"I intend to get the names out," Petra said. "All of them. Before the victors' historians burn the record. The ledger is confiscated, but my father copied forward what mattered, and I set type, and there are misprints and waste sheets in every shop in this town, and parish registers don't burn as easily as chanceries because the priests won't allow it. Names survive if someone decides they should." Her thumb stilled on the compass lid. "And I intend to learn what my father knew. All of it. The cellar space wasn't empty because he failed to fill it, Frau Kessler. It was empty because he emptied it — recently, I think. Before he died. Which means he moved whatever was there to somewhere else, or gave it to someone else, and left me four words and a wrong-pointing needle to follow him by."

"He always said you were the one with the eyes," Marta said, and was surprised at how much it cost her to say it, and how much it paid.

"Then help me read them."

Marta rose, took the second candle she had decided against lighting, and lit it now. Two flames. Let Frau Eberhardt gossip. She carried it to the corner cabinet, opened the drawer beneath the false linings Johann had installed for storing documents he officially never possessed, and drew out a bundle tied in sailcloth — proofs, letters, a page of Hartmann's own hand listing way-stations on the eastern road, each named only by trade and by bearing, the bearings written as compass corrections.

"Grief is instruction," Marta said, setting the bundle beside the compass, "if you let it teach instead of only ache. Johann taught me that. Now sit closer, Petra Voss, and bring the light. If we mean to rebuild a book, we begin the way all books begin—"

She untied the sailcloth.

"—with someone willing to commit it to memory first."

Outside, down by the bridge, the drums began again — but faster now, a different rhythm altogether, the rhythm that meant movement, muster, requisition. Petra's head came up. Marta put one steadying hand flat on the table, over the compass, feeling through the wood the faint tremor of the needle turning east.

"Tomorrow," she said, "the army will start burning in earnest. Tonight, we start setting type."
