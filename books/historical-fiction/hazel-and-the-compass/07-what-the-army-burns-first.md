## 7. What the Army Burns First

The smell reached the print shop before the smoke did.

Petra had been standing at the stone sink, scrubbing the last of the red ink from her knuckles — a useless habit, since she would only stain them again within the hour — when the wind shifted and carried it in through the cracked window over the drying racks. It was not woodsmoke. She had grown up on woodsmoke, on the autumn smell of the baker's ovens and the winter smell of every hearth between the bridge and the mill. This was something else, sweeter and fouler at once, with an undertone like scorched glue and hot metal, and beneath all of it a papery char that her body recognized before her mind consent to.

Paper burning. A great deal of paper.

She dried her hands on her apron without thinking about it and went to the window. From here she could see past the cooper's yard, over the tiled roofs of the tanners' row, to the chancery annex — that long low building with its green-shuttered windows where the guild records had been kept for as long as there had been a guild, where her father had walked twice a year with his hat in his hands to renew the shop's license, where Herr Brandt worked at his desk behind frosted glass like a man preserved in resin.

Soldiers stood in the annex yard. Not many of them. Six or eight, moving with the unhurried economy of men doing work they had done before in other towns. Two of them were carrying armloads of documents out through the double doors and stacking them in the courtyard against the wall, building what looked from this distance like a low, untidy cairn. A third stood apart with a lantern, though it was mid-afternoon and the light was perfectly sufficient, and Petra understood after a moment that the lantern was not for seeing by. It was for lighting with.

Her first thought, absurdly, was of the ink. The annex cellar held a store of registration paper, good rag stock stamped with the ducal watermark, and some part of her mind — the part that had run the shop's accounts since she was fourteen — began calculating what a fire in that quarter might cost, whether the flames could jump the alley to the tanneries, whether the vats would catch, whether the whole eastern row of the town would go up like a wick. That was the direction her thoughts ran first: practical, sideways, away. She was very good at running them that way. She had been practicing since her father died.

Then one of the soldiers crouched, and the lantern touched the cairn, and the smoke thickened into a column, and Petra stopped calculating.

* * *

She did not remember crossing the shop floor. She found herself at the front door with her hand on the latch, and Anton's voice came from the stairs — he had been up in the loft sorting their father's remaining stock of leads — saying, "Petra. Petra, don't."

"They're burning the annex."

"I can see the window you're standing beside." He came down quickly, taking the last three steps at once, and put his hand flat against the door above her head, holding it shut without touching her. He had gotten tall enough to do that in the past two years, her brother, who still in her mind's eye was the boy who'd cried when their mother's canary died. "There are eight soldiers in that yard and forty more in the square. What is it you imagine you'll do?"

"It's the registry," she said. "Anton, that's the registry —"

"It's the annex," he said. "It's papers. Papers burn. That's what armies do with them." His voice was steady but she heard underneath it the same thing she'd heard in it for weeks now, a kind of vibrating readiness, and she hated it suddenly, hated how alive he sounded while something in the yard turned to ash. "Better it burns than sits in their archive. Ask any man on the bridge."

"The ledger isn't in the annex," she said, and stopped.

It was out of her mouth before she had decided to say it. Three weeks since the requisition, three weeks since Herr Brandt signed the confiscation receipt with his hand shaking so badly the ink blotted twice, three weeks since the guild ledger — the fat, swollen, much-mended book that held every journeyman's indenture record, every fine levied for unlicensed printing, every name flagged in the margin with her father's careful cross-hatching, including, on page 214, in the column for debts of dissent, *Anton Voss* — had been carted off to the chancery strongroom as evidence. Three weeks of knowing and saying nothing. She had told herself it was diligence. She checked the receipt weekly, she knew which office held it, she had memorized the clerk's notation number. Dread dressed up as housekeeping. And now the housekeeping was burning.

"What did you say?" Anton asked.

"The guild ledger," she said, and her voice came out strange and level, the compositor's voice, the one she used when she read copy aloud line by line and could not afford to feel it. "They took it three weeks ago. It's held in the strongroom under the annex. I've known since the day they took it."

His face went through several expressions too fast for her to name any of them. Then he said, quietly, "You've known. About my name."

"Yes."

"And said nothing."

"There was nothing to say."

He laughed, one short breath through his nose, not with any humor in it, and turned back toward the stairs as if he meant to go up again, and then turned around. Through the window behind him the smoke column had fattened and gone black at the edges. Somewhere a woman was calling a child's name across the tanners' row, over and over, not frightened yet, just insistent, the way mothers call.

"You watched me argue politics at that table every night," Anton said. "You sat there cutting type while I talked myself blue about what was coming, and the whole time you knew my name was written down somewhere with a mark next to it."

"If I'd told you, you'd have done something," Petra said. "You'd have crossed the bridge, or spoken at the meeting, or written another of your pamphlets, and it would have made no difference except to make it worse. Waiting was the only sensible—"

"Sensible." He said the word as if tasting spoiled meat. "Petra, look out the window."

"I've seen it."

"No," he said. "Look at it."

And because he was her brother and because some old reflex obeyed him, she looked. The cairn in the annex yard had collapsed inward as it burned, and through the falling of it she could see, briefly, sheets — whole sheets, quires, bound signatures — lifting on the heat and opening as they rose like pale birds, flaring, going dark, dropping as flakes. One of them, a large folio leaf, rode the updraft clear over the wall and hung for a moment above the tannery roofs, turning slowly, and Petra's eyes followed it with the involuntary tracking instinct of a woman who had spent her life watching paper move through machines, and she thought with perfect clarity: *that page has writing on it. Someone's hand set that writing down, someone else's hand read it, and now it is becoming weather.*

The leaf burned out midair. What fell into the alley behind the cooper's yard was a scrap of black nothing, smaller than her palm.

"Three weeks ago you were right," Anton said. He had come to stand beside her at the window, shoulder to shoulder the way they'd stood at their father's coffin. "Waiting was sensible. There was a ledger in a strongroom, catalogued, receipted, safe. Whatever happened, the names survived it. Now—" He gestured at the smoke without looking at it. "Now waiting is just another way of letting it happen. Do you understand the difference?"

She understood it. She had been understanding it for some time, in the way one understands a debt coming due — intellectually, at a distance, with a settled refusal to open the account book. The requisition had been endurable because it was paperwork. Soldiers in the square, drums at the bridge, even the arrests she'd heard whispered about among the widows at market — these had shapes, edges, procedures. Things could be appealed. Receipts could be consulted. Her whole education, her whole inheritance from a father who believed that a well-set page was a small argument for order in the world, had trained her to believe that institutions, however hostile, were at least legible. You could read them. You could find your place in the form, sign in the box, wait out the term.

But fire was not an institution. Fire did not keep records. The army had come and taken the ledger as evidence, and evidence implied a trial, and a trial implied a future in which the names still mattered enough to be weighed — and now, with a lantern and a stack of papers in an afternoon, the future itself was being edited. They were not destroying the town's memory out of rage. Rage makes mistakes. This was tidy. This was scheduled. Somewhere a captain had written in his own ledger, in his own careful margin, *annex materials, superfluous, dispose*, and a soldier had struck a match at the appointed hour, and history was being proofread and corrected, and the corrections were falling out of the sky as ash.

"Page 214," Petra said.

Anton looked at her.

"Your name is on page 214. Third column from the left margin. The entries run chronologically and yours is the fourth from the top, cross-referenced to the fine of '41 and to the deposition your printer's witness gave." She heard herself reciting it and could not stop; it was the only way she knew to hold a thing, to set it precisely. "I have read that page perhaps two hundred times. I could set it in type from memory. But the rest of the book—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "The rest of the book I skimmed. Like everyone. Like a person reading a newspaper."

Down the street, the church bell began to ring — not the alarm, she realized after a moment, only the hour, four strokes, ordinary and obscene. In the annex yard the soldiers had finished. One of them kicked the last embers flat with his boot, another fetched a bucket, and the steam that rose where water met ash was white and brief and smelled, when the wind brought it, faintly of wet libraries.

* * *

That evening she did not light the lamp over the composing stone. She lit the small candle instead, the stub she used for checking the state of the type in the cases at closing time, and she carried it into the back room where her father's effects still sat in the crates she had never finished going through — his coats in one, his tools distributed between two others, the drawer of odds and ends that every craftsman accumulates over thirty years and that she had been unable to face because facing it meant deciding what a life comes to when it is over.

She had gone through his coats already. She had not gone through the drawer.

Now she tipped it out onto the table in a soft clatter of brass and bone: awl blades, a broken loupe, wax tablets, pencil stubs sharpened with a knife, a thimble that had not been her mother's, a folded square of oilcloth tied with waxed thread. And a compass. A brass compass, heavy for its size, the case worn smooth at the hinges where a thumb had worried it open and shut across years, the lid engraved deeply enough to survive polishing — a name she did not recognize, and beneath it, in smaller letters, a date eleven years old.

She sat with it in her cupped hands while the candle guttered, and outside, faintly, the town went on making its ordinary noises, doors latching, a dog complaining, the night watch calling the half hour in a voice that had learned to sound unconcerned. The smoke smell had gotten into everything. It was in the wool of her apron, in the hair she hadn't washed since morning, in the grain of the table under her fingers. Her father had sat at this table. Her father had opened this drawer and shut it, hundreds of times probably, and all those times the compass had been lying there under the awl blades, and he had known whose it was, and what the engraving meant, and why it was kept wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a drawer instead of hanging by the door like an honest tool — and he had never told her, and she had never asked, because asking was not something their family did, because keeping your head down was not a policy but a constitution, inherited like the type cases themselves.

She thought of page 214. She thought of the folio leaf turning over the tannery roofs, opening as it rose, going dark.

There were men in Frankfurt whose names were in no ledger at all, Anton said, because they had never let themselves be registered anywhere. There were presses across the border that printed what no licensed press dared. And there had been, eleven years ago, during the last crackdown — she had been twelve, she remembered the year chiefly because of the price of lead — a printer in this town whose name got crossed out of the guild rolls so thoroughly that even the memory of him had been made contraband. Her father had come home late that winter, twice, smelling of river water, and had said nothing, ever, about where he had been.

Petra unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside, folded small as a seed, was a note in her father's hand — the hand she knew better than her own, the upright serifs of a man who lettered for a living — and it pointed downward. Toward the cellar. Toward the floor stones.

She picked up the candle and stood, and her legs were steadier than she expected them to be, and she understood that sometime between the afternoon and now, between the smoke column and this candle, something in her had finished breaking and begun, instead, to arrange itself. Passivity had not died dramatically. It had simply been rendered obsolete, the way a form of type is distributed when the edition is cancelled — every sort returned to its case, the page wiped clean, the press ready for whatever composition came next.

At the cellar door she paused, one hand on the cold latch, and listened to the house above her: her brother's floorboards, the settling beams, the tick of the stove. Names were being burned in courtyards all over this occupied country tonight, she thought, and the men who burned them believed that fire was the end of a record. They had never stood inside a print shop. They did not know that a name, once truly learned, is set in the most durable matrix there is — and that a woman who has spent her life cutting letters can rebuild any page she refuses to forget.

She lifted the latch and went down into the dark, carrying the flame ahead of her like a promise.
