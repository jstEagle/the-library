## 8. Proof Sheets in the Cellar

Herr Brandt had signed his name four hundred and eleven times in nineteen years of service to the town of Gundersheim, and he could remember the precise weight of every pen stroke that had ever cost him sleep. There was the winter he certified the death of a miller's boy drowned under the ice — a formality, but the mother's eyes had followed him home. There was the tax roll of 1844, when he had entered a widow's single cow as two to satisfy a quota from Koblenz. He had told himself then that paper was paper, that ink did not bleed, that a clerk's duty was to the record and not to the recorded.

He had no such comfort now. The receipt for the guild ledger sat in the archive of his memory like a coal sewn into his coat lining. *Received, by order of the military commission, one (1) register of the Printers' and Typographers' Guild of Gundersheim, containing names, assessments, and annotations.* His signature beneath it, shaking so badly the descenders looked like they were trying to flee the page.

Three days he had carried that coal. Three days of walking past the requisitioned chancery where the ledger now lay in a locked cabinet among confiscated papers, tagged and docketed, waiting — and he knew what it waited for, because he had seen the manifests, because he had heard Captain Reiss say *evidence* in the flat tone other men used for firewood. Evidence was burned after trial. And there would be no trial for men whose only crime was written in a dead guild-master's careful hand.

Tonight he went to the print shop on Färbergasse with a leather satchel under his greatcoat, and every step of the way he expected a hand on his shoulder.

* * *

The shop was dark except for one window upstairs, where a lamp burned behind a curtain the color of weak tea. Brandt stood in the alley beside the building longer than he cared to admit, listening to the patrolling boots on the square two streets over, counting the seconds between them the way he counted entries in a column. Forty-one, forty-two. The rhythm held. Somewhere a dog barked and was silenced by its owner's voice.

He had prepared a speech in the chancery that afternoon, composing it over the fair copies he was supposed to be transcribing: *Fräulein Voss, I have done a terrible thing, or rather I have done my duty, which is sometimes the same thing, and I have come to undo it.* It sounded absurd even inside his own skull. Clerks did not come to printers' daughters at night bearing contraband. Clerks grew old at their desks and were buried with unblemished ledgers and unmourned.

But there was the misprint. That was the thing that had found him, or that he had found — he still wasn't sure which way the discovery ran, whether God had placed it in his path or whether he had spent thirty years training his eye to catch errors and the error had simply been caught, the way a net catches whatever swims through.

It happened thus: the army had ordered duplicates made of certain seized documents before removal to Koblenz. Standard procedure. A copyist — not Brandt himself; a junior man named Vogel with ink-stained cuffs and a soldier's boredom — had been set to transcribe portions of the guild ledger onto official proof sheets, the heavy rag stock kept in the chancery stores for proclamations. The work was meant to be perfect. The work was not perfect. On the second evening Vogel had misaligned the press frame — or rather the hand-press they'd commandeered from the chancery itself, a small platen machine usually reserved for receipts — and a column had printed skewed, the letters doubling at the edge, ghosting. Vogel had cursed, pulled the bad sheets from the stack, and dumped them in the waste bin to be pulped, as regulation demanded.

Regulation also demanded that someone empty the waste bins each Friday. That someone was Brandt, because Brandt had never learned to refuse a chore, and because being invisible in small ways made one invisible in large ones.

He had lifted the spoiled proofs out with both hands, heart hammering, and carried them to his desk under the pretense of sorting salvage paper. Nine sheets. Fragments of columns. Names — he recognized them, oh yes, he had lived in this town forty years and the names were neighbors, were the fathers of children he'd baptized forms for, were men who had once repaired his stove. *Anton Voss*, flagged twice: conscription assessment, and beneath it in the old guild-master's shorthand, a debt of dissent going back to '31, to a pamphlet Brandt remembered reading in secret with a candle shielded behind a book.

Nine sheets, misprinted, incomplete, wrong — and more than the army possessed of conscience, which was nothing.

So here he stood in the dark of Färbergasse with the satchel against his ribs, rehearsing absurd speeches.

* * *

The side door of the print shop opened before he knocked. Petra Voss stood in the gap with the lamp raised, and Brandt saw that she had known — had perhaps watched him dither in the alley from the upstairs window this whole time. She was not what he expected. He had imagined grief would have bent her; her father buried barely a month, her brother marked, soldiers in her square. But she stood straight as a piece of standing type, and her eyes went first not to his face but to the satchel.

"You're the clerk," she said. Not a question. "You signed for the ledger."

"I did." His voice came out thinner than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Fräulein Voss, may I—"

She stepped back from the doorway. That was all. It was, he understood later, the most generous invitation he received that year.

Inside, the shop smelled of ink and oil and cold metal, the particular smell of a place where language was manufactured. He had passed it a hundred times without entering; clerks and printers occupied adjacent professions and spoke to each other approximately never. Type cases lined the walls in their wooden cabinets, hundreds of shallow drawers, each divided into cells, each cell holding its little leaden alphabet. In the corner stood the shop's own press under a canvas sheet, like a horse turned out to pasture.

Petra set the lamp on the composing stone and waited.

Brandt unbuckled the satchel with fingers that refused to be steady. "There was an error," he said. "A copying error. Misregistered proofs. They should have gone to the pulp bin, but—" He stopped. Lied badly. Started again, because it seemed suddenly important, desperately important, that she hear the truth of it. "I took them. Deliberately. I emptied the bin and I put these in my desk and I told Vogel the pulper was broken and I would see to it Monday, and it is now Thursday, and I am a thief of government property, and I have come to ask you what a man does next, because I find I don't know."

He laid the nine sheets out on the composing stone one at a time, smoothing each flat with the flat of his hand, an archivist's gesture, tender and useless.

The lamplight took them. Skewed columns, doubled letters, ink pale in places where the platen had kissed unevenly — but legible, mostly, if you knew how to read around the damage, and Brandt knew, and he could see already in the set of Petra Voss's shoulders that she knew too, better than he did.

Her finger came down on a name. Her brother's. She said nothing for a long moment. Then, quietly, in a voice with no tremor in it at all, which frightened him more than weeping would have:

"They'll burn it. The ledger. You've seen the orders."

"I've filed the orders," Brandt said. "That is worse."

* * *

They worked at the stone by lamplight with the shutters closed and a wool blanket hung over the door gap, because Petra Voss thought of things like light escaping under doors and Brandt, who thought in filing systems, did not.

The proofs gave them perhaps a third of the ledger's contents — fragments, edges, the middle columns of pages whose beginnings and ends had fed the pulp bin. Anton Voss's entry was complete enough: name, age, trade, the conscription flag, the old annotation. Around it, half-legible: *Kessler, Georg — printer — deceased — [illegible] — materials destroyed, estate flagged.* Brandt read that one aloud and heard Petra's breath change.

"His widow lives on Kirchgasse," Petra said. "Anna Kessler."

"I know the house. I've taxed it."

"Then tomorrow you'll call on her. As a clerk." She looked up, and in the lamp's low light her face was all planes and decision. "As a clerk who has discovered an irregularity in an estate assessment. You'll need to ask her questions. Official questions. She'll answer them."

Brandt stared at her. "Fräulein Voss, I cannot interview half the town about a ledger I stole."

"You didn't steal it. You rescued it." She drew one of the proofs toward her and tilted it against the lamp, squinting at the doubled letters, and he watched her do something remarkable: she read the ghost impressions, the faint second-strike offsets where the misalignment had transferred ink to the sheet beneath in the stack. Mirror-image text, reversed, barely there — but she read it fluently, the way he read marginalia. "This one has Kessler's assessment figures. And here—" she pulled another sheet, held the two together at an angle, matching a fragment of a surname. "These aren't nine sheets. They're pieces of eleven pages. We can rebuild the structure. The gaps we fill from elsewhere."

"From where?" Brandt asked, and heard how the question was already surrender. "The parish registers? The tax rolls? Fräulein Voss, those are in the chancery, under seal, three doors from Captain Reiss's office."

"The parish priest keeps his own duplicate of baptisms. The baker Hoffmann has a memory like a granary — full and organized. The apprentices carried guild messages for years; some of them will remember entries." She said it all flatly, itemizing, and he realized she had been doing this arithmetic for three days while he had been doing his own arithmetic, which consisted entirely of calculating the length of rope that would reach from a clerk to the ground.

"And when the army notices the proofs are missing," he said. "When Vogel remembers the pulper."

"Then we are finished, and so is everyone whose name is in that ledger." She set the proofs down and looked at him across the composing stone, across the little city of lead letters sleeping in their cases, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle. "Herr Brandt. You brought me a third of the truth tonight. Do you want to carry the rest, or do you want to go home and sign your name four hundred and twelve times and try to sleep?"

He wanted, with a ferocity that surprised him, to carry the rest.

* * *

They worked until the candle had burned down twice and been replaced twice from Petra's hoarded stock. The method they fell into was hers: she read the damaged proofs aloud — both the true impression and the ghost offset, cross-checking one against the other — while Brandt transcribed onto plain paper in his clerk's hand, that small relentless script drilled into him at fourteen, the one skill of his life that had never until tonight served anyone.

Where the record broke off mid-entry, they bracketed it. *[Name uncertain — confirm]*. *[Assessment figure illegible — Hoffmann?]*. *[Date of annotation missing — parish?]*. By the third hour the plain paper had grown a skeleton of the ledger, ribs of certainty and gaps of question, and Brandt found he could no longer look at the gaps without hearing the furnace in the chancery yard, where on Tuesday they had burned a cartload of pamphlets and the smoke had smelled, absurdly, of glue.

"There were two hundred and six names," he said at some black hour. "I docketed it. Two hundred and six entries, plus appendices of debts and confiscations. We have perhaps sixty confirmed."

"Then we need a hundred and forty-six more, and we need them before Falk's commission finishes its inventory." She rubbed ink from her thumb with a rag, an automatic gesture, a printer's gesture, her father's gesture — he saw the resemblance to the dead man suddenly, in the hands. "The commission arrived today. You saw them."

He had. Four wagons, an academic in a fine grey coat directing soldiers through the chancery annex with a cataloguer's courtesy that had chilled Brandt worse than any captain's bluster. Dr. Falk, the manifest said. Historiographer. Attached to the commission *for the scientific evaluation of subversive documentation*. Brandt had filed that phrase too. He suspected he would be filing it in his nightmares.

"Falk will burn what he cannot use," Petra said. "What he cannot use is everything that proves the other side existed. Which is the ledger. Which is us." She said *us* without emphasis, and Brandt felt it land in his chest like a stamp on wet paper, sealing something.

At dawn-grey o'clock they parted the work: she wrapped the proofs in oilcloth and hid them — he did not ask where; a clerk knows better than to know things — and Brandt folded his transcription into his coat, against his heart, which was either sentimentality or the safest pocket in Gundersheim depending on how the week went.

At the door she stopped him with a word.

"Herr Brandt. Why?"

He stood with his hand on the latch and considered lying again, and found he had exhausted his supply. "In 1831," he said, "a pamphlet was printed in this town criticizing the press ordinances. I read it. I was twenty-three. I agreed with every line of it, and I said nothing, and I filed nothing, and I told myself a clerk's silence was neutrality." He worked the latch open a centimeter, closed it again. "Your father printed that pamphlet. I learned that much years ago and never let myself learn more. I have been neutral for seventeen years, Fräulein Voss. It turns out neutrality is just complicity with slower handwriting."

Petra studied him for a moment in the last of the lamplight. Then she said, "Tomorrow, Kirchgasse. Bring your official questions. And bring paper — she'll talk faster than we can write."

He nodded. Outside, the sky was the grey of unsized rag paper, and the patrol boots on the square kept their rhythm, forty-one, forty-two, and Brandt walked home through them feeling, for the first time in nineteen years of service, like a man whose column finally balanced.

He was three streets from the chancery when he saw the notice freshly pasted to the guardhouse wall, its ink still tacky in the damp air: *By order of the military commission, all persons possessing knowledge of seditious publications are required to report such knowledge within seventy-two hours.*

Seventy-two hours. Below it, in smaller type, the penalty for concealment.

Brandt read it twice, memorized it — a clerk's habit, though this time the record he was keeping was of the thing coming for him — and walked on. Tomorrow, Kirchgasse. He only hoped the widow Kessler would answer her door faster than he had answered his conscience.
