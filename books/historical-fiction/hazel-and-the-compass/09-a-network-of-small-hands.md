## 9. A Network of Small Hands

Petra had always believed that a print shop was quiet at night. She had been wrong. It only ever seemed quiet to someone who did not know how to listen.

Now, standing at the composing stone with four candles shuttered behind the type cases so their light fell in thin blades instead of broad pools, she heard everything: the tick of cooling metal, the settling of the old beams above, the breathing of six people who should not have been in her shop after curfew and were. Widow Kessler sat nearest the stove with her hands folded, as if attending a funeral. Herr Brandt stood by the door with his coat still buttoned, one ear turned to the street, his requisition papers spread on the nearest case like evidence he could not stop confessing to. The baker's son — Jakob, though everyone else called him the boy — crouched over a proof sheet with his lips moving, committing columns of names to memory the way other men committed psalms.

And two apprentices, Liesel and Corin, fourteen and sixteen, sat cross-legged on the floor sorting misprinted fragments into piles as neatly as they sorted type: *ledger pages here, parish records there, the widow's letters in the shoebox.*

"Again," Petra said quietly. "From the top."

Jakob closed his eyes. "Vogel, Matthias. Cooper. Age thirty-one. Flagged March, forty-four, for the pamphlet affair. Two years' debt of dissent." His voice was flat and careful, a boy reciting arithmetic that might get him shot. "Kessler, Anselm. Bookbinder. Deceased — but his name stays on the list because the army doesn't strike a name until it strikes the man."

"That's enough for tonight."

"It isn't," Jakob said, and kept going.

Petra let him. That was the strange thing she was learning about conspiracy: it did not feel like fire and trumpets. It felt like this — lamplight, repetition, a boy's voice naming men who could not yet be named aloud in daylight. Her father had spent thirty years teaching letters to stand up straight in their cases. Now she was teaching them to lie low, to travel light, to hide.

She thought of Anton, asleep upstairs — or lying awake, which amounted to the same thing these days — and of the ledger's line beside his name that Brandt had reconstructed from a misprint: *Voss, Anton. Compositor. Flagged for assembly, sedition suspected.* Suspected. The word had a smell to it, like wet gunpowder.

"Tomorrow," she said, when Jakob finally ran out of breath, "Liesel carries the first finished quire to the widow's. Sewn into her apron lining. Corin takes nothing — he simply walks past the chancery annex twice and tells us what wagons come and go. Nobody carries anything they cannot afford to lose. Nobody knows more than their own small piece."

"And you?" Widow Kessler asked, mild as milk. "What don't you know, child?"

Petra looked at the brass compass lying on the stone, its lid shut, its engraved name turned face-down. "I don't know how this ends," she said. "But I know it doesn't end in that annex furnace."

* * *

Corin walked past the chancery annex at half past seven in the morning, an armful of clean rags for the Voss shop slung over his shoulder, whistling something tuneless, a boy with no business anywhere.

He counted without appearing to count. One wagon at the annex door, canvas-backed, guarded by a corporal who was warming his hands and paying attention to nothing but his fingers. Two soldiers carrying document boxes from the side entrance — he noted the boxes went into the wagon, not into the furnace shed, which meant today was transport day, not burning day. A clerk he didn't know checking a list against the boxes. And Dr. Falk's commission cart, the one with the fine painted panels, standing under the archway where it had stood for three days now, like a fat spider that had decided the web was comfortable.

He turned at the fountain, came back, and passed the annex a second time. This time he allowed himself to look at the furnace shed — a low brick building against the annex wall, its chimney breathing a thin grey thread even at this hour. Always lit. They kept it hot the way a cook keeps a stew going, ready for whatever the day brought.

A soldier called out, "You there. What's in the sack?"

"Rags," Corin said, and opened it without being asked, showing the folded printer's wipes, ink-blackened, smelling of turpentine. The soldier recoiled from the smell and waved him off with two fingers, and Corin walked on whistling his tuneless whistle while his heart hammered so hard he could feel it in his teeth.

That was the whole art of it, Petra had told him. Be boring. Be the most boring boy in the Rhineland. Soldiers remember interesting faces; they forget dull ones instantly.

By nine he was in the shop's back room, reporting in a low rush while Liesel sewed. He had expected praise. What he got was better and worse: Petra listened, nodded once, and said, "Good. Same route tomorrow. Don't vary it — variation is what gets people noticed." No praise at all. Just trust, issued like coin, and he found he wanted to earn more of it the way he'd once wanted festival sugar.

He was nobody. Six months ago he'd been nobody and knew it and hated it. Now he was nobody, and it was the most useful thing about him.

* * *

Liesel carried names in her apron the way other girls carried sweets.

She had stitched a false lining herself, watching her grandmother's hands do the same for smuggled tea during the blockade years, and the seam was so fine that Widow Kessler herself had run a thumb along it and pronounced it worthy of a convent. Inside it rode the first finished quire: eleven pages, forty lines each, every name set in ten-point brevier by Petra's own hand, printed on the shop's proof press in runs of two — one to keep moving, one to keep hiding, so that no single seizure could kill the record.

The walk to the widow's house took eleven minutes through streets full of soldiers and ordinary terror. Liesel had learned to read the street now, the way she read galley proofs: the patrol at the corner meant delay by the fountain; the empty stretch before the churchyard meant walk slowly, look bored; Frau Imhoff's step meant smile, greet her loudly about the weather, because Frau Imhoff repeated everything she heard to anyone in a uniform.

"You're pale, child," Frau Imhoff said. "Are they feeding you at the Voss shop?"

"Bread and ink, mostly," Liesel said, and the old woman laughed and let her go.

At the widow's door, Liesel was admitted by the side entrance, and the quire changed hands in the hallway without either of them looking down at it — a bundle passed like a dish of borrowed flour. Widow Kessler fed her soup anyway, because a girl who walks past patrols deserves soup, and while Liesel ate, the widow took the pages into the parlor.

They were hidden in a bookbinder's board — Anselm Kessler's last unfinished job, a folio Bible cover he had died before completing. The widow slit the hollow between the boards' outer layer and the stiffener, laid the folded quire inside, and glued it shut with paste she mixed herself, plain flour and water, the same paste that held every book in town together. Then she set the great Bible cover upright on the shelf among Anselm's other unfinished works, where it had sat gathering honest dust for two years, and stepped back.

"There," she said to the room, or perhaps to Anselm, whose portrait in tintype watched from the mantel with the patient severity of all dead husbands. "You hid proof sheets under your very bed and got yourself killed for it, and I told you then it was clumsy work. Look how it's done properly."

Her grief had become a craft too, Petra realized when she heard the report that evening. Everyone in the network had one. Corin's was boredom. Liesel's was needlework. Jakob's was memory. Brandt's was paperwork — the terrible, beautiful paperwork of the occupation itself, turned inside out. And the widow's was this: the domestic arts of concealment, learned across a long marriage to a man who never explained anything, practiced now with a steadiness that made Petra ashamed of her own shaking hands.

"We need somewhere else," Petra said. "One hiding place is one grave."

"I know," the widow said. "I've written to my sister in Bingen. She has a press-shed and a dead husband of her own. Grief keeps house well in that family." She looked at Petra over her spectacles. "And you? Where will yours go, if they come for the shop?"

Petra touched her pocket, where the compass rode. "Somewhere my father prepared," she said, not knowing yet if it was true.

* * *

Jakob memorized the way his father kneaded dough: by rhythm, by weight, by the feel of a thing being right.

His father thought he was learning accounts. In a sense he was — he sat in the bakery's back room each evening with the shop ledger open on his knees, and behind the legitimate columns of flour and lard he copied, in a hand so small it was nearly code, whatever fragment Petra had given him that morning. But copying was only the beginning. Copying could be seized. So every night he read his own transcription until his eyes swam, then burned the paper in the oven's ash-box, and the next morning recited the column back to Petra or to Corin, word for word, name for name, date for date.

*Hoffmann, Georg. Wheelwright. Flagged September, '46. Debt of dissent, one year, commuted to surveillance.* He could say them in his sleep now. Sometimes he did; his father had taken to waking him gently when he mumbled, and once had stood a long moment in the doorway listening to his son recite strangers' names in the dark, and had said nothing at all, only gone back downstairs, and the next morning there had been a second roll on Jakob's plate, buttered, which was as close as his father came to saying *I know, and God forgive us both.*

It was Jakob who noticed the discrepancy. He lay awake on the eighth night running the column against itself the way he checked the till, and found that the reconstructed list held thirty-eight names where Brandt's earliest proof-sheet fragment had implied thirty-nine. One man missing. Not lost — missing, as in never entered, or entered and deliberately dropped.

He brought it to Petra at dawn, embarrassed, certain he'd miscounted, ready to be sent home.

She didn't send him home. She pulled the candle close, checked his figures twice, and then sat very still for a long moment with her thumb pressed to her lower lip.

"Thirty-nine," she said finally. "There's a name that isn't surviving because it was never supposed to survive. Someone struck it before the army ever saw the ledger." She looked up at him, and Jakob saw something in her face he hadn't seen before — not gratitude exactly, but recognition, the look of a master printer finding a perfect letter in a bad casting. "You understand what you just did?"

"Heat expands the metal," Jakob said, bewildered. "Dough rises. Lists don't change themselves."

"No," Petra said softly. "People change them. And that means one of the men who handled this ledger is not what he seems."

* * *

Brandt came last, as he always did, entering the back room with the air of a man arriving at his own trial. He had begun to look thinner. The occupation had given him a nervous cough that he suppressed whenever a superior was near, and the suppression had become so constant that even alone now he swallowed the cough rather than permit it, his body colonized all the way down.

He brought the requisition duplicate files, as instructed — the carbon-paper world of manifests, storage inventories, transport authorizations — and he laid them out on the composing stone with fingers that trembled only slightly less than they had on the day he signed the seizure receipt.

"The army's own records confirm twenty-two of the names," he reported. "Their paperwork is thorough. They are building a case against every man on that list, and they are filing it properly, because proper filing is how clerks stay alive." He gave a small, awful laugh. "I have spent my career learning exactly how a man is destroyed by documents. It seems I am to spend what remains of it learning how he is saved by them."

"Then learn fast," Petra said, without heat. It was not cruelty; it was the register the network ran on now, brisk and unsentimental, tenderness expressed entirely through competence. "The transport lists. When?"

"Not before the new moon. There are procedures." He hesitated. "And there is the matter of the thirty-ninth name."

Petra's head came up sharply — Jakob must have talked, or Liesel, the news traveling through their small hands faster than any official channel. "You know about that?"

Brandt was quiet for a moment. Through the wall came the faint rhythmic knock of the proof press, Corin working late.

"When the guild ledger was compiled," he said carefully, "there was a review. Names could be struck by petition. Most petitions were refused. One was granted — I saw the marginal note before the annex took custody. Struck by order of..." He stopped. His throat worked. "By order of the office I serve."

"Whose name?"

"That, Fräulein Voss, I do not know. The entry was cut from the page — physically cut, with a knife, cleanly, by someone who had access before me." He met her eyes, and in his exhausted face was something almost like fear offered as a gift. "But I know this much: whoever struck it protected a man on your list. And whoever holds a compass your father left hidden may find that the road it points down passes through that question."

Petra's hand went again to her pocket. The brass was warm from her body, the engraved name she still could not read without her chest tightening.

That night, after the others had gone out one by one into the curfewed streets, after she had banked the stove and shuttered the candles and listened to the shop settle into its seeming-quiet, she set the compass on the composing stone beneath the last candle and turned it over, and for the hundredth time read the engraving — *F. Reiner, Frankfurt* — and beneath it, in her father's cramped hand on the slip of paper she'd found folded inside the lid, the words she still could not make behave:

*When the record burns, ask the needle where the letters went east.*

She had assumed it was a direction. North, south, some escape route, some map.

But Jakob had taught her something tonight without meaning to. Lists don't change themselves. Words don't hide unless someone hides them. And a compass maker does not engrave a city's name on brass for the sake of the needle.

She turned the instrument in the light and, for the first time, looked not at the needle but at the card beneath it — at what she had taken for decoration, the fine engraved filigree around the rim — and saw that the filigree was not decoration at all.

It was writing. Tiny, dense, running in a ring like a ring of type waiting to be composed.

And the first legible word of it was *Reiner*.
