## 10. The Compass Points East

The note had been waiting for her all along. That was the thought that kept returning to Petra Voss as she sat in the cellar with a candle stub guttering on an upturned crate and her father's letter unfolded across her knees — the thought that he had known, somehow, that she would come looking, and had arranged his secrets in the order in which she would need them.

She had found the note three days after the compass itself, tucked inside the hollow spine of her father's Bible, which was a joke so unlike him that it took her a full evening to understand it. Her father had not opened that Bible in thirty years except to press flowers or flatten receipts; she knew because as a child she had been the one sent to fetch it from the high shelf when the parish clerk came calling, and she had once, out of childish curiosity, fanned its pages and released a small avalanche of paper: a theatre ticket from 1817, a lock of hair tied with thread, a bill of lading for a shipment of red cloth that no shop in the town had ever sold. She should have understood then. Children understand nothing, and are forgiven for it.

Now the note lay open under her eyes, written in the cramped leftward-slanting hand he used only for things he did not want read aloud.

*Petra* — it began, which was strange enough, because he addressed letters to her formally, *Lieber Fräulein Tochter*, as if affection were a debt he dared not incur. *If you are reading this, then I am past explaining myself, and the thing in the box must explain for me.*

The thing in the box. The brass compass, sitting now on the crate beside the candle, its lid closed, dull gold in the weak light. She had carried it down here at dusk, wrapped in her apron like contraband, though there was no one to see and the soldiers on the square took no interest in widows' errands. The cellar smelled of damp stone, size glue, and old ink — the smell of every winter of her childhood — and it struck her, not for the first time since the requisition, that the army had taken the ledger and the annex had gone up in smoke and Anton's name sat somewhere in a soldier's list like a coin in a strongbox, and yet the most dangerous object in the town might be this small disc of brass that fit in her palm.

*You will have noticed,* the note continued, *that the needle does not seek north. Do not oil it. Do not polish the face. Turn the outer ring until the third mark aligns with the arrow cut into the bezel, and then look not at the needle but beneath it.*

Below this instruction, in the same cramped hand, came a line that made her set the note down and press her palms flat against her knees.

*I was not always what you believed me to be. Forgive me the disguise. It kept you fed and it kept me alive, and I would make the same choice again, though God knows it cost more than money. The road east is still open. The men on the line are still true. Trust the widow. Trust no one who asks twice.*

There was no signature. There was instead a small stamped mark, pressed so lightly into the paper that she needed the candle held close to see it: a circle, and within the circle a hand pointing — east.

Petra sat for a long moment in the dark-smelling cellar, listening to the drip of the eaves somewhere above, and felt the ground of her life shift by a degree so slight that only everything built upon it registered the change.

Her father had been a type-cutter. A good one, a quiet one, a man whose hands were stained to the second knuckle and whose opinions, when he offered any at all, were of the mildest and most weatherproofed kind. He had paid his guild dues. He had doffed his cap to the chancery clerks. He had voted, insofar as such votes existed, the way the magistrate wished, and he had taught his daughter that the way to survive a bad harvest of liberty was to keep your rows straight and your mouth shut. This was the man she had buried in March. This was the man she had mourned without reservation, because there had been nothing in him to forgive.

And now: *I was not always what you believed me to be.*

She picked up the compass.

It was heavier than it looked, as good instruments are. The case was scratched from decades of pockets; the hinge worked smoothly, oiled by some hand more recent than her father's death — his own, presumably, in those last weeks when he had known he was dying and had said nothing about anything. Inside, the face was not the face of an ordinary compass. Where the cardinal points should have been engraved she found instead a ring of small characters, letters and numerals interleaved, and at the center a needle that swung lazily, indifferently, settling wherever it pleased like a drunkard shown the door. Not north. Never north. She had watched it for hours over the past days, holding it level on the kitchen table after the shop was shuttered, and it pointed nowhere useful — once toward the stove, once toward Anton's empty chair, once toward the window and the river beyond it, as if mocking her.

Do not look at the needle, her father had written. Look beneath it.

She turned the outer ring. It moved with a fine ratcheted click, forty stops or so around, and each stop brought a different pairing of the engraved characters against fixed marks on the inner plate. The third mark — she counted carefully, twice — aligned with the arrow cut into the bezel, a scratch so shallow it might have been damage, might have been deliberate, might have been both.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. But when she tilted the compass toward the candle flame, angling it the way one angles a mirror, the light fell through the glass and caught something beneath the dial plate that no amount of direct looking would show: lines. Engraved lines, hidden under the printed card of the dial, visible only when light passed through at an angle — a watermark of brass, a ghost map lying underneath the instrument's honest face.

Petra forgot to breathe.

She rotated the compass slowly, chasing the angle, and the ghost map assembled itself piece by piece in the shifting light. A coastline — no, not a coast, a river, broad and confident, running diagonally across the dial. Tributaries. Small circles where tributaries met the main stream, and beside certain circles, tiny stamped symbols: an anchor, a millwheel, a church spire. And along the river's course, east and slightly south, a chain of minute points, each point connected to the next by a hairline scratch, running from a symbol near the top of the dial — a symbol shaped like two crossed hammers, which she recognized, because it hung painted on the sign outside her own door — down and eastward until the lines converged on a final mark at the dial's edge: a stylized tower, and beneath it, in letters almost too small to read, the word *FRANKFURT* spelled out in the old style, half-worn by thumbs.

The river was the Main. The towns along it were the towns along it. The route ran from her father's own doorstep, town by mill by ferry crossing, all the way to the free city where the presses printed what the principalities forbade.

She set the compass down very carefully, as one sets down something sleeping.

*The men on the line are still true.*

A courier line. All those years. All those years of the shop's ordinary business — playbills, indentures, hymnals for the parish, price lists for the corn factor — and threaded invisibly through it, her father had been a link in a chain running east. She thought of the odd shipments she had catalogued as a girl: crates marked as type metal that weighed too little, parcels of "waste paper" that went out on the Frankfurt barge and never came back, the occasional stranger who stayed a night in the loft and left before dawn and whom her father described, always, with the same unconvincing phrase, *an old apprentice of mine, passing through.* She had been eleven. She had believed him. She had believed him for twenty years.

And the smuggled reformer — the name engraved inside the compass lid, *E. Hartmann, 1832*, the name the Widow Kessler had finally given substance to when Petra had brought the instrument to her kitchen and watched the old woman's face go white and then grimly, terribly calm — Hartmann had been carried out of the country on this line, or one like it, after the crackdown of '32, passed hand to hand through cellars and grain barges and haylofts until he surfaced in Frankfurt and lived to print again. Her father had carried him, perhaps. Had stood, maybe, exactly where Petra now stood, holding this exact compass, learning the same secret in the same lamplight. The thought arrived with a force that was not quite grief and not quite pride and occupied the territory between.

She thought of her mother, dead fifteen years, who had never once asked about the waste paper.

She thought of herself, setting type at the composing stone while her father read the news aloud and edited it as he read — softening this, omitting that, and she had assumed it was prudence. It had been practice. He had been teaching her, all those evenings, how to handle dangerous words without flinching, and she had never known, and now he was in the churchyard under the yew and could never be asked, and the asking had become the ache at the center of her days.

*Forgive me the disguise.*

"You kept us fed," she said aloud, and the cellar took the words and gave back silence. "You kept Anton fed." And Anton — the thought landed cold, the way the worst thoughts do, arriving already dressed in consequence — Anton had inherited none of this. Anton shouted on street corners and wrote fiery columns for pamphlets printed by other men and believed, with the whole ardent surface of himself, that he was the first Voss to put his neck out. He did not know the family trade went deeper than type. He did not know the network existed. If the soldiers' lists swallowed him —

She made herself pick up the note again. There was more on the reverse, written smaller, denser, the hand of a man writing quickly by candlelight who expected his reader to be doing the same.

*The ring has four settings besides the false ones. Third mark: the route. Seventh: the safe houses, keyed to the signs they keep — look for the anchor at Seligenstadt, the mill at Steinheim, the spire at Höchst. Eleventh: the words to say at each door. Fifteenth:* — and here the handwriting tightened further — *the eleventh setting is yours alone. I will trust you to know when. Do not use it before you must. The compass serves memory, not navigation. A needle points; a record remains. Keep the record, child. Names are the only country that cannot be annexed.*

Names are the only country that cannot be annexed.

She turned the ring to the seventh mark and read the ghost map again, slower, committing it to parts: the crossing at the weir, the mill with its wheel on the north bank, the ferryman's cottage whose garden grew — she smiled despite herself, a wet-eyed smile — rosemary, which her father had always claimed was for cooking. The eleventh setting she did not touch. Some doors a daughter is not ready to open, and her hand knew it even if her mind did not.

The candle was drowning in its own wax. Upstairs, faintly, she heard the shop door — Anton, back late from the bridge meetings, smelling of pipe smoke and other men's arguments, calling out her name with the particular brightness he used now to disguise how afraid he was. She should go up. She should bank the fire, scold him gently, listen to half of what he said and refuse the rest, as she always had. Routine was a kind of armor; she had believed that all her life, and the belief was dying hard.

But first she folded the note along its old creases and slid it back where it could not be found by anyone who did not already know to look. Then she took up the compass, and instead of closing it, held it flat one last time above the dying flame, and let the light fall through, and looked not at the route, not at the safe houses, but at the word at the end of every line the instrument contained.

Frankfurt. Free presses. Men who would print a list of names if someone could bring them the list — and would print it in an edition of thousands, and sew it into the world where no commission, no historian, no burning annex could ever gather it all back up.

The ledger was gone. The proofs were scattered. But names, her father had written, and she understood now that he had written it to her specifically, across the grave, across the years — names were the only country that could be rebuilt, so long as one person carried the map to the presses.

He had not merely left her a shop. He had left her a road.

Petra closed the compass, wrapped it in her apron, and stood in the dark listening to her own heart slow itself into resolve. Then she blew out the candle and climbed the stairs toward her brother's voice, carrying the route east in her pocket and the beginning of a plan assembling itself behind her eyes, detail by detail, the way she set type — one character, one line, one page at a time.

Anton was standing at the hearth, coat still on, a crumpled paper in his fist and a look on his face she had never seen before — not the bright recklessness of the bridge, not the forced cheer of the last weeks, but something older and colder. He held the paper out to her without a word.

It was a printed notice. Fresh ink. An order for assembly, signed by the occupation command, naming the time and place — and at the bottom, among the required witnesses and sureties, a summons for questioning, addressed to *Voss, journeyman printer, of this town.*
