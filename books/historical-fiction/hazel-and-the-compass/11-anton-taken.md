## 11. Anton Taken

The meeting was held in the back room of the tannery, where the smell of lye and lime covered everything and made a man's eyes water so badly that no one could be sure who was watching whom. Anton Voss had come to think of that stink as a kind of safety. You could not weep in a tannery yard; you could only blink. It kept the men honest, or at least it kept them from sentiment.

There were eleven of them that night, crowded between stacked hides and the great wooden vats, and the single candle they were permitted by their own caution threw shadows that climbed the walls like things alive. Herr Wegener, the saddler, had brought news from Mainz. Old Krämer had brought wine, which he poured into tin cups with a hand that shook more than the candle did. And Anton had brought what he always brought: his own voice, and the memory of every argument their father had ever lost with the censor's office, filed away and sharpened for use.

"You cannot petition them," Anton was saying, when the door came open. "Petitioning is a courtesy they extend to themselves. You write to a government as you speak to a deaf uncle — loudly, slowly, and knowing already what he will answer."

There was laughter, brief and low. He would remember the laughter afterward. That was the cruelty of memory, he learned in the weeks that followed: it preserved the wrong things. Not the sound of boots on the stair, but the laugh of the saddler's boy — not a boy at all, he corrected himself, a man of thirty with grey coming into his whiskers — not the cold, but the taste of the wine, sour and thin, going down as the first lantern light broke under the door.

They came in fives. Anton counted them without deciding to; the counting was something his father had put in him, the printer's habit of tallying whatever passed before the eye. Five through the door, five more behind, lanterns raised, muskets unhung but not yet levelled. An officer in a grey coat, younger than Anton expected, with a face like a man reading a list — because he was reading a list. Anton saw the paper in his gloved hand, saw the way his thumb moved down it, and understood before the officer spoke whose names were on it.

"Anton Voss." Not a question. The thumb had stopped.

"Here," said Anton, absurdly, as if answering a roll call at school, and heard the word come out of him in another man's voice.

They did not tie him. That frightened him more than irons would have. They took his arm above the elbow, one on each side, in the grip of men who did this nightly and had grown bored of it, and walked him out through the tannery yard past the vats, past the hides hanging like flayed weather, past young Wegener standing against the wall with his hands open and empty and his face gone the colour of the lime pits. No one shouted. That was the worst of it. Eleven men, and not one shout, only the shuffling quiet of people watching arithmetic happen to someone else.

The night outside was very clear. Anton had a moment, walking between his escorts down the lane toward the square, to notice that the stars were out in unusual number, that frost was forming on the bucket rims at doorways, that somewhere a dog was barking in the flat, untroubled rhythm of a dog that barks every night at nothing. The world went on being ordinary. He had always supposed that catastrophe would arrive on a changed earth, under different skies. Instead the sky was the same sky, and his breath hung white in front of him, and a sentry at the corner glanced up from warming his hands and looked away again with the studied indifference of a man who has decided not to see what he is seeing.

* * *

The room they put him in had been a grain store before the requisition. He knew it because he had drunk beer in its office once, years ago, negotiating an order of broadsheets with old Mahler, who had since emigrated to Basel and was, Anton thought with a strange stab of envy, probably asleep right now in a warm bed above a free press. The sacks were mostly gone. A few remained, shoved into a corner, and the men who were already there had dragged them together into a kind of nest against the wall. There were nine of them in the room. He recognized four by sight and three by name, and the last two he would come to know better than he wanted to over the days that followed.

"Voss," said Krämer, already there, already grey-faced, patting the sacks beside him. "Sit. The floor takes the heat out of you faster than you'd believe."

Anton sat. His body performed all the necessary functions of sitting without any instruction from him. Somewhere in the building a door closed, and the sound of it — heavy, iron-fitted, final — arranged itself in his chest alongside his heartbeat, so that for a while he could not tell which was which.

"How many do they have?" he asked.

"All of them," Krämer said. "Wegener. The two Fischer brothers. The schoolmaster from Plittersdorf, God help us, he was only visiting his sister." The old man rubbed his face with both hands, and Anton watched the gesture and understood that Krämer was not rubbing away fatigue but checking, as a man checks a wound, that his face was still his own. "They read the names off the ledger. Off the guild ledger, Anton. Your name is in it twice, I'd wager. Once for the debts of dissent and once for the press."

"Three times," Anton said, "if they count the pamphlet I denied in March," and was surprised to hear himself attempt a joke, and more surprised when two of the strangers laughed — short, choked laughs, the laughter of men standing at a graveside, but laughter. It went around the room like a coal passed hand to hand. For a moment they were nine men in a cellar telling jokes, and the door was just a door.

Then the lantern in the corridor passed their barred window, laying bars of moving light across the floor inside, and everyone stopped talking at once, and did not begin again.

* * *

The days in the grain store had no shape. That was the thing no one had warned him about — not the fear, which he had expected and which turned out to be survivable, arriving in waves like weather, but the shapelessness. Meals came when they came, bread and a soup with more salt in it than anything else, carried in by a soldier who would not meet anyone's eyes. Water came in a pail that served for drinking and washing and the other necessity, and shame came with it the first time, and then, mercifully, stopped coming. Men talked and stopped talking. Someone sang very quietly on the second night, an old vineyard song, and got three lines in before Krämer touched his knee, and the song folded itself away into silence like a letter returned to its envelope.

Anton filled the shapelessness the only way he knew. He set type in his head.

It was their father's discipline, taught to him across the composing stone when Anton was eleven: *If you cannot sleep, compose. Compose your catechism, compose the Psalms, compose the letter you are too angry to send. A compositor who can hold a page in his mind is never unarmed.* So Anton composed. He set the town's grievances in twelve-point and locked them in a chase. He set his father's obituary properly this time, since grief had rushed him at the funeral and he had never done the old man the justice of good spacing. He set a letter to Petra — began it a dozen times, distributed it differently each time, justified the lines in his head until they stood straight as soldiers, and every version ended the same way, with the same sentence he could not make fit the measure: *I should have listened to you about dinner.*

Because that was the argument they'd been having for a year, the gentle persistent war between them. Anton talked politics at the table and Petra cut type through it, her graver whispering along the steel, and she would say, without looking up, *Anton, eat,* or, *Anton, the bridge is not a philosophy,* and he had loved her for it and dismissed her for it in the same breath. She believes the world can be kept small enough to be safe, he had told himself. She believes if we only keep our heads below the windowsill, the firing will pass us by. And he had believed the opposite with all the confidence of a man who had never yet been made to test it.

Now, in the grain store, listening to the Fischers argue in whispers about whether the commandant would ship them to the fortress at Mainz or hold them for the commission from Cologne, Anton found his certainty dissolving into its components and discovered that most of it had been vanity. His sister had not been timid. She had been precise. She had weighed the world every morning the way she weighed a piece of type, and found it wanting, and gone on working anyway, which took more spine than shouting in tanneries. He thought of her alone now in the shop, and of the soldiers in the square, and he had to stop composing and put his head against the cold wall and breathe until the wall stopped seeming to move.

"Your sister," Krämer said quietly beside him. It was not quite a question.

"She'll manage," Anton said. "She's the capable one."

Krämer made a sound that might have been agreement. Around them the others slept or pretended to, nine men breathing in the dark at slightly different speeds, and Anton lay among them and composed the same letter over and over, and never once found the closing line.

* * *

On the fourth day — or what he believed was the fourth day; time in the grain store moved like water under ice, detectable only by its pressure — they called his name.

Not through the door. An escort came in for him, two soldiers and the young officer with the list, the one whose face was a page. The room went still in a particular way that Anton felt rather than heard: nine men each privately relieved it was not yet him, and ashamed of the relief, and unable to look at him as he stood up. He understood it completely. He forgave it completely. He would have done exactly the same. But understanding did not make the doorway any less dark as they walked him through it.

The corridor ran the length of the building. At its end a second door, and beside that door, leaning against the plaster with the patience of furniture, a sergeant eating an apple. The apple was red and glossy and obscene in the grey corridor. Anton fixated on it — the small wet sounds of it, the precision with which the sergeant cut slices with a clasp knife — and realized he was doing it deliberately, that his mind had seized on the apple as a drowning man seizes driftwood, because to think about the door at the end of the corridor was more than he could manage and remain upright.

The office beyond the door had been a counting room. Ledgers still lined one wall, spines outward, confiscated and catalogued in somebody's neat hand. A desk. Two chairs. Behind the desk sat a captain he did not know, and beside the desk, half in shadow, sat a man he did know, and the knowing of him dropped through Anton's stomach like a coin down a well.

Herr Lenz. Notary. Church elder. A man who had dined at their father's table twice and praised the widow Kessler's plum cake. A man whose signature appeared on charitable subscriptions beside the names of men now sitting in the grain store. He wore civilian clothes and an expression of mild regret, the expression of a man attending the funeral of someone he respected but did not love, and there was a paper open in front of him covered in writing that was not in a clerk's hand at all but in a cramped, hurried script Anton recognized with a sickening lurch: informer's shorthand, denunciation, the private alphabet of betrayal.

"Anton Voss," the captain said. He was older than the list-officer, weathered, with a voice pitched for parade grounds softened now to something almost conversational, which was worse. "Sit down. This need not take long. Herr Lenz has prepared some notes, and I have a transport manifest to complete by Friday, and you have an opportunity here that your companions squandered. Sit."

Anton sat. His legs performed the function again without consulting him. Across the desk the captain turned a paper around so it faced him: the guild ledger page, copied fair, and there in the middle column his own name, *Anton Voss, typesetter,* and beside it in the third column the old debt of dissent — his father's, actually, incurred the year Anton was fourteen, inherited like the shop and the type cases and the smell of ink — and beneath it, added recently in a different ink, a second entry. His own. March. The pamphlet he had denied.

"Do you deny it still?" Lenz asked. His voice was soft, apologetic, the voice of a man asking after someone's health. "Denial is a debt too, Anton. It accrues interest. Look at me. We knew each other once, at your father's table. I am trying to save you the fortress."

And Anton looked at him — at the notary's soft hands on the informer's pages, at the apple-red regret in his face — and opened his mouth to answer, and found that the answer had already been set, locked in the chase, justified and true, waiting in him all this time like a page ready for the press.

The captain leaned forward and said, "Begin with the names of the men at the tannery," and the room waited.
