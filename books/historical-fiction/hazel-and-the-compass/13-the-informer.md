## 13. The Informer

The count came up short on a Tuesday.

Petra had made the count a ritual by then, as fixed in her hands as the morning fire or the evening bolt. Forty-one pages of the reconstructed ledger existed in the world — she knew the number the way she knew the depth of her own type cases, without looking, without thinking, the knowledge living somewhere below thought in the muscles of her fingers. Eleven pages locked in the widow Kessler's flour chest. Nine sewn into the lining of Frau Brenner's mattress at the mill house. Seven carried piecemeal in the sleeves and bodices of apprentices who crossed the town twice daily with deliveries of ordinary printed matter, wedding announcements and grain prices, their contraband riding against their ribs like second hearts. And fourteen here, in the cellar beneath the shop, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of her father's old paper chest.

Forty-one. She counted them every Tuesday and Friday, and for six weeks the number had held like a well-set line of type, immovable, true.

On Tuesday the fourteenth of March, she counted forty.

She counted again. Her fingers had gone strange, too careful, touching each packet as though it might flinch. Forty. She unwrapped the oilcloth entirely and spread the packets across the paper chest's lid, laid them out like specimens on a dissecting table, and went through them one by one against the master list she kept in her head because she dared not write it down. The page with the Hagenow brothers — present. The page of the bridge crowd, ninety-three names in Anton's own hand before his hand was taken from him — present. The parish records copied by the sexton's daughter, the debts of dissent transcribed from Brandt's misprinted proofs, the widow's letters with their coded marginalia —

The missing page was one of the widow's. The fourth letter, the one that named the men sheltered in the spring of '31, the crackdown before this one, the names of men who had since grown older, some of them fathers now, whose sons would inherit both the debt and the danger. It was among the most damning pages they possessed, and it was gone.

Petra sat down on the cellar step and let the cold of the stone come up through her skirts into her spine, and she did not move for a long time.

* * *

The question was not *who*. That was the terrible clarity of it, the thing she understood within the first minute of sitting still. The network was small — eleven people, twelve counting herself, each one chosen the way she chose type: tested, fitted, trusted to hold under pressure. No stranger had touched the copies. Which meant the page had not been lost. Lost pages were found again; lost pages turned up behind chests, in the bottoms of baskets, swept into corners. A page that vanished cleanly, without trace, without the small honest chaos of accident, had been *taken*, and it had been taken by one of the twelve.

And there was only one of the twelve who had reason.

Johannes Meyer. The baker's son. Nineteen years old, broad-shouldered from the kneading troughs, with flour permanently worked into the creases of his knuckles and a way of listening that made you feel your words were being weighed on a scale. He had joined the network in February, brought in by his father, old Meyer, who had known her father thirty years and wept openly at the funeral. Johannes carried messages between the shop and the mill house three times a week. He was reliable, punctual, careful — and he had a father whose name appeared on the third page of the reconstructed ledger, flagged not for conscription but for something older, a fine levied in '31 that had never been paid because the family could not pay it, a debt of dissent that the army's clerks could resurrect at will, converting an old grievance into a new arrest.

Petra had seen the arithmetic of it before she'd wanted to. She had seen it and set it aside, telling herself that fear was no proof, that everyone in the network had someone on those pages, that suspicion was a luxury the work could not afford.

Now the work itself had told her otherwise.

* * *

She said nothing on Wednesday. She said nothing on Thursday. She rose, fired the press, set type for a funeral announcement — real work, safe work, the kind that kept the shop looking like what it pretended to be — and all day the question moved through her like a splinter working toward the skin.

Confront him, and if she was wrong, she had wounded an ally and taught him that trust in the network was conditional, revocable, a thing that could be withdrawn in a cellar by lamplight. Confront him, and if she was right, she gained nothing unless he confessed — a guilty boy would deny, deny, deny, and then warn whoever held the copy that discovery had begun. Protect him — say nothing, watch him — and every day the page stayed loose was a day it could reach the commandant's desk, a day the names on it could be read aloud in a room where men with lists and seals decided what those names were worth.

There was a third option, and she hated it most of all, which was how she knew it was the serious one. She could go to the widow Kessler. The widow had buried a husband who died hiding proof sheets; the widow had looked at Petra across her kitchen table six weeks ago and asked, *what do you intend to do*, in a voice that accepted any answer except none. The widow would know what to do with a suspected informer. The widow had lived through '31. She knew what networks did when they sprang leaks.

But the widow's answer would be the old answer, the harsh one, the answer written in her husband's grave. And Johannes Meyer was nineteen years old and had flour in the creases of his knuckles, and Petra kept seeing him the way she'd seen him last Sunday, laughing in the bakery doorway while his father boxed rolls for the widow, saying *Frau Kessler, you'll be the death of my margins* — some joke about bread and books that Petra hadn't caught, that nobody had caught, that had been nothing, just a boy being young in a town where being young had become a crime.

Thursday night she climbed to the loft above the shop where Anton used to sleep, back when sleeping above the press was still only poverty and not risk, and she stood in the dark among his abandoned things — a jacket, a clay pipe, three books stacked spine-out — and she tried to think the way he thought. Anton argued politics over dinner once, loudly, until she refused to listen, and she had spent months believing his loudness was a weakness. But loudness was only honesty wearing armor. Anton would have walked to the bakery tomorrow morning and put the question to Johannes's face, because a face was evidence too, and because waiting was its own kind of betrayal — of the eleven who were true, whose lives hung on the same thread as the false one.

She was not Anton. But she was her father's daughter, and her father had taught her that a compositor's first duty was accuracy: you did not guess at a letter, you *looked*, you took the piece in hand and turned it to the light.

Tomorrow she would look.

* * *

Friday morning she sent for him the way she always did, with a bundle of printed matter that needed carrying to the mill house — genuine work, an errand Johannes had run a dozen times. He came at half past seven, stamping snow off his boots in the doorway, breath steaming, cheeks red from the cold, and he smiled at her the way he always smiled, easy and open, and Petra felt the whole weight of her suspicion suddenly absurd, unbearable, a stone she wanted to put down.

Then she saw his eyes go, for half a heartbeat, to the cellar door.

It was nothing. It was less than nothing — a glance, a flicker, the kind of look anyone gives a closed door in a familiar room. But she had spent twenty years reading type upside down and backward, training her eye to catch the single wrong glyph in a page of ten thousand right ones, and the glance registered in her the way a wrong letter registers: instantly, totally, with a small cold click like a composing stick closing on metal.

"Come down," she said. "I need help moving the paper stock."

He hesitated. One breath's worth. Then: "Of course."

The cellar was lit by a single lamp and smelled of ink and cold earth and the particular dryness of stored paper, a smell that had been the smell of safety her whole life. Johannes ducked under the low beam and stood among the shelves, big hands hanging at his sides, and Petra lifted the lamp so its light fell between them instead of on either face.

"The fourth letter," she said. "The widow's. The one naming the men sheltered in 'thirty-one."

She watched him the way she read proofs. His shoulders did not change. His breathing did not change. But his hands — his hands curled, slowly, the fingers closing toward the palms as though around something that wasn't there anymore, and she thought: *there, there it is, the body confessing what the mouth is arranging to deny.*

"What about it?" he said.

"It's gone."

The silence lasted three seconds. She counted them the way she counted em quads. On the third second his face did something complicated — relief and terror passing over it together like weather — and then he said, "Gone? Petra, are you sure? Maybe it was misfiled, maybe—"

"I don't misfile." Her voice came out flat and quiet, quieter than she intended, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Eleven people have touched that page. You're one of them."

"You think I—" He stopped. Started again. "You think I took it."

"I think your father's name is on the third page," Petra said. "I think the army can turn a thirty-year-old fine into a cell. I think a copy of the fourth letter left this cellar sometime in the last five days, and I think you know more about it than you're saying, and I need you to tell me which of those things is true, because eleven other people are betting their lives on the answer."

His jaw worked. He looked at the lamp, at the shelves, at the cellar door behind him — measuring, she realized with a lurch, the distance to it, whether he could get past her, whether it had come to that. Then something in him simply gave, the way dough gives under a fist, all at once.

"I didn't take it," he said. "I swear it on my mother. But I know who did."

* * *

He told her standing up, in the lamp light, with the smell of ink all around them, and the telling took less time than the walk from the bakery to the shop.

His father. Old Meyer, who had wept at the funeral, who had vouched for his own son into the network — old Meyer had gone to the commandant's office nine days ago. Not to inform. To bargain. He had learned — Johannes didn't say how, and Petra hadn't needed to ask; the baker sold bread to the garrison kitchen, and garrison kitchens talked — that the commission's clerks were compiling their own lists, cross-checking the seized guild ledger against parish records, and that old Meyer's unpaid fine of 1831 sat in the file like a loaded chamber. He had gone to ask, humbly, what a man might do to have such an old matter *overlooked*. And the captain had smiled and said that overlooked matters required *demonstrations of loyalty*, and had mentioned, casually, that rumors spoke of seditious printing still active in the town, and that a man with a son strong enough to carry heavy loads might hear things.

"He didn't tell me," Johannes said. His voice had gone rough. "I found out because he started asking me questions. Where does the bundle go. Who waits at the mill house. How many pages. He thought — God forgive him — he thought if he learned enough to give them, they'd leave us alone, and if he never learned anything true, no harm done. He was trying to be innocent and guilty at the same time. So I—" He swallowed. "So I gave him a page. A false one. I wrote it myself, two nights, copying the widow's style of entry, names and dates, all invented. I let him believe he'd stolen it from my coat while I slept. He took it to the captain on Monday. And the real page—"

"Is where?"

"Gone." He met her eyes at last, and there was no evasion left in his face, only exhaustion and fear and something underneath them that looked terribly like shame. "My father took the real one too. He found it folded in my coat along with the false one — I was going to destroy it, I swear, I was waiting for the right night — and he must have thought it was his chance to give them something true after all, something that proved his loyalty better than my forgery ever could. He took both. I couldn't stop him without telling him everything, and if he knew the truth — that his own son fed him a lie to protect the network — Petra, he'd break. He'd break completely. He's not a traitor. He's a frightened old man who loves me more than he fears them, and that's the most dangerous thing in this town."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere overhead, faintly, the shop clock struck eight, and Petra stood very still and listened to the sound die away, and understood that everything had changed shape while staying exactly the same size.

The page was already in the commandant's possession. Had been since Monday. Four days. Four days in which no soldiers had come to the shop, no arrests had been made, no doors kicked in — which meant either that the page was being verified, quietly, name by name against parish records, or that it was already being acted upon, slowly, in the army's own unhurried manner, men marked for later, for transport night, for the moment when the lists would be settled all at once.

Or — and this thought arrived last and coldest — the captain had recognized the forgery for what it was, and was letting the game run, watching the network through old Meyer's frightened hands, feeding it rope.

"Your father doesn't know the page was false?" she asked.

"He thinks he gave them the widow's letter. He's been sick with it all week. He thinks he's bought our lives with other people's names."

"And the captain?"

Johannes shook his head. "That's what frightens me. Papa says the captain thanked him. Thanked him, Petra. Paid him. Slid coins across the desk like it was a market transaction. And then said — these were his words — *we may have further use for a man with eyes in the bakeries.*"

Petra closed her eyes. In the dark behind them she saw the whole board at once, the way she sometimes saw a full forme of type before setting it: old Meyer, compromised and owned, a paid ear planted in the one household that touched every message route; the forged page in the commandant's files, a lie the army believed or didn't; the real page gone wherever frightened men hide things they cannot use and cannot burn; and above it all, Dr. Falk's commission arriving in the town with its civil questions and its burning annexes, its patient appetite for archives.

The informer was not the boy standing in front of her. The informer was a frightened father who loved his son, and the army had made him a key without his knowing what door it opened.

"Here is what happens now," Petra said, opening her eyes. "You go home. You say nothing. Your father tells you nothing more, because from tonight you make sure he has nothing true to find — your coat carries nothing, your pockets carry nothing, you become the dullest courier in Germany. Every scrap that touches you from this hour passes through my hands first, and most of it will be blank paper. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"If the captain sends your father hunting again, we feed him what we choose. If the real page surfaces — if your father hid it somewhere, if he panics and tries to return it, if he goes back to that office — I want to know within the hour. Can you do that? Watch your own father?"

Johannes's face crumpled and reassembled itself, the way dough does, and when it settled it was older. "I've been watching him for a week," he said. "It's how I found out about the forgery. Yes. I can do it."

"One more thing." She lifted the lamp higher, and the light steadied between them. "The others. The widow, Frau Brenner, the apprentices. They don't learn of this. Not yet. Fear spreads faster than ink, and one whisper of an informer and the network strangles itself. This stays in this cellar — you, me, and God, and God gets the abridged version."

He almost smiled. Almost. "And if it goes wrong? If they find out I knew and said nothing?"

"Then they'll be angry with me," Petra said, "because the decision was mine. Go. Take the bundle to the mill house. Walk slowly. Be boring."

He shouldered the bundle of printed matter — harmless, all of it harmless, and that was the point now — and ducked under the beam, and his boots faded up the stairs and out into the street, and Petra stood alone in the cellar with the lamp and the thirty-nine remaining pages and the cold crawling up through the floor stones.

Thirty-nine. The number had changed forever; even recovered, the ledger would never again be whole in her mind, would always carry this wound in its margin. She began wrapping the packets back into the oilcloth, and her hands were steady, and she was grateful to them for that.

It was only when she reached the bottom of the chest that she saw it — a fold of paper tucked beneath the false bottom's lip, placed there recently, placed deliberately, in a hand she did not recognize. Not her father's. Not the widow's. Not Brandt's careful clerk's script.

She unfolded it by the lamp. Three lines, in pencil, in a woman's hand:

*They have the letter. They also have a clerk who reads. Ask Brandt what he signed on the ninth of March, and ask him why the manifest for the prisoners' transport bears two dates.*

Petra read it twice. Then a third time, her lips moving silently over the words, while upstairs the shop clock struck the quarter hour and somewhere across the snowy roofs of the town, in a house that smelled of bread, an old man lay awake beside a page he had stolen to save his son — and in another house, a clerk who signed things sat at his desk with a lamp burning far too late, and Petra Voss understood that the leak in her network was the smallest crack in a wall that was beginning, everywhere at once, to come down.
