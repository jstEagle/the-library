## 14. Confession at the Mill

The old flour mill below the Weinberg road had not ground anything since before the boy was born, but the smell of it stayed in the timbers — a ghost of sweetness gone sour, rye and dust and the faint animal breath of grain that had rotted in its sacks thirty years ago. Josef Brandt — no relation to the clerk, though the whole town had joked about it once, before jokes became dangerous — had chosen the mill because no one chose the mill. The army did not requisition ruins. Dr. Falk's commission did not interview ghosts. Even the crows had better places to be.

He was seventeen, and he had been carrying his guilt for eleven days now, and it weighed more than the sack of flour he had hauled up this road every morning of his life since he was nine.

He arrived first. He had wanted to arrive first, so that he could arrange himself against the ruined millstone like a man with nothing to hide, hands loose at his sides, face arranged into the blankness he practiced in the shaving mirror. But when he heard footsteps on the frozen lane outside — one set only, light, unhurried, deliberate as a typesetter counting ems — every arrangement fell out of him at once. His hands would not stay loose. He pressed them flat against the cold stone behind him and felt the mill's whole dead machinery shudder once, or imagined it did.

Petra Voss came through the doorway the way she must have come through her shop door every morning of her life: sidelong, checking the light, checking the street behind her, checking him last because she had already decided what he was and was merely confirming it.

She wore her father's coat. She always wore her father's coat now, the shoulders stuffed with wool to make them fit, and something about seeing her in it — the dead man's bulk around her living smallness — made Josef's confession climb his throat before he had said a single word.

"You came," he said. Stupidly. As if there had been any question.

"You asked me to come." Her voice was even. Not cold — he would have preferred cold. Cold was a wall, and you could stand against a wall. Hers was worse: it was the voice she used, he realized, when she was setting type. Measured. Careful. Each word lifted into place with tweezers. "You wrote that it was urgent. You wrote it in your father's hand, which was clever, and stupid, because your father hasn't written anything but bread orders since October."

"I taught myself his letters." It came out before he could stop it, an absurd little boast, and he saw her register it — *he can forge* — filed away somewhere behind her eyes with everything else she knew. "I'm sorry. I didn't know how else to get you alone. If I came to the shop—"

"Widow Kessler sleeps with a poker by her bed," Petra said. "The apprentices are watched. Herr Brandt is watched most of all." She let that sit a moment. "So. The mill."

"The mill."

The snow came through the broken roof in slow threads, settling on the millstone between them, on the place where the grain used to pour. Josef looked at it instead of looking at her. He had rehearsed this. On the walk up from the bakery, in the dark, with his breath standing white in front of him, he had rehearsed every word, ordered his sins chronologically like a good Lutheran, and now, standing in the actual presence of the woman he had sinned against, the order collapsed and all of it came at once, tumbling, undignified, a boy's confession and not a man's.

"It was me," he said. "The missing pages. The copy from the Kessler house — the one with the Hoffmann boys and the schoolmaster on it. I took it. I copied it, and I took it."

* * *

He had thought, all these years, that he understood fear, because he had grown up with a father who woke before the fires were lit and stood in the bakery doorway listening for boots, and because Josef had inherited that listening the way other boys inherited a chin or a temper. Fear was the sound of boots. Fear was his father going quiet whenever soldiers passed the window. Fear was the ledger itself, though Josef had never seen it — just the idea of it, the town's whole memory of who had said what, written down in a clerk's fair hand, waiting like a loaded gun in a drawer.

But he had never understood what it was to be afraid *for* someone until the night the sergeant came to the bakery.

That was where the confession went, once it started — backward, into the telling, and Petra Voss stood among the millstone and the falling snow and let him tell it, her face giving away nothing, and Josef found that the telling was both unbearable and the only thing left that he could bear.

"They came on the twelfth. Two of them and the sergeant, and they didn't knock the way soldiers knock, they knocked the way neighbors knock, friendly, and that was worse. My father had flour to his elbows. They made him keep working while they talked. That was the worst part, I think — they talked to him about *bread*. Quarters for the garrison, prices, whether he could guarantee delivery to the bridge fortification twice daily. Very civil. And then the sergeant picked up one of my father's knives off the board — a lame knife, dull, wouldn't have cut butter — and he held it and he looked at my father's hands and he said, 'Bakers are hard to replace in winter. Sons are easier to come by.'

"And then he put the knife down and they left. And my father didn't say anything. He baked all night and he didn't say anything, and I stood in the corner by the starter crocks and I understood that they knew. Not about your pages — about *me*. About the meetings. Somebody had told them the baker's son runs messages, and they didn't need to arrest anybody. They just needed to say one sentence holding a knife."

Josef's voice cracked on the last of it and he hated himself for the crack, straightened his back the way his father straightened dough, kneaded himself back into shape.

"So I went to them. That's the part you need to hear first, before anything else, because everything else follows from it and you should know what kind of coward you're dealing with. I went to the sergeant myself. Volunteered. I told him I could get access to papers — that people trusted me because I'm nobody, because I deliver bread, because a baker's son is furniture in every house in this town. And I told him I'd bring him whatever I could carry if he crossed my father's name off whatever list it lives on."

The snow kept coming down through the roof. Somewhere under the floor, in the mill's flooded undercroft, water moved against stone with a sound like slow breathing.

"He laughed at me," Josef said. "The sergeant. He said lists don't work that way, names don't come off, my father's name isn't even on anything yet — that's how little my father matters to them, Petra, he doesn't matter enough to be on a list — and then he said, 'but bring me what you find anyway, boy, and we'll see what your diligence is worth.' So I hadn't even sold you for anything. That's the joke. I risked all of you for nothing. For a maybe. For the chance that someday, if I brought them enough paper, my father might matter just enough to be spared."

He heard how it sounded. He had heard how it sounded every night for eleven days, lying awake above the ovens with the heat rising through the floorboards and his own words replaying in the dark.

"But I didn't give them what I copied," he said, and here his voice steadied, because this part — this one part — was true and clean and he clung to it the way a drowning man clings to the one plank that floats. "The page from the Kessler house. I copied it, yes. I sat in the privy with a pencil stub and copied all of it, the Hoffmanns, the schoolmaster, the columns, everything, my hand shaking so badly the copy looks like a child's writing. And I gave the copy to the sergeant. But the original —" he reached inside his coat, slowly, so she could see every movement, so she would know he was not drawing anything but paper — "the original I hid. It's been hidden since the twelfth. It never left my possession, and it never entered theirs."

He held it out. Folded twice, creased wrong, damp at one corner from where he had carried it against his chest through eleven days and eleven nights of ovens and frost and fear. Petra did not take it at first. She looked at it the way she must have looked at a line of type someone else had set — reading it, weighing it, finding the errors.

Then she unfolded it, right there in the falling snow, and read it, and Josef watched her face while she confirmed that the names were all there, that nothing was lost, that the schoolmaster's name — which existed nowhere else now, he knew, having asked without asking — had survived its journey inside a coward's coat.

* * *

"You hid it," she said at last. "For eleven days."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And there it was — the question he had dreaded more than any accusation, because accusations had answers and this one, he feared, did not. He opened his mouth and closed it. The rehearsal had covered anger, contempt, the cold arithmetic of betrayal and punishment. It had not covered *why*.

"I don't know," he said, and meant it, and then, because lying to her now would be the true and final treason: "No. That's a lie. I know. I couldn't do it. When I had the copy finished and the original in my other hand, and it was time to leave one and take one — I stood in the Kesslers' privy and I couldn't choose. So I cheated. I gave them the lie and kept the truth. I thought—" He laughed, and it was a horrible sound in the dead mill. "I thought I could serve both sides forever. Keep my father safe and keep your names safe, carry water for the sergeant and bread for you, and never let either side see that the boy in the middle was splitting in half. That's what I did, Petra. Not treachery exactly. Something worse and more ordinary. I tried to remain two people."

She folded the page along its wrong creases, carefully, taking ownership of it with her fingers, and he understood that the page was forgiven even if he might not be, and this was somehow harder to bear than anger would have been.

"The schoolmaster," she said. "You're certain the sergeant has only your copy?"

"Certain as I can be. He doesn't read well — I've seen him hold papers upside down. That's partly why I got away with it. And he keeps his papers in the barracks strongbox, and I've watched, and he doesn't cross-reference. He counts them. I gave him four pages' worth of copying over these weeks and he counted four pages and looked satisfied." Josef swallowed. "Which means he thinks he knows things he doesn't actually know. Which means eventually he'll act on something wrong, and when that goes badly for him, he'll start asking where the copies came from. And the answer will be the baker's son. I've bought my father perhaps two weeks, and after that I've bought myself a cell, or worse, and I've brought all of that down on your network besides. So." He spread his hands. The millstone received the gesture without comment. "Now you know everything. I'll write it out and sign it if you want it for your records. I'll stand wherever you tell me to stand and say whatever you tell me to say. I only ask—" His voice failed on the request, and he had to start again. "My father. Whatever happens to me. My father's name stays off the transport manifest. That's all. That's the whole of what I came to beg for."

* * *

For a long moment Petra Voss said nothing at all, and Josef Brandt discovered that silence, in the mouth of someone who worked with words for a living, could be heavier than any speech.

She moved, finally — not toward him but around the millstone, her boots crunching the snow that had gathered on the floor, one full slow circuit, the way she walked, he thought, when she was thinking. Composing. Setting something in her mind the way she set it in a composing stick, piece by piece, until it would hold.

"Do you know what I did when I found Anton's name in the ledger?" she said. "Before all of this. Before the soldiers, before any of it. I found his name flagged in my father's guild book — conscription, old debts of dissent — and I read it three times, and then I closed the book and I said nothing. For days. I watched him argue politics across the dinner table and I kept my mouth shut, because saying it aloud would make it real, and I chose the comfort of routine over my own brother's warning." She stopped her circuit and faced him across the stone. "So when I weigh what you did, Josef, understand that I am weighing it against myself. Against the cowardice I already know from the inside. Yours was bigger and costlier than mine. But it came from the same place — the wish to protect what you love by pretending the danger isn't watching you. And I cannot hang you for a sin I've committed in smaller coin."

"Petra—"

"I'm not finished." But her voice had changed; the typesetter's measure had cracked somewhere, and what came through the crack was tired, and human, and young. "Here is what I can offer you, and it isn't absolution, so don't take it as that. I forgive you. I do — I can feel it being true even as I say it, and I hate that, because I would rather hate you, hating is simpler and hating doesn't keep anyone up at night. I forgive you what you did. What I cannot do, ever, is forget it. Every time a message crosses this network now, some part of me will check it twice. Every new person the widows bring in, I will wonder. You've done that. You've installed the doubt in me permanently, the way a flaw is installed in a font — every cast from that matrix will carry it forever. That is your sentence, if I have the right to sentence anyone, and I probably don't, but I'm the one holding the names so I suppose I do."

She held out the recovered page.

"And this is your penance, if you want a real one. You know the sergeant's habits, his routines, the way his mind works — badly, and upside down. You are going to help us use that. You're going to keep delivering to him, Josef. Copies. Wrong ones. Beautiful, plausible, useless ones. You bought two weeks with your treachery; you're going to spend them buying us time instead, because in"—she paused, calculating, and Josef saw her do it, saw her count days like she counted lines of type—"in roughly a fortnight there is a transport manifest that has to go wrong in very specific ways, and a man named Brandt whose nerve we cannot afford to test, and a column of prisoners including my brother who have to arrive in Frankfurt *after* forty copies of a ledger have already left this town in grain sacks. You are inside the enemy's confidence, such as it is. God help us both, you're the only one who is."

Josef stared at her. The snow settled on the millstone between them, on the folded page, on everything.

"You want me to spy for you," he said. "On them. Using the trust I built betraying you."

"I want you to spend your sin," Petra said. "It's the only currency it has."

And Josef Brandt, seventeen years old, flour in his blood and fear in his bones, began — unbelievably, uncontrollably — to laugh, the wild relieved laughter of a boy who has confessed his worst and been handed work instead of a rope, and Petra almost, almost smiled back.

Then the laughter died in his throat, because through the mill's broken roof, past the falling snow, he had caught sight of the lane below the Weinberg road — and standing at the bend, half-hidden by the bare hedge, motionless in the gray morning light, was a figure in an officer's greatcoat, watching the mill.

Watching them.

"Petra," he breathed. "Don't turn around. Don't either of us move."
