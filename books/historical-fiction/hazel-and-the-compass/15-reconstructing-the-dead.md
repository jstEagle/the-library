## 15. Reconstructing the Dead

By the third week of the occupation, the cellar beneath the Voss shop had become a room of candles and whispers.

Petra had not intended it so. It had begun with Brandt's proof sheets spread across her father's old workbench — misprints and spoiled pulls that the chancery clerk had smuggled out under his coat, folded wrong-side-out so no soldier searching him would see guild lettering. Then the widow Kessler had arrived one evening with a bundle of letters tied in butcher's twine, and after her came the baker with his son, who would not meet anyone's eyes, and after them two apprentices whose names Petra had never bothered to learn until they were carrying her conspiracy in their sleeves.

Now there were seven of them on any given night, and the workbench was not large enough, and the single lamp had been supplemented by three candle stubs rescued from the church sacristy, and the air was thick with tallow smoke and the sour-sweet breath of men who had walked through a garrisoned town after curfew to sit in a hole in the ground and copy names.

"Vogel," said the baker, his eyes closed, his lips moving as though in prayer. "Christian Vogel, cooper, born 1801. Flagged for the speech at the Rathaus steps — no, wait." He opened his eyes. "The petition. It was the petition, spring of '46. The speech at the Rathaus was Hoffmann."

"It was both," said Widow Kessler quietly. She did not look up from the letter in her lap. She read everything twice, once aloud for the record and once silently, checking herself against herself, because she had learned — from a husband buried without a stone — that memory was a thing that lied to you kindly if you let it. "Vogel spoke at the petition meeting and again at the steps. My husband was at both. He wrote to me about both."

The baker bowed his head and began again.

Petra sat apart from them, at the composing stone she had carried down into the cellar in three pieces over four nights, reassembling it in the dark the way her father had taught her to assemble it blind — *a compositor knows his stone like his own face, Petra; if you need light to find your type, you are not a compositor, you are only a person holding letters.* She had a galley now, and a case, and drawers of type brought down piecemeal: enough twelve-point roman to set perhaps two pages at a time before she had to redistribute.

She was setting the names. That was understood among them, though no one had assigned it. Brandt copied documents; the widow verified; the baker and his son memorized and carried; the apprentices ran messages and kept watch above. But the names themselves — the actual letters that made the actual men — those passed through Petra's hands alone.

There was a reason for it, and the reason was grief, and she had stopped pretending otherwise.

* * *

Her father had died in September with ink under his fingernails, which was correct, which was the only way a Voss should die. She had grieved him the way she did everything: thoroughly, privately, and with her hands busy. She had sorted his effects. She had kept the shop running. She had found the compass in November and had not yet forgiven him for it.

But this — setting the dead into type — was a different order of mourning, and she had not been prepared for it.

It happened the first time she composed a line and reached for the sorts and realized whose name it was. *Lukas Meyer, bookbinder, aged forty-four, debtor to the guild for dues unpaid during the ban, marked also for a pamphlet.* She knew Lukas. He had bound her father's ledgers for twenty years. He had once repaired, free of charge, the hymnal her mother had ruined with rain the day of her own wedding, and he had done it while telling Petra a long involved story about a goat in the cathedral square, and he had died in February of the fever, and here were his eleven letters waiting in the case, and her fingers went to them the way fingers go to a wound.

*L-u-k-a-s space M-e-y-e-r.*

Eleven letters. Forty seconds of work. A whole man.

She set the line. She locked it in the galley. Her hands were steady, because her father had trained her hands to be steady, and somewhere in the middle of that steadiness something broke open in her chest and she understood what she was doing: she was burying Lukas Meyer properly, at last, in lead instead of earth, where the fire could not dig him up.

After that night she never set a name carelessly again. Some nights she set only six or seven lines in three hours, because each name required its own silence first — a beat of remembrance, a small internal bow — and the others learned to work around her pauses without asking about them. The widow Kessler understood best. Once, when Petra stopped with her hand in the case over the letters *H-e-i-n-r-i-c-h*, the widow simply lowered her voice and continued reading in the tone people use in churches, and when Petra drew the sorts out and set them, neither of them said anything at all.

* * *

The proofs were the skeleton. Brandt had brought eleven sheets in that first delivery, and more since — misprints from the guild's own press, pulled years ago and kept, God knew why, in a drawer Brandt was supposed to have emptied in '46. They covered perhaps a third of the ledger's flagged entries: names, trades, the grounds of the marking. Conscription lists, dissent debts, pamphlets attributed, assemblies attended. Enough to prove that the ledger existed and what shape it had.

But a proof sheet is a fragment, and the army held the original, and the army had started burning things.

So the flesh came from elsewhere. From the widow's letters, mostly — her Heinrich had written to her from hiding in '44 and '45 with the anxious thoroughness of a man who suspected he would not survive to testify, listing who had printed what, who had paid, who had been seen at which meeting. From the parish registers, which Father Anselm copied in his own hand and passed to them inside a Bible, page by page, baptisms and marriages matched to names so they could confirm ages and trades. From the baker's memory, which turned out to be extraordinary — he could not read well, and so he had spent thirty years memorizing instead: accounts, orders, prices, faces, debts. He remembered the guild ledger from the one time he'd been summoned before it, and he remembered the men around him in that room, and he could recite, eyes shut, half the conscription list of '44 because he had lain awake for a month afterward repeating it to himself in terror.

"What good is fear," he said once, sheepishly, when the widow complimented him, "if you don't keep something from it."

And from Anton — this was the part Petra carried like a stone under her ribs — from Anton's own recollections, written in a ciphered hand and passed to her through the apprentices on the two occasions a month she was permitted to visit him at the shop's front room under the eyes of the billeted lieutenant. Anton could not know why she asked, so carefully, so obliquely, about the summer meetings, about who had signed the address to the Frankfurt parliament, about whether Weber the saddler had been present on the fourth of June or only the fifth. He answered readily, glad of the interest, proud almost, narrating the revolution he believed was still coming — and each answer fed the ledger that might save him, and he did not know, and Petra smiled across the table at her brother and carried his ignorance home like a coal she could not put down.

*Weber, Josef, saddler, born 1812. Present 4 June, address to Frankfurt signed in his shop. Marked.*

She set that line herself, though the widow could have read it faster. Some lines she would not delegate even to the future.

* * *

They argued, sometimes. That surprised her at first — she had imagined conspiracy as seamless, all of one will — but grief is not seamless and neither is memory, and on the ninth night the baker and Brandt nearly came to blows over whether young Peter Hoffman had been marked for the petition or for the debt, and whether it mattered.

"It matters," Brandt said, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, and everyone went still, because they had all learned that Brandt's composure was thin ice over deep water. "It matters because when — *if* — this ledger is ever read, it will be read as evidence. If we write Hoffman marked for sedition when he was only marked for debt, we have made a seditious man out of a poor one. We have convicted him ourselves. We are not copying the ledger, Frau Voss." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet and furious. "We are testifying."

That sentence changed the character of the work. Petra felt it land in the room like a dropped weight. Afterward the checking doubled. Every uncertain entry got a mark in the margin — a small cross in pencil, invisible at arm's length — meaning *disputed, verify, do not trust me here.* The widow began keeping a second document alongside the fair copy: a list of doubts. *Hoffman: petition or debt? Vogel: year of birth unconfirmed. Steiner: may have fled before the marking; if fled, entry should note it.* It was, Petra thought, the honestest document she had ever touched. A ledger of the ledger's own fallibility. Somewhere in Frankfurt, someday, some reader would find forty copies of the reconstructed register, and behind them, sewn between the pages of certain copies, these lists of uncertainty — because they had decided, all seven of them, in a candlelit cellar, that a record which claimed perfect knowledge would be just another kind of tyranny.

"We cannot give them the truth entire," the widow said. "So we will give them the truth plus our doubt, and let them finish the arithmetic."

* * *

On the twelfth night, Petra set the last name of the first full signature.

She had saved it. She had known for days that it was coming, the way you know a difficult passage is approaching in a piece of music, and she had arranged the evening's work so that the other entries around it were finished early, so that when it came she would be alone with it. The others withdrew by unspoken agreement — the baker first, then the apprentices, then Brandt, who hesitated at the foot of the stairs and then climbed them without looking back. The widow stayed. The widow always stayed.

The lamp was low. The cellar smelled of tallow and lead and damp stone and, faintly, of ink, because Petra kept a small tin of her father's ink down there though she had no press — she rubbed a fingertip of it along the edge of the composing stone each night before beginning, an old superstition, a blessing of the tools.

*A-n-t-o-n space V-o-s-s.*

Printer. Born 1820. Marked for conscription; marked further for the address to Frankfurt, for the meeting of the fourth of June, for distribution of prohibited pamphlets in the autumn of the disturbances. Debtor to no one. Son of Georg Voss. Brother of—

She stopped. Her hand hovered over the case.

The ledger did not record brothers. The ledger recorded men. But her hands knew a different form than the guild's; her hands had been setting text since she was seven; and there was room, if one composed tightly, for one more line, in smaller type, in a style no requisitioning officer would ever read closely.

She set it: *Brother of Petra Voss, printer, who sets this name.*

It was not evidence. It violated every rule of the neutral testimony Brandt had taught them. It was the single dishonest line in forty honest copies, and she did not care. Let the historians in Frankfurt puzzle over it. Let them understand, eventually, that behind every entry in their precious reconstructed register stood a person who had set it, and that the setting of a brother's name in one's own hand is a sentence no archive can parse.

She locked the galley. The quoins tightened under the key with the small decisive click she loved better than any sound in the world except, perhaps, Anton laughing.

"He would set his own differently," the widow said from her chair by the wall. She had watched the whole thing, and her voice held no judgment, only the mild curiosity of someone very old examining something very young.

"Yes," Petra said. "He'd have added something foolish. A motto. *Liberty or the Rhine*, probably."

"And you?"

"I'm not the one who has to survive being read," Petra said. "I'm the one doing the writing."

The widow laughed — softly, so the boards above would not carry it — and rose, and gathered her letters into their twine, and at the stair she paused. "Tomorrow, then. The fair copy goes to the copyists. Three hands, three houses, no one carrying more than four pages at a time."

"Four pages each," Petra agreed. "Forty copies total."

"Forty copies," the widow repeated, as though pronouncing a benediction, and her footsteps faded up the stairs, and the candle guttered, and Petra sat alone in the cellar with a galley full of the dead.

She ran her thumb along the locked type. It was cold and hard and perfectly, permanently itself. Fire could take paper. Soldiers could take ledgers. Historians could burn archives and write their tidy victors' accounts. But lead cast in a matrix held its shape, and a line of type distributed and reset and distributed again was a thing that could pass through fire a hundred times and come out the other side still spelling *Anton Voss, printer, born 1820.*

She thought her father would have understood that. She thought, for the first time since finding the brass compass, that maybe everything he had hidden — the compass, the note, whatever route it pointed toward — had been built on exactly this understanding: that the way you defeat a fire is not to hide from it but to make your words heavy enough, and numerous enough, and scattered far enough, that the fire cannot find them all.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the galley waited on the stone, patient as the dead, and upstairs in her mind she could already hear the next problem assembling itself like type in a stick: forty copies required a press, and the shop's press sat under guard in the requisitioned front room, and the courier line to Frankfurt was a rumor and a compass point, and the transport lists for the arrested men were posted on the chancery door every Monday morning.

Monday was two days away.

She climbed the stairs with the darkness closing behind her, and in her chest, dread and hope had begun, at last, to set themselves in the same line.
