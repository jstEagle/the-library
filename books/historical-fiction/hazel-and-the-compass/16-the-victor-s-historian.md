## 16. The Victor's Historian

Dr. Ernst Falk had burned archives in four towns before this one, and he had learned that the work went best when one treated it as scholarship rather than punishment. Fire was indiscriminate; a historian was not. A historian knew what deserved to survive and what deserved to be forgotten, and the difference between those two categories was, in the end, the whole of his profession. Everything else — the tramping of boots in chancery corridors, the young officers who enjoyed this sort of thing too much, the weeping clerks — was incidental.

He arrived in the printing town on a Tuesday in October, with two wagons, an assistant named Weber who read too many novels, and letters of commission signed with enough seals to open any door in the province. The town received him the way towns always did after an occupation: with a silence that had been rehearsed. Shopkeepers inclined their heads. Children were pulled indoors by their sleeves. At the inn, the innkeeper gave him the good room without being asked, which told Falk more about the temper of the place than any informer's report could have.

The occupation itself had gone quietly here, by all accounts. There had been drums at the bridge in the spring, a day or two of republican dreaming, and then the army had come and requisitioned what it needed and posted its orders, and the town had folded back into its habits like paper under a press. Falk found this encouraging. Towns that had fought were difficult to write about; they left too many contradictions. Towns that had submitted could be given clean narratives, and clean narratives were the foundation of a durable peace.

His commission was straightforward, spelled out in the covering letter he reread that first evening by candlelight: to compile the official account of the recent disorders in the Rhineland districts, establishing for posterity the sequence of events, the identity of principal agitators, and the disposition of seditious materials. In plain terms: write the history, and in writing it, finish it. An event was not truly over when the soldiers left. It was over when no document remained to contradict the account of it.

Weber appeared at the door with the inventory from the chancery annex.

"They've been keeping everything, sir," the young man said, with the tone of a man reporting a moral failing. "Ledgers, petitions, guild records, correspondence. Decades of it. There are boxes in the cellar that haven't been opened since the last crackdown."

"Of course they've been keeping everything," Falk said mildly. "That is what clerks do. It is why clerks exist." He closed the commission letter. "We'll begin with the annex tomorrow. Cellar first."

* * *

The burning took four days, and Falk conducted it the way he conducted everything: with lists.

The method was simple in principle and demanded care in practice. Anything that documented sedition — petitions for constitutional reform, club minutes, smuggled pamphlets, the correspondence of known agitators — was to be destroyed, because such documents made martyrs. Anything that merely recorded ordinary civic life — tax rolls, property transfers, the mundane traffic of a provincial town — could stay, because ordinary life was the ally of order; it suggested that history was made of harvests and fees rather than ideas. And anything ambiguous was to be brought to him personally. He did not delegate ambiguity. Ambiguity was where bad histories came from.

He stood at the yard behind the annex each morning in his good coat while soldiers fed the fire and Weber read out provenance from the tags tied to each bundle. The smoke rose straight up in the still air, gray against gray sky, and the smell drifted across the square toward the shops. Falk noticed that the townspeople altered their routes to avoid passing the yard, taking the lane behind the church instead, and he noted this too, in his working journal, as evidence of the population's disposition. He did not think of it as fear. Fear implied that the people understood themselves to be objects of the fire. He preferred to believe they understood themselves as its beneficiaries. History that hurt going down was like medicine: the bitterness proved the dose.

On the second day, Weber brought him a bundle tagged *Guild of Printers and Typefounders — registers.*

"The ledger's not among them, sir," Weber said. "There's a receipt. The guild register was seized as evidence during the requisition. Signed out by a Herr Brandt, chancery clerk."

"Then it's in the evidence store."

"It would be, sir. Except the evidence store was cleared in September. Anything not attached to a pending tribunal was marked for destruction." Weber hesitated, and Falk watched the young man decide whether to say the next thing. "The pending tribunals include the transport cases. Men being moved to Frankfurt. Their files are still live."

"So the ledger may still exist."

"It may, sir. Or it may have gone into the general burn in September, before anyone thought to preserve it. There's no way to know without checking the manifest against the ash."

Falk took the bundle and weighed it in his hands. Printer's guild registers were exactly the kind of document his commission existed to suppress: membership rolls, apprenticeship records, marks of work, fines levied for printing unlicensed matter. A register told you who could set type, and who had set it, and when. In a district that had produced pamphlets by the hundredweight last spring, that was not history. That was ammunition.

"We'll want it," he said. "Find out which it is. Burned or stored. If stored, I'll examine it before it goes anywhere near a fire." He paused. "And find out who kept the guild's other records. Printers are sentimental about their trade. There will be proofs somewhere, waste sheets, personal papers. Families keep things."

"Yes, sir." Weber wrote it down. Then, because he was young and read novels: "Do you ever wonder, sir, whether some of it ought to be kept? For the record. Objectively."

Falk looked at him with genuine fondness. "Every historian wonders that, Weber, until he understands what a record is. A record is not the past. A record is a claim about the past, made by someone, for reasons. Our task is to ensure that the claims remaining in the world are the ones consistent with the state that shed its blood to make them possible. That is not censorship. That is authorship." He handed back the tag. "Cellar next."

* * *

It was on the fourth day, with the annex cellar emptied and the yard fire down to its white heart, that he interviewed the printers.

He did it properly — that is, civilly. He sent a polite notice to the surviving members of the guild requesting their assistance with the historical record, and he received them in the chancery's best room, with coffee, one at a time, and asked questions that sounded like the questions of a curious scholar and were, in fact, a net laid over the whole town. Who had worked which presses. Where old matrices had come from. Whether there had been, in the years before the troubles, visitors from outside — men connected with the banned reformers, men whose names might appear in old registers under other trades. He listened to answers with his hands folded and his face arranged into attentive sympathy, and he let the silences do half the interrogating, because people filled silences, and what they used to fill them was almost always true.

Most of them told him nothing useful, either because they knew nothing useful or because grief had closed them like fists. One old journeyman wept about his late master and described the man's skill at cutting type in loving technical detail that was useless and, in its way, touching. A young widow of the trade talked steadily about ink formulas and would not meet his eyes. Falk catalogued each of them in his journal with a single word apiece — *empty, guarded, frightened* — and thought the town nearly finished.

The last interview of the day was a woman named Voss.

She came in at the appointed hour precisely, which interested him at once; most people came early, fawning, or late, defiantly. She was perhaps thirty, dressed in dark practical wool, with ink stains at her fingertips so habitual they seemed less like dirt than like coloring. She sat when invited, accepted coffee when offered, and looked at him with a directness that he logged even as he smiled at her.

"Fraulein Voss. Or is it — the register suggests a brother. Anton Voss. Your family ran the print shop on the market lane."

"My father ran it. Since his death, I have." She said it without emphasis, the way one reports weather. "My brother works the press."

"Your father was respected, I understand. Forty years at the trade."

"He was."

"And you cut type yourself? That is unusual in a woman."

"It is unusual anywhere, now." Something crossed her face — brief, private, gone. "Few can do it since the shortages. Good steel is scarce."

Falk unfolded his questions gently, one at a time. Had the shop printed unlicensed matter in the spring? She said it had printed what was commissioned and nothing else, and her voice did not change, and he believed her in the way one believes a well-set page: the composition was flawless, which was itself information. Had her father corresponded with printers in other towns, in Frankfurt, in the Palatinate? Correspondence, she said, was mostly invoices. Had he kept his old proof sheets and waste? Paper was expensive, she said; waste went back into the pot. Every answer was reasonable, every answer was smooth, and Falk found himself leaning slightly forward, the way a man leans toward a note held just slightly wrong in an otherwise perfect performance.

"Your father must have been a careful man," Falk said, "to run forty years in a regulated trade without ever once setting a line he shouldn't have."

"He was a careful man," she agreed.

"Careful men keep things."

"Careful men burn things, Doctor." She met his eyes then, fully, and for a moment he saw something in her expression that he had not seen in any face in this town — not fear, not defiance, but a kind of settled patience, the look of a compositor reading a line of someone else's type. "You'd know better than I what becomes of paper."

He laughed, genuinely, and poured her more coffee. "Touché, Fraulein Voss. Yes — I confess most of what crosses my desk ends in the yard behind this building. Though I'd argue it isn't destruction. Selection. The alternative to selection is a past so crowded with paper that no one can find the truth in it. I am, if you like, editing."

"Editing," she repeated, tasting the word.

"History is written by survivors, Fraulein. That has always been true. My commission merely ensures the survivors are organized." He smiled and closed his notebook, and saw her glance flick to it — a small motion, the eyes only, noted and released. "One last question. Your guild register was seized during the requisition. You'd have no objection to my consulting it? Names, dates, workmarks. Purely scholarly interest."

"The register belongs to the guild," she said. "If the army has it, the army knows best where it is."

"A diplomat's answer." He stood, and she stood, and he bowed the small bow one gives a tradeswoman, and she returned it with exactly correct depth, and he watched her leave through the tall window, crossing the square with an even stride, not hurrying, pausing once to adjust a parcel under her arm.

Weber appeared at his elbow with the day's list. "The Voss woman, sir? Anything?"

Falk considered. Everything about her had been smooth. Her answers, her manner, her timing. And that was precisely what troubled him, though he could not yet have said why, and so he did what he always did with a discrepancy too small to name: he wrote it down.

"Cautious," he said aloud. "Competent. Possibly nothing." He picked up his pen and added, in his neat scholar's hand: *Keeps her hands. Watch the shop.*

"Also, sir — news from the evidence store. The transport manifest for the Frankfurt prisoners is being drawn up. Anton Voss is on it. His file was flagged in the spring. Sedition, and an old debt of dissent from his father's time."

Falk looked out the window again. The woman was gone around the church lane now, the one the townspeople used to avoid his fire. The square was empty except for a boy sweeping the steps of the customs house, whose sealed rooms, he had been told that morning, held the last of the region's confiscated papers pending his review — including, quite possibly, a printer's guild ledger with the Voss name entered twice in two different decades.

"Then the account writes itself," Falk said pleasantly. "Brother transported, sister cooperative, town quiet." But the word *almost* had lodged somewhere under his composure, small as a splinter of type metal, and he found that evening, reviewing his journal by candlelight, that his pen kept returning to the same line: *No one burns paper that carefully unless there is paper worth burning.* He had assumed he had come to this town to finish its story.

It occurred to him, as the candle guttered, that the story might not be finished at all — that it might, at this very moment, be being written somewhere else, in lamplight, by other hands, and that the first draft of history he had been sent to compose was racing a second draft he had not yet found. He snuffed the candle and decided he would begin with the customs house.
