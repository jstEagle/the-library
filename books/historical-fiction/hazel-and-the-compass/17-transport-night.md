## 17. Transport Night

The order arrived at half past four in the afternoon, folded twice and sealed with more wax than it deserved, and Herr Andreas Brandt read it three times before his hands stopped enough to set it down.

*Transport of detained persons to be advanced two days. Departure this night, nine o'clock, from the market square. Manifest to be prepared by the undersigned clerk of the commission.*

Two days. The words sat on the page with the bland authority of all military paper, as though moving forty men two days early were a matter of no more consequence than advancing a delivery of flour. Brandt looked at the clock on the chancery wall — a dull, ticking thing he had wound faithfully every Monday for eleven years — and calculated what two days meant. Two days was the difference between the courier line being ready and not ready. Two days was the margin Petra Voss had asked for and believed she had. Two days was the width of a grave, if one were inclined to think in such terms, and Brandt had been thinking in such terms for weeks now, ever since the night he had carried misprinted proof sheets through the rain to a print shop that smelled of lamp oil and fear.

He read the order a fourth time, though there was nothing new in it. Then he blotted it, filed the original in the commission's strongbox where it belonged, and took out a clean sheet of commission paper.

His hand did not shake. That surprised him. He had expected — when this moment came, and he had known for some time that it would come — that his body would betray him first, that the tremor which had plagued him since the requisition would finally announce his guilt to any observer. But his hand was steady. His mind, in fact, had never been clearer. It was as though some part of him had been rehearsing this hour in secret, the way a man rehearses a confession, and now that the hour had arrived it simply stepped forward and took charge.

*Manifest of persons to be conveyed under escort,* he wrote. *Departure: ninth hour, Thursday.* And then, in the space where the date belonged, he wrote Thursday's date instead of Tuesday's.

It was such a small thing. A single alteration in a column of figures, the kind of clerical adjustment made ten thousand times a year in every customs house and chancery in the Confederation — corrections, amendments, the ordinary friction of administration. No guard would question it. No officer compared a manifest against its originating order; orders were for officers, manifests for clerks, and the two classes of men moved in separate orbits like planets that had agreed never to meet. By the time anyone discovered the discrepancy, if anyone ever did, the transport would be two days' worth of road from anywhere, and the discovery would implicate only the man who had signed the paper.

Him. Only him. That was the arithmetic he had done in the rain-soaked walk home last week, and he did it again now, sitting in the lamplight: whatever came of this, let it land on one head. He had a sister in Boppard who would grieve him, and a brother he had not spoken to since their father's funeral, and neither of them had done anything wrong. They would suffer the shame of association, which was unfair, but unfairness was the currency of the age and he had decided — God help him, he had actually *decided*, sometime between the proof sheets and tonight — that he was done paying his debts in that coin.

He worked through the manifest line by line. Forty-one names. He knew them all now, knew them better than he wished to: the names from the old guild ledger that the army had seized in the spring, the debts of dissent and conscription that the occupation had converted into cells in the converted granary behind the church. Anton Voss, printer, aged twenty-four — Brandt wrote the name and felt the peculiar vertigo of writing the name of a man whose sister he had come to know in lamplight and conspiracy, a woman who trusted him with her brother's life on the strength of nothing but his signature and his guilt.

There were other names too. Weber, the saddler, who had once published a pamphlet so mild that even Brandt had laughed at its timidity. The Fischer brothers, stonemasons, taken for attending a meeting that had in fact been a wedding supper. Old Kellner, sixty-one, whose inclusion on the list Brandt suspected was pure administrative tidiness — the commission disliked loose ends, and Kellner had been a loose end since 1832. Forty-one men, and every name a small weight on Brandt's conscience, and every weight long since converted into ballast for the ship he was now steering deliberately toward the rocks.

At seven o'clock the sergeant of the escort came for the manifest, a broad red-faced man named Hulse who smelled of the guardroom stove. Brandt handed it over and watched Hulse fold it without reading it, tuck it into his tunic, and leave, and only then did Brandt permit himself to breathe.

Then he put on his coat, took his hat and his stick, and walked out into the evening to commit his second crime of the day, which was telling a printer's sister that she had lost two days.

* * *

The shop was dark except for the cellar light leaking up through the floorboards, and when Brandt knocked the special knock — three, pause, two, devised by Petra because she said soldiers could never resist knocking in fours — the trapdoor opened and her face appeared in the square of yellow light, ink on her cheekbone, hair pinned up with what appeared to be a pencil.

"You've heard," she said, before he could speak. She had heard. Of course she had heard; drums carried, and the granary guards had been doubling watch since noon, and a town under occupation develops ears faster than any other organ.

"Nine o'clock," Brandt said, climbing down into the cellar. "Not Thursday. Tonight."

She stood very still among the type cases, and Brandt watched her absorb the blow the way a wall absorbs a thrown stone — no flinching, no sound, just the impact taken somewhere deep in the masonry. Around her the cellar told the story of the past month: composing sticks laid out in rows, proof sheets annotated in three different hands, the widow Kessler's copies sewn into oilcloth bundles, a parish register borrowed from Father Anselm and open to its baptismal pages. Forty-one names, or nearly, resurrected from memory and paper and the stubbornness of people who had no right to be brave and were brave anyway.

"Forty minutes," Petra said at last.

"The escort forms in the square at nine. They'll march the prisoners from the granary to the bridge road."

"Then we have less than an hour, and you shouldn't be here." She was already turning to the table, already sorting, her hands moving with the terrible efficiency of someone who had run out of time to waste any. "Brandt. The manifest. Is it done?"

"It's dated Thursday."

Her hands stopped. For a moment she looked at him across the cellar, and he saw something in her face he had not seen before — not gratitude, which he could have borne, but recognition, the look of one conspirator measuring another and finding the measurement sufficient. It was worse than gratitude. It bound him.

"If they discover it," she said quietly, "they'll hang you."

"They'll have to catch me first." It was meant as levity and came out as nothing of the kind. "Petra — the delay buys you two days, but only if the transport leaves believing it left on schedule. There will be no second chance. Whatever goes to Frankfurt goes tonight, or the falsification is for nothing."

"I know." She pressed both palms flat against the type case in front of her, the way one steadies oneself against the deck of a ship. "The copies are nearly ready. The sewing finished yesterday. It's the route that worries me — the mill courier went out this morning and hasn't reported back, and I don't know whether that means the line is compromised or merely slow."

"And your brother?"

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice. "Is in the granary. Yes."

"He doesn't know about—" Brandt hesitated. "About any of this? The ledger, the copies?"

"He knows what he needs to know, which is nothing." Her voice was perfectly level, and Brandt understood that this levelness had cost her something to construct. "If he is interrogated, he can honestly say he knows nothing, because I never told him. It was the only protection I could give him." She picked up a bundle of oilcloth, weighed it in her hands, set it down again. "Go home, Brandt. Be visible. If you're seen near the shop tonight, everything you risked on that manifest dies with us."

He climbed the ladder and paused at the trapdoor, looking back down at her — a small figure in a pool of lamplight surrounded by the resurrected dead of her town, forty-one names in type and thread, and above her head the ordinary ceiling of an ordinary shop in an occupied town on the eve of its erasure.

"Thursday," he said. "In the manifest, they believe it is Thursday. Someone should tell them it isn't."

She almost smiled. "Go."

* * *

At twenty minutes to nine, Anton Voss stood in the granary yard with thirty-nine other men and listened to the change of plan being read aloud by a lieutenant with a cold.

They had been told Thursday. Every man in that yard had spent the intervening hours in the particular torture of the condemned who are given dates — arranging farewells in the mind, rationing courage, telling themselves that Thursday meant two more dawns, two more bowls of the gray soup, two more chances for something to intervene. Now the lieutenant, sniffling, said nine o'clock tonight, and Anton felt the bottom drop out of the world with a sensation he would remember all his life: not terror exactly, but the sick lurch of a stair that isn't where the foot expects it.

Around him the men stirred like cattle sensing weather. Weber began quietly to pray. Young Fischer — the younger of the two brothers, barely nineteen — asked no one in particular whether anyone had informed his mother, and no one answered, because no one knew, because knowing things was precisely what none of them were permitted anymore.

Anton did the only thing he could do, which was what he had trained himself to do for weeks: he watched, and he memorized, and he kept his face empty. The escort was eight men and Sergeant Hulse, who had searched Anton on arrival with theatrical thoroughness and missed the pencil stub sewn into his coat seam because Anton had sewn it there for exactly such occasions. Lanterns. Ropes to link the prisoners in pairs. The bridge road, which passed within forty paces of the Voss print shop — Anton had marked that geography on his first day in the granary, mapping the town from memory the way he had once learned a forme of type, letter by letter, position by position.

He did not know about the manifest. He did not know anything, which was the gift his sister had given him, and yet standing in the yard with the lanterns swaying he found himself thinking of her anyway, thinking of the shop dark on his left as they would pass it, thinking: *she will be watching from somewhere, and I cannot even look.*

They formed up at nine. The ropes went wrist to wrist. The gate of the granary opened on the night, and the column moved into the square, and the boots of forty men and eight guards made a sound like slow surf on the cobbles.

As they reached the corner by the cooper's yard, a lantern passed close on Anton's right side, held by one of the younger guards, a heavyset fellow with chapped hands who had once, unaccountably, looked away during a search. Anton had spent three days cultivating that look-away. He had talked to the man about hunting, about dogs, about nothing at all, until the guard's attention had learned to slide off him the way water slides off wax. Now, as the lantern swung level, Anton brought his bound hands up as if to shift his rope, and pressed a small folded twist of paper into the gap between the glove's cuff and the man's sleeve — and the guard, blessedly, magnificently, did not look down, did not break stride, scratched his ear with the same gloved hand as though the paper were an itch, and walked on.

The note had taken Anton four nights to compose and one minute to write. It said, in characters small enough to fit inside a shirt button:

*They move us early. Whatever you intended — do it now. Do not wait. Do not come to the bridge. Burn this. I am proud to share your name. — A.*

He did not know if it would reach her. That was the whole of his situation, reduced to its elements: a scrap of paper traveling by glove toward a shop he could not look at, carrying the sum of everything he could not say. The column turned onto the bridge road. Somewhere ahead the river ran black under the stars, running north toward the low countries, south toward Frankfurt, toward presses that printed freely and men who read what they printed. Anton walked with his eyes fixed on the rope ahead of him, on the wrists of the saddler Weber swinging gently in rhythm with his own, and he counted the paces to the print shop the way he had once counted lines of type: thirty, twenty-five, twenty—

He did not turn his head. It cost him more than the whole march had cost him, and he paid it, and kept walking.

* * *

The guard's name was Lutz, and he had a wife who bought bread from the baker whose son copied pages for Petra Voss, and it was through this improbable chain — wife, bread, son, cellar — that the note arrived at the print shop door at half past ten that night, damp from a glove and folded to the size of a thumbnail.

Petra read it by the cellar lamp while the widow Kessler held the candle steady and Brandt's manifest lay on the table between them like a confession.

"Do it now," the widow repeated. "Do not wait."

"He's telling me not to come to the bridge." Petra's voice sounded strange to her own ears, flattened, as though heard through a wall. She refolded the note, and her hands wanted to shake, and she did not permit them to, because the bundles had to be sewn and the courier had not reported and there was no longer any time in the world except this time. "So I won't. Kessler — the Frankfurt bundles go tonight, all of them, the mill route or no answer from the mill. We use the grain sacks."

"And you?"

Petra looked at the type case where the ledger's final forme stood locked in its chase — forty-one names and the debts and dates and sentences attached to them, her father's craft holding her brother's life in letters half an inch high. Somewhere to the east, iron wheels were turning on the bridge road, carrying Anton into the dark, and every instinct in her body screamed to follow, to run, to stand in the road. Instead she picked up the composing stick.

"He told me not to come to the bridge," she said. "So I'm going to do the only thing harder than coming. I'm going to stay here and finish it — and then I'm going to need a way into the customs house, and the only key I have is made of brass."

The widow blew out one of the two candles, halving the light, and in the remaining flame her face was old and calm and certain.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "you had better find out exactly how much a historian loves his provenance."
