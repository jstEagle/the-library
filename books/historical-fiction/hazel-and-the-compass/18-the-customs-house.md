## 18. The Customs House

The news came to her in the shape of a boy who never stopped moving.

Willem Acker, the baker's son, arrived at the shop after dark with flour still dusted in the seams of his collar, and he did not do his usual trick of checking the street twice before he knocked — he simply came through the door as if the door had been waiting for him, and that alone told Petra everything before he opened his mouth.

"They took Johann at the mill," he said. "The ferryman's boy. And they had the route."

Petra was standing at the stone sink, washing ink from her hands in water gone gray as pewter. She did not stop washing them. Her hands kept moving in the cold water while her mind went somewhere else entirely — down the river road, through the alder stands, to the hollow tree at the third milestone where copies had passed twice a week for a month, into the hands of the ferryman's family, onto the flat-bottomed boats that ran cloth and cheese down to Frankfurt and brought back paper and, once, a crate of French newspapers with their margins full of argument.

"Which route did they have?" she asked.

"The whole line. The tree, the mill, the boats." Willem's voice cracked and steadied itself the way young voices do when they have practiced steadiness for weeks. "Johann won't say anything — you know Johann — but it doesn't matter what he says. It matters where they looked next. Frau Kessler sent me. She says the widow's house will be watched by morning, and every house they know about, and that we should stop running copies because now they know what a copy looks like when it moves."

*Stop running copies.* As if the ledger were a thing one could simply set down and leave in a drawer until the weather changed. Petra dried her hands on her apron and looked at the boy — at his father's son, who had copied pages with a traitor's pen and hidden originals out of some stubborn decency no one had asked of him — and felt the peculiar tenderness that conspiracy breeds, and beneath the tenderness something colder and more useful: arithmetic.

There were forty pages of reconstructed ledger sewn into oilcloth under the floor of the composing room. There were perhaps three days before the commission's soldiers worked outward from the ferryman's arrest to every name Brandt's paperwork had ever touched. There was one road to Frankfurt that did not run through the mill or the bridge or any of the watched doors.

It ran through the customs house.

* * *

She had walked past the customs house ten thousand times without seeing it, the way one stops seeing one's own face. It sat at the foot of Bridge Street like a fat grey ledger itself, two stories of quarried stone with a colonnade added by some architect who had loved order more than beauty, and behind its colonnade, since October, the commission of Dr. Ernst Falk had established its offices. She knew this because everyone knew it, and because knowing it had never seemed to matter.

Now it mattered. Now she stood across the street in the shadow of the cooper's yard at seven in the evening, watching lamplight move behind the tall windows on the second floor, and performed her arithmetic again with new numbers.

The courier line was dead. The river was watched. Any bundle leaving town by road would be opened at the tollgate by men whose careers now depended on finding exactly such a bundle. The only place in the town where papers moved freely — where sealed evidence was carried from room to room by clerks who did not read what they carried, because reading it was not their duty — was the customs house itself. Falk had made it an ark of confiscated documents. He catalogued, he sealed, he stored. The army had burned the chancery annex in the first weeks; Falk had arrived and taught them that burning was waste, that paper properly seized was ammunition for the histories yet to be written. Everything seditious in the district now flowed toward him, and it flowed *in*, and almost nothing flowed *out*.

Almost nothing. But things did flow out eventually, she had learned from Brandt — manifests went to Mainz, duplicate receipts went to the provincial archive, correspondence went to Falk's superiors, all of it carried through the ordinary post by ordinary couriers who did not know and were not curious. The customs house had its own small river of paper, and Petra Voss needed to put one package into that river, and she needed one hour inside the building to prepare it, because the ledger could not simply be posted like a letter. It had to be broken into quires, disguised, addressed in hands that raised no suspicion, slipped among the commission's own outbound correspondence so thoroughly that pulling one thread would unravel the commission's own business.

One hour. Unwatched. Inside the most guarded building in the Rhineland.

She had come to the cooper's yard to look at the thing and confirm that looking at it was madness, and instead she had begun, against her own will, to solve it — her father's habit, Anton called it, *the Voss curse of treating impossibility as a faulty press that merely needs adjusting* — and it was while she stood calculating guard rotations from the rhythm of pipe smoke at the colonnade's edge that she saw the second light go on in the second-floor window, and saw Dr. Falk's silhouette pass across it, unhurried, carrying something small in both hands the way a priest carries a reliquary.

She did not need to see the object. She already knew its weight. She had carried it for months.

* * *

Herr Brandt came to the shop at nine, hood up, hands in his sleeves, looking like a man who had aged a decade since autumn. He would not sit. He stood by the cold stove and spoke low and fast.

"Falk has been asking about provenance," he said. "That is his word — *provenance*. He has taken a scholar's interest in the confiscated items. Most of it bores him — pamphlets, minutes, letters home from boys who will hang for them. But he has heard a story. The story of a printer smuggled out in 'thirty-three, after the last crackdown, who left certain effects behind. A compass, brass, engraved. Falk believes the compass belonged to the man himself, and that it may carry annotations — routes, contacts, marks in the metal. To Falk that compass would be the keystone of a chapter. The physical relic. The thing historians kill for." Brandt's eyes found hers in the lamplight and did not flinch, which was how she knew he had guessed long ago. "You have it. You have always had it. I am not asking. I am telling you that he wants it, and that wanting has made him careless in one particular: he receives visitors now. Scholars receive visitors. Soldiers do not."

"The compass is not for sale," Petra said, though even as she said it her hand had gone to her breast pocket, and Brandt's eyes followed the hand, and neither of them pretended anything afterward.

"No," Brandt agreed softly. "But it might be for trade."

* * *

Her father's workbench still held the groove worn by his forearm, a shallow polished valley in the oak that Petra had never been able to fill with her own arm no matter how many hours she leaned there. She sat at it that night with the compass open on the bench between her palms, and the candle flame shivered in the brass lid, and she let herself look at the thing honestly for the first time since the cellar.

It was not beautiful. It was a working instrument, dented at the hinge, its needle clouded nearly white with age, its glass scratched by thirty years of pockets. The engraving on the inner lid — the name she had not recognized the night she found it, *Elias Hartmann*, and beneath it a date and a word she had spent weeks learning to read as a route rather than a sentence — caught the light and threw it back thin and gold. This had been in her father's keeping for fifteen years. Before that, in the widow Kessler's keeping. Before that, in Elias Hartmann's pocket as he crossed borders in the dark, carrying names and arguments through a Europe that wanted him silenced. Her father had never used it to find north. He had kept it the way one keeps a promise — wrapped in oilcloth, buried in a drawer of effects, alive.

And she was going to hand it to the man who burned archives.

The thought arrived in her body before her mind, as grief does: a hollowness under the ribs, a wrongness in the hands, as if she had already surrendered it and reached for it and found the pocket empty. She understood then, with a clarity that felt like cruelty, what her father had known when he hid the thing instead of using it — that some objects are not tools but tethers, and that cutting a tether does not free the anchored boat; it only lets the current take it. If she gave Falk the compass, she gave him the last piece of her father she had never known, the secret life she was still unwrapping chapter by chapter, the note in his hand that had pointed her to the cellar and thence to everything: the network, the proof sheets, the route east, the whole geography of his courage. She would be trading the map of her inheritance for a stack of paper.

A stack of paper with forty names on it. Forty-one with hers. Two hundred and six, if one counted the parish records and the widow's letters, every man the ledger had marked, every debt of dissent, every boy who would otherwise vanish into the transport wagons and the victors' amnesia with nothing left behind but a gap in a burned book.

She closed the compass. The hinge clicked like type falling into a stick.

"You sentimental fool," she said aloud to the empty workshop, in her father's own tone, the one he had used on himself at the bench when a form wouldn't lock and midnight was coming. "Paper is the only immortality we ever had."

* * *

She went at midmorning, in her good black dress and her mother's shawl, carrying the compass in a plain linen bag like a woman bringing eggs to market, and she presented herself at the customs house door and gave her name to the soldier on duty with the composure of a woman delivering exactly what she promised herself she would deliver and nothing more.

"Fraulein Voss." The soldier checked a list. She had expected to wait hours; instead she waited eleven minutes on a wooden bench beneath a coat of arms whose heraldic beasts looked embarrassed by their duties, and then a clerk — not Brandt, a stranger with a Silesian accent — led her up the stairs to Falk's rooms.

Dr. Ernst Falk received her in a chamber that smelled of sealing wax and cold coffee. He was smaller than she had imagined, precise, spectacles catching the window light, and he rose when she entered and offered a chair with the genuine courtesy of a man who believed civilization consisted largely of chairs being offered. Papers covered every surface in meticulous drifts. On the wall hung a survey map of the district annotated in red — she permitted herself one glance and no more.

"Fraulein Voss." He seated himself and folded his hands. "Daughter of Georg Voss, printer. I have your father's death registered here, incidentally, and his guild standing. A respected craftsman. I have been meaning to visit your establishment — a town's printing history interests me greatly. But you have come to me first, and uninvited, which suggests purpose."

"I've come to sell you something, Doctor."

His eyebrows rose a polite fraction. "I am not authorized to purchase."

"You're not authorized to want, either, and you've been wanting this for weeks." She set the linen bag on his desk, untied it, and lifted out the compass with both hands, the way he had carried it past his window two nights ago, and she watched the transformation in his face — the scholar arriving beneath the bureaucrat like water rising under ice. He did not reach for it. He made himself not reach for it, and she granted him that struggle its full minute.

"Elias Hartmann's compass," she said. "Engraved. With annotations. The provenance is documented in the widow Kessler's letters, which you have already seized and misfiled under correspondence, third drawer, green tape. You suspected it existed. You hoped it survived." She turned it in the light so the engraving flashed. "It survived. It survived because my father hid it from men like you for fifteen years, and now I am giving it to you, and I imagine you are wondering why."

Falk removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, replaced them. When he spoke his voice had gone quiet and careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice he intends to test with each step.

"Why indeed."

"Because I want one hour in this building tonight. One hour, one room, one lamp, no soldier, no clerk, no record of my presence in your log beyond a routine delivery receipt that you will write yourself. In exchange, the compass is yours — outright, documented, with my written attestation of its chain of custody, signed and witnessed, which will make it the centerpiece of your collection and unimpeachable in any academic dispute for the next hundred years."

The silence that followed had texture. Somewhere below, a door slammed; boots crossed the colonnade. Falk looked at the compass, and Petra watched him perform the historian's fatal arithmetic: the object against the hour, the relic against the risk, the chapter he would write against the regulation he would break. He was not a cruel man — she had learned to distinguish, in these months, cruelty from mere thoroughness, and Falk was thorough the way winter is thorough, indifferent rather than malicious — and thorough men could be bargained with, because thoroughness always contained a private ledger of its own debts and desires.

"If anyone learns of this," Falk said slowly, "my commission ends and worse follows."

"If anyone learns of it, tell them the widow sold it and fled. Tell them whatever your history requires. You are, after all, the man writing it." She heard her own voice and marveled at it — level, dry, her father's voice, the voice he had used with magistrates and suppliers, the voice of a woman with nothing further to lose. "Doctor. You burn archives. I set type. We both know what lasts. Tonight, for one hour, let us pretend we are colleagues."

He almost smiled. That was the terrible thing — he *almost smiled*, recognizing in her something of his own species, the keeper of records, and she felt the recognition land on her skin like filth and made herself stand still beneath it.

"One hour," Falk said. "Tonight, after the changing of the watch at eleven. The records room on the ground floor, east wing. I will walk you in myself and lock you nowhere, Fraulein, because I will be directly upstairs with your compass, and if you take so much as a seal unstamped beyond what your hour requires, I will hear of it before you clear the square." He drew the compass toward him at last, and his fingertips settled on the brass with a reverence that turned her stomach. "It's lighter than I expected," he murmured, mostly to himself. "They usually are."

Petra rose. At the door she paused, because there was one thing left and she could not leave without saying it, not for strategy but for her own soul's bookkeeping.

"He was my father," she said, not turning around. "The man who hid this. Whatever your history says about men like him, remember that you got your keystone from his daughter, and that she gave it up for forty names you will never think are worth the trade. Put that in your chapter if you can find a footnote honest enough to hold it."

She descended the stairs into the grey morning without waiting for an answer, and behind her, through the tall window of the second floor, she could feel — she did not need to turn and see it — the small golden glint of the compass already resting in Dr. Falk's cupped hands, going where her father's note had once pointed her: east, always east, though never quite the way anyone intended.

* * *

Brandt met her at the corner of Bridge Street, breathless, hatless, a man visibly composing his resignation in his head. "He agreed? He actually—"

"At eleven. East wing records room. One hour." She kept walking; he fell in beside her. "Get me the commission's outbound mail schedule from tomorrow's manifest, the addresses of the Frankfurt correspondents, and forty blank envelopes from the requisition stores. Bring them to the widow's by nine. Tell the others: tonight the ledger leaves this town inside Falk's own postbag, or none of us ever speaks its name again."

"And the compass?" Brandt asked quietly, and there was such gentleness in it that she nearly broke stride.

"Gone," Petra said. "Traded. Which means—" and here she found, to her surprise, that the words came steady, that the hollowness under her ribs had already begun to close over, the way a composed page closes over its own knots and flaws, invisible from the reading side, "—which means that from tonight forward, everything my father ever hid in silence is either in that man's glass case or on its way to a free press in Frankfurt. There is no longer anywhere for those names to hide. So we had better make certain they travel well."

At eleven o'clock the church bells rang the change of watch, and a side door of the customs house opened, and a clerk with a Silesian accent beckoned her into the smell of wax and paper, and somewhere above her head, in a lamplit room, Dr. Ernst Falk was turning her father's compass over and over in his careful hands.

Petra carried her oilcloth packet under her shawl, and she did not look back at the door.
