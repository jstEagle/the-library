## 19. Setting the Record

The press smelled of other men's decisions.

That was the first thing Petra noticed when the courier — a customs officer whose name she would never learn, whose gloved hand had simply pointed her down the stairs and closed the door behind her — left her alone with it. A Washington hand-press, newer than her father's, its bed polished by use, its ink plate still tacky from the day's work on manifests and duty schedules. Someone had wiped it down carelessly. Someone had assumed no one but clerks would ever stand here.

She set her satchel on the floor with the reverence of a woman laying an infant in a cradle.

Upstairs, muffled through two floors of oak and plaster, she could hear Dr. Falk's voice — animated, pleased, the voice of a man showing off a prize to himself. He had taken the compass up to his rooms in the commission's quarters as though it were a relic destined for a glass case, and perhaps he was right. Perhaps that was all it had ever been meant for: brass and glass and a hinged lid worn smooth by her father's thumb. She had carried it through every chapter of her life since the cellar, and now it sat on a scholar's desk one floor above her while she did the thing it had actually been made to point toward.

*It doesn't point north,* her father had never said, because he had never told her anything at all aloud. But the note beneath the lining of the case had said it plainly enough, once she finally learned to read what he'd hidden there. *It points where the presses are free.* And tonight, ironically, it had pointed here — to a press in the belly of the enemy's own custom house, unguarded for exactly one hour, because a historian's vanity had been purchased with a dead man's instrument.

Petra took off her shawl, folded it, set it aside. She rolled her sleeves the way she had every morning of her working life since she was nine years old.

Then she opened the satchel.

The reconstructed ledger lay wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into its binding by Widow Kessler's own needle — the widow had insisted, saying that if the book was going to lie about being official, it ought at least be sewn like scripture. Forty pages of names. Three hundred and eleven men, each with his trade, his age, the charge against him or the debt of dissent he carried, the date the original entry had been made. Anton Voss, compositor, twenty-three, marked *for transport*, sat on page nineteen between a cooper and a schoolteacher, set in type by his own sister's hands.

Three hundred and eleven names. The army held the original ledger in a sealed crate upstairs, catalogued as evidence, awaiting Dr. Falk's scholarly fire. By morning the crate would go into the annex furnace with everything else that remembered too much. But these forty copies — these would be scattered like seed before the frost could take them.

One hour. She had printed an edition of broadsheets in less, in the old days, when the shop was running full. But she had never worked alone, never worked shaking, never worked with her brother's name in her type case and a man who burned archives humming contentedly above her head.

*Begin where you always begin,* her father used to say when she panicked at a rush order. *With the forme.*

She began with the forme.

* * *

Setting the type was the part no one could have done but her.

Brandt had copied the ledger's contents fair and true onto paper — his clerk's hand, so careful now it looked engraved — but paper was testimony, and testimony could be denied, dismissed, burned. Type was different. Type was mechanical truth. You could burn a printed page only by burning every copy, and every copy was identical, and identity was the thing that made a lie expensive.

So she composed. She stood at the customs house's own type cabinet — they kept a modest jobbing face for notices and duty stamps — and she built the first forme page by page, galley by galley, her fingers moving with the blind certainty of thirty years' inheritance compressed into ten years' practice. The letters came to her hand without being sought, the way water finds a channel. *Heinrich Albrecht Baumann, wheelwright.* She had never met Baumann. She knew him now better than his own mother probably knew him: born 1801, fined twice for pamphleteering, flagged for conscription though he walked with a limp. Somewhere in this town Baumann was hiding in a cellar or standing in a line of prisoners, not knowing that at this moment a woman he'd never spoken to was setting his name in twelve-point Caslon so that no one could ever say it had never been written.

Her fingers did not shake at the type cases. Her fingers never shook at the type cases. It was only in the pauses — reaching for the quoins, waiting for the ink — that the shaking found her, and each time she closed the pause by locking the chase, tightening the screws, giving the tremor something solid to push against.

Above her, a chair scraped. Footsteps crossed the ceiling, unhurried, and paused directly overhead. Petra went still, one hand flat on the forme, listening to her own blood.

Falk's voice came faintly through the floorboards, addressing no one — the man talked to himself the way scholars do, narrating his own thoughts as if history were already taking dictation. She couldn't make out the words. Perhaps he was admiring the compass. Perhaps he was describing it aloud, noting its provenance, composing the footnote he would write about how it came into the commission's possession. Let him. Every word he spent on the brass was a word he wasn't spending downstairs.

The footsteps moved away. Petra exhaled through her teeth, inked the rollers, and pulled the first proof.

* * *

The press spoke, and she answered.

That was the rhythm of it, once the forme was locked and bedded and the paper dampened right: the press demanded and she obeyed, demanded and obeyed, and somewhere in the obedience the fear stopped being hers. Pull. Release. Turn. Lay the fresh sheet. Pull. The iron arm came down and the platen bit and the sheet took the ink and three hundred and eleven names existed once more in the world, and then again, and then again. There was no room inside that rhythm for dread. There was barely room for breath. This was what she had never been able to explain to Anton, who thought the shop was just their father's cage — that the press was a kind of tide you could stand inside, that grief and terror both drowned in it if you gave yourself wholly to the count.

Sheet seven. Sheet eight. She was printing two pages per forme, working through the ledger in sixteen formes, forty sheets each — six hundred and forty pulls in an hour, if her arithmetic held and her arms did. They were strong arms. Her father had never once let her believe they shouldn't be. *A printer's daughter pulls her weight in lead,* he said, and she had hated him a little for it at thirteen, and loved him entirely for it at every age since.

By the third forme she had found the faster motion, the one where the tympan turned itself under her hand and the sheet found its points almost without guidance, as if the paper wanted to be printed. Sheet after sheet came off the stone and went into the drying stack, grey ink on rag paper, still glistening. The smell rose around her — linseed and lampblack and the vinegar-sharp damp of sizing — and it was her father's print shop smell, it was the smell of her entire childhood, and it filled a government building that had been requisitioned to erase her town, and she thought: *this is what the fire can't digest. This is what comes out the other side of every crackdown since Gutenberg knelt in his own ink. Somebody, somewhere, setting it again.*

A drop of sweat slid off her jaw and landed at the margin of sheet twenty-two. She let it dry there. Imperfect. Human. Let Falk find it someday, if he ever compared copies — a flaw in the record, a woman's salt in the margins of the men he'd tried to unwrite.

Between the fifth and sixth forme, the ceiling went quiet, and she heard the front door of the customs house open and close. Voices outside — a guard's challenge, answered by a voice she recognized with a lurch: Brandt. He was supposed to be at the chancery, falsifying the manifest, keeping Anton's transport bogged in its own paperwork for another precious day. What was he doing here?

The door above opened again. Footsteps on the stair — two sets, one hesitant, and then a knock on the basement door, soft, coded: the pattern the network used, three-and-two, borrowed from the widows' knitting songs.

Petra set the bar down, crossed the room, opened the door a hand's width.

Brandt stood in the corridor with a lantern and a face the color of wet paper. Beside him, hooded and small, was the baker's son.

"The boy brought the last pages," Brandt whispered. "The ones he hid. His father's column and the parish records — the ones we thought lost." He swallowed. "Also I could not stay up there. In that building. With him." His eyes flicked upward, meaning Falk, meaning the whole edifice of lawful evil grinding slowly through its evening above them. "I will stand at the stair. No one comes down."

"Your manifest—"

"Is filed. Delayed until Thursday, if anyone reads it honestly, which no one will until it's too late to matter." He almost smiled; it died halfway. "Go back to your work, Frau Voss. You are doing mine."

The boy handed the pages over with both hands, the way you hand over something hot. Petra took them, and for a moment she held his gaze, and what passed between them was the whole strange covenant of the past weeks: he had betrayed and unbetrayed within the same trembling week, and mercy had been extended to him that he had not asked for and could not repay, and this was simply what the network was now. Small hands. Forgiven debts. Everyone compromised, therefore no one could be bought.

She shut the door. Set the new pages on the stone. Inked her rollers.

Pull. Release. Turn. Lay the sheet.

* * *

Somewhere around the twelfth forme, her body began to hate her, and she let it.

Her shoulders burned. The web between thumb and forefinger had split days ago from type work and had reopened, and each pull pressed the wound against the wooden bar until she could feel her pulse in it, a small red metronome keeping time with the press. The lamp burned low and she trimmed the wick without stopping, one-handed, the way her father had taught her — *never stop the press for light, the light will wait, the press will not* — and the flame steadied and threw her shadow huge across the whitewashed wall, a giant woman bent over a machine, pulling the names of her town out of darkness one impression at a time.

She counted in formes now instead of minutes. Ten done. Twelve. Fourteen. The drying stacks rose around her in loose towers, sheets laid tissue-thin between blotters, and the room filled with the particular warmth of fresh print, and Petra understood — not as metaphor but as fact, as physical fact the way the weight of the bar was fact — that she would remember this hour for the rest of her life, and that remembering it later would be the thing that kept her upright. Not the triumph of it. The labor. The plain, unglamorous, muscular labor of refusing.

Because that was what the histories would never record, whichever side wrote them. That resistance was not drumbeats on bridges. It was this: a woman alone in a basement, arms failing, finger bleeding, printing the thirty-first copy of a book that officially did not exist, while upstairs a civilized man admired his new compass and believed, in the bottom of his well-credentialed heart, that he had already won.

Fifteen formes. She washed the press — quickly, badly, it would need a proper cleaning tomorrow by whoever the clerks sent — and locked the final forme, the last two columns, the colophon she had argued with herself about for three formes and finally included, small, centered, in eight-point:

*Set and printed in the occupied customs house of [town], in the year 1848. The original was seized; this was not. Count your copies against the graves.*

No. Too bitter. She had already locked the line before the doubt came, and she considered breaking the forme, picking it apart letter by letter, and decided — with a ruthlessness that felt foreign and correct — to leave it. Let the copies carry a little bitterness. Bitterness was honest. The men in this ledger had earned the right to be bitter in their own memorial.

She pulled the first sheet of the final forme and held it up to the lamp, and the light came through the rag paper, and the columns of names glowed like windows in a dark house.

Anton Voss. Page nineteen. There he was, in ink, forty times over by morning.

"You're published, little brother," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it, and pulled the next sheet.

* * *

Thirty-eight copies were stacked and sewn-adjacent — folded, collated, weighted under the press stone — when the ceiling above her creaked in a way that was not Falk pacing.

Petra froze, hand on the stack.

Footsteps, slow, deliberate, crossing to the top of the basement stair. Then silence. Then — distinct, unhurried, terribly courteous — three knocks on the door at the head of the stairs. Not the network's pattern. Not any pattern. Just knocking, the way a man knocks when he knows perfectly well someone is home.

"Frau Voss." Falk's voice came down the stairwell pleasantly, pitched for quiet, almost kind. "I don't know what you're doing down there, and I find I don't especially wish to. My hour, however, concludes shortly, and my escort will return for you."

A pause. The floorboards shifted, the sound of a man weighing something and finding it agreeable.

"For what it's worth," he called down, "the compass is a beautiful piece of work. Whoever engraved it understood provenance is worth more than testimony. Testimony can be contradicted." A dry, satisfied breath, almost a laugh. "An object, once owned, belongs to whoever holds it. Do come up when you're finished."

His footsteps retreated across the ceiling. A door closed.

Petra stood in the lamplight with her heart slamming and her forty copies under the stone, and she understood two things at once, with perfect clarity. First: Falk had known. Perhaps from the moment she handed him the compass in the customs hall — known what she was, what the hour was for, and sold it to her anyway for the price of his beloved provenance, because a scholar's hunger is a blacker market than any smuggler's.

And second: the escort coming for her would arrive within minutes, and the copies were still here, and the grain sacks downriver would leave at dawn with or without them.

She picked up the oilcloth and began to wrap.
