## 21. Aftermath

The frost came early that year, silvering the broken tiles of the chancery roof before anyone had climbed up to mend them, and on the morning Petra Voss counted her town's losses she did it the only way she knew how: in columns.

She had taken one of her father's old order books — the blank ones, meant for invoices that would never again be written in his hand — and ruled it into three columns with a straightedge and a steady stroke of pencil. The first column held names. The second held fates. The third she left empty, because she could not decide what belonged there. *Compensation* seemed obscene. *Debt* was closer to the truth but too small a word for what the army had taken with its requisitions and its fires and its two days of transport wagons grinding over the bridge stones.

By the third week of November the first column had ninety-one names in it.

She wrote them at the shop table, in the grey hour before the apprentices arrived, when the stove had burned down to red coals and the light through the windows was the color of watered milk. *Georg Brandt.* She had written his name on the fourth day, when they pulled the customs house records out of the flooded cellar where Falk's clerks had dumped them, and found among the requisition papers three manifests altered in a hand everyone in town knew. He had not waited to be arrested. He had walked to the garrison himself, hat in hand like a man reporting a lost dog, and told the commandant what he had done. They had not hanged him. They had not even imprisoned him long. Instead they dismissed him from his post, fined him everything he owned, and turned him out into the street as a warning, which was perhaps crueler than the rope, because now Herr Brandt stood every morning at the market fountain with nothing to do but be seen.

*Widow Anna Kessler.* Fate: *shop closed, health failing.* But Petra could not make her pencil add the words she knew were coming, so she wrote instead in the third column — the column that had finally found its purpose without her deciding it — *taught me everything I know about keeping paper dry.*

That was the third column's use, she discovered. Not judgment. Not payment. Only what remained.

*Anton Voss.* Fate: *transported. Returned.*

She had written *returned* with the nib barely touching the paper, as if pressing harder might jinx it, the way pressing harder on type could punch through the damp sheet and ruin an hour's work. Anton had come back eleven days ago, walking up the road from the river landing in a coat that no longer fit him because he no longer filled it, his hair cropped short by prison shears, his hands still ink-dark in the creases no amount of washing reached. He had seen his own name in print. That was the thing he kept saying, in the first days, whenever the silence in the shop grew heavy enough to frighten the apprentices. *I saw my name in print, Pet. In Frankfurt. On a broadsheet sold in the open market, eight hellers, and the man who sold it to me didn't know my face.* He said it the way other men described miracles or shipwrecks, with the same stunned gratitude, and Petra understood that the two things had become, for him, indistinguishable.

The Frankfurt printers had set forty names from the grain-sack copies. Not all ninety-one — paper was dear and courage rationed even in free cities — but forty, and Anton's was among them, set in a face so like their father's letters that Anton swore he could pick out which composites the typesetter favored. *They got the umlaut wrong,* he'd told her, laughing until he wept, the first night home, both of them sitting up in the kitchen where the stove still drew and the kettle still sang and everything else about the world had changed. *After everything, after the manifest and the transport and six weeks on a work detail outside Mainz, they got my name almost right and the umlaut wrong, and I have never loved a misprint more in my life.*

Now he slept past noon most days and woke shouting some nights, and Petra let him sleep and did not mention the shouting, because grief in the Voss family had always been conducted privately, in separate rooms, like prayer.

* * *

The shop reopened under the widow's sign.

It had been Kessler's idea, delivered in a voice thinned to thread by the illness in her chest, on one of the last afternoons she could sit upright in her chair by the window. *Your father's sign says Voss & Son, and your father is dead, and your son* — she had caught herself, that flicker of the old sharpness softening nothing but itself — *your brother is half-dead and was never the printer anyway. You are. Put your own mark over the door, girl, or put mine, because I'll tell you what mine means: it means a woman kept this trade alive through fire. Let them read that over the threshold every time they come in.*

So Petra painted it herself, on the board her father had hung thirty years ago and never repainted: **Kessler & Voss**, in white letters on the deep green she remembered from the widow's shopfront in her childhood, the one that had sold ledgers and songbooks and, quietly, other things. The apprentices held the ladder and argued about whether the ampersand was properly proportioned, which was the kind of argument Petra encouraged, because arguments about ampersands were arguments about a world in which ampersands mattered, and she wanted that world back if she could not have the old one.

Jakob came nearly every day. He was fourteen now, taller, quieter, and he still would not meet her eyes for longer than a breath, though she had told him twice — once at the mill, once at the graveside when they buried his grandfather in the same cold ground that was filling faster than any winter in memory — that what he carried in his sleeve had saved more names than the army had crossed off. The baker paid for his son's keep at the press by delivering flour and, once a week, a sack in which the bottom third was not flour at all but folded sheets from Frankfurt, coming down the river now openly enough, since the occupation had loosened into administration and administration into the ordinary gray machinery of government. Some of those sheets went up on the shop wall behind the counter, under glass, where customers could stand and find their husbands' names, their brothers' names, set in type in a city that had never surrendered. Petra watched them do it, one after another, through that whole first month. She never hurried them. Reading your own grief in print took exactly as long as it took.

The press itself had survived, which she still could not entirely explain. Falk's men had fired the shop on the night of the theft, and the flames had eaten the storeroom and the loft and the north wall before the bucket line from the tannery pond beat it down, but the press — her father's press, iron-framed, older than she was — had stood in the corner nearest the stone chimney and taken only scorch along one cheek. The type cases had fared worse; half the sorts were fused or cracked, and for a week Petra picked through the wreckage with gloves on, sorting metal like a woman sorting bones. But type can be recast. Her father had taught her that before he taught her anything else. *Metal remembers nothing, Petra. That's its mercy. It takes whatever shape you give it and holds that shape against anything. People are the opposite, God help us, and that's why we do what we do — so that somewhere in the world, something holds its shape.*

She thought of that sentence now more often than any other thing he'd said. She thought of it standing at the melting pot while the apprentices slept, casting new sorts by lamplight, the lead smell rising sweet and terrible, the metal remembering nothing and holding everything.

* * *

On a bright hard morning in December, with the frost thick on the insides of the windows until Jakob scraped portholes in it, Anton came down the stairs shaved and dressed and steady-eyed, and asked if there was work.

Petra looked up from the composing stick in her hands. She was setting the funeral notice for Widow Kessler, who had died in her sleep four days earlier with the last of her letters bundled and labeled in her own hand on the table beside her bed, addressed to *the shop*. Setting that notice had taken Petra most of two mornings, not because the text was long but because she kept stopping to choose each piece of type with a care she usually reserved for currency.

"There's always work," she said. "There's a town full of people who need to prove they exist."

"I mean real work." He crossed to the type wall, ran his fingers along the cases the way a man greets horses, and stopped at the case their father had reserved for display faces, the ornamental one nobody had ordered from in years. "I've been thinking. In Mainz, on the work detail, there was a man from the free corps, a student, and he had nothing, no papers, no name anyone would honor, and what kept him alive was that I wrote his story down. Just in a notebook. Just facts. But he used to ask me to read it back to him at night." Anton's hand rested flat on the wooden frame of the case. "Names are not decoration, Pet. I know you know that. But I want to do more than notices. I want to set the whole ledger again — the ninety-one, with their fates, the way you've been keeping it in Father's order book — and print it properly. A register. Bound copies. One for the parish chest, one for Frankfurt, one for every family that wants it. Let Falk write his history. Ours will be sitting next to it on every shelf, and his will have to argue."

Petra set the composing stick down very carefully, because her hands had begun to shake and she would not let him see, not yet, not on this of all mornings.

"The third column," she said. "You'd need a third column."

"That's what I'm counting on you for. You're the only one who knows what goes in it."

Through the scraped porthole in the window, she could see the square: the market stalls going up, the fountain running again, Herr Brandt at his post by the water with his ruined hands folded, watching the town learn to walk past him without either cursing or thanking him, just walking past, alive. Smoke rose from chimneys, ordinary smoke, bread-smoke and wood-smoke, and the bridge stones wore their frost like a bandage over an old wound. Somewhere a child was shrieking with laughter about the ice on the troughs. The army drums had not sounded in five weeks. The silence where they had been was itself a kind of music, and Petra found she could hear it now without flinching.

"Yes," she said. "We'll do it. But we do it right — proofs checked twice, no name misspelled, no fate softened. If we're going to hold the shape, we hold it true."

Anton smiled, and for a moment he looked like their father so strongly that Petra had to turn back to her stick.

"Then pull the parish records," he said, reaching for his coat again. "Brandt keeps the duplicate registers from the chancery — he salvaged them out of the flood cellar before Falk's men could finish. He's been waiting months for someone to ask him. And Pet—" he paused at the door, breath fogging in the cold of the shop, "—there's a letter on the table upstairs. Came with the last sack from Frankfurt. It's addressed to the mistress of the house, and I didn't open it, but I saw the seal, and it's not a printer's seal."

He went out into the white morning. Petra stood a moment with the funeral notice half-set in her hands — *Anna Kessler, who kept faith* — and then she wiped her fingers clean, hung her apron on its nail, and climbed the stairs to see who in Frankfurt, with a wax seal and a steady hand, was writing to a printer in a burned town.

The envelope lay on the kitchen table where he'd said, the seal unbroken, deep green wax pressed with a device she did not recognize: a compass rose, and beneath it, tooled fine, a single word. *Hazel.*
