## 22. Hazel and the Compass

The apprentice arrived in March, when the river still carried ice and the town still counted its springs by what had survived the winter before. Petra Voss was fifty-nine years old that March, her hair gone the color of wood ash, her hands still certain on the composing stick, and she watched from the shop window as a girl of perhaps fifteen came up the cobbled street carrying a single carpet bag and an expression of ferocious determination, as though she had argued her way across three counties to be standing precisely there.

She had, it turned out. Her name was Hazel Brenner, she announced on the doorstep, and her mother's cousin's widow had once known a woman named Kessler, and somewhere between those two facts a letter had been written and answered and a journey undertaken, and here Hazel stood, chin lifted, waiting to be told she belonged.

Petra looked at her a long moment. The girl had ink on her already — not printer's ink but the cheap iron-gall of a school slate, ground into the crease of her right thumb, the mark of someone who wrote more than she was asked to. Petra had learned, over thirty years, to read hands the way other people read faces.

"You'll sweep first," Petra said. "Then you'll learn to set your own name backward."

Hazel's eyes went bright. "Backward?"

"Everything in this trade is backward," Petra said, standing aside to let her in. "That is the first lesson. The second is that backward things are sometimes truer than forward ones."

* * *

The shop had changed and had not changed. The press was newer — a flatbed iron beast purchased with the money from four good years of almanacs — but it sat where the old hand press had sat, against the north wall where the light came steady and cold through windows that had never once been clean to Petra's satisfaction. The type cases still lined the back wall in their long ranks, small dark drawers of letters, and Petra had never allowed anyone to reorganize them, because her fingers knew their geography the way a sleeper knows his own bed in the dark.

Her father had built those cases. Or some of them. Others had come with the shop before him, and one — the third from the left, its brass pull worn smooth — had held, for one night in 1849, a ledger's worth of names in galley form, locked away while soldiers drank in the square outside.

Hazel found that drawer within her first week, because Hazel found everything within her first week. She was the kind of apprentice who asked why the quoins were called quoins, who wanted to know what had happened to the burn scar on Petra's left forearm, who read the specimen books on the shelf during her dinner hour instead of resting. Petra had trained apprentices before — three of them, all gone on to presses of their own or marriages or the wider world — but none of them had looked at the shop the way Hazel did, as if it were a text she intended to copy out entire.

"It's just a compass," Hazel said one afternoon, holding it up. Petra had left it on the workbench while she washed her hands; she did that now, sometimes, deliberately, an old habit of testing. "It doesn't point north."

"Doesn't it?"

Hazel frowned and turned the instrument in her palm. It was a handsome thing even now, the brass gone soft and honey-dark with handling, the hinged lid opening on a needle that swung lazily, agreeably, in whatever direction pleased it. Engraved inside the lid, worn nearly to a whisper, were two lines: a name — *E. Marwitz* — and beneath it a string of characters that had taken Petra eleven years of her father's life and three of her own to understand.

"It points wherever I turn it," Hazel said. "It's broken."

"It isn't broken." Petra dried her hands and came to stand beside her. "Sit down, child. You've earned the whole lesson, and I find at my age I'd rather give it while my mouth still works properly."

* * *

They sat in the two chairs by the stove, because this was a story told sitting down, and Petra had learned that from Widow Kessler, may she rest in whatever printing house heaven reserved for the stubborn. Outside, the street noise rose and fell — cart wheels, a dog, the bell of the new church that had replaced the one burned in '49 — and Petra let it rise and fall around them like water around a stone.

"That compass belonged to Ernst Marwitz," she said. "A printer from Mannheim. He was smuggled out of the country in a wine wagon in 1832, after the last crackdown, before I was born. My father helped load the barrels. He never told me that either — I learned it from a widow, years later, after he was dead. That was his way. He kept things so deep inside himself that even he sometimes lost track of where they were."

Hazel turned the compass over. "And the needle really is broken?"

"The needle is fine. The needle points wherever a needle points. That was never the point of it." Petra reached out and touched the engraved characters, her thumbnail finding the grooves the way her fingers found the third case from the left. "Marwitz hid a route inside the engraving. A courier line — safe houses, distances, turns of the road — running east to Frankfurt, where there were free presses willing to print what no one here dared print. My father carried that compass for thirty years without ever using it. Or perhaps by never using it. Keeping it was the use."

"But you used it."

"I used it," Petra said. "In the spring of 1849, when the army held this town and a commission of historians was burning every paper that contradicted the account they'd already decided to write. There was a guild ledger — do you know what I'm about to say, Hazel? Has Kessler's name reached you with more than a letter attached?"

"Some of it." The girl's voice went careful. "They say a print shop burned. They say copies of something went downriver in grain sacks. They say—" she hesitated, then finished, because Hazel always finished, "—they say the woman who did it traded a treasure for one hour at a customs-house press."

"They say correctly." Petra smiled, and felt the smile settle into the lines of her face like type settling into a chase. "The treasure was this compass. Dr. Falk — the historian, a civilized man, which is the most chilling thing I can teach you about men — wanted it desperately. Provenance, he called it. A document's pedigree. He could write the revolution out of existence, but a compass carried by Marwitz proved that the men he was erasing had existed, had been worth smuggling, had been worth a bribe. So I gave it to him for one unwatched hour, and in that hour we printed forty copies of the ledger on the customs house's own press. Every name. Every man marked for conscription, every debt of dissent, every grudge the authorities had entered against their own neighbors in a book they thought was theirs alone."

"And Anton," Hazel said softly. "Your brother. His name was in it."

"His name was in it." Petra let that sit. Even now, after everything, the saying of it moved something in her chest — not grief anymore, exactly. More the way a well-set page sits in the hand: heavy, balanced, true. "He saw his own name set in type in Frankfurt that autumn. A transported man, reading in a foreign city that he had not been erased. He told me once it was like being handed back his own shadow."

* * *

The light shifted toward evening, and Petra lit the lamp, and Hazel did not ask to be excused, which Petra noted with approval. Apprentices who stayed for the ending of stories were apprentices who would stay for the harder endings — the misprints, the deadlines, the years when the trade paid in patience rather than coin.

"Here is what I actually want you to understand," Petra said, taking the compass from the girl's hands. It was lighter than it looked; it had always been lighter than it looked. "For a long time I believed I had inherited a tool. A secret map, a weapon, a key. And it was those things, for one year of my life. But I have owned it for thirty years since, and it has pointed nothing but whatever direction I happen to be facing, and I have come to understand what my father was doing when he kept it."

"What?" 

"He was keeping it." Petra opened the lid fully and tilted it toward the lamplight, so that the engraving caught the flame. "A compass is an instrument that tells you what direction to attend to. North. East. Home. This one says: *attend*. It points at what must be remembered. When my father held it, it pointed him toward Marwitz, and the obligation of a man saved. When I held it, it pointed me toward a ledger full of neighbors' names and the fact that no one else was going to save them. The needle was never navigation, child. The needle was memory. It swings wherever you face it because memory does the same — it goes where you choose to look, and its whole purpose is to make sure you look at all."

Hazel was quiet. Then: "But Falk has it. If he has it—"

Petra laughed — a real laugh, surprising even herself with its warmth. "Falk died in 1861, rich, respected, and wrong. His history is still in print. It is also a lie, and everyone who matters knows it is a lie, because forty copies of the truth have been circulating for thirty years — quoted, copied, reprinted in Frankfurt and Leipzig and, last I heard, translated into French. He bought the compass thinking he had bought provenance. What he had actually bought was the only object in the world whose loss made the papers immortal. You cannot burn a thing that has forty mothers, Hazel. Remember that. It is perhaps the most useful thing I know."

"And this is—" Hazel's eyes had gone to the shelf.

"Yes," Petra said. "Go on. You were going to ask anyway."

On the open shelf above the type cases, where anyone who walked into the shop could see it — where customers waited, where apprentices swept beneath it, where the dust settled honestly on it every week — stood a book. Not hidden in a false-bottomed drawer, not sewn into a grain sack, not buried under the cellar floor beside the proof sheets that had started everything. Standing upright. Spine outward. A plain binding, the title stamped in ordinary gold leaf that had dulled with age: *Guild Ledger of the Printers' Town, Reconstructed MDCCCXLIX.*

The forty-first copy. The one Petra had kept.

"It rests there openly," Petra said, "because hiding it was necessary for exactly as long as hiding it took, and not one day more. A secret kept past its need becomes a sickness. We hid the names to save them; we print the names to honor them; and any day some scholar with dusty cuffs wanders in and asks to see it, I take it down and let him read until closing time. Two men have written pamphlets from it. One wrote a bad novel. I corrected his typography in the margins and sent it back."

* * *

She taught Hazel the engraving that night, all the same. Some things a keeper keeps even when keeping is no longer required, because the keeping itself is the inheritance. They bent over the compass together at the workbench, the lamp trimmed low, and Petra showed her how the flourishes were not flourishes — how the scrollwork that looked decorative concealed distances in leagues, how the little cross-stroke above the fourth character marked the turning at the mill road, how the whole of it resolved, once you knew the cipher Kessler's husband had died protecting, into a line running east and east and east until it ended at a door in Frankfurt with a press behind it.

Hazel copied the cipher into her notebook, her slate-stained thumb pressing hard, and then she looked up with the expression Petra had already learned to expect from her — the expression of a girl about to ask the question that mattered.

"Why teach me this? The route is dead. The courier line hasn't run in twenty years. Everyone in it is—"

"Dead, dispersed, or deaf," Petra agreed. "Yes. And someday the compass will be yours, and the cipher will be useless, and you will keep both anyway. Because the function of this object was never navigation and never secrecy and never even memory, in the end. Its function is *succession*. It is a pointer, and pointers must point at something, and what it points at is whoever stands next in line and needs to learn that records do not defend themselves. Someone always has to carry the names. In 1849 it was widows and bakers and a clerk with shaking hands. In whatever year it happens next — and it will happen, Hazel; the burning always comes back around, dressed in better manners — it will be you, or someone like you, and I would rather you learned on a dead cipher than on living people. Mistakes are cheaper that way."

Hazel absorbed this in silence. Then she set the compass gently on the bench, aligned its useless needle toward the shelf, toward the ledger standing open-faced to the room, and said, "It points there."

"It always has," said Petra.

* * *

Spring came on. Hazel learned the press, and the ink, and the terrible arithmetic of kerning; she learned which customers paid and which merely apologized; she learned to sweep the way Petra insisted, corners first. In May she set her first complete page alone — a funeral notice, of all things, for a man she'd never met — and pulled the proof and stared at it a long time, at her own words marching backward-then-forward across the sheet, and Petra watching from the window thought: there. That look. Thirty years ago a widow wore it, asking me what I intended to do. Twenty years ago I wore it, teaching the third apprentice. It passes. It has always passed, hand to hand, like a compass too light for what it carries.

At the end of May, the mail coach brought two things on the same morning, which Petra would remember afterward as the sort of coincidence her brother Anton refused to believe in and she herself had long ago stopped questioning. The first was a letter from Frankfurt: a historical society, requesting permission to reprint selections from the reconstructed ledger in a new edition, with scholarly apparatus, "so that the record may stand against the official histories in perpetuity." Anton, whose eyes were failing but whose signature was still beautifully legible, had already added his consent at the bottom.

The second was a package, wrapped in brown paper and tied with twine, postmarked from a university town far to the north. Inside, cushioned in excelsior, lay a brass compass — this compass, the compass, its honey-dark lid scratched now, its hinge loose, its needle swinging idly toward whatever faced it — and a note in a spidery academic hand:

*Found among the effects of the late Dr. F., catalogued and deemed without provenance, as it appears to be merely a compass. Perhaps your society can make use of it. No charge.*

Petra sat with it in her two hands for a long while. Then she crossed the shop, took the ledger down from its open shelf, laid it on the workbench beside the returned instrument, and called across the clatter of the press:

"Hazel. Put down the brayer. There's something you should hold."
