## 1. The Weight of Names

The convoy came back on a Tuesday, and by Thursday the army had decided most of it had never left.

Juniper Lockridge learned this the way she learned everything at the clearing station—gradually, then all at once. A clerk from the transport office appeared at the ward tent flap on Thursday morning with a clipboard and an apologetic cough, and asked Sister Fairweather to strike eleven men from the admission roll. Clerical error, he said. Duplicate entries. The numbers had come through wrong from the embarkation side.

Eleven men. Juniper had sponged the mud off nine of them herself.

She said nothing. She watched Sister Fairweather draw the line through the entries with the particular blankness nurses learn—the face you wear when the thing happening in front of you is unbearable and there are forty more things waiting behind it—and when the clerk left, the ward carried on as wards do. Morphine was drawn up. Dressings were changed. Someone laughed near the stove because a private named Weller told a joke about his own foot, which was gone now, and laughter in a ward is not cruelty but survival, and everyone understood that.

But Juniper could not stop seeing the line drawn through the names.

* * *

She had been at Hazel Bay four months. The clearing station sat on the headland above the town—a cluster of tents and requisitioned buildings that smelled of carbolic and salt, because everything at Hazel Bay smelled of salt eventually. The war had come to this coast gently at first: hospital ships in the harbor, convalescent huts on the green, the fishing fleet still going out at dawn because fish did not care about fronts. Then the spring offensive had pushed the wounded down the line faster than the trains could take them north, and Hazel Bay had become what it was now—a place where men arrived broken and either mended or didn't, and the paperwork of their breaking moved through channels Juniper was not supposed to see.

But she saw it. She was twenty-six and precise and had kept accounts for her father's chandlery before the war, and precision is a kind of sight. She noticed when a man's admission was dated wrong. She noticed when a death was recorded before the man had died, or after he'd already been evacuated. She noticed, above all, when names vanished.

A name, she had come to understand, was the one thing the army could not requisition, though God knew it tried. It could take your boots, your leave, your teeth, your mind, your foot—Private Weller's joke again, unbidden. It could take your home; she'd watched the billeting officers chalk crosses on doors along the harbor road. But a name belonged to a man before the army got him and would belong to him after the army was done, and if the record of it was struck through in pencil by a bored clerk with cold hands, then something true had been stolen, and Juniper Lockridge did not tolerate theft.

That night, after the last rounds, she sat on her cot with a blanket over her knees and a small ledger open on her lap. It had been a shop ledger once—her father's, surplus, its first pages filled with figures for tar and rope in a hand not hers. She ruled a fresh page with her pencil, made columns, and wrote.

*Hollis, Edward. Private, 2nd/7th. Admitted 14th, shrapnel, left thigh. Struck from roll 16th.*

*Bramley, Owen. Sapper. Admitted 14th, gas. Struck 16th.*

Nine names. The tenth she had to reconstruct from memory—the man in bed fourteen who never spoke and whose tags she'd read because someone had to know who he was. *Dunmore, T.* The eleventh was the boy who died on the table Tuesday night while Dr. Pryce worked with both sleeves off, and whose death certificate, when Juniper checked the book, recorded him under a number instead.

She wrote until her hand ached, and the candle guttered, and outside her hut the harbor light swept its slow arc across the water, patient as it had been for a hundred years.

* * *

Maren caught her at it on the third night.

"You're writing letters," Maren said from the tent flap, shaking rain off her cape. "At midnight. By candlelight. To whom, exactly? You've told me twice you have no one."

"I have someone," Juniper said, closing the ledger too late. "I have you."

"Flatterer." Maren hung the cape on the peg and crossed the small hut in three strides, because Maren Voss did nothing in four strides when two would serve. She was Danish-born, army-trained, ten years older than Juniper and unimpressed by everything except competence and good coffee, and she dropped onto the end of the cot without asking, the way people do when they've decided they belong somewhere. Her eyes went to the ledger. "That's not letters."

"No."

"That's a register."

"It's a copy of the admissions roll," Juniper said. "More or less."

Maren took it from her—not snatching, just taking, the way senior sisters took charts—and turned the pages. Juniper let her. There was no point explaining; either Maren would see it or she wouldn't, and explanation only ever muddied the things that mattered.

Maren saw it. Juniper watched her face do the arithmetic: the dates, the struck lines, the neat annotations in Juniper's level hand noting where the official roll disagreed with itself. Maren had worked the embarkation office at Calais for eight months before requesting transfer to the ward. She knew the shape of the paperwork better than Juniper did.

"Where did you get the struck entries?"

"Clerk from transport comes Thursdays."

"And they just—tell you?"

"Sister tells the ward. We adjust our beds." Juniper heard the edge in her own voice and did not file it down. "Fourteen beds, Maren. Eleven men struck in one week. The roll says we have fewer patients than we have blankets. I've counted both."

Maren set the ledger down on the blanket between them, carefully, as if it might go off. Rain drummed on the canvas roof. Somewhere down the hill a sentry called the hour, and beneath everything the sea kept up its long indifferent work against the seawall.

"You know why they do it," Maren said quietly. It wasn't quite a question.

"Convoy went out over strength. Somebody signed for more men than existed on paper, so the extra ones"—Juniper tapped the page—"have to stop existing. Or the salvage contracts don't balance. Or the shipping allocations. Some ledger somewhere has to reconcile, and theirs reconciles easier than mine."

Salvage. She'd pieced that together in fragments—a word overheard, a requisition order misfiled on Sister's desk, the fact that of the men struck from the rolls, none had been discharged, transferred, or buried. They had simply ceased, between one Thursday and the next. Men who die leave bodies and effects and grieving sergeants. These eleven had left nothing at all, as though the tide had taken them cleanly.

"They'll be posted missing," Maren said. "Missing is kind, comparatively. Missing lets mothers hope."

"Mothers shouldn't have to hope on a clerical fiction." Juniper took the ledger back. "Their sons existed. I bathed them. I know Hollis has a wife in Falmouth and Bramley can't abide the smell of oranges since the gas. A name is the smallest thing a man owns and it's the only thing nobody should be able to take off him."

Maren was quiet a long moment. Then, in the practical tone Juniper had already learned meant affection: "You understand what you're holding. If anyone finds that book, it isn't a notebook anymore. It's evidence. Evidence against officers, against the transport pool, possibly against half the civil administration of this coast, because nothing runs this smoothly without local help."

"I've thought about it."

"Have you thought about burning it?"

Every night, in point of fact. She'd thought about how easy it would be—one match, one tin chimney, one small fire among all the fires a war makes legitimate—and how in the morning the eleven men would be exactly as erased as everyone wanted them, and she would sleep, probably, eventually.

"I've thought about it," she said. "I keep coming back to the same thing. Everything else about this war gets requisitioned. Houses, boats, horses, harvests, men. Paper especially—paper moves armies. But a name written down by someone who saw the man alive—" she pressed her palm flat on the ledger's cover, worn cloth over board, warm from her knee— "that's not army property. That's testimony. And testimony doesn't burn just because it's inconvenient."

"You sound like a woman building a case."

"I sound like a woman keeping count," Juniper said. "Somebody has to. The army counts what it wants to keep. Someone ought to count the rest."

Maren looked at her for a long time in the candlelight, and whatever she was weighing, she weighed it fully, because Maren Voss never did anything by halves. Then she reached into her cape pocket, produced a stub of pencil, and held it out.

"Hollis," she said. "Wife's name is Edith. He talks about her when the fever takes him. If you're keeping count, keep all of it."

Juniper wrote it down. *Hollis, Edward. Wife: Edith, Falmouth.*

When Maren had gone to her own hut, Juniper sat a while longer, listening to the rain and the sea and the far-off rumble of lorries on the coast road. Eleven names, and she had begun to suspect they were only the beginning—the first page of something much larger, moving through Hazel Bay like a current under the harbor's skin. Down in the town, lights still burned in the magistrate's window past any honest hour, and she had noticed, because noticing was what she did, that Aldous Crane had begun receiving army visitors who did not sign the visitors' book.

She blew out the candle. In the dark, the ledger lay on her chest like something with a heartbeat.

Three days later, the army came down the hill and took the town.
