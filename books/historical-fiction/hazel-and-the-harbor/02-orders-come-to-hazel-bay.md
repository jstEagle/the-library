## 2. Orders Come to Hazel Bay

The trucks came at dawn on a Tuesday, which was wrong twice over. The army did not travel on Tuesdays—Tuesdays were for supply ledgers and sick parade—and the army never came at dawn. Dawn belonged to the fishing fleet, to the slap of herring on wet planks, to the women hauling ice down the ramp in wheelbarrows. Juniper Lockridge had learned the military's rhythms the way sailors learn weather, and the rhythm said: nothing moves before ten.

Yet there they were, four lorries grinding down the coast road with their headlights still lit against the pale morning, mud up to the axles, soldiers riding the tailboards with their boots dangling. Behind them came a staff car flying a pennant she didn't recognize, and behind that, absurdly, a requisition wagon already empty and hungry.

She was standing in the dispensary doorway with a basin of boiled linen in her arms when the first truck braked outside the harbor office. She counted men. She couldn't help it; counting had become the shape of her mind since the convoy. Eleven, twelve, fourteen. A lieutenant jumped down, consulted a paper, looked up at the town as if checking it against an invoice.

Hazel Bay woke around them without understanding yet what it was waking into. Smoke from breakfast fires. Gulls working the tide line. Old Hollis Barnes arguing with his son about a net that needed mending, his voice carrying across the water the way it had for forty years. Juniper stood very still and watched the lieutenant hand his paper to the harbor master, and watched the harbor master's shoulders change shape as he read it.

The basin of linen went cold in her arms before anyone spoke to her.

* * *

By noon they had nailed proclamations to every door in the lower town, and the town had gathered in the square to hear them read aloud, though half could read well enough themselves and wished they couldn't.

"By order of the Admiralty Supply Directorate, acting under emergency wartime powers—" The lieutenant's voice was flat, administrative, the voice of a man reading someone else's grocery list. "All private dwellings fronting the harbor shall be surrendered for military occupation within seventy-two hours. All seaworthy vessels shall be impressed into coastal service with compensation to be assessed at a later date. All municipal records, registries, deeds, charts, correspondence, and documents of whatever kind shall be surrendered to the designated officer and destroyed by fire, effective immediately."

Destroyed by fire. The words dropped into the square like a stone into still water, and the ripples went outward in faces. Mrs. Trethowan clutched her market basket as if deeds might be hidden among the cabbages. Young Peter Quill started forward and was pulled back by two sets of hands. Somewhere near the back, a woman began quietly to weep—not loudly, not for show, just the exhausted leaking of a person who had already given sons to the war and now found the war wanted her kitchen.

Juniper stood with the nurses from the convalescent station, Maren close at her shoulder. "Deeds," Maren murmured. "They're burning deeds. Why would you burn deeds?"

"So no one can prove what they owned," Juniper said, and heard how it sounded even as she said it, and saw Maren go pale.

It wasn't only the proclamation that turned the square silent. It was who stood beside the lieutenant while he read it. Aldous Crane, magistrate of Hazel Bay, in his best coat and his chain of office polished bright, nodding along to each clause like a man confirming an order at dinner. When the lieutenant finished, Crane stepped forward and raised his hands, and smiled—the wide, warm, practiced smile he used at regattas and harvest fairs.

"Citizens of Hazel Bay," he called. "These are extraordinary times, and extraordinary measures. His Majesty's officers have come to us as partners, not conquerors. I have every assurance that compensation will be fair and generous. I ask you all to cooperate fully, promptly, and cheerfully."

Cheerfully. Juniper watched his face while he said it, and something in her chest went cold and clear, the way water goes when it stops moving. She had seen that particular brightness once before—on a quartermaster's face at the casualty clearing station, the day the convoy's true numbers vanished from the rolls and the major's clerk smiled too easily and too often. She had written the name *Aldous Crane* in her ledger that night, months ago, on instinct alone, a guess dressed up as gossip. Now the guess stood in the square wearing a chain of office, welcoming the fire.

"Cheerful," Maren said under her breath. "He said cheerful."

"He's been corresponding with the Supply Directorate since February," Juniper said. "I've seen his letters pass through the field post."

Maren turned to look at her. "How—"

"Field post passes through our station. Clerks talk. I listen." Which was true, and also not the whole truth, because what she had really done was copy dates and figures into a ledger she kept wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard, and she was not ready to say that out loud in a square full of soldiers.

* * *

They burned the records on Wednesday, in the yard behind the magistrate's hall, in a proper iron incinerator that Crane had somehow arranged to have delivered within a day—a fact Juniper noted, filed, and turned over in her mind like a coin she suspected of being shaved. The deliveries took hours. Registers of births. Registers of deaths. The fishing licenses going back to 1861. Harbor charts annotated in three generations of different handwriting. Tithe books, school logs, the marriage registry with its flyleaf inscribed by some long-dead curate: *May all herein find joy.*

A corporal fed the pages in with a poker-faced efficiency, and a sergeant checked names off a manifest, and Crane supervised from the steps with his hands clasped behind him, watching the smoke with an expression Juniper could not at first classify. Then she could. It was relief. He watched the burning the way a man watches a fever break.

"The past is heavy cargo," he remarked to the lieutenant, pleasantly, as Juniper passed carrying a stretcher of salvaged bandages from the hall's medical stores. "A town should travel light into the future."

"A town should know what it owns," Juniper said.

It was out before she could weigh it. Crane's head turned. For one moment the warmth left his face entirely, and underneath it was something quick and assessing, a fisherman's eye estimating the weight of a catch. Then the warmth returned, seamless.

"Nurse Lockridge." He knew her name. She noted that too. "Your station has done fine work. The Directoriate values its medical personnel most highly." He let the sentence sit, shaped almost like a compliment, functioning exactly like a warning. "Do carry on."

She carried on. Her hands were steady. She was proud of that later, that her hands were steady, walking past the incinerator with its smell of scorched vellum while sixty years of Hazel Bay curled upward in gray ribbons. But that night she knelt on the floor of her room in the nurses' cottage, prised up the board, and unwrapped the oilcloth, and read her own ledger by candlelight with the window curtained.

Thirty-one names. Men struck from the rolls after the convoy disaster, alive or dead or worse than either, unaccounted for. Dates. Ship numbers. Transfer notations copied from scraps and slips and one stolen requisition form. And now, added in fresh ink: *Crane — incinerator ordered 3 weeks prior to arrival of officers. He knew. He asked for this.*

The ledger weighed perhaps nine ounces. It had no official standing whatsoever. It was, in the eyes of any officer in that town, so much kindling waiting for its turn at the iron box.

And that was precisely why her heart beat hard against her ribs as she rewrapped it. Because the proclamation had said *documents of whatever kind*, and she understood now—it landed in her fully, finally—that the burning was not housekeeping but erasure. The dead were already struck from the rolls. Next would be the deeds, so no one could prove whose house stood where; then the registries, so no one could prove who had lived in it; then anything, anywhere, in any hand, that contradicted the tidy story the Supply Directorate intended to tell about why Hazel Bay's harbor, deep and sheltered and improbably perfect for salvage operations, had suddenly become necessary to the war effort.

Her ledger was the last place those thirty-one names existed together in ink.

She thought of the major's clerk at the clearing station, smiling over the corrected rolls. *Paper burns,* he'd told her, kindly, when she questioned the omissions. *Names survive where it matters.* He had meant heaven, or memory, or some comfortable somewhere else. But names did not survive on their own. Names survived because somebody wrote them down and kept them, at cost, against the fire.

Outside, a patrol went past the cottage, boots unhurried on the cobbles. She blew out the candle and sat in the dark with the ledger against her chest, feeling it become, between one breath and the next, a different object entirely. Not a record anymore. Contraband. Nine ounces of evidence with thirty-one lives folded inside it, hidden three feet from where a corporal's torch beam swept the window gap and slid on.

* * *

Thursday the seizures began, and the town's rhythm broke like a plate.

They came for the houses first, chalking numbers on doors—*R7, R12, R19*—and families carried their belongings into the street in the gray drizzle and stood beside them not knowing where to go, because the whole town was seized and there was nowhere in it that wasn't requisitioned. The Trethowans stacked their furniture under sailcloth. Widow Penhale sat on her own doorstep until a soldier politely, endlessly, told her to move, and she moved four feet and sat again.

Then the boats. That was worse to watch. The navy launch stood off the mole while a boarding party went skiff to skiff with a manifest, painting white numbers on hulls that already had names—*Gannet, Sweet Promise, Sarah Ann*—names painted by fathers on sons, names blessed at the quay every spring. The *Sarah Ann*'s owner, Dan Coble, stood on the quay and shouted that compensation was owed, and the boarding officer referred him to a schedule of assessment posted at the harbor office, and Dan shouted that he'd built that boat with his own hands across eleven winters, and the officer, not unkindly, told him to take it up with the assessors, and everyone in the crowd knew by then that the assessors' office would burn its own records if it suited the Directorate, and that there would be nothing left to take anything up to.

"Cheerfully," Maren said beside Juniper, watching. "Remember? Cheerfully and prompt."

"Promptly," Juniper corrected automatically, and then heard herself, and almost laughed, and didn't.

Old Hollis Barnes was the only man who fought it outright, standing in his own deckhouse with a boat hook, and they took him away talking, not violently, just taken, driven off toward the garrison road in a truck while his son stood on the quay doing nothing at all with his whole body shaking. The soldiers were polite. That was the terrible thing Juniper kept arriving at: how polite it all was, how orderly, chalk on doors, numbers on hulls, receipts issued for confiscated nets. Erasure performed with excellent manners.

By evening the harbor lay strange under the lamplight, half its boats gone around the point to the naval anchorage, the rest chained to their moorings under guard. No lamps on the water. No laughter from the quayside pubs, which were quiet as chapels. Hazel Bay had always had a sound underneath its sounds—the sea, yes, but also the work on top of the sea, mending and hauling and auctioning and arguing—and tonight the work was gone and the silence underneath was enormous.

Juniper walked home along the high path with Maren, past the darkened houses with their chalked numbers, and stopped at the rail where the whole harbor showed below: the black water, the guarded boats, the incinerator's cold chimney against the last light.

"They'll come for the station next," Maren said. "Medical records are documents of whatever kind."

"I know."

"And yours aren't medical."

"No," Juniper said. "Mine aren't."

Maren was quiet a moment. The wind came up the path smelling of salt and cold ash. "Juniper. What happens to a person who's caught keeping records the army has ordered burned?"

Juniper looked out at the harbor, at the town that had taken her in when her own commission felt like a costume, at the lit window of the Penn cottage where a dockworker named Silas was visible mending something by lamplight with a small girl beside him learning the work. People who had shown up at the convalescent station with broth and firewood and no questions asked. People now numbered, chained, and scheduled for assessment.

"She's a person holding contraband," Juniper said slowly, "in a town under martial order, with a magistrate who knows her name." She tightened her grip on the rail. "And she has nine days' worth of decisions to make faster than that."

"Why nine days?" Maren asked.

Because Juniper had seen the notice posted at the harbor office that afternoon, small among the large ones: a naval board of inquiry convening in the city, terms of reference unstated, submissions of evidence invited from any party. Invited. In writing. With a date.

"Because," she said, turning from the rail, "the truth is going to need a passport—and I'm going to need help carrying it."
