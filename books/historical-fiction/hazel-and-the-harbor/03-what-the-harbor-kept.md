## 3. What the Harbor Kept

Silas Penn had hauled a thousand crates off the *Merrivale* and her sister boats, and he knew by weight alone what each of them carried. Herring. Cod. Ice. A crate that should have run forty pounds and came up against his forearms at thirty-eight meant one thing: somebody had put something inside that wasn't fish.

He didn't say so. He just set the crate down on the packing table in the back of Hollis's shed, out of the lamplight, and waited.

The nurse — Lockridge, though she'd told him to call her Juniper, which he hadn't yet done and didn't intend to for another week at least — stood with her hands folded tight enough that her knuckles had gone the color of the ice chips scattered on the floor. Tilly hovered near her elbow like a small determined shadow. Outside, the tide was going out over the flats, and somewhere down the quay a soldier was laughing at something, and Silas found he could hear both sounds at once and hated them equally.

"You looked," Juniper said. Not accusing. Just noting it, the way she seemed to note everything.

"I lift things for a living." He wiped his hands on his apron. "A man learns what forty pounds of herring feels like."

Tilly snorted. Juniper's mouth twitched, then went serious again, and she reached past him into the crate and lifted out the ledger.

Silas had heard about the book from Maren Quill, who'd mentioned it once, sideways, the way you'd mention a leak in a hull — quietly, where no one official could hear. But hearing about a thing and watching a slim young woman hold it under a fish-packing lamp were different animals entirely. It was bound in gray cloth gone soft at the corners, swollen with whatever was stitched inside, and she held it the way some men held a christening child: careful, and like it might be taken from her.

"You know what happens to us if they find this," Silas said.

"Yes."

"In wartime, harboring contraband—"

"It isn't contraband." She opened the book on the salt-stained table, and the lamplight fell across page after page of names, written in a hand so steady it made him uneasy. "It's the opposite of contraband. It's the only part of those men the army hasn't requisitioned yet."

Silas looked at the names. He'd have kept looking all night if Tilly hadn't leaned in first, tracing one with a finger too quick to stop.

"That's Mr. Voss," she said. "He mended nets. My dad used to drink with him."

Juniper went still. "You knew him?"

"Everybody knew everybody here, before your lot built the hospital on the hill." Tilly said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "He had a laugh like a gull getting strangled."

Silas put his hand on his niece's shoulder, mostly to stop himself from saying something he'd regret to a woman in a nurse's uniform. But Juniper didn't bristle. She turned the book toward Tilly, and Silas watched the girl read her way down the column, and watched her face do the arithmetic of grief — one name, two names, five — before she stepped back and pressed her lips together and said nothing else at all.

That was when Silas decided. He didn't decide the way Juniper probably decided things, with lists and reasons. He decided the way a man decides to bail a leaking boat: not because the math works out, but because stopping means drowning faster.

"All right," he said. "The ice crate. You want it to travel as fish."

"Fish travels everywhere," Juniper said. "Soldiers don't open fish. Maren says the smell is better than any seal."

"Maren Quill," Silas said, "has spent too much time around sailors if she believes that." He pulled the second crate toward him, the empty one, and began fitting the false bottom he'd built years ago for a smuggling run he'd never speak of to a woman in uniform. "But she's right about soldiers. Now tell me why. Tell me why a book of names matters more than your neck. Because I've got a girl to keep alive, and I need to understand what I'm risking her for."

* * *

She told him while they worked. That was the trick of it, Silas thought later — she talked while her hands packed sawdust around the ledger's hiding place, so that the story came out level and unhurried, mixed in with the ordinary business of nails and ice, as if grief were just another cargo to be stowed properly.

The convoy, she said. The one in March that the papers called a success because the tonnage arrived. What the papers didn't count was the men. Forty-one wounded soldiers transferred off the hospital ship at Hazel Bay for convalescence, entered on the rolls, fed from army stores — and then, after the storm took the pier, struck from every list she could find. Vanished administratively. As if the sea had taken them along with the pilings.

"But they weren't drowned," Silas said. His hands had stopped working without his permission.

"No."

"Because I've seen men since. Down at the east slip, loading at night. Army lorries come down, and men go aboard the salvage barge, and I thought—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I thought they were prisoners, maybe. Or deserters being sent somewhere worse."

"They're your erased names," Juniper said quietly. "I checked the manifests through a friend in the quartermaster's office. The barge crews are drawn from the same rolls that lost their entries. Men who officially don't exist can be worked officially anywhere. There's a wreck out past the shoals — a munition ship, or something the Admiralty doesn't want named — and someone is salvaging it off the record, selling what comes up, and using dead-on-paper soldiers to raise it."

The shed was very quiet. Ice ticked somewhere in the drains. Out on the water a bell buoy clanked its slow patient count.

"Voss," Tilly said, from the doorway. "Mr. Voss is on that barge?"

"Likely," Silas said, before Juniper could soften it, because the girl deserved truth even when it tasted like bilge. Then, to Juniper: "And Crane. The magistrate signed the requisition orders with a smile on him. You're telling me he knows."

"He profits," Juniper said. "I don't have proof of that yet. But the requisitions strip this town of boats and homes, and the salvage operation needs a quiet harbor with no fishing fleet asking questions. Hazel Bay isn't being seized, Mr. Penn. It's being emptied. There's a difference, and the difference is money."

Silas fitted the last slat into the false bottom and pressed it home. Thirty-nine pounds now. Two pounds of herring on top, real ones, gutted and packed honest. Underneath, forty-one names and whatever it was names turned into when you stacked them together.

"My brother," he said. He hadn't planned to say it. It came out anyway, the way water comes through a seam. "Tilly's father. Lost at the war start, but I got the telegram, so at least I knew. These families—" He gestured at the crate. "They don't even get to know. They get nothing. An empty chair and an empty record and everyone assuming the worst quietly."

"That's why I keep the ledger," Juniper said. "A man's name is the one thing they can't requisition. Everything else — his coat, his pay, his grave — they can take or fudge or burn. But write the name down, in ink, witnessed, and he exists whether they like it or not. That's the whole of it. That's all I've got."

It wasn't much, Silas thought. One book, one nurse, one dockworker, one girl. Against an army.

But he'd been to sea long enough to know that most rescues started looking exactly that lopsided.

"Then we keep it here," he said. "In the ice crate, rotated fresh daily — ice melts, so either Maren or I swap it every morning among the outbound boxes, and no pattern, no schedule. Tilly—"

"I want to help."

"—carries nothing, goes nowhere, tells nobody, including especially her friend Pip who couldn't keep a secret if you sewed it into him." Tilly's face did the mutinous thing, then the calculating thing, then settled on grudging acceptance, which was as good as oaths got from a twelve-year-old.

"And you?" Juniper asked. "Why are you helping me? You don't know me."

Silas considered the question honestly, because she'd earned honesty and he suspected she'd catch anything less. "Because three weeks ago this town ran on herring and gossip and whose kids were whose, and now there are soldiers on the quay and a magistrate smiling like he's been paid for it, and everyone's walking around like the weather's turned permanent. You're the only person I've seen fight it. So." He shrugged. "Strangers become conspirators. That's how it goes. Usually over drink, but salted fish will serve."

Tilly laughed — a real one, brief and startling — and even Juniper smiled, tired and true, and something shifted in the shed that Silas couldn't have named but recognized anyway. It was the same thing that happened when a crew came together after a bad season. Not trust, not yet. Just the beginning shape of it, the decision to pull the same oars.

They worked until the small hours. By lamplight they moved the ledger into its new home, packed the crate into the ice house behind a wall of boxed cod, and burned Juniper's notes in the stove while Tilly fed the flames page by page like it was a funeral and she meant it. When they finished, the three of them stood in the cold shed smelling of salt and smoke, co-conspirators now, and none of them quite knew what to say to close the occasion.

It was Tilly who broke it, tugging Juniper's sleeve.

"If Mr. Voss is alive on that barge," she said, "does that mean the names in your book aren't dead? They're just... hidden?"

Juniper looked at the stove, where the ashes of her notes were going gray. Silas watched her choose her words with the care of a woman defusing something.

"It means," she said finally, "that the book isn't a memorial anymore. It's a rescue."

Silas felt those words settle into him and stay, heavy as ballast.

And it was three mornings later, swapping the ice at dawn with the fog still lying on the water like a held breath, that he saw the army lorries rolling down toward the east slip again — and behind them, riding in the second lorry with her hands folded and her face like winter, Magistrate Crane's housekeeper, carrying a message that would reach the magistrate himself by noon:

that a nurse at the convalescent hospital had been seen, more than once, carrying a gray-covered book into the fish sheds.
