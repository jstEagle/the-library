## 4. Three Checkpoints

Maren had worn the uniform for three years and had never once thought of it as armor. It was wool that itched, boots that blistered, a cap that left a red band across her forehead by the end of a twelve-hour shift. It was a way to be useful. Now, standing at the window of Silas Penn's back room with Juniper's ledger open on the table behind her, she looked at her own reflection in the darkened glass — sister nurse, gray cloak, red cross on the armband — and thought: *I could carry it. They'd never stop me.*

The realization frightened her more than the checkpoints did.

Behind her, the room was full. It should not have been full; Silas's back room was meant for rope and tar pots, not seven people and a lamp turned low. But Hazel Bay had learned in two weeks how to fold itself smaller. Tilly sat cross-legged on a coil of hemp, chin propped on her fists, pretending with the fierce discipline of a ten-year-old that she wasn't listening to every word. Old Hewitt from the herring boats had brought a pencil stub and a scrap of sailcloth. Juniper stood at the table with her hands flat on either side of the ledger, as if it might get up and walk without her.

Nine days. That was what the fishmonger's boy had come whispering at dusk: the naval inquiry would sit in the city on the ninth day, and after that the salvage operation would be signed into law like weather, like tide, like something no one had chosen.

"Three checkpoints between here and the inquiry," Maren said, turning from the window. "Everyone knows it. Nobody's said it out loud all week, so I'll say it."

She took the pencil from Hewitt and drew on the sailcloth, because her hands needed the work.

"The causeway first. It's the only road out of the bay, so they've built it up proper — sandbags, wire, a corporal who looks bored and isn't. He checks papers going out. Everything leaving Hazel Bay gets opened." She drew a second mark, far along the coast. "The ferry yard. The ferry's been requisitioned, but they run it twice a day for troops, and there's a garrison sergeant there with a list longer than my arm. And last—" She hesitated over the third mark, then made it hard, pressing down. "The garrison bridge. That one's not a checkpoint so much as a statement. Stone arches, searchlights, a captain who signs everything personally. Nothing crosses that bridge unless he wants it crossed."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a soldier's boots rang on the quay, unhurried, a man walking through a town that belonged to him.

"So we don't use the road," Hewitt said.

"The road is the only way," Juniper said quietly. "The estuary narrows past the ferry yard. Cliffs below the bridge. You'd need wings."

"We could try the water," Silas said. He said little, and when he spoke it came out slow, like something hauled up from depth. "I've rowed that estuary since before I could swim. There's a night I could slip past the ferry yard."

"And the bridge?" Maren asked. "Searchlights on the water too, Silas. And if they pull you out of the water with that book on you, you're not a nurse with papers. You're a smuggler caught red-handed. They can do what they like."

"Then it wants a courier who can't be touched," Hewitt said, and everyone looked at Maren before she had finished saying it.

She let them look. She had known this moment was coming since she'd seen her own face in the glass, and she had already argued herself through it twice in the dark, once losing and once winning. Now, out loud, in front of all of them, she made it sound simple.

"My uniform opens doors your boats can't. Nurses move between stations all the time — I've got travel authority to the city hospital, signed months ago, still valid. A red cross and a medical bag. They open the bag, they see dressings, morphine tins, a change of stockings. They apologize for delaying me." She tapped the sailcloth map. "I go through all three checkpoints in one day, on the morning supply rotation, and I'm at the inquiry steps before the lamps are lit."

"It's not your fight," Silas said. It wasn't an objection. It was him giving her the door.

"It stopped being only your fight the night Crane burned the parish register on the quay with the whole town watching." She heard her own voice come out harder than she intended, and softened nothing. "I wrote some of those names down too, Silas. After the convoy. Juniper wasn't the only one keeping count; she was just the only one brave enough to write it in a book instead of on her heart where it could be mislaid."

Juniper looked up at that. Something passed between them — not gratitude exactly, Maren thought. Recognition. Two women who had counted the same dead and refused to stop.

Tilly raised her hand, solemn as a magistrate, and waited until Silas nodded permission.

"What about a decoy?"

Every adult in the room turned to look at a child sitting on a coil of rope, and Tilly, unbothered, explained: "At school, when Mrs. Ellery catches someone passing notes, whoever holds the empty hand never gets blamed. If the ledger's real, shouldn't there be a book that isn't?" She shrugged. "Soldiers look until they find something. Then they stop looking."

The room was quiet long enough that Maren heard the harbor breathing against the pilings. Out of the mouths of babes, as her mother used to say, and God help us that it takes a fatherless child to teach tactics.

"She's right," Maren said slowly. "They find something, they stop. Every search I've ever walked through works that way. Give them a prize and they'll close their hands around it."

"Then we make them a prize," Juniper said. She pulled a second, cheap notebook from her coat — the kind schoolchildren used — and set it beside the ledger. "Same size. Same cover, aged with tea. Names in it too, but wrong ones. Plausible ones. If it comes to a choice, let them seize a lie and feel clever about it."

* * *

They worked through the night, and Maren understood, somewhere past the middle of it, that this was what belonging felt like from the inside: lamplight, salted fish, six pairs of hands doing small necessary things toward one impossible end.

Juniper copied names in a careful counterfeit hand, missingpellings deliberate, dates subtly wrong, until the decoy looked like exactly what a frightened woman might keep. Hewitt drew the route again on better paper, marking sentry rotations guessed from two weeks of watching. Silas soaked a canvas satchel in fish brine until it smelled convincingly of his trade, then lined a false bottom into Maren's medical bag with a surgeon's neatness he had no business owning. Tilly, forbidden from anything else, was given the task of aging the decoy's cover with cold tea and did it with the concentration of a girl painting an icon.

And Maren memorized. That was her part, and she took it seriously as scripture: every name in the true ledger, in order, so that if the worst happened — if the bag was taken, if she was searched beyond apology — the record would still exist, carried in the one place no corporal could open.

"Say them back," Juniper said near dawn, and Maren said them back, all forty-one, while the others went still around the table like people at a graveside. When she finished, Juniper closed the ledger and held it a moment before sliding it into the satchel's false bottom.

"You understand what you're carrying," Juniper said.

"Names," Maren said.

"Lives that the army has decided didn't happen. If you're caught with it, they won't court-martial you. They'll simply erase you too, and there'll be no one left to keep the count."

"I know." Maren tied the satchel strings flat against her ribs. "That's why it has to be me. You'd all be erased. I'll just be delayed."

* * *

Two days later, on the last evening before the supply rotation, the plan came apart an inch at a time, the way plans do, and Maren saw the shape of it too late.

Word came down from the garrison that all outbound travelers would now be processed at the causeway checkpoint personally, including medical personnel, including clergy, including women. A new order, freshly typed, posted on the customs house door where the whole town could read its own narrowing world. And beneath the order, a notice that turned Maren's blood to seawater:

*All persons holding diaries, ledgers, or private correspondence bearing upon military operations will surrender such documents at checkpoint one. Possession thereafter constitutes espionage.*

"They know," Juniper said. She had read the notice once, twice, and her voice stayed level in the way Maren had learned meant the opposite. "Someone told them a ledger exists. Not what's in it. Not who carries it. Just that it does."

Crane, Maren thought. It had Crane's handwriting all over it, his tidy malice dressed in army type. He couldn't prove anything yet, so he would make the whole town a search party against itself.

At Silas's back room, the sailcloth map lay on the table under the lamp, three checkpoints marked like wounds. Nine days had become seven.

"They'll open the medical bag now," Hewitt said grimly. "Uniform won't save you if they're searching everyone properly."

"It might," Tilly said.

They all looked at her again.

"Mum always said soldiers don't search what they pity," Tilly said. Her voice was steady; her hands, Maren noticed, were not. "Let them find something sad instead of something secret."

Nobody asked her to explain. Nobody wanted to be the one to understand what she meant.

Silas put his hand flat on the map, over the first mark, the causeway, and left it there a long moment, as if he could hold the road shut by pressure alone.

"Low tide's Thursday, dawn," he said. "Wreaths travel to graves without questions. My brother's marker's out past the checkpoint line, and Tilly's due to lay flowers, orders of nobody, permission of nobody, because grief doesn't carry papers."

Maren looked at the child who had taught them decoys, who was now being fitted, by the slow turn of every adult head in the room, for something heavier than any of them had the right to ask.

"Thursday," she said. "Dawn. The causeway first."

Outside, the searchlights came on across the garrison bridge, sweeping the black water with patient, idiot confidence, looking for a book.
