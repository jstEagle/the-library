## 5. The Causeway at Low Tide

Tilly had counted the steps of the causeway since she was six years old, when her father first set her on the sea wall and told her to walk it like a tightrope, arms out, chin up, eyes on the far end and nowhere else. *Don't look down at the water*, he'd said. *The water only wants you to fall.*

Two hundred and eleven steps at low tide. She'd counted them again this morning before the fog lifted, walking the route empty-handed so her feet would know it without the weight. Now the weight was in her hands, wrapped in wire and pine boughs, and it was heavier than any wreath had a right to be.

It didn't look heavy. That was the whole point.

Uncle Silas had built the frame himself, bending green hazel into a hoop no different from the dozens of wreaths Hazel Bay had hung on its doors since the war began. Inside the hoop, where flowers should have gone, sat the ledger—wrapped in oilcloth, then in sacking, then bound tight with tarred twine until the shape of it vanished into the green. Maren had stripped the winter jasmine from the churchyard wall and worked the stems through the boughs until the wreath looked mourned-over, properly wept on, the kind of thing a girl might carry two miles to lay on a grave because there was no one older to do it for her.

"It's a good lie," Maren had said last night, kneeling on Silas's kitchen floor with sap on her fingers. "The best kind. It's nearly true."

Tilly hadn't answered. Her father wasn't in the churchyard. He was under the sea somewhere off the Heads, where the mines got his trawler in the second year of the war, and the army had never sent so much as a card. But she understood what Maren meant. A girl carrying a wreath for her drowned father—that was a story so sad and so ordinary that no corporal in a starched collar would want to touch it. Grief was the one cargo that moved through checkpoints free.

She just had to carry it like it was real.

And it was real, that was the thing. The wreath was for her father whether or not the ledger rode inside it. She'd told them that at the kitchen table, flat and plain, and Uncle Silas had looked at her the way he did when she gutted a fish faster than the grown men at the next bench—surprised, and trying not to show it.

"You don't have to be the one," Juniper had said. Juniper always said things gently, like she was setting each word down on a cloth.

"Nobody else can be the one," Tilly said. "Maren's uniform is wrong now—the garrison's watching for nurses. You're on their lists, Miss Lockridge, you know you are. And Uncle Silas has hands like an anchor chain. Anybody looks at him twice." She shrugged, the way she did when the men at the fish market made a fuss over her. "I'm nobody. Nobody checks nobody."

That had been last night. This morning the fog was lifting off the flats in long grey ribbons, and the tide was out, and the causeway stood up out of the water like a spine.

*Two hundred and eleven steps. Eyes on the far end.*

* * *

The checkpoint sat at the landward end, where the causeway narrowed to the width of a cart. They'd thrown up a hut of fresh-sawn boards that still smelled of pitch, sandbags stacked at the corners, a barrier arm made from a boat's spar. Two sentries, and behind them a corporal with a red-striped cap band who was doing something fussy and important to a clipboard.

Tilly knew him by sight already. Corporal Dawes. He'd stopped her auntie Bess on the market road last week and turned out her basket—herring, all of it, spilled into the mud while he looked for nothing. He was the kind of soldier who'd been given a little power the way other men were given a cold, and it sat in him just as badly.

She walked. Not fast, not slow. The way you walked to a grave.

"Halt." Dawes didn't look up from his clipboard right away. When he did, his eyes went over her the way they'd gone over Auntie Bess's basket—top to bottom, finding everything wanting. "Where d'you think you're going, missy?"

"The churchyard." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. She made it bigger. "To my dad. He's got a stone there. I go Thursdays."

"There's no traffic across the causeway except authorized." Dawes flipped a page he hadn't been reading. "Army order forty-one."

"He's dead," Tilly said. "Your order didn't say anything about that."

One of the sentries snorted and turned it into a cough. Dawes's neck went pink above his collar.

"Set that down." He nodded at the wreath. "All persons and effects crossing military installations are subject to inspection."

*Inspection.* The word went through her like a hook through the gills. She'd practiced this part—Silas had made her practice, holding the wreath out to him in the kitchen, saying *there, see, only boughs and flowers*—but practicing and standing here were different animals entirely. If he pulled the jasmine aside, if he pressed his thumb into the sacking, if the twine slipped—

Her hands wouldn't move.

Then they did. She set the wreath down on the sea wall, careful, the way you'd set down something sleeping, and stepped back, and folded her hands in front of her skirt so he couldn't see them shaking.

Dawes picked it up one-handed, testing the weight, and she watched his eyebrows move.

"That's heavy for flowers," he said.

"It's the wet," Tilly said. "Fog this morning. Everything's soaked through." And God, oh God, it was even true—she'd carried it along the shore road with dew coming down off the boughs, and the outer sacking was damp clean through. Truth was better armor than lying. She'd learned that from Juniper, though Juniper probably didn't know she'd taught it.

Dawes turned the wreath in his hands. The jasmine shivered. His thumb found the wire frame, followed it around the hoop, and stopped where the sacking bulged thickest, where the ledger's spine pressed against the boughs like a swallowed stone.

He was going to pull it apart. Tilly saw it happen in her head before it happened in the world: the twine unwinding, the oilcloth, the names sliding out onto the causeway stones for the tide to take, and after that the soldiers coming up the hill to Silas's house with their rifles unslung—

A voice came down the causeway like a gull dropping out of the sky.

"I have told you once, Tom Cardew, and I will not tell you again—you will not beach that punt on my moorings, because the last time you beached it you cracked two planks and blamed the tide—"

Old Ned Fennimore, seventy if he was a day, storming along the sea wall with a boathook in one fist and his trousers rolled to the knee, and hard behind him came Tom Cardew, half a boat's length younger and twice as loud, shouting back that Ned's moorings were Crown property now anyway, seized under requisition, so what did Ned care whose planks got cracked—

They collided into the checkpoint like weather. Ned swung the boathook; Tom caught it; the two of them went round and round between the sandbags, swearing in the old harbor language that predates decency, and both sentries turned as one man to watch, because a fight is a fight wherever you find it, and there had been very little entertainment on the causeway since the army came.

Only Dawes held back, wreath in hand, torn between duty and the show—and Tilly saw the exact moment the show won, because he was young under the cap band, and young men look at fights the way dogs look at doors.

"Break it up," he said, stepping toward them. "Break it up, I said—"

Tilly took the wreath off the sea wall.

She didn't run. Running was the one thing Silas had forbidden above all others—*nothing makes a soldier look twice like a person in a hurry*—so she walked, two hundred and eleven steps with her chin up and her eyes fixed on the far end where the churchyard cypress showed above the wall, and behind her the quarrel roared and crashed and Ned's boathook clattered on the stones, and not one of them—not Dawes, not the sentries—looked back at the small girl in the black dress going about her grief.

The far end came. It always came; her father had taught her that too. She passed the last stone of the causeway and stepped onto the shore path, and only then did her legs turn to water, and she sat down hard in the tall grass with the wreath hugged to her chest, breathing like she'd swum the estuary.

Inside the boughs, faint as a heartbeat, she could feel the ledger's edge against her ribs.

*Forty-one names,* Juniper had said. *Men the army says were never there.* Tilly pressed her cheek against the sacking. *You're safe. You're safe. Don't tell anyone I said so.*

In the churchyard she laid the wreath on her father's stone—lifted the ledger out first and pushed it deep into the ivy at the monument's base, where Juniper would collect it after dark—and knelt on the wet grass with her hands folded, and found, to her own surprise, that she was actually praying. Not for her father exactly. For all of them, the ones with names on paper and the one with a name on stone.

She stayed a good while. It seemed the respectful thing, and also the safest: a girl visiting a grave should stay a while.

When she finally walked home, cutting along the top lane, she heard the causeway before she saw it—Ned and Tom still at it, or at it again, their voices carrying clear across the flats. By the time she reached the bend above the road, they'd let themselves be dragged apart by both sentries, and Corporal Dawes was berating them about army orders, and Tom Cardew was demanding, red-faced, to know why the army could seize a man's boat but couldn't keep two neighbors from settling their own accounts.

Everyone laughing now, even the sentries. Even Dawes, sourly, wanting the whole business gone and forgotten.

Tilly crouched in the hedge and watched, and understood, with a cold clean clarity that frightened her a little, that she had crossed a line this morning and could not cross back. She had lied to a soldier with contraband in her arms and gotten away with it, and it had felt like tightrope-walking, like her father's hand under her elbows, like the far end of the beam coming closer step by step.

She also heard what Ned said last, pitched to carry, as the soldiers waved him off:

"Two more crossings yet, Cardew! You'll not be rid of me till every mooring in this harbor's mine!"

Two more crossings. The ferry yard. The garrison bridge.

Tilly ran the rest of the way home to tell them the plan had held—and that Corporal Dawes had touched the ledger's spine with his own thumb, and would be looking at every wreath, every basket, every mourning dress from now on.

The next one through would need more than a lie.

They'd need a miracle with a coffin in it.
