## 6. Crane's Bargain

He came to the cottage at dusk, without escort, wearing his good coat.

Juniper knew Aldous Crane by sight the way everyone in Hazel Bay did—by the silver-topped cane he didn't need and the smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of his face. She had watched him shake hands with Colonel Merrick on the quay the day the requisition began, watched him clap the man's shoulder like an old friend welcoming him home from war. Now here was the magistrate himself on her doorstep, hat in hand, looking for all the world like a neighbor come to borrow sugar.

"Nurse Lockridge." He said her name as though tasting it. "May I come in? There's a wind off the water tonight that would strip paint."

She stepped aside because refusing would tell him more than admitting him. He crossed her small sitting room with the unhurried gait of a man who already owned whatever room he entered, paused at the window to admire the harbor—*his* harbor, she thought, or soon to be—and settled into the chair opposite hers without being asked.

"You'll forgive the late hour. My days are no longer my own since the army arrived. I imagine yours aren't either."

"I'm on duty at six," Juniper said. "Say what you've come to say."

He smiled again, that half-second early. "Direct. Good. I've always admired directness in a woman; it saves such tedious negotiation." He set his hat on his knee and smoothed the brim with one gloved thumb. "I'm told you keep a ledger."

The room did not tilt. She had spent two years learning not to flinch—at amputations, at the sound of men calling for mothers who were three hundred miles away—so she did not flinch now. But inside, cold moved through her like tide through a channel: *he knows.*

"A ledger of what?" she asked.

"Come now." He leaned back, folding his hands over the cane. "Names. Soldiers' names. Men struck from the rolls after the March convoy. I'm told your handwriting is quite legible, quite disciplined—the hand of a professional woman. The kind of hand that keeps records even when it isn't asked to."

"Who told you?"

Crane waved the question away like smoke. "Hazel Bay is a small town, Nurse Lockridge. Small towns are transparent. Every secret passes through four or five houses before breakfast, and I hear most of them." He paused, and something shifted in his face—not softer, exactly, but more attentive, the way a fisherman watches where the water darkens. "What you may not appreciate is how many people could be made to suffer for what you're carrying."

"There is nothing to carry."

"Then this will be a very short conversation, and we can both have our suppers." He waited. He was good at waiting; she'd grant him that. The clock on her mantel ticked like something counting down. Outside, a gull cried once over the darkening water, and somewhere down the lane a door slammed—soldiers turning families out of their homes for the night billets, the new rhythm of Hazel Bay, boots where nets used to be.

Juniper sat still and let him wait instead.

After a moment, Crane sighed, almost fondly. "Very well. Let me be plainer, since plainness seems to be your currency. In nine days there will be a naval inquiry in the city. You may have heard—I understand certain parties in this town have designs on attending." Her face gave nothing away, and he nodded as if she'd confirmed everything. "That inquiry will examine the requisition of this port, the disposition of impressed vessels, and—incidentally—the administrative irregularities following the March convoy. Irregularities. That is the official term. *Irregularities.*"

"And you're worried about irregularities," Juniper said. "A magistrate's conscience keeping him up nights."

"My conscience sleeps beautifully." He said it without a trace of shame, almost cheerfully, and that was the moment she truly understood what she was dealing with. Not a corrupt man tormented by corruption—a comfortable man, one who had long ago arranged his soul the way he arranged his ledgers, every entry balanced, nothing left outstanding. "I'm worried about *waste*, Nurse Lockridge. About a town burning itself down around a piece of paper that changes nothing."

"If it changes nothing," she said quietly, "why are you here?"

For the first time, the smile slipped. Just slightly. Just enough.

* * *

He recovered quickly, but she had seen it—the flicker under the varnish—and she held to it like a handhold on a cliff.

Crane rose, walked to her window, and looked out at the harbor with the air of a man surveying property. The last light was going copper on the water. Three army transports rode at anchor where the fishing fleet should have been, and along the quay, lamplighters moved between seized buildings like ghosts tending other ghosts' houses.

"Do you know what happens to a place like this when the war ends?" he asked, not turning. "Nothing. It rots politely. The boats get older, the young men leave or don't come back, and the government forgets Hazel Bay exists until it needs something from it again. I have spent eleven years as magistrate of this town trying to keep it alive with my bare hands. Do you know what the army paid me to do? To smooth the transfer. To make it orderly. To ensure that when the salvage operation needed harbor space and labor, no one inconvenienced them with petitions and protests and—" he turned, and his eyes went to her, sharp as a gutting knife "—*ledgers*."

"Salvage," Juniper repeated. Silas had suspected it; Maren had traced the supply orders; but hearing it aloud from Crane's mouth was different. Hearing it was confirmation. Confirmation was a weapon. "You mean the men struck from the rolls. Pressed onto the hulks in the outer roads. Salvage crews."

"Recovery operations," Crane said mildly. "Contracted work. Perfectly legal under emergency statutes that I helped interpret for the military governor. Those men are being paid, fed, housed. Better than some of them ever were here. And when their service concludes, they'll be returned to the rolls—or compensated—according to schedule."

"Their families think they're dead."

"Their families think they're dead," Crane agreed, "because dead men's wives don't petition, don't protest, don't write to the Admiralty asking inconvenient questions. Grief is quiet, Nurse Lockridge. It is the most governable condition known to man. Whereas hope—" he shook his head with real regret "—hope is a fire in a wooden town."

Juniper stood. Her legs were steadier than she expected. "Get out of my house."

"No." He didn't move. "Because you haven't heard the offer yet, and you're too intelligent to send me off before the offer. Here it is, plainly, as one professional to another. Hand the ledger to me—tonight, quietly, no witnesses—and tomorrow morning your file at the nursing directorate becomes clean. Wholly clean. Your superintendant's objections to your field conduct vanish. Your commission, which certain officers have been circling like gulls, is safe. No inquiry summons you. No garrison searches your effects. You finish your service with distinction and go wherever women of your training go after wars—London, probably. A ward of your own. A life."

He said it gently. That was the obscene part—he offered to buy her with her own future, wrapped and ribboned, and expected gratitude.

"And Hazel Bay?" she asked.

"Hazel Bay is a rounding error." He spread his hands, magnanimous. "In five years it will be a coaling station with a memorial plaque, and no one will remember the names except the stones. You cannot stop the sea from rising, Nurse Lockridge. You can only decide whether you drown with the townsfolk or climb aboard a perfectly sound vessel."

"You mean whether I sell them."

"I mean whether you *survive*." For the first time something raw came into his voice—not anger, but impatience, the frustration of a man watching someone refuse obvious arithmetic. "Because here is the part you haven't considered, in all your brave bookkeeping. That ledger doesn't exist alone. Names on paper require hands to carry paper. Hands belong to people. People can be named." He picked up his hat from his knee and turned it slowly, considering. "When the garrison learns that ledger exists—and they will learn, tonight, within the hour, because I will tell them if you force me—they will not content themselves with hunting a notebook. They will want the courier. The accomplices. The fishermen who've been rowing at odd hours. The nurse in army uniform who volunteers for courier duty no one assigned her to. The child seen carrying a mourning wreath through the causeway checkpoint last week."

The cold went through Juniper then, all the way through, and came out the far side as something harder.

Tilly. He had watched Tilly walk through the causeway with her father's wreath, and he had filed her away, twelve years old, in the column marked *leverage.*

"You would name a child," Juniper said.

"I would *protect* a child," Crane corrected, "by ensuring the garrison never has reason to look at her. That is what I'm offering—all of it, the whole package. Immunity for you, invisibility for them, a quiet end to a quiet affair. One book, burned in a barrel behind the customs house, and by Thursday it's as though none of it ever happened. The men on the hulks stay on schedule. The town stays orderly. Everyone lives." He set the hat on his head, adjusting the angle in the window's reflection like a man dressing for dinner. "Or you keep your paper. You keep your principles. And I keep my records too, Nurse Lockridge—I know which houses the conspirators sleep in, and I will read those names aloud to Colonel Merrick myself, with great precision, in my best magistrate's voice. And then we'll see whose ledger costs more."

The clock ticked. The copper light died off the water. Down in the harbor, a transport's anchor chain rattled out link by link into the dark, a sound like something enormous being lowered onto something fragile.

"Nine days," Juniper said.

"Eight, now." Crane smiled his half-second-early smile and moved toward the door. At the threshold he paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at her with something that in a better man might have been respect. "I'll call tomorrow for your answer. Sleep on it. Brave women so rarely grasp that courage has interest rates." Then, softly, almost kindly: "Everyone you love, in exchange for one book. It isn't even a difficult sum."

The door closed behind him. Juniper stood in the dark of her sitting room until his footsteps faded down the lane, then lit no lamp, packed no bag. She went instead to the loose stone behind the flour bin and took out the oilcloth bundle, and held it, and felt its weight change in her hands—it had never felt heavy before, and now it weighed like a sleeping household, like Tilly's breath in the icehouse loft, like Maren's laugh, like Silas's scarred hands hauling a net.

*One book,* Crane had said. *It isn't even a difficult sum.*

He was wrong about the arithmetic. But he had told her something valuable without meaning to: he was afraid. A man confident of winning does not ride to a nurse's cottage at dusk to bargain. A man confident of winning burns the ledger's keeper, not the ledger.

Which meant there was still time—but less of it than nine days, and every hour she kept silent, Crane was writing his own list of names.

She wrapped the bundle again and went to find Silas.
