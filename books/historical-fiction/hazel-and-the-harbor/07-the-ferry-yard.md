## 7. The Ferry Yard

Silas Penn had spent thirty years learning what the estuary would forgive and what it wouldn't. It forgave a late tide, a torn net, a man's bad temper. It did not forgive carelessness after dark, when the channel markers went dark too and the mudflats reached up like hands to take the keel. He knew this the way he knew his own knuckles — by having broken against it enough times.

So when Juniper laid out the plan in the back of his fish shed, he said nothing for a long while. Just turned his pipe over in his fingers and looked at the ice crate where the ledger slept under a foot of herring.

"You're thinking," Maren said. She stood with her arms crossed, her nurse's uniform brushed clean for once, though she'd rolled the sleeves the way she always did when she meant business.

"I'm thinking a woman in uniform walks through a checkpoint every day without a second look," Silas said. "And I'm thinking a rowboat on black water is another thing entirely."

"That's why there are two ledgers," Tilly said from atop the salt barrels, legs swinging. She was eleven and had appointed herself to every conversation whether invited or not. "One real and one not-real."

"One true and one lie," Juniper corrected gently. She'd made the decoy herself, three nights running, copying names into an old shipping manifest until her hand cramped. To Silas it looked identical. That was the point, and also the problem. "Maren carries the false one through the ferry yard. If they search her, they find paper and believe they've found everything. Meanwhile Silas takes the true one across the water, around the point, to old Hetta's landing. No checkpoint on the water because they don't think anyone's fool enough to row at night."

"They'd be right," Silas said.

"Silas." Maren crossed the shed and put her hand flat on his chest, over the pocket where he kept Tilly's mother's locket, which he kept because Tilly asked him to and because some things you carry so you don't have to say them. "You taught me to gut a mackerel. You can teach me nothing about fear that I haven't already got. Let me do the part I'm built for."

He wanted to argue. He was good at arguing; he'd raised a brother and then buried him and then raised the brother's daughter on argument and salt fish. But Maren had that look she got on the ward, the one that didn't ask permission from the world.

"The ferry yard's got a new corporal," he said instead. "Young. Nervous men search harder than confident ones."

"Then I'll give him something to find," Maren said, "and let him feel clever about it."

* * *

They built the crossing out of everything the harbor had ever taught them.

Silas took the dory — not his own boat, which the army had stamped with a requisition number and chained at the town quay like a stolen horse, but old Bram Tewkesbury's leaky skiff that nobody official had bothered to count because Bram himself hadn't been counted since the convoy went down. The ledger rode in an oilskin wrap inside the bait box, under a layer of squid that made even Silas wrinkle his nose. The smell was the plan. No soldier alive would dig past rotting squid on a guess.

Tilly came down to the mud stairs to see him off, which he'd forbidden, which meant of course she came. She pressed something into his hand: a button off her father's coat, brass, worn smooth.

"For luck," she said. "Da said the sea keeps what you give it, so give it something small."

"He said that?"

"He said it about coins. But it works for buttons."

Silas closed his fist around it. The moon was a fingernail paring, low over the water tower, and the tide was making — coming in, which would help him on the crossing and fight him on the landing, and he'd have to time both. He pushed off into the black.

The estuary at night was a country with no roads. He rowed by the sound of the water against the pilings, by the smell of the creek mouths, by the memory of a hundred years of Penn men doing exactly this with contraband or comfort depending on the decade. Twice the skiff scraped something soft — a sandbar shifting, the estuary rearranging its furniture the way it did — and twice he poled off and held his breath and listened for the garrison. Nothing. The sentries at the ferry yard were watching the road, because roads were where trouble was supposed to come from.

Halfway across, he let himself think about Maren walking through the yard in daylight, and his stroke faltered.

That was the shape of guilt, he was learning. Not a wave that hit you all at once but a current underneath, steady, pulling sideways. He was safe in the dark doing the brave thing, and she was in the light doing the braver one, and if it went wrong it would go wrong for her first and loudest. He thought of Juniper's face when she'd handed over the decoy — how she'd held it a moment too long, like a mother handing over a child dressed as a sacrifice.

"Row," he told himself, and rowed.

Hetta's landing was a rotted platform on the far shore where an oyster woman had lived alone since before the war and intended to live alone long after it. She was waiting with a lantern hooded, as arranged, and she took the bait box without a word and stowed it under her floorboards among jars of smoked eel, and then she fed him tea strong enough to strip paint, and only then did she speak.

"They took the Kestrel boys' boat today," she said. "Impounded at the quay. No reason given."

Silas felt the tea go cold in him. "Which Kestrel boy?"

"The loud one. The one who caused the quarrel at the causeway for your girl." She looked at him over the rim of her cup with eyes like a cormorant's. "The garrison's learning faces, Silas. That's what boats impounded means. They're not collecting wood. They're collecting grudges."

* * *

He was back across the estuary before dawn, beaching the skiff behind the fish shed, and the first thing wrong was the quiet. Hazel Bay at five in the morning was never quiet — there were gulls and pumps and the fish carts and Mrs. Alderyne beating her mats like she was personally at war with dust. This morning there was only boots. Boots in the lane, many pairs, moving in step.

Tilly burst through the shed door before he could hide the skiff properly, and her face told him everything before her mouth caught up.

"They took her," she said. "At the ferry yard. Yesterday noon."

The words landed one at a time, each with its own weight. *Took.* *Her.* *Noon.* While he'd been drinking Hetta's tea under floorboard-adjacent safety, Maren had been standing in front of a nervous corporal with a forged ledger in her medical bag.

"The decoy?" His voice came out gravel. "Did they find—"

"They found it." Tilly's chin was trembling and she was holding it still by main force, the way she did, the way her father used to. "Juniper says that's what matters. They think it's the real one. They marched her to the garrison and everyone watched and nobody—" Her voice cracked clean through. "Nobody did anything, Uncle Silas. Everybody just looked at their boots."

He knelt and pulled her in, and she let him, which she almost never did anymore, being eleven and done with being held. Over her head he saw Juniper in the doorway, gray-faced, still in yesterday's clothes, and behind her the lane full of soldiers' shadows.

"They searched her bag," Juniper said quietly. "She let them. She gave them the speech we practiced — records of the dead, personal effects, she was carrying them to the parish priest. And the corporal read three names and went white, and called his sergeant, and they took her." She stopped. Started again, flatter. "She didn't look for me in the crowd. She made sure not to. So they wouldn't learn my face."

Silas rose slowly. His knees complained; he ignored them. "The garrison will open that ledger and match names against their rolls, and the rolls won't have those names, because the rolls are the lie. Which means either they decide your book is nonsense—" 

"—or they realize someone went to a great deal of trouble to forge a list of men who officially don't exist," Juniper finished. "And ask why."

"And meanwhile they've got Maren, who knows all our faces and none of our names, and they'll keep her until they know which is which."

By seven the whole harbor knew the shape of it. The garrison had strung wire along the quay. Every boat was to be inspected before launching; every crossing of the estuary, including the ferry itself, suspended indefinitely. A notice went up on the customs house door in letters tall enough to read from a moving cart:

**BY ORDER OF THE GARRISON COMMANDER: ALL CROSSINGS SUSPENDED. ALL PERSONS LEAVING HAZEL BAY REQUIRE WRITTEN PASS. ANY PERSON FOUND CONVEYING DOCUMENTS, MAPS, OR WRITTEN MATTER ACROSS THE ESTUARY WILL BE DETAINED FOR TRIAL.**

*Trial.* They'd stopped pretending it was administration.

Silas stood in front of the notice with Tilly's hand in his and read it twice, and what he felt wasn't fear, or wasn't only fear. Underneath it, shameful and warm, was a terrible arithmetic: the plan had worked. The decoy was in garrison hands. The true ledger lay under Hetta's floorboards, past no checkpoint now because every checkpoint was closed, and the inquiry was six days away, and between here and there was black water that no one was permitted to cross and that therefore no one was watching closely enough.

"They've locked the door," he murmured.

"What?" Tilly said.

"Nothing, chick." He squeezed her hand. "Go find Juniper. Tell her we need to talk about Hetta's tea."

But as he turned, a corporal detached himself from the wire line and fell into step beside him, young-faced and apologetic and absolutely immovable, and said, "Mr. Penn. The magistrate would like a word. About your boat. And about who's been borrowing boats."

Behind the customs house glass, Aldous Crane stood watching, hands folded, wearing the patient smile of a man who has finally been dealt the card he was waiting for.
