## 8. What Cannot Be Carried

They met in the loft above Hetta's tea room, where the rafters were low and the window faced away from the harbor, because no one wanted to look at the water just now. The garrison bridge had gone impassable in a single afternoon — wire strung across both approaches, sandbags stacked into a proper emplacement, a machine gun that nobody would name out loud mounted on the parapet like a crow that had decided to stay. Nine days until the inquiry. Three days since Maren hadn't come home.

Juniper stood at the head of the table they'd dragged up from the kitchen, and she watched her family argue itself apart.

"Burn it." Bram Voss said it first, the way somebody always has to. He was the oldest of the dockworkers, hands like knotted rope, and he said it gently, which was worse. "Burn it, Juniper, and we go tonight. Silas's cousin has a boat laid up in Saltern Creek — engine quiet as a sewing machine. We're past the light before midnight. You could be in the city by Thursday, testify at the inquiry yourself, walk in the front door with nothing but your mouth."

"And leave the ledger here for Crane to find anyway," Juniper said.

"He won't find it if there's nothing to find."

"There's always something to find, Bram. That's what I've learned this year. When a record exists, men like Crane can destroy it and call it tidying. But the moment anyone whispers there *was* a record—" She stopped, made herself finish. "He's already looking. You saw Silas taken in for questioning about borrowed boats. He doesn't know where the ledger is. That's the only reason Silas walked back out of that customs house."

Tilly, curled on the window seat with her knees to her chest, said nothing. She'd barely spoken since the ferry yard. Since Maren.

Hetta set down the tray of cups she'd carried up with the particular fierceness of a woman who could not fix anything else. "Then we split it. Copy the names. One copy goes by sea with Bram's group, one stays hidden. If either survives—"

"A copy is a rumor," Juniper said. "A copy is something Crane's lawyers can worry soft at an inquiry. *How do we know these are true entries? Where is the original? Who authenticated them?* The ledger isn't paper, Hetta. It's the fact that forty-one men existed and the army says they didn't. It has to be *the* ledger, in the room, or all of this—" she gestured at the loft, the town below, the wire glinting at the far end of the harbor "—was for nothing."

"Then say it plainly," Bram said. "You're asking us to carry it through the last checkpoint, and the last checkpoint is a bridge with a gun on it, and every crossing is being searched now, and Maren—" His voice caught on the name and he pushed through it. "Maren is sitting in a cell because we thought a uniform was cover. So what do we carry it in? What's left?"

Juniper had been turning that question over for three days, turning it the way you turn a stone in your pocket until the sharp edges wear somewhere familiar. And last night, lying awake in Hetta's spare room listening to the garrison boots on the quay, she had finally seen it.

"The answer's been under our feet the whole town," she said. "We stop hiding it."

* * *

The silence after lasted long enough that someone downstairs dropped a cup.

"Explain," Hetta said.

"Every search assumes contraband is concealed. Small package. False bottom. Wreath." Tilly flinched at that, and Juniper gentled her voice without stopping. "So they search small things, and they search people with reason to be nervous, and they wave through anything big enough to be obvious, because obvious things have already been cleared. Nobody walks a secret through a checkpoint in broad daylight inside something too heavy to lift."

Bram's eyebrows had climbed halfway up his weathered forehead. "Go on."

"Old Nan Pellow's husband has been lying in the chapel vault since spring, waiting on a stone for the grave. Half the town knows the coffin's there; the army knows it too, they posted a notice when the churchyard was requisitioned. A burial party is ordinary. A burial party is *grief*, and even Crane hesitates to put his hands on grief in front of forty witnesses." Juniper laid her palm flat on the table. "We move the coffin Saturday morning. Burial at ten, procession leaves the chapel at half nine, down Fore Street, over the garrison bridge, because it's the only road to the churchyard now and everyone knows it. The ledger rides inside, beneath the lining, wrapped in oilcloth. Nan walks at the front as chief mourner. And the whole of Hazel Bay walks behind her."

"You'd need every hand in that procession to be ours," Bram said slowly. "Every single one willing to lie to a corporal's face with a gun watching."

"They're not lying," Tilly said.

Everyone turned. She hadn't moved from the window seat, but she'd lifted her chin, and there was something in her face Juniper hadn't seen since the ferry yard — the flat, stubborn courage of a girl who had carried a mourning wreath through a checkpoint at thirteen years old because her father deserved flowers.

"It's not a lie," Tilly said again. "Nan *is* burying her husband. We *are* mourners. The ledger's just — it's the other thing in the coffin. The thing that needs burying properly too. All those men. Nobody buried *them*."

Juniper felt her throat close. Of all of them, it was the child who understood it first: that the ledger was not cargo. It was the dead, being given their procession at last.

* * *

They worked through the night, and the work kept despair at arm's length, which was all any of them could manage.

Silas came at two in the morning, released with a warning and a tail that he'd lost in the herring sheds, and he brought news that sat in the room like cold iron. "They've moved Maren to the garrison lockup. Not the magistrate's cells — army custody. That means charges, Juniper. Means she'll be held until after the inquiry, held precisely so she can't testify, and Crane will see to it the paperwork says whatever keeps her there." He rubbed the raw place on his wrist where the cuff had been. "I'm sorry. It was my plan, my rowing, my fault—"

"Stop," Juniper said, and it came out harder than she meant, so she said it softer. "Stop. Maren knew the risk when she took the decoy. She *chose* it, Silas, because she's a soldier and a nurse and she thought her uniform would buy the rest of us time, and it did. We used the time badly, but we used it." She looked around the lamplit loft — Bram and Hetta, Silas with his salt-burned hands, Tilly asleep at last against Hetta's shoulder. "Nobody in this room gets to carry the guilt alone. That's the whole point of a family. That's what I've decided it means, anyway."

Hetta smiled faintly into her teacup. "Decided. Just like that."

"Just like that. I've spent my whole life believing belonging was something you earned and kept and guarded, like a deed to a house. Something you could lose by leaving, or by staying too long, or by being found out." Juniper turned the ledger over in her hands, its oilcloth wrapping worn pale at the corners from three days in ice and fish and fear. "But I don't think it works like that. I think it's more like the harbor wall. Somebody lays a stone, and then somebody else lays the next one, and it only holds because everybody's stone is in it. Maren laid hers. Tilly laid hers. Now it's mine."

Bram snorted, but gently. "Poetry from the ledger-keeper."

"Somebody has to keep things around here."

* * *

Friday evening, Juniper went alone to the chapel, because the lining had to be sewn by the person who would answer for it, and she would not ask another soul to hold a needle over a coffin.

Nan Pellow met her among the vault shadows, small and straight-backed and dry-eyed in the way of women who had done their crying privately decades ago. Her husband's coffin rested on trestles, plain pine, mended once at the foot where the wood had split. Nan drew back the lid herself.

"Fifty-one years I slept beside that man," she said. "Six more inches of him won't trouble me." Then, watching Juniper measure the space beneath the lining: "Tell me what I'm carrying, Nurse. Not the details. Just the weight of it."

"Forty-one names," Juniper said. "Men the army erased. Some of them pressed into salvage work they never came home from. All of them husbands or sons or brothers to somebody, and none of them allowed a stone."

Nan was quiet a while. Down in the town, the garrison bugle sounded the evening call, thin and wrong over rooftops it had no business sounding over.

"My Thomas worked the harbor forty years," she said at last. "When they took the boats, they took the last of him, near enough. I've been waiting on this funeral since April and hating every day of the delay." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible and calm. "Saturday, I'll be burying two things, then. Best day's work this town's done all year."

They sewed the oilcloth packet flat against the coffin floor together, Nan's stitches as neat as sail canvas, and laid the lining over it, and Juniper closed the lid and stood with her hand resting on the pine and could not, for a long moment, tell which weight she was feeling — the paper or the dead.

* * *

Saturday came up gray and still, the kind of tide-flat morning that makes everything sound closer: gull-cries, boot-heels, the far clank of the wire being tightened on the bridge.

By half eight, Fore Street was filling. They came in black and dark blue and Sunday best gone gray with salt — fishermen and their wives, dockers off shift who shouldn't have had shifts to be off, Hetta in her good coat, Bram with his cap in his hands like any mourner. Old Dr. Ellery, who hadn't walked unaided since winter, came leaning on two grandsons. Word had gone out the way word moves in Hazel Bay, in glances and half-sentences and the laying of one stone upon another, and by nine there were better than sixty of them gathered outside the chapel doors, and more coming down the lanes.

Juniper stood at the chapel steps and looked at them and understood, finally, with a clarity that felt like cold water, why she would not burn the ledger even to save her own commission, her own skin. The names were the point — but the names had never traveled alone. Every one of them had pulled a town behind it like a wake.

Silas appeared at her elbow, collar stiff, jaw set. "Corporal's posted at the bridge head. Crane's there himself — arrived ten minutes ago in the magistrate's car, watching the approach. He knows a procession this size is unusual. He may not know why yet."

"Then we give him nothing unusual," Juniper said. "Only the biggest funeral Hazel Bay's ever seen, and every honest reason in the world for it."

The chapel doors opened. Nan Pellow came out first, upright as a mast, and behind her four bearers took up the coffin, plain pine, heavier than it looked, and the procession formed behind them the way water finds a channel.

They turned toward the harbor, toward the bridge, toward the wire and the sandbags and the man in the magistrate's coat waiting with his patient smile — and Juniper fell into step, and the whole of Hazel Bay walked with her.
