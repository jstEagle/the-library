## 9. The Garrison Bridge

They buried no one that morning, and every soul in Hazel Bay came to mourn him anyway.

Juniper stood at the head of the procession in her nurse's gray, the one garment the garrison had never thought to forbid, and looked back down Chapel Lane at what the town had made of itself. Fishermen who hadn't spoken in a decade walked shoulder to shoulder. The chandlery's widow had sewn black armbands for half the harbor before dawn. Old Tam Osgood, who swore he'd never wear a collar again this side of the grave, had put one on. Tilly had braided ribbon into her hair, mourning-black, and Silas had shaved.

For a lie, it was the truest thing Hazel Bay had done in years.

The coffin rode on Osgood's cart, drawn by the mule that had survived two requisition inspections through sheer ugliness. The box itself was elm, borrowed from the undertaker with the story that they were burying Tam's brother, dead of pneumonia at the almshouse — plausible, uncheckable, since the garrison had burned the parish registers in the spring. Inside, beneath a shovelful of sand and a folded flag, lay forty-one names in Juniper's hand.

She had copied the ledger out three times and hidden the originals where only salt water could find them if she failed. What crossed the bridge today was her own fair copy, wrapped in oilcloth, and it weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"Juniper." Maren fell in beside her — freed on Tuesday, thinner, her uniform exchanged for civvies so the garrison couldn't claim she'd deserted. "Silas says Crane's at the bridge himself."

"I know."

"You knew before I told you."

"Tilly saw his motorcar go past the fish market an hour ago." Juniper kept her eyes forward. "He wants to look us in the face while he decides which of us to break."

* * *

The garrison bridge was the last crossing, and Crane had turned it into a throat: sandbags stacked at both ends, a lorry parked broadside, bayonets catching the gray morning light. The corporal from the causeway — the suspicious one, the one Tilly had slipped past in spring — stood at the barricade looking like a man who had been told this time there would be no fisherman's quarrel to cover a mistake.

Crane waited under an umbrella it did not need, in a coat worth more than a trawler. He watched the funeral procession come down the hill road with the expression of a man watching weather he intended to profit from.

Juniper counted heads as they approached. Two hundred mourners, perhaps more. Every boat crew in the harbor, walking slow behind the coffin. The plan was simple enough to be either brilliant or fatal: a crowd too large to search, a grief too public to insult, a coffin too sacred to open without making the army into graverobbers before the whole county's eyes. The town itself was the hiding place. You could not search two hundred people at once, and Crane knew it, and that was why he had come — not to search, but to find the one seam and pull it.

"Halt," the corporal called, and the procession halted like a tide meeting a wall.

Crane stepped around the sandbags, umbrella furled now, tapping it against his palm. "A burial," he said pleasantly. "How the town loves its rituals. And whom are we burying?"

"Josiah Osgood," Tam said, stepping forward with a death certificate so beautifully forged that Juniper had nearly wept when Silas's cousin produced it. "My brother. Pneumonia. Certified at the almshouse."

"The almshouse." Crane smiled at the certificate without taking it. "Which burned. With all its records. In the fire ordered by military authority." He let the silence do its work. "Convenient, Mr. Osgood. Everything in this town is convenient lately. Open the coffin."

"No," said Juniper.

She said it quietly, but the bridge heard it, and behind her two hundred people heard it, and the word went down the hill road like a stone dropped in still water. She stepped out of the line of mourners, past Tam's white knuckles on the cart rail, and stopped three feet from Crane.

"Magistrate Crane." She let the title sit between them like something rotten. "You know me."

"Do I?"

"You sat across my kitchen table in March and offered me immunity for a ledger you pretended not to believe existed. You threatened to name my friends as accomplices. So yes, Magistrate. You know exactly whose funeral this is, because it is yours, and everyone here has come to watch it pass."

The corporal's hand moved toward his holster. Crane lifted one finger a half-inch and the man froze.

"Mrs. Lockridge," Crane said softly. "Or shall we use the name the army struck from your commission? Nurse Lockridge, dismissed for falsifying records — that was the wording, wasn't it, when in fact you merely corrected them?" He turned a slow half-circle, addressing the mourners like a jury he'd already bought. "This woman is asking you to commit a crime against the Crown on the strength of a grudge. There is no ledger. Even if there were, it proves nothing. And this coffin—" he rapped the elm with his umbrella handle, twice, "—contains either a dead man or a dead woman's reputation, and I will know which."

"Then look," said Juniper.

The whole bridge seemed to lean in.

"Search it," she said. "Here. Now. Not privately, not in the guardhouse where things get lost. In front of your own officers, in front of two hundred witnesses, in front of God and the county coroner's man, who is standing right there by the lorry with his notebook already out." She pointed, and Crane's gaze followed despite himself — because the coroner's man was real, summoned by Maren three days ago with a letter signed by a naval captain awaiting testimony, and he had been standing at the edge of the checkpoint all morning writing down every word.

"You'll open it yourself, then," Crane said. "Confessing—"

"I'm offering you the choice you never gave anyone else." Her voice didn't shake; she'd spent three years learning to speak over men screaming, and this was quieter work. "Open the coffin and find a body, and I go to prison for desecration and fraud, and your scheme survives. Open it and find paper, and you have searched a mourning coffin before your own garrison and the press will have it by Thursday — and then you must explain to your paymasters why a naval inquiry already holds copies of whatever you destroy. Because it does, Aldous. Copies exist that neither you nor I can burn."

That was the lie. That was the knife's edge. The inquiry held nothing yet. Nine days of planning had come down to this: whether Crane believed the copies existed. Whether his greed was faster than his caution. Whether a man who had sold a town would gamble his last shred of deniability on a public search.

Crane looked at the coffin. At the coroner's man. At his own corporal, whose suspicion had been wrong once already on this road and whose report, if this went badly, would need someone to blame.

Juniper watched him do the arithmetic and understood, with a clarity that felt almost gentle, that he was not weighing right and wrong at all. He was weighing versions of himself. A magistrate who searched coffins was a magistrate in the newspapers. A magistrate who waved through a funeral was a magistrate with his scheme intact and his options open.

"Let them pass," Crane said.

"Sir—" the corporal began.

"The dead," Crane said loudly, "are owed their dignity, and this town is owed an example of order. Escort the procession across. No interference." He stepped back, umbrella resuming its tap against his palm, and his eyes found Juniper's one last time. What lived in them was not defeat. She had beaten him today and she knew — the way you know weather coming in off the water — that he had simply filed this loss among his assets.

The cart rolled onto the bridge. The town followed, two hundred strong, and the coffin passed over the planks with forty-one names sleeping inside it, and nobody breathed until the far end.

* * *

At the crossroads above the valley, where the military road gave way to the king's highway and the garrison's reach ended, Silas stopped the mule and took off his cap.

Tilly climbed up beside the coffin and laid her palm flat on the lid, the way she used to on her father's shoulder when she wanted to wake him gently.

"He'd have liked it," she said. "A funeral with everybody there."

Juniper laughed, or something near it, and found she was crying without any warning at all. Maren put an arm around her. Tam Osgood produced a flask from somewhere inside his collar and passed it down the line, and for a full minute the whole strange family of them just stood in the road, alive, unhalted, the ledger riding light as ash in the box between them.

"Nine days," Silas said at last, snapping the reins. "Inquiry sits on the ninth. We make the city by the seventh and rest."

But Juniper was looking back down the valley, where a motorcar had started up behind them and turned — not toward the garrison, but toward the city road. Toward the same inquiry. Toward nine days in which Aldous Crane could build any story he liked about the mad dismissed nurse and her coffin of lies.

"He's going ahead of us," she said.

Maren followed her gaze. "To testify his version first."

"To bury us in advance." Juniper climbed onto the cart and settled herself beside the coffin, her hand finding the elm's grain. "So we'd best make sure the names arrive louder than he does."

The mule leaned into its collar, and Hazel Bay's funeral rolled on toward the city, carrying the only dead man who had ever mattered more alive than gone.
