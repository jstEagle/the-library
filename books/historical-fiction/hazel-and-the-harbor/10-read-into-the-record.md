## 10. Read Into the Record

The inquiry chamber in the city had once been a customs house, and it still smelled of salt and old paper—two things Juniper trusted more than any oath. The room was long and gray, lit by high windows that let down columns of cold light, and at its far end sat three naval officers behind a table scarred by a century of elbows. Somewhere behind her, filling the public benches to standing room, were the people of Hazel Bay: Silas with his hands scrubbed raw for the occasion, Tilly pressed against his side in a dress that had been Tilly's mother's, fishermen who still moved like men expecting a checkpoint at every doorway.

The ledger lay on the table before her, wrapped in oilcloth. It had crossed three checkpoints to get here. It had crossed them inside ice and wreaths and finally inside a coffin, carried on the shoulders of a town that had decided, all at once and without discussion, that some things would not burn.

"State your name and service," said the officer in the center. Rear Admiral Hollis, they called him, though Juniper had stopped trusting titles somewhere between a convoy disaster and a magistrate's smile.

"Juniper Lockridge. Nursing Sister, Third Field Hospital. Formerly." The word *formerly* still caught in her throat. Crane had seen to that—he'd written to the nursing board before she'd even left Hazel Bay, and her commission had been struck with the same administrative ease he'd used on forty-one names.

"You understand that testimony given here is given under penalty."

"I understand that names were taken off the rolls," Juniper said, "and I intend to put them back on. Out loud. All of them."

Hollis studied her a moment. "Proceed."

She unwrapped the oilcloth. Her hands did not shake, which surprised her; they had shaken the night she'd first written in this book, alone in a supply tent while the convoy burned on the water, and they had shaken outside the garrison bridge while Aldous Crane ran his gloved hand along a coffin lid and made his choice to protect his scheme rather than search it before his own officers. But now, with everything already lost or risked, her hands were steady as a carpenter's.

She opened the ledger to the first page.

"Corporal Edmund Voss," she read. "Struck from the rolls eleven days after the convoy at Gull Reach. Witnessed alive thereafter by two orderlies at the salvage depot. Private Arthur Kemple. Struck the same day. Lance Corporal—" She paused, not for courage but because Maren should have been sitting in the front row, and instead there was an empty place where Silas had laid Maren's uniform cap, brought from her cell in a guard's fist like evidence of nothing at all.

"Read it all," said someone from the benches. It was old Wenna Trevail, who had sold fish at the harbor mouth for forty years. "Every name. Make them hear every one."

So she did.

It took an hour and a half. Forty-one names, each one spoken clearly into the gray light, each one followed by what she knew: date struck, date last seen, where the salvage manifests placed them afterward. As she read, a clerk at the side table cross-checked requisition orders, and Juniper watched something happen in his face that she had watched happen in faces her whole career—the moment a person stops being able to pretend. Page after page. Name after name. The numbers in Crane's accounts matched the numbers missing from the rolls so neatly that the arithmetic itself seemed to blush.

Crane was there, of course. He sat with a lawyer at a smaller table, and for the first half hour he wore his courtroom face, the warm one, the one that had greeted the army officers on the quay with wine and handshakes. But names have weight when they are read aloud in sequence. By the twentieth name he was consulting papers that no longer helped him. By the thirty-fourth, his lawyer had begun leaning away from him slightly, the way you lean from a man standing too close to a collapsing wall.

"Magistrate Crane," Admiral Hollis said at last, when the final name—Tilly's father, Robert Penn, struck nine days before the convoy ever happened—had settled into the record. "You'll account for these figures."

"I account," Crane said, and Juniper heard it, the small crack under the warmth, "for a town's best interests during wartime. Requisitions require flexibility. The dead require—"

"The dead," Juniper said, before she could stop herself, "require their names back."

Hollis raised a hand—not rebuke, exactly. Adjournment. The chamber emptied slowly, and through the windows Juniper saw gulls wheeling over rooftops that belonged to a city she didn't love, and thought: tomorrow. Tomorrow the accounts, the warrants, the salvage ledgers with their false columns. Tonight there was only waiting.

* * *

They waited two days in a boarding house that smelled of beeswax and boiled linen. On the second evening, a naval runner came to the door with paper in his hand, and Silas took it and read it three times and then handed it to Juniper without a word, because his voice had gone somewhere and wouldn't come back.

Maren Quill. Released, the order said. Charges dismissed. Restitution of commission recommended pending review.

Juniper was at the city hospital gates before she fully remembered leaving the boarding house. Maren came out through the visitors' door thinner and paler, in civilian clothes that hung wrong on her, and for a moment they only looked at each other across ten feet of wet cobblestones.

"They took my cap," Maren said. "I want you to know I'm not forgiving that. The rest of it, maybe. Not the cap."

Juniper laughed, and then she was crying, and then they were holding each other in the gatehouse shadow while nurses streamed past pretending very hard not to look.

"You came back," Maren said into her shoulder. "After the bridge. You could have run with the boat. Silas told me—you stood up in front of Crane's whole garrison and said your own name."

"It seemed fair," Juniper managed. "I'd spent two years writing down everyone else's."

* * *

The inquiry's finding came down on a Tuesday: the salvage operation unlawful, the erasures fraudulent, the requisitions of Hazel Bay rescinded in full. Crane was remanded for trial on charges that would take longer to list than to read. A lieutenant delivered copies to the boarding house, and Tilly, who had appointed herself courier of all good news since the causeway, ran the length of the harbor road with the paper held over her head like a lantern.

The town came home to its own houses. That was the strange, ordinary miracle of it—boats went back out within the week, and laundry reappeared on lines that had stood empty for months, and Mrs. Cardew-Pryce resumed a feud with her neighbor about a fence that had survived an army occupation better than most marriages. Juniper walked the quay in the first week and felt the town close around her, not like a door but like a tide, indifferent and welcoming at once.

Silas found her at the harbor mouth where the breakwater met the sea wall.

"We're putting up a stone," he said. "Granite, from the quarry at Trethick. Harbor memorial. Every name." He looked out at the water. "Carved deep. Weather won't have them."

"I'd like that," Juniper said.

"Tilly wants you to do the reading. At the unveiling." He paused. "She says you're the only one who knows how they go."

* * *

The stone went up in spring. It faced the water, because Silas insisted the men should be able to see their own harbor, and it bore forty-one names cut three inches deep by a mason who refused payment and then wept onto the wet granite and pretended he hadn't. Tilly cut the ribbon, because nobody dared argue with Tilly anymore. Wenna Trevail sang, badly and beautifully. Maren stood in her restored uniform with her new cap at the correct insolent angle, and when Juniper caught her eye, Maren touched the brim once, and that was the whole speech.

Juniper read the names again. This time it took eleven minutes, because the crowd read along with her, murmuring each name back like a wave returning what the tide owed, and when she reached the last one—Robert Penn—Tilly's small voice said it loudest of all.

Afterward, people drifted down to the boats and the tables and the business of living, which is what memorials are actually for. Juniper stayed at the stone a while, running her thumb through the carved letters, feeling their depth.

She had spent years believing that belonging was a thing you packed carefully and carried—that if you held it tight enough, kept it hidden well enough, smuggled it past enough checkpoints, it would arrive intact wherever you were going. But here was the thing the ledger had never taught her: the book was paper. It could have burned. What couldn't burn was this—a mason refusing payment, a child shouting her father's name, a nurse touching her cap brim, forty-one names going home. Belonging wasn't cargo. It was what got built around you while you were busy building for everyone else.

She turned from the stone and walked down toward the quay, toward Silas and Maren and the smell of paint and tar, toward a house on Harrow Lane that Mrs. Cardew-Pryce had declared, in writing, to be available for let at terms insulting to both parties, and therefore perfect.

Tilly came pounding after her, breathless, holding something out—a fresh notebook, cheap paper, blue cover.

"For the next ones," Tilly said. "In case anyone else gets erased. You'd notice. You always notice."

Juniper took it. The cover was still stiff, the pages empty, the whole thing light as a promise.

"Yes," she said, tucking it under her arm and turning toward the harbor, where the evening boats were coming in with the lights of Hazel Bay waking along the shore to meet them. "Let's find out who needs keeping."
