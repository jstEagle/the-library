## 1. The Weight of Lead

There is a particular hour in the Dunmore print shop — just past dawn, before the stove has properly taken, when the ink is too cold to flow and the whole room smells of lampblack and wet stone — when I am certain I was made for this work. Not born for it. Made. The way a letter is made: cut, filed, fitted into its place, and expected to hold.

My name is Freya Dunmore, and unless you have stood over a composing stick until your thumbs ached, you will never know my name at all. That is the bargain of my craft. Every handbill in Vellmark that announces a lost dog or a lecture on phrenology, every playbill for the Corvane Theatre, every label on every bottle of Dr. Pell's Celebrated Tonic — some part of those passed through my hands first, and not one soul in this city could pick me from a crowd. I set type the way other women set tables: invisibly, precisely, so that no one ever thinks to notice.

I have decided this is a kind of glory. Some mornings I even believe it.

* * *

The shop sits on Quillman's Row, wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sells nothing but buttons, which in Vellmark counts as ambition. My father, Aldous Dunmore, was cutting a new face when I came down that morning — an italic, he said, commissioned by a bookseller with more taste than money — and he had the look he always had at that work: bent over his graver like a man praying into the steel.

"Your 'e' is drifting," I told him, peering over his shoulder.

"My 'e' is *expressive*."

"Your 'e' is drunk. It leans like a sailor on pay night."

He laughed without looking up. That was our liturgy, most mornings. He would defend his letters; I would insult them; neither of us would yield, because yielding would mean admitting we were two people doing one job and might as well say so out loud. Aldous Dunmore cuts faces the way God supposedly made rivers — patiently, and with no explanation offered. I grind his matrices, cast his slugs, and keep the accounts, which is to say I keep him fed while he makes beauty nobody asked for.

It is good work. I want to be clear about that, because everything after will make it sound otherwise. In 1847, in a city where half the quays stand idle and the other half smell of tar and grievance, a woman who can earn her bread by her hands and her eye is fortunate. I knew that. I counted my fortune nightly, in the little book by my bed, the way misers count coin.

What I did not count — what I kept very carefully off the page — was everything else.

* * *

Tam came in at half past eight, smelling of river fog and someone else's tobacco, carrying a parcel under his arm the way a man carries a parcel he'd rather not discuss.

"You're late," I said.

"I'm punctual," said Tam, "for a different appointment." He was nineteen and wore his collar turned up against a wind that hadn't started yet, the way he wore everything: slightly ahead of the weather. "Father. There's a gentleman at the Anchorline wants three hundred bills run off by Friday. Playbills, he says."

"He says," my father repeated mildly, still cutting.

"He says a great many things. He pays in silver, though, and silver doesn't lie."

"Silver lies constantly," I said. "That's rather its nature."

Tam grinned at me — he has our mother's grin, all teeth and no apology — and set the parcel down on the stone bench with a care that contradicted his whole manner. Then he was gone again, out into the street, off to deliver proofs to Herr Vogel at the sign of the Blue Owl and, presumably, to do whatever else Tam does between errands, which is a country I have chosen not to map.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is about to become expensive: I knew my brother ran messages for men whose names were never written down. Everyone on Quillman's Row knew. Tam carried sealed packets to the tannery district and came back lighter in pocket and heavier in secrets, and when Father asked, Tam said "printer's business," and Father nodded, and I looked at my type case and thought about my letters.

Willful blindness, they would call it later, if anyone dared call it anything. I prefer to think of it as discipline. A compositor learns early that the eye must go letter by letter, line by line, and never skip ahead to the end of the page. Skip ahead and you transpose. Skip ahead and you ruin the whole forme. So I read my life the way I read copy: one word at a time, refusing the sentence it was building toward.

* * *

By noon the light through the shop's front window had gone the color of dishwater, and the soldiers came up Quillman's Row two by two, which was new.

Not soldiers generally — Vellmark had garrison troops the way it had pigeons, everywhere and beneath notice. But these walked in step, muskets shouldered, and stopped at the corner by the button shop, and one of them unrolled something and pasted it to the shutter there. A proclamation. I could read it upside down through the glass, which is a small professional vanity of mine: *by order of the magistrate, all public assemblies are prohibited until further notice.*

"Further notice," I repeated. "As if notice were a thing you could post."

"They've posted four since Michaelmas," Father said. He had come to the window with his graver still in his fist, which he did sometimes, forgetting he held it. "Each one larger than the last. By spring they'll need the whole wall."

"And by spring the wall will be torn down for barricades, if the dockmen have their way."

"Freya."

"I'm only saying what everyone says."

"That's why I'd rather you didn't." He went back to his bench, but he didn't sit. He stood looking at his unfinished italic the way a man looks at a letter he doesn't want to answer. "The bridge closes soon. You know that. First hard freeze and the wardens chain it, and then we're inside the walls until thaw whether the times suit us or not."

"The times don't suit us now."

"No," he said. "So let's make them a quiet time instead. Bread, ink, and no opinions. Can you manage that?"

I said I could. I was lying, but only a little, and only in the direction of comfort, which is the most forgivable direction a lie can point.

Here is what I actually managed that afternoon: I composed and pulled two hundred bills for a lecture on the improvement of prison conditions, which the magistrate's office had approved, and which struck me as rather like commissioning a portrait of a fire from the arsonist. I recast a dozen worn sorts. I ate bread and cheese standing up. And when Tam blew back in at dusk with snow in his hair though it wasn't snowing yet — river spray freezing as it fell, the bridge water already wearing its winter skin — I asked him, casually, the way you ask a question you don't want answered:

"How's the river?"

"Closing," he said. "Wardens were measuring the ice at the north pier. Another week, maybe less." He warmed his hands at the stove, and for a moment his face did something complicated — excitement and dread sharing the same chair — and then it smoothed over, the way young faces can. "Everything freezes eventually."

"Everything thaws," I said.

"Not everything," said Tam, and laughed, and I chose to hear it as a joke.

* * *

That evening I stayed late at the stone, casting slugs by lamplight while Father took the day's accounts upstairs. The shop at night is my favorite church: the cases ranked along the wall like a choir, each compartment labeled in Father's small precise hand, the press waiting under its canvas sheet like something sleeping. Lead has a smell, did you know? Sweetish, faintly rotten, like the inside of a lock. I have loved it since I was six years old, when Father first let me pour metal into a mould and I understood, with a child's total conviction, that I was making words solid. That words could be *held*.

I was thinking about that — about solidity, about holding — when I noticed the case.

Third row, left side, between the long-s and the ampersands. It sat wrong. Not crooked — everything in this shop sits exactly — but *full*. Cases empty out over the course of a day; this one had filled. Someone had been setting type and redistributing it, and the distribution didn't match my ledger of the shop's contents, and I keep a ledger of everything, including, apparently, myself.

I slid the case out. The compartments held type I didn't recognize. Not Father's italic, not any face we used for customers. A compact, dense letterform, economical, designed — I saw it at once, professionally, helplessly admiring — to fit a great deal of text into a small space. Pamphlet type. The kind of face you cut when you have much to say and little time to say it in, and expect the reading to happen fast, in doorways, by people running.

I stood there a long moment with the case in my hands, lead ticking softly as it cooled in my blood, and I heard Father's tread overhead, steady as a clock.

*He says a great many things,* Tam had said of the gentleman at the Anchorline. *He pays in silver.*

I put the case back. Exactly where it had sat. I trimmed the lamp, banked the stove, and climbed the stairs to supper, where Father asked how the casting went and I said *fine*, and meant it, and hated how easily the word came.

One word at a time, I told myself. Don't skip ahead to the end of the page.

But that night, lying awake in the dark above the shop, I found my eye had done the unforgivable thing. It had skipped ahead. Somewhere down the line of this winter, in type I couldn't yet read, a sentence was being set that had our name in it — Dunmore, in that dense, hurried, pamphlet face — and I lay there listening to the river freeze, understanding that when the forme was finally locked and the press came down, there would be no correcting it.

No proof. No revision. Only the pull.
