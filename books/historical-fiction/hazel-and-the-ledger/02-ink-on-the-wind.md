## 2. Ink on the Wind

By the first week of true cold, the rumors had stopped being rumors the way a rumor stops being anything: it acquired boots.

Soldiers came to the printing quarter on a Tuesday — I remember it was a Tuesday because we printed the shipping notices on Tuesdays and I resented every interruption of my Tuesdays with a small, private, entirely unchristian fury. They came in grey coats with the magistrate's sash stitched at the shoulder, six of them, and they did not storm anything. That was the unsettling part. They simply stood at the corner of Quill Lane and Brakewell like furniture that had been delivered and forgotten, and they watched.

Watched who came in. Watched who went out. Watched Tam dart past them with a package under his arm, and — this is the detail I chewed on for days afterward — one of them nodded at him. Not a hostile nod. A nod between men who have an arrangement, or at least an understanding that arrangements might be had.

I watched all of this through the shop window with a composing stick in my hand and ink to my second knuckle, and I did what I always did with things I did not wish to know. I turned back to my case.

There is a particular mercy in type. Lead does not argue. A letter sits in its slot exactly where you put it and says exactly what you make it say, no more, and if the world outside the window has gone strange and grey-coated, well — the world inside the case remains alphabetized. Twelve-point Baskerville, lower-case e's in their compartment like seeds. I have known people to find God in less orderly circumstances.

"Freya." My father's voice from the back room, that particular mildness he used when he wanted something and hoped I wouldn't notice him wanting it. "Have you seen my green apron?"

"You're wearing it."

"Ah." He looked down at it as though it had appeared there by witchcraft, and smiled his absent smile, and went back to whatever he was doing behind the door I was not invited behind.

I want to be clear about the kind of man Aldous Dunmore appeared to be, because appearance is half of any tragedy and I did not yet know I was living in one. He was a lean, stooped, ink-stained gentleman of fifty-odd years who quoted Horace at inappropriate moments and forgot to eat lunch and paid his apprentices in candied ginger and praise. The city knew him as the finest cutter of typefaces in Vellmark, possibly on the river, possibly anywhere — a craftsman so absorbed in the perfection of a serif that he could not reasonably be expected to notice a revolution even if it happened in his own back room.

That last part, as it turned out, was not appearance at all. It was camouflage. But I get ahead of myself, which is the unforgivable thing, and I promised myself after that sleepless night that I would go one word at a time, like a reader who respects the author.

* * *

The stranger came on Thursday.

He arrived at the slack hour, mid-afternoon, when the light through the window goes the color of weak tea and the press is quiet and a body's thoughts start wandering where they shouldn't. The bell over the door gave its little cough and a man stepped in out of the wind — big through the shoulders, seaman's hands, a coat too good for a seaman and a voice too soft for his size.

"I'm told Dunmore's does careful work," he said. "Discreet work."

"We do all work," I said. "Care comes free."

My father emerged from the back wiping his hands, and something passed across his face — quick, gone before I could name it, though later I would name it recognition. "Sir. You've business?"

The man set a folded paper on the counter. "A pamphlet. Five hundred copies. The copy's written out fair." He tapped it once with a blunt finger. "You'll note the specification."

My father unfolded it. And here is where I must confess to the sin of professional pride, because it was my pride that ruined me — my eye went over his shoulder the way it had been trained to do since I was nine years old, reading specifications the way other girls read fortunes in tea leaves.

Paper stock: laid, heavy, the smugglers' grade that comes up the river untaxed. Format: octavo, folded twice, stitched. And the typeface specified, drawn in a little marginal sketch the way commissioners sometimes do when they care —

I knew that face. Not the man's face. The *type's* face.

It was a dense, hurried, slightly forward-leaning text face with a spur on the lowercase g like a hitch in a gallop and terminals cut short as if the letters couldn't be bothered to finish their sentences. It was ugly and urgent and utterly distinctive, and I knew it because three years ago I had spent a fortnight helping my father cast matrices for a face he told me was a private commission, a commercial job, a nothing — and because I had noticed, even then, that the finished type never appeared in the shop's day book. No entry. No sale. No customer. Just drawer seven, kept locked, kept full, kept quiet.

Drawer seven. The pamphlet face. Sitting in our back room like a loaded thing.

"Fine face," the stranger was saying. "Whoever cut it understood urgency. Most type stands about waiting to be read. This stuff walks up to you."

"It's available," my father said, mildly, evenly, in the voice he used for prices.

"It'll be collected, not delivered."

"Naturally."

They settled it in under a minute — sum, date, terms — while I stood at my case sorting e's and listening to my own heartbeat arrange itself into something faster than twelve point. The stranger left the way he'd come, soft-voiced, taking the wind with him. The bell coughed. The door shut.

My father gathered up the commission paper and turned toward the back room.

"Papa," I said. "What pamphlet takes drawer seven?"

I had not meant to say it aloud. There is a difference, I have learned, between knowing a thing and saying it; saying it builds the gallows and steps onto it. But the words were out, hanging in the tea-colored light, and my father stopped with his hand on the door.

He didn't pretend. That's what I keep returning to, all these months later — he didn't insult me with pretending. He just looked at me over his shoulder with those mild, ink-rimmed eyes and said, "It pays well, Freya. Better than shipping notices." A pause. "Better than we've been paid since your mother lived."

"And what does it say? The pamphlet?"

"That's not your concern."

"When it's set in type I ground and cast, it becomes my concern."

"No." Gentle. Absolute. A door closing as quietly as doors close in our family — without slam, without splinter, just the soft click of a thing decided. "Set the shipping notices. We'll speak of this no further."

And he went into the back room, and I heard — distinctly, unmistakably, for the first time letting me hear — the lock of drawer seven turning.

* * *

I did not set the shipping notices. I stood at my case and sorted letters I had already sorted and thought about willful blindness, which is a subject I am now something of a scholar in.

Because here is the arithmetic I had been refusing to do, laid out plain at last on the composing stone of my mind. Money had come into this house for three years that the day book never recorded. My father's private commissions had grown more frequent, his hours in the back room longer, his manner when soldiers were mentioned more elaborately indifferent. Tam had begun running errands for men whose packages were wrapped in oilcloth and weighed nothing a boy should be carrying. We ate well through two bad trade winters. Our roof got mended. Nobody asked questions, least of all me, because the answers were paying for the coal.

Sedition, then. My father had been printing sedition — or something adjacent to it, something that walked up to you in a hurried italic g — and he had been printing it in type I had helped make, in a shop I loved, with coin that bought my bread, and the entire edifice rested on my willingness to look at letters instead of looking at him.

I am not going to pretend I felt noble horror. I felt fear, cold and specific, the fear of a woman doing sums: five hundred copies, a stranger with seaman's hands, grey coats at the corner of Quill Lane watching who came in and who went out. In Lyon they hang printers. Even in tolerant old Vellmark, with its river manners and its bribable gates, the magistrate's patience for unauthorized pamphlets in a winter of bread riots runs about as deep as the frost line. My father wasn't gambling with his life. He'd been gambling with all of ours, and he'd been winning until Thursday, and Thursday only proved that the streak had to end sometime, because streaks always do.

Through the window I could see the soldiers at the corner, stamping against the cold. One of them laughed at something. The sound didn't carry; I only saw the shape of it, white breath and tilted heads, men making the best of a dull posting.

Across the lane, Tam emerged from the alley behind the chandler's with that easy slouch of his, empty-handed now, whistling. He passed the soldiers without a glance. One of them touched his hat brim — lazy, friendly, familiar.

Familiar.

I put down my composing stick. I took up my apron. I walked into the back room, where my father sat at drawer seven with the commission before him, already composing the stranger's pamphlet in his head — I could see it in his lips, reading the copy soundlessly, and there was something almost happy in his face, the happiness of a craftsman with good work ahead of him, and that frightened me more than everything else combined.

"The bridge," I said. "Has anyone told you what they're saying about the bridge?"

He looked up. "What about it?"

"They're saying the ice has taken two of the piers. That when the winter closure comes, it may not open again in spring at all. That the garrison means to seal the gates regardless — river, road, all of it. A closed city, Papa. Closed is closed."

The mildness in his face flickered — the first crack, hairline, the kind you only see if you know where to look, and God help me, I had spent nineteen years learning exactly where to look in everything except my own family.

"Well," he said lightly, turning back to his type. "Rumors. The river freezes and everyone becomes a prophet."

But his hands, when he reached for the matrix, were not quite steady. And I stood in the doorway of the room where our secrets lived in a locked drawer, and I understood with terrible clarity that the sentence being set down the line of this winter had moved closer — that it was no longer somewhere off the edge of the page but near enough to read.

Two days later, the winter bridge closed early, by order of the magistrate, and the gates of Vellmark were shut behind us.
