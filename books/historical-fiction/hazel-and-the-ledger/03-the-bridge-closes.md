## 3. The Bridge Closes

The morning the bridge closed, I was standing on it when they shut it.

That is the sort of thing that happens to a man who is always exactly where he shouldn't be. I had gone out before dawn to settle an account with a man named Corvus who deals in things that have no bills of sale, and I had lingered — because Corvus talks the way molasses pours, and because there is a pleasure in being paid twice for one errand that I have never learned to resist. So I was leaning on the frost-slick railing of the winter bridge, watching the river push its gray ice down toward the sea, when the garrison drums started.

At first I thought it was the mill. Then I thought it was a funeral. Then I saw the soldiers.

They came onto the bridge from both ends at once, forty men or more in Vellmark blue, and they brought with them a wagonload of timber and chains and a sergeant whose voice carried across the water like the crack of a whip against a wall. *By order of Magistrate Hollen,* he said, or shouted rather, *the winter bridge is closed to all traffic until spring thaw.* And then the soldiers began building a barricade right there on the span, hammering planks into a fence as neat as a coffin lid, while the crowd that had gathered — dockhands, fishwives, a few gentlemen out too early for their own reputations — stood in the cold and watched their city become an island.

I should describe my feelings here, I suppose. A better brother would have felt fear. A wiser man would have felt dread. What I felt, standing there with Corvus's coins warm in my glove, was something I can only call attention. The way you attend to a card game when you realize you've been dealt the winning hand and nobody else has noticed yet.

Because I knew something that sergeant didn't. Something most of Vellmark wouldn't know for weeks, until hunger taught them.

I knew the routes.

* * *

Let me explain what I am, since no one else in my family ever has. Freya is the artist; everyone says so, though she'd deny it with her ears going red. Father is the craftsman, the name behind the shop's good standing, the Dunmore who shakes hands with aldermen. And Tam? Tam is the one who knows which cellar doors open onto other cellars, which customs men take their brandy at which hour, which of the night ferrymen will carry a crate without asking what breathes inside it.

I am fourteen years old, and I have been running the edges of this city since I was eleven, when Father decided my legs were cheaper than a courier and Freya wasn't looking closely enough to stop me. It began honestly enough — ink from the chandler's, paper from the warehouse past Salt Row. But paper leads to pamphlets and pamphlets lead to questions, and questions lead to men like Corvus, who pay in silver and never ask a boy whether his father approves of him.

Here is what I know about Vellmark's geography, the geography that matters: the winter bridge is the only crossing east of the mill race, and the north gate is the only road out toward the coast, and between them the city sits in its bend of the river like a coin in a closed fist. When the bridge shuts and the gates seal, nothing lawful gets out. Grain comes in by the magistrate's license and at the magistrate's price. News goes nowhere. And anyone who wants passage to the spring boats — the smugglers' sloops that lie upriver at Halfmoon Cut, waiting for thaw — must go through channels that do not appear on any map worth hanging in an office.

There are four such channels. I know all four. Two run through cellars I could walk blindfolded, one runs through the good graces of a harbor sergeant whose debts I hold in a little book of my own — oh, yes, I keep a ledger too, though mine fits in my coat pocket — and the fourth runs through Corvus himself, who is less a man than a tollgate wearing a waistcoat.

So when the drums stopped and the barricade stood finished and the crowd began drifting homeward with the particular silence of people doing arithmetic about their larders, I stayed on the bridge a moment longer, and I did my own arithmetic.

One city, sealed. One spring boat, if the rumors held true. And how many souls in Vellmark who would give everything they owned for a berth on it?

* * *

I went home the long way, through Tanner's Court, because I wanted to see the gate.

The north gate was already changing. There were soldiers stacking muskets against the tollhouse like firewood, and a clerk from the magistrate's office nailing up a notice that flapped in the wind — *THE CITY IS CLOSED UNTIL FURTHER ORDER. PASSAGE BY PERMIT ONLY* — and beneath the notice, already gathering, a knot of merchants in good coats saying the word *permit* to each other over and over, the way you might say a curse word quietly, testing its weight.

A boy from the bakery line was selling hot rolls at the corner, shouting his price doubled, and nobody even frowned at him for it. That's Vellmark for you. We adjust fast. Grief takes us a day and gouging takes us an hour.

I bought a roll I couldn't afford and ate it slowly, watching the clerk finish his nailing, and I thought: every soul in this square is now a beggar waiting on the same mercy, and I am not. That is not pride, whatever it sounds like. Or perhaps it is. I was fourteen. I reserve the right to have been proud.

* * *

Home smelled of lead and lamp oil, as always. The shop was dark, but the workroom behind it glowed, and there was Father at his frame, cutting, and there was Freya at the casting bench with her sleeves rolled, her hands moving through the motions of her work the way other girls' hands move through prayers. Neither of them looked up when I came in. They never do, not at first. In our family you announce yourself by what you're carrying.

"Bridge is shut," I said.

"I heard the drums," Freya said, not lifting her eyes from the mold.

"Gates too. North gate's got a garrison detail billeted in the tollhouse. They'll be there till thaw." I set Corvus's payment on Father's desk, minus the portion that was mine, which I kept, and minus another portion I had already spent on the roll, which I did not mention. "Whole city's sealed. Nothing in, nothing out."

Father did look up then. His hands went still over the graver, and for a moment his face did something complicated — I watched it happen, the way you watch weather. Concern, calculation, and under both of them, faint as a watermark, something that looked almost like satisfaction. My father has always believed that trouble is a commodity like any other, and that the man who stays calm while others panic sets his own price. He taught me half of everything I know by accident.

"The boats," he said. "The spring boats. Are they still —"

"At Halfmoon Cut, far as anyone knows. They'll run at thaw regardless. Smugglers don't much care whose flag is on the bridge." I leaned in the doorway and did not say the next part quickly, because I had been rehearsing it since the bridge and I wanted it to land properly, the way Father lands a punchline in company, soft and devastating. "But there's a catch. Word at the docks is the *Merrow* — that's the big one, the one that usually takes a dozen fares — she's taking six berths this year instead. Six. Because sealed cities make nervous captains, and nervous captains run lighter."

Freya had stopped working. She was looking at me now, and I felt the look the way you feel a draft under a door.

"And the other boats?" she asked.

"Gone or committed. The *Sable Ann* signed with the grain factors weeks back — they saw this coming, someone with sense sold them the idea early. The two river skiffs won't cross once the garrison posts pickets at the cut, which they will, come February." I shrugged, a shrug I had practiced against my bedroom wall. "It's the *Merrow* or nothing. Six berths for a city of thirty thousand. And I know the man holding the list."

The stove ticked. Freya set down her ladle very carefully, the way she does when she doesn't trust her voice, and Father took off his spectacles and polished them on his apron, which is what he does when he doesn't want anyone to see his eyes.

"One seat," I said, into the quiet. "Maybe two, if the captain's mood improves by March. But you'd best plan on one."

I said *you'd best plan on one*, you understand. Not *we*. I chose those words the way Freya chooses typefaces, each letter set deliberately, and I told myself afterward that I hadn't known why. That is not quite true either. I knew. I simply hadn't let the knowing stand up where I could see it.

Father put his spectacles back on and looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him arrive, comfortably, at the conclusion I had built for him: that his clever son would manage this, that Tam knew the routes and the men and the arithmetic, that whatever needed arranging would be arranged by the one member of the family who moved freely through the sealed world. He nodded once. He said, "Then we're fortunate to have you," and went back to his cutting, and that was that. Settled. The way a debt gets settled in a ledger — silently, in numbers, without ceremony.

Freya didn't nod. She looked at me across the casting bench with her sleeves still rolled and her hands still shining with lead-dust, and she asked, softly, "Who told you it was one?"

"A man at the quay."

"Which man?"

"Just a man." I smiled my shop smile, the one that opens cellar doors. "You worry about your letters, Fri. Leave the city to me."

She kept looking at me a moment longer than was comfortable. Freya has a way of looking that feels like being read — like she's proofing you against some original and finding errors. Then she turned back to her mold, and the lead hissed, and the conversation was over.

* * *

That night I lay in my bed above the shop and listened to the wind coming off the river, and I did the arithmetic again, honestly this time, alone in the dark where honesty costs nothing.

Six berths. Thirty thousand souls. Father would need one, obviously — Father, who without the shop was just an old man with ink-stained fingers and enemies. And Freya — well, Freya. I turned that coin over and over. Freya, who had never once in her life done anything dangerous, anything bold, anything that required a route or a password or a man on the list. Freya, who would wait too long to ask for her place because asking is a kind of risk and she has never taken one. Freya, who would step aside anyway, I caught myself thinking, because that is what Freya does — she endures, she stays, she keeps the presses running. She'd want the berth used by someone who could survive out there. Someone useful. Someone like me.

You can justify anything at fourteen, in the dark, with the wind covering your tracks. I fell asleep mid-justification and dreamed of the river breaking up in spring, ice groaning and splitting, and a boat pulling away from a quay, and I was on it, and I did not look back at whoever stood on the stones.

In the morning there were soldiers on Salt Row, going door to door with a paper in the clerk's hand, asking names. And in the afternoon a stranger came into the shop, a stranger with a soft hat and hard eyes, and commissioned a pamphlet — and I watched from the stairwell as Father named his price and shook on it, and I recognized the man's accent from Halfmoon Cut, from the very quay where the *Merrow* rode at anchor, and I understood that my arithmetic had just acquired a complication I had not planned for.

Because the stranger didn't leave a deposit. He left a name, whispered in Father's ear, and Father's face, when he turned back to his frame, was the color of the paper he printed on.
