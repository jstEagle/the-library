## 4. What Father Hid

Father kept the good candles locked in the third drawer of his desk, which was absurd, because anyone who had grown up in this shop knew the key lived in the ink tin on the second shelf, beneath the crust of dried varnish that had been there since before Tam was born. I had stolen candles from that drawer perhaps forty times in my life. I knew its contents as well as I knew my own teeth: tallow stubs, a coil of wick, a letter from my mother's sister that nobody read twice, and a small brass weight stamped with a crown that Father claimed was a paperweight and I suspected was stolen.

So when I opened it that Thursday evening and found it empty except for a folded scrap of paper with *MARTIN'S* written on it in Father's hand, I stood there for a long moment feeling the way you feel when you miss a stair.

The candle wasn't the point. The candle was never the point. The point was that my father had begun hiding things from me, and he had done it badly, which meant he had done it quickly, which meant he was frightened.

I took the candle anyway. Sentiment is no reason to sit in the dark.

Below me, through the floorboards, I could hear him at the stone floor of the composing room, moving type around in that particular shuffling rhythm he fell into when he wanted to seem busy. He'd been seeming busy for three days. Ever since the stranger with the soft voice and the pamphlet — ever since I had set that pamphlet in proof and felt, under my own fingers, the unmistakable signature of my father's hand: the deep-cut serifs, that stubborn old Caslon he swore he'd melted down years ago.

He hadn't melted anything down. He never melts anything down. My father keeps everything — proofs, plates, debts, grudges — the way other men keep saints' bones. I should have known better than to believe a single thing he'd ever thrown away.

* * *

Here is what I told myself while I pretended to look for the plates: I told myself I was protecting him. That if the soldiers came back to the quarter — and they were coming back; you could set your watch by the boots now, tramping past the shutters every quarter hour — it would be better for everyone if the sedition were found by family first. It sounded noble. It even felt noble, in the way lies do when you cut them well and set them deep enough to hold a page.

The truth is less flattering. The truth is that pride is also an appetite, and mine wanted feeding. Twenty-two years of setting other people's words in metal, of being the invisible girl behind the visible page, and here was a mystery in my own house with my own father's fingerprints all over it. Of course I went looking. Any compositor worth her bodkin would have done the same. We are trained, all of us, to find the flaw in the forme — the turned letter, the pie'd line, the thing that doesn't belong. Show us order and we will hunt the disorder in it until our eyes ache.

The shop had three places a man could hide something the size of a pamphlet plate. The cellar, first — but the cellar floods in February, and Father loved his copper too much to trust it to water. The loft above the drying racks, where the paper stock sweated out its summers — possible, but he'd stopped climbing that ladder since his knee went bad last winter, and frightened men do not choose difficult stairs. Which left the press itself.

A press has a belly. Under the bed of my father's Stanhope there was a gap he used for storing the oiled rags, and I had crawled under it exactly once in my life, at nine years old, to escape a beating for having dropped a full galley of eight-point Baskerville. I remembered the smell: oil, iron, mouse. I remembered the cold of the stone against my back. What I did not remember — because why would I, at nine — was the loose board set into the wall behind the rags, the one with the shallow recess behind it, big enough for a strongbox or a Bible or a stack of plates wrapped in waxed cloth.

I lay down on the floor like a penitent and reached in, and my fingers found not metal but leather. A book. Old leather, cracked along the spine, swollen with damp and use, tied shut with the same green string Father used for bundling quires. I pulled it out into the candlelight, sat up against the press frame, and untied it.

I want to say I understood at once what I was holding. I didn't. At first it looked like any shop ledger, because that is precisely what it was pretending to be: columns, dates, sums, entries in my father's cramped upright hand. *Received of Capt. Herron, 3/6. Paid Mrs. Quill for board, 12s.* The dull bookkeeping of a small business barely keeping its nose above water. I almost put it back. I almost laughed at myself.

Then I read the names.

Not customers. Not the chandler, not the coal merchant. *Alderman Voss. Clerk Rennick. Captain Ostrer of the Watch. The Deputy Collector of Wharfage.* Names I knew from the gazette, from the magistrates' court lists, from the front windows of fine houses on Cantrell Row. And beside each name, in figures so neat they might have been devotional, sums that no printer on this earth had ever been paid for printing.

I turned pages. The candle guttered. Somewhere above me Tam came in, said something to Father, went out again; the door bell rang and neither of them called down to ask where I was, which suited me, because by then I could not have answered.

It was not a record of bribes received. It was worse than that. It was a record of bribes *paid and owed*, a double-entry account of corruption, and my father had kept both sides of it with a bookkeeper's conscience. Here was Voss, the alderman who dedicated orphanages and kissed babies at the Michaelmas fair, entered quarterly like rent: *A. Voss — retainer — £14 — for silence re: wharf ledgers.* Here was Rennick, the city clerk whose signature made every seizure of smuggled goods legal: *Rennick — paid — £9 — "the usual consideration."* Here, on a page dated two years back, in ink so fresh-looking it might have bled yesterday:

*Magistrate Corven — balance outstanding — £200 — held against services rendered.*

Two hundred pounds. My father earned perhaps sixty in a good year, counting the smuggling work he never spoke of and I never asked about. Two hundred pounds was a house. Two hundred pounds was Tam set up in trade, was me married with a dowry, was ten winters of firewood. Two hundred pounds was a sum a man like Aldous Dunmore did not hold. It was a sum he *owed*, or was *held against* — the phrasing would not resolve, and I began to understand that it was not meant to. Ambiguity was the product. My father sold ambiguity. He kept accounts for men who needed someone honest-handed to write down their dishonesty, and somewhere along the way — I could see it happening, entry by entry, year by year, like a slow tide filling a cellar — he had stopped being the man who wrote the numbers and become a man who could be drowned by them.

And there, near the end, in the last quarter's entries, the new handwriting. Not Father's. A harder hand, slanted, confident, the letters of a man who signs death warrants without a second pen. Entries in that hand referred to entries in Father's. *See p. 41 re: Ostrer.* *Balance confirmed — C.*

Page 41. I turned back. Page 41 listed six names, four of which I recognized from the pamphlet I had set three nights ago — the pamphlet naming the men who had sold the grain contracts out from under the city while the price of bread climbed toward heaven. The stranger's pamphlet had not been composed from rumor. It had been composed from *this*. Someone had copied these pages, given them wings, and my father's secret type had printed them.

Which meant my father had not merely been keeping the ledger. He had leaked it. Or someone had stolen from him. Either way, the magistrate's name was in this book beside a figure of two hundred pounds, and the magistrate now knew — Father's fear these three days said he knew — that the book existed.

I sat under the press with the ledger open on my knees and felt something inside me rearrange itself, quiet and permanent, the way type shifts in a loosely locked chase — nothing falls, not yet, everything still prints, but the forme is unsound and one knock will scatter it.

I had built my contentment the way I built a page: piece by piece, believing each piece solid, never looking at the whole forme. My father cutting type at dawn, humming. Tam running errands with coins in both pockets. The smell of ink, the pride of invisible craft. All of it true, and all of it mortgaged. Every comfortable month of my life had been financed, in some measure I could now calculate to the farthing, by the silence in this book. He had gambled with the magistrate's money and the smugglers' patience and the Watch's blindness, and he had staked the only things he actually owned: Tam's neck, my neck, his own.

And the worst of it — the part I have not confessed to anyone, and will confess only to this page — is that for one full minute, sitting in the dark among the oiled rags, I hated him cleanly and completely, and it was almost a relief. Betrayal, I learned that night, is a kind of gift at first. It explains everything. The locked drawer, the busy shuffling, the way he watched my hands when I handled the pamphlet proofs. It gave shape to all the unease I had spent years declining to examine.

Then the minute passed, and love came back the way it does, heavier for the beating, dragging its questions behind it. Where were the plates? Had the stranger copied the pages or stolen them? Did Tam know? Above all — the question that made my hands go cold on the leather — *who else knew the book existed?*

Because a ledger is only paper until it is evidence. But in the wrong hands, in a sealed city, in a winter when men were already being taken from their beds for lesser documents — this book was a rope with my father's name knotted into it. Sixty pounds a year against two hundred in bribes. They wouldn't even need a trial. The river was right there.

I heard Father's step on the stairs and I did what any Dunmore does instinctively: I hid the evidence. The ledger went back behind the board, the board went flush, the rags went over it, and I was standing in the middle of the composing room holding a candlestick and wearing an expression of mild irritation about the missing tallow when he came down.

He stopped on the bottom step. He looked at the candle in my hand. He looked at my face. And I watched him decide — I saw the decision happen, a flicker behind the eyes, quick as a compositor catching a turned letter — whether to believe me.

"The third drawer," he said quietly. "I thought I moved them."

"You moved the candles," I said. "You left the note."

Something went out of his shoulders. Relief, or resignation; from below, in that light, they look alike. "Freya," he began, and then stopped, because there are sentences a man cannot finish once his daughter has read his handwriting in a book like that, and I think we both understood, in the smell of ink and lamp oil between us, that I knew. Not the details. Not yet. But the shape of it. The forme of it.

"There's a man coming tomorrow," he said instead. "A customer. I need you at the case, and I need whatever he says to stay between him and me."

"Father—"

"He pays double." He said it the way he said *good morning*, steady, reasonable, terrible. "We'll want the money this spring."

That night I did not sleep. I lay in the loft above the drying racks — my own hiding place, though mine held nothing but a cache of penny dreadfuls and a bottle of cherry brandy Tam thought I didn't know about — and I listened to the garrison boots pass on the cobbles, twelve men, then silence, then twelve more. And I made myself a promise with the clean, cold clarity of a freshly cast slug: before another soul in this city decided what that ledger was worth, I was going to read every page of it.

Including page 41. Including the name *C.*

Including, though I did not know it yet, a column near the end, in Tam's handwriting, that would break my heart in a way the rest of it never managed.

But all of that was still to come. For now there was only the dark, the boots, the river waiting under the ice, and my father downstairs, awake too, pacing his composing room like a man rehearsing a lie.
