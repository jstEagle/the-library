## 5. The Magistrate's Offer

The summons came on a Tuesday, which I have always considered the most dishonest day of the week. Mondays at least make no pretense. Tuesdays arrive dressed as ordinary.

The boy who brought it wore the magistrate's colors and would not meet my eye, which told me more than the letter did. A summons to the Hall of Assize, addressed not to *Printer Dunmore* but to *Aldous Dunmore, gentleman* — the flourish of a man being polite to a corpse before the burial. I read it twice by the shop window while Freya cut type three feet away, her burin whispering against the steel, and Tam was out on his errands, God knew which set of them, and I thought: *here it is, then. The bill has come due, and they have sent it on a Tuesday.*

"Who's it from?" Freya asked without looking up. She has always known the sound of bad paper. She set it herself, after all — she can tell a summons from an invoice by the weight of it alone.

"The wool merchant's guild," I said. "A dispute over a shipping mark."

She nodded and went back to her letters, and I went upstairs and put on my good coat, and if my hands shook a little at the buttons, well, I am fifty-three years old and the winter has been long. Any man's hands might shake at the buttons in February.

* *

Magistrate Corven received me in a room with a fire so large it was nearly a performance. He sat beside it with his boots stretched out like a man at his own hearth, though the Hall belonged to the city, and so did I, in every way that mattered now. Two clerks sat at a side table with their pens already inked. They did not look at me either. Nobody in Vellmark was meeting anybody's eye this winter; we had become a city of people studying their shoes.

"Dunmore." He said my name the way one reads a wine label. "Sit. You'll take brandy?"

"I'll sit."

"Brandy anyway." He poured two glasses himself, which was its own kind of message. Magistrates do not pour. Men pour for other men when they are about to ask something beneath both of them, and want it understood that the pouring makes them even. "How is the trade? Ink scarce? Paper?"

"Paper's always scarce. It's why we print on both sides."

He smiled. He had a good smile, Corven, wide and warm as a bake-oven, and about as safe to put your hand into. "I'll come to it, then, since you're a man who likes both sides of things. We found one of your pamphlets, Dunmore. Not the ones you print for the guild, not your almanacs, not poor Mrs. Hessel's funeral verses — I mean the other work. The work with the type you cut yourself." He held up a folded sheet between two fingers, gingerly, as though it might go off. "The one calling the grain tariff a theft. The one naming names."

I looked at it. There was no point denying it; he would not have shown it to me unless denial had stopped being useful several days ago. "That's a crude copy," I said. "My kerning is better."

"So it is. So it is." He set it down on the table between us, squared its edges like a man arranging a dinner service. "Now. Here is what I know, and you will forgive me if I recite it rather than chat, because the clerks need it plain for the record they may or may not keep, depending." He leaned forward. The fire threw his shadow up the wall behind him, taller than any man has a right to be. "You have been printing sedition for eight months. Payment routed through Tam's errands and the smugglers on the south quay. And — this is the part that interests me, Dunmore, the part that kept me awake of a night, thinking of you, thinking of your clever daughter cutting letters like a nun cuts relics — you have kept accounts. A ledger. Names on both sides of it. What officials took what. Which of my colleagues owes whom. Who signed the grain order. Who pocketed the bridge tolls all these years the ice kept the city sealed."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the square a guard called the hour, and his voice sounded very far away, as though it belonged to a different city, a warmer year.

"A printer keeps books," I said. "Ink, paper—"

"Ink and paper." He laughed, genuinely delighted, and I understood suddenly why men confessed to him. Not from fear. From the terrible intimacy of being *seen*. "Ink and paper. Is that what you'd call the name of Deputy Wrayl? Because I've read it, Aldous. In your hand. With dates and sums, in your hand, in a book hidden behind the case of twelve-point pica in your back room — third shelf, left side, under the rag bundle. Don't look surprised. I pay better than the smugglers do, and I ask fewer questions about cargo."

There was nothing to do then but drink the brandy. It was good brandy. Of course it was; he could afford it, and so could everyone whose names were in my book, and that was precisely the arithmetic that had brought me to this chair.

"What happens to me," I said, "if you've read it?"

"That depends entirely," Corven said, "on what happens next."

* *

Here is what I will set down, since I have set down everything else in that book, and a man should keep honest books even about his own dishonesty.

I began printing the seditious sheets for money. That part is simple and shames nobody who has watched a city strangle itself through a closed bridge — ink costs, bread costs more. But somewhere in the autumn I stopped being a hired press and became a collector. Every pamphlet I printed came with gossip attached, and every piece of gossip came with a name, and I am a bookkeeper by temperament if not by trade. My wife died owing nothing to anyone, and I decided I would never be so vulnerable again, and so I built myself a fortress out of other men's sins. I wrote down every debt, every bribe, every signature sold and favor bought, and I told myself it was insurance. Protection. Leverage.

I told myself it was for the children.

You can justify anything, if you frame it small enough. Burn a barn to save the house. Name a stranger to save a son. I have had eighteen months of practice and I could teach a class.

Corven laid it out for me plainly, in the manner of a man offering a fair price for a horse he intends to work to death. Surrender the ledger. Name the others — the smugglers, the typesetters on the south bank, whoever fed me the grain-order signatures. Do that, and the pamphlets become an old mistake, paid for. No charges. No charges against Tam, whose errands were known to every watchman on the quay anyway. No charges against Freya, who was, Corven observed with that oven-warm smile, *innocent of everything except excellence*, and wasn't that a lovely thing, a daughter worth protecting.

"And the spring boat," he said, turning his glass by its stem. "The last berth out before the ice breaks. I know you've been saving for passage. Two berths, Dunmore. One for whichever child you choose, and passage-money besides, and papers that won't be questioned at the gangplank. All of it yours, the day the ledger is on my desk and the names are in my mouth instead of yours."

"Why," I said, "do you want my book badly enough to buy it twice?"

"Because spring is coming," he said, "and when the bridge opens, everything written in that book walks across it into the light. I would rather it walked into my desk. There is a new commission forming in the capital to investigate the administration here, and I intend to be the man who assisted it, not the man it assisted upon. Your ledger is my testimony, Dunmore. Yours is just the hand that wrote it." He spread his palms, reasonable as a Sunday sermon. "You lose nothing but the property of other men's crimes. I gain a career. Your children gain a father and a future. The city gains stability. Everyone is improved. It is, when you think of it, the most charitable transaction available to either of us this winter."

* *

I walked home the long way, along the frozen canal, because the short way passes the quay where Tam does his business and I could not have borne seeing it. The ice groaned under the city the way it has all month — a long, low sound, like furniture moving in a room above yours. Some nights I think the whole of Vellmark is only waiting for the thaw to find out what it has been standing on.

Let me say what is true, since truth is apparently the currency now.

If I give him the book, the men on the south quay hang. Old Piet, who taught Tam the culverts. The Wrayl deputy's clerk, a girl of nineteen who copied signatures for bread. Half a dozen others whose names I gathered like a miser gathers coins — useless, hoarded, warm only to the touch of the hand that holds them. Corven will burn them all to keep his chair, and I will have handed him the kindling, and my children will live.

My children will live.

I have said that sentence to myself perhaps four hundred times since Tuesday evening, walking, lying awake, standing in the shop at dawn with my thumb on the third shelf, left side, feeling the rag bundle that is not rags. Four hundred times, and it has not once stopped sounding like a coin I am stealing from another man's pocket.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about sacrifice: it is easy to spend when you are spending someone else. I told myself I was a father protecting his family, and that is even true, but it is true the way a counterfeit coin is true — right weight, right ring, wrong metal. The metal underneath is this: I built a weapon out of other men's secrets, and now that the weapon has turned toward my own throat, I propose to fire it outward one last time and let strangers absorb the shot.

Freya would not do it. I know my daughter. If she found that book — and she will, she finds everything, she has eyes like type sorts, small and sharp and everywhere — she would not trade it. She would print it. God help me, I can see her doing it, setting each damning line by hand, pulling proofs in the dark with her sleeves rolled up, that quiet stubborn pride of hers finally aimed at something. She thinks I don't see her. I see everything she does. It is the whole trouble between fathers and daughters: they inherit our silence along with our craft.

And Tam — Tam would sell the book *and* the berth *and* me, and sleep well after, and call it sense.

So it falls to me, the coward in the middle, the man of compromise, the keeper of accounts. Give Corven his testimony. Take the papers. Put Freya on the boat and follow when I can, or follow when I cannot, which amounts to the same thing with the ending changed.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow I will open the case, lift out the rag bundle, and carry my life's worst work down to the Hall like a good tradesman delivering an order.

I had my hand on the shop door when I saw the lamp burning late inside, low and steady, and through the frosted pane the small bent silhouette of my daughter at the type cases — searching, patiently, methodically, the way she does everything —

— third shelf, left side.
