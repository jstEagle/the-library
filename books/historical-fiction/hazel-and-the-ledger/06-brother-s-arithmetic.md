## 6. Brother's Arithmetic

My father kept his lies in the same place he kept his glue pot, which tells you something about how he thought of them: as tools of the trade, no different from a brayer or a quoin key. I found the ledger on a Tuesday, three days after the bridge closed, because I had gone looking for the good beeswax and instead found the whole ruin of our family bound in calf leather with a brass clasp gone green.

I want it understood that I did not go looking for it. That matters to me more than it should.

The shop was empty that afternoon. Freya had gone to the type case room to cut matrices — she does that thing where she hums while she works, like the letters themselves were singing through her, and I have never told her that I can hear it through two floors and that it is the only honest music in Vellmark. Father was off at the Anchor & Fid, drinking with men who claimed to be merchants. The fog sat against the windows like a creditor.

I was after the beeswax because the sledge runners needed dressing if I meant to make my rounds before the curfew bell. Tam Dunmore, courier of everything legal and most things not — pamphlets for the Chartist society, sealed letters for the smugglers at Gray Wharf, a bundle of French newspapers that smelled of another world. I know every alley in this city the way Freya knows every letterform. Hers is the cleaner knowledge. Mine pays better.

The wax wasn't in its drawer. Neither was it in the second drawer, or the cabinet under the stairs. I ended up kneeling in my father's office with my sleeves rolled to the elbow, hauling out reams of spoiled paper, and there, beneath a false bottom I'd have sworn didn't exist until my knuckle found its edge, lay the ledger.

I should have put it back. Any sensible man would have put it back.

I opened it.

Now — I have carried letters for magistrates and for men who wanted magistrates dead. I am not naive about how Vellmark runs. It runs on debts the way a mill runs on water; everyone knows this and everyone agrees not to say it aloud. But knowing a thing in your spine is different from seeing it set down in a fair hand with dates and sums. There it all was. Alderman Crouse, forty pounds, paid quarterly, marked *for looking away*. Captain of the gate watch, Harrow, twenty-five and a cask of brandy each solstice, marked *for the winter list* — which meant the list of whose cargo got inspected and whose got waved through. Page after page. And not just officials. Printers. Smugglers. A chandler's widow. Names, amounts, dates going back eleven years, in my father's writing getting steadier as the years went on, which struck me as obscene. A man's hand ought to shake when he writes down what ruins people.

And then, near the end, in fresher ink, the entry that stopped my breath: *Magistrate Quill — see memoranda.* No sum. Just his name, alone on the line, underlined twice.

I sat back on my heels among the spoiled paper and did arithmetic. Not the moral kind — I'm not built for the moral kind — the practical kind. This ledger could hang my father. It could also buy him out of Vellmark, or buy me out, or buy half the corporation ten times over depending on whose hands held it. It was, I realized, holding the thing by its green clasp, worth exactly as much as whoever held it could bargain. Nothing more, nothing less. Everything in this city has a price; here was the price list itself.

I heard Freya humming through the floorboards. I put the ledger back under the false bottom, wax untouched, and went out into the fog to think about what I'd become willing to do.

* * *

Here is what nobody in my family will admit: we were already drowning before the bridge closed. The shop takes three jobs to pay for every one it gets, and the third job lately has been sedition. I knew about the pamphlets before Freya did — she thinks I don't notice anything but coin, and I let her think it, the way you let a blindfolded person keep their dignity. Type doesn't lie to the man who carries its output. I'd been running those pamphlets to the radical societies since summer, telling myself it was just freight.

So when the stranger came asking after a pamphlet in Father's secret typeface, and Freya started sleeping badly, I did my own version of sleeping badly: I counted. What we owed. What we had. What the magistrate's men were paying informants these days — because yes, I knew that too; a boy who runs errands for everyone hears everyone's rates.

The summons came for Father on Thursday. I know because I followed him. He walked to the courthouse like a man walking to his own funeral and trying to look like he was going somewhere pleasanter, and he came out ninety minutes later with a face I'd never seen on him, gray and smooth, like poured lead before it sets.

That night he told us nothing. Freya asked twice what the magistrate wanted; he said *taxes, nothing but taxes*, and looked at the fire, and Freya let it go because she loves him, and love is a kind of blindness, and I am the family's eyes.

I followed him again Friday. Not far. Just far enough to the chandlery on Rope Walk where he met a man I knew by sight: Sergeant Locke, of the magistrate's provost detail, a broad soft-spoken man who tips well for deliveries and remembers names. They spoke for perhaps four minutes. When Father left, he walked lighter. When Locke left, he stood a moment in the doorway looking after him, and then, because God mocks the eavesdropper, Locke turned and looked directly at me across the street.

I could have run. Running confirms guilt, and besides, my legs have never once failed me professionally. Instead I crossed over and wished him good morning like we were acquaintances, because in a way we were.

"You're the Dunmore boy," he said. "The runner."

"Tam. At your service, mostly."

He almost smiled. "Mostly." He studied me the way you study a ledger line to see if the figures add up. "Your father's a hard man to read."

"He taught me printing," I said. "You learn to mirror everything."

Another almost-smile. Then: "There's a boat out in spring. One berth spoken for through Gray Wharf, maybe a second if the ice breaks early. You'd know about that, being in the carrying trade."

I said nothing, which is itself an answer, and Locke nodded slowly as though I'd given him change for a sovereign.

"Magistrate keeps a warm corner for cooperative families," he said. "Warm corners are cheaper than cold cells. You might mention that to your father." He tipped his hat and went, and I stood in the fog doing arithmetic again, and this time the figures frightened me, because the column I was adding up was myself.

* * *

Let me be precise about what happened over the following week, because I intend, someday, to defend myself, and even a bad defense needs accurate facts.

I did not sell my father out. I want that recorded. What I did was smaller, and smaller is worse.

I sold the routes.

Tuesday, I told Locke's clerk — a nervous ink-stained lad named Pell who meets informants behind the fish market — that the Chartist pamphlets moved on Thursdays along Cooper's Alley to a cellar behind the cooperage. This cost my father nothing directly; that route belonged to the radical societies, and the men who printed there were not men I loved, but they were not men I hated either. They were men who paid me sixpence a run and thanked me by name. Pell gave me half a crown. It was more than eight runs' profit, and it weighed less than a pamphlet.

Wednesday, I dressed the sledge runners with the beeswax I'd originally been seeking, and made my rounds, and delivered a sealed letter for the smugglers at Gray Wharf, and shook hands with the boat master, and confirmed what Locke had hinted: one berth bought, papers pending, sailing when the ice broke. Mine. Bought weeks ago with money Father didn't ask the source of, because Father has learned not to ask about money that arrives.

Thursday, the cooperage cellar was raided. Two men taken. The pamphlet press smashed in the street where children could watch, as a lesson written in iron instead of ink.

Friday, I bought a meat pie with the half crown and could not eat it.

You'll note the progression. Routes, then men. Each step small. Each step payable. That's how it goes, betrayal — it's not a leap, it's an installment plan. Nobody wakes up a traitor. They wake up owing, and they pay a little at a time, and the debt collector is always yourself.

But here is the figure I couldn't get to balance. The magistrate's offer, as Father had relayed it to Mother's memory and to the empty room — I'd listened outside doors long enough to reconstruct it: surrender the ledger, name the co-conspirators, and the family walks free. Both children safe. No berth needed. No boat. No choice between Freya and me.

It was generous. It was, by Vellmark standards, nearly tender. And it would not hold, because I had seen the ledger, and I had seen the entry: *Magistrate Quill — see memoranda.* The moment Father handed it over, the moment his name left his mouth attached to other names, Quill owned him body and ledger both, and witnesses to a magistrate's debts do not walk free in any city I've carried mail through. The offer wasn't mercy. It was a purchase order for two lives, priced at zero.

Unless the ledger changed hands differently. Unless someone else — someone quicker than a sixty-year-old printer, someone who already knew the wharves and the guards and the exact weight of the thing — got to Sergeant Locke first and offered not the ledger but a portion of it. Enough names to please Quill. Not enough to finish us. A partial sale, like selling chapters of a book.

I knew which names I could spare. I'd carried their letters.

Saturday night I stood behind the fish market with my collar up and Pell's lantern bobbing toward me, and I named three names — a chandler, a carter, a woman who ran letters to the river villages — and took a crown for each, and felt nothing, which frightened me more than feeling anything, because I had budgeted for guilt and found my account empty. Then I named a fourth. A printer. An old apprentice of Father's, a gentle man named Josiah Crane who once taught me to ink a roller without wasting a drop.

Pell wrote it down. "The magistrate will want the big names eventually," he said, blowing on the wet ink. "The ledger itself."

"The ledger's not for sale," I said. "Not by me."

"Everything's for sale in Vellmark, Dunmore. Bridge proves it. Even the bridge closed for a price."

I walked home the long way, along the quay, past the shipwrights' yard where the spring boat sat on the stocks under canvas like a promise. My berth. My arithmetic said I could still come out of this whole: family spared by negotiation, self spared by the boat, conscience spared by never having touched the ledger itself. Three columns, all balanced.

Then I turned the corner into Printing Row and saw the light burning in our shop window at midnight, and my father's shadow bent over his desk, and beside it a second shadow — Freya's, still, patient, cutting or reading or simply watching him. And through the glass I watched them for a while, the two people my installments were supposed to protect, and I thought about Josiah Crane teaching a boy to ink a roller, and about the two men from the cooperage, and about the woman who ran letters to the river villages, whose name I hadn't even known, only her rate.

The arithmetic was wrong. I saw it plainly, standing in the snow like a beggar outside my own life. Somewhere back down the column I had carried a digit wrong, and now every sum above it was poisoned, and there was no correcting an error in the middle of a page — any compositor knows you must reset the whole line.

Inside, Freya lifted her head suddenly, as if she'd heard my thoughts through the glass, and looked straight at the window.

I stepped back into the dark, heart slamming, and began, for the first time in my life, to be afraid of what I'd become — because the next installment was coming due, and I knew exactly what the collector would ask for next.

Father's ledger. Or Freya's share of the truth.
