## 7. Night of the Presses

The first stone came through the window at half past nine, which I know because I was mending the case clock at the time, and when the glass went it stopped the clock's pendulum mid-swing. My father would later insist — to himself, in his cell, wherever they put him — that the riot came out of a clear sky. It did not. It had been building all week the way weather builds, and like weather, everyone in Vellmark spoke about it constantly and did nothing.

I looked at the hole in the window. Cold air poured through it, smelling of river and smoke and something sharper underneath. Through the gap I could see torchlight on Copperlane, and hear a sound I had only ever heard once before, at my mother's funeral: a great many people making one noise.

"Freya." My father was in the doorway of the back room, still in his apron, a composing stick in his hand as though he meant to set type for the apocalypse. "Go upstairs."

"Will you come with me?"

"I have to see to the press."

"You have to see to *yourself*," I said, and I want it recorded that even then, even with the city coming apart forty yards from our door, some part of me was keeping score of his foolishness, filing it away the way I filed everything. "They're burning the Rookery presses right now. Tam said—"

"Tam says many things." He was already moving toward the press, that beloved iron monster we'd named Goliath because my father claimed it took three men and a miracle to move it. "The plates aren't here, Freya. Whatever they want, they won't find it here."

That was true, and it was also a lie, and I did not yet know which parts were which. That had been the shape of my life for two months now: true things wrapped around lies like paper around a wire stitch.

I should have gone upstairs. Any sensible person would have gone upstairs. But you cannot live your whole life being sensible in other people's stories, and something in me — call it stubbornness, call it the printer's instinct that says *the job is not finished until the job is finished* — turned my feet instead toward the type room.

The ledger was where I had left it. Under the false bottom of the ink cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth, fat as a Bible and twice as damning. I had found it there in February, and in the weeks since I had done what I do best: nothing, visibly. I had cut typefaces and set formes and swept the shop, and every night I had gone to bed with the thing's existence sitting in my chest like a swallowed nail. Names. Magistrates. Sums. The arithmetic of an entire city sold off in installments, written in my father's careful hand — the same hand that taught me my letters.

I had not decided what to do with it. I am not sure I believed decision was required. Things in Vellmark stayed as they were; that was practically the city charter.

The second stone took out the door glass.

* * *

They came in through the front the way water comes through a cracked dam — not all at once but in a finding, probing rush, and then all at once. I heard Goliath take the first blow, heard iron sing, and I flattened myself behind the type racks in the back room with the ledger against my stomach and the oilcloth crushed under my arms, and I waited to die or be robbed or worse, and what happened instead was almost comical.

They wrecked the place.

Not efficiently. Not with purpose. A dozen men and boys, drunk on rum and their own numbers, breaking everything breakable because breaking was the night's whole program. They tipped the ink barrels. They smashed the specimen cases — twenty years of my father's finest work, alphabets he'd cut with a graver finer than a surgeon's, gone under boots in ninety seconds. Somebody found the store of paper and somebody else found the brandy my father thought he'd hidden, and by the time they got to Goliath they were less a mob than a parade.

From behind the type racks I watched a boy no older than Tam try to pry a chase loose from the press bed and swear when the quoins wouldn't give. Another man — I knew his face from the market, a fish porter with hands like spades — simply stood in the middle of the ruined shop turning over a galley of set type, letting the letters rain through his fingers onto the floor, and there was such puzzled tenderness in the gesture that I nearly laughed, or wept, or both.

"*Where's Dunmore?*" someone shouted, and my heart performed a maneuver I did not care for.

But the answer came from the street — *"Gone! Took the family out the back hours ago!"* — which was a lie, and a useful one, though I never learned who told it. Perhaps Tam. Tam was always out the back of things.

My father was not in the shop. I realized this slowly, the way you realize you've been burned: after the pain. He had been behind me in the doorway, and then he hadn't. Either he had fled upstairs as instructed — unthinkable — or he had slipped out through the yard to move the plates, the accursed pamphlet plates, the sedition that paid our debts, choosing the poison over the child. I would like to tell you I was surprised. I was instead visited by a clarity so cold it felt almost holy: *he will always choose the plates.*

The mob grew bored. Boredom is the mob's natural terminus; rage burns hot but it doesn't bank well. They poured out the shattered front doors toward the magistrate's court on Copperlane, dragging the fish porter with them, still trailing letters, and the noise moved off down the street like a tide going out.

I counted to one hundred. Then I got up, put the ledger inside my shirt, and went to work.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about terror: it is excellent for concentration. My hands did not shake. I noticed this with a kind of detached professional interest, the way I might notice that a new alloy held its edge better than expected. Every craft I had — every hour my father had spent teaching me to cut a matrix true, to cast lead at exactly the heat where it runs like silver water and no hotter — assembled itself and asked for orders.

The ledger could not stay in the shop. The shop was dead; I understood that standing in the wreckage of it, ankle-deep in my own alphabet. And it could not go home, because home was the same building. And it could not be carried about the city in oilcloth like a stolen ham, because the city tomorrow morning would be crawling with soldiers and informers and every species of rat that flourishes after a fire.

It needed to become something no one would look at twice.

Type founders hide things in type. This is not wisdom, it is simply fact. A case of type is heavy, dull, and sacred to a printer — no soldier ransacks a case of ten-point brevier unless he can read it, and the men who'd want this ledger read could hire others to search for it. If I cast the ledger's pages as slugs — solid lines of lead, reversed and mirrored, illegible to any eye but a compositor's — and set them into the body of a ordinary case among ordinary sorts, the most dangerous document in Vellmark would sit on a shelf wearing the plain face of inventory.

It would also, I should note, be nearly useless. To read it you'd need to pull every slug, clean every shank, proof each line. Weeks of work. But uselessness was rather the point; I wasn't publishing tonight. I was hiding a knife where only I could find the handle.

So I worked. God help me, I *enjoyed* working — that is the confession this chapter requires. Outside, Vellmark burned in perhaps six places; through the broken window the sky over Copperlane was the color of a forge at drawing heat. Inside, I sat at the casting bench with a candle stub and my father's molds and I composed the ledger's most damning pages line by line, and my hands were steady, and there came over me a feeling I had no name for yet. It was not bravery. Bravery implies fear mastered, and what I felt was fear fully intact, simply given a job.

*Magistrate Corvel — received — £40 — the quay tolls.* Cast. *Aldous Dunmore to the Hon. P. Reave — debt entered, interest waived — consideration: silence.* Cast. Name after name, bribe after bribe, running backward in mirror-writing like everything else in my trade, like everything else in my family.

Somewhere in the third hour Tam came in through the wrecked doorway, pale, smelling of smoke, and stopped dead at the sight of me: his sister at the casting bench in a gutted shop, calmly pouring molten lead by candlelight amid the ruins.

"Freya." His voice cracked on it. "Christ. I thought—" He didn't finish. He looked at the mold, at the slugs, at the ledger open on the bench, and I watched him understand in real time what I was holding, watched the arithmetic start behind his eyes — I knew that look; I'd been watching him do sums since he was six. "You shouldn't have that here. You should sink it in the river."

"Then it's fortunate," I said, "that I'm not asking your advice."

"They'll hang him for it. They'll hang *all* of us."

"Then we had best be certain it's worth it." I set the slug I'd just cast into the case — third row from the top, between two columns of long s's — and looked up at my brother, and felt something settle in me that had been unsettled since February. "Go to bed, Tam."

He went. For now, he went.

By dawn I had hidden eleven pages inside a case labeled ORDINARY BODIES — JOBbing SORTS, DAMAGED, and I sat in the gray light with my burned thumb in my mouth and my father's chair empty and Goliath scarred but standing, and I understood that something in me had closed like a quoins tightening — that I had spent my whole life enduring this city, and that endurance was over.

The bell of St. Werburgh's began to toll. Not for service. Six strokes, pause, six more — the alarm pattern, the one that meant soldiers in the streets and the gates shut even in daylight.

And underneath the bell, faint from the direction of the river, I heard the thing that pulled me upright out of the chair: a whistle. Three notes, repeated. Tam's whistle — the smugglers' signal he used to think I didn't know.

My brother was calling in a debt, and I very much feared I knew whose name he'd be paying with.
