## 8. The Price of Names

*Tam*

There is an arithmetic to everything, my sister notwithstanding. Freya counts letters; I count exits. She learned her numbers from our father's type cases, all those little compartments of order. I learned mine from the smugglers who used to buy me gin and ask me questions, and from watching which doors in Vellmark had guards outside them and which had none.

The magistrate's men had a door on Candlewick Row with no guard at all. Just a clerk named Pell, thin as a rule, who drank his tea like it had wronged him and kept a book even thinner than our father's.

"You want the berth papers," Pell said. "We want names."

"I want," I said, "to be paid in advance."

He looked at me over the rim of his cup with something like respect. It is a curious thing, being underestimated your whole life because your sister is the clever one and your father is the genius. There are freedoms in it. Nobody watches the boy who carries parcels. Nobody wonders what the boy knows.

I gave him four names that first day. Small ones. Men who had already fled or would flee soon anyway — debts owed to Aldous Dunmore by men whose ruin would cost the world nothing. Pell wrote them down and slid an envelope across the table, and inside was a receipt for one berth on the *Coriander*, sailing when the ice broke, and my hands did not shake at all, which surprised me.

I had expected them to shake. I had rehearsed the shaking. In the rehearsals I always stopped at the moment I thought of Father, and in the rehearsals I always turned back. Rehearsals, it turns out, are lies you tell yourself in a warm room.

* * *

*Freya*

I knew something was wrong before I knew what it was. That's the printer's instinct: you feel the press go out of register before you see the smear.

It began with small absences. Tam gone at hours he could not explain. New coins in his pocket, bright ones, the kind that don't come from running errands. He'd taken to wearing his collar buttoned to the top, which in my brother has always been the uniform of a man hiding something at the throat.

And then Henrik Voss was arrested.

I want you to understand what Henrik Voss was to us, because the word *friend* is too small a crate to carry it. Henrik had apprenticed under Father thirty years ago. When Mother died, it was Henrik who stood in our shop for three days straight setting type for the funeral notices so Father wouldn't have to, because Father's hands had gone strange and wouldn't hold a composing stick steady. Henrik printed pamphlets for the reform men out of a cellar on Tanners' Lane and never once, in all the years I've known him, asked Father to explain where his money came from. Some friendships are built on questions politely never asked.

They took him on a Tuesday. Six soldiers, no warrant shown, his cellar presses broken open with hatchets before his apprentices were even out of the building. His wife Maren came to our shop that evening with sawdust still in her hair and told us in a voice like a dropped plate exactly how it happened, and who had been seen speaking with the magistrate's clerk three days prior, and she did not look at Tam when she said it, which was worse than if she had. A person will forgive you for accusing them. Being carefully not accused — that stays in the blood.

Father went white and said nothing. Father had been going white and saying nothing for a week now; whatever the magistrate had said to him behind closed doors had gotten into him like damp into plaster. But I watched Tam. And Tam watched the floor.

That night I followed him.

* * *

*Tam*

Here is what nobody tells you about selling people: it doesn't feel like selling. It feels like tidying.

The men I named were already lost, I told myself. Vellmark was coming down around everyone's ears — you could hear it at night now, the crowds moving through the quarters like water finding cracks. The bridge was shut, the gates sealed, and every man on my list was standing in a city filling up with soldiers. Their names didn't doom them. The winter did. I was just being paid to read the weather aloud.

Pell had given me the second installment: the berth paper itself, signed, plus coin enough to live on until the ice broke. "One more name," he'd said, "and we stop asking." One more name. As if there were a bottom to the sack. As if he didn't know perfectly well that a man who's sold four names will sell fourteen, because after the fourth the arithmetic changes — not what each name is worth, but what *you* are worth without them. A man with no names left to sell is just a witness. Witnesses drown.

I didn't plan to give him the last name. I want that written somewhere. I sat on the frozen quay below the grain warehouses with the berth paper inside my coat, warming me like a stolen loaf, and I told myself: this is where it ends. Four names. Enough.

Then I heard footsteps and it was Freya, and everything I had told myself went into the river.

* * *

*Freya*

He was sitting on the bollards by the coal wharf, where the ice along the quay had gone grey and ridged like old scar tissue. He heard me coming — of course he did, I've never once managed to sneak up on him — but he didn't turn around. That alone told me everything.

"Say it isn't true," I said.

"Which part?" My brother has many gifts; choosing courage over wit has never been among them.

"Henrik."

"He'll hang or he won't," Tam said, to the river. "Hanging him or not hanging him won't change whether this city burns. It's all burning, Frey. I just found a boat."

"The *Coriander*."

He turned then. "You know about the boat."

"I know about everything, Tam. I found the ledger. I found the pamphlet plates. I know Father sold sedition to half the radicals in Vellmark and bribes from the other half. I have known since before the bridge closed, and I have been carrying it alone while you—" My voice cracked, which humiliated me, so I let it crack properly and made it sound like fury instead. "While you were doing sums."

"It's not sums—"

"It is *exactly* sums! You're the one who taught me that everything in this family gets counted. Who does the errand, who takes the risk, who eats last. Well, here's my count, brother: one Henrik Voss, who set type for Mother's funeral notices so Father wouldn't break. One Maren, who is upstairs right now in a house full of soldiers' boots because of you. Four names I don't even want to know. What did they pay per head? Or do they charge by weight, like the fishmongers?"

He stood up off the bollard and for a moment I thought he'd hit me, and for a stranger moment I wanted him to, because a blow is at least an honest entry in a book.

"You want to talk about ledgers?" he said, low and shaking. "Fine. Let's open ours. Who got the schooling? Freya. Who got Father's secrets? Freya. Who does he look at across the shop like she's the only child he ever actually *made*? Every day of my life I've been the errand boy in a family of authors, and now the whole city's sinking and there's one seat on the last boat, and for once — *for once* — the arithmetic comes out in my favor, and suddenly I'm the villain?"

"Yes," I said. "When the arithmetic costs other people's blood? Yes, Tam. That is precisely when a person becomes the villain."

The wind came off the river and cut between us, and neither of us moved. Downstream the bridge stood dark and barred, lanterns on its towers like the eyes of something that had been fed too well for too long.

"They took Father's ledger," he said quietly. "Pell told me. The magistrate has it."

The cold went through me then, real cold, colder than the river air. "That's impossible. Father swore—"

"Father's been swearing things all winter." Tam pulled his collar up. "Maybe he traded it. Maybe he traded it for a second berth, did you ever think of that? Did you ever think that maybe I'm not the only one in this family making deals?"

I hadn't. God help me, I hadn't. I had built a whole architecture of blame with Tam alone at the center of it, and the possibility that Father might be buying our safety with other men's necks — that he might have already done it, quietly, in some office smelling of sealing wax — that possibility fell through me like a stone through thin ice.

"When does the *Coriander* sail?" I heard myself ask.

"When the ice breaks. Two berths now, supposedly. Though berths have a way of becoming one." He looked at me, and beneath all the flint in his face there was my brother, aged eleven, holding out half of a stolen apple. "Come with me, Frey. Whatever you're planning — and I know you're planning something, you get Father's face when you're planning — whatever it is, don't. Get on the boat. Let the city eat itself."

"And leave Henrik's wife to the soldiers. Leave the ledger to the magistrate."

"The ledger's already gone."

"Paper can be reprinted," I said. "That's rather the whole point of my family."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. Then he walked away down the quay toward the lights of the harbor, and I stood alone on the ice-rimed stones with the river muttering under its shell of frost, and I understood that whatever came next would happen fast, and that I would have to do it without either of the two men I had spent my life trusting — one because he'd sold himself, and one because I no longer knew what he'd sold.

Behind me, in the printing quarter, a church bell began ringing at the wrong hour.
