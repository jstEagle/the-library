## 9. The Last Impression

There is a moment in every print run—I have known this since I was twelve years old—when the press is inked and the forme is locked and the paper waits, blank as a held breath, and you understand that whatever happens next cannot be unmade. Ink does not apologize. Paper does not forget. My father taught me that before he taught me to read, or perhaps in the same breath, and I thought of him now, standing at his own press in the dark of our ransacked shop, doing the thing he had spent twenty years teaching me never to do.

I was printing the ledger.

The case sat open on the stone floor beside me, the one I had cast myself in the terrible weeks after the riot, each slug of type bearing a name, a sum, a date. Magistrate Corven's name. The customs men. The garrison paymaster. Every bribe my father had recorded in his neat, secretive hand, every debt owed by every powerful man in Vellmark, set in metal by the daughter who had once believed her father merely cut beautiful letterforms for a living.

The press made its sound—the great wooden exhale I had fallen asleep to all my life—and the first sheet came away printed. I hung it to dry on the line where we usually strung pamphlets on the price of flour.

*THE LEDGER OF VELLMARK,* read the headline, in a face my father had cut himself, years ago, and sold to no one. *Being a True Account of Debts Owed and Debts Paid in the City of Vellmark, Set Down by One Who Kept the Books.*

"One who kept the books," I said aloud to the empty shop, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, like something broken rattling in a drawer.

* * *

It took four hours to print three hundred copies. I worked by two candles and knew exactly what would happen to me if I were found: the same thing that would happen to Father if he were found with the original ledger, except that I would not even have the dignity of a secret. I would be caught red-handed, or rather red-fingered, because ink gets into everything, into the creases of your knuckles and under your nails and into the whorls of your fingerprints, so that a printer can never truly deny her work. We are the only criminals who sign their crimes in a thousand copies.

I did not let myself think about Father. That was the discipline of it, the craft of it. You do not think about the reader when you are setting type; you think about the line length, the kerning, the pressure of the platen. Thinking about the reader is how you make mistakes. So I thought about line lengths while my father sat somewhere in the magistrate's house answering questions I did not want to imagine, and I thought about ink coverage while he either named names or refused to, and I pulled the lever of the press eleven hundred times—one pull per sheet, plus the misprints—and I did not think about my father at all, except constantly.

Tam had not come home.

That was the other thing I was not thinking about.

* * *

Dawn came up gray over the printing quarter, and I went out with three hundred broadsheets folded into a fishmonger's crate I had bought from a woman whose eyes asked no questions because I overpaid her.

Here is what nobody tells you about sedition: it is mostly carrying. Heavy, awkward, paper-scented carrying, through streets slick with frozen slop, past soldiers who were not yet looking for a girl with a crate because they did not yet know they should be. I left broadsheets on the steps of the Fishermen's Chapel. I left them weighted under cobblestones on Corn Row. I tucked them into the doors of bakeries before the ovens were lit, slid them under the workshop doors of the ropewalk, stacked them by the public pump where the water women gathered. In the market square I climbed the fountain—it was frozen; I nearly killed myself, and history would have been much shorter—and left a neat pile on the lip of the basin, squared corners, the way Father always insisted a finished job be delivered.

By eight o'clock the city was reading itself.

I stood at the corner of Cordwainer Street and watched a dockhand read my work, his lips moving, his face going through expressions like weather. Beside him an old woman read over his shoulder, and her hand rose slowly to her mouth. A clerk walked by, saw the headline, stopped, picked up a copy with the furtive speed of a man stealing bread, and folded it into his coat—and then unfolded it right there on the street because he could not wait, because his name might be in it, or his master's name, or the name of the man who owned his debt.

They say the truth will out. What they do not say is how fast, once it outs. By noon there were broadsheets nailed to the garrison gate. By one o'clock someone had burned an effigy of Magistrate Corven on the quay—not a good effigy, a sack with a chalked face, but enthusiasm counts for more than craftsmanship in revolutions. By two o'clock the shops were shutting, the bells were ringing wrong—wrong pattern, the old pattern, the pre-Corven pattern, which some body of men must have remembered and agreed upon without ever saying so aloud—and the city of Vellmark tipped over into the thing it had been promising to become all winter.

I stood in the crowd on the quay and felt nothing like triumph. I felt the way I feel pulling a proof: the tense, sick attention of a craftsman watching to see whether the thing she made is true, whether the impression took, whether all those hours of invisible labor amount to anything legible in the world.

The impression had taken. God help us all, it had taken.

* * *

The reprisal came at dusk, as reprisals do, preferring bad light and short odds.

I heard the garrison boots on our street from two blocks away, and I ran anyway, though some cold arithmetic in me already knew. Our door stood open. Our shop—my shop now, I supposed, in the way grief assigns property—was stripped again, worse than the rioters had managed, the press gouged with a bayonet as if soldiers too understood that a press is a weapon and this was how you spiked one.

Father's chair was overturned. His coat was gone from its peg.

Mrs. Helt, our neighbor, stood in her doorway wringing her apron, and told me in the flat voice people use for terrible news: they had come for Aldous Dunmore at four o'clock, quietly, no mob, just six soldiers and a sergeant who apologized. Apologized. He had been taken to the magistrate's house, and then, when the magistrate's house began to be besieged by citizens holding broadsheets, moved to the garrison, and Mrs. Helt did not know more, except that Aldous had gone out the door with his chin up and his hands clasped behind his back like a man going to lecture an unruly class.

"He said to tell you—" Mrs. Helt frowned, retrieving it. "He said, *tell Freya the impression is good.*"

He knew. Of course he knew. He had seen the broadsheet in the streets before they came for him—he must have—and known instantly whose work it was, because who else in Vellmark could have set that secret typeface? He had looked at his daughter's sedition and called it good.

I did not cry. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger accounts such things: I stood in my ruined shop and I did not cry, because crying was a luxury priced beyond my means, and because I still had one errand, and the errand had a tide.

* * *

The smugglers' boat was to leave on the midnight ebb from the coal stairs below the customs house, where the ice was broken and the water ran black. Tam had told me about the berth weeks ago, in his careful, sideways way, and I had pretended not to understand that he meant to keep it for himself. Some things you pretend not to understand because understanding would require acting, and you are not ready to act. I am done with not being ready.

The quay was madness and I moved through it like a minnow. Fires on the customs house steps. The magistrate's effigy burning again, better-made this time. Soldiers everywhere and no orders, because the orders came from the magistrate and the magistrate's word was worth exactly what my broadsheet said it was worth: thirty pounds, paid quarterly, to the garrison paymaster, receipt entered and witnessed.

And there, at the coal stairs, holding a lantern and a folded paper, stood my brother.

He looked terrible. He looked wonderful. He looked like Tam, which has always meant both things at once. He was thinner, and there was a cut healing over his eyebrow, and when he saw me his face did the thing faces do when a man has rehearsed a speech and then discovers the audience.

"Freya."

"Where is Father?" As if I didn't know. As if I hadn't carried the question down the whole dark quay like a second lantern.

"They've taken him." He said it quickly, wanting it behind us. "Listen—the boat comes at the ebb. There's one berth. I have the papers." He held them up, and his hand shook, and I watched him notice me noticing. "One berth, Freya. One. It was always going to be—someone had to—"

"Someone," I said. "Yes. Someone. And it was always going to be you, wasn't it, Tam? You counted it out weeks ago. I could see you counting at supper."

"I got the papers, didn't I?" And there it was, the old corrosive brightness, ambition wearing loyalty's coat. "You think these come free? You think the berth holds itself? I paid—"

"I know what you paid." I stepped closer. On the water behind him the boat was already coming in, oars muffled, a shadow among shadows. "Wick the Younger is in the garrison because of what you paid. They took Father the same day you finished your bargaining. Did you sell him too, Tam? Or was he part of the package?"

His face closed like a door. "I protected what I could."

"You protected yourself. It's all right." I said it gently, and gentleness, my mother used to say, is the sharpest thing in a house. "I'm not here to take the berth from you. I only came to see you off."

That surprised him genuinely. Poor Tam, who had rehearsed for a fight and gotten forgiveness instead, which is far harder to carry.

But I had not come only to see him off. I had come because of what I had learned at my press in the small hours, the lesson ink had been trying to teach me all night: that everything costs, and you must choose what you can afford to lose. I had published the truth and lost my father. Tam had purchased a berth and lost everything else. Between the two of us we had proven the family theorem, the one Father never wrote down: love, like everything else in Vellmark, goes in the ledger, and every entry is a debt.

"Go," I said, and turned toward the crowd, toward the fires, toward the garrison where my father sat in a cell waiting to learn the price of names.

"Freya." My brother's voice behind me, strange and cracked. "The papers—they're for—wait—"

But that is the tide, and the ebb waits for no reconciliation, and I did not look back.
