## 10. Spring Water

The morning the bridge broke, I woke with ink on my cheek and a slug of lead in my pocket, and I thought: *this is how it ends — not with trumpets, but with smudges.*

I had not slept so much as drifted near sleep, the way a boat drifts near a dock without ever quite touching it. All night the city had roared beyond the shutters — the magistrate's men burning their own records in the customs house to keep them from the mob, which told you everything about what those records were worth. The light through the curtain slats was that particular gray of a Vellmark spring, the color of dishwater and forgiveness.

The type-slug sat on the table where I'd left it, catching what little light there was. One letter. One character cut by my own hand, from my own matrix, in a face no other printer in the world owned. Father had taught me that a typeface was a voice made portable. He had not mentioned that a voice, once portable, could be carried into exile like a knife hidden in a hem.

I put it back in my pocket. It weighed less than a coin and more than a cathedral, which is the whole trouble with lead.

* * *

Getting to the quay required crossing a city that had come undone at its seams, and I will not pretend I did it bravely. I flinched at every shout. I took alleys I'd been warned about since childhood. Twice I pressed myself into doorways while crowds went past — not soldiers now, not even really rioters anymore, just people, hundreds of people, walking with the strange unhurried purpose of folk who have decided all at once that they are no longer afraid, which is the most frightening weather a city can produce.

The smoke over the printing quarter was ours. Ours meaning mine, meaning the shop, meaning the press Father had bought before I was born and oiled every Sunday like a sullen parishioner. Someone had put a torch to it in the reprisals, or possibly in celebration — by then the two fires looked identical. I did not cry. I noted, with the detached interest of a compositor checking another woman's proofs, that I did not cry, and that some part of me had already grieved the shop somewhere around chapter three of this disaster and had nothing left in the account.

What I carried instead was the broadsheet. Forty copies of it, folded against my ribs under my coat, because a printer's instinct dies hard: if you have set truth in metal once, you want it in paper, many times, everywhere. The rest of the run had gone out over the city in the night — into taverns, under doors, into the hands of anyone who could read and half of those who couldn't. By dawn the magistrate's name was being chanted in the fish market with a rope appended as punctuation. By noon he had fled his own courthouse dressed, rumor insisted, as a woman, which the crowd found far more damning than the bribes.

The ledger itself I had left behind in the type case, sealed in its coffin of fresh-cast sorts, and the fire could do with it what it liked. Paper accuses; paper also convicts, and hangs, and I had seen enough hanging arithmetic for one winter. Let the metal keep the secret. Metal melts quietly.

* * *

The quay was a fistfight with a shoreline.

Every boat that floated was overloaded, and several that didn't float were being argued over anyway. Smugglers who had spent the winter cursing the closed bridge now charged prices that would have embarrassed a ransom note, and got them. I looked for the *Grey Heron* — Tam's boat, our boat, the one berth, the arithmetic we had never once said aloud to each other and both known by heart — and found her at the far pilings, taking on the last of her passengers, her captain glaring at the sky as though the tide were a personal insult.

And there was Tam.

He stood at the gangplank with the berth papers in his hand, and I saw him before he saw me, which granted me a luxury I had not had since autumn: the chance to look at my brother honestly, without either of us performing. He was thinner. His coat was wrong — too fine at the collar, too worn at the cuffs, a garment assembled from two lives. There was a cut over his eyebrow, scabbed and forgotten. He held the papers the way Father used to hold a galleysheet, flat and careful, as if the words might rearrange themselves out of spite.

This was the boy who had sold names. I had said things to him on the frozen quay in February that I would take back if my pride allowed, and my pride has never allowed anything in its life. He had put Soren Vetch in a cell — good, gentle, dull Soren, who mended rollers and sang hymns off-key — and Soren was not yet free, and might never be, and Tam had done it one small transaction at a time, each one reasonable, each one survivable, until the sum of them was not.

And yet.

I want to tell this part plainly, because everything else in this story I can tell with a wry twist or an ironic aside, the armor of my trade, and this part deserves no armor.

Tam turned. He saw me. And his face did something I had never seen it do: it stopped calculating. All the shrewdness, all the dark excitement, all the ledger-keeping behind his eyes — it simply quit, like a press when the power fails, and what was left underneath was a fifteen-year-old boy on a cold dock who had sold his soul in installments and discovered the payments didn't buy what they promised.

"Frey." His voice cracked on it. "You're alive."

"Barely. It's a long story. I printed most of it."

He almost laughed. Then he looked at the papers in his hand, and then at the boat, and then at me, and I watched him do the sum. One berth. Two Dunmores. The equation that had hung over our family all winter, that we had circled like cats around a saucer, never lapping while the other watched.

"Room for one," he said.

"I know."

"Father—" He stopped. We had both heard by then. Everyone had heard; grief travels faster than news in a small city. Taken in the reprisal, two nights past, when they couldn't find the printer of the broadsheet and settled for her father. They had taken him from the ruins of the shop with the composure of a man who had perhaps been expecting the knock for years. I had not been permitted to see him. I had been busy being a fugitive, which is a full-time occupation with poor wages.

"He'd want you to go," Tam said.

"Father wanted many things. Mostly he wanted not to be asked to choose, which is why he hid ledgers in type cases and let his children do the choosing for him." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to. "You go, Tam. You have the papers."

"No."

It came out fast, before he could dress it up, and then he stood there looking surprised at himself, a man who had jumped off his own shadow. He pushed the berth papers into my hands. I tried to push them back. He was stronger than me — always had been, all elbows and appetite — and he simply held my wrists until I stopped, gently, the way you restrain someone you love from touching a hot stove.

"I did the sums," he said. "All winter I did the sums, and I always came out ahead. That's how I knew they were wrong. A sum that only ever favors you isn't arithmetic, Frey. It's prayer." He swallowed. "Soren Vetch is in a cell because of me. You're on that boat because of me, and this time the trade goes the right direction, and I'll — I'll carry that instead. It's heavy, but it's mine. I'm owed nothing and I know it now, which is more than I knew in February."

"Tam—"

"The crowd will cover me. Crowds always covered me; it's the one thing I'm good at." He smiled, and it was Father's smile, the rueful one, the one that cost extra. "Go print something. Somewhere they need it done. Go be invisible loudly."

He kissed the top of my head — he had grown taller than me sometime during the winter without asking permission — turned me by the shoulders toward the gangplank, and gave me a shove that was half brotherly and half tidal.

I looked back twice. The first time he was walking into the crowd with his wrong coat and his scabbed eyebrow, hands in his pockets, and the crowd took him the way water takes a dropped coin. The second time there was only the crowd.

* * *

The *Grey Heron* cast off within the hour, riding low and listing, carrying more fear than cargo. The captain muttered that he'd never seen the channel so empty of patrol boats, which should have comforted us and instead made everyone aboard glance at the horizon as if expecting the sea itself to produce soldiers.

We passed the broken bridge mid-afternoon.

The ice had claimed it in the night — or the mob, depending on whom you believed, and by then nobody believed anybody, which is what victory looks like up close. Great pale slabs of it ground against the pilings. The middle span had folded into the current like a page creased backward, and the river ran through the gap brown and swollen and indifferent, the first honest thing Vellmark had produced all year.

Spring water. That's what Father called the thaw, every year of my childhood, standing in the shop doorway with his sleeves rolled. *Spring water moves the millwheel, Frey-girl.* He meant it about the river then. He had no idea he meant it about everything.

I stood at the stern rail until the bridge was small, then smaller, then a gray line, then gone, and the city with it — the smoke, the bells, the chanting, my whole life reduced to a place-name other people would ask about. The passengers around me wept or slept or counted their purses. I did none of these. I took out the type-slug and held it in my open palm and let the river wind have its way with my hair.

One letter. I tilted it to the light.

An asterisk. Of course it was. Of all the characters in the case, my hands had chosen, without my noticing, the mark printers use when the story runs long and must be continued elsewhere. Father would have called it fate. Tam would have called it a coincidence worth exploiting. I call it what it is: the only joke the winter told, and I laughed at it alone at the stern rail with salt on my face that I will maintain, to my dying day, was spray.

Behind me: a drowned quarter and a freed city and a brother dissolved into a crowd and a father waiting under the ground for the truth he paid for. Ahead of me: a coast I had never seen, a language I half spoke, and forty broadsheets folded against my ribs, because a printer's instinct dies hard.

The wheel turned. Spring water moved the millwheel.

In my pocket, the asterisk kept its silence, patient as lead, waiting — as everything I loved had waited — to be set in order, and printed, and sent out into the world.
