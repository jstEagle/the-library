## 2. Requisition

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Nothing of consequence ever happened in Vellum's Reach on a Tuesday. Market day was Thursday; hangings, when the crown bothered to send a magistrate, were reliably scheduled for the first Saturday of the month, as if grief too could be made punctual. But there they were at half past eight in the morning, forty men in slate-grey coats drilling in the square beneath my window while I was still in my nightshirt, ink-stained to the elbow because I had fallen asleep over the final coastline and dreamed, apparently, in indigo.

I had finished it. That was the thought I clung to while the drums went through me like a second pulse. Somewhere around the fourth watch of the previous night, with the lamp guttering and my hand cramping into a claw, I had laid down the last stroke of the eastern shore — a coastline three years in the making, every estuary and shingle bank rendered in Master Ellery's hand or as near to it as mine could come. Three years of copying a dead man's eye until my own eye began to see the way his had: the land not as it was but as it ought to be remembered.

And now the army had arrived to be remembered at us.

From the workshop window I watched them form their lines. They were not a fighting company — no one fights in Vellum's Reach, we are a river town whose principal exports are eels, paper, and mild theological dispute — but a survey detachment, which is somehow worse. Fighting men want your blood. Survey men want your *papers*. Blood heals, more or less. Papers do not.

Below, in the shop proper, old Hesper was already turning the sign to CLOSED, though she must have known that a piece of painted board would stop the crown about as effectively as a curt word stops weather. Through the floorboards I heard her sweeping, brisk and defiant, the way she swept when Father Aldwyn said something heretical in his sermon. Sweep the dust about. That was Hesper's whole philosophy of resistance.

I should explain what I kept upstairs, since I did not fully understand it myself yet.

In the locked workshop above the map shop of Crane & Ellery — the ampersand now doing lonely service for a man eleven months dead — stood Master Ellery's great atlas, open on its stand, finished at last after my three years of careful servitude. And beside it, in a cedar case he had built himself and forbidden me under pain of his considerable displeasure to open, lay the thing he called only "the margins." Six slim boxes of papers, keyed shut, each one fitted into the atlas's spine-side shelf as though the book had been designed around them. Which, I came to understand much later, it had.

He had told me once, near the end, with the terrible clarity of a man tidying up: *"You don't open those, Xanthe. You keep them dry, you keep them dark, and if anyone asks, they're drafting templates."* Then he had died, in the manner of masters everywhere — inconveniently, mid-sentence, leaving the interesting parts undone.

So I had spent three years being dutiful about a mystery. I knew how to rule a line to a sixteenth of an inch. I knew nothing whatsoever about what was worth ruling a line for.

* * *

By nine the drums had stopped and a herald had nailed a proclamation to the market cross with four nails, one for each corner of the sheet, as if the paper might otherwise escape. Hesper read it aloud to the gathering crowd in her shopfront voice, the one she used for announcing deliveries:

"BY ORDER OF THE LORD PROTECTOR'S COMMISSION FOR THE SURVEY OF THE NORTHERN MARCHES: all maps, plats, ledgers, surveys, deeds, correspondence, and writings of whatever kind within the town of Vellum's Reach and its liberties are hereby declared property of the Crown. Surrender is required within three days. Retention of such materials constitutes concealment of Crown property and will be prosecuted."

A silence followed, of the particular Vellum's Reach variety — the silence of forty people simultaneously calculating whether their grandmother's recipes counted as "writings of whatever kind."

Then a voice from the back — Tammas Ockham, the cooper, who has never once in his life let a silence go unbroken: "Does this include ballads?"

"It includes," said a new voice, "whatever the Commission determines it includes."

Captain Aldous Vane stepped out from between two of his men, and I confess my first thought was uncharitable: *they sent someone handsome.* It seemed like cheating. He was perhaps thirty-five, tall without stooping to announce it, in a grey coat better cut than his rank strictly required, with the sort of level, patient gaze that made you feel he had already read you and found you mostly adequate, which was worse than finding you wanting. He held the proclamation's twin copy loosely in one gloved hand, the way other men hold nothing at all.

"The town clerk will attend me," he said. "Everyone else is free to go about their business. This is not an occupation, good people. It is an inventory."

He said the word *inventory* the way a priest says *absolution*, and the crowd dispersed with exactly the wrong speed — too quick, everyone suddenly fascinated by errands they'd forgotten for years.

I stayed at my window, nightshirt and all, and watched Clerk Petchy scurry across the square with his ledger hugged to his chest like a grandchild. Petchy, who had sold me my last three quires of paper and shorted me on every one. Petchy, who knew every hearth, every birth, every boundary stone in the town and its liberties, because writing it down was his entire soul. He handed that soul over in the square, opening the book across his forearms for the captain's inspection, and I saw Vane turn pages with one finger, unhurried, reading our whole town the way I read a chart — for what the symbols concealed.

His finger stopped on some entry. He looked up. Not around — *up*, at the row of buildings facing the square, and his gaze traveled along them with the deliberation of a man checking items against a list, and stopped where mine was stopped.

At the map shop.

Even at forty yards I would have sworn I saw the small satisfaction of a tick mark being placed.

* * *

They gave us until noon to surrender the shop's holdings, which I took as either courtesy or contempt. By half past nine I was dressed and downstairs, and Hesper and I were engaged in the world's least convincing performance of business as usual.

"They'll take the plates," Hesper said, sweeping the same clean stretch of floor for the fourth time. "Copper ones especially. And the ledgers, accounts and all, which'll please nobody since your master kept the accounts in cipher anyway—"

"He did?" I said, with the bright innocence of a man who absolutely knew.

"Don't play the fool, boy, it doesn't suit you the way it suits most." She leaned on the broom. "What else is up there?"

"Drafting templates."

She looked at me for a long moment. Hesper had swept this shop for thirty years, through two floods and one change of dynasty, and there was nothing in Vellum's Reach she hadn't already seen twice.

"Templates," she repeated. "Well. If I were a young man with templates upstairs, and the crown coming at noon, I'd want those templates somewhere a soldier wouldn't think to look. Soldiers look in drawers, in floors, in false book backs — they've a whole catalogue of clever places, and their officers have cleverer." She resumed sweeping. "Which is why I never hide anything anywhere clever. I put things where they're dull."

And she nodded toward the corner behind the counter, where the wall maps lived.

Now. Every map shop in the world has wall maps — the big printed sheets of county and coast, three feet by four, sold to merchants and magistrates and anyone who wants the world explained at a glance. Ours hung in a rack, rolled, maybe forty of them, and among the newest was a batch of the Northern Marches sheets printed from Master Ellery's own plates. They were, in the commercial sense, worthless: out of date, misregistered at the edges, rejected by the printer. We kept them because throwing away paper in a paper town invites bad luck and worse gossip.

I looked at that rack for a long time.

Here is what I did, and I set it down plainly because everything afterward grew from it. I went upstairs. I took the cedar case from the atlas's shelf, and for one moment — one only — I stood with it in my hands and nearly opened it, right then, because it occurred to me that I might soon not have the chance, and it seemed cowardly to guard a secret I didn't have the courage to know.

But there was no time to learn a secret. There was only time to keep one. So instead I wrapped the case in oilcloth, and oiled cloth does two things well: it keeps water out and attention off. I chose the oldest, dullest wall map in the rack — a survey of the Marches so outdated it still showed the old ferry crossing that had been silted up since before I was born — and I slit its backing seam with a bone folder, laid the case flat against the paper's heart, pasted the seam closed with the flour glue we used for backing linen, and rolled the whole thing tight. Then I buried it in the middle of the rack, third roll from the bottom, between a chart of herring shoals and a parish tithing map so boring I have never once managed to finish looking at it.

Dull places. God keep Hesper, and her broom.

By eleven the workshop looked exactly like what it was: a dead craftsman's unfinished business. The atlas lay open on its stand — that I could not hide, and did not try, and would not have managed, since it weighed as much as a sleeping dog. Everything else was honest. Copper plates in their press drawer. Account books in cipher. Three years of my own working sketches, each one initialed, each one a small confession that I had been taught by the best.

* * *

Captain Vane arrived at noon precisely, with two soldiers and Clerk Petchy trailing behind like a remora. He removed his gloves in the doorway, which I later learned meant he intended to touch things.

"Young man," he said, glancing at a paper in Petchy's hands, "you'd be the apprentice. Xanthe Crane."

"Journeyman, sir. Since Master Ellery's death."

"Journeyman Crane." A small correction, filed away. "My condolences on your loss. Delayed, I know, but I find condolences improve with age, like vinegar." He smiled to show this was wit, and I smiled to show I had been educated, and somewhere behind my sternum fear sat down and got comfortable.

He moved through the shop the way water moves — into corners, along shelves, pooling briefly at anything dense. He drew out the account books, paged them, paused at the cipher. "Your master's invention?"

"A merchant's shorthand, sir. For prices."

"Mm." He closed the book and did not put it back where he found it, which I noted, and have continued noting in a general way ever since. In the workshop he stood before the atlas a long while without speaking, and I watched his face do something complicated and quickly put away — the look of a man who knows the value of things and has found one priced beyond his salary.

"This is the Ellery atlas," he said. "The northern commission. Unfinished."

"Finished now, sir. I completed it. Last night, as it happens." I heard my own voice say this and could have strangled it, because it sounded like pride, and pride sounds like an invitation.

But Vane only studied the final coastline, and then me, and then the coastline again, and something passed over his expression like cloud-shadow over a surveyed field. Whatever he had expected to find unfinished, he had expected to find it unfinished, and here was the shore complete down to the last shingle bar. His finger hovered over the eastern margin — right where the atlas's spine-shelf had held the cedar case for years, right where the empty slot now gaped like a missing tooth — and rested there a moment on the blank paper.

"Where are the drafting templates?" he asked mildly.

Petchy looked up. I felt the sweat start between my shoulders and had a wild, lucid moment of gratitude toward my own mouth, which said, with the flatness of long habit: "Master Ellery burned them, sir, near the end. He was fastidious about waste paper. Said a workshop full of scraps was a firetrap."

"Fastidious," Vane repeated. He turned the word over. Around him the room waited, and I understood with total clarity that he could have the racks searched, the floors lifted, my hands examined for glue, all before supper — and that he was not going to, and could not have told you why, any more than I could have told you why I lied except that some lies are load-bearing and you only find out which ones when the building shakes.

"For the moment," he said, pulling his gloves back on, "the atlas remains here. Sealed premises, Journeyman Crane — you may work, you may not remove materials. There will be a guard outside. Consider him a courtesy." At the door he paused. "Oh — and Clerk Petchy tells me you give lessons. Compass-work, lettering? To the children on Copper Lane."

The floor tilted very slightly. "Some of the neighbors' children, sir."

"How civic." He settled his collar in the dark glass of the window, adjusting a reflection. "The Commission values education. One learns so much about a community from what it teaches its children to measure."

Then he was gone, and his boots went down the stairs in perfect time, metronome-steady, and Hesper came up from below and stood beside me at the workbench, and neither of us said anything for a while. Down in the square the soldiers had resumed drilling, their cadence calling back through the floorboards like a slow heartbeat belonging to some larger animal that had lain down on top of the town.

"Well," said Hesper finally, and picked up her broom.

I went to the window. Third roll from the bottom of the rack, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping inside a map of a country that no longer quite existed — and I thought about what I had told Vane, that Master Ellery burned his scraps. It had come out of me smooth and instant, and it had been believed, or at least banked, and I realized I had just discovered something about myself that I could not undiscover: that under sufficient pressure, I was a man who could lie to power with steady hands.

My master would have said that wasn't a discovery at all. He'd have said I'd been apprenticed to it for three years.

That evening, after the guard outside had been relieved and re-posted, I climbed to the workshop one last time and stood before the atlas, finished and useless, masterpiece and hostage. And in the quiet, with the drill-yards silent and the river muttering to itself below, I heard again the proclamation's phrase: *writings of whatever kind.*

Whatever kind. As if paper could be seized without seizing what was written on hearts, without taking the names people call each other across courtyards, the names shouted at weddings, the names carved — I stopped. My hand was resting, without my having instructed it, on the empty spine-slot where the cedar case had slept for years.

Names. Why would a captain of the survey care about a box of names?

I did not sleep that night. I sat with the atlas and a lamp and I traced, over and over, the marginal annotations Master Ellery had made on his proofs — tiny marks, ticks and dashes, the kind any draftsman scatters like breath — and I began, very slowly, to count them, and to notice that they clustered where no village stood.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would find out what I had been keeping dry and dark all this time. Because Captain Vane wanted it, and powerful men do not send forty soldiers for templates — and because a promise is only a whisper until someone dangerous starts listening for it.
