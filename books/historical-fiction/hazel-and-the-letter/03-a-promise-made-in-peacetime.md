## 3. A Promise Made in Peacetime

My master had a way of asking for things that made refusal feel like bad manners rather than rebellion. He never said *do this*. He would say, *Xanthe, I wonder if you'd be a good fellow and*, and then name something so enormous that the "good fellow" part seemed like a clerical error. He did this the autumn before he died, when the light came slantwise through the workshop windows and turned everything gold except his hands, which had gone the color of old vellum.

He was dying even then, though neither of us said so out loud. We were very polite about it. In Vellum's Reach we could be polite about anything — floods, funerals, the slow theft of a border people's land — provided it happened to someone else.

"Xanthe," he said, not looking up from the margin he was gilding, "I wonder if you'd be a good fellow and remember everything I'm about to tell you, because I intend to tell it only once."

I was grinding lapis at the time. I remember the blue going around and around in the mortar, and how I thought, absurdly, that I ought to finish the batch first. Duty is a strange animal; it arrives whenever it likes but insists on being fed on schedule.

"Remembering is most of my job," I said.

"Not this." He set down the gilding brush. He had beautiful hands for a man who claimed to despise ornament — long, precise, ink-stained at every knuckle like a confession written in his own skin. "This is not craft. This is promise."

* * *

That was six years ago now, or five — grief makes poor arithmetic of the years — and I thought of it again this morning because the town crier stood in the square below my window and read out a list of names in a voice like a man reciting prices at market.

The declaration had come two days after Captain Vane's requisition order, as though the paperwork had merely been waiting for its excuse. *By order of the Crown, the persons and properties of the border settlements north of the Vell are henceforth declared hostile*, and so forth, and so forth, in the florid legal hand that governments use when they mean to do murder politely. The crier's second list was shorter. It named individuals. Enemies of the crown, to be apprehended wherever found.

Sefa Ellery's name was fourth from the top.

I heard it from the window of the room where I have slept for eleven years, in the shop where I apprenticed under her father, holding the mortar while her father's voice went quiet, and I confess my first thought was not noble. My first thought was a very small, very practical horror: *the ledger names her people, and now her people's names are evidence, and she is on a list, and I am sitting here with three hundred lives folded into map margins like love letters no one was meant to read.*

My second thought was the oath. Promises are like that. First you feel the danger, then you feel the debt, and they arrive together, like soldiers.

But I am getting ahead of myself. Let me go back to the blue powder and the dying man, because a promise should be understood in the weather it was made in.

* * *

"You know I have a daughter," Ellery said.

"I know you had a wife," I said carefully, "who died."

"A wife who died, yes, and a daughter who did not die, though some days I speak of her as if she had, which is the sort of cowardice men mistake for grief." He wiped his brush on his sleeve — a habit that used to make me wince — and looked at me directly for the first time in weeks. His eyes were still fierce. Illness took everything else first, like an invader burning the outlying farms before the city. "Her name is Sefa. She married against my wishes, into a family of the river folk, the ones the survey maps keep forgetting to draw. She chose them over me. She was right to."

I said nothing. There was nothing wise to say, and silence at least has the virtue of not being stupid.

"The atlas you are helping me finish — the great work, the masterpiece, all the words patrons use when they want a discount — is not what I told the Guild it is." He got up, slowly, one hand pressed to the table, and crossed to the back wall where the margin-cases sat in their row, sealed with wax and wire, each one fitted into the spine of the atlas's housing like organs in a body. He had forbidden me to touch them since my first week. I had built my curiosity a small room and locked it in, the way one does. "Every annotation in those margins, every little flourish I've had you copy without understanding — there is a cipher in them, boy. A ledger. Names."

"Names of whom?"

"The river folk. The border families. Every household, every birth, every marriage, every grave, going back ninety years. Written so that any clerk with a trained eye could read them and no clerk ever will, because none of them look at margins. Margins are where the decoration goes. Margins are beneath notice." He smiled, and it was terrible, because it was the smile of a man who had spent thirty years being underestimated and had finally found a use for it. "Land can be taken by drawing a line. But people — people are harder to unpeople, unless nobody wrote them down. That's the whole trick of conquest, Xanthe. You don't defeat your enemy. You make him illegible."

The lapis in the mortar had gone untouched. I remember the color of it exactly — that deep, patient blue — because I stared at it for most of what followed.

"So," he said, "if anything happens to me — and things do happen, boy, ask any dead man — the atlas must reach Sefa. Not the Guild. Not the town archive, not the magistrate, not your dear mother, God rest her suspicion. Sefa. Across the border. Because her people are in these pages, and these pages are the only proof on earth that they have been here longer than the Crown has had a treasury. Tenure, Xanthe. Proof of tenure. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I think you're telling me to commit treason," I said.

"I'm telling you to keep a promise to a mapmaker," he said. "Treason is just what the government calls it afterward."

* * *

Here is what I remember most sharply about that afternoon, sharper than his voice, sharper than the smell of the lapis: he made me swear with my hand flat on the sealed margin-case, and the wax was cool under my palm, and I felt — I am not a mystical man, I draft coastlines for a living — I felt that I was resting my hand on something alive. Something sleeping. Three hundred heartbeats folded into paper.

"And you'll tell me nothing of the cipher?" I asked.

"No."

"Then how am I to explain it to her?"

"She'll know," he said. "She helped me devise it when she was sixteen, before the quarrel. That's the joke of it, boy. The thing that estranged us is the same thing that will reconcile us. Families are like rivers. You can dam them, divert them, refuse to draw them on your maps, but they keep going somewhere."

He died fourteen months later, in early spring, with the atlas still short one coastline and his daughter still unreconciled, and I did what dutiful apprentices do: I kept my head down, finished what I could, touched nothing, and told myself that a promise deferred is not a promise broken. I became very skilled at that particular arithmetic. Three years of it. You can balance almost anything if you carry the remainder indefinitely.

* * *

Present day. The morning after the crier read the lists.

Tamsin arrived before the shop opened, letting herself in with the key I gave her last winter for fetching pigments, and stood in the doorway of the workshop with her cap crooked and her face doing something I had never seen it do, which was nothing at all. Tamsin's face always does something. It is her chief instrument.

"They posted copies at the grain exchange," she said. "With descriptions. Sefa Ellery, née of this parish, height medium, complexion dark, *may be identified by a scar on the left forearm*. They've written her like a stolen horse."

"I heard the crier."

"There's more." She came in properly and shut the door, and lowered her voice though there was no one to hear; the shop below had been empty since the requisition, the shelves stripped of everything Vane's clerks had considered promising, which was everything. "The proclamation says the river settlements are to be 'surveyed and regularized.' Surveyed, Xanthe. You know what follows a survey."

I knew. I had drawn the aftermath of surveys in other counties, hired and not asked questions. A survey finds the boundaries. Then the boundaries find the deeds wanting. Then the deeds find the families wanting, and the families find the road, and the land finds new owners whose names are printed in a fine bold hand. Erasure by instrument. My master had taught me the trick of it and I had learned it so well I could recognize it happening to other people from a comfortable distance.

"And she's across the border," I said. "Which is now enemy territory. Which means if she sets foot on this side—"

"—she isn't setting foot anywhere, probably. The crossings are watched. Brannoc says the ferry hasn't run north in a week." She said it lightly, but I caught the word *Brannoc* and filed it, because Tamsin does not mention the ferryman casually. Tamsin mentions nothing casually.

"Tamsin," I said, "why do you know whether the ferry has run north?"

"Because the dye-house buys madder root from the far bank twice a season, and because I listen, and because—" she stopped herself, which she also never does. Then she looked at the margin-cases along the back wall, sealed in their wax and wire, and her face finally did something. It went very still and very young. "You never told me what's in them."

"No."

"But you know."

"Yes."

She waited. I will say this for Tamsin: she can wait like furniture. I had watched her outlast a Guild examiner, a customs officer, and one memorable wholesaler from Marsh Hollow, and she outlasted me in about forty seconds, which is less patience than recognition. She already knew. She wanted to hear me say it, perhaps because saying it would make it hers too, and she deserved to choose her burdens rather than have them assigned.

"My master hid a ledger in the margins," I said. "Names of the border families. Births, marriages, graves. Ninety years of proof that they belong to the land the Crown has just decided they don't. If Vane reads it, it becomes a roll of targets. If Sefa gets it, it becomes a defense. And Sefa is now on an arrest list, and I am standing in a requisitioned workshop holding the only copy of both the crime and the pardon, and I swore—"

My voice did something inconvenient. I stopped, breathed like a draftsman — in through the nose, steady the hand — and started again.

"I swore to a dying man that it would reach her. Six years ago, in peacetime, when treason was a hypothetical and she was just an estranged daughter and not item four on a bill of particulars. He made it sound simple. He was dying; dying men get to make things sound simple. It's their last prerogative."

"And now?" Tamsin asked.

"Now the simple thing is the dangerous thing." I looked at my hands. They are good hands; they can letter a legend fine enough to fool a jeweler. They were shaking anyway. "It turns out a promise doesn't care what the weather's like when it comes due. It's not a fair-weather instrument. It's more like—"

"Like a map," Tamsin said, with the merciless literalness of someone who has spent years grinding pigment for mapmakers. "It shows you where you are. Not where you'd prefer to be."

I laughed, which surprised us both, and the laugh broke whatever had been holding in my chest since the crier's list — not the fear, the fear stayed, fear is load-bearing — but something beside the fear, some smaller, meaner thing that had been whispering for three years that a promise to a dead man was a debt no court would enforce.

"He said families are like rivers," I said. "You can leave them off the map, but they keep going somewhere."

"Your master," said Tamsin, "sounds like he enjoyed saying portentous things to apprentices."

"He practiced on daughters first, apparently. Come here. Help me move the wall map."

She frowned. "Why?"

"Because yesterday, when Vane's sergeants inventoried the workshop, they rolled up every chart on the racks and never once looked at the large wall map of the northern counties, because it's mounted, and mounted maps are furniture. Furniture is beneath notice." I was already working the mounting loose at the corners, my heart going like a drum in a march. "Margins, Tamsin. It's always margins."

We drew the great sheet down flat across the worktable, face down, and I slit the backing linen with a bone folder, and Tamsin held the lamp, and between the linen and the paper — in the largest margin in the workshop — I laid the first of the margin-cases, wrapped in oiled cloth, flat as a pressed flower.

She watched me do it and did not ask why I wasn't simply running south with the cases tonight, though I saw the question cross her face like weather. Good. Let the question live there. I had asked it myself at four in the morning and answered it the only way that survived daylight: because a courier can be stopped, but a requisitioned workshop with an unfinished atlas must eventually be inspected by a captain who wants his masterpiece completed — and while he waits for his prize, the prize's teeth stay hidden in his own walls.

Besides. There was the matter of the oath, and the matter of the woman on the list, and the growing suspicion — arriving cold, the way true things do — that my master had known all of this in advance. Had counted on it, even. Dying men get to make things sound simple. Living men get to find out they weren't.

Tamsin trimmed the linen edge square, smoothed it down, and stepped back. The wall map hung innocent again, all coastline and county lines, the northern reaches blank where the surveyors' knowledge ran out.

"Well," she said. "What happens now?"

Below us, out in the square, boots. Many boots, in step, coming up the street toward Copper Lane, where the river folk kept their houses and their children and their ninety unrecorded years.

"Now," I said, taking up my coat, "I go find out whether Old Brannoc still remembers my master's letters."
