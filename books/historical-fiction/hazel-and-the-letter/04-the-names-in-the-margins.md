## 4. The Names in the Margins

The key had been under the ink shelf for three years, and I had spent those three years pretending not to know it.

That is a particular kind of dishonesty, the kind you practice against yourself. Every morning I knelt at the low cupboard to fetch the lamp oil, and every morning my eyes passed over the loose board at the back where Ellery had tucked a small iron key on the last afternoon of his life, and every morning I looked away with the practiced ease of a man who has trained his gaze like a spaniel. *Not yet,* I would tell myself. *He said when the atlas was done.* And the atlas was never done, because finishing it meant opening what he had sealed, and opening it meant learning why a dying man had gripped my wrist hard enough to bruise.

But the atlas was finished now. The last coastline lay cured under its dust of pounce, the northern shore of the Merewater rendered in that fine trembling line Ellery had taught me — steady hand, breathing heart, he used to say, though he never explained which was which. There was nothing left to ink. Nothing left to grind, to stretch, to rule. The workshop was silent in the way of a room that has run out of excuses for you.

So on the fourth night after Vane's men tramped through the square below, I moved the ink shelf, prised up the board, and took the key.

It was warmer than I expected. Things left alone go cold; I have handled enough dead men's tools to know. But the key sat in my palm as if it had been waiting near a fire, and I choose to believe that is sentiment rather than the damp from the wall, because a man needs one or two beliefs that don't survive inspection.

* * *

The margin-cases were exactly where he'd left them: four long boxes of oiled cedar, each the length of my forearm, strapped shut with leather and wax, stacked beneath the false bottom of the plan chest like coffins for very thin people. He had forbidden me them absolutely. *You do not open these, Xanthe, not for curiosity, not for need, not if I die and they bury me badly and the town comes asking questions.* He had made me swear it with my hand on the atlas itself, which in retrospect should have told me the two were married. A promise sworn on a map is sworn on territory. Territory does not forgive.

I cut the first strap with the bone folder he'd given me my first week, and the wax crumbled like old bread, and I felt like a burglar in a house I had lived in since I was fourteen.

Inside: not papers. Maps. Or rather, the maps' shadows — thin sheets of proof copy, the working pulls Ellery had taken as the atlas grew, each one crowded in its margins with annotations in his small, slanted hand. Distances. Soundings. Boundary perambulations. The ordinary marginalia of a careful cartographer, except that the more I read, the less ordinary any of it was.

*H. of the Alder row, mason, holds by copyhold from the old grant, witness W. Ferrar.*

*Marta Ostrey and her sister's children, wintered at the eel-stakes, three years continuous.*

*The Torv family, freeholders before the Crown claimed otherwise — see the assize record, Michaelmas term, disputed and never settled.*

Names. Not places. Names, dozens of them on the first sheet alone, woven into the geography like threads through cloth — a well here, and beside the well the name of the woman who had kept it; a ford there, and beside the ford the family who had held grazing rights on both banks since before anyone alive could remember. Each annotation keyed with a tiny symbol, a circle or a triangle or a barred cross, and the symbols repeated, building some pattern I couldn't yet read.

I sat back on my stool and looked at the four cases and understood, in the way you understand a thing all at once after years of refusing to understand it slowly, that the atlas was never a book of maps.

It was a register wearing a map's clothes.

Ellery had spent twenty years charting the border country, and in every margin, in cipher dressed as survey notes, he had written down the border folk themselves — the families the crown called squatters, the holdings the new surveys called vacant, the tenancies that existed only in memory and in the testimony of dead witnesses. Proof. If dispossession was done with paper, then here was paper to undo it. And if the wrong reader got hold of it —

If the wrong reader got hold of it, it was not proof. It was a list.

I thought of Captain Vane's requisition order, the neat clerk's hand enumerating everything in my keeping as crown property, and my hands went cold around a sheet of proof copy forty years old. Somewhere below me soldiers drilled in the square. Their boots kept time like a slow pulse.

* * *

Tamsin found me at dawn, which is to say she let herself in with her own key, put a sack of rolls on the plan chest, and looked at what was spread across every flat surface in the room before saying anything at all.

"Well," she said. "You've been busy."

"Tamsin—"

"No." She held up an ink-stained finger. She had been my fellow apprentice back when we both chased the same wages and different dreams — hers involved a dye-house and eventually her own premises, mine involved this room — and she knew the workshop better than most priests know their churches. "Don't explain yet. Let me look."

She looked. Tamsin reads quickly and judges slowly, which is the reverse of everyone else in this town, and I watched her move along the tables, tracing margins with one fingertip the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. At the third table she stopped.

"These are the Ostreys," she said quietly. "Marta Ostrey. She sold my mother worsted, before. Before the declaration."

"Yes."

"And this—" she turned a sheet toward me, tapping a barred cross beside the name *Ferrar*. "This same mark is on the mill ledger. I've seen it. In the tally column, the old tally column, before the new steward burned them."

"The symbols repeat across documents," I said. My voice came out strange, half awe and half ash. "It's a cipher. The map annotations are the text; the marks are how you read them. Ellery built a whole language out of survey shorthand and hid a community inside it."

Tamsin set the sheet down very carefully, as if it might go off.

"Xanthe. Do you know what this is?"

"It's evidence."

"It's a death warrant with a ribbon on it." She turned, and her face had gone the color of the paper. "You said Vane requisitioned everything. Everything maps, everything ledgers. If he finds this—"

"He'd need the cipher."

"Would he? He has clerks. Clerks are clever people who get paid either way." She pulled a stool over and sat down hard, and for a moment we were just two tired tradespeople in a cold room full of other people's lives. Then she said, "How many names?"

"I don't know. Hundreds. Perhaps more. Four cases, maybe sixty sheets each, eight or ten names a sheet, and some annotated twice." I had counted in the small hours, compulsively, the way you count stairs in the dark. "Seven hundred. Give or take."

"Seven hundred people," Tamsin said, "who exist right now only because a dead man wrote them down in code."

Neither of us said the rest of it, so I will say it here, where no one can hear: seven hundred people whose existence was, as of Captain Vane's arrival, a crime to document. Enemy blood on enemy land. The declaration had turned their names from records into charges.

"We have to copy them," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. "Copy them where? On what? You've seen the requisition order — your paper is crown paper now, technically. Your ink is crown ink." A bitter little laugh. "We'd be forging treason in the king's own inventory."

"Then we'll use yours. Dye-house stock, dye-house patterns. Nobody audits calico patterns." I was already pulling clean sheets from the drawer, and my hands were steadier than they had any right to be. "Two copies. One stays hidden here, one leaves this town even if I have to carry it in my shirt. Ellery promised these names to someone, Tamsin. I intend to find out who, and deliver."

"You know who."

I did. I had known since the night Ellery died and said her name — *Sefa, tell Sefa* — and I had spent three years pretending that was a comfort rather than an instruction. But that is another page, and I wasn't ready to turn it.

"Start with the Ferrar sheet," I said instead. "Copy the annotations exactly, marks and all. Don't try to read ahead. Reading ahead is how mistakes happen."

"Reading ahead," Tamsin said, dipping her pen, "is how people decide whose side they're on."

* * *

We copied for six days.

I will not pretend those days were noble. Most of them were tedious, cramped, and lit badly, and my back developed opinions it shared freely. Copying cipher is worse than copying plain text because you cannot skim; every barred cross must be a barred cross, every abbreviation exact, or the whole thread unravels for whoever reads it after you. By the third day Tamsin and I had stopped talking entirely and communicated in grunts and the passing of blotting paper, like two oxen ploughing the same furrow.

And underneath the tedium, all six days, ran the other thing. The thing I keep coming back to.

There is a particular vertigo in holding a secret large enough to crush you. I had inherited debts before — Ellery left plenty, mostly financial, one romantic — but this was different. Every sheet I copied made the weight stranger, because it wasn't my secret. It belonged to seven hundred people who didn't know it existed, and to one dead man who had decided, without consulting me, that I could be trusted with their lives. Awe is too warm a word for what I felt. Terror is too cold. What I felt was closer to standing in a doorway holding something enormous and being unable to go forward or back, while somewhere down the street, boots kept time.

On the fifth day I found my own name.

It was in the margin of a chart of the river bends, in Ellery's latest hand, the one from his final year: *Apprentice X.C., b. Vellum's Reach, bound to the trade; trusts him with everything.* No cipher around it. Plain as daylight, as if he'd wanted whoever came after to find it without a key.

I sat with that for a while. *Trusts him with everything.* Easy to write, master. Harder to be.

"What is it?" Tamsin asked, because I suppose I'd stopped moving.

"Nothing. An error in the soundings."

"Liar," she said mildly, and kept copying.

* * *

On the sixth evening, with perhaps two-fifths of the cases transcribed, we heard boots on the stair — the heavy, unhurried kind that do not belong to apprentices or creditors. Tamsin blew the lamp before I'd finished standing. We stood together in the dark among the tables, listening, and I confess my heart conducted a brief and undignified percussion section.

The boots paused at the landing. A key scratched at the outer door — the army had its own keys now, of course they did — and then, mercifully, receded downward again. A patrol walking its rounds. Nothing more.

In the dark, Tamsin exhaled against my ear. "We can't keep doing this here."

"I know."

"Do you? Because knowing and doing have never been on speaking terms in this workshop." Her hand found my sleeve. "Xanthe. Two-fifths copied. Half a secret is worse than none — it's none, plus evidence of intent. We need the rest faster, or we need it gone."

"We'll finish," I said. "Three more days. Maybe four."

Below us in the square, faint through the floorboards, the patrol's boots changed step, and a voice called something about the watch rotation, and I heard — clearly, unmistakably — a sergeant answer, *"Captain Vane wants the workshop lists by Friday. All papers catalogued. He's a thorough one."*

Thorough. Yes. I had seen his eyes move across my shelves the day he came, reading spines like a scholar, pricing the room.

Tamsin's grip tightened on my sleeve. In the darkness neither of us needed lamplight to know what the other was thinking: we were racing a man whose job was patience, and we were copying by candle-end.

"Three days," Tamsin repeated, in a voice that meant she'd already decided to risk her own name alongside mine, and I was too grateful and too ashamed to say so out loud.

When she had gone I relit the lamp, sat down before the second case, and drew the next sheet toward me. At the top of the margin, in Ellery's hand, a single annotation I hadn't noticed before, keyed with the barred cross:

*S. to inherit the whole. Send north when safe.*

Send north. Across the border. To a daughter declared enemy of the crown, whose name I had good reason to believe was already written on a list very much shorter than seven hundred.

I picked up my pen. My hand, at last, was shaking.
