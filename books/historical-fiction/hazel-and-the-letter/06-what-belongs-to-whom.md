## 6. What Belongs to Whom

The army took the Harrow house on a Tuesday, which struck me as an oddly administrative day for theft.

I watched from the workshop window while a corporal with a clipboard — a *clipboard*, as though dispossession were an inventory problem — nailed the requisition notice to the Harrows' door. Maren Harrow stood in her garden still holding her trowel, dirt to the wrist, watching two soldiers carry her mother's linen chest down her own front steps and into a wagon. She did not scream. That was the part I couldn't shake later. She just stood there among her beans like a woman deciding whether the frost had come early, and when the wagon rolled she went back to her knees in the soil, because what else does a body know how to do.

Three days after Vane's visit, the river road had gone grey with notices. Every third doorpost sprouted the crown's paper: *By order of His Majesty's Commission for the Survey of Border Territories, the following holdings are assumed into custodianship pending determination of title.* Custodianship. It has a lovely legal ring to it. So does *pending*. The language of empire is always gentle right up until the wagon arrives.

I inked coastlines and pretended not to watch, which by then was my principal skill. I could draw a shore blind. I could also lie with my face, mostly, though Tamsin claimed my left eyebrow had opinions it couldn't govern.

* * *

They began coming to the shop on Thursday.

Not soldiers. Neighbors.

Old Wenna Fitch first, before the shutters were even fully open, standing in the doorway of the map shop below the workshop with her shawl clutched at her throat like a woman arriving to report a death. Which, in a sense, she was.

"Crane," she said. "You're the mapmaker now."

"I'm the apprentice," I said, because grief and cowardice sound remarkably alike when spoken aloud.

"Your master's dead three years. You're the mapmaker." She came in without invitation, the way old women do, and put both hands flat on the counter. "Wenna. The Fitch holding, river side, past the willow weir. Ellery surveyed it in — what year would it be —"

"Year of the Twin Floods."

"That's the one." Her voice dropped. "The commission posted notice yesterday. They say our title's *unclear*. Fifty years my family's paid rent on that land, and suddenly it's unclear." She looked at me with the terrible directness of the frightened elderly. "You have his field books. You have the originals. I need a copy of my own boundary lines. Certified. Sealed. Whatever makes it paper they can't wave away."

And there it was — the thing I'd been walking around all week the way you walk around a drunk asleep on stairs. My safety, such as it was, rested on being useful to Captain Aldous Vane for exactly as long as it took me to finish his atlas. Everyone else's safety apparently rested on being erased from it.

"Mistress Fitch," I said carefully, "the workshop is under guard."

"I saw the guard. He's nineteen and bored." She leaned closer. "Ellery drew my father's boundaries with his own hand. He stood in our kitchen drinking my mother's plum wine and told us no line he drew would ever be moved by a man who hadn't walked the ground. Well. Men are moving them now who've never walked anything but a parade square. Give me my lines, boy."

I want to tell you I refused her. I want to tell you I was prudent, that I thought of the margin-cases hidden inside the rolled wall map not four feet from where we stood, of the cipher half-decoded in my head, of Tamsin downstairs copying names onto onion-skin with hands steadier than mine. I refused her in spirit. In body, I heard myself say: "Come back at dusk. The west door. Bring nothing written."

She squeezed my wrist hard enough to leave marks, and left, and I sat down on Ellery's old stool and understood that I had just become something new. Not a mapmaker. A smuggler of the obvious. A man selling people back their own lives at the price of his neck.

* * *

By Saturday there were nine of them. By Sunday, fourteen.

They came at dusk through the west door in ones and twos, hat-brims low, and I copied boundary lines until my eyes ran. The Fitch holding. The Copper Lane gardens. The marsh-edge pasture that belonged to nobody and therefore, under the new arithmetic, to the crown. I worked from Ellery's field books, and here is what I learned about my master in his absence: he had annotated everything. Not just lines and bearings — he wrote down whose stone stood at which corner, who had mended the fence in which year, which families had grazed which common since before the town kept records. He had drawn the land *peopled*. I had spent three years finishing his coastlines and never once looked closely at his inland work, because inland work is boring, and I was young, and boring is the disguise secrets wear best.

Tamsin found me at midnight surrounded by copies, my right hand cramped into a claw.

"You look like a man printing money," she said.

"Worse. I'm printing *truth*." I flexed my fingers. "Do you know what the commission's clerks do? They compare old surveys against new ones, and wherever the old record is thin — where Ellery wrote a bearing but not a name, where tenure was customary rather than deeded — they declare it *unclear*. And unclear becomes crown land. And crown land becomes —" I stopped. I had seen the surveyors' stakes going in along the marsh road. I knew where crown land became something else. It became lots, and the lots had buyers, and I would bet my remaining good hand that one of the buyers had silver buttons and a captain's commission.

Tamsin pulled out a stool and sat, and for once didn't touch the papers. She'd been careful about that since the copying began — her discipline frightened me more than any soldier. "How many copies have you made?"

"Fifteen. Sixteen, if this one dries."

"How many families are on the arrest list?"

I didn't answer. We both knew the list was pinned to the garrison door every morning, and grew like ivy.

"That's the arithmetic, then," she said quietly. "You can copy boundary lines until your hand falls off, and it helps fifteen families keep what's already theirs on paper. Or—" she nodded toward the wall map where the margin-cases slept, "—that helps hundreds stay alive at all. You can't do both forever, Xanthe. Ink is ink, but it isn't infinite."

"It's a few nights' work."

"It's a few nights you're not decoding. And every night, another name goes up on the garrison door." She said it without heat, which is how she said the worst things. "I'm not telling you to stop helping them. I'm telling you to know what you're spending."

* * *

Here is the guilt I carried that week, and I'll set it down plainly because it deserves daylight: my safety had always been bought with other people's erasure.

I don't mean anything dramatic by that. I mean that for three years I lived above a secret I wasn't trusted with, in comfort, doing beautiful work, while Ellery's cipher sat sealed in its cases and the border folk — the people in those margins, the people whose names I couldn't yet read — lived unrecorded. Invisible is a kind of safety, if you're the one holding the pen. Their invisibility was my peace. Their unwritten-ness was my quiet mornings and my steady bread and my master's benign, maddening instruction: *"Ink nothing before its time, Xanthe. Paper is patient; men are not."*

Paper is patient. I'll grant him that. But I had begun to suspect that Ellery's patience — his beautiful, deliberate, three-year-long patience — had been purchased with the same currency the commission was using now: other people's risk. He died protecting the ledger and I called it sacrifice, but there are worse readings. There is the reading where a man hoards evidence because evidence is power, and tells himself he's waiting for the right moment, and the right moment requires his heir to be ready, and heirs take years, and meanwhile the names sit in the dark.

I hated myself a little for thinking it. I hated him a little too. Grief, I find, is generous that way — it lets you be angry at the dead without their objecting.

* * *

Monday evening, the boy came.

His name was Pietell, and he was eleven, and I had taught him compasses the winter before on the shop floor — how to box the needle, how to walk a bearing through the market crowd for the sheer joy of arriving precisely at the sweet stall. His mother sold eels. His grandfather was in Ellery's margins, though I wouldn't know that for certain until weeks later, when the cipher finally gave the name up: *Pietell's line, marsh-folk, seven generations at the reed cut.* Seven generations, reduced by a clerk to *unclear*.

He didn't come to the west door like the others. He knocked on the front like a customer, because he was eleven and didn't know better, and the bored nineteen-year-old guard let him in because children are furniture.

"They took the Kessler boat," he said. "Grandda's boat. They're burning the stakes out of it." Boats were marked, in the commission's scheme, as vessels of illegal crossing — never mind that the same boat had carried eels to the same market twice a week since before the clerk drawing up the notices was born. "Ma says don't bother you. Ma says everyone's bothering you." He looked at his shoes. "But you taught me the compass trick, so I thought — I thought if anyone knows where the lines really are —"

"Pietell."

"— it's you."

I crouched to his height, which put my face level with the shelf where Ellery's field books lived, spines cracked, patient as paper. "Your family's lines are in these books. Written proper, with names and years. Do you understand what that means? It means when this is over — and it will be over, things like this end — there is proof. Your grandfather held that marsh before there was a commission to misunderstand it."

"When is it over?" he asked, with the awful simple cruelty of children's questions.

I had no honest answer, so I gave him an honest non-answer: "Sooner if I get back to work." And he nodded, satisfied, the way you do when an adult says something that sounds like a plan, and went home through the dusk past the garrison door where the list hung, his name not yet on it, his grandfather's surely coming.

I sat back down and copied boundary lines until my lamp ran dry, and hated every elegant stroke, because every elegant stroke was a bandage on a wound that needed the whole ledger.

* * *

Tamsin came at two in the morning, which meant she had decided something.

She climbed to the workshop with a shuttered candle and set it down among my scattered copies, and I knew before she spoke. I had known since Sunday, honestly. There is a particular set to a person's mouth when they have rehearsed an argument all day.

"Burn it," she said.

"The ledger?"

"All of it. The cases, the copies, the cipher work. Everything that ties you — ties *us* — to names." She knelt and laid her hands flat on the floorboards, the way she did when grounding herself. "Xanthe. Listen to the shape of the week. Vane wants the atlas finished in three days. The arrests on the river are accelerating. Sefa's name is on the list now — I saw it, second page, under her married clan mark. If Vane finds the ledger, hundreds die. If he merely *suspects* it exists, hundreds die faster, because he'll tear this town apart bolt by bolt. The only outcome where the names survive is one where the paper doesn't."

"And the people?" I asked. "The people whose tenure is only provable on that paper? Burn it and the commission wins by default. Unclear. Crown land. Gone."

"They're *going*, Xanthe." For the first time her voice cracked, and I realized she had been carrying this alone longer than me. "They're going whether the paper burns or not. You can't fight a requisition with annotations. What you can do is not be hanged beside it." She grabbed my sleeve. "We run. Tonight. North road, then west, before Vane finishes whatever game he's playing with the atlas deadline. You, me, the road. No paper. Nothing to find."

I looked at my hands. Ink to the second knuckle, permanent as far as I could tell; I had stopped scrubbing at it months ago. Fifteen families had copies of their own boundaries because of these hands. One boy believed the truth lived in these books because of this mouth. Somewhere north, a woman I'd met four times in my life and loved without consultation was marked enemy of the crown, waiting on a promise made in peacetime by a man now three years dead.

"You go," I said. It came out rougher than I meant. "Tamsin, go. I'll open the dye-house accounts to you, whatever coin there is —"

"Don't you dare be noble at me at two in the morning."

"I'm not being noble. I'm being arithmetic." I pulled the wall map aside, and the margin-cases sat in their hiding place, plain pine, utterly ordinary, the most dangerous objects in Vellum's Reach. "If I burn this, I burn the only thing Sefa can use to prove her people existed. I promised Ellery. I promised *her*, before she was anyone's enemy. And if I run, I'm doing exactly what I accused my master of — keeping my hands clean while the names sit in the dark." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "My safety was bought with their erasure, Tamsin. Three years of good bread and quiet mornings. I'm done paying that price. I'd rather pay theirs."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she did the last thing I expected: she picked up her candle and went to the window, and checked the street, and drew the curtain, and turned around with her jaw set in the way I would learn, over the following fortnight, meant she had decided to be braver than me.

"Then we don't burn it," she said, "and we don't run. We move faster. I can hide copies in the dye-house — cloth goes out to four market towns, nobody searches bales of indigo. And you—" she pointed at me, "—you have two days left on Vane's courtesy. Whatever you're going to do with that atlas besides finish it beautifully, you had better start doing tonight."

It was sound counsel. It was brave counsel. I agreed to all of it, and only afterward, watching her descend the dark stair with her candle guttering, did it occur to me to wonder why she'd changed her mind so completely between the floorboards and the window.

I found out soon enough. Before dawn, boots on the river road, lanterns swinging in pairs, and the sound I will hear for the rest of my life: the knock — no, not even a knock, simply the *opening* — of doors along Copper Lane, and a corporal's flat voice reading names aloud into the dark. The list had grown again overnight.

And pinned beneath the morning's notices on the garrison door, fresh ink, official seal, a proclamation I would not read until noon but already felt like a hand at my throat:

*Whereas it has pleased the Commission to report the apprehension, at the northern crossings, of one Sefa Ellery, declared enemy of the crown —*
