## 7. The Ferryman's Fee

The ferryman's hut sat where the river widened and forgot itself among the reeds, at the end of a path that had clearly decided some years ago to stop being a path and become a suggestion. I found it by memory more than by sight — Master Ellery had walked me out here once, in the first spring of my apprenticeship, to show me how a river could be drawn three different ways and be true each time. *Depends what you're mapping for*, he'd said. *A soldier maps the ford. A merchant maps the depth. The reed-cutter maps the places the water forgets to look.*

I was not, on that grey morning with frost still clinging to the cress at my ankles, entirely sure which of the three I was. Possibly all of them. Possibly none.

Old Brannoc was mending a net outside his hut, or performing the appearance of mending a net, which in Vellum's Reach amounted to much the same thing. He did not look up when my boots cracked the ice-skin on the mud. He did not look up when I stopped six feet away. He looked up only when I said his name, and then he looked at me the way a man looks at a bill he suspects he has already paid.

"Crane," he said. "The apprentice."

"The apprentice," I agreed. "Late of the workshop above the map shop. Currently of nowhere in particular."

"Everyone's currently of nowhere in particular these days." He bit a thread loose with his teeth and spat it into the water, where it lay on the surface like a small black vein. "You've come about letters."

Not a question. I confess it knocked me back a step. Three years since Ellery died, and here was a man who could still count the correspondence of the dead.

"You carried them north," I said. "His letters. For — I don't know how long."

"Eleven years." Brannoc set down the net at last and studied me with eyes the colour of old pewter, gone milky at the rim. "Every quarter-day, sealed twice, and never once did I read one. You may make of that whatever flatters you. It flattered me nothing. Sealed-twice letters pay better than read ones, and they keep the reader out of trouble besides." He paused. "Mostly."

"I need you to carry something north again."

"Ah." He picked up the net. Set it down again, deliberately this time, the way a man puts down a thing he intends not to pick up. "No."

"You haven't asked what."

"Don't need to. Since the declaration, boy, every crossing between here and the border stone has a lantern on it and a bored young man behind the lantern, and bored young men are the worst kind, because they go looking for interest. Whatever you're carrying, you're carrying it because Captain Vane wants it. And anything Captain Vane wants, I want precisely as far away from my boat as the tide allows." He squinted at the river as if consulting it. "Which is a long way, mind. But not across a border."

"Brannoc—" I began.

"Ellery's dead." He said it flatly, without cruelty, the way you'd report weather. "Dead men don't pay ferry fees, and neither do their ghosts, and you, Crane, are wearing your master's ghost like a coat two sizes too big. I can see the sleeves hanging past your hands. Go home. Draw your coastlines. Let the river decide whose side it's on; it usually picks nobody."

I have been dismissed by masters, insulted by soldiers, ignored by magistrates. None of it stung quite the way being dismissed by an old man with a net did, and I think the reason is that he was right, or near enough right that the difference didn't matter. I stood there in the cold with the reeds ticking against each other and felt the whole scheme I'd built in the dark hours — the cases, the marsh, the border, Sefa — reveal itself as exactly what it was: a promise made of hope instead of plan.

But I had come with something. That was the difference between yesterday's Xanthe and today's. Yesterday I had nothing but grief and a locked workshop. Today I had ink under my nails and one idea, and though ideas are cheap from cartographers, this one I'd paid for with three sleepless nights.

"Then let me pay you differently," I said. "Not coin."

"Coin's all anyone takes."

"Coin's all anyone *counts*." I crouched, pulled my satchel around, and drew out the roll of paper I'd wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. "You told me once — no. You told *him* once, and he told me, because he told me everything twice, once so I'd learn it and once so I'd learn him. You said there were channels through Blackmere Marsh that no surveyor had ever charted, because no surveyor ever asked a ferryman. Channels deep enough for a loaded punt at any tide. Ways through the marsh that don't touch a single road."

Brannoc's face did something complicated. Old men are supposed to be easy to draw — all their weather is on the surface — but his closed like a harbour in fog.

"That knowledge is mine," he said. "Earned. Not given."

"I'm not asking for it. I'm asking to trade for it." I unwrapped the roll and flattened it on the wet planking of his jetty, weighting the corners with four mussel shells that presented themselves conveniently. "Because I made it last night. From memory. Every channel you showed him, and every one he worked out himself and never told you he knew, and three that I suspect you've never seen, because he saw them at low water in a drought year when even your keel couldn't have floated."

That was a lie. Two of the channels were guesses dressed in confident linework. But a mapmaker learns early that confidence is half the cargo.

The ferryman looked at the map for a long time. The wind moved the reeds; the river moved itself; Brannoc did not move at all, except for his eyes, which went along every line the way a man runs his thumb along the edge of a blade to test it. I watched him find the drought-year channels and stop. Watched him frown. Watched him understand that either I was lying or Ellery had kept secrets from the keeper of secrets, and that both possibilities displeased him equally.

"This is worth money," he said finally. Quietly. Almost accusing.

"It's worth everything," I said. "It's the only copy of channels that no soldier owns, and if you take it to the crown with my description on it, you'll be paid a captain's bounty and I'll be hanged, so we both know you won't. Which means its only value is the value you put on keeping it — and on the man who can draw you another one whenever the river changes its mind." I rolled the corner back so he could see the margin, where I'd written, small and neat: *For B., who carried the letters.* "Maps rot, Brannoc. Rivers change. You'll need it redrawn someday. I'm the only hand alive that knows this water."

"Ellery—"

"Is eleven years of ash," I said, gentler than I meant to, because grief will make you gentle in the strangest places. "I'm sorry. But the letters are done being carried, and now something else needs carrying, and I have run out of people to ask. You were the last name on his list. Literally. There's a page in his ledger with ferry fees written down it like a psalm, and yours is the final entry, and beneath it he wrote: *if the boy ever comes, hear him out. He overpays.*"

Brannoc stared at me. Then, slowly, horribly, he began to laugh — a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge remembering it used to swing.

"*He overpays*," the old man wheezed. "Gods preserve us, that's exactly what he wrote to me too, word for word, eleven years ago, when he first asked me north. *Hear him out. He overpays.* Man wrote the same sentence to everyone, like a prayer, like a superstition." He wiped his eye with the back of his wrist. "And here you are, overpaying. First thing in the morning. Before breakfast."

"So you'll hear me out?"

"I heard you out. I heard every word, including the ones you thought were clever." He stood, joints reporting one by one, and looked north along the river toward the border stone none of us could see from here but all of us could feel, lately, the way you feel a storm behind a hill. "Here's what I'll give you for your map, Crane, and I warn you it isn't passage, because passage is death this month. I'll give you truth, which is cheaper and lasts longer."

He walked to the water's edge and pointed with the broken end of his net-pole.

"Since the declaration, there's a lantern at Miller's Crossing. One at the weir. One at the ford below the mill, moved there Tuesday — they move them, that's the trick of it, never trust a crossing two nights running. A patrol boat goes upriver at dusk and comes back at dawn, lazy as a bishop, because they've got orders to watch the crossings and no orders to watch the marsh, because the army doesn't believe the marsh is crossable." His mouth bent. "The army has never believed in the marsh. That has been the marsh's whole fortune for two hundred years. Your master understood that. I'd hoped you did too."

"I'm learning."

"Learn faster." He turned. "The declaration wasn't just words on a garrison door, boy. Sefa's people — the border folk, the ones with names nobody in this town would say aloud before, and now say quieter than ever — they've stopped coming to market. Stopped fishing the east bank. Last week the Harrow family's cottage burned, and nobody fetched water, and nobody asked why, and the magistrate wrote it down as *chimney fire* in a book that will never be read." His pewter eyes fixed on me. "Whatever you're carrying north, it isn't letters. And whoever it's for, she's on somebody's list now, because everybody is on somebody's list now. So the question isn't whether I'll carry you. The question is whether you've thought past the carrying. Because I can get you to the border stone, maybe, in fog, at low water, if the patrol drinks too much. Getting you *back* — or getting her *out* — that's a different fee entirely, and I haven't seen the money for it yet, and neither have you."

The wind came off the water and went through me like I was one of Ellery's transparent overlays, and for a moment I felt exactly like one: all linework, no substance, drawn over a country that hadn't agreed to hold me.

"No," I admitted. "I haven't thought past it. I've thought *into* it, which is what he taught me — get the detail true and the shape will follow. But the shape hasn't followed yet."

Brannoc grunted. He picked up my map, rolled it with a ferryman's economy — no wasted motion, no crease — and slid it inside his coat.

"For the channels," he said, "you get the truth. For the truth, you get one more thing, free, because Ellery overpaid me eleven years running and I'm still working off the balance." He jerked his chin toward the hut. "There's a punt behind it, bottom half-rotted, that I've been meaning to sink properly for a season. Tonight it sinks somewhere useful instead. Reed bed off the second eyot, upstream side, under the drowned willow. If a man happened to be poling through the marsh at dusk and happened to need a boat that no ledger records, that boat would be there, and it would float, because I'd have fixed it, because I fix things badly on purpose and well by accident."

"And the cases?" I asked. "If a man had — cargo. Sealed. Heavy."

"Then the man poles the drowned-willow channel, not the main channel, and he does it at slack water when the patrol boat is eating its supper, and he prays to whatever god cartographers pray to." Brannoc looked at me a long moment, and something in the old pewter shifted, softened, became briefly the man who had carried eleven years of sealed letters without opening one. "Cartographers pray to the map, boy. *Be true. Be true.* That's the whole liturgy. Say it over your oar and you'll do."

He went back to his net. Conversation over; the ferryman's fee collected, the ferryman's change rendered.

I walked back along the suggestion-of-a-path with the frost melting into mud around my boots and the truth sitting in my chest heavier than the cases would sit in the punt: that I had bought an alliance with a piece of paper, and that pieces of paper were suddenly the most dangerous currency in Vellum's Reach, and that I had just handed the most dangerous piece of all to a man whose loyalty rested on eleven years of a dead man's overpayment and one sentence in a ledger.

*If the boy ever comes, hear him out.*

He had known. Three years ago, dying, Ellery had known exactly which door I would knock on first, and had gone ahead of me and left it unbolted.

I stopped at the top of the levee and looked back. Brannoc was no longer mending the net. He was standing at the water's edge reading my map by the grey light, and as I watched, he raised one hand — not a wave, exactly. More like a salute. Or a tally mark.

Tonight, then. Slack water. The drowned willow.

I turned toward town and was halfway down the levee path before I saw the soldiers.

Two of them, at the mouth of Ferry Lane, going door to door with a list, and the doors opening the way doors open for requisition: slowly, reluctantly, one hinge-squeak at a time. They had not reached the levee yet. But the third soldier was already climbing it, boots loud on the boards, a rolled paper under his arm, walking straight toward me with the unhurried confidence of a man delivering rather than asking —

and I recognized the roll under his arm, because I had drawn it.

It was a wall map. My wall map. The one with the hollow roller.

And it was unrolled in his hands, and the margin-cases were not in it.
