## 8. Inventory of a Dead Man

There is a particular pleasure, I have found, in reading a dead man's letters that has nothing to do with grief and everything to do with power. The living guard their correspondence like magpies guard shiny things — shrieking, flapping, dying for it if pressed. The dead extend no such objections. Master Oren Ellery had been dead three years, and his letters lay in my quarters now in two cratefuls, dusted with plaster from the requisitioned workshop, smelling faintly of lamp oil and the river, waiting to be read by a stranger who owed him nothing.

I poured myself a modest glass of the quartermaster's wine — modest because the quartermaster counts the bottles, not out of any virtue of my own — and began at the top.

Let me say this for Ellery: he was orderly even in death's aftermath. His correspondence was sorted by hand into bundles tied with string: trade invoices; letters from the Guild of Charterwrights, all of them dull enough to be used as a sedative; a small bundle of what appeared to be fan mail from gentlemen in far cities praising his coastlines with the breathless devotion men usually reserve for mistresses or generals. I read three of those for instruction in vanity, then set them aside.

The army's work in Vellum's Reach was, on paper, simple. Requisition the town, inventory its papers, establish which lands along the border belonged to whom by right of crown grant, and disabuse the local population of their charming notion that living somewhere for six generations constituted ownership. I had been given this posting because I was thorough and because more glamorous officers had declined it. Both facts suited me. A man does the quiet work while others chase glory; then one morning the quiet work turns out to have been the whole war, and the glamorous officers are still captains of cavalry somewhere, blowing their own trumpets because no one else will.

I untied the next bundle. Personal correspondence, mostly. A sister in Marrowfield who wrote about her garden at length and received replies of perhaps forty words each, which told me Ellery was either frugal with ink or fond of his sister only in moderation. Letters from suppliers of vellum, indigo, silverpoint styluses. One letter, water-stained, from a woman signing herself only *B.*, whose contents suggested that the master cartographer had, at least once in his life, drawn something other than maps. I raised an eyebrow at the dead man. He did not blush. That is the great advantage of the dead over the living; they never embarrass, no matter what you find.

Then, near the bottom of the second crate, beneath a bundle of receipts for ox-hide rollers, I found a letter that made me sit up straighter in my chair, and I will confess that sitting up straighter felt good. There is a thrill in this work, reader, and I will not pretend otherwise. Some men feel it at cards, some at the kill, some when a woman across a table decides to lie with her whole face. I feel it at the moment a pattern shows itself through the noise — the instant when scattered facts stop being gravel and become a road. Call it corrupt if you like. I call it vocation.

The letter was unsigned, dated eleven years back, written in a cramped cipher-hand that resolved, after some effort with a frequency count and a bottle and a half of the quartermaster's wine, into plain text. I copied it out onto my own paper so I could read it cleanly:

*The inheritance is not the atlas but what sleeps in it. You understand why it cannot travel as itself. When your apprentice is ready, tell him nothing until you must. Names are heavier than mountains, old friend, and I would not wish that weight on anyone — yet someone must carry it, and you were always better at carrying than I.*

Well.

*Names are heavier than mountains.* I read the phrase four times. An atlas is geography — coasts, rivers, elevations, the vanity of princes rendered in ink. What did it mean for names to sleep in one? Place-names, surely. Every atlas is full of place-names. But you do not encode place-names in cipher and smuggle them as an *inheritance*, and you do not write, in a letter that is itself enciphered, that they cannot *travel as themselves*. Men hide gold because it travels well. They hide words only when the words are dangerous — and words are dangerous, in my long experience, precisely when they prove something. Debt. Title. Descent. Belonging.

I sat back and considered the ceiling beam, which had a water stain shaped rather like a hunting dog pointing east.

Ellery had been the finest mapmaker between here and the capital. For thirty years he had surveyed this border country — every ford, every boundary stone, every holding. And this border country happened to be home to a people the crown had recently, and conveniently, decided were enemies. If a man wished to dispossess several hundred families, he would need to know where they lived, how long they had lived there, and under what tenure — and there existed exactly one man in the province who had spent three decades writing all of that down.

Unless the mapmaker had been writing down something else entirely.

*What sleeps in it.*

I am not a superstitious man, but I will admit the phrase got its hooks into me. Sleeps. As though the atlas were not a document but a creature, and we had requisitioned a den rather than a workshop.

* * *

By morning I had the workshop's inventory spread across my table, and I found it interesting in the way that a missing stair is interesting — that is, I nearly broke my neck on it.

The inventory, taken by Corporal Fenn with the diligence of a man who wanted his supper, listed: four wall maps (rolled); eleven loose sheets; instruments case, brass, complete; pigments, assorted; one press; two crates correspondence (transferred to garrison, receipt attached); one atlas, unfinished, bound in green calf, retained in workshop pending completion.

One atlas, unfinished.

I had seen it myself during my inspection — I make a point of looking at everything personally, since inventories are written by the survivors of whatever went wrong. The great atlas of the border provinces, Ellery's masterwork, three decades in the making. Magnificent. Except that the final coastline — the northern stretch, along the very border our new declaration concerned — was blank. Bare vellum, ruled and primed, awaiting ink.

A dead man's unfinished masterpiece. On its face, a tragedy of mortality: he died before the last stroke. But dead men do not leave their greatest work *blank* by accident, and apprentices do not spend three years in a locked workshop above a shuttered shop failing to finish a single coastline unless something prevented them. Diligence? Sentiment? Or caution — the sense that the final coastline was the lock, and the rest of the book the strongbox?

I sent for the requisition ledgers. All of them. Town records, parish rolls, tithe books, the guild registers, the ferry tolls, the dye-house accounts, everything the clerks of Vellum's Reach had surrendered with the resigned faces of men handing over their mothers' linen. It came up the hill in a handcart, and I spent the day among it, and it was the happiest day I had spent since receiving this commission, which tells you either everything or nothing about me.

Here is what I did, and forgive me the technical pleasure of it. I took the atlas — Fenn carried it up under my eye, wearing white gloves that made him look like a footman at a poor funeral — and I began with the marginalia.

Every cartographer annotates. Little notes in the margins: soundings, dates of survey, corrections, remarks on bridges (*"collapsed, 3rd summer of the drought"*), remarks on landowners (*"—'s fence encroaches; see deed dispute, ref. parish file"*). Ellery's margins were famous for their wit; the gentleman admirers in far cities praised them. I read them closely, looking for the wit, and instead I found arithmetic.

Not overtly. Nothing so vulgar. But here and there, tucked beside a village or a bend of river, a small notation in a fine hand: a number, a fraction, a date rendered oddly — *"held 9 score & 4," "by grant 2nd Aldermoor, 61," "witness'd, M.H., 3rd hearing."* To a casual eye, scholarly clutter. To a man cross-referencing against parish rolls — and I am that man, heaven help me — those notations were testimony. Nine score and four: a hundred and eighty years of holding. By grant of the second Aldermoor court: legal title, ancient and locally recognized. Witnessed at a hearing: adjudicated, recorded, *proven*. Ellery had been building a case in his margins, family by family, farm by farm, the way other men build walls — stone by patient stone, against a flood he saw coming before anyone else did.

I checked six notations against the tithe books. Six for six. The Halloway croft, marked in the margin *"held 7 score,"* appeared in the parish roll under exactly that tenure. The mill at Copper Lane, annotated *"freehold by 3rd charter, reaffirmed,"* matched a charter abstract in the guild register. Each scribble in the margin was a deed in disguise — or rather, not a disguise at all, but a seed, dormant, waiting for someone with the key to plant it in a courtroom.

And the key — the cipher that turned mere annotation into open testimony — was referenced in my enciphered letter as *the inheritance*, and was nowhere in the seized papers. I had Fenn search the workshop twice, then a third time myself, on my knees with a candle, feeling along the underside of every shelf like a burglar with a commission. Nothing. Which meant the key lived either in a hiding place I had not found, or — and here the wine of yesterday advised me shrewdly — in a head.

Apprentices inherit from masters. That is the whole arrangement; it is practically law. *When your apprentice is ready, tell him nothing until you must.* The Crane boy. Three years in that workshop, keeping the shutters closed, finishing nothing, asking no one for help. I had taken him for a grieving mediocrity. Grieving, certainly. Mediocre, I now suspected, not remotely — a mediocre man finishes the coastline, collects his fee, and drinks himself pleasant. This one was sitting on a strongbox and keeping the lid shut, and doing it well enough that a captain of the crown stood in the room and saw only an unfinished map.

I confess the realization delighted me more than it should have. There is no flattery quite like a worthy opponent; being hunted by fools is mutton, being hunted by the competent is venison, and I had been eating mutton for years.

But let me be clear about duty, because I intend to be clear about it to others as well. Some might say: these border folk have held their lands for centuries, the margins prove it, let them keep their farms. A sentimental argument, and sentiment is the tax the weak levy on the strong. My commission states that the crown's title supersedes all local claims and that the declarations regarding enemy persons are to be enforced without partiality. If Ellery's atlas contains evidence useful to the crown's enemies, then the atlas is not a masterpiece but munitions, and I am not a vandal for seizing it but a soldier for securing it. I have found that nearly any action can be housed comfortably inside the word *duty*. It is a large house. It has rooms for everyone.

* * *

I worked through the afternoon, and the pattern widened like a river reaching flat country.

The marginalia clustered. That was the next thing I noticed. The annotations were thickest along the border valleys — Ashmill, the reed marshes, the Copper Lane quarter, the terraces above the ford — and thinnest in the loyal town proper, where Ellery needed only note bridge repairs. The dense clusters corresponded, family by family, to the households the new declaration had already unsettled. Coincidence is a coin that spends badly; I do not accept it twice. Ellery had mapped *those people* — their holdings, their tenures, their generations — with the loving precision of a man cataloguing what he expected someone to try to erase.

And someone was going to try. Someone already had; the requisition was mine to execute. Which meant that somewhere in this town, in a locked room above a shuttered shop, slept a document that could undo in one courtroom morning what the crown had spent a season arranging. Not with armies. With *margins*. Ink, reader. The most dangerous cargo in this province is currently sitting quietly in green calfskin, and it does not rattle.

I began a list. Not of names — I had plenty of those from the requisitioned rolls. Of questions.

Where was the cipher key? In the apprentice's head, most likely; possibly in a hiding place; possibly — the thought arrived unbidden and stayed — already gone north, toward the daughter.

Ah, yes. The daughter. Sefa Ellery, estranged these many years, resident across the border among just such communities as filled my margin-clusters. I had her name on a watch list before I ever heard of the atlas; she had come to my attention as kin of enemy-affiliated persons, which in this season is a category with all the comfort of a noose sized generously. Now her name acquired a second interest. *Tell him nothing until you must* — and to whom would an apprentice be told anything, in the end, except through the father's last instructions, which would run north, to her? The inheritance was meant to reach Sefa Ellery. I would have bet my commission on it, and I do not bet what I cannot afford.

Which gave me, I realized with the slow warmth of a stove in winter, not merely a hunt but a *shape*. I did not need to find the cipher. I needed to find the courier route. Papers travel; heads travel; both must use roads, ferries, crossings. Old Brannoc's ferry had carried Ellery's private correspondence north for years — I had found the toll entries, regular as a heartbeat, flagged in the ferry ledger under goods described as *charts, waste paper, sundries*. Waste paper, north, monthly, for a decade. No man ships waste paper upstream at cost. The route existed. It was probably still warm.

I wrote the word *BRANNOC* on my slate, looked at it a while, and did not wipe it off.

Toward evening Corporal Fenn knocked, announced dinner, and observed that the workshop apprentice had requested three days' grace to complete the atlas, per my earlier courtesy. I had granted it partly from genuine aesthetic appetite — I do want the thing finished; a masterpiece should not die half-drawn — and partly, if I am honest, because a trap works best when the bait is allowed to groom itself.

"How does he occupy his days?" I asked.

"Frets over the coastline, sir. Keeps to the loft. The dye-house girl brings him meals."

"The dye-house girl."

"Tamsin Ferrow, sir. Apprentice dyer. They grew up together, by all accounts."

I thanked Fenn and noted it. Two of them, then, at the center of the web, plus a ferryman, plus a daughter across the line, plus whatever network of the border folk had passed Ellery's testimony north as *waste paper* all these years. A small apparatus. Quiet, disciplined, patient. Built by a dead man who had known exactly what was coming and laid his provisions accordingly.

I could almost admire him. Indeed, I did admire him, in the way one admires an engineer whose bridge one is about to demolish — professionally, without malice, and without the slightest hesitation.

Because here is the arithmetic as I work it. If the margins hold proof of tenure, then whoever controls the atlas controls the fate of several hundred families — whether they stand on their own ground as owners or walk off it as erased. The crown holds the atlas now. The crown holds it *incompletely*, lacking the key, and keys do not stay in heads forever; heads sleep, heads trust, heads can be frightened or flattered or traded. I had three days of the boy's courtesy running, a ferryman's name on my slate, a dye-house to inspect, and a watch list with one name on it that had just become the most important name in the province.

Duty, I have said, is a large house. I lit the lamp, poured the last of the counted bottles, and settled in among the dead man's papers like a man coming home.

Somewhere south of my window, in a dark workshop above a dark shop, a young man was presumably sleeping the sleep of the dutiful, believing his secret still slept with it.

I intended, first thing tomorrow, to introduce myself to his dream.
