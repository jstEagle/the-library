## 9. Three Days of Ink

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that arrives when you have been awake so long that your own hands stop feeling like yours and start feeling like hired help. By the second night of Vane's three-day courtesy, my hands had unionized. They worked without consultation, dipping and dragging, blotting and burnishing, while the rest of me sat somewhere behind my own eyes like a landlord who had long since stopped collecting rent.

The garrison had installed two soldiers in my workshop. Not in it, exactly — in the doorway of it, which is worse, because a man in your doorway becomes furniture you cannot move and cannot ignore. One was called Hobb and one was called nothing; the second never gave a name and I decided he was embarrassed by it. They took turns sleeping against the doorframe with their pikes across their knees like brooms nobody intended to sweep with.

"Doesn't it bore you," Hobb asked me on the first evening, watching me grind lapis into powder, "doing the same thing all day?"

"It's the only thing I've ever done that doesn't lie to me," I said.

He considered this for longer than most soldiers would have. "My boots lie to me," he said finally. "They say they're broken in. They're not."

I liked Hobb immediately, which was inconvenient. You cannot afford to like the men guarding you. Likeness is a leak in the hull.

* * *

Here is what Captain Aldous Vane believed he was doing by giving me three days: he believed he was being magnanimous. He believed he was demonstrating to a frightened artisan that the crown rewards cooperation, that a finished atlas presented to his superiors would shine more gloriously with my willing hand on its final coastline than with some quartermaster's stamp on a seized half-thing. He said as much on the first morning, standing in the workshop with his hands clasped behind him like a man at a funeral he'd personally arranged.

"A courtesy," he repeated, tapping the blank stretch of coastline where Ellery had died before he could ink it. "Complete it. Make it worthy of him."

"I'll do my best," I said, and meant it in every direction he didn't.

Because here is what I was actually doing, and I confess I did not fully understand it myself until somewhere past midnight on the first day, when fatigue strips the varnish off a man and shows you the wood underneath.

An atlas is not a picture. That is the first thing Ellery ever taught me, before I'd earned the right to hold a pen over vellum. *A map is an argument,* he said. *Every line drawn is a hundred lines refused. The question is never what to include, boy — it's what you can bear to leave out.* I had nodded the way apprentices nod, comprehensively understanding nothing.

But three years of finishing his work teaches you things. It teaches you that a coastline is a claim. It teaches you that where you put the dot marked *Ashmill* determines whether the king's surveyors walk one mile east or one mile west of the actual Ashmill, whether they find forty hearths smoking or an empty valley with an old shepherd's hut in it. It teaches you that precision is a form of speech, and that a man who controls coordinates controls where soldiers sleep.

Two villages sat in that atlas whose names I had promised myself I would not look at too hard. Thornlee, up in the northern fold of the hills. And Cadder's Well, which wasn't on any crown ledger at all — Ellery had mapped it from memory, from the testimony of people who walked there, because no official surveyor had ever bothered. Both were border-folk settlements. Both would appear, sooner or later, in whatever arithmetic Vane was running in his office with his requisitioned ledgers.

And both, as of the second dawn, existed one mile from where I drew them.

It took me four hours to move Thornlee convincingly. This is the part no one understands about forgery and sabotage, which are cousins: the easy crime is changing a number. The craft is making the world around the number agree with the lie. I moved Thornlee and then I moved its river tributary, adjusted the contour hatching of two hillsides, shifted a bridge, re-rendered a marsh edge so that the wetland now sat where the village used to be and honest pasture sat where the marsh had been. A scouting party reading my map would march to the marked spot and find — if I had done my work — a fold of empty hillside, and conclude either that the village had moved or emptied, and either conclusion would cost them a day, and a day, in the arithmetic of dispossession, might be everything.

Cadder's Well was harder, because Cadder's Well had no corroborating features to rearrange. I had to invent them. I gave the false location a spring — wells travel badly, but springs are plausible anywhere in those hills — and a stand of alders, and a sheep-track. I made the false Cadder's Well *more* believable than the true one. That felt like a betrayal of the craft for about an hour, until I understood that it wasn't. Ellery had taught me the craft on maps that told truths he wasn't allowed to tell openly. The truth lived in the margins. The lies lived on the face of the map, wearing the crown's own uniform. I was only continuing the tradition, in the opposite direction, at the opposite audience.

When I finished the false spring I sat back and my neck cracked like green firewood and Hobb said, from the doorway, "You smiled just now. Never seen a man smile at a hillside before."

"It's a good hillside," I said.

* * *

Tamsin came twice a day with food, because the garrison permitted the workshop's other apprentice to bring meals, and because Tamsin had correctly guessed that a man working eighteen hours under guard will forget to eat and then make mistakes. She brought bread and hard cheese and a clay jug of small beer, and she set them down among my pots of pigment with the delicacy of someone defusing something.

She also carried messages out. We had established this wordlessly, the way you establish anything dangerous: not by discussing it. Each day she left with a wrapped parcel of "waste trimmings" — vellum edges, blotting paper — and each parcel contained, folded inside the blotting sheets, copies of the margin-names that we had begun deciphering together back when the world still had a shape I recognized. Half the copies already slept in her dye-house, sewn into bolt cloth bound for market towns. The rest went out through her hands a few sheets at a time, small enough to be forgettable if anyone searched her.

"You've changed something," she said on the second evening, low, not looking at the atlas because neither of us looked at things directly anymore. Direct looking was for peacetime.

"The spring at Cadder's Well is new."

Her jaw tightened. She kept wiping her hands on her apron the way she always did, though her hands were clean; it was what she did instead of screaming, I think. "Xanthe. If they catch that —"

"They won't catch it. That's rather the point."

"They'll catch *something*. Vane isn't stupid. Stupid men don't give courtesies." She tore the bread in half with unnecessary violence and shoved the larger piece at me. "Eat. And listen. Hobb talks. The one without a name doesn't talk, which frightens me more. And there's a clerk coming tomorrow — I heard it in the yard — a survey clerk, going over your materials while you work. He'll have the requisition ledgers open next to your coastlines. If he cross-references—"

"He won't find anything wrong with the coastline. The coastline is perfect."

"That's what worries me." She stood, brushing crumbs from her apron, and for a moment she looked older than me by a decade, though she was two years my junior. "Perfect things get examined, Xanthe. Flawed things get filed away." Then, softer, almost against her will: "It's good work, though. Whatever it is. I can tell by your hands."

She left with her parcel of trimmings, and I watched her pass the nameless soldier, and I saw her nod to him exactly as she'd nod to anyone, ordinary as rain, and I understood that courage does not announce itself. It wears an apron and complains about bread and walks past armed men carrying stolen names folded into blotting paper.

* * *

The third night was the coastline itself. The blank stretch Ellery left. I had been saving it, partly from reverence and partly from cowardice, and by midnight I had run out of excuses for both.

This was the shore of our own river country, the last uninked reach of a masterwork nine years in the making. I knew how Ellery would have drawn it — I had watched him draw a hundred coastlines, had absorbed the particular rhythm of his hand, the way his hatching thickened toward the waterline like breath gathering before speech. Every apprentice believes he knows his master's hand. Most are wrong. But I had spent three years being wrong in increasingly fine gradations, and somewhere in there the wrongness had worn down to something very nearly right.

So I inked his coastline. And as I worked — reed bed, shingle bar, the cutbank below the ferry crossing where Old Brannoc ran his flat-bottomed shame of a boat — I found I was crying, quietly, without drama, the way you bleed from a shallow cut: annoying, constant, beneath notice. Hobb pretended to be asleep. Perhaps he was. Men asleep at their posts see a great deal.

Because here is the thing I understood only with the last stroke: the atlas was finished, and I had killed it. Everything Ellery had built — every truthful line, every patient coordinate, the whole luminous argument of the thing — I had just completed into a weapon pointed at the people it was made to protect. Vane would carry this object to his superiors and it would be beautiful and it would be believed and it would be wrong in exactly the places that mattered. My master's masterpiece, finished by his apprentice into an instrument of the crown's appetite. There was no version of this where my hands came clean. There was only the choice of which dirt to carry.

I blotted the final line. I wrote, in the corner cartouche, the attribution Ellery had specified years ago: *Completed by Xanthe Crane, in the hand and manner of Master Oren Ellery.* Underneath, because I am weak and sentimental and because no one reads colophons, I added a single character of the cipher — a mark indistinguishable from a flourishes-tail unless you knew the key, which lived in my skull and nowhere else. It said, in the language of dead margins: *one mile east of here, the truth.*

Then I slept for two hours on the floor with my head on the blotting roll, and dreamed of nothing, which I took as mercy.

* * *

Vane came at mid-morning on the fourth day, punctual as a debt. He turned the atlas pages with cotton gloves on, slowly, and I watched his face for the flicker that would mean he'd found Thornlee sitting in the wrong fold of hills.

He found nothing. He saw what I had wanted him to see: a finished masterwork, flawless, worthy of display. His gloved finger paused on the new spring at Cadder's Well.

"Lovely detail," he said. "Your master's eye?"

"My master's hand, sir. As best I could carry it."

"And the coastline — complete at last. He would be proud." He closed the atlas and looked at me, and his eyes were pleasant the way a snare is well-oiled. "You understand, Crane, that a document of this quality invites scrutiny. Learned men will study it. Survey clerks. Every coordinate confirmed in time."

"Then it's fortunate," I said, "that every coordinate is true."

He held my gaze a moment longer than comfort allows, and I thought — absurdly, hysterically — of Tamsin's warning. Perfect things get examined. I let my tiredness show, let my hands tremble slightly, gave him a tired craftsman relieved to be done. He bought it, or appeared to, and appeared-to is the currency we were all trading in that week.

"A courier collects it at dusk," he said. "You've done the crown a service, Crane. Services are remembered." He paused at the door. "As are errors. Eventually."

He left. Hobb stretched and yawned and said, to nobody, "Be glad to see the back of that thing," and I agreed that yes, the back of it, indeed.

At noon Tamsin brought bread I couldn't eat. She set it down and looked at the closed atlas and then at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her go very still.

"It's done," I said. "Both of them. A full mile."

"Then it's done," she said softly, and began wrapping the day's parcel of trimmings with steady hands. "Dye-house tonight. Last of the copies."

She was halfway to the door when the nameless soldier stepped into her path, and behind him in the yard, unhurried, came the sound of boots — many boots, and the rattle of a requisition cart being wheeled toward the dye-house district, and a sergeant's voice calling out a street name.

Copper Lane.
