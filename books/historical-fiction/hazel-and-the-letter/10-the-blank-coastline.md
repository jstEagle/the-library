## 10. The Blank Coastline

There is a particular pleasure, I have found, in presenting a thing to one's superiors that they did not know existed and cannot possibly deserve. It is the pleasure of the magician, the merchant, and the moderately ambitious officer all at once, and I confess I have built what passes for my career upon it. So when I carried Ellery's atlas into the provincial headquarters at Greywater House — wrapped in oilcloth, borne like an infant, though considerably better behaved — I permitted myself a small anticipatory warmth. Not triumph. Triumph is vulgar before the fact. Call it the warmth of a man who has done his arithmetic.

Colonel Merrick Stave received me in the long room with the tall windows, the ones that look out on nothing in particular so that visitors have nothing to look at but him. He is a heavy man with the complexion of good parchment and the habit of letting silence do his interrogating for him. Around the table sat the surveyor-general's men, three of them, grey as their instruments, and Captain Loy of the engineers, who once beat me at cards and has regretted our friendship ever since, presumably because he cannot do it twice.

"Captain Vane," Stave said, "you requisitioned a town."

"I secured its records, Colonel. There is a difference."

"Is there." He gestured at the table. "Show me."

I unwrapped the atlas the way one opens a reliquary, which is to say slowly, and with the private knowledge that the reverence is the point. And it must be said — I will say it now, here, where no one can accuse me of modesty — that even I was struck by it again, seeing it under that light. The great folio lay open to its title spread, and the rivers ran down the vellum in blues so cold they seemed recently melted, and the hills sat in their hachured ranks like sleeping cattle, and every margin was alive with the little annotations of a man who could not bear to leave a place undescribed: *ferry here at low water*, *alder wood, cut yearly*, *well, sweet, never fails*.

"The masterwork of Aldous Ellery," I said. "Twenty years in the making. The finest cartographic record of the borderlands in existence, and now, by requisition, crown property."

The surveyor-general's men leaned in the way lean men lean over anything, and for a quarter hour I was, by proxy, a genius. Loy whistled at the coastal surveys. One of the grey men read marginalia aloud in the tone of a man reading someone else's letters, which he was. Stave turned pages with one finger, careful as a surgeon.

"You say it was unfinished."

"It was. The apprentice completed it under supervision. Three days, under my eye. The final coastline — the northern marsh coast, the stretch no proper survey ever covered — was blank when we arrived. Ellery died before drawing it. The boy finished it from his master's working notes."

"And you verified the content?"

"I verified the craftsmanship," I said, which was true, and which I believed at the time to be the same thing. "You do not ask whether a forged coin spends; you ask whether it rings."

Stave looked at me over the atlas for a moment. He has eyes the colour of dishwater and uses them the way other men use pauses.

"Ring it anyway," he said, and ordered the northern sheets copied for the scouting companies moving up the valley.

* * *

I would like to report that I sensed the disaster coming. I did not. What I sensed was dinner, and then the satisfaction of drafting my report in language calibrated to make requisition sound like rescue. For four days the atlas performed beautifully. Patrols went out with copied sheets. The boundary lines along the river were read aloud to householders whose faces taught me more about the border folk than any ledger had — that particular blankness of people being shown the paper that unhouses them. Power, I have learned, is mostly a matter of watching faces while other men read documents.

On the fifth day, Sergeant Quill came back from the north with his boots wrong.

I want to be precise about this, because everything afterward began there. Quill is not a man given to metaphor or complaint; he reports terrain the way a horse reports ground, with his feet. He came into my office at dusk, stood at something less than attention, and said, "Sir, your map's a liar."

"Careful, Sergeant."

"Begging pardon, sir, but it is. The sheet puts Hollowmere village a mile east of where God put it. We marched the coordinate and found marsh and a stand of dead alder. No village. Then we walked west because Corporal Dane has a nose for chimney smoke, and there it sits, a full mile off your vellum, sir. A mile." He said *a mile* the way other men say *mutiny*.

"A surveying error," I said. "Ellery's notes were—"

"No, sir." He set his copy of the sheet on my desk and put one blunt finger on the coordinate column. "Errors drift. This doesn't drift. Look at the others either side — true as scripture. Just this one, moved clean and neat and drawn over so you'd never see the seam. That's not a mistake. That's a signature somebody didn't sign."

I looked at the numbers for a long time. Quill waited, because Quill always waits; it is his single virtue and he exercises it relentlessly.

Here is what went through me, and I record it honestly because dishonesty with oneself is the one extravagance I have never been able to afford. First came the small cold drop in the stomach that every officer knows — the drop that says *your name is now attached to this*. Then came pride, which arrived hot behind the cold, insisting loudly that *my* workshop, *my* supervision, could not have produced an error. And then, last, quiet and reasonable and far more dangerous than either, came the thought: *of course it isn't an error.*

Because I had known. Let no one say Aldous Vane cannot see his own hand at cards. I had stood in that workshop and watched the apprentice Crane work those three days, and I had admired him — I had *commended* him, God help me, told Loy the boy had his master's line without his master's tremble. I had watched a young man finish, in seventy-two hours, a coastline his master had left blank through twenty years of possession of every note, draft, and memory required to draw it. I had asked myself once, briefly, how that was possible, and I had answered myself with flattery: grief makes industrious. Duty fills blanks. I wanted the atlas finished, and so I let the finishing be explained to me in the voice I most enjoy hearing.

That is the trouble with wanting things. You become easy to lie to.

"Thank you, Sergeant," I said. "Say nothing of this below stairs."

He went. I sat with the sheet until the lamplight gave out excuses for looking at anything else, and somewhere past midnight I stopped being an officer reviewing an error and became something else, something with narrower interests. Someone in that workshop had moved a village a mile across the face of the earth with a pen. Not to save themselves — a mile of marsh protects no one from a patrol with legs. They had done it the way a man leaves a nail proud of a board on purpose: so that later, anyone walking the plank would know. So that *I* would know. It was a message written in the only ink that could not be intercepted, and it said: *if this one coordinate is false, captain, ask yourself about the rest.*

* * *

The next morning I went back to Vellum's Reach, and I did not go as a courtesy.

The town wore requisition the way a coat wears out — at the elbows first. The square had acquired the particular litter of occupation: tether-lines, crate-stacks, a card game going badly under the eaves of the wool hall. The map shop stood shut, its windows whitewashed by my own order, and I unlocked it myself, which caused the two men I'd brought to exchange the glance soldiers exchange when an officer starts doing his own work.

"Inventory," I told them, "is a form of conversation. Wait outside."

The workshop above smelled as it had before — linseed, size, the ghost of pipe smoke in the rafters. Ellery's chair still sat at the great table with the air of a man momentarily out of doors. I had been through this room twice already, and both times I had gone through it as a requisitioner, which is to say as a thief with paperwork: shelves, drawers, presses, done in an afternoon, signed in duplicate. Now I went through it as a man who had been lied to, which is slower and far more thorough.

I started with the finished atlas itself, because the lie lived there. I took the northern coastline sheet to the window and raked it with light sideways, the old forger's trick, and found what Quill's finger had promised: the seam. One coastline, drawn in three days, worked over with such patience that the alteration had been buried under hachures and stipple like a body under a garden. And the burying itself told me things. A hurried man hides a lie crudely. This lie had been hidden *beautifully*. Whoever made it had spent as much art concealing the falsehood as telling the truth — which meant the falsehood was not a lapse. It was a policy.

Then I did what I should have done on my first visit: I read the margins. All of them. I sat in Ellery's dead man's chair and went through the folio page by page with the requisitioned town ledgers open beside me, and I let the two documents talk to each other. It took until the light failed and some hours past it.

Consider what I found. In the parish register, a family named Hallow pays rent on a riverside plot; in the atlas margin, against that same bend of river: *Hallow's holding, three generations, well and orchard marked*. In the tithe ledger, a scatter of names the clerks wrote carelessly, foreign-seeming, half-erased; in the margins, those same names again, spelled properly, attached to wells, fords, alder stands, ferry rights — each one a small annotation saying, in the politest possible hand, *this person belongs to this place and this place to them*. Individually, nothing. Marginalia. Decoration. Together — and this is the sentence I wrote in my own notebook that night, and my handwriting went stiff doing it — *together they are a claim of tenure drawn in the crown's own cartographic grammar.* An atlas is evidence. Evidence is power. Ellery had spent twenty years building a case and binding it in calfskin, and I had carried it to my colonel like a trophy and put it in his hands.

Or almost. The thought came then, cold as the marsh water Quill had marched through: if one coordinate was moved, what else was moved? What else might be missing? I checked the index against the pages. Complete. I checked the cipher-suggestive annotations — the ones I had begun, only begun, to suspect were more than sentiment — and they were there, all of them, or all I knew to look for. But the margin-cases themselves, the sealed document cases Ellery had kept locked and which I remembered from my first inspection, were gone. Vanished between my first inventory and my second. Either they had never contained what I hoped, or someone had moved them ahead of me.

Someone was working against me with my own timetable, inside my own cordon, under my own courtesy. The word *courtesy* sat in my mouth like a coin of base metal.

* * *

Three suspects. Let me be fair to the dead and unfair to the living in equal measure.

First, the master. Dead three years, and I am willing to believe many things about Aldous Ellery, but not that he altered a coordinate last Tuesday. Whatever the atlas was, he finished making it before I ever came to town. He is the author of the disease, not the recent symptom.

Second, the girl Tamsin — the dye-house woman, half-apprentice, half-hanger-on, the one who inked washes and swept floors and perched on the stool by the window like a wren. She has access. She has, I noted in my file on this town, a brother among the Copper Lane families, which gives her motive with the dull obviousness of a sermon. But she has not the hand. I pulled every practice sheet, every discarded proof from the scrap bin — hoarders, cartographers; their bins are diaries — and laid the hands side by side. Hers is competent and slightly heavy, a washer's hand, a hand that colours in. The altered coastline sings. It does not sing in her voice.

Third, Xanthe Crane.

I thought about the boy for a long time that night, with the lamp down and the atlas shut under my hands like a Bible at a trial. Here is what an honest accounting requires me to admit: he fooled me, and he fooled me with the instrument I least guard against, which is excellence. I had watched him work and seen only what I wanted a defeated apprentice to be — diligent, grieving, grateful for the task, harmless. I had eaten supper across from him and listened to him talk about pigments, and I had thought: *this one has surrendered; this one keeps his head down like a sensible animal.* And underneath that supposition, all unknowing, I had filed away the truth and declined to read it: that a man who spends three years alone finishing a dead master's masterpiece is not keeping his head down. He is keeping something *else* down, under his hands, day after day, and a man who can do that can do anything requiring patience.

Patience. That was the word that settled it, if settling was what I did. The alteration had taken patience of a very high order. The hiding of it had taken more. The margin-cases had vanished with a patience I recognized now because I finally understood I was not looking at a frightened clerk's improvisations — I was looking at a plan older than my arrival. Ellery had built something and trained someone to carry it, and I had walked into the middle of a succession and called it a workshop.

I do not anger quickly; anger is expensive and I buy carefully. What I felt that night was nearer to injury — the specific, smarting injury of a man who prides himself on reading people discovering that he has been read instead, thoroughly, for weeks, by a person he classified as furniture. Wounded pride is an undignified wound and I have no intention of dignifying mine further except to note its effect: it narrowed me. Suspicion, in the hands of a vain man, becomes certainty with very little encouragement, and encouragement was not lacking.

Because there was one more thing, and it is the thing I keep returning to. On the third evening of the boy's labours, I had stood in that workshop and offered him — lightly, as one offers anything — the observation that his master had left the hardest coast blank for twenty years, and asked him how it felt to finish it. And he had said, without looking up: *"It felt like it was waiting for someone."*

I had taken it for a remark about the map.

I now believe it was a remark about me.

* * *

By morning I had drafted, crossed out, and redrafted my report to Colonel Stave three times. The version that finally went north admitted a discrepancy in one coordinate, attributed it to ambiguities in the deceased cartographer's field notes, and assured the colonel that corrective verification was underway. It was, in its way, a masterpiece of the same art I had just been shown: a falsehood beautifully buried under stipple. I despise myself a little for the symmetry, and I sent it anyway, because a man who has just discovered his trophy contains a bomb does not inform the man he handed it to.

But a report buys time, not victory. Time, in my experience, is the only currency that always comes due.

So I set the machine in motion, quietly, piece by piece, the way one sets a clock. Quill's patrols would walk every coordinate on the northern sheets, all of them, and report deviations directly to me and no one else. The workshop would remain closed but guarded — guarded, not sealed; I wanted whoever was working against me to believe the room might be entered. The requisitioned ledgers would come to my office in batches, and I would sit nights cross-referencing margins against registers until I could recite the border families' holdings better than they could. And the dye-house, with its bolt cloth bound for market towns, would acquire a new inspector, because a woman with a brother on Copper Lane and access to outgoing freight is not merely a suspect. She is a thread, and threads are for pulling.

As for Xanthe Crane — I did not arrest him. Understand this, because everyone to whom I have since told the story gets it wrong. Arrest is what you do when you want an answer. I did not yet want an answer. I wanted the *thing*, the whole inheritance, cases and cipher and all, and an arrested man gives you only what torture or bargain shakes loose — lees, dregs, the vinegar at the bottom of the cask. No. I would watch him. I would let him move, and follow the movement to whatever he was protecting, because a man guarding a treasure will always, eventually, glance at it. Pride had made me careless once. Obsession would make me patient now, and I told myself that this was different, that this was rigor, that a hunter who studies his quarry is not obsessed but thorough.

It is a very convenient distinction. I recommend it to no one.

On the last evening of that week I walked past the map shop at dusk, unescorted, hatless, a captain taking the air. Through the whitewash seams the window glowed faintly — someone had a lamp lit in the workshop above, though the order said the premises stayed dark. A shadow crossed the light and paused, the shape of a head bent over a table, and I stood in the street like a fool in a play, watching a rectangle of dim gold, feeling the extraordinary pull of a room I could not enter and a secret I could not read.

And I understood, with a clarity that felt almost festive, that I no longer cared overmuch about the atlas as an instrument of policy. Stave could have the folio. The crown could have its boundaries. What I wanted — what I lay awake wanting, with a hunger I recognized the way one recognizes a fever from an old illness — was to *win*. To take hold of whatever Ellery had built and this quiet boy was carrying, and prove that no margin, however cunning, no coordinate, however beautiful, no promise, however sworn, stands against a man who refuses to be deceived twice.

Below the window, on the door, someone had chalked, in a child's hand, a single letter among the usual graffiti scrawls. I noticed it only as I turned away.

*H.*

I stood in the cold a moment longer than a man ought to, memorizing a chalk mark.

The arrests on Copper Lane would begin Monday.
