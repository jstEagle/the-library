## 11. Arrests on Copper Lane

I have drawn perhaps four hundred maps in my life, and I would like it noted — by whom, I don't know; God has never struck me as a reader of marginalia — that I was proud of none of them more than the Copper Lane survey. It was one of the first Ellery let me finish alone. Every door lintel measured twice, every garden plot shaded in a green I mixed myself because his green was too smug for so modest a street. I knew which chimney smoked crooked and whose well ran brackish in August. For a season I knew Copper Lane better than my own hands.

On the morning the soldiers came to it, they carried my map.

They had it stretched on a board like a serving tray, two corporals holding it level while a sergeant walked the lane with a stylus, ticking houses off against my inkwork. Tick, the Widow Marchmont. Tick, the Hollis family, three doors and a tannery shed. Tick, tick, tick — my neat little rectangles becoming cages with every stroke of someone else's pen. There is a particular nausea in watching your own careful work used as an instrument against the things you were careful about. I imagine it is how a locksmith feels when his design appears on a gallows key.

I stood at the workshop window with a cup of Tamsin's terrible chicory going cold in my hand and told myself, as one does, that there was nothing I could reasonably do. Reasonably. What a word. It has carried me past more cowardice than any other syllable I own.

* * *

The trouble began, as most troubles do, with paperwork done properly.

The requisition had been in force eleven days. Eleven days of soldiers in the square, of the town clerk's office glowing late into the night like a lantern full of clerks, of Vane's men carrying rolled documents through the streets with the tenderness of pallbearers. They had requisitioned the granary ledgers, the tithe rolls, the guild registers — all the dull paper a town accumulates without noticing it has become a biography.

And among them, inevitably, the surveyor's copies. Because when Master Ellery took commissions — boundary disputes, inheritance divisions, a new fence line here, a disputed weir there — he kept fair copies for the town archive. My hand is on half of them. The army now owned forty sheets that said, in loving detail, exactly who lived where along the river, whose grandfather had paid for which field, and — this was the part I had not thought about until I saw a corporal squinting at one — precisely which of those holdings sat inside the old border commons. The commons where the border folk grazed their sheep before anyone decided sheep needed papers.

Vane didn't need my margin-cases to begin dispossessing people. He only needed the ordinary maps, the respectable ones, the ones that had hung unremarked in the clerk's office for twenty years doing nothing more sinister than being accurate. Accuracy, it turns out, is not neutral. Accuracy is ammunition that waits politely to be loaded.

By the third hour of the morning, the lane had filled with the specific silence of people trying to be furniture.

* * *

I went down. I want that recorded too, since I am keeping accounts this morning: I went down the stairs, and I told myself it was to fetch water, and both Tamsin and I knew it wasn't, and she said nothing, which is her way of saying everything.

Copper Lane runs from the tanner's yard to the mill race, and in the years of drills and musters I have watched it host festivals, fistfights, two weddings, and one memorable funeral where the mourners got lost. It has never hosted anything like this: eight soldiers moving house-to-house with a list, and families coming out onto their own doorsteps carrying what their hands grabbed first. The Widow Marchmont had her Bible and a pot of salt pork. The eldest Hollis boy had the tannery's account book, held against his chest like a shield that had already failed. Nobody was screaming. That was the worst of it. Screaming at least means you believe someone might hear.

"Papers," the sergeant said at each door, not unkindly, which somehow made it worse. Unkindness gives you something to hate. Politeness just gives you the situation.

"Papers saying what?" asked Widow Marchmont, who is seventy if she is a day and has outlived three husbands and every crown official previously sent to vex her.

"Papers of tenure, mistress. Deeds, leases, receipts of rent. Anything showing your claim."

"My father built this house with these hands and no receipt at all."

"Then I'm sorry for it," said the sergeant, and he marked something on his board, and two soldiers went past her into the house she'd been born in, and she stood in the lane holding her salt pork like the last honest thing in Vellum's Reach.

I watched my map being consulted again. The sergeant called a question up the street — "Is the Marsh cottage within the old survey or outside it?" — and a corporal turned the board toward the light and read the answer off the corner of my sheet, off a note I had written at nineteen years old in my best imitation of Ellery's hand: *Marsh cottage, parcel 14, held under commons grant, disputed.*

Disputed. I had written *disputed*, with a little flourish, because Ellery liked us to flag ambiguities. I had meant it as honesty. The army read it as invitation. Somewhere in the clerk's office there was now a list, and on that list every parcel marked *disputed* was presumably a parcel awaiting improvement, and on some other list derived from that list were the names of the people standing in this lane watching their lives get ticked.

You cannot know, unless it has happened to you, what it does to a trade when you see it used against itself. A map is supposed to be a way of caring about a place. That is the whole of it; that is why apprenticeships take seven years. And here it was, inverted, a way of not-caring executed with tremendous efficiency.

* * *

Then I saw Piotr.

He came around the corner from the mill race at a run, which he does every morning because Piotr does everything at a run, twelve years old and all elbows and questions. I taught him compasses the winter before last, up in the workshop, on afternoons when his mother thought he was at the wharf. He'd come with cold red hands and draw circles that started confident and ended drunk, and I'd laugh at him and he'd laugh back and do it again until the circle closed. By spring he could take a bearing off the church spire and find north blindfolded. He called it "the trick." He showed it to other children behind the dye-house. I remember thinking, with the small warm vanity of teachers everywhere, *there, that will outlast me.*

Piotr skidded to a stop at the mouth of the lane because soldiers were there, and even a running boy knows a wall when he sees one. But the sergeant saw him, and consulted the board, and my stomach dropped through the cobbles before the man even spoke, because I knew — I knew the way you know a bearing — whose name was near whose on those requisitioned sheets. The Hollises' tannery shed backed onto the Marchmont garden. The Marchmont garden ran to the commons path. The commons path was the border folk's old road, and Piotr's mother was born on it.

"You. Boy. Family name?"

Piotr looked at the soldiers, then — and this is the part I cannot stop seeing — he looked up at the workshop window, at me, the way he used to look at me over a wobbling compass needle. As if I were the one who knew where north was.

"Run," I said. I don't know if I said it aloud. My mouth made the shape.

He didn't run. Twelve-year-olds are brave in all the wrong directions. He stood his ground and gave his name, and the sergeant found it on the list — not his name, actually, his mother's, but lists are generous that way, lists collect whole households — and a soldier took his shoulder, gently, the way you'd steady a cart, and Piotr was folded into the line of families with the Marchmonts and the Hollises, and the column began to move toward the square.

I did nothing. I stood in a doorway holding an empty cup, and the column passed within ten feet of me, and Piotr saw me there, and his face did something complicated — relief, first, absurdly, because the grown-up who knows compasses was present, and then the slower arithmetic underneath it, the beginning understanding that presence was not the same as rescue.

They took them to the square. All of them. The proclamation boards went up by noon: persons unable to demonstrate crown-recognized tenure upon lands adjacent to the former border commons would be removed pending review. Removed. As if tenure were a fever and the cure was a cart.

* * *

Tamsin found me in the workshop afterward, not working. There is a posture for not-working that looks from outside very like working — bent over a table, pen in hand, staring at a page you have been looking at for an hour without your eyes taking in a single line of it. She stood in the doorway until I acknowledged her, because she is a great believer in letting shame have its privacy.

"They took Piotr," I said.

"I know."

"He can't even spell tenure. He asked me once what it meant and I gave him the legal definition because I thought it was funny."

"It is funny," Tamsin said, in the flat voice she uses when something is true and unbearable. She came and sat on the edge of the table, which Ellery forbade and I never enforced, and looked down at the margin-case lying open between us — the second one, the one we'd copied half of before the guards on the stairs made night work the only work. Hundreds of names. Ellery's cipher crawling across my transcription pages like something newly hatched and helpless.

"The names in there," she said. "How many are mothers and grandmothers? How many are boys?"

"Most of them," I said. "That's rather the point of a people. They come with children attached."

"And every day they sit in this box—"

"I know what you're going to say."

"Then I'll say the other thing instead." She picked up one of my transcription pages and held it like a summons. "Brannoc. Tonight. You've been 'arranging it' for six days. Brannoc arranges eels faster than you arrange courage."

"Brannoc wants payment I don't have, across crossings that are watched, in fog season, with garrison patrols doubled since the arrests began — Tamsin, I've been doing the sums."

"Do different sums." She set the page down and tapped it. "Because the sums you're doing now have a boy from Copper Lane sitting in a pen in the square, and every morning you wait adds names to Vane's list faster than you can copy them out of Ellery's cipher. That's the arithmetic nobody wants. You're not protecting the ledger by waiting, Xanthe. You're rationing it. You're deciding, one sunrise at a time, how many people get to stay prisoners while you make the escape perfect."

I wanted to be angry. Anger would have been restful. Instead I heard Ellery in my head, saying the thing he said about errors of measure: *a wrong number repeated carefully is still wrong.* I had been repeating my caution carefully for six days, each morning finding a fresh reason, each reason dressed in reasonable clothes, and meanwhile the sergeant's stylus went tick, tick, tick down the lanes, and my maps — my accurate, honest, careful maps — told it where to walk.

"I taught that boy to find north," I said. It came out smaller than I meant.

"Then find it," Tamsin said.

She left me the transcription pages and took the guilt with her as far as the door, though of course guilt doesn't travel; it stays where it's put.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of despairing, because despair is a luxury for people with finished errands: I worked.

I pulled every sheet of mine that might already sit in the clerk's requisition pile and reconstructed, from memory, what Vane's men could read off them. Which parcels flagged *disputed*. Which households bordered commons land. Which names on Ellery's secret ledger matched names on the town's public records — and the match was nearly total, because that had always been Ellery's method, hiding the persecuted in plain sight among the paperwork of the respectable, so that proving their tenure would require nothing more than reading what was already written. Genius, in peacetime. In wartime, a round-up route drawn by the victim's own teacher.

Then I opened the margin-case and did the thing I should have done on the first morning: I stopped copying in order and started copying by urgency. Names of householders on the commons first. Names with children attached. Names that appeared on the arrest proclamations nailed to the church door — yes, I checked, I stood in the square with my collar up and read Vane's list against Ellery's, and where the lists overlapped I wrote those entries three times over, on separate sheets, in separate hands, because paper tears and ink runs and one copy is a hope but five copies is a fact.

Somewhere past midnight Tamsin came back with bread and news, in reverse order of importance. The news: the prisoners from Copper Lane were being held in the wool warehouse, not the cells — too many for the cells — and the garrison had posted a removal order for the week's end. Removal where, the order did not say. Orders that omit destinations are the kind history later reads aloud in courtrooms, if any courtroom survives to read them.

"So it's not a review," I said. "It's a schedule."

"It's a deadline," said Tamsin, "for everyone, including you."

I looked at the transcription pages spread across my master's old table, at the hundreds of names I had freed from cipher only to leave them locked in a box, and I understood — finally, fully, the way you understand a bearing only when you've walked miles in the wrong direction — that Ellery had left me more than a secret. He'd left me a debt accruing interest, and the interest was paid in neighbors.

I blew out the lamp, because lamplight in that window had become a reportable event, and in the dark I put my hand flat on the margin-cases the way you'd check a sleeping child for fever.

"Brannoc," I said to Tamsin in the black. "Tomorrow night. Whatever he asks."

"Even if it's everything?"

"Especially then. I've learned this week what everything costs when you keep it."

What I did not yet know — what I would learn with bread still in my pocket and the marsh fog still hours away — was that tomorrow night the crossing would not merely be watched. It would be baited.
