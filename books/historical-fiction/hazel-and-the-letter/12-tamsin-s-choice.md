## 12. Tamsin's Choice

The soldier who came to the dye-house on Tuesday had clean boots, which was how I knew he'd never worked a day in his life. Everyone else in Vellum's Reach wore the mud of the town like a second skin — the river silt got into everything, into your hair, your bread, your opinions — but this one stepped through our doorway as though the world were obligated to stay tidy in front of him.

"We're closing early today," he said to nobody in particular, and everybody in particular.

Marda, who owned the dye-house and had owned it longer than the crown had owned anything, looked at him the way she looked at a batch of wool that had come in damp. "Closing costs money," she said.

"The crown will compensate you for lost hours." He said it the way a man says *the rain will stop eventually* — true, perhaps, but not because anyone promised.

I kept my head down over the vat. This is a skill I perfected young: hands busy, eyes lowered, mouth shut. My mother called it womanly virtue. It is actually just survival with better manners. I stirred indigo and thought about nothing, which is the hardest kind of thinking there is.

They took me to the garrison at noon. Not dragged — *invited*, which is somehow worse, because an invitation implies they expect you to walk in on your own feet and walk out changed.

* * *

Captain Aldous Vane received me in a room with a map of the town pinned to the wall, which I found either reassuring or insulting, depending on how much you think maps are about territory or about watching. He had wine set out. Two cups. That detail frightened me more than irons would have. You don't pour wine for someone you intend to hurt. You pour wine for someone you intend to *keep*.

"Mistress Tamsin Ferrow," he said, reading my name off a paper as if tasting it. "Journeyman dyer. Apprentice to the atlas workshop three days out of seven. Friend of Xanthe Crane."

"Neighbor," I said.

"Friend," he repeated, with the patience of a man who has already read my letters, if I had any, and my ledgers, which I did. "Sit, please."

I sat. The chair was better than any chair in my mother's house and I resented it immediately.

He told me things I already knew, in the tone men use when they want you to understand that knowing them is a courtesy they are extending to you. That the border folk had been declared enemies of the crown. That names had been listed. That certain documents in Master Ellery's workshop contained marginalia of unusual interest. That Xanthe Crane was completing the atlas under guard and doing so, apparently, brilliantly.

"He's very good," Vane said. "Better than his master, some of my surveyors say. Isn't that remarkable? An apprentice surpassing a master. It makes you wonder what else apprentices keep from their teachers."

I said nothing. In the dye-house we have a rule about stirring: stir too fast and you bruise the color, stir too slow and it never takes. Silence is the same. You have to find the speed at which nothing curdles.

Vane leaned back. "Here is what courtesy looks like, Mistress Ferrow. There is a list. Your name is not yet on it. Names move up and down lists; it is the nature of lists. If you were to notice, during your days in that workshop, where Master Crane keeps his private papers — whether he leaves the workshop at odd hours, whom he speaks to by the river — then your name would move in only one direction. Down, and off."

"And if I don't notice anything?"

"Then you'll be a very ordinary woman," he said pleasantly, "and ordinary women stand in lines when we ask them to."

He gave me three days. He gave me a token — a brass button, of all ridiculous things, stamped with the crown's survey mark — and told me to bring it back either warm from my hand or cold in an envelope of silence. I still don't know which option meant what. I suspect that was the point. Vane deals in ambiguity the way Marda deals in mordant: measured precisely, applied to make things permanent.

At the door he stopped me. "You dye cloth," he said. "Blue, mostly, I'm told. Indigo fades strangely, doesn't it? It goes green before it goes white."

"It goes the way it wants," I said, and left before he could decide whether that was insolence or ignorance. With Vane, the safest thing is to let him do the deciding slowly.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened in the next two days, because honesty is the only inheritance I have and I mean to spend all of it.

I considered saying yes.

There. Written down. I sat in my narrow bed above the dye-house with the brass button in my palm — warm from my hand, curse it — and I did arithmetic. Not the arithmetic of the heart. The arithmetic of the market. One apprentice girl, twenty years old, no land, no standing, a widowed mother with bad lungs. Against her: a captain with soldiers and a mandate and a smile like a ledger balanced in advance. For her: a quiet man who inks coastlines and says maybe forty words a day, half of them apologies.

If I gave Xanthe to Vane, my mother would eat through winter. Through several winters. Immunity, Vane had said, means more than my name off a list — it means a stipend, a recommendation, a life laundered clean.

And if I didn't, and Xanthe fell anyway, and they found my fingerprints anywhere near his fall, then my mother would stand in lines when they asked her to.

I turned the button over. Over again. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, the vats breathed their sour mineral breath through the floorboards, the way they always do, patient as livestock. And I thought — this is the part I am proudest of, so attend — I thought: *what would Xanthe do?*

And the answer arrived instantly, which annoyed me enormously. Xanthe would do the slow, stupid, unbearably decent thing. Xanthe would spend three years finishing a dead man's atlas without ever once being thanked, because a promise is a promise even when the promiser is dead and the promised is declared an enemy of everything the promiser's town answers to. Xanthe would carry the whole weight of the world in his ink-stained hands and say, when asked why, *someone has to hold it level.*

I am not Xanthe. I am faster than Xanthe, and harder, and funnier at funerals. But that night I discovered something ordinary people rarely get to discover about themselves: that underneath the arithmetic sits a stone, and the stone does not do arithmetic. It just sits there. Being stone.

I put the button in my sewing box, under the thimbles, and went to sleep like a woman who had made a decision she hadn't quite admitted to yet.

* * *

The problem with refusing Vane quietly is that refusing him quietly changes nothing. He would still get the atlas, still get the ledger, still unroll the margins and read out names like a hangman reading a menu — Hessa of Copper Lane, Brannoc the ferryman, the widow Ondrey, the boy Pip who once asked me what the little needle in the compass loved so much about north. Every name copied out in my own careful hand during those long nights when Xanthe finally opened Ellery's margin-cases and we saw, together, what the old man had actually been making all those years. Not a map. A census wearing a map's clothes.

Half those copies lived in the workshop, folded inside Xanthe's coat or hidden behind the false back of the plan-chest, and every hour they lived there they grew heavier. I could feel it from across the town, the way you feel weather coming in a scar.

But the other half — the fair copies, the ones we'd made against exactly this kind of day — those lived in a place I knew better than anyone: rolled into the cardboard core of three bolts of undyed wool, stacked in the dye-house's drying loft, waiting for the market run to Marrenford and Colder's Cross.

It had been my idea originally, weeks ago, back when it was a clever joke and not contraband. "Nobody searches cloth going *out* of town," I'd told Xanthe. "They search what comes in — grain, weapons, letters. Nobody suspects wool. Wool is innocent. Wool has never betrayed anybody."

Xanthe had looked at me with that expression he gets, like a man watching water find its level, and said, "You're wasted on dyes."

"I'm *employed* in dyes," I said. "Wasted is a different word."

So Wednesday morning, while the soldiers at the workshop door counted Xanthe's brushstrokes, I stood in Marda's drying loft with a fish knife and my heart going like a fulling-mill, and I slit open the first bolt along its stitching.

Here is a thing I learned that morning: fear lives in your wrists. Not your chest — your chest just reports what your wrists tell it. Mine were shaking so badly the knife chattered against the cardboard core. And here is a second thing: work quiets fear the same way it quiets everything else. Fold, roll, wrap, tie. The names slid into the wool's pale heart — Hessa, Brannoc, Ondrey, Pip, four hundred others, written small and neat in a cipher only two living people could read, wrapped in cloth that had never lied to anyone.

Marda came up the ladder halfway through. She looked at the opened bolt, at the papers vanishing into it, at me kneeling there with the knife in my teeth because my hands were full.

She was eighty if she was a day, and she had buried a husband and two sons and every illusion she'd ever owned, and she stood there a long moment breathing through her nose.

"The blue lot," she said finally. "The Marrenford order. They go out tomorrow at first light, on Dree's cart."

I nodded, mouth too dry for words.

"My sons," she said, in the tone she used for reporting weather, "were born on Copper Lane." She picked up the second bolt and held it steady while I worked. "Tighter on the end ties. Cloth travels badly loose."

That was the whole of the speech. Some courage announces itself with trumpets. Most of it smells like lanolin and says almost nothing.

* * *

We finished by lamplight. Three bolts, each with its paper heart, each re-stitched by my own hand — and I'll tell you my stitching has never been so fine, before or since; terror is excellent for tension regulation. We packed them at the bottom of the cart beneath the coarse gray wool nobody buys but everybody needs, and Dree himself — old, deaf, loyal to nothing but the road — cracked his whip at first light Thursday while a bored corporal poked at the top layer and waved us through the gate with the enthusiasm of a man waving goodbye to his own breakfast.

I watched the cart until the bend in the road took it. Then I went back and washed the vats, because the vats don't care about history.

* * *

Which left the question of the button.

Three days, Vane had given me. On the third evening I walked to the garrison with my chin at the angle my mother calls *difficult* and my stomach performing operations it had not rehearsed. I had decided what to bring him: nothing. No observations, no betrayals, just the truth shaped into uselessness — *he works, he eats, he inks coastlines, Captain; the man is boring beyond the crown's power to describe.*

But the gate sergeant looked at my face and shook his head before I'd said a word.

"No visitors for the workshop lot tonight. Captain's orders. There's been—" he paused, choosing, "—a development."

"A development," I repeated.

"Cart went missing off the Marrenford road," he said, and my whole body turned to one enormous ear. "Or so they think. Driver swears it was where he left it. Cargo's another matter." He shrugged, a shrug that contained an entire career's worth of not-caring. "Come back tomorrow. Maybe the world will be simpler."

Maybe. Or maybe somewhere between here and Marrenford, a cart sat in a ditch with its honest wool scattered like clouds, while three bolts traveled on by mule and by foot and by means I didn't ask about, toward market towns where nobody reads ciphers but plenty of people can be taught.

I walked home past the workshop. The light was still on above the map shop — Xanthe, working under guard, beautiful and doomed and level-handed. I stood in the street a moment, wanting to knock, wanting to tell him *half of them are safe now, half the names are traveling north dressed as wool*, but the soldier at the door watched me with flat professional eyes, and besides — telling Xanthe would only make him do something noble, and nobility, in my experience, is how the best people get caught.

So I didn't knock. I went home to my mother and my sewing box, where the brass button lay cold under the thimbles, and I took it out and held it a long time, wondering what to do with a promise I no longer intended to keep.

In the end I dropped it into the indigo vat.

It went in bright and came up black, and by morning it was green at the edges, going the way it wanted.

That was Friday. On Saturday morning, before the mist had lifted off the river, the soldiers came to the dye-house for me — not invited this time — and Marda stood in the doorway with her arms crossed like a woman pricing grief, and the sergeant said the words I suppose I had been earning all week:

"Tamsin Ferrow. The Captain would like a word about inventory."

Inventory. As if I were wool. As if we all were.

I put down my stirring stick, wiped my hands on my apron — blue to the elbow, dyed through the skin, the mark of my trade that no requisition order in the world could seize — and I walked out through my own doorway with my wrists, at last, perfectly steady.
