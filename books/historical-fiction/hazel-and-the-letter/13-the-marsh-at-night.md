## 13. The Marsh at Night

There is a particular silence that falls over a town occupied by an army, and I had learned it the way you learn a new dialect: slowly, then all at once. It is not the absence of sound. Boots still ring on cobbles, wagons still groan over the bridge, someone's dog still barks itself hoarse every night at nothing. No — it is the absence of *answer*. Nobody calls back. Nobody sings across the square. The whole of Vellum's Reach had become a room full of people holding their breath, and I had been holding mine for eleven days.

So when Old Brannoc said, in his gravel way, "We go tonight," my first feeling was not courage. It was relief so sharp it was nearly pain, like unclenching a jaw after the dentist.

We met at the third slip below the tannery, where the river runs brown and patient and the willows lean far enough to make a ceiling of shadow. Brannoc's boat was the usual disgrace: flat-bottomed, patched in three places with what looked like church donation envelopes lacquered over, and carrying perhaps two inches of freeboard on a good day, which this emphatically was not. The margin-cases sat amidships under sacking and a coil of net that smelled of fish and older regrets.

"Three cases," Brannoc said, counting them by touch rather than light, because we dared none. "Heavy for paper."

"Paper is heavy," I said. "Ask anyone who's carried a grudge."

He made a noise that might have been laughter or the onset of drowning. In Brannoc's case the two were often indistinguishable.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the one luxury I have never been billed for: I did not sleep the night before, but not from fear of the soldiers. I lay on my cot above the workshop listening to the garrison clock mark the quarters, and I thought about what was actually leaving my hands at midnight. Three wooden cases. Inside them, copied into Tamsin's careful hand and mine, four hundred and six names — farmers, ferrymen, midwives, a boy who liked compasses — names that existed nowhere else in the world except in the margins of a dead man's atlas and now, briefly, in the bottom of a rotting boat.

Master Ellery used to say that a map is a promise the land makes to itself. He said it while drinking, mostly, which is when men say their truest things. And I understood, lying awake, that I had spent three years finishing his promise without ever once asking myself whether I could survive delivering it. There is a difference between guarding a secret and carrying it. A guard can put it down. A carrier cannot, not until he finds the shoulders it belongs to.

At half past one, we pushed off.

* * *

The river at night is a different country. I have drawn it a hundred times from memory — its bends, its shoals, the church spire that looks like a needle from the water — but drawing is not knowing. Knowing is the cold coming up through the boards into your knees. Knowing is Brannoc poling us through the mist with the unhurried rhythm of a man who has done something illegal or nearly illegal every night of his life and considers variety overrated.

"The crossings are watched," he'd told me the day we bargained. "Watched since the declaration. But watched is not sealed. A watch has eyes. Eyes look at roads, at bridges, at the ferry proper." He tapped his temple. "Nobody watches the marsh. Nobody who isn't paid to be miserable enough."

The marsh began a mile upriver, where the current gave up its opinions and spread out among the reeds. To a soldier with a lantern it was a wall of grey-green nothing. To me it was the most legible document in the county. Ellery had taught me the channels the way other masters teach scripture: *the Serpent, the Needle's Eye, the Coffin, the Long Cut.* Channels that appear only at certain tides, that open and close like arguments, that exist on no crown survey because no crown surveyor had ever been fool enough to spend a February wading in them. My master's last great joke: the most important map he ever made was the one he kept entirely inside other people's heads, because a map in the head cannot be requisitioned.

"Left, past the dead alder," I whispered.

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure it's what Ellery wrote. Whether the alder agrees is another matter."

It agreed. The channel opened ahead of us like a held door, and for twenty minutes I allowed myself the dangerous luxury of thinking this might actually work. That was my mistake, and I record it here as a caution to whoever reads this, if anyone ever does: never think *this might work* on a river at night. The river hears it and takes offense.

The lantern came around the bend of Harrow Point like a slow yellow eye opening.

* * *

I have been afraid before. When Vane first walked into the workshop I was afraid, and when the proclamation went up naming Sefa's people enemy I was afraid, and when they took the Copper Lane boy I was afraid in a way that changed my handwriting permanently. But all those fears had walls around them — a room, a routine, the fiction that busy hands were safe hands.

This fear had no walls. This fear came across open water at us, reflected on the current, and there was nowhere to be that was not exactly where we were.

"Down," Brannoc breathed, and I went flat against the cases, cheek to wet sacking, and he swung the pole and slid us sideways into the reeds with a grace I would not have credited him sober, which he may not have been.

The patrol boat came on. I could hear everything with terrible clarity: the creak of oars in locks, the slosh of a boathook feeling along the reed line, a soldier's voice, bored, saying something about cold, and another voice telling him to shut it and sweep his light. The lantern's glow came through the reed stems and laid stripes across my knuckles, and I remember thinking, absurdly, that my hands were ink-stained even now, three days after I'd scrubbed them, that some part of me would be brown at the fingers until I died, and that this was somehow the thing most likely to betray me — not the boat, not the cases, but the evidence of a trade written into my skin.

They were going to find us. I did the arithmetic without wanting to: reeds thin enough to stripe light are reeds thin enough to show a hull. Twenty feet of water behind us, thirty of marsh, then the long drown of the fen to the border, which might as well have been the moon. Four hundred and six names in three boxes between my chest and a lantern.

And then the second thought arrived, the one I am not proud of and will not pretend I didn't have: *sink them. Sink the cases now, before the lantern gets closer, and swim, and live.* I could do it. I could kick the nearest case overboard and be in the water before the splash finished, and the dark would take me the way it takes everything eventually, and I would be alive and empty-handed and the names would be at the bottom of the marsh where no captain could read them either.

Loss as a strategy. Erasure as rescue. Master Ellery's ghost, wherever it was keeping house, would have had opinions.

The boathook scraped reeds eight feet away.

"Brannoc," I mouthed, meaning *what do we do*, and Brannoc — old, damp, unkillable Brannoc — did the bravest stupid thing I have ever witnessed. He stood up.

Not all the way. Just enough that his voice came out of the reeds low and aggrieved, the voice of a man whose night has already gone badly: "If that's the revenue cutter, I've got eels and no license, and you can write me up, but mind the nets."

The lantern swung toward him. And I — because apparently cowardice and heroism share a doorway and I stumbled through the wrong one — did not sink the cases. Instead I slid the nearest one off the gunwale into the water as gently as I have ever done anything, and then the second, and held them both under by their rope handles, letting them fill, letting them go down into the black water and the blacker mud beneath it, and the third followed my hands down like a coffin accepting its occupant.

Above me Brannoc was arguing eels. He was magnificent. He quoted regulations that did not exist with the confidence of a man who invented them that evening, complained about his lumbago, offered the soldiers brandy he did not have, and generally conducted a one-man filibuster while I let the marsh swallow everything Ellery had left me.

The soldier at the lantern was young. I saw his face through the reed stems — chapped, sleepy, seventeen maybe, with the look of a boy who had joined the army because of a girl or a poster and found out too late about the cold. He peered past Brannoc into our little raft of darkness and I looked back and I swear on my master's grave the boy knew. His eyes went to the reeds where the cases had gone down, then away, deliberately, the way eyes go when they have decided not to have seen.

"Eels," he said finally, disgusted, to his sergeant. "Nothing but eels and an old liar."

"Move on," said the sergeant, who had the voice of a man who had stopped being curious around the same year he stopped being young. "Vane wants the bridges, not the fen. Nobody's mad enough to run cargo through the Coffin."

*Nobody's mad enough.* They poled away, the lantern shrinking to a smudge, then a rumor, then nothing, and the night closed over the place where they'd been like water over a stone.

* * *

We did not speak for a long time. Brannoc sat down heavily and breathed the way men breathe after performing, and I knelt in the bilgewater with my hands still shaped around handles that weren't there anymore.

"They're down," I said, finally, because someone had to say it out loud and make it true. "Serpent channel, east bank, off the dead alder. Six feet, maybe less at low water. The mud will hold them."

"Mud holds everything," Brannoc said. "That's its profession." He spat over the side, ceremonially. "You sank your inheritance, lad."

"No," I said, and was surprised to find the word come out steady. "I lent it to the marsh."

Here is the thing I discovered that night, lying in the bottom of that disgraceful boat while the mist rebuilt itself around us: I felt lighter. Not happy — grief was very much present, sitting on my chest like the proverbial cat — but lighter, the way you feel after setting down luggage you've carried so long you'd forgotten it was luggage. For eleven days the cases had been a weight in my workshop, a weight in my ribs, a target painted on every wall I stood near. Now they were in the mud, invisible, unreadable, and the only map to them lived in one skull apiece, mine and Brannoc's, and skulls, whatever else may be said of them, cannot be requisitioned.

Vane could burn the workshop. He could seize every sheet of paper in Vellum's Reach and read them all by lamplight until his eyes gave out, and he would find geography, beautiful and useless, and not one name. The ledger had become a rumor. Rumors are the one cargo no soldier has ever successfully confiscated.

"Don't get philosophical," Brannoc warned, reading my face in the way old boatmen read weather. "Lighter feels good right up until you notice you've nothing to row with. Those names don't feed anyone at the bottom of a channel. Low water's in three days. We come back with ropes and no lanterns, and we carry them the rest of the way, and *then* you can feel things about it."

"I know."

"And the young one saw you."

"I know that too."

Brannoc was quiet a moment, working his jaw around this. "Then we'd best hope he keeps deciding not to have seen it. Boys like that decide again every morning."

We turned back down the Long Cut with the current politely assisting, and I lay against the wet sacking where the cases had been and memorized the way home without needing to, because it was already in me, stitched between the Serpent and the Needle's Eye next to everything else my master had deposited there. The marsh breathed around us. Somewhere a bittern made its one dry note, like a stick breaking in a world with no fires.

I thought of Sefa, across the border, not knowing that her father's promise was currently six feet under a reed bed with fish nosing the lid. I thought of Tamsin asleep — I hoped asleep — above the dye-house with her half of the names hidden in bolt cloth, and how her safety and mine were now separate cargoes on separate boats, which was the whole point and also the thing that frightened me most, because I could no longer vouch for her and she could no longer vouch for me, and trust, once distributed, is trust you can lose piece by piece.

Three days. Low water in three days, and ropes, and no lanterns, and a border that had grown teeth since the declaration.

I should have known better than to count on three days. In an occupied town, three days is not a span of time; it is a currency, and the garrison sets the exchange rate, and it is always robbery.

We reached the third slip below the tannery as the sky went the colour of dirty pewter. Brannoc tied off, looked at me once with those estuary eyes, and said only, "Don't be seen being hopeful. It shows."

I climbed the tannery stair into the waking town, and I was halfway to the workshop before I understood why the street felt wrong: it was the lamps. Lamps burning in the garrison windows at dawn, and figures moving behind them, and a squad already formed in the square with that particular unhurried readiness of soldiers who have been given a list of addresses.

And nailed to the courthouse door, wet paste still shining, a new proclamation — and the first name on it, in Captain Aldous Vane's precise, vindictive hand, was not Sefa's, and not mine.

It was Tamsin's.
