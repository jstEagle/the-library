## 14. Interrogation by Lamplight

I have always believed that a man learns more from a gentle question than a hard one. Pain closes the mouth and opens the eyes, and the eyes are where the lies live. Kindness does the opposite. Kindness makes a person want to fill the silence, to be helpful, to justify themselves in the voice of someone they've decided is reasonable. I have broken more cases open with a cup of tea than any man in this garrison has with his fists, and I say so without pride, because pride is for men who need their methods admired rather than their results.

So when Tamsin Weller was brought into my office at dusk — dye-stained to the elbow, chin up, the particular stiffness of someone who has rehearsed being arrested — I had two chairs set out, a lamp trimmed low and warm, and a decanter of plum brandy that I do not myself drink.

"Sit," I said. "Please."

She sat the way apprentices sit: on the front third of the chair, spine straight, hands folded as if awaiting instruction. She looked at the brandy and then at me with an expression I can only describe as arithmetic.

"That's not poisoned," I said.

"I know," she said. "You'd never waste it."

I confess she made me laugh, which was inconvenient, because laughing is a currency and I had intended to spend none of it. "You're the dyer's apprentice at Halloran's. Four years in the shop. No family in town beyond an aunt who sells eggs. You were taken on at the workshop last spring to mix inks for Master Crane." I let each fact land separately, like coins counted onto a table. "You are, by every measure I have, an ordinary young woman of no consequence whatsoever."

"Thank you," she said.

"It wasn't a compliment. It was an observation about your options. Ordinary people of no consequence have exactly one thing worth trading, and it isn't gold." I poured two measures anyway and pushed one toward her. "It's information. And information about Xanthe Crane is currently the most valuable commodity between here and the border stone."

She looked at the brandy a long moment. Then she picked it up and drank it all, neat, without flinching, which told me something too: she had decided in advance that whatever happened tonight, she would rather meet it warm.

* * *

Let me be honest about the position I was in, since honesty costs me nothing on paper and everything everywhere else.

Three days prior, a scouting party under Lieutenant Corr had ridden out to confirm the siting of Marlow's Ford against the new atlas and found instead a valley that did not match its own map — a mill where the chart showed open water, a ford a mile east of where my beautiful finished coastline insisted it must be. The atlas I had presented to Colonel Ashe with both hands, like a bride. The atlas whose errors now sat in the colonel's memory like a splinter he kept turning over. He had not shouted. Men like Ashe never shout. He had simply said, "Find whoever did this, Captain, before I conclude it was you," and smiled with the lower half of his face only.

Which meant I needed a traitor, quickly, and preferably one who was not me.

The workshop held three candidates. Crane himself, whose guilt I could taste but not yet prove; the dead master's ghost, who was conveniently unavailable for arrest; and the ink-girl, who had access to every page before it dried. I had watched her across the market square for two days. She never ran. She never looked over her shoulder. People carrying secrets walk carefully, but she walked like a woman with nothing in her pockets at all, and that worried me more than skittishness would have.

Arrest her anyway. Procedure is a net; you cast it wide and see what thrashes.

* * *

"You understand why you're here," I said.

"Because you arrested me."

I let the silence do its work. Silence, in my experience, is the only interrogator who never overplays his hand. She looked at the lamp flame, at my hands, at the map tube leaning against the wall behind my desk — deliberately placed there, deliberately empty — and gave away precisely nothing except a small tightening at the corner of her mouth that might have been amusement.

"You've been mixing inks for Xanthe Crane for the better part of a year," I said. "You know his habits. His hours. Where he goes when he leaves the workshop. What he carries." I leaned back. "Here is what I'm offering, and I'll offer it once, plainly. Tell me what he's done with the master's papers, and your name never appears on a list again. Not the requisition lists. Not the census. Not the lists that follow those lists — and there are always lists that follow, Miss Weller. You'd keep your trade. Your aunt keeps her stall. In five years this will be a story you tell, and you'll tell it well, because you'll be the woman who was clever enough to survive it."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you remain what I said you were. A person of no consequence. And persons of no consequence, in wartime, get moved around the way cargo moves. Without consultation."

She nodded slowly, as though considering it fairly, and I will admit that for perhaps four seconds I believed I had her. Then she said, "May I see paper?"

"Paper?"

"If I'm to give you an inventory of Master Crane's movements, I'd rather write it than say it. I have a poor memory for talk and a good one for lists. It's why they hired me. Pigments, quantities, dates." She spread her hands, apologetic. "Say it aloud and I'll garble something and you'll hang me for a mistake I didn't mean."

Everything about the request was reasonable. That is how I knew it was bait. But procedure is procedure, and refusing a cooperative witness a pen looks worse in a report than indulging her, so I gave her paper, and ink from my own well — I am not a fool in every particular — and I watched her write.

She wrote for ten minutes. Steady hand, no crossings-out, the even pressure of someone accustomed to keeping accounts. When she finished she turned the page toward me and folded her hands again.

It was an inventory. It was, in its way, a masterpiece.

*Inks supplied to X. Crane, workshop above the map shop, by month:* and beneath it, columns of pigments, dates of delivery, proportions of gum arabic to gall, notes on drying times in humid weather. Page after page. It was meticulous. It was verifiable — I dispatched a clerk to check the dye-house ledger while she sat watching me with those patient eyes, and the clerk came back and murmured that the figures matched. Of course they matched. Forged documents fail on their fabric; hers was woven entirely of true thread, yards and yards of it, and nowhere in all those immaculate columns was there a single fact that mattered.

"There's no information here," I said at last.

"There's inventory," she said. "You asked what I knew about the workshop. That's what I know. Ink." A pause, delicately measured. "Captain."

I should have been furious. Instead — and I record this against myself — I felt a flicker of something adjacent to respect, which is a weakness in my trade and one I ordinarily root out of subordinates. She had given me nothing but the shape of giving, filled with substance, weighty, worthless. A map of the workshop drawn at the scale of its inkwells.

"Miss Weller," I said, "do you know what happens to clever women in towns under requisition?"

"They go on being clever," she said, "carefully."

* * *

I dismissed her at midnight — released, not detained; a show of good faith that cost me nothing and might, I reasoned, make her chatty later — and sat alone with her inventory and my cold tea and the specific misery of a man who has just been politely robbed.

Colonel Ashe would want progress by week's end. Progress, in his vocabulary, meant names in custody and property in crown hands and, above all, the ledger — the word had begun appearing in Ellery's old correspondence like mold through bread, *the inheritance, the margin-work, what he kept in the edges* — and I had an atlas full of edges I could no longer trust and an apprentice-mapmaker who had walked into my garrison once already wearing the face of a man deciding something.

I went back through Ellery's seized letters for the fourth time. Correspondence is where the guilty are caught; people write down what they cannot say, and dead men are the worst offenders, having no further need of caution. There it was again, in a letter to some cousin in the northern hills: *the work is not the maps, Aldous — forgive the familiarity, we knew each other once — the work is what lives beside them.* Beside them. Margins. Names.

And beneath that letter, misfiled among receipts, another — older, different hand, a woman's — thanking Ellery for news of "home" and signed with a single initial: **S**.

Sefa. The estranged daughter. Across the border, in the very territory we had just declared enemy ground, in the very country whose villages my altered atlas had begun to misplace. I sat with that initial for a long while, turning my cup, and the lamp burned low, and somewhere in the dark of that office a plan assembled itself the way weather assembles over water — silently, and all at once.

I could not find Sefa. The border was closed, my credentials strained, my superiors impatient. But I did not need to find her. I needed only to announce that I had.

* * *

Consider the mechanics of it, because cruelty that admits to being cruelty fails; cruelty must wear the uniform of procedure or it accomplishes nothing.

If I proclaimed that Sefa Ellery had been taken at the crossing — taken, questioned, held — I would accomplish three things at a stroke. First, I would satisfy Ashe that the investigation had expanded beyond my failures; a man pursuing the daughter of the traitor looks like a man with momentum. Second, I would flush the border community, who would hear the news within days and move their people, and moving people leaves tracks, and tracks are findable. Third — and this was the thought I lingered over, the way one lingers over the last swallow of something one shouldn't have opened — I would test the apprentice.

Xanthe Crane had come to my garrison once, uninvited, offering bargains he did not have the cards to make. A man does that twice only for love or terror, and the proclamation would supply the terror. If the promise Ellery extracted from him was real — and I had come to believe it was the load-bearing wall of the whole affair — then news of Sefa's capture would bring him back through my door within a week, and this time I would be ready, and this time the bargain would be mine to structure.

That he might know it was a lie — that he might weigh the risk and stay away — was a possibility I examined and set aside. Men who keep promises do not audit them. That is the whole weakness of honorable men, and I have built a career on it.

There remained the matter of Tamsin Weller.

I had let her go, and letting her go had bought me nothing but a stack of pigment recipes. Worse, she had left my office having demonstrated, in front of my own clerk, that the captain of requisition could be outmaneuvered by a dyer's apprentice with a steady pen. Small humiliations travel. By morning half the garrison would know, in the distorted way garrisons know things, that the ink-girl had beaten the interrogation, and reputations in a garrison are load-bearing structures too.

Ashe wanted an example. Very well. An example is also procedure, properly dressed.

* * *

At dawn I sent Corporal Reeve back to the dye-house with a warrant for Tamsin Weller's detention — *detention*, mind, nothing so crude as arrest; the word matters in the paperwork — on charges of falsifying crown inventories, which had the elegant quality of being technically true. Her forged document sat in my strongbox, authentic in every column, incriminating in its totality: a detailed record of materials supplied to a suspect workshop, in the suspect's service, in the suspect's interest, produced under questioning by the suspect's associate. I had watched her make the evidence against herself and thank me for the pen.

She did not cry when they brought her in. I had half expected tears, and their absence annoyed me in a way I chose not to examine. She stood in the same chair, in the same gray light, and when I explained — gently, regretfully, the very portrait of a man constrained by regulation — that her earlier statement had been reviewed and found materially false, and that materially false statements to a requisition officer carried consequences, she said only:

"The figures were accurate. Check them again."

"The figures were accurate," I agreed. "The inventory was a lie. That distinction is going to be difficult for you."

"Not really," she said. "I work with dyes, Captain. Everything true in my trade is a lie about what color things are. Madder doesn't make red. It makes red *possible*." She looked at me with those level, unhurried eyes. "You have paper that says things. I've never once confused it with the thing itself."

I sent her to the holding room under the granary and instructed that she be treated correctly — fed, warm, unmarked. Not mercy. Optics. A bruised prisoner generates sympathy; a comfortable one generates questions, and questions generate bargains, and bargains are my native country.

Then I sat down and drafted two documents.

The first was the proclamation: *Whereas Sefa Ellery, daughter of the traitor Josiah Ellery and agent of hostile interests, has been apprehended at the North Crossing by crown forces...* I wrote it in my best official hand, the round, blameless script that makes cruelty look like clerical necessity, and I dated it forward two days to allow for printing, and I read it aloud to my empty office to hear how it sounded. It sounded like justice, which is the trick of the phrasing. Every atrocity I have ever witnessed wore the grammar of a court order.

The second document was shorter. A note, unsigned, undelivered — yet. To be slipped to the workshop when the proclamation went up, or perhaps simply left for rumor to carry, since rumor carries faster and deniably:

*She asked for you, by name, before they moved her.*

A lie, of course. Almost certainly a lie. But the finest lies are not inventions; they are arrangements of things already true — and it was true that Xanthe Crane loved someone north of the border, and true that he had traded his safety once already for her name, and true that men so constituted do not wait to verify proclamations.

I sealed both documents and set them in the tray and blew out the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the garrison wake around me — boots on gravel, the granary door, a cart leaving for the marsh road.

Somewhere in this town, a young man was finishing a masterpiece for me, believing he had time. I intended to take that belief from him by noon.

Cruelty, dressed properly, keeps better hours than honesty ever did.
