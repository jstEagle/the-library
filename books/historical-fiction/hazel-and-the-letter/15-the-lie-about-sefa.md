## 15. The Lie About Sefa

The proclamation went up on the market cross at dawn, which was the first thing wrong about it. Nothing true in Vellum's Reach had ever been posted before noon. Truth in this town kept the hours of a drunkard — it stumbled in late, slurring, apologizing. Only lies arrived punctually.

I heard it from Tamsin's cousin, who heard it from a fishwife, who read it aloud to half the quay because she was one of the few who could and enjoyed the power of it. *By order of Captain Aldous Vane, garrison commander: the enemy agent Sefa Ellery, daughter of the traitor cartographer, has been apprehended at the North Ford crossing. She will be held for trial.*

Held for trial. In this town, under this captain, that phrase meant roughly what *slightly damp* meant — technically accurate and utterly beside the point.

I was mending nets with Brannoc when the news reached us, because that was my cover now, apparently: an apprentice mapmaker pretending to be a bad fisherman. I dropped a stitch. Then I dropped another. Brannoc put one enormous hand over mine on the netting and held it flat against the twine until I stopped shaking.

"Boy," he said, "you've got the face of a man doing arithmetic."

"I'm always doing arithmetic."

"Not like this. This is the kind where you already know the answer and you're checking it hoping you wrote it down wrong."

He wasn't wrong. Because here is the arithmetic, laid out plain: Sefa Ellery had been across the border for three days. Three days of hard travel through marsh and pine, with Brannoc's nephew as guide, moving only at night, sleeping by day in a peat-cutter's hut. The North Ford crossing was two days' walk beyond where her route even touched the river. For her to have been taken there, she would have had to double back, alone, past her own guides, toward the country that wanted her hanged — for no reason anyone could name.

Which is to say: the proclamation was a lie so badly constructed that even the fishwife squinted at it. And yet my hands would not stop shaking, because a lie does not need to be well constructed. It only needs to be believed by the right person. And the right person, in this case, was me.

* * *

Let me tell you what Vane understood about me before I understood it myself, because credit where credit is due, and I have hated few men with such thoroughness that I grudge them nothing, not even their competence.

Captain Aldous Vane had spent weeks learning me the way I might learn a coastline. He'd noted the soundings. He knew where I shelved and where I ran deep. He knew that Tamsin sat in his interrogation room giving him nothing but a forged inventory and a face like a locked door, and he knew that none of it mattered, because I was not going to be moved by anything done to me.

But do something to *her* — a woman I'd met perhaps six times, whose letters I'd spent three years copying into cipher, whom I loved the way you love a room you've never entered but know every measurement of — and I would come running with a lantern in each hand asking where the fire was.

He didn't learn that from surveillance. He learned it from Ellery's letters. Every letter I ever ferried north for my master was, in effect, a confession of exactly this weakness, written out fair and signed. *My daughter asks after you. My daughter sends you this sketch of a heron. My daughter says your coastlines run too confident.* Three years of a man telling his apprentice, obliquely and forever, *you love my daughter; she is the fixed point; navigate accordingly.*

Vane read those letters. Vane did his arithmetic. And then he posted a lie at dawn, punctual as sin.

* * *

I walked up from the river with Brannoc's hand still ghosting over mine, and I said, "She could be taken."

"She couldn't," Brannoc said. "Not at the Ford. Not three days out."

"Unless something went wrong. Unless the peat-cutter talked. Unless—"

"Boy." He turned me around by the shoulder, gently, the way you'd turn a cart. "If they'd truly taken her, they wouldn't announce it. They'd sit on it quiet as a grave and use her proper. A proclamation means they want you to *know*. Now ask yourself what a man like Vane wants you to do once you know."

I asked myself. I answered myself. The answer sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

He wants me to come to him. That was the whole design, elegant as a compass rose. He'd failed to break me with arrests, failed to catch me at the marsh, failed to crack Tamsin, failed to find the sunken cases in the reed bed though his men dredged half the river and came up with mud and one very indignant pike. So he stopped trying to take the fortress and instead hung a banner off the wall that said, in effect, *surrender or the girl dies*, and trusted that love would do what force could not.

And here is the humiliating part, the part I will carry to whatever grave I get: it would work. It was already working. I was halfway to the garrison in my head before Brannoc finished speaking.

Because I knew it was bait, and knowing changed nothing, and that is the whole of what I have learned about love since. You can chart the trap. You can measure it, annotate it, draw its margins in your own neat hand. You walk into it anyway, eyes open, because the alternative is a life spent being the sort of man who calculated correctly while someone drowned.

* * *

Tamsin caught me at the dye-house door. She'd been released two days prior — Vane letting her go the way you release a hawk with bells on it — and she took one look at my face and planted herself in front of me like a fence post.

"No," she said.

"You don't know what I—"

"Your face knows. Your face has been shouting since the fishwife got to the word *apprehended*. You're going to the garrison."

"Tamsin—"

"If you go in there, everything we've done is worth nothing. The copies in the bolt cloth, the cases in the reeds, the coordinate I lied about with soldiers standing behind me reading over my shoulder — all of it, spent, so you can feel useful in a cell." Her hands were stained blue to the wrist, indigo to the elbow, and when she grabbed my sleeve she left prints on it, small blue fingerprints, evidence of her everywhere. "Xanthe. Listen to me. If Sefa were truly taken, Vane would trade her quietly. He'd send one man, one note, no proclamations. Proclamations are for audiences. Ask yourself who the audience is."

"You," I said miserably. "Me. Everyone."

"And what does the audience do?"

I looked at the cobbles. The cobbles offered no strategic counsel whatsoever.

"He's betting his whole hand," Tamsin said, softer now, which was worse, "that you'll choose her over the ledger. And the moment you do, he doesn't need the ledger hunted anymore. It walks into his office wearing your coat."

"That's the trouble," I said. "It isn't a bet. Bets can be declined."

She stared at me. Then she did the worst thing, the cruelest, most effective thing: she stopped arguing and just stood there, blue-handed, letting me see how much it cost her to let me go. Which I did anyway. Which we both knew I would.

"Tell me where you're sinking the rest of the copies," I said. "In case."

"No," said Tamsin. "You taught me that. Never write down what you can carry."

I laughed, which surprised us both, and went to surrender.

* * *

The garrison had been a wool merchant's house before the requisition, and it still smelled faintly of lanolin under the boot-oil and ink, as if the building itself remembered a more honest trade. A corporal with a nose like a knot in timber took my name without surprise. Of course there was no surprise. There was probably a slot in Vane's desk labeled *Crane, X. — expected*, filed between *ink, black* and *candles, tallow*.

They gave me tea. That detail undid me more than the irons would have — nobody offers tea to a prisoner. They offer tea to a guest who is about to be billed. I sat in the little office that had been the merchant's parlor, with its good hearth and its view of the square where I'd learned to draw letters in chalk on slate, and I drank the tea, and I waited.

Vane came in unhurried, closed the door himself, and sat opposite me with the ease of a man who has never once in his life been early or late but simply *when intended*.

"Master Crane," he said. "You've saved me a carriage."

"Has she been taken?" I said. No preamble. Preamble is for people with time.

"What do you think?"

"I think you posted a lie at dawn."

"And yet." He spread his hands, a conjuror revealing the empty cup. "Here you sit."

"Yes," I said. "Here I sit."

For a long moment neither of us said anything, and in that silence I understood something that rearranged the furniture of my fear. He wasn't gloating. He looked almost tired, almost gentle, the way men look when a thing has gone precisely as planned and planning is the loneliest part of their profession. He studied me the way I study an old map — not for information but for provenance, for the thumbprints of everyone who'd handled it before.

"You know she wasn't taken," he said at last. "You worked it out on the walk over, or before. You're not stupid; your master's letters say so often enough. So explain it to me, Master Crane, as one professional to another, because I confess it puzzles me. Why am I sitting across from a man who knows he's been lied to?"

"Because you weren't lying to me," I said. "You were lying to the situation. The proclamation wasn't meant to convince me Sefa was captured. It was meant to make her capture *possible*. Once it's announced, it can be arranged. If I stay free, she becomes real — a patrol gets lucky, or unlucky, whichever serves you. If I come in, she stays a fiction. Either way, the announcement does its work. It was a wager on my caution, Captain, and you set the odds so that caution cost more than folly."

Vane sat back. Something moved behind his face — not admiration exactly; closer to the discomfort of a man finding his own method handed back to him with better joinery.

"Your master," he said, "would have been proud. Or appalled. With Ellery I understand it was often both."

"Don't speak about him."

"I've read everything he ever wrote," Vane said mildly. "Every letter. Every marginal scrawl I could seize. I know a man better from his paper than his neighbors ever do from his face. He was afraid, near the end. Afraid and certain, which is the worst combination in a human being. Certain of you, especially." He laced his fingers. "So. Terms. You came offering the atlas's location. I'm listening."

"The atlas for Sefa," I said. "Her safe passage south, written order, your seal, delivered before I give you anything. And Tamsin Dull's name struck from every list, and the Copper Lane prisoners released to their homes."

"That's a great deal of purchase for one book."

"It isn't a book. It's the reason you're still in this town instead of explaining yourself in the capital. It's the only trophy that makes your requisition look like strategy rather than vandalism. You don't need the names in the margins, Captain — you need the object. Something to lay on a table with a flourish. Am I wrong?"

He didn't answer, which is the loudest way a man like Vane answers.

I leaned forward. My heart was going like a ferryman's drum, but my voice came out steady, and I remember being distantly proud of it, the way you're proud of a straight line drawn on a pitching deck. "The atlas exists. One copy. Finished, except for the parts you'll never read without me. Its location lives in one head, and the head is currently drinking your tea. Write the order. Free them. And I'll walk you to it myself."

"And if I simply hold you," Vane said, "until the wanting-out of you loosens your tongue? Water, dark rooms, time. Time is remarkably persuasive."

"You could try. But I've had three years of practice keeping a secret from people gentler than you, and I'll tell you what I told the loneliness: it keeps better than I do." I set down the cup. My hand, traitor to the last, rattled it against the saucer. "Besides, Captain — you've already tried patience. Patience brought you nothing but a blank coastline and a wrong coordinate. You're a careful man. Be careful now."

Vane regarded me for a long moment. Somewhere below, boots crossed the yard; somewhere above, gulls argued over the chimney pots. Then he smiled, and it was the smile that frightened me — not cruel, not triumphant, but *interested*, the expression of a man who has found a flaw in a map and intends to survey it thoroughly.

"There is no order," he said pleasantly, "and no release. There is only this: you will take me to the atlas, and afterward we will discuss what you've earned. That is not negotiation, Master Crane. That is sequence."

"And if I've memorized nothing? If the location died with the walk here?"

"Then you've wasted a morning," said Vane, rising, "and I've wasted a proclamation, and we'll both have to live with the disappointment. Shall we?"

He opened the door for me himself. Such courtesy. Such procedure. And as I passed him, he added, softly, almost kindly, words pitched for me alone:

"For what it's worth — she was never at the Ford. But check the date on that proclamation again before you hate me entirely. I posted it this morning. And your ferryman's nephew left the marsh *last night*."

The corridor stretched ahead of me, doors and lamplight and the smell of old wool, and I walked down it toward my own ruin trying to do the arithmetic, trying to decide whether I'd just been threatened or — far worse — *thanked*.
