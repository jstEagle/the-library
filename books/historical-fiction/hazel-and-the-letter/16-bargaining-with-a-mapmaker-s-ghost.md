## 16. Bargaining With a Mapmaker's Ghost

*Vane*

The atlas sat on my desk like a confession I could not read.

I had laid it open to the final page — the coastline Ellery had left blank and his apprentice had not — and run my thumb along the ink. Fresh enough to smudge. Beautiful enough to grieve over, if I were the sort of man who grieved over coastlines. The survey report from Captain Merek's scouting party lay beside it, all wrong terrain and embarrassed apologies: a village that should have stood at the river's bend standing a mile east of where the map promised, in a hollow full of marsh grass and confusion.

Someone had moved a village with a pen. I found I almost admired it.

The apprentice had come willingly. That was the part that unsettled me, and I do not unsettle easily. I had built the trap of Sefa Crane's capture with some care — the proclamation nailed to every door, the timing arranged so grief would still be raw — and he had walked into it with the flat calm of a man delivering a parcel.

"Show him in," I said.

* * *

*Xanthe*

The garrison office smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, and Captain Aldous Vane sat behind a desk that had been arranged for effect — the way a stage is arranged, everything angled toward an audience that hadn't arrived yet. The atlas lay open before him. My coastline. Three years of my life under his thumb.

I made myself look at it without flinching. That was the first move, I decided. Whatever game this was, it was played on paper, and paper was my country.

"Master Crane," Vane said, gesturing to the chair across from him. "You've saved me a march into the marsh. I appreciate economy."

"I've come about Sefa," I said. "You have her. I have the atlas. I imagine you can see the shape of the evening from there."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Something worse. Pleasure, but pleasure held back, the way a man holds back a good hand at cards until the pot is worth taking.

"Sit down," he said again, softly. "Please."

* * *

*Vane*

He sat. Good. I had wondered whether he would be one of those men who needed to be broken before he could be spoken to, and it would have been a shame. Ellery's apprentice, come to trade his master's life's work for a woman — there was a certain architecture to it. I wanted the conversation intact.

"You understand," I said, resting my hand on the atlas, "that this is already crown property. By requisition. Signed, sealed, filed. You are offering me something I own."

"I'm offering you something you can't use," Xanthe said. "There's a difference, Captain, and it's the whole difference. You've had this book for two days. Have you read it?"

I let the silence do my work for me. He waited it out better than most. Mapmakers, I thought. Men who spend their lives measuring distances learn patience the way soldiers learn to sleep in mud.

"The marginalia," he went on, when I gave him nothing. "The annotations along the coasts and rivers. Every one of them is ciphered. Coordinates within coordinates. Without the key, it's decoration. With it—" He shrugged, a small, precise gesture, like a compass needle settling. "With it, it's the names of four hundred families, their holdings, their boundaries, their claims going back sixty years. Everything you need to unmake them legally. Or everything they need to survive you. Depending on whose hands it sits in."

"And the key?"

"In my head."

* * *

*Xanthe*

I will admit — because there is no one left to admit things to — that I enjoyed saying it more than a man in my position should have. His face didn't change, but his hand lifted off the atlas by half an inch, as though the book had grown warm.

That was when he smiled, and I learned what kind of smile it was: the kind that arrives just before bad news, clearing its throat.

"Before we discuss keys," Vane said, "there is a small matter of accounting." He opened a drawer — slowly, theatrically; the man loved an audience even when the audience was only me — and took out a single sheet of paper. "Your sister. Or whatever she is to you. Sefa Crane, taken at the Thornmere crossing, three days ago. It's all in the proclamation. You read it?"

"I read it."

"Then you'll want to know her condition. Her whereabouts. Whether she has been—" He paused delicately. "—processed."

My hands were steady on my knees. I had spent three years learning steadiness from a dead man's example; it seemed suddenly a poor inheritance.

"Where is she?" I said.

"There is no Thornmere crossing," said Captain Vane, almost gently. "Not since spring. The bridge came down in the floods and hasn't been rebuilt. Nobody was taken anywhere. The proclamation was a letter written to one reader." He folded his hands. "You. And here you are, Master Crane, sitting in my office, having delivered yourself and confessed your attachment to the ledger in the same hour. I confess I expected more resistance. I'm told you lost a patrol in the marsh last week."

"They walked into fog," I said. "Fog is very loyal to people who feed it."

For a moment — a real moment, I'd swear to it — something that was almost laughter crossed his face and was executed on arrival.

* * *

*Van­e*

He took it well, which annoyed me. A man learns his bait has failed and does not rage, does not bargain wildly — he simply recalculates, the way water recalculates around a stone. I began to understand why Merek's patrol had come home wet and empty-handed.

"So," I said. "Sefa Crane is beyond the border, alive, and entirely out of my reach. Your promise to her is safe from me. What remains in this room is the atlas and the man who can read it."

"Then we're negotiating after all."

"We were always negotiating. I merely preferred you arrive believing your stakes were higher than they are."

"You lied to me," he said. There was no heat in it. It had the tone of a man noting an error on a chart — *this reef is not where marked.*

"I performed," I corrected. "It is a courtesy of my profession. As finishing a map in three days under guard was a courtesy of yours."

His jaw tightened. There it was. I set the hook lightly.

"You altered two coordinates," I said. "Perhaps more. The survey caught one. How many did it miss, I wonder? How many villages are currently a mile from themselves?" I turned the atlas toward him. "This book is beautiful and it is false, and I cannot tell which lines are true without you. So. Let us speak plainly, mapmaker, since plainness seems to be your dialect."

"An unusual dialect for an officer."

"I keep it caged. Now." I leaned back. "Here is what I believe. You cannot give me the key, because the moment I have it, you are worthless — and worthless men in my line of work are hanged or forgotten, and often both. I cannot simply kill you and search for the key elsewhere, because Ellery kept his secrets the way he kept his workshop: locked, and the key swallowed. Am I close?"

"You're a mile off," Xanthe said. "But then, your maps usually are."

* * *

*Xanthe*

He didn't rise to it. That frightened me more than any temper would have. I had come into that office prepared to bargain with a wolf and found instead an accountant, and accountants are harder to outrun.

"Let me tell you what I see," I said, because while he was assembling his next sentence, I intended to assemble mine. "You took this town with a signature. You seized houses, ledgers, correspondence — a whole river of paper. And yet here you sit, needing one book and one apprentice, because the paper doesn't answer your questions. You need me the way a thief needs a fence. Only the fence knows what's stolen."

"Careful."

"No," I said. "You've had your turn at careful. Here is mine." I put my palm flat on the atlas, over the coastline I had inked by lamplight while a soldier picked his teeth three feet away. "This book is worthless to you without the cipher. The cipher is worthless without this book. And I am worthless to you once both are yours — which means the moment this transaction completes, Captain, my life becomes an expense. I've read enough ledgers to know what happens to expenses."

Vane studied me for a long moment. The lamp guttered and righted itself between us.

"You assume," he said at last, "that I want the ledger destroyed. Or exploited. That my interest ends with names and land grants." He rose and went to the window, hands clasped behind him. "My superiors want results, Master Crane. Results are documents with seals on them. If I deliver the border communities dispossessed, I am promoted. If I deliver nothing, I am retired — and a man like me does not retire; he is retired, onto a small pension in a small town where everyone knows what became of him." He turned back. "You and I are in nearly the same position. We each hold something the other needs, and neither of us can afford to release it."

"That's not a bargain. That's a stalemate."

"It is a marriage," Vane said, "of the sort common in my profession. Two parties bound by mutual capability for harm." He sat down again and, to my genuine astonishment, pushed a decanter and two glasses across the desk. "Drink?"

"I don't drink with men who forge proclamations about my—"

"About the woman you crossed a border country to save, yes. Though save from what, I wonder, since she was never in danger." He poured one glass anyway, and did not push it. "Consider our situation honestly, Crane. You cannot leave the garrison with the atlas; my men would take it from you at the door, and take you with it, and I would spend an unpleasant year extracting a cipher from a stubborn corpse's associates — the dye-girl, perhaps, or the ferryman. You cannot destroy it; your master's ghost would haunt you, and besides, you'd lose your only leverage. I cannot force the key from you quickly enough to matter, and I cannot explain to my superiors why their prize atlas is decorated nonsense. We are," he said, with something dangerously near sincerity, "each other's ruin, and each other's ransom."

I looked at the brandy. I did not touch it.

"Then here is my offer," I said. "You withdraw the requisition. You release Tamsin. You release the families taken from Copper Lane, and you let the proclamation die quietly — no more arrests, no more seizures. In exchange, I decode the ledger. Not for you to hang them with — for you to *close* it. The names become land records, quiet ones, filed away. Their tenure proven, their existence legal on paper forever. Isn't that what a man like you wants? Not blood. Paperwork. An ending nobody upstairs can question."

* * *

*Vane*

For a moment — a long one — I let myself consider it. That is the honest record, and I keep one, even if only here. The offer was elegant. Dispossession by other means, with the crown's own seals blessing it. Four hundred families made permanent by the very document meant to erase them, and Aldous Vane the man who closed the file. Promotion without corpses. Even my sleep might have approved.

And it was, of course, impossible.

"You misunderstand my position," I said. "The requisition isn't mine to withdraw. It comes from above me. The arrests on Copper Lane were ordered before I arrived — I merely chose the streets. If I release those families, I announce to my superiors that either their captain has gone soft or their operation has failed, and both announcements end my career and begin someone worse's. Do you imagine my replacement would sit across a desk from you offering brandy?" I shook my head. "No. Whatever we settle tonight, it must look like victory from above. That is the constraint, Crane. Work within it or there is no conversation to be had."

Xanthe was quiet for a while. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a sentry called the hour.

"There's a phrase cartographers use," he said finally. "*Terra incognita.* The blank space at the edge of the map. Old maps used to write *here be dragons* in the emptiness. You know why?"

"Humor?"

"Because a blank is unbearable," he said. "Men would rather invent monsters than admit they don't know what's there. You have a blank in your report, Captain. One altered coordinate, a lost patrol, a workshop that keeps producing beautiful lies. Your superiors can't tolerate the blank. They'll send someone to fill it — with dragons, with my hanging, with whatever's nearest. You know that. You're living on borrowed blankness."

I will not pretend the observation didn't land where it aimed.

"So the question," he went on, leaning forward, "is not whether you need me. You do — you said as much yourself, in your roundabout officer's way. The question is how long you can afford to need me before the dragons arrive. My price hasn't changed. But my patience is shorter than it was an hour ago, because you taught me tonight that your promises are printed, not meant."

* * *

*Vane*

There it was, then. Two men at a desk, each holding a knife at the other's future, each discovering that the knife was also the rope holding up his own floor. I had interrogated smugglers, fanatics, and one genuinely frightening grandmother, and none of them had ever made me feel like a man negotiating with his own reflection.

"I will need time," I said. Which was true, and also bought me time, and I have never in my life confused those two things.

"Then take it," Xanthe said. "But understand what you're holding while you wait. You think you seized Ellery's atlas. You didn't. You seized Ellery's *body*. The atlas was never the thing he made. The thing he made is sitting in your chair, drinking none of your brandy, and it does not forget, and it does not forgive, and it has had three years' practice at being patient."

He stood. I let him. At the door he paused, and for the first time all evening he looked tired — genuinely tired, the tiredness of a man who had carried something heavy across a dark country and found the far bank guarded by an idiot with excellent manners.

"My sister," he said. "When this is done — however it's done — I want to know she heard it ended well. That's not leverage. That's just a thing I'm telling you, so you know what kind of man you're bargaining with."

"A mapmaker's ghost," I said quietly, mostly to myself, after the door had closed behind him.

I sat alone with the atlas a long while. Then I sent for the duty clerk and gave two orders. The first was that Master Crane be confined comfortably — fed, warm, unharmed. The second was that a rider leave immediately for the border stations with a single question: had anyone, at any crossing, in any month, recorded the arrival of a woman named Sefa Crane?

Because here is what my clever mapmaker had missed, standing so proudly in his certainty: I had lied about the capture, yes. But I had never claimed to know where she truly was.

If she existed, she could be found. And if she could be found, then the blank at the edge of my map was about to be filled — and I intended to hold the pen.
