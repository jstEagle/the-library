## 17. The Reed Bed at Low Water

They gave me back my boots for the occasion, which I took as a compliment. A man about to drown in a marsh does not need boots, and yet Captain Vane's sergeant laced them onto my feet himself, tugging each knot like a man who had been told — correctly — that I could not be trusted to tie my own.

"Captain's orders," the sergeant said. "You walk in comfortable. You think better comfortable."

Vane had not come to the marsh himself. That was the first mercy of the morning, and I did not trust it. He had stood at the garrison gate while they loaded me into a cart between six soldiers, and he had looked at me the way a man looks at a ledger that has begun, quietly, to lie to him.

"Three years you spent on that atlas," he'd said. "A man who loves a thing that much doesn't sink it in a reed bed without weeping over it. So you'll show me where it lies, apprentice, and you'll weep if you like. I'll allow tears."

I had said nothing, because I had learned from him that silence reads as either guilt or dignity, depending on the light, and the dawn light was on my side.

So: six soldiers, one sergeant with kind hands and poor instincts, one cart, one prisoner who knew every channel, cut, and drowned oak stump between Vellum's Reach and the border stone — and above us a fog coming off the water in slow white ropes, the kind of fog the marsh makes only twice a year, when the river runs warm and the air off the fens runs cold, and the two meet over the shallows and cannot agree which of them should be rising.

I had drawn this weather into three maps before I was nineteen. My master used to say the marsh had a moods-page all its own, though we never dared print it, since no crown surveyor would credit *fog, habitual, loyal to neither king nor eel*.

The cases lay where Brannoc and I had left them, or near enough — the reed bed off the Sallow Cut, where the water ran two feet deep over mud that pretended to be bottom and wasn't. Two hours' poling by night; longer by day, in a cart, with soldiers who believed the marsh began where the road gave out.

It does not begin there. That is the whole of what I know, and today it would be the whole of everything.

* * *

The sergeant was called Wrenn, and he was not a fool, which made it harder and also more interesting. He had grown up somewhere dry. He walked the plank-roads with his knees stiff and his eyes on the water, as though it might rise up and declare itself an enemy combatant.

"You're certain it's here," he said, when we reached the third sluice-gate.

"Certain as a man can be about a place he sank things at night in terror," I said. "Everything looks different by daylight. There." I pointed down a channel that branched three ways ahead, all three branches looking exactly alike unless you knew that only the middle one kept its depth past midsummer. "Past that stand of alders."

Wrenn consulted the map his superiors had given him — a crown survey, forty years old, drawn by some unfortunate who had clearly done his work from a boat in high water and gone home early. It showed the Sallow Cut running straight. The Sallow Cut has never once run straight. It is the most dishonest stretch of water I know, and I love it dearly.

"This says—" Wrenn began.

"That map is wrong," I said pleasantly. "It's been wrong since before either of us was born. If you'd like to follow it, I'll follow you, and we can explain to the Captain together why his ledger is feeding the eels."

He looked at me for a long moment. I gave him my best apprentice face: honest, weary, cooperative, the face of a man whose whole life has been spent making other people's marks precise. It is astonishing how far a competent expression will carry you. Competence is the most trusted disguise there is.

"We go your way," he said finally. "Two men on you at all times."

"Of course," I agreed, and thought: *good, keep them close, keep them where the ground can hear them.*

Because here is the thing about the marsh that no requisition order captures: it keeps accounts. Every footstep is entered. The ground between the sluice and the alders is a lattice of peat cuttings flooded fifty years ago, floored with silt, roofed with reed-mat that has knit itself green and confident-looking. To a stranger it is a field. To me it was a page I had inked myself, and I knew precisely where the blank spaces were.

I led them down the left-hand branch, not the true one, because the left-hand branch passed close along the mat, and I wanted the soldiers bunched and heavy and walking where the water remembered being land but the land disagreed.

* * *

The fog met us properly at the alders.

It came up the channel the way tide comes, without malice, without hurry, folding around the trunks until the world contracted to perhaps twenty feet in any direction, then fifteen, then ten. Somewhere behind us the cart-horses began to complain in the flat, offended tone horses reserve for weather.

"Sergeant," one of the soldiers said, and his voice had gone thin. "Sergeant, I can't see the bank."

"The bank is under your boots, Fenn."

"Yes, sergeant. That's what worries me."

I stopped at the place I had chosen the night before, lying awake in my cell, drawing the route on the inside of my skull with the same care I had once given coastlines. Here. Where the reeds grew tall enough to swallow a man standing. Where the mat ran thin over the old cutting. Where, thirty paces north through the reeds, a sunken punt waited under a screen of rush, because Old Brannoc had put it there two nights ago without being asked, on the principle — his principle — that a ferryman always puts a boat where a man will need one, the same as God puts rain where crops do.

"The cases are here," I said, and pointed at the reed bed. "About four feet down. Mud below that, and below the mud, nothing anyone wants to find. I'll go in. You hold the rope."

We had a rope. They had insisted on it, which struck me as the single funniest thing the army had done all week: tethering the prisoner so he couldn't slip away, in a country where slipping away was not something a body did so much as something the marsh permitted, occasionally, as a favor.

Fenn held one end. Another soldier, a big quiet man called Dree, waded in after me to make sure I didn't dive for the cases and simply keep going. Good thinking, Dree. Correct in every respect except the water temperature, the visibility, and the ground.

I went in to my waist. The cold closed over me like a hand, and I gasped, honestly, and heard Wrenn say, satisfied, "That's cold enough to keep a man honest," and I thought: *you have no idea.*

I found the first case by feel — the leather strap Ellery himself had stitched, still firm after days in the water. I raised it dripping into the reeds, and the soldiers crowded to look, because men are men, and a box pulled from a marsh exerts a pull on every eye within a mile. Even in fog. Especially in fog; the fog gives you nothing else to look at.

"There's a second case," I said, sinking again. "Deeper. The current shifted it."

And while they leaned out over the water, all five of them now, weights gathered at the edge of a page I had drawn blank —

I let go of the rope.

Not dramatically. Nothing so legible as a struggle. I simply slipped under, as a man does when his boots lose their grip on silt, and came up three feet away in the reeds with only my face above water, and then moved — low, fast, on memory — along the channel I had swum as a boy, the one that runs behind the reed-screen where the mat holds a swimmer's weight if you spread yourself flat and let the water carry you.

Behind me, Dree went in to the chest, which was what the ground had been waiting for. Then Fenn, going to help him, found the second blank space. The shouting started almost at once, and it was remarkable how quickly it stopped sounding like orders and started sounding like boys calling for their mothers in a language the marsh does not speak.

I did not look back. I want that recorded. Whatever else I am — thief, liar, traitor to the crown by this morning's arithmetic — I did not stay to watch them founder, though I heard the rope go slack, and heard someone cry that the prisoner was gone, and heard Wrenn's voice, steady as a bell in fog, calling roll call in waist-deep water, counting his men by name because that was the only map he had left.

I have thought about that voice since. I suspect I will think about it again.

* * *

The punt was where Brannoc had left it, half-full of rain, pole laid across the thwarts. I bailed with my hat — my good hat, ruined now, one more item on the account the crown could bill me for — and poled north through channels that exist on no paper anywhere except the ones I had already smuggled out, which is a sentence I intend to enjoy for the rest of my life.

The fog worked for me now instead of against me. That is the reversal I had counted on: fog punishes men who need to see, and shelters men who already know. I poled by the feel of the current against the blade, by the taste of the water — peat here, clay there, the iron tang where the old cutting bled into the Sallow — by the sound of the reeds changing when the channel widened. Three years of drawing this place had taught me its grammar. The soldiers had brought a dictionary, and dictionaries are no use in conversation.

Twice I heard pursuit — splashing, cursing, the crack of a plank-road taking weight it hadn't agreed to. Both times I sat still among the reeds and let the fog decide whether I existed. Both times it decided I didn't. Mastery of a place, I understood then, is not a matter of owning it. Nobody owns the marsh; the marsh tolerates tenants. Mastery is knowing which questions it will answer and which it will decline.

The second case rode in the punt's bow, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a sin. Inside it were names — hundreds of them, families of the border folk, written small in margins by a dead man's hand so that no power could read them off the face of the earth. I had carried that weight once by accident, as an inheritance I hadn't asked for. I carried it now by choice, and it weighed less.

Somewhere behind me, the patrol was learning what I had learned at eleven years old with a soaked boot and my master laughing at me from the bank: that the marsh forgives nothing and forgets nothing, and that the only honest map of it is the one drawn with your own fear.

* * *

Brannoc waited at the far bank, exactly where he'd said he would, smoking a pipe that smelled as if it had been lit during some previous war and never extinguished.

"You're late," he said.

"I was retrieving stolen goods from a hostile army. Forgive the delay."

"Stolen goods." He looked at the case, then at me — soaked, hatless, shaking with cold or triumph, possibly both. "Ellery stole those names fair and square, boy. We're just moving house." He held out a blanket that had never in its life been clean and was nonetheless the finest garment I had ever worn. "Soldiers?"

"Foundering," I said. "Some of them may need pulling out. Wrenn — the sergeant — he kept his head. Counted his men aloud."

Brannoc considered this, puffing. "Then he's worth drowning slowly," he decided, "and no more than that. In?"

"In."

I took up the pole. Behind us the fog was already closing over our wake, stitching the water shut behind us like a seam, and ahead — north, past the last watched crossing, toward the border stone and the woman my promise had been waiting three years to reach — the current ran easy.

One case recovered. One still sunk, or claimed by the marsh, or already in a soldier's hands — I wouldn't know until I knew, and I refused, for one hour, one morning, to borrow that grief in advance.

Brannoc read my face anyway. Ferryman's privilege.

"The other case," he said. Not a question.

"If the marsh kept it, the marsh kept it safe. No one else knows where it lies now — not even me. The channel shifted it once. It can shift it again." I said it and realized, hearing my own voice, that I believed it. "But the names that matter most are in this one, Brannoc. Ellery wrote the elders first. The family heads. The tenure-records."

Something moved in the old man's face — not hope exactly; hope is younger than what moved in his face.

"Then pole, boy," he said quietly. "Pole like the whole valley's watching. Because there's a woman at the border stone who's been reading her father's silences for twenty years, and she'll want to see what he was too stubborn to send her alive."

I set the pole and pushed, and the marsh, in its own time, in its own indifferent way, let us go.
