## 20. Aftermath in Ink and Ash

They released me on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were dye days. In three years at the dye-house I had never once missed one, and it seemed fitting that the crown should hand me back to my life on the one morning my hands would be least useful to it — stained blue to the wrist, nails the color of a drowned sky.

The soldier at the garrison door handed me a folded paper instead of an apology. It was stamped twice and signed once, and it said, in the language of men who have never had to apologize for anything, that no charges would be pressed against me *at this time*. The last two words were doing all the work, the way a single thread holds up a hem.

"You're free to go," he said.

"Am I," I said.

He looked at my hands, then at my face, then decided he had somewhere else to be. Wise man. Everyone who has ever met me has eventually decided he had somewhere else to be.

I walked out into the square and the light hit me like a slap I'd been promised for weeks. I had expected the town to be different. It was different. That's the trouble with expectations; they're only ever wrong in the direction you didn't want.

* * *

Vellum's Reach was half-emptied and stirring, the way a fire stirs when you've thrown water on it but not enough. Doors stood open on rooms stripped bare. A woman I did not recognize was sweeping grit from a threshold that no longer belonged to anyone with the authority to complain about it. Down by the quay, the requisitioned houses along the river stood with their shutters hanging crooked, their gardens gone to seed, their windows holding nobody's reflection.

And everywhere, people. Not many. Enough.

They came out slowly, the way folk come out after a storm to assess what the river took. Old Pellier stood in his doorway with a hand pressed flat against the post, as if checking the building still meant him. The Widow Sarn had reopened her bread stall with exactly four loaves and a queue of forty, and was handling the arithmetic with the serenity of a woman who had survived two husbands and intended to survive a captain as well.

Nobody spoke to me at first. Then Jory Marta's boy — the cooper's son, fourteen, all elbows — broke from a knot of returned prisoners and came at me down the street, and for one absurd moment I thought I was about to be arrested again.

"Tamsin." He caught himself two steps short, suddenly shy, suddenly aware of the crowd watching. "You're out. They let you out."

"They let me out," I confirmed.

"We heard—" He stopped. Started again. "The ones who came back from Copper Lane. They said there was a mapmaker. An apprentice. He went to the garrison on his own feet and traded the atlas for us. All of us. Every name."

"He did," I said, because it was true, and because saying it out loud made it more true than it had been sitting inside me like swallowed glass.

"They say he gave up everything. His master's work. Three years of it. Just—handed it over."

"Yes."

Jory looked past me, toward the workshop above the map shop, its windows dark. "Why would anyone do that?"

I thought about telling him it was love. I thought about telling him it was duty. Both were true and neither was sufficient, the way a map is accurate and still not the country.

"Because he'd learned to read what maps are for," I said. "And Captain Vane never did."

That got passed down the street faster than bread at the Widow Sarn's stall, and by evening half the town believed I'd said something profound. Wonders will never cease.

* * *

The dye-house was just as I'd left it, which is to say the vats needed scrubbing, the ledgers needed faking — we had always kept two sets, one for the excise men and one for God — and Maret fell on me with both arms and then immediately with questions.

"Three weeks," she said, holding me at arm's length. "Three weeks they kept you, and you come back looking like you've been away overnight. What did they do to you?"

"They offered me tea," I said. "It was terrible tea. That was the worst of it."

Maret stared. "They say you sat across from Captain Vane himself."

"I did."

"And?"

"He was very reasonable. That's how you know to be afraid of a man, Maret. When he's reasonable. Angry men want things you can give them. Reasonable men want things you can't."

She crossed herself with a hand still smelling of mordant, which I have always felt was the appropriate way to be religious in a dye-house.

I worked until my arms ached, and the ache was a mercy. There is a particular kind of relief that hurts going down, like cold water on a burn, and I drowned myself in work rather than feel it properly. Because here is what I could not say to Jory or to Maret or to anyone: I had sat in that lamplit room and given Vane nothing, and I had walked out free, and Xanthe Crane had walked in on his own feet and given up everything, and the difference between us was not courage.

The difference was that I had been clever and he had been good, and only one of those things survives a lamplit room.

I had hidden half the copied names in bolt cloth bound for market towns. Half. I had kept the other half close, meaning to bargain with them if it came to it, meaning — some part of me, the part I don't like to introduce at parties — to survive. And Xanthe had carried his whole self into the garrison and set it on the table like a man laying down coins he knew were counterfeit to everyone but him.

Relief, I discovered, tastes bitter when you haven't earned it clean.

* * *

The prisoners came back over the following days in ones and twos, blinking, thinner, carrying their lives in kerchiefs. Copper Lane filled up again by half. The boy who had been taken — Fen, the one Xanthe taught to read compasses, though the town told it now as if he'd taught all the children in Vellum's Reach astronomy and languages besides, grief improves a story considerably — came back with his hands cracked from labor and his eyes older than his years.

Fen found me at the dye-house on my fourth day back. He didn't ask about the interrogation, which I was grateful for. Instead he asked:

"Is it true he's gone north? Over the border?"

"That's what they say."

"With her? With the cartographer's daughter?"

"With her," I said, "if the stories hold together, which stories mostly don't, but this one I'd wager on."

Fen turned a pebble under his boot, thinking. Then: "My da says a man who trades everything must have nothing left. But I don't think that's right. I think he traded everything because he'd finally worked out what he had."

Out of the mouths of boys who nearly got themselves taken. I gave him a copper for the honesty and he pretended not to pocket it, and we were both comfortable in the pretense.

* * *

I saw Vane once more, and I will set it down plainly because it deserves plainness.

It was market day, a week after my release, and the garrison had sent a clerk to the requisition office with a cart — inventorying, always inventorying, as if the town were a warehouse and its people so much stock. Vane came to supervise. He wore his best coat. He smiled at the Widow Sarn, who declined to smile back with a skill I intend to study.

I was near enough to hear the clerk say, "The atlas, sir? Where shall we log it?"

"File it with the sealed holdings," Vane said. "Standard classification."

"The marginalia, sir? The late cartographer's annotations? There's some question whether they contain—"

"They contain geography," Vane said, in a voice like a door closing. "Notes on coastlines and elevations. Of historical interest. File it."

The clerk, wisely, filed it.

And there it was, the whole thing laid bare in eleven words of conversation: power holding paper instead of people. Vane had the masterpiece of a dead man and the ruin of a live one, and he could read neither. Every name in those margins was meaningless to him — not hidden, merely illegible, which is worse. He had won the argument and lost the sentence it belonged to. He would sit in his office for years with Ellery's life's work in a drawer, occasionally taking it out and turning the pages like a man counting another man's money, and he would never once understand why the counting came up short.

I confess I enjoyed the watching of it more than a charitable woman should.

But here is the thing about watching your enemy fail: it doesn't fill the space where your friend used to be. The drawer closed. The cart rolled away. Vane mounted his horse and rode toward the garrison with his spine straight as a rule-line, and I stood in the market square among four loaves' worth of neighbors, holding my freedom like a garment someone else had paid for, and I thought: *he gets to keep the atlas and forget it, and I get to remember everything.*

Which of us was punished worse, I leave to the theologians.

* * *

That night I did something I had been avoiding since my release. I went up the outside stair to the workshop above the map shop.

The lock hung broken where the soldiers had forced it. Inside, the smell was the same — ink, size, tallow, the ghost of Master Ellery's pipe — and that smell undid me more thoroughly than any lamplit room had managed. They had searched it, of course. Drawers pulled, papers fanned, the great table scarred where someone had set a bayonet down without thinking. But they hadn't understood what they were looking at. Nobody had taken the pens, the blank sheets, the jars of ground lapis Xanthe had hoarded like a dragon. To a soldier, a workshop is furniture. To a craftsman, it's a language, and languages die only when the last reader does.

Xanthe's last ink pot still sat open at his place, dried to a black crust. Beside it, weighted under a stone, a sheet of paper.

Not a letter. A practice sheet — coordinates, trial flourishes, the skeleton of a coastline drawn and redrawn. And in the corner, small, the way apprentices sign things they don't expect anyone to read: *for T., if she comes looking.*

I sat down on Ellery's old stool and laughed until it turned into the other thing, and I let it, alone, in the dark, where nobody could report it.

Clever to the last, that man. Even his absence was addressed correctly.

* * *

Spring came early that year, or the town chose to believe it did, which amounts to nearly the same weather. The requisition orders were never formally withdrawn — Vane had traded his withdrawal at the border stone for the atlas, but paperwork, like dye, stains permanently — yet enforcement simply thinned, the way frost does. Soldiers stopped coming to Copper Lane. The clerk at the requisition office developed a remarkable difficulty reading his own lists. Empires retreat by inches, and every inch is somebody's garden.

The returned prisoners told the story of the trade at the border stone so many times it polished smooth, and in the polished version the apprentice stood tall against the sunset and the captain wept. The truer version, as I heard it from Fen's father, who heard it from a carter who had been hauling wool near the crossing: a thin man with ink-stained fingers had put a wrapped bundle on a boundary stone and stepped back, and said something nobody caught, and walked north, and that was all. No thunder. Maps rarely make noise when they change hands. That's rather the point of them.

I stayed at the dye-house. Maret made me partner, which she announced by shoving a second ledger across the table and saying, "Your handwriting's better than mine, and you've experience with forgery." I have never received a finer compliment.

And I waited. I told myself I wasn't waiting. I sorted cloth and cheated the excise men and taught Fen's sister her letters, and all the while one ear was cocked toward the north road the way a dog cocks an ear toward a door.

Because there was one thing the returned prisoners' stories never settled, one thread left dangling, and it worried at me like a loose warp. The atlas was worthless without the cipher key — Vane had filed it unread precisely because the key lived in a head now three counties away. Which meant the names were safe. Which meant the community across the border held its proof. Which meant, by any honest accounting, the story was finished.

So why couldn't I shake the feeling that it wasn't?

Then, on a bright loud morning in the third week of March, the Widow Sarn's grandson came pelting into the dye-house with his cap in his fist, out of breath, delighted, terrified, all the conditions of a boy bearing news.

"There's a rider at the gate," he gasped. "Army rider. Sealed packet. And Tamsin — it's got *your name* on it."

I wiped my hands on my apron. Blue to the wrist, always blue to the wrist.

"Well," I said. "Let's see what the crown wants with a dyer."
