## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The dead were easier to count than the living. Jonah Underhill had learned that in his first month at Wrenfield Manor, when the wagons came twice a day and the man who kept the regimental ledger — a captain's clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a talent for looking elsewhere — wrote down only the numbers that flattered the column he served. Forty-one men carried in from the ford at Marle. The clerk recorded twenty-six. The other fifteen had died on the road or in the yard or on the table where Jonah held the lantern, and those fifteen simply ceased to exist, as far as the Army of the Republic was concerned. They had never been wounded. They had never been carried up the gravel drive under the bare arms of the orchard trees. They had never been named.

Jonah had stood at the window of Ward Two and watched the clerk close his book with a small satisfied snap, like a man finishing a meal, and something in him had quietly and permanently changed.

He did not make a decision. He had learned long ago that decisions were loud things, and loud things got people noticed, and being noticed at Wrenfield was how you ended up reassigned to a plague depot or a penal labor company or a shallow grave behind the stables. What he did instead was smaller. That night, after lights-out, he knelt beside the bed in the narrow room he shared with two other nurses, pried up the floorboard at the corner nearest the wall where the plaster had crumbled away to brick, and laid inside it a schoolboy's exercise book he had bought three years earlier, before the war, for purposes he could no longer remember.

On its first used page, in his smallest hand, he wrote:

*Marle Ford, 14th October. Carried in: 41. Recorded: 26.*

*Unrecorded:* and then the names.

He knew all fifteen. He had asked each man his name while he packed the wounds or held the basin or sat through the last hour of the night shift doing the one thing no one trained you for and everyone needed. *Private Ellery Voss. Private Tam Wicker. Corporal Ansel Doyle.* He wrote them in pencil because pencil could be erased, though he knew even as he wrote it that he would never erase them. The erasing was the enemy's work. His was the keeping.

* * *

In the morning the war resumed its usual business of arriving at the door.

Wrenfield Manor stood on a low rise above the river meadows, a grey Georgian house with a portico too grand for its county and grounds gone half-wild since the army requisitioned it. Behind the house an orchard ran down the slope in untidy rows — apple, pear, a few stubborn quince — planted by some dead owner who had cared about symmetry and now neglected by a government that cared about nothing but throughput. In autumn the windfalls rotted sweet among the grass, and the smell drifted in through the open ward windows and mixed with carbolic and gangrene until the whole hospital breathed a single complicated air. The patients called it the orchard smell. Some of them said it was the last good thing they expected to know.

Jonah made his rounds. He had been a nurse for nine years, which meant he had learned the trade back when men became nurses only out of desperation or disgrace, and he had been neither; he had simply been a farm boy with steady hands and a horror of waste, and an old surgeon in a charity hospital had looked at those hands and said, *You'll do.* Now he was thirty-four, lean, unremarkable in a brown way — brown hair, brown coat, a face people described afterward as *the nurse's* without being able to add anything to it. He considered this his uniform. He had polished it deliberately, the way another man might polish buttons.

"Underhill." Sister Brack, the matron, stood at the end of Ward Three with her list. She was not cruel. Cruelty required attention, and Sister Brack's attention was fully mortgaged to efficiency. "The new convalescents go in here. Six of them. The major goes in the small room off the east corridor."

"Major?" They didn't put majors in ward beds. Majors got rooms.

"Aldous Vane. Wounded hand, some business at a place called Harrow Bend. The colonel wants him comfortable." She said the name the way she read a supply inventory, and moved on, and Jonah wrote nothing down because there was nothing yet to write, only a place name passing through his ears like a bird through an empty room. Harrow Bend. He would think of it later, and wish he had caught it then, held it, looked at it properly. But you could not look at everything. That was the arithmetic of the place: you saved your looking for the ones still breathing.

The six new convalescents came up from the yard on stretchers and one of them, a boy of maybe nineteen with a bandaged throat, gripped Jonah's wrist as they lifted him and mouthed something with no sound in it. Later, when the morphine had him, Jonah leaned close and heard him working at the same word over and over, a coin worrying a pocket: *Milo. Milo. Milo.*

"Your name's Milo?" Jonah asked.

The boy's eyes opened partway. There was a long pause, the length of a man deciding whether anything was safe.

"Rell," he said. "Milo Rell. Don't let them lose it."

"I won't," Jonah said, and meant it in the way he meant everything now, which was privately and completely and with no evidence anyone would ever be permitted to see.

That night the floorboard came up again. *Milo Rell,* he wrote. *Throat wound. Expected to live. Recorded in regimental ledger: yes.* He paused. It was strange to write a name that the official record already held; the ledger under the floorboard existed for the others, the struck-through ones, the vanished. But he wrote Milo Rell anyway, and beneath it: *Asked me not to let them lose it.* Because the boy had asked, and because Jonah was beginning to understand that his book was not really about correcting columns. It was about the asking.

* * *

The weeks shortened toward winter. The wagons kept coming. The orchard went gold, then bare, then white, and the wards filled and emptied and filled again, and Jonah's book grew fat in the dark under the corner floorboard.

By December it held more than two hundred names. Some were soldiers whose deaths had gone unwritten; more, lately, were civilians, because the war had entered its late stage, the ugly arithmetic stage, where armies stopped pretending the difference between combatant and noncombatant mattered to anyone but the clerks. A miller from Osterlin. Four children with one surname — Dace — struck from a casualty annex because, the clerk had explained to the surgeon with a shrug, *the annex covers military losses only,* as if the children had been killed by something other than shellfire, as if the shell had checked their papers first.

Jonah copied every name within hours of hearing it, sometimes within minutes, kneeling in his room with a stub of candle shielded by his cupped hand, writing so small the letters were nearly braille. He had systems. He always would have systems; invisibility was a craft, and craft meant method. He never wrote during daylight. He never wrote the same night a search had been announced. He kept the pencil sharpened to a sliver so the sound of it never carried. He memorized the page he was on so that if the book were taken he could reproduce the last week from memory, though he knew — he was honest enough with himself to know — that memory alone would fail him eventually, that the whole point of paper was that it remembered better than flesh.

What he did not have was a plan. The book was not building toward anything. It was not going to be published or presented or smuggled across a border; he had no border to smuggle it across, no capital that wanted it, no tribunal waiting for evidence. The war would end someday, probably, the way wars did, badly and then suddenly, and someone would write the official history, and it would be built out of ledgers exactly like the clerk's, with their small satisfied snaps, and the two hundred names under the floorboard would stay under the floorboard until the house fell down or he did.

And still he wrote. He had stopped asking himself why, mostly. On bad nights he suspected it was the simplest thing about him: a farm boy's horror of waste, extended at last to human material. Names were a crop. You did not let a harvest rot in the field because the market had decided not to buy.

"Underhill." Dr. Pemm, the senior surgeon, found him at the dispensary shelf, counting out lint. Pemm was sixty, gouty, and had survived three armies by treating all of them as weather. "You're wanted in the east corridor. The major's hand has turned."

Jonah gathered the tray. The major's room smelled of brandy and clean linen and, underneath, the particular sweetness he had learned to dread. Aldous Vane lay propped against pillows with the easy grace of a man who made even fever look composed. He was perhaps forty, handsome in a way that photographs would have loved, his unwounded hand resting on the counterpane like a thing posed there. The injured left hand was swaddled to the fingers. Two orderlies hovered uselessly near the wall.

"You're the quiet one," Vane said, before Jonah had set the tray down. His voice was warm. It was a voice built for mess halls and promotion boards, a voice people followed into things. "Sister tells me you don't talk. I like that. I'm talked at all day by men who have nothing to say." He smiled, and the smile included Jonah the way lamplight includes a chair. "They tell me the hand may go."

"The hand may go," Jonah agreed. He began unwrapping the dressing.

It was a bad wound, poorly debrided somewhere forward, and it had turned in the way Pemm had meant. Jonah worked without speaking. Vane watched him, and Jonah felt the watching the way you feel weather change — not hostile, merely attentive, a man cataloguing his surroundings out of lifelong habit.

"Harrow Bend," Vane said conversationally, while Jonah lifted the ruined fingers and assessed what would have to come off. "You've heard of it?"

"No, sir."

"No? It'll be in the papers soon. Good news for once. We cleared a hornet's nest." Something crossed the handsome face — not guilt; Jonah would spend many nights failing to name it precisely, and would settle finally on *satisfaction rehearsing itself as reluctance*. "Cost us, though. These village actions are the dirtiest work there is. Nobody writes poems about them." He flexed his right hand on the coverlet. "Well. You'd know about costs better than I would. You keep the tally, I imagine."

"I keep the patients, sir," Jonah said.

Vane laughed, delighted. "Keep the patients, he says. Sister Brack is wasted on you, Underhill. There should be a dozen of you." He submitted to the redressing with the mild patience of a much-admired man enduring a minor inconvenience. At the door, with the fresh bandage already browning at its center, he added, pleasantly, as an afterthought:

"There was a girl from Harrow Bend, we think, headed this direction. A child. If she turns up anywhere on your rounds—" the smile again, brief, reasonable, "—you'll send word to Captain Lassiter at division. Nothing sinister. She's a stray, and strays need accounting. That's all any of this ever is, Underhill. Accounting."

The door closed. Jonah stood a moment with his hands still smelling of the major's wound, and the word *Harrow Bend* turned over once in his mind and settled, like sediment.

* * *

That night the snow came early and heavy, closing the roads, and the wards went quiet in the way Jonah liked best — the deep quiet of men sleeping because they had earned it. He made his final round at eleven, adjusted a drain, wetted a cracked lip, and stood a moment at the window of Ward Two looking down at the orchard, where the snow was filling the tree rows so evenly it seemed to be settling a debt. Then he went to his room, knelt at the corner board, and brought the book up into the candlelight.

He turned past the miller, past the Dace children, past two hundred small careful entries, looking — he did not know why until his hand stopped — for Harrow Bend. There was nothing. Of course there was nothing; no casualties from Harrow Bend had ever passed through Wrenfield, no clerk had ever snapped a book shut over them, because whatever had happened at Harrow Bend appeared to have produced, in the entire machinery of the Republic's records, not one number at all.

A village action, Vane had called it. Cleared a hornet's nest. Good news for once.

And a missing girl, a stray, needing only accounting — with the account, somehow, already closed before she was found.

Jonah sat back on his heels. Below the candle's circle the book lay open, patient and full. He thought of Major Vane's hand resting on the counterpane like a posed thing, and of the warmth in the voice, and of the fact that a decorated officer with a wound worth a medal had been sent to convalesce in a requisitioned manor in a backwater county — sent, in effect, to be admired — in the same season that a village thirty miles upriver had ceased to generate paperwork.

He did not write anything that night. For the first time since the floorboard first came up, he had nothing to enter, and the absence frightened him worse than any entry ever had, because for the first time the blankness was not neglect.

It was design.

He slid the book back into its cavity, pressed the board home with his palm, and blew out the candle. Through the wall he could hear the east corridor, faintly: footsteps, a laugh, the murmur of a charming man being agreeable to an orderly at midnight.

Three days later, at dusk, in the sleet and the last light, an aide in a colonel's uniform drove a cart up the service road to the manor's side door, set a mud-streaked child down on the step as one sets down a parcel, handed Jonah a sealed note, and rode back into the weather without waiting for a receipt.
