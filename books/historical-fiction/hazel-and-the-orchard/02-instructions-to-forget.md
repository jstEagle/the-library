## 2. Instructions to Forget

The rain came in off the orchard in long grey sheets, and with it came the horse.

Jonah heard it before he saw it — the wet slap of hooves on the gravel path that ran from the manor's front steps down toward the road. He was on his knees in the linen closet, counting the bandages that would not be enough for the week, and the sound pulled him upright the way certain sounds always did: a wagon too heavy, a gate too slow, anything that meant more bodies arriving.

He set down the ledger of supplies — the honest one, the one the officers read — and went to the window.

A single rider. Regimental grey, collar up against the rain, and behind his saddle a shape so small and hunched that Jonah's mind refused it for a moment, tried to make it a bundle, a sack of grain, anything but what it was.

He was down the stairs before he'd decided to go.

The front hall of Wrenfield Manor had been built for a different century's purposes: tall windows, a marble floor gone cold as river stone, a chandelier that no one had lit since the army arrived because candle wax was requisitioned too. Jonah crossed it with his wet-printed footprints and pulled open one leaf of the great double doors.

The aide was already dismounting. Young, dry under an oilcloth cape, the kind of face that hadn't learned yet to hide what it thought of its errands. He looked at the hospital, then at the rain, then at Jonah's apron, and made his calculation quickly.

"You the one in charge of admissions?"

"I'm the nurse on duty. Major Hobbes is the physician. If there's a case—"

"There's no case." The aide reached behind the saddle and lifted the bundle down, and the bundle resolved, horribly, into a girl. Six years old, perhaps seven. Mud to her knees, mud on her hands, a smear of it dried along her jaw like a second skin. She wore a man's coat cut down badly at the shoulders, and she did not cry, did not shiver, did not do any of the things a child does when she is cold and wet and has been riding for hours. She simply stood where she was put, the way a post stands.

Jonah crouched to her level. "Hello."

Nothing. Not shyness — he knew shyness, saw it every week when village children were brought in to sit with wounded fathers. This was something else. Her eyes went through him, past him, fixed on some point in the middle distance where nothing was, and stayed there.

"She doesn't talk," the aide said. "Or she won't. We've had her four days." He said it the way you'd report a lame mule.

"Where did she come from?"

The aide swung back up into the saddle before answering, which told Jonah he was not supposed to answer. But he was young, and the rain made men generous, or careless, and he reached inside his cape and produced an envelope. Sealed. The wax carried no crest, which was itself a kind of message.

"From Colonel Merrick's office. You're to give it to whoever keeps your records." His eyes flicked to Jonah's hands, ink-stained at the fingers even now, and something in them sharpened slightly, then let go. "Read it and forget it. That's the whole instruction, Nurse. Forget the face. Ask nothing. She eats, she sleeps, she stays out of the reports. If anyone comes asking after her — and no one will — you never saw her delivered."

"Who is she?"

The aide gathered his reins. For half a second his composure cracked, and underneath it Jonah saw something that might have been nausea, or might have been the memory of four days in a camp with a silent child.

"Harrow Bend," he said. "That's all it says where she's from, and I'll thank you not to write it down."

Then he was gone, hooves throwing gravel, the grey of him swallowed by the grey of the morning, and Jonah stood in the doorway holding an envelope that was already soaking through, with a child beside him who weighed nothing and said nothing and smelled of woodsmoke and river mud.

* * *

Harrow Bend.

He turned the name over while he walked her inside, while he sat her by the kitchen fire and put bread and drippings in her hands, which she held without eating. Harrow Bend. He knew the name the way you know a word you've read once and can't place. It was on the tip of every map of the eastern valley. It was a market village, a bridge over a slow brown river, a church with a squat tower. He had never seen it in a report.

And Jonah read everything. That was the thing about him that his colleagues mistook for diligence and his superiors mistook for ambition and that was, in truth, neither. Fourteen months at Wrenfield had taught him the architecture of the paperwork: how casualty lists flowed down from the front like water through channels, getting thinner and cleaner as they went; how a regiment's returns would arrive at the manor trailing corrections; how certain names acquired a strange habit of disappearing between the field copy and the fair copy. He had started keeping his own list eleven months ago, in a hand smaller than his natural one, written at night by candle stub. A lieutenant struck from the rolls after Kessel Ford. Two brothers, both dead at Pike Rill, entered in the official record as one man. A woman shot on her own doorstep at Aldermeer, recorded as collateral, unnumbered.

Forty-three names now. He knew each one. And in fourteen months of reading every scrap of paper that crossed Wrenfield, Harrow Bend had appeared exactly nowhere. No garrison returns. No requisitions drawn on its granary. No casualties. Nothing. A village of several hundred souls, sitting on a bridge the army needed, and the war's entire apparatus of record-keeping had stepped around it the way a man steps around a body in the road.

The envelope had gone soft in the rain. He opened it carefully, at his desk in the admissions room, with the door shut.

*To the officer administering Wrenfield Hospital:*

*The bearer of this note is delivered into your care without regimental status. She is not to be entered on any roll. She is not to be interviewed by chaplains, journalists, or Ministry personnel. Her presence is a humanitarian charge only.*

*You are instructed to forget the face. Ask nothing. Compliance is expected.*

*— By order, Col. E. Merrick, Eastern Command*

There was no signature line filled in beneath the printed name. There was no reason given. There was, Jonah noticed, sitting very still with the paper trembling faintly in his hand, no mention anywhere of how the child had come to be motherless, fatherless, mute, and important enough to bury in paperwork.

Forget the face. As if a face could be forgotten by order. As if the orders were the problem — as if some clerk somewhere believed that the whole machinery of the army ran on remembering, and that a single line of instruction could unremember a girl.

He folded the note along its crease. Then he did the other thing, the thing he had done forty-three times before, the thing that had stopped feeling like courage months ago and had become simply what his hands did. He took a fresh slip of paper and wrote:

*Delivered this day to Wrenfield by aide of Col. Merrick's staff. Girl, approx. 6 yrs. Mute. Sole stated survivor, Harrow Bend. No report of Harrow Bend exists in any record at this manor. Instruction attached: forget the face, ask nothing.*

He signed it with no name at all — a dash, a date — and folded it in among the others, and sat for a long moment afterward listening to the rain.

Sole survivor. Of what? There was no battle in the valley. There had been no battle in the valley since the spring, and none here. Villages did not produce sole survivors by accident. Sole survivors came from fires, from floods, from plague ships —

From firing lines.

The thought arrived fully formed and cold, the way the truest thoughts do, and he hated it immediately, and he could not unhave it.

* * *

She would not go to the children's ward. He understood why within an hour of trying: the ward held thirty beds, most of them occupied by men missing arms or lungs or the ability to sleep quietly, and the noise of it — groans, calls for mothers long dead, the particular wet rattle that meant the week was claimed — would have been punishment to anyone, let alone a child who had already heard whatever she had heard. So he broke a rule he didn't remember writing and installed her instead in the small linen room adjoining Ward Six, on a cot between shelves of folded sheets, where the smell was lavender and soap and nothing died.

Marisol Quill brought blankets without being asked. She was the groundskeeper's daughter and the manor's unofficial cook, a broad-faced, quick-eyed woman who moved through the hospital's corridors carrying soup and judgment in equal measure, and who had, in fourteen months, asked Jonah precisely zero questions about anything. She laid the blankets over the girl, tucked them with a briskness that was almost fierce, and studied her for a moment.

"Whose is she?"

"No one's," Jonah said. "Now ours, I suppose."

"Mm." Marisol straightened. "She hasn't said a word?"

"Not one."

"Well." Marisol brushed the girl's hair back from her forehead with two fingers, gently, the way you'd touch something you weren't sure wouldn't break. "Some words cost too much for a while. She'll spend when she's ready." And she left, and the girl watched the door close behind her, and then watched Jonah, and then watched nothing again.

He tried, over the following days, in the gaps between his rounds, everything his limited imagination offered. Food first — she ate when no one watched, he discovered by leaving and returning quickly, small fast bites like an animal at a trapline. Then warmth, quiet, routine: he gave her the task of folding the smallest linens, and her hands did it neatly, mechanically, well. He spoke to her constantly, a running low commentary on dressings and dosages and the weather, not because he thought she listened but because silence shared is still company. Once, reading aloud a letter to a dying corporal in Ward Six — the man's sister describing an apple harvest — Jonah glanced up and found the girl standing in the doorway, utterly still, watching the corporal's face with an attention that frightened him. Not grief. Recognition. As if she were checking something. Counting.

On the fifth night he found her awake, sitting up on the cot in the dark, and instead of sending her back down he sat on the floor beside her with his back against the shelving, and the two of them listened together to the wind working through the orchard, the bare branches knocking like polite fists on the shutter of the world.

"You're safe here," he said, which was a lie of the ordinary kind, the kind nurses tell.

She turned her head and looked at him. In the dark her eyes were just glints, but he felt the look land anyway, felt its weight and its terrible steadiness, and he understood suddenly that she was not broken in the way the doctors would have said, not stunned into emptiness. Something in her was intact and waiting, coiled around whatever she had seen, keeping it the way he kept his names — hidden, guarded, alive.

She had been ordered forgotten by a colonel. Which meant someone above that colonel believed she had seen something worth forgetting. Which meant the thing she carried in her silence was dangerous to powerful men.

Which meant, Jonah thought, his stomach turning slowly over, that the most dangerous object in Wrenfield Manor was seven years old and sleeping between shelves of pillowcases.

He got up, lit no candle, and went back to his rooms. From beneath the third floorboard by the window he took out his ledger — the real one, the growing one — and read through the forty-three names by feel rather than light, his finger moving down the columns like a man taking inventory of a debt. Then, below the last entry, in his smallest hand, he added a forty-fourth line that was not quite a name:

*Harrow Bend. Number unknown. Witness: one child.*

He slid the board back into place and lay down and did not sleep, and near dawn he heard, faintly through the wall, a sound he had never heard from her before — not a word, nothing so large as that. Just a hum. A small, tuneless, persistent hum, the sound of a child keeping herself company in the dark, and it went on for a long time, and it was somehow worse than weeping would have been.

Three days later, a carriage came up the gravel through the thinning rain, flying the pennant of Eastern Command, and Jonah, watching from the dispensary window, saw the officer step down into the courtyard: tall, handsome, grey-gloved to the elbow, smiling at the commandant's wife before his boots had even settled.

Major Aldous Vane had come to Wrenfield to convalesce.
