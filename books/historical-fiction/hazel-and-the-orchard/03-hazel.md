## 3. Hazel

The door of the world is brown and it has a latch and the latch has a sound like a tooth coming out.

Before the door there was a cart. Before the cart there was the square. Before the square—

Stop. That is the wrong direction. Faces come before doors but faces are the thing you do not keep.

*Forget the face,* the paper man said, or the boot man said, or the air said. Forget is a word shaped like a hand pushing. Hazel knows how to be pushed. She has been pushed through three counties and one river and one night so dark it had a texture, like wet wool on her eyes.

So she forgets.

She forgets with her whole body. She forgets so well that when the tall nurse with the tired hands bends down to look at her she can say nothing, absolutely nothing, because saying would be remembering and she has been instructed. Orders are orders. Even orphans know that.

The tall nurse smells of soap that has given up. Underneath it: smoke, but old smoke, smoke from long ago, smoke baked into him the way dirt bakes into knees. He does not ask questions. This is the first good thing Wrenfield does.

He gives her bread with the crusts still on.

* * *

Bread in the belly changes things. Bread says: you will be here tomorrow, because tomorrow you will be hungry again and here is where the bread lives.

Hazel counts the rooms by their smells. The ward where men lie in rows smells of carbolic and blood gone quiet. The corridor smells of floor polish fighting a losing war against the same old smoke. The kitchen is a country all its own — onions, scorched milk, the iron breath of the big stove. She learns the kitchen fastest because kitchens speak her language. Kitchens say: sit here. Kitchens say: hold this. Nobody needs you to talk in a kitchen.

The woman who runs the kitchen has flour to her elbows and eyes that have seen everything twice. She does not say *poor lamb*. Hazel has been *poor lamb*-ed at by eleven women in three counties and one of them cried on her and the crying was heavy, it was weather, it was a rain she could not stand up under. This woman only puts a bowl in front of her and keeps working and hums something low and off-key, and the humming fills the room the way water fills a cup, all the way to the edges, without asking anything of anyone.

Hazel eats. The spoon is tin and dented on one side. She turns the dent toward herself. It fits her mouth better that way.

* * *

The tall nurse moves through the wards like a man walking through his own thoughts.

Hazel follows him.

Not at first on purpose. First it is because he is the one with the bread. Then it is because he goes places and the going is interesting and standing still is where the badness catches up with you — she learned that on the road, that stillness is a smell, that if you stop moving too long in a doorway or a market square someone's head will turn toward you like a weathervane finding wind.

But after a while it is on purpose.

Because he does something with his face.

When the officers come through — boots, belts, jingling like church but wrong — the tall nurse makes his face into a window. Nothing behind the glass. You could look and look and see nothing but ward and bedsheets and duty, duty, duty. And then the boots go away and for one blink the window becomes a door again and there is a person behind it, quick and burning, before the glass slides back.

Hazel knows that trick.

She invented it herself, or her mother did, or every small person in every bad year invents it fresh, the way spiders remake webs. You make yourself into a thing people look *past*. A stool. A bundle. A shadow that belongs to the wall. It works best if you are small. She is very good at small.

So she watches him watch the boots, and neither of them says anything about it ever, and this is how they become friends, though no one would be able to say when.

* * *

His rounds have a shape. Shapes are safe. She learns the shape.

Morning: he starts at the far end of Ward One and moves bed to bed, and at each bed he stops and does not hurry, even when there are forty more beds, even when the bell of duty is ringing somewhere. He touches wrists. He speaks low. Some of the men he speaks to cannot answer anymore but he speaks anyway, and their chests rise differently under the blankets after, like something in them heard.

Midday: bandages. The unwinding of a bandage is a story being told backwards. Hazel stands by the wall and reads the stories. Pink wound, angry wound, wound gone quiet as a pond. He never flinches. Once a corporal grabs his wrist mid-unwinding and squeezes until the nurse's knuckles whiten, and the nurse lets him squeeze, and says, "Go on, take what you need," and the man's grip loosens by degrees like a rope paying out, and afterward neither of them mentions it.

Evening: lamp-lighting, water-changing, and then the slow last pass down the rows where the dying ones lie. He lingers longest there. At each bed his lips move sometimes, barely, and Hazel, pressed into the shadow of the linen cupboard, reads his lips the way she used to read her father's across the square when the mill noise was up:

A name. Each time, a name. Different every time. Spoken like a coin laid gently on a table.

She starts keeping them. Not meaning to. Names stick to her like burrs. Her mother said once, exasperated, fond: *You'll remember everything, Hazel Wren, and it'll be the ruin of you.* Well. Here it is, the ruin, arriving early, wearing a nurse's apron and smelling of surrendered soap.

* * *

The trouble with names is they want friends.

One name leads to a face. A face leads to a voice. A voice leads to the square, and she has instructions about the square.

But the faces of Wrenfield are different. These are new faces, wounded faces, faces that arrived after, faces that were not there. She checks each one against the list in her chest, the list she must not have, and none of them match, and each non-match is a small mercy she did not know she was asking for.

Except.

There is a man in the side room, the private room off Ward Two, the one with flowers somebody keeps bringing. He has a bandaged hand wrapped up white as a bishop's collar. He has a laugh you can hear through walls, warm as a hearth, and everyone lights up when he laughs, even the men with holes in them; laughter must be rationed in a place like this and he spends his freely.

Hazel sees him in the corridor one morning, sitting in the good chair by the window, holding court. His boots are polished. His uniform hangs like it was poured on him. When he smiles at the matron she flushes like a girl.

And then he reaches for his teacup with the bandaged hand and the sleeve shifts back and there it is, at the wrist, under the clean white edge:

Grey. Wool. Glove.

The square comes back all at once, the way it always does, sideways and all together: the well. The smoke making its slow grey rope into the sky. Neighbors kneeling. The sound — not shots first, first the *order*, a voice like a door closing, and then the shots, and the pigeons going up all at once like the square itself had flinched.

And a glove. Grey wool. Raised, level, unhurried. The way a man signals a coach to halt.

Hazel's legs take her backward into the alcove before her mind catches up. Stool. Bundle. Shadow that belongs to the wall. The officer — the patient — the laughing man — glances down the corridor, and his eyes pass over her the way light passes over dust on a windowsill, and move on.

She forgets her own breathing for a while. That is a skill too.

* * *

That night she cannot sleep in the cot in the attic room that smells of apples and mouse. The dark has the texture again, the wet-wool texture, and behind the wool the square waits, patient as wells are patient.

There is charcoal in the schoolroom basket. She saw it days ago and filed it away, because filing things away is what she does instead of speaking.

She takes the stubs downstairs barefoot, past the sleeping house, past the kitchen where the banked stove ticks like a cooling engine, into the ward corridor where one lamp burns low over the nurses' desk. Paper is locked up — she tried drawers already, another night, another failure — but the backs of the daily lists hang in a stack on a nail, spent, already read, marked *void* in someone's tidy hand.

Void. Blank enough.

She kneels on the floor with the paper flat and draws the way water finds drains: fast, downhill, certain.

First the square itself, the stones, the fountain with the lion's tongue worn off. Then the well, because the well is the middle of everything, the well is the sun this picture orbits. Then the people. She does their kneeling shapes carefully, because kneeling was most of what happened; kneeling and waiting and the rope of smoke. She gives them their faces — Mr. Tallow the smith with his beard like a shoebush, the priest, the twins from the bakery, her mother's particular shoulders, her mother's particular hands crossed at the back —

Her hand shakes and she lets it shake. Shaking is allowed. Shaking is not speaking.

Then the soldiers. Row of them, rifles, helmets, all the same, drawn the same, because that day they were the same, interchangeable as teeth in a gear. She does not bother with their faces. Their faces were not the point.

The point is the glove.

She draws it big. Bigger than correct. Grey wool, cuff, the raised arm implied in two hard lines, the finger extended — pointing, or halting, or choosing, she never saw which, she was behind the trough with her hands over her ears and her eyes doing the thing eyes do when they cannot stop. The glove is the biggest thing in the picture because in her chest it is still the biggest thing in the world.

Smoke last. Charcoal smudged soft with the heel of her hand, a rope of it climbing off the top edge of the page into nowhere, because that smoke went up and up and never came down anywhere, and nobody wrote it anywhere, and that is the part that sits in her throat like a swallowed stone.

She is done. The stone is still there. Drawing gets it out of the chest but not out of the body; it just relocates the weight, chest to paper, which is at least a weight you can fold up and hide under a mattress, which she does, behind a loose slat, next to the mouse smell, where things that are true can wait.

* * *

In the morning the tall nurse finds the drawing.

Not hidden-drawn. She leaves the stack of void lists on the desk corner, hers on top, face up. Because — she thinks it through while eating her porridge, the dent of the spoon turned inward — because he writes names at night when he thinks no one sees, she has watched his lips do it, and a person who writes names might be a person who looks at pictures properly.

He picks it up on his way past the desk. She watches from the kitchen doorway with her bowl, not breathing much.

He stops.

She sees his face do a thing. Not the window. Not the door. Something else — a crack running through both, glass faulting from corner to corner, quiet, structural. He looks at the drawing for a long time. Longer than a nurse looks at paper. His thumb comes to rest on the smudged rope of smoke, right where it climbs off the page, and stays there.

Then he raises his eyes, slow, over the top edge of the paper, and finds her in the doorway.

Everyone looks at Hazel. Looking is nothing; she has been looked at by customs men and quartermasters and women who wanted to cry on her, and looking has never once required an answer.

But this is not looking. This is him seeing her the way she saw him — window, door, and now whatever is behind both — and his eyes go from her face to the drawing and back, and his mouth opens on a question she can see forming before any words come, the question everyone eventually asks, the question with instructions attached:

And Hazel, who has been told to forget, who has forgotten so well she has left a whole village lying on a nurse's desk in charcoal — Hazel looks at Major Aldous Vane's drawing of a grey glove, then at the tall nurse's cracked-open face, and shakes her head once, slowly, and puts one finger against her own lips.

Because the man with the bandaged hand is coming down the corridor, laughing, and his grey-gloved wrist is swinging at his side like a pendulum counting something down.
