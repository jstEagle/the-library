## 4. The Grey Glove

The officers' ward occupied the east wing, where the sun came through windows that had never been boarded and the beds were set wider apart, as though rank required room to breathe. I had worked the east wing exactly twice since arriving at Wrenfield, both times to fill in for a quartermaster nurse down with fever, and both times I had kept my eyes on my work and my mouth shut and left within the hour. It was not a place for a man like me. Men like me belonged in Ward Six, where the dying had no one to impress.

So it was pure accident — a shortage of clean linen, nothing more — that put me in the east wing on the morning Major Aldous Vane decided to notice me.

He was propped against his pillows reading a newspaper three days old, his left hand swaddled and resting on a board across his lap like something borrowed from another man. He was younger than I expected. Thirty-five perhaps, maybe less, with the kind of face that war photographs flatter without trying: square-jawed, steady-eyed, a scar through one eyebrow that he'd earned, according to the orderlies, holding a bridge at Cassel Ford while half his company fell around him. The men adored him. Even the wounded from other regiments asked after him. *How's the Major? Is the Major mending?* As though his recovery were a shared project, something the whole hospital could take pride in.

"Underhill," he said, when I brought his water basin. He said it before I'd given my name. "You're the quiet one."

"Sir?"

"The quiet one. The one who doesn't talk." He smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated, the smile of a man who had learned exactly how much of it to deploy in any given room. "I like that in a nurse. The chatty ones make my hand ache." He flexed the bandaged fingers slightly and winced on purpose, so I would know he was joking. "How is it today, then?"

I looked at the wound beneath the dressing. A sabre cut or shrapnel — the report said shrapnel, though reports lie by reflex — running across the back of the hand, healed angry but healing. "Well enough, sir. Another week of dressings and you'll want the surgeon's opinion about the stiffness."

"Stiffness. Yes." He watched me change the dressing with an attention I found difficult to name. Not vanity. Something more like appraisal — the way a man reads a map for roads he might need later. "You've done this before. Field hospitals?"

"Some."

"Harrow way?" He said it lightly, tossing it out like a coin. "I heard you served up north."

The linen in my hands did not tremble, because I made it not tremble. "Various places, sir."

"Various places." He nodded slowly, as if filing the phrase somewhere. "Well. You're wasted in Six, Underhill. All those poor devils, and none of them well enough to appreciate discretion." The smile again, warmer now, aimed directly at me. "A man who knows how to keep quiet is worth ten who can't."

I thanked him and gathered the basin and left, and my pulse didn't settle until I was down the service stairs with the door closed behind me.

That night I took up the floorboard in the linen closet and read what Hazel had drawn, though I no longer needed to. I had been carrying it in my head for days, the way you carry a splinter too deep to pull: the village square rendered in a child's charcoal, the crooked well, the doors standing open, the soldiers in a line with their rifles up, and above them all, larger than any face, a single grey glove, raised, about to fall.

I had assumed the glove meant an officer she had seen from a distance. Officers wore grey gloves the way rain wears clouds; there must be hundreds within twenty miles of Wrenfield. But distance narrows things. A child sees the glove because the glove is what moves. The rest is uniform. The glove is the part that gives orders.

Now the officer attached to one particular grey glove was sleeping thirty feet above my ward, beloved by every man in the building, and he knew my name.

* * *

I want to be honest about how recognition actually arrives, because the accounts I have read — the few there are — always describe it as thunderclap, revelation, the scales falling. For me it arrived as arithmetic.

Three days after the dressing change, I was in the records room, ostensibly restocking admission forms, actually copying a supply requisition into memory for later transcription. The manor kept regimental paperwork in a locked cabinet I could not open, but casualty summaries circulated loose, and officers' convalescence files were stacked on the open shelving like laundry waiting to be folded. I had thumbed past the Vane file a dozen times. That evening I stopped thumbing.

*Aldous R. Vane, Major.* Distinguished Service Cross, Cassel Ford. Commendation, two citations, a mention in dispatches. And then, further down, in the flat bureaucratic language that does the work of erasure without ever appearing to do anything at all:

*Detached duty, southern sector, October–November.* During which period, per the movement logs I had spent months memorizing, three villages in the river valley went from inhabited to unmentioned. Harrow Bend among them. The same weeks Hazel appeared mud-streaked at our door with a note instructing us to forget her face.

There was no order in the file. Of course there wasn't. Orders that need recording are orders that expect to survive scrutiny, and this one had been written to leave no shadow on paper. But the dates aligned the way bones align in a hand — you don't see the proof, you see the structure underneath, and once you have seen it you cannot stop seeing it.

I stood in the records room with the file open and understood, in the calmest possible voice inside my own skull, that the man whose dressing I had changed had emptied a village. That the glove in Hazel's drawing fit him. That when he raised that bandaged hand to wave at the orderlies in the corridor — and they waved back, every one, grinning — he was performing the same gesture the charcoal had frozen forever.

I put the file back precisely where it had been, squared its corners with the others because my hands wanted occupation, and walked back to Ward Six past the chapel, where someone was playing the harmonium badly, and I thought: he has a scar from holding a bridge. Men died around him and he held. That is what courage looks like to the people writing commendations.

It had apparently looked like something else at Harrow Bend.

* * *

After that, I began to watch him the way I imagined a debt collector watches a debtor — patiently, without appetite, noting everything.

Vane took his meals in the small dining room with the other ambulatory officers, but twice a week he walked the wards. This was, I learned, a habit the whole hospital spoke of fondly. He moved bed to bed with jokes and cigarettes and a memory for names and regiments and wives' Christian names, and the men lit up like lamps when he came near. *Major remembered my brother. Major asked after the harvest at home.* He distributed morale the way other men distributed rations, carefully, everyone getting some.

And he was good at it. That was the thing I could not get around. If I had met him at a dinner table, if Hazel had never come to my door, if the ledger under my floorboard contained only fevers and gangrene and honest deaths — I would have liked him. I might even have trusted him. Charm of that quality is indistinguishable from decency until you are given a reason to check the arithmetic.

On the fourth of his ward walks, he reached me at the far end of Six, where Corporal Ennis lay sleeping off morphine.

"Underhill." He lowered his voice to match the room, which was itself a kind of courtesy, and stood looking down at Ennis with an expression of grave sympathy that I believed completely and hated completely. "This one's from the valley, isn't he? Southern sector."

"He's from everywhere, sir. Like most of them."

"Mm." He turned his grey-gloved hand over — he wore the glove now even indoors, over the healing wound, a fine kid glove the colour of weather — and studied it as though it might speak. "Strange business, the southern sector. You hear stories."

"What sort of stories, sir?"

"Oh." He smiled at the sleeping corporal rather than at me, which somehow made it worse. "The sort orderlies tell each other. Rumors among the staff. I collect them, you know. Rumors. Useful things — they tell you where a war actually happened, as opposed to where the dispatches say it did." He glanced at me then, mildly, pleasantly. "You hear much of that? Talk among the nurses?"

"I hear complaints about the food, sir."

He laughed — genuinely, I think, a short warm bark he quickly hushed for the patients' sake — and patted the foot of Ennis's bed with two gloved fingers. "Food complaints. Yes, that's the army entire." He moved on toward the next bed, then paused, half-turned. "If it ever becomes something else, Underhill — talk, rumor, gossip of any consequence — my door is open. Officers in my position depend on men who hear everything and say little. There's a career in it, for the right sort."

He said it like a gift. He walked on down the ward collecting smiles.

* * *

Marisol Quill saw it before I said anything, because Marisol saw everything and said almost none of it. We were in the kitchen yard at dusk, she scalding pots, I hauling water, which was a chore I took voluntarily whenever my head needed somewhere dumb to go.

"You've stopped eating Tuesdays," she said, without looking up.

"Tuesdays?"

"Supper. Tuesdays you push it around." She scrubbed. "Also you've started taking the east wing water. Also you look at the Major's window when you think nobody counts where you look." She set a pot in the rack and finally turned. She had flour on her jaw and eyes older than the rest of her. "Want to tell me?"

"No."

"All right." She went back to the pots. Then: "It's him, isn't it. The drawing."

I stood with the yoke cutting into my shoulders and considered the cost of lying to Marisol Quill, and found I couldn't afford it. "The dates line up," I said. "Nothing else lines up. Dates aren't proof."

"Dates hanged a man at Ashmoor when I was twelve," she said. "According to my father, who was there, and who says dates are the only thing that ever hangs anybody." She plunged another pot. "What are you going to do?"

"Nothing. Record. Keep my head." I hoisted the yoke. "He's a decorated officer convalescing in a house full of men who worship him. I'm a nurse who keeps an illegal ledger under a floorboard. There's no version of 'do something' that ends anywhere but a ditch."

She nodded slowly, as though I'd confirmed something. "Then keep your head," she said. "But Jonah—" and it was the first time she had used my name, and we both pretended not to notice— "he's started asking about *you*. Marta in the laundry heard him at supper. Asking what ward you keep, what hours, whether you have family. Whether anyone would miss you if they posted you somewhere." She rinsed the last pot, laid it gently in the rack. "Men like that don't ask unless they're already deciding."

* * *

He came to Ward Six again on Sunday, off-schedule, when I was alone at the desk updating temperature charts.

The ward was quiet — most of the men at the window watching a game of catch in the yard, the rest asleep. Vane moved along the row unhurried, exchanging a word here and there, and arrived at my desk the way a man arrives at a destination he has already chosen.

"Nurse Underhill." He sat down uninvited on the stool beside the desk, settling his gloved hand on his knee. Up close, without an audience, the charm banked itself into something quieter and more attentive, and I understood that the charm was the public instrument and this — this mild, patient regard — was the working one. "I've been meaning to ask. Your handwriting. It's excellent. Where does a field nurse learn book-hand like that?"

"My father was a parish clerk, sir."

"A clerk. Records, then. Ledgers." His eyes rested on the temperature chart in front of me, on my hand resting flat beside it. "You'd be surprised how rare that is. A man comfortable with records. Most people treat paper as a nuisance. Treats names as—" he searched, found it— "as furniture. They walk past names all day and never once wonder who wrote them down, or who struck them out." He said it lightly, companionably, two professionals discussing trade. "You wonder. I can tell. You have the look of a man who wonders."

"I write temperatures, sir."

"You write temperatures," he agreed, smiling. "For now." He rose, straightened his coat with one hand, and paused before leaving, and for a moment — just a moment — he looked down the ward at the sleeping men, at the watchers at the window, at all those bodies arranged in rows awaiting either recovery or paperwork, and his face did something unguarded. Not guilt. Nothing so useful as guilt. It was closer to ownership: the flat satisfaction of a man surveying ground he considers his.

"Thirty feet," he said, apropos of nothing, turning back to me with the full charm restored. "That's the ceiling height in here, did you know? Measured it myself, first week. Beautiful old house. Deserves to be remembered properly, don't you think? When the historians come. Everything in its right account."

He wished me good afternoon and left. My chart pen had been hovering over the page the whole time, and when I looked down I saw I had written the same digit six times in a column where it belonged once.

That night I lifted the floorboard and added nothing, because there was nothing yet that would stand as record — only dates, only gloves, only a man asking after my handwriting. Instead I sat in the dark of the linen closet with Hazel's charcoal drawing spread on my knees, and I listened to the house settle above me, floor by floor, beam by beam, until I heard the particular slow tread cross the east wing overhead, pause at the window, and move on toward bed.

The man who ordered Harrow Bend was sleeping thirty feet away. And in the morning, when I changed his dressing, I would hold his hand in mine, and he would thank me for it.

Above my head, faint through two floors of old oak, I heard — or thought I heard, and would spend years deciding — a drawer close softly, and a chair drawn up, and a nib begin to scratch. Someone else in this house was keeping an account.
