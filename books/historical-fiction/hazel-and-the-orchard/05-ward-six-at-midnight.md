## 5. Ward Six at Midnight

The lamps in Ward Six were trimmed to half-wick after ten o'clock — regulation, posted on the door in a colonel's careful hand, as though darkness itself had been prescribed by a physician. I never obeyed it. The dying deserve light. Not much, but some. A candle stub on the far table, shaded with a tin plate so the glow fell only where I wanted it: on faces, on charts, on the page when I needed to write.

Sergeant Elias Brack had been dying for nine days, which is its own kind of discipline. He'd taken shrapnel through the belly at a bridge nobody could later agree on the name of, and the surgeons had opened him, looked at what was inside, and closed him again with the gentleness men reserve for hopeless cases. After that it was morphine and water and time. He should have gone quiet days ago. Instead he lay in the corner cot by the window, breathing in shallow pulls, and waited for something.

I learned early in this work that the dying wait for things. Permission. Forgiveness. Sometimes just an audience. The ones who fought their whole lives to be unremarkable suddenly need, at the end, to be witnessed — and there was no one left to witness them except a nurse with soft shoes and a habit of listening.

I sat with him at midnight because I couldn't sleep, which was most nights, and because his breathing had changed that evening — longer pauses between the pulls, as if he kept forgetting the trick of it and had to start over.

"Wrenfield," he said.

I leaned in. "Here, Sergeant."

His eyes found me slowly, the way a compass needle settles. He was maybe forty. Grey stubble, a farm man's hands, a tattoo on his wrist that might have been an anchor or a fence post, the ink blurred with age.

"You're the one that writes."

My chest did something complicated and brief. "I keep records, yes."

"Not their records." His hand came off the blanket, an inch of motion that cost him visibly. "Yours."

There are moments when you understand that your life has been noticed — that the invisibility you cultivated so carefully was never as complete as you believed. I felt that moment arrive like cold air under a door.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, which was stupid, because we both knew exactly what I meant, and lying to a dying man is a poor way to spend his last hours.

"Harlow told me." Harlow — the private who'd died in Ward Six three weeks back, whose name I had written down along with the words *shot leaving post, no report filed*. "Before he went. Said there's a nurse here keeps a true count. Said if I ever got my voice back, I should find him."

The candle guttered in a draft from the window sash. I didn't move to fix it. My heart was going hard and quick, the way it does before a search or an inspection, and I made myself sit still and let the silence do its work.

"Find me, then," I said.

* * *

Brack talked for the better part of two hours, and I want to set down here, as faithfully as I can, how it went — not the polished testimony I would later write into the ledger, cleaned of coughing and morphine fog, but the shape of it, the terrible ordinary shape of a man confessing at four fathoms deep.

He started with the village. "Harrow Bend. You know it?"

I knew the name the way you know a word you've heard in another room — half-caught, never explained. It sat in the back of my ledger's second section, under a heading I'd titled *Absences*: places that appeared in no report, villages that fed garrisons and vanished from correspondence, roads marked on maps that no dispatch ever mentioned. Harrow Bend was there twice underlined. I had nothing else. No names. No numbers.

"Tell me about it," I said.

"Little place. River bends around it, that's the name. Mills, orchards." He stopped, and something moved across his face that wasn't pain, or wasn't only pain. "We was quartered there six weeks. Good people. Fed us out of their own cellars when the supply line broke. Mrs. —" and here his voice failed him, not with grief but with the specific paralysis of a man who has forbidden himself a memory and finds it arriving anyway. He swallowed. Started again lower down. "The major came in October."

"Vane."

Something happened in his eyes. "You know him."

"He's convalescing here. Wounded hand."

Brack was quiet a long while. The pause between breaths stretched; I counted to nine before the next pull came. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to the register men use in trenches, the low carry-nothing tone.

"He come to the square on the morning of the ninth. Stood us all up in front of the well. Said there'd been sabotage on the mill road. A culvert blown. Two dead, drivers, boys of nineteen." His hand worked at the blanket. "Wasn't us done it. Wasn't the villagers neither — I'd stake my pension on it, what's left of it. Partisans, maybe, come and gone. Didn't matter. Vane stood there with his grey gloves on — always wore them, even indoors, some gentleman thing — and he said reprisals were authorized under standing order such-and-such, and he read it out like scripture, and then he said the quota."

"The quota."

"Ten lives for two. Village charter says harbor no enemy, that's how he dressed it. Said anyone could step forward and name names and spare the rest." Brack's face had gone slick with sweat despite the cold room. "Nobody named nobody. You want to know why? Because they'd already given up two of their own sons to the army two years prior, and they thought — God help them — they thought being loyal meant keeping their mouths shut. Standing quiet. Like school."

I was writing now. I'd gotten the ledger out without deciding to; my hands had made the decision the way practiced hands do, lifting the floorboard behind the linen press in the alcove, sliding the book out, wrapping back the oilcloth. I wrote in the dark-side shorthand I'd developed for this exact purpose — initials, slashes, keywords I could reconstruct later. But I found I was writing slower than usual, because part of me wanted to get every word exactly as he said it, and part of me wanted to stop hearing it.

"Someone refused," I said. I don't know where the sentence came from. Perhaps from Hazel's charcoal drawing, still folded in my coat pocket — soldiers, smoke, and among the ranked figures one man turned away, arms at his sides, refusing the geometry.

Brack looked at me for a long time. The morphine was thick in him by then; his pupils were small and dark and very old.

"Lieutenant Cairns. Our own officer. Twenty-three years old, churchgoing, wrote poetry I expect." A ghost of a smile. "He stepped out of the line and said the order was unlawful. Said he'd been to the school too, same as Vane, and he remembered the part Vane forgot. Vane didn't shout at him. That's the thing everyone gets wrong about him. He doesn't shout. He got quiet and pleasant, the way he does, and he said, 'Thank you, Lieutenant. Your objection is noted.' And then he had Cairns disarmed and put in the firing line with the others."

The candle flame stood perfectly still. Even the draft seemed to have stopped to listen.

"Twenty-six souls in that square, Sergeant. Is that right?"

"Twenty-six. I counted. I've counted every night since." His voice cracked on it, finally, one hairline fracture. "They stood them against the wall of the malting shed. Families together. There was a girl —"

He stopped. Fully stopped. And I understood that whatever came next was the thing he had been waiting nine days to say and would die rather than say, and that it had a face attached to it, and that the face was why he was dying with his eyes open.

"There was a girl," he said again, barely sound at all. "She held my hand. She thought — she thought the line-up was a census. Kept asking me her family's details so she wouldn't get them wrong. Age, birthdays. Asked me to remember them in case she mixed them up."

I could not write for a moment. My pen had stopped on the page and left a small black bloom of ink, and I looked at it and thought, absurdly, that it looked like the period at the end of a sentence that should never have been completed.

"She got away," I said. Not a question. I knew. I had fed her porridge that morning and watched her memorize the ward the way other children memorize songs.

Brack's eyes came around to me with the last of his focus gathered into them, and I saw him arrive at the truth I'd been carrying for weeks, and I saw that it undid him and steadied him in the same instant.

"The one at the door," he whispered. "The child they brought in."

"Hazel."

"Don't let them —" and the sentence collapsed into a cough that bent him nearly double, that brought blood up thin and rust-colored into the cloth I held for him. When it passed he was greyer, and smaller, and he gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both. "Don't let them make her invisible. Whatever they do to the rest of us. She saw the glove. She saw it plain. She's the record now. Keep the record."

"I will," I said. "On my life, Sergeant. I will."

He nodded, satisfied, a man who has delivered his parcel and obtained a signature. Within twenty minutes he was sleeping, really sleeping, and within three hours he was dead. I noted the time, filled out the chart in regulation language, and then, before I covered his face, I opened the ledger and wrote beneath his fragments:

*Sergeant Elias Brack, garrison of Harrow Bend. Witness. Died 3:10 a.m., having given full testimony. Refusal: Lt. Cairns (fate unknown). Count confirmed: 26. The girl held his hand. He asks that she be kept visible.*

Then I wrapped the book in its oilcloth, returned it to the cavity behind the linen press, seated the floorboard, and swept the dust line smooth with my sleeve, the way I had done perhaps two hundred times before.

* * *

It was on the walk back that I felt it — or rather, on the return past the alcove at dawn, when the ward was waking and orderlies were hauling water, and I glanced at the linen press the way a man glances at his own house from the street, checking that everything sits as it should.

The dust line was wrong.

Not smeared. Not scuffed. *Re-seated.* The board sat a hair's breadth proud of its neighbors, the gap along its north edge wider by the width of a fingernail. Someone had lifted it and put it back carefully — more carefully than haste would manage. Someone who knew what a disturbed board looks like, and had taken the trouble to disguise the disturbance, and had almost succeeded.

I did not go to it. That was the first rule of my craft and I obeyed it now with my pulse hammering in my ears: never check your cache when you are observed, and never, under any circumstances, show the room that the cache matters to you. I crossed to the water stove, poured myself tea I didn't want, and drank it standing by the window while the winter light came up grey over the orchard, and I ran the arithmetic of the night.

Who? The orderlies were searched men, supervised, without cause to enter the storage alcove except on laundry days, and last night was not one. Matron kept keys and no curiosity. Marisol came through with bread at five, but Marisol's business was the kitchen passage, not the press alcove — and besides, she had looked me in the eye too many times for it to be her. Which left the ward itself. A patient ambulatory enough to move at night. Or a visitor. Or —

Or the aide. Vane's aide, Captain Fenn, who had been seen twice this week walking the corridors at hours no convalescent keeps, who carried a leather folio everywhere and had once, I now remembered with a chill that started in my scalp, asked Sister Prewitt which storerooms backed onto Ward Six. Asking about damp, he'd said. Reporting on conditions for the Ministry.

Conditions. Damp. Floorboards.

I set the cup down. My hands were steady, which surprised me; inside, everything was running like water finding cracks.

Here is what I understood, standing in the grey light with the ward clattering awake around me: the ledger had been safe for fourteen months precisely because no one imagined it existed. That protection was spent. Brack had known about it. Harlow had known about it before him. Word travels through dying men the way damp travels through plaster — silently, everywhere — and somewhere in that spreading stain, someone with authority had put a hand to the wall and felt it give.

I could move the book today. Behind the loose brick in the linen room, under the third flagstone in the chapel vestry, into the orchard wrapped in oilcloth inside the hollow of the old pear trunk that lightning had killed two winters ago. Any of it. All of it. But moving it meant touching it in daylight, and touching it in daylight meant being seen touching it, and whoever had re-seated my floorboard with a fingernail's precision was almost certainly counting on exactly that — watching the cache, whichever cache, waiting for the guilty hand to reach for it.

So instead I did nothing. I made my rounds. I changed dressings. I smiled at the corporal who was regaining feeling in his frostbitten feet and regretted it loudly. And all the while, underneath the ordinary surface of the morning, I was aware of the manor around me as I had never been before: every corridor a sightline, every kindness possibly a probe, every creak of the old house a question about who else was awake in it.

At seven o'clock, crossing the entrance hall, I passed Captain Fenn coming down the stairs. He wished me good morning with perfect courtesy. His boots were clean. His cuffs were clean. But there was a fine smudge of coal dust along the seam of his right glove, and the storage alcove behind Ward Six opened, as it happened, onto the passage where the coal scuttles were kept.

I smiled back and said the weather looked like snow.

That afternoon, Sergeant Brack was buried in the orchard plot with eleven other men, the chaplain hurrying because the ground was freezing. I stood at the head of the grave with my hands folded and my face arranged into professional sorrow, and I recited to myself, silently, the only rite I had to offer: *Elias Brack, witness. He counted. He asked that she be kept visible.*

And as the first spadeful rang off the coffin lid, I looked up through the bare black lattice of the apple trees toward the manor's east wing, and saw, in the window of the room where Major Aldous Vane took his convalescence, a figure standing quite still, watching the burial with his hands clasped behind his back — the wounded hand, the grey glove — and even at that distance I would have sworn he was looking not at the grave, but at me.
