## 6. The Orchard Keeper's Daughter

Marisol Quill could tell a man's sleep from the way he carried his bucket.

Her father had taught her that, back when her whole education was the orchard: how a tree carries water in its posture, how a branch that droops at noon has roots that are drowning or starving, and how you don't ask the tree what's wrong — you look at the ground, the drainage, the things done to it while nobody was watching. People were no different. The soldiers who came through the kitchen for their ration of bread and dripping told her everything with their shoulders. The ones who slept walked loose. The ones who didn't walk like men hauling a bucket of water they refused to set down.

Nurse Underhill had been hauling his bucket for three weeks now. Ever since the child.

She'd noticed it first at the stove, because she noticed everything at the stove; it was her parish, her pulpit, her window onto the whole rotten machinery of Wrenfield Manor. He'd come in for the hot water he was too proud to send an orderly for, and he'd stood at the hearth with his hands hanging, staring into the coals long enough that she'd had to say his name twice. A man who doesn't hear his own name is a man carrying something heavier than water.

So she watched him. Not obviously — Marisol Quill hadn't gotten through two years of war in a requisitioned house full of officers by being obvious. She watched him the way she watched the plum trees for wasps: sideways, patiently, while appearing to do something else entirely.

What she saw was this: Jonah Underhill came down to the kitchen later and later each night. He took his tea but forgot to drink it. He stood in the yard some evenings looking up at the east wing windows as if counting them, or weighing them, and then he'd go inside instead of walking the orchard path the way he used to when the weather was kind. And twice — twice — she had seen him stop mid-stride in the corridor outside Ward Six, press his palm flat against the wall, and stand there breathing like a man talking himself out of running.

The night the child arrived, Marisol had been awake anyway. She was always awake on delivery days; the carts came late and the drivers came hungry, and there was no sense pretending otherwise. So she'd seen the aide from the window of her little room above the dairy — seen the colonel's man set a girl down on the gravel like a sack of oats he was glad to be rid of, hand over a note, and ride off without once looking back at the small muddy figure standing alone in the dark.

Forget the face, ask nothing. That was what the note said, according to the matron, who had read it aloud in the tone of a woman reciting a recipe she didn't care for but would follow all the same.

Well. Marisol Quill had been raised by an orchard keeper, and orchard keepers understood one thing better than any officer alive: you cannot tell a root where not to grow.

* * *

The girl came to the kitchen on the third day, the way stray cats come — sidelong, silent, already halfway certain she'd be chased off.

Marisol didn't chase her. She didn't even turn around. She kept peeling apples, letting the parings fall in long unbroken spirals into the basin, and after a while she said, to the room in general, "If you're going to stand there, you may as well be warm. There's a stool by the oven that doesn't bite."

No answer. But ten minutes later, when Marisol turned to fetch the salt, the stool was occupied and the girl was studying the room the way a soldier studies a map — corners first, exits second, faces last.

"Marisol," she said, tapping her own chest with a floury hand. "Quill. I cook here. Badly, some say, though those people are liars and I know where they sleep."

Nothing. Not a flicker. The girl's eyes were grey as November and just as likely to make you shiver, and they went through Marisol the way light goes through a window — registering everything, catching on nothing.

Undeterred, Marisol set a bowl of stewed apple and a hunk of bread on the table beside her, close enough to reach, far enough away that taking it would be the girl's own decision. Then she went back to her apples and talked the whole time — about nothing, about the price of suet, about how Corporal Evans pretended to hate her barley soup but asked for thirds, about how the oven door stuck unless you lifted and pushed at the same time, which was true of most worthwhile things in this world.

The girl ate the apple. Left the bread. Came back the next day, and the day after that.

By the end of the week she was peeling potatoes at the corner of the big table, solemn as a magistrate, and Marisol had learned her name — Hazel, from the matron's ledger, nothing else, no surname worth the ink — and had decided, privately, firmly, and without consulting anyone, that the child was hers now in whatever way a child can belong to a woman who owns nothing but a cast-iron skillet and her father's stubbornness.

She never asked. That was the covenant, unspoken and unwritten: food given, questions withheld. The girl had come with orders to be forgotten, and Marisol had looked at those enormous watchful eyes and understood that forgetting was a thing being done *to* her, not by her, and that the kindest rebellion available to a cook was simply remembering someone on purpose. Feeding her. Teaching her that the third floorboard by the pantry squeaked so she could avoid it. Saving her the heel of the honey loaf because the heel was the best part and everyone else was too foolish to know it.

Some nights Hazel didn't go to the attic pallet the matron had assigned her. Some nights she appeared in the kitchen doorway after midnight, drawn by God knew what dream, and Marisol would lift the blanket off her own feet and make room without either of them saying a word. The girl slept like something expecting to be moved along — one hand curled, jaw tight even in sleep — and every time Marisol lay awake listening to that small strained breathing she found herself thinking thoughts that would get a woman shot if she said them aloud.

Thoughts like: *what was done to you, little one? And who benefits from my not knowing?*

* * *

It rained the evening she finally spoke to Underhill properly, which was fitting, because the rain emptied the yard and gave them the only privacy Wrenfield ever offered — the illusion of it.

He came to the kitchen near eleven, wet to the knees, carrying his bucket of wash-water with exactly the posture she'd been cataloguing for weeks. Set it down inside the door. Stood there a moment in the lamplight with his face doing that thing it did — nothing, precisely nothing, arranged very carefully to look like calm.

"There's broth left," Marisol said, not looking up from the loaves she was shaping for morning. "And before you say you're not hungry, consider that I've heard that sentence from forty dying men and they were all lying too."

"I wasn't going to say it."

"You were composing it." She nodded at the stool. "Sit. You'll take the heat off my back and I'll take the weight off yours, and neither of us will mention it again."

A pause — the particular pause of a careful man deciding whether a trap is sprung or merely possible. Then the stool creaked under him and the kitchen felt different with a second heartbeat in it, warmer and more dangerous at once.

Marisol worked. He drank the broth. The rain ticked against the shutters and the fire shifted and somewhere upstairs a man in Ward Four moaned through his bandages, the ordinary music of the house.

"You don't sleep," she said at last, conversationally, larding the loaves. "I'm not asking why. I'm telling you what I see, because I see everything in this house and it saves time for people to know that. You don't sleep, and it isn't the patients. I've nursed enough to know grief-sleeplessness from the other kind. Grief looks down. Yours looks over its shoulder."

The spoon stopped halfway to his mouth. Just for a breath.

"My father kept the orchard here thirty years," she went on. "Before the army took the house. He used to say a tree tells you its trouble in the leaves before it ever tells you in the fruit. You're all leaves lately, Nurse Underhill. Drooping at noon. Looking over your shoulder." She tied off the cloth and finally turned to face him, wiping her hands on her apron. "And the little one eats at my table every day, and doesn't speak, and watches you on your rounds like you're the last lit window in a burning street. So."

"So?" His voice was quiet. Careful men had quiet voices the way knives had quiet edges.

"So I'm not asking anything. That's rather the point." She sat down across the table from him, close enough that the lamp threw both their shadows tall against the flour bins. "But I'll tell you what I am offering, because my father raised me to be direct with things that matter. There are people in this house who count things. Papers, stores, names. And there are people who don't. I'm one of the ones who doesn't. Nobody counts the cook. Nobody has ever once in this war counted the cook. Which means I can move through places you can't, hold things you shouldn't, and forget — genuinely, honestly, with a clear conscience — conversations we've never had."

Jonah Underhill looked at her for a long moment. Up close, in honest lamplight, his exhaustion had a structure to it, almost architectural — a man built around a hollow. She thought: *there's a cellar in that man with something heavy stored in it,* and did not let one word of the thought reach her face.

"Why would you?" he said finally. "You don't know me. You don't know what I—" He stopped himself, neatly, the way a man steps back from a stair edge in the dark. "Whatever you imagine I'm carrying, getting near it is how ordinary people end up in trouble with extraordinary people."

"I've met extraordinary people." Marisol shrugged. "They eat soup like everyone else. They bleed red when the kitchen knife slips during their dressing-downs, which I've witnessed twice." She leaned forward. "Here's my reason, since you want one. That child sleeps in my bed half the nights because she wakes up screaming without sound — did you know that? Screams with her mouth wide open and makes no noise at all, like somebody taught her that sound gets you found. Somebody taught her that, Nurse. And whoever did it put a note on her saying *forget.* I have spent two years keeping my head down and my pots clean and telling myself the war was happening out there somewhere." Her voice stayed level; her father would have been proud. "It stopped happening out there the day she stood on your gravel. So I'm in, whether you recruit me or not. The only question on the table is whether you carry that bucket alone or let somebody help set it down between stops."

The rain filled the silence. He studied her the way she'd seen him study a chart — thorough, clinical, looking for the lie or the weakness. She let him look. She'd been looked at by worse than tired nurses.

"It would be safer for you," he said at last, "if you hated me. If you reported me. If—"

"If I did any of those things I'd have done them already, and you'd know it, because you'd be gone and the little one would be eating gruel in some Ministry institution by Friday." She stood, gathered his empty bowl, and paused behind his shoulder just long enough to say the rest quietly, into the steam rising off the wash-water. "An alliance of silences, Nurse Underhill. You don't tell me what you write at midnight. I don't tell you what I hear at the officers' table. Neither of us tells anyone about the other. And the girl — the girl gets fed, and warmed, and kept, by both of us, until the world remembers how to be safe for her. Or longer. Whichever comes first."

She felt more than saw him exhale — the smallest release of a load, quickly snatched back, like a man allowing himself one bite of bread in a famine year.

"Alliance of silences," he repeated, tasting the words.

"Mind the third floorboard by the pantry," Marisol said, returning to her loaves. "It squeaks. And come Tuesday, tell the matron the child does better with routine — she'll give her kitchen hours if a responsible party asks. You strike everyone as responsible. It's the best disguise in this whole bloody house."

When she glanced back a minute later he was gone, and his bucket was gone, and the stool still held the faint warmth of a man who might, tonight, actually lie down.

Hazel appeared in the doorway twenty minutes later, woken by some dream or drawn by instinct, and Marisol lifted the blanket without a word.

* * *

Three mornings later, the alliance had its first fruit, and the fruit was bitter.

Marisol was clearing the officers' breakfast things — moving unseen, as promised, invisible as steam — when Major Vane's aide said to his companion, in the bored voice of a man reporting weather: "Someone's been asking the orderlies about a nurse who writes at night. Writes, mind you, not reads. The Major finds it interesting."

The plates in Marisol's hands did not tremble. Two years of practice held them steady as stone, and her face held nothing at all, and she went on stacking china like a woman thinking only of gravy.

But that night, when Jonah came down for his broth, she set the bowl in front of him and said, softly, "We should talk about floorboards. All of them. Every place in this house where a thing can be found — starting anywhere except the place you're thinking of right now."

And she watched the color leave his face and return, and knew her guess had landed somewhere near the truth, and understood that the cautious warmth she'd offered by the fire had just become something else entirely.

Something with a clock ticking inside it.
