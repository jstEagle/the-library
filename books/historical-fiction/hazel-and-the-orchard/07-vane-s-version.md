## 7. Vane's Version

Major Aldous Vane had a theory about hospitals: they were theatres in which everyone insisted on playing tragedy when comedy would have been kinder. Wrenfield Manor suited the genre. Its long windows admitted a grey, apologetic light; its corridors smelled of carbolic and boiled linen; its patients lay in rows like an audience that had come to see themselves die and been disappointed by the pacing.

He walked the ward at ten in the morning because walking was what his hand surgeon prescribed, and because a convalescing officer who stayed in his room became furniture. Furniture got forgotten. Vane had no intention of being forgotten.

His left hand was wrapped to the wrist in white bandages, the fingers stiff as candle wax beneath. A musket ball through the palm at—well. The official account said *reconnaissance action near the eastern ford*, which was true in the sense that a portrait is true. It captured the light. It omitted the flies.

"Good morning, Major," said a corporal whose jaw had been wired shut, and Vane paused at his bedside anyway, because pausing was half of command. He tapped two fingers of his right hand against the man's blanket rail, twice, like a benediction. The corporal's eyes shone. Vane moved on, collecting the shine.

They loved him here. That was not vanity; it was inventory. He had learned early that men forgave almost anything of an officer who remembered their names, their villages, the girl they'd meant to marry in the spring that kept failing to arrive. He carried these details the way other men carried ammunition. He spent them carefully.

The nurse was at the far window, changing a dressing.

Underhill. There was a study in grey. Vane had catalogued him within a week of arriving: quiet, competent, unhandsome in the way that made men trust him, with hands that moved over wounds like someone reading scripture aloud to himself. He never spoke unless spoken to. He never asked for anything. In twenty years of service, Vane had found that men who wanted nothing were either saints or spies, and he had met very few saints.

*Harrow Bend.*

The word arrived, as it always did now, without being summoned. He kept it in a small locked room at the back of his mind, and some nights it rattled the door all night long, and some nights—he had noticed this with clinical interest—it was silent, and those were the good nights, though he could not have told you why they differed.

Here is what happened at Harrow Bend, in the version Aldous Vane told himself, which was also the version he intended to see written into the official record:

The village was a supply depot for irregulars. This was documented. Caches were found in the tithe barn—grain, powder, forty rifles with the regiment's serial numbers filed away, which told you everything about the character of the men who'd held them. The garrison there had mutinied three weeks prior. Mutiny in wartime is not a political disagreement; it is a wound in the army's own flesh, and wounds left untended kill the whole body. He had arrived with two companies on a Tuesday morning under a sky the colour of dishwater, and he had offered terms. Terms. Full amnesty for enlisted men who laid down arms. He had stood in the village square with a white handkerchief on his sabre and read the proclamation aloud, and he had waited a full quarter hour, timing it on his watch, while the villagers watched him from doorways with the flat, patient stares of people who had already decided.

A shot came from the well-house. His aide took it in the shoulder—a boy of nineteen, screaming in a way that turned every man on the square from an officer's company into a mob waiting only for permission.

Vane did not give them permission. That was important, and he clung to it the way drowning men cling to driftwood that will not float. What he gave them was an order to clear the square, and clearing a square of armed mutineers is not a gentle arithmetic, and somewhere between the order and its execution the arithmetic stopped being his. By the time he regained the thread of it—the exact moment, he remembered, was when he saw the grey glove of his own right hand raised in front of his face and realized he had been holding it up for some time, whether to signal halt or something else he would never determine—the well-house was burning and the square was not a square anymore but a ledger of its own, a column of dead no one would ever total correctly.

Forty-one civilians. Or thirty-eight. Or fifty-two. The counts disagreed with each other the way witnesses do, and the disagreement itself became his shield: nothing so uncertain can be prosecuted, only regretted.

He had filed the report himself. *Skirmish at Harrow Bend. Irregulars dispersed. Casualties moderate.* He struck the civilian figures entirely—not out of dishonesty, he told himself, but out of mercy. Numbers like that, printed in a dispatch, would follow every widow in the county like a debt collector. Better the war end and the dead rest uncounted. Better silence than arithmetic.

This was the version he believed on Tuesdays. On other days of the week, the grey glove rose in front of his face again, and he woke with his unwounded hand closed around the bedframe, and he did not think about it at all, which was the closest thing to thinking about it that he permitted.

*Forget the face*, he had written on the note for the child, and the phrase had come to him with the ease of an old tune, and he had felt—this was the strange part, the part he examined at arm's length and always set down quickly—relieved. Relieved that she was alive. Relieved, in some chamber of himself he kept locked even from himself, that one pair of eyes from Harrow Bend still opened in the mornings. As though her survival were a payment on a debt. As though a child deposited at a hospital door with instructions to be forgotten could ever constitute restitution.

She had seen the glove. He knew this the way one knows weather coming: not through evidence but through the ache in old breaks. The aide who'd taken the shot had reported a girl in the square, standing very still by the well, watching. Watching *him*, specifically, the aide thought—watching his raised hand. Children fixate. Children misunderstand. And yet.

Vane reached the window where Underhill worked.

"The Merrow boy," Vane said, nodding toward the bed behind them. "He slept through the night?"

"Most of it." Underhill did not look up from the dressing he was folding. His voice was exactly as Vane remembered it from every previous conversation: level, unhurried, a voice with no hooks in it for a listener to catch.

"And the sergeant in Ward Six? Corrigan."

"He sleeps mostly. Talks in his sleep."

"What does he say?"

Underhill looked up then, and Vane gave him the smile that had opened doors in four counties and closed just as many. It was a good smile. He had practised it in a mirror once, years ago, during a promotion board, and then resolved never to practise anything again, because practise showed. What showed instead now was warmth with a blade folded inside it, and Underhill received it the way a wall receives weather.

"Names," Underhill said. "He asks for names."

"A man of sentiment."

"A man dying, sir."

"Yes." Vane let the word sit between them a moment, unhurried. "There are rumors among the orderlies."

Underhill returned to his folding. "Orderlies rumor everything. Last month they had the cook poisoning the colonel with bad eels."

"Eels," Vane repeated, as though tasting the word, and laughed—one short breath of genuine amusement, because it *was* funny, and because laughter was the sound that disarmed men faster than any other. "You're not curious, Nurse Underhill. Most men would ask what the rumors concerned."

"I find most rumors concern nothing I can treat."

"Ah." Vane turned to face the window, presenting his profile—the strong nose, the greying temple, the whole handsome ruin of him—to the grey orchard beyond the glass. Half-wild apple trees, unreclaimed since before the war, their branches knuckled and black. Somewhere among them, he knew, laundry flapped and orderlies smoked and the ordinary machinery of the manor ground on. "But you are a keeper of records, aren't you? The matron tells me your lists are immaculate. Not a name misplaced. Not a death unentered." He glanced back. "That kind of precision is rare. Rarer than courage, in my experience. Courage is common as rain. Precision is a virtue."

"I write down what happens, Major. It's the job."

"It is the job," Vane agreed warmly. "And yet reports get written above both our heads, don't they, and names get—" he made a small gesture with his bandaged hand, winced convincingly, continued with the right— "*condensed.* The Ministry prefers summary to detail. I've fought that preference my whole career and lost more often than I've won. So when rumors circulate among your orderlies—rumors concerning a certain village, a certain action last autumn—I confess I take an interest. Because rumors are condensed too. Condensed until nothing true remains."

Underhill folded the last of the dressing and set it in the basket at his feet. When he straightened, his face was the same face. That was what unsettled Vane, though he'd have shot any subordinate who suggested such a thing unsettled him: the man's face did not change. Not a flicker. Either he knew nothing—and dull men did exist, God knew—or he knew everything and had known how to hold still since before Vane learned to.

"If a patient talks in his sleep," Underhill said, "I listen long enough to know if he needs laudanum. After that it's his, isn't it? What a man says when he's going. Belongs to nobody else."

Vane studied him for a slow count of five.

"You have a good heart," he said at last, softly, and meant it, and hated that he meant it, and filed the hatred under discipline. "I intend to recommend you for head nurse. Effective the first of the month. Ward authority, dispensary keys, a private room at the end of the east corridor. The matron is worn out and the Ministry wants the manor run properly before the historians descend on us—they arrive within the fortnight, you know, three of them, come to collect our papers and compose the war into something teachable." He smiled again, smaller this time, confidential, conspiratorial. "I should like this place to present its best face. An orderly house. Precise ledgers. Nothing embarrassing in the margins. You understand me, I think, better than most."

"I'm grateful for the consideration, Major."

"You'll find the pay improves, and the hours worsen, and the responsibility lands on you like a dropped piano. But it's yours." He extended his right hand, and after a pause precisely calibrated to seem like humility rather than reluctance, Underhill took it. His grip was dry, cool, firm. A nurse's grip. A man accustomed to holding things that were slipping.

"Head nurse," Vane repeated, releasing it. "Every ward answering to you. Every record passing through your hands first." He watched the words land—watched, with the particular pleasure of a fencer who has felt his blade find cloth rather than flesh, the tiny delay in Underhill's breathing, invisible to anyone not looking for it. "I have high hopes, Nurse. High hopes for us both."

He walked on down the ward, tapping blanket rails, trading names and villages and girls' names in springtime, gathering shines. Behind him he left the promotion hanging in the air like incense: a gift, freely given, that would smell of honour for exactly as long as it took Underhill to realize it was a leash—and by then, Vane reflected comfortably, the collar would already sit easy, because the finest leathers are supple, and the best chains are duties.

In Ward Six, past the curtain, Sergeant Corrigan was sleeping and asking for names. Vane did not go in. He had heard, through channels he did not document, that Corrigan had served at Harrow Bend, and had refused something there, and was now spending his final weeks paying the refusal forward in murmurs to whoever leaned close.

Rumors among the orderlies. Condensed. Nothing true remaining.

Except that the nurse with the immaculate lists had asked him directly what Corrigan said in his sleep—which no innocent man does, innocently—and had answered with a principle about the property of dying men, which was either philosophy or a wall.

Vane stood at the ward's far door and looked back down the row of beds to the window where Underhill had resumed his work, small and grey and precise against the light.

*You keep names,* he thought. *So do I. The difference between us is that mine are living.*

It was a fine thought. He gave it, generously, to the room.

That afternoon he wrote the recommendation himself, in his careful right-handed script, and sealed it, and set it on the tray of the aide who had taken the bullet in the shoulder at Harrow Bend—who had screamed on the square, nineteen years old, and who now ran errands with his arm strapped and his devotion absolute and his memory, Vane trusted, mercifully blurred.

The letter went down at four o'clock.

By six, Vane had learned from a kitchen boy that the groundskeeper's daughter had been seen carrying bread into the orchard at dusk, followed by a small figure who never spoke, and who wore her hair the way little girls at Harrow Bend wore theirs.

He sat with his wine untouched, watching the dark come down over the apple trees, and decided—reasonably, generously, in the spirit of an orderly house—that it was time he paid his respects to the kitchen.
