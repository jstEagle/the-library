## 8. The Burn Pile

The orders came on a Tuesday, in the colonel's own hand, delivered by a corporal who did not meet anyone's eyes.

*All non-regimental papers at Wrenfield Manor to be collected and destroyed by order of the Ministry of Reconstruction. No personal correspondence, diaries, account books, or unofficial records to be retained. Compliance expected prior to arrival of the Historical Commission.*

Jonah read the notice pinned to the ward door and understood it for what it was: not housekeeping. Erasure, scheduled in advance, with a date.

He had seen burnings before. In the second winter of the war, a supply depot near Corleigh had torched three years of muster rolls because a clerk had falsified them and no one wanted the fraud examined. Jonah had stood in the crowd watching paper curl into black moths and thought, distantly, that somewhere in those flames were four hundred men whose mothers would never learn where they fell. The fire had been very efficient. Grief, he had learned, was rarely permitted the same efficiency. Grief took its time.

Now the fire was coming to Wrenfield, and this time Jonah had things in it worth more than muster rolls.

He finished changing Sergeant Brice's dressing, noted the wound was closing clean, told Brice he'd be walking by month's end — a lie he suspected Brice saw through and forgave him for — and went looking for Marisol Quill.

She was in the kitchen as always, up to her forearms in flour, and Hazel was on her stool in the corner as always, drawing. Jonah had learned to read the girl's charcoal pages the way other men read weather. Lately she had been drawing the orchard: trees with too many branches, apples rendered as small fierce circles, a figure with an apron that could only be Marisol. She was building a record of her own, in her own hand, of the world as it currently declined to hurt her. He never asked to see the older drawings. She kept them folded beneath her stool like documents in a vault, and he respected a fellow archivist's security arrangements.

"Burn order," he said quietly.

Marisol didn't stop working. She had a way of going very still while appearing busy, hands moving through routine motion while everything behind her eyes recalculated. "What counts as non-regimental?"

"Everything the army didn't issue."

"The men's letters home."

"Collected Thursday. Burned behind the orchard, I'd guess, where the wind takes the ash away from the house."

She wiped her hands slowly. "There's a woman in Ward Two who's written her husband every day since spring. He's been dead since April. She writes to him anyway." A pause. "Someone should tell her before the cart comes, so she can say goodbye to the letters. They're hers. They're the last thing that's hers."

"I'll tell her," Jonah said. "And then I'm going to do something about it."

Marisol looked at him then, fully, the flour-dusted appraisal of a woman deciding how much trouble a man was worth. "You've been doing something about things for months, Mr. Underhill, in your quiet way. I've watched you write at night. I don't ask what. But if you're about to do it louder, I'd like to know whether my kitchen is part of it."

"It isn't. Not yet." And then, because the words were already true and hiding them from her felt like a smaller kind of lie than the ones he told daily: "But it may need to be."

She nodded once, as if he'd confirmed a delivery schedule, and went back to her dough. That was Marisol. She took catastrophes the way she took recipes — measured, unhurried, certain there was a method even when nobody had written one down.

Hazel had stopped drawing. She was watching him with those grave dark eyes, and after a moment she held up her page: the orchard, but with fire in it now. Small red scribbles at the tree line, smoke rising in long grey strokes off the page's edge.

Jonah felt something cold walk down his spine.

"You saw that already," he said softly. "Didn't you."

She turned the page over. On the back, in her cramped child-hand, was a single word she had copied from somewhere — from a notice, from an overheard conversation, from the general atmosphere of dread that hung around the officers' wing lately:

*BURN.*

* * *

The cart came Thursday morning, as promised, drawn by a bony grey mule and driven by a private who looked barely old enough to shave. Two orderlies went room to room ahead of it with sacks, and Jonah made himself follow, because someone owed these papers the dignity of witnesses.

It was worse than he had prepared for.

In Ward Two, the letter-writer — Mrs. Calloway, forty-six, shattered ankle from a collapsing wall at Dunmore, the sort of patient who would heal physically and never otherwise — had already been told, by Jonah himself, two days prior. She had spent those two days reading every letter one final time. When the orderly reached for the bundle, she held it against her chest a moment longer, then handed it over with terrible composure and said, "Tell them the ink was good ink. I paid extra for it during the shortage. Tell them that, if anyone asks."

No one would ask. That was the whole architecture of the thing.

They took Private Ennor's sketchbook — forty pages of the manor grounds, his fellow patients, a self-portrait in which he'd given himself both hands intact. They took the chaplain's assistant Tull's sermons, six months of them, preached to wards of dying men because there had been no chapel and no living chaplain to hear them. They took Corporal Voss's account book, which was not really an account book; Voss had kept a running tally of every man in his company lost, wounded, promoted, transferred, desertions distinguished from deaths with a small careful cross, and the tally ran to nine pages. They took letters from men who would die at Wrenfield before any answer could come, letters their families would now never receive, so that some mothers and wives would spend the rest of their lives believing silence meant desertion or indifference rather than death.

Jonah carried three sacks out himself. He wanted the weight in his arms. He wanted to know exactly what fifty pounds of erased lives felt like.

The burn site was behind the orchard's far wall, in a hollow where the ground sloped down to a dry streambed — chosen, clearly, so the smoke would drift away from the manor and the ash would fall where no one walked. A detail of four soldiers waited with oil. The driver stacked the sacks with a strange gentleness, almost ceremonial, and Jonah thought: even the instruments of erasure sometimes flinch.

Then the oil went on, and the match came out, and ceremony ended.

Paper does not burn the way wood burns. Wood resists, crackles, argues. Paper simply goes — one moment whole, the next a bright lacework of edges, and then gone. From ten feet away Jonah could not tell Mrs. Calloway's letters from Voss's tally from Ennor's self-portrait with its imagined hands. Fire is perfectly democratic. It took everything with the same appetite and left the same grey snow.

He stood there through all of it. The soldiers glanced at him once or twice — the tall quiet nurse with the still face — but burning paper invites watchers the way accidents do, and they assumed his presence was morbid curiosity. Let them. What he was actually doing was memorizing the shape of the operation: the location, the timing, the number of men, the fact that they worked from a list and burned in the order collected, the fact that the ash pit would presumably be used again, because a Ministry that schedules one bonfire schedules more.

The Historical Commission was coming. Everything unofficial would be ash before the historians' wagons crossed the bridge. The official record would then be assembled exclusively from official materials — reports written by officers, casualty lists approved by officers, accounts curated by men like Major Aldous Vane, whose version of Harrow Bend Jonah had heard with his own ears over dinner, delivered with charm and claret and not one word of truth in it.

That was the mathematics of it, laid plain at last: whatever was not in the official papers would not exist. Not disputed. Not contested. *Nonexistent.* Harrow Bend would become a village that had never been, its well just a well, its square just a square, and Hazel a mad orphan's fancy if she ever spoke of it, because children's testimony against a decorated major's report weighs exactly nothing.

Unless there were other papers.

Jonah walked back through the orchard slowly, letting the soldiers finish their work behind him. The apple trees were heavy now, late summer fat with fruit nobody had time to pick, and the grass between the rows was waist-high in places. Wrenfield's orchard was half-wild the way everything half-forgotten was wild, and he had loved it for that without examining why. Now he understood. It was the one place on the grounds that answered to no ledger. Trees bore fruit whether or not the quartermaster counted it. Windfalls rotted into the earth unrecorded and fed next year's crop all the same. The orchard was proof that the world could keep producing outside the official account.

If anything was going to survive what was coming, it would have to learn the orchard's trick: exist outside the paperwork.

By the time he reached the kitchen garden he had stopped grieving in the general direction of history and started grieving in the specific direction of plan. The feeling was familiar — it was the same shift he'd felt the night he first wrote a struck name into his ledger, the transition from witnessing to keeping — but sharper now, because the timeline had collapsed. Before the burn order, he'd had the luxury of patience: gather testimony, wait out the war, find a safe road for the truth someday. *Someday* was dead. There was a date on the wall now. The Commission arrived, and everything unofficial died the day before.

Which meant the ledger under the floorboard needed to leave the manor entirely. Which meant a hiding place outside walls, outside searches, outside the tidy jurisdiction of men with lists. Which meant — his mind kept circling back to it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth — the cider press.

The old press stood in the far corner of the orchard, a stone-and-timber relic from the manor's productive years, abandoned now because the army had better uses for labor than cider. It had a pressing bed, a screw mechanism gone stiff with rust, and a base built of fitted stones with gaps between them wide enough for a hand. Nobody went there. Nobody had reason to.

Or it meant moving the ledger off the grounds altogether, which meant help, which meant trust, which was the one commodity Jonah had spent five years refusing to spend.

He counted his assets the way he counted dressings, coldly. One: Marisol Quill, who had offered an alliance of silences and had, he suspected, been waiting for him to upgrade it to an alliance of actions. Two: Hazel, who knew things no adult could testify to and who watched everything and forgot nothing — a child who had survived Harrow Bend precisely because she'd learned to be furniture, and who might be the only witness in the country whose memory predated the official version. Three: the dying men of Ward Six, who had begun, at his careful prompting, to say things worth writing down. Four: the burn pile itself, which had shown him the enemy's method, and method was always exploitable. They burned by schedule. Schedules could be beaten.

His liabilities were equally countable. Major Aldous Vane, convalescing thirty feet from Jonah's ward, decorated, beloved, charming, and — per Hazel's charcoal hand — the owner of a grey glove last seen at a massacre. Vane's aide, who watched Jonah too closely. The approaching Commission, which sounded like salvation and was in fact the deadline. And underneath all of it, the floorboard, disturbed once already, someone having knelt there in the night — a violation he had neither identified nor forgiven.

He passed the kitchen on his way in. Through the window he saw Marisol kneading bread, Hazel beside her on the stool, and the two of them talking — actually talking, Hazel's mouth moving, Marisol leaning down to listen. Jonah stopped dead in the yard. He had spent weeks hoping for that voice, praying for it in the practical unsentimental way he prayed for patients, and now that it had apparently arrived — given to Marisol first, which was right, which was fitting, given to the woman who fed people without demanding their stories — he found he could hardly breathe for wanting to hear what she was saying.

Not yet. If he burst in now he'd startle her back into silence for another month. Whatever Hazel was saying, she was saying it safely, at her own pace, to her own chosen person. He would get it later, complete, transcribed, preserved. Patience. Patience had been his entire discipline for five years.

But patience had a new expiration date, and standing in the yard with ash still drifting faintly on the breeze from beyond the orchard wall, Jonah Underhill admitted something he had been refusing to admit since the first name went into the ledger:

Recording was no longer enough. A record hidden under a floorboard was just evidence awaiting a fire. To keep the names — truly keep them, the way the orchard kept its fruit, outside everyone's counting — he would have to move them, copy them, seed them into hands he couldn't control, and accept that secrecy and survival had finally become opposites. The truth would only live if it stopped being his secret.

He went inside, climbed to his narrow room above the ward, knelt, and lifted the loose board.

The ledger lay where it always lay, wrapped in oilcloth, thinner than the enormity of what it held. He weighed it in his hands — lighter than fifty pounds of letters, heavier than anything on the cart — and for the first time he did not put it back.

He tucked it inside his coat instead, against his chest, where it rose and fell with his breathing, and went down to find Marisol, because the plan had just acquired its first accomplice, and there was no floorboard in England deep enough for what came next.
