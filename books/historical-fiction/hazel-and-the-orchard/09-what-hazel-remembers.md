## 9. What Hazel Remembers

Jonah had learned to measure his nights in half-hours. Half an hour to finish rounds on Ward Six, half an hour to sit with the men who couldn't sleep and pretend he wasn't counting their breaths, half an hour at the end for the ledger. It was a system that kept him upright and kept him hollow, both of which the work required.

On the third night after the burn order arrived — though he didn't know it yet; the dispatch was still two days out from Wrenfield — he came down from the wards past midnight and found the kitchen lamp burning low.

He should have walked on. That was the rule he'd built for himself: see nothing, be seen by no one, keep your curiosity as flat as your voice. But Marisol Quill's silhouette moved behind the frosted pane of the kitchen door with a stillness he had never seen in her, and through the gap where the frame had warped away from its casing, he heard Hazel's voice.

He stopped with his hand on the latch and did not go in.

He told himself afterward that he hadn't meant to eavesdrop, which was true in the way most convenient lies are true. He had meant only to make certain the girl was well. What he did instead was stand in the cold corridor with his coat unbuttoned and listen to a child speak for the first time since she'd been set down on Wrenfield's step like a parcel nobody would sign for.

* * *

Inside, Marisol sat across the scrubbed table from Hazel with a plate of bread gone cold between them. She had learned not to hover over the girl, not to fill silences, not to touch without warning. She had also learned that Hazel worked with her hands when she spoke — or when she almost spoke — folding scraps of cloth into smaller and smaller squares until they vanished in her fist.

Tonight the cloth squares lay forgotten. Tonight Hazel was looking at the fire.

"You don't have to," Marisol said. "I want you to know that. You never have to say anything you don't want to say."

"I know." The voice was small but level, like something carried carefully across a room. "You said I could talk to the hens if I wanted. Nobody listens to hens."

Marisol smiled. "That's the truth of it."

"The man in grey said nobody would listen either." Hazel unfolded one of her cloth squares. "So it wouldn't matter what I said. He said it to the whole square. He had a loud voice but it wasn't shouting-loud. It was church-loud."

The fire popped. Marisol kept her hands flat on the table, very still, the way you hold still for a wild thing deciding whether to come closer.

"Was this in your village?" she asked softly. "Harrow Bend?"

"You know the name of it." Not accusatory. Observing.

"It was on your collar," Marisol lied gently, because there had been nothing on the collar, and because some mercies are worth the cost. "Go on, if you're going. Or don't. Bread's better warm, but it keeps."

Hazel tore a corner of the bread and ate it, and Jonah understood from the long pause that followed that she was arranging something inside herself, setting her memories in rows the way she set her charcoal sticks in order of length before she drew.

"There were four soldiers who knocked first," she said. "They were polite. They asked Papa whose well was whose. Because the wells all look alike except ours has the iron hoop and the Tanners' has wood. Papa answered them. Then more soldiers came and the polite ones weren't polite anymore, and the officer got down off his horse."

She folded another square.

"He had grey gloves. Not brown like everyone else's. Grey ones that went up under his sleeves, and they made a sound when he moved his hands. Like paper." Her fingers rubbed together, demonstrating. "Papa made me stand behind the Tanners' door because I'm small and the hinge was broken so there was a crack. He pressed my head against his leg first. He said, whatever happens, count."

"Count?" Marisol's voice barely carried.

"That's what we do. Mama taught me. When the shelling came last spring we counted the gaps between the booms, and if the gaps got longer we could come out. So I counted. Through the door crack. I counted everything."

"What did you count?"

"People," Hazel said. "First forty-one. That's how many were in the square. Mrs. Aldery and her baby, that's forty-two and forty-three, except the baby doesn't get counted separate where we're from, so forty-three was Mr. Pell the schoolmaster. Then the officer made everyone kneel by the well. So I counted kneeling too. Thirty-eight knelt. Three didn't because they were old and couldn't get down, and a soldier helped them, which was strange, because then he stepped back in the line with the others like he hadn't helped at all."

Marisol's hands had begun, very slightly, to shake. Jonah, in the dark of the corridor, found he had put his own palm flat against the doorframe as if bracing against weather.

"Then the officer read from a paper," Hazel went on. "Church-loud. He read names. Our names. Mrs. Aldery. Mr. Pell. The Tanners. The Fenns. Old Sorrel who lived by the bridge. He read eleven names and said the rest of us were guilty of the same crime by silence, which Papa says isn't a real crime anywhere except—" she paused, searching, "—except wherever he was from."

"And then?"

"Then the shooting started at the far end of the line and moved toward our door, and Papa turned me around and pushed me down the well shaft. There's a ledge. For cleaning. Papa stood on it when he cleaned the bucket chains. He said stay on the ledge and count to five hundred and don't come up no matter what, and if anyone looks down, be water." She said *be water* the way children recite charms, words given to them whole by adults who needed them to stick. "Water doesn't have a face."

The cloth square in her hands had become a knot.

"It took a long time for it to stop," she said. "Longer than five hundred. I lost my place twice. When I climbed up, the well rope had been cut short and tied off, so I had to use the stones. Everyone was lying down like it was nap time except they were in the wrong places, in the open, in the square, and the blood had run into the well grate, which meant it was running down toward me, and I thought—" her voice did not break; it thinned, like wire drawn fine, "—I thought they were filling up my hiding place. With everyone. Like the well was a pocket."

Marisol rose without seeming to decide to, crossed the room, and knelt beside the chair. She didn't take the child's hands. She laid her own on the table, palm up, an offering rather than a claim.

"Who did you find after?" she whispered.

"A dog first. Then nobody. I stayed in the orchard above the village three days. I ate apples even though they weren't ready. On the fourth day I went back down because I thought maybe it was like the shelling and everyone was just waiting for the gaps to get long enough." She looked at Marisol directly then, and her eyes were terribly clear. "But they didn't dig anyone out. They didn't even fill the well back in. They just left. And then a cart came with men who threw lime on everything, and one of them saw me and shouted, and I ran, and after that I walked toward where the war was loudest, because I thought, if I find the right soldier, the one who reads names, he'll know everyone's names, and he'll fix the mistake."

"The mistake being—"

"That he read only eleven," Hazel said. "There were forty-three. Somebody has to read the other thirty-two or they'll think those people never existed. And then I can't exist properly either, because Papa always said we only really exist if someone remembers us right."

She smoothed the knotted cloth flat against the table with one small hand, pressing out creases that wouldn't leave.

"The man with the grey gloves," she said. "He had a medal ribbon. Red with white edges. And a scar on his wrist like a hook. And he smelled of oil of cloves. Papa said officers smell of oil of cloves when they've been to the dentist in the capital, and only rich ones go." A pause. "I would know him again. Anywhere. Even if I only heard his gloves."

* * *

Jonah did not remember walking back upstairs. His body had simply removed itself from the corridor and carried him along the ward passage while his mind stayed kneeling at that kitchen table, listening to a seven-year-old enumerate her dead with the terrible bookkeeping precision of a child who had been taught that counting was survival.

Forty-three. Eleven names read aloud. Thirty-two erased — not by bullets alone but by omission, by the quiet arithmetic of official reports that listed Harrow Bend as *an engagement resulting in regrettable civilian losses, numbers indeterminate.* Indeterminate. He had copied that word himself, months ago, transcribing the district summary into his ledger with a note: **numbers NOT indeterminate — count unavailable.** He had thought that was the work. Write down what was missing. Keep the names that survived. Stay invisible. Stay safe.

He stood now at the window at the end of Ward Six, where Sergeant Brice slept in the shallow tide between morphine and waking, and looked out at the orchard, black and silver under a thin moon, and understood that his work had been a coward's version of remembering all along.

A ledger under a floorboard. Names written in pencil, in a code of his own devising, hidden where no one would ever read them unless he died and someone luckier dug. He had called it defiance. It had been archiving. Archiving was what you did when you had already conceded the present tense — when you had decided the record mattered more than the living, because the record couldn't be punished and the living could.

Hazel had walked out of a massacre and crossed a burning country not to hide, but to find someone who would *read the names.* A child of seven had done more than a grown man with a floorboard and a pencil. She hadn't conceded anything. She was still fighting for the thirty-two.

And the man who had read eleven names — church-loud, grey gloves, red ribbon edged in white, scar like a hook, oil of cloves — was sleeping thirty feet and one floor away, convalescing on the honor of a grateful army, charming the matrons, signing the reports that said *indeterminate.*

Major Aldous Vane.

Jonah had suspected. The drawing — smoke, soldiers, the grey glove rendered in furious charcoal strokes — had made suspicion unavoidable. But suspicion was survivable. It floated at a distance, a hypothesis, a thing you could carry without it changing your weight. What he had heard tonight was not suspicion. It was testimony. Specific, dated, placed. A wrist scar. A dental scent. An officer dismounting in a village square and reading his neighbors' death warrants off a prepared list, which meant it was planned, which meant there were orders, which meant there was a chain of paper somewhere with Vane's name on it, and that paper would burn with everything else unless—

Unless someone moved it first.

Sergeant Brice stirred and murmured. Jonah went to him automatically, wetted his lips, adjusted the blanket, checked the dressing on a wound that was slowly, patiently winning. Brice's eyes opened halfway.

"You look like a man seen a ghost, Nurse Underhill," he rasped.

"Worse," Jonah said. "I've heard one."

Brice studied him with the flat frankness of the dying, who no longer spend energy on politeness. "You've been writing things down. I've watched you. Don't insult us both by denying it." He waited until Jonah neither confirmed nor fled. "I was at Harrow Bend. Garrison detail. I was on the wall when the column came in, and I refused the order, and Corporal Dengate refused louder, and they put us both against the granary for it." His mouth twisted. "Only one of us got up again. You want to know what happened at Harrow Bend? Ask me tomorrow, before the light's good. My memory works best at dawn lately. And bring your book, Underhill. Bring it out where it can be read."

He closed his eyes, spent. Within a minute his breathing evened into sleep.

Jonah stood a long moment in the dark ward, listening to the ragged chorus of men breathing, and felt the last strut of his careful neutrality give way beneath him — not with a crash but quietly, the way a floorboard goes, all at once and without ceremony.

He would not write this down and hide it. Writing down was finished. Tomorrow at dawn he would take a deposition from a dying sergeant with the lamp lit and the page turned outward, and Marisol's hand steadying the pen, and Hazel keeping vigil because she had earned the right to hear every name. They would build the record in the open, in the ward, among witnesses, where it could not be quietly buried with him.

And then he would deal with Major Vane.

Not with a knife — he was a nurse; his whole craft was against it. With paper. With testimony. With forty-three names spoken aloud in rooms where powerful men were trying very hard to hear none of them. It would likely destroy him. Reassignment, prison, worse; men like Vane did not tolerate loose pages. He found, examining himself honestly the way he examined a wound, that he no longer minded. Invisibility had been a craft. It had also been a hiding place, and hiding places, he now understood, were just graves you occupied early.

Down the corridor, faint under the ward sounds, he could still almost hear it: a child's voice, church-quiet, saying *somebody has to read the other thirty-two.*

At dawn, Jonah Underhill intended to start reading.
