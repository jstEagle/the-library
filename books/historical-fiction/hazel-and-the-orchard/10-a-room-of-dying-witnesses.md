## 10. A Room of Dying Witnesses

The corporal died on a Tuesday, which is how I learned to stop waiting for the right time.

There is no right time to ask a man to spend his last breaths making a record no one has authorized. There is only the time you have. And Corporal Elmer Voss had eleven days of it left when I pulled the screen around his cot and asked him if he would tell me what happened at Harrow Bend.

He looked at me the way dying men look at nurses who wake them—mildly offended, then amused, then something else. Something older.

"You're Underhill," he said. "The one who writes at night."

My hand went still on his pulse. "I don't know what you mean."

"Course you don't." He smiled with half his mouth; the other half had stopped working some days ago. "You think nobody sees you. That's your whole trick, isn't it? The invisible man." His eyes went to my apron pocket, where the corner of nothing showed, because there was nothing there—I'd learned that much discipline. "Saw you copying names off the death lists before the officers struck them. Thought you were a spy for the other side. Took me three weeks to work out which side that was."

"And which side is that?"

"The side of the dead." He said it without bitterness, the way a man reports the weather. "Somebody ought to be on it."

I sat down on the stool beside his cot. Through the screen I could hear the ward going about its business—the rattle of the trolley, Marisol's voice from the kitchen passage calling someone to come eat before it went cold, the small wet breathing of forty men who would mostly not see spring. I had done this before, in a way. Every name in my ledger was an interrogation conducted in silence, a question asked of paperwork instead of people. Paperwork lies politely. People lie generously, out of fear or shame or the simple animal wish to have been somewhere else. But they also tell the truth, sometimes, if you sit down and let them.

"I'm not collecting accusations," I said. "I want names. What you saw. Who you saw. If all you can give me is a street and a smell, I'll take it."

Voss studied me for a long moment. Then he said, "Get your book."

* * *

We started at dusk, when the officers withdraw to the east wing and the ward belongs to the orderlies. I wrote while he spoke, and he spoke slowly, the way men do when they are carrying something heavy downhill and must set it down carefully or not at all.

He had not been at Harrow Bend itself. He had been in the second column, two miles out, close enough to hear it. Close enough, he said, to hear the pause after the first volley—the pause where the birds should have gone up and didn't.

"There was a woman came up the road toward us," he said. "Running. An officer's aide rode her down rather than stop. I remember thinking—that's the moment it becomes real, isn't it. When you run over somebody to keep it quiet." His breathing caught. I waited. Waiting was the whole skill. "Afterward we bivouacked near the village well. You could smell it. They told us it was cholera. Cholera in a burned square in October." He laughed once, a dry click. "I've had cholera, sir. It doesn't smell like that."

I wrote it as he gave it: *second column, two miles, heard the volley, woman ridden down on the Harrow road by an officer's aide, bivouac near the well.* I did not write *the officer* yet, because he hadn't said it. I have learned not to lead witnesses. A led testimony is worth less than none; it's a mirror, and mirrors show whoever is holding them.

"Who gave the order?" I asked, finally, when the silence had ripened.

"I don't know." And then, because dying men are past the economy of the living: "But I know whose aide rode the woman down. Tam Rennie. And Rennie served Vane. Everyone knew Rennie served Vane, because Rennie wouldn't stop saying so, even after."

"After what?"

"After they transferred him for it." His eyes found mine, and there was apology in them, apology for having so little to give. "That's all I have, Underhill. A smell, a horse, a transfer. Is it enough?"

"It's more than the Ministry will have," I said, and regretted it the moment it left my mouth, because it made me a conspirator out loud, in front of a witness. Voss just nodded, satisfied, like a man who has successfully donated blood.

Marisol appeared at the end of the screen with a cup of barley water. She read the whole scene in one pass—me, the notebook, the dying corporal—and her face did the thing it does, which is nothing, which is everything. She set the cup down and stood there.

"He should eat," she said. "And you should write neater. If a man's last words are going into a book, they deserve better handwriting than a prescription."

Then she took the pencil out of my hand, pulled the stool over, and began transcribing the rest of Voss's account herself, in her round, stubborn, kitchen-ledger script, asking him to slow down when he slurred, correcting dates against the ward register she kept in her head. She had been keeping accounts at Wrenfield since before the army arrived. Of course she would keep these.

When Voss slept, she handed the pages back to me and said, "The chaplain's assistant next. Prewitt. He's been trying to confess to me for a week and I keep telling him I'm not ordained. You are, practically."

"I'm a nurse."

"You're a man with a book," she said. "In this place, that's ordination."

* * *

Prewitt was twenty-three and looked seventeen, and he cried before I finished asking the first question, which I had expected, and kept talking through the crying, which I had not.

He had been at Harrow Bend. Not in the column—in the village, behind the officers, carrying the chaplain's kit, because the plan had included a chaplain, which means the plan had included bodies. That detail alone was worth the risk of the whole enterprise. Somebody had planned for the dead the way quartermasters plan for boots.

"There was a list," he said. His hands moved as he talked, conducting some invisible choir. "A written list, sir. Names of men in the village who'd sheltered the other side's wounded over the summer. The major had it in a leather folio. He read the names aloud in the square. Like a—" he swallowed— "like a muster. And for every name, men were brought out of the houses. It was orderly. That's the thing nobody—everyone thinks it was madness, but it wasn't, it was *order*, it was the worst kind, it was order doing exactly what order does best."

"Who read the names?"

"You know who read the names." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, furious at himself, perhaps, for making me ask twice. "Major Vane. Grey gloves. He used to pull the left glove tighter before he spoke, like a man settling his cuff before dinner. I can still—" his hands made the gesture, small and precise— "I can still see it. The tug. Every night I see the tug."

Beside the cot, where she had installed herself two evenings ago and refused all instruction to leave, Hazel sat very still. She had the doll in her lap. Her eyes were on Prewitt's hands.

"Hazel," I said gently, "you don't have to be here."

She didn't move. She never obeyed instructions she didn't agree with; she simply declined to acknowledge them. Marisol, arriving with fresh water, put a hand on the girl's shoulder—not to remove her, I noticed, but to anchor her.

"She's listening," Marisol said quietly. "Let her listen. She's the only one in this room who'll still be alive to say it in twenty years."

The arithmetic of that landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. I almost lost my place on the page.

Prewitt gave me the rest in fragments, each one polished by repetition: the folio, the muster of names, the well ("they used the old well behind the smithy, it was deep, and the major said—" here he stopped, and I thought he would refuse, and then he spent the words anyway— "he said, 'Wells hold water, not evidence.'"), the refusal by a lieutenant—I got the name, Brack, though Prewitt didn't know what became of him—and the firing line for the men who'd been mustered, and the fire afterward, set to make the record match the story: plague, uprising, whatever the dispatch finally settled on.

"He dictated the dispatch that same evening," Prewitt said. "At supper. I carried the ink. He wrote it like a man writing a menu. 'Regrettable necessity.' Fourteen words. I counted them. I count things now, when I'm frightened. There were fourteen words in the official account of ninety-one people."

Ninety-one. I wrote it down. My ledger had thirty-eight names for Harrow Bend, gathered from struck entries and rumor and Hazel's charcoal squares. Ninety-one was a number from inside the square. It was the number the village itself would have given, if villages could testify.

"Will you sign it?" I asked him. "What you've told me. Sign your name under it."

He looked at me as if I'd offered him communion. "No one's ever asked for my name on anything except laundry lists."

He signed. Marisel witnessed. Hazel, unasked, pressed one small finger below the signatures, leaving a faint grey smudge—charcoal dust, from her drawing—so that the page carried all three of them in some form: ink, ink, ash.

* * *

The woman from the village was named Tamsin Broke, and she was not dying of anything the war had done to her body directly, but the body keeps its own ledgers, and hers was closing. She had been brought in six weeks ago with a shell wound to the hip, and infection had made of the wound a long slow argument her blood was losing.

She was the first witness I'd taken who had been *in* the square rather than near it, and I confess I approached her differently—with a gentleness I hadn't earned, tiptoeing through the questions, offering her exits at every turn. She refused all of them.

"Nurse," she said, on the third evening, "stop being careful with me. I've been shot. Careful is worse."

So I stopped. And she talked, propped on her elbows, in a flat steady voice, about the morning the soldiers came: the muster of names read aloud, her husband's name among them—he had buried two deserters in his orchard in June and told no one, and someone had told anyway—and the walking to the wall, and the sound.

I won't transcribe here what she said about the sound. It is in the deposition, in her own words, because she insisted on giving it plainly, and Marisol wrote every word without flinching, and I will not edit a dead woman's testimony to spare anyone who reads this. But I will say what she said at the end, when I asked whether she wanted anything omitted:

"Put it all in. Every word. Because that's what they're counting on, isn't it? That nobody can bear to write it down, and so it turns to smoke, and smoke turns to nothing, and nothing turns to 'regrettable necessity.'" She lay back, exhausted, her hand finding the edge of the blanket. "Words are all any of us have left now. Don't you dare ration mine."

Hazel sat vigil through all of it. By the fourth evening the others had begun arranging themselves around her presence without comment—an extra stool dragged near, a shawl appearing over her shoulders, Marisol's suppers arriving with two portions instead of one. Word travels in wards the way damp travels through walls. The men knew what the screens meant. Some pretended not to; most, I think, found it eased them. There is a difference between dying anonymously and dying *into* something. We were building something into which a man could die, and it was made of paper and lamplight and a mute child's patience and a cook's terrible handwriting, and it was sturdier than any dispatch the Ministry would ever file.

One night I looked up from the pages and saw the room truly: Prewitt asleep with his signed statement folded under his pillow like a letter to God; Voss's breathing audible even through two screens; Tamsin awake, watching Hazel sketch by candle-end; Marisol mending a shirt that belonged to no one in particular, keeping vigil over the vigils. Five people and a stack of paper, holding the door against forgetting.

Found family, Marisol would have called it, if I'd had the courage to say such a thing aloud, which I didn't. But the shape of it was unmistakable. I had spent years making myself a crack in the floorboard, and here was a household built inside the crack.

* * *

It was Hazel who taught me the last lesson of that week, though she taught it silently, as she taught everything.

She had begun organizing the depositions. Not reading them—or so I believed—but sorting them: Tamsin's pages flattened, weighted under the doll; Prewitt's squared to the table's edge; the charcoal drawings interleaved like maps accompanying a text. One evening I found her sitting cross-legged on the floor with the whole thin archive spread around her, and she was pointing—at Voss's account, then at her drawing of the road, then at Prewitt's mention of Rennie's horse—connecting them. Witness to witness to witness. Building, with a child's finger, the thing I had been too cautious to build: a single account, assembled out of many mouths, each corroborating the next until the seams disappeared and it became not fragments but a *record*.

"She's editing you," Marisol said from the doorway, and there was a laugh in her voice, and something underneath the laugh that wasn't laughing at all.

"She's better at this than I am."

"Course she is. She's the archive, love. We're just the clerks."

I gathered the pages, squared them, and wrapped them in oilcloth. And it was while I was tying the string that I understood, with the particular cold clarity that comes after warmth, what we had become. Not a hospital. A target. Every signature in that oilcloth bundle was a man painting a mark on his own back, and the youngest of my witnesses couldn't lift her head above the parapet at all, and the man whose aide rode down a woman on the Harrow Road slept thirty feet away with a folio full of names and a habit of winning.

"We need to move faster," I told Marisol. "Before the historians come. Before whatever's coming comes."

"We'll need help hiding it, then. Properly hidden. Not floorboards." She took the bundle from me, weighed it in her hands like bread dough. "My father knows places in the orchard older than this manor. And Hazel—" she glanced at the girl, already back at her drawing, the candle flame doubled in her dark eyes— "Hazel can stand lookout better than any sentry they've got. Nobody ever watches the child nobody watches."

I should have known then that the quiet couldn't hold. Instead I banked the fire, and we blew out the candles, and the ward settled into its breathing.

It was Prewitt who broke it, two mornings later. I came on duty to find Marisol at the end of his cot, very still, holding a folded paper she would not let the orderlies touch, and looking at me across forty feet of ward with an expression I had never seen on her face.

"Underhill," she said. "He's been writing all night. You'd better come read what the boy remembered."
