## 11. The Historians Arrive

They came on a Tuesday, in three carriages with the Ministry's grey-and-gold painted on the doors, and I knew before anyone spoke a word that we were finished pretending Wrenfield was anything but what it had always been: a place where things went to be forgotten.

I was changing dressings in Ward Two when Marisol came to find me. She didn't say it outright. She never did. She set down the tray she'd carried past four soldiers without being challenged—another small miracle of her invisibility—and said, "Visitors. Fancy ones. Vane's already combing his hair."

"Combing it," I repeated.

"With both hands," she said, and left.

I finished the dressing. That was the discipline of it, the thing no one tells you about fear: you finish the dressing first. The boy in the bed—Corporal Enns, nineteen, one lung—watched my hands work and then watched my face.

"You look like you've seen a well-dressed ghost," he managed.

"Worse," I said. "A well-dressed living one."

* * *

By the time I reached the front hall they had already begun their transformation of the manor, and I mean that literally. Three historians stood in the entrance while orderlies hurried about them like stagehands, and Major Aldous Vane—his wounded hand gloved in grey kid leather, immaculate—held forth in the warm voice he used for donors and widows.

"Gentlemen. Wrenfield has been honored to serve." He gestured at the staircase as though presenting a painting. "The house retains much of its original character. We felt it important not to disturb the dignity of the place."

The dignity of the place. I leaned against the doorframe and looked at the three of them properly. The eldest was silver-haired, heavyset, with the satisfied air of a man who had already decided what happened and merely needed witnesses to confirm it. The middle one carried a satchel and a folding table's worth of forms strapped to his back like a mule. And the youngest—youngest by fifteen years at least—stood slightly apart, turning his hat in his hands, looking not at Vane but at the corridor beyond him, where a line of cots was visible through an open door.

He was looking at the patients. Neither of the other two had looked at the patients yet.

"Gentleman," Vane said, and then he saw me, and his smile did not change but something behind it adjusted, the way a helmsman adjusts for current. "Ah. Underhill. Our head nurse."

The word *head*. The leash pulled taut and I felt it at my throat even as I bowed.

"This is Mr. Harrowgate," Vane continued, indicating the elder, "Mr. Pell, and Mr. Ashcombe. Ministry of Records and Reconciliation. They've come to compile the official account of Wrenfield's service to the war effort."

"An honor," I said, because it was expected, and because I had long ago learned that the most useful lies are the ones everyone can see through equally.

Harrowgate shook my hand with the grip of a man greeting a servant he would not remember. "You'll have records, I assume. Ledgers of admission, discharge, death?"

"I keep the ledgers of death myself," I said. "No one else wanted the work."

"How industrious." He smiled at Vane. "You see? Some institutions still take pride in thoroughness."

Vane's smile widened. Somewhere beneath it, I understood, we were both thinking of the same floorboard.

* * *

They toured the manor all morning, and I use the word *tour* precisely, because that is what it was: a curated route through a fiction. Vane led them down corridors that had been scrubbed, past wards where the worst cases had been moved out of sight an hour earlier—I know because I was given the task of moving them, and I did it, and I hated myself with a clean and efficient hatred while I did it. Enns went to the east wing on a stretcher, complaining the whole way, which I found bracing. The dying are permitted complaints. It's one of their few remaining privileges.

"The mortality figures," Pell was saying as they walked, pencil hovering over his forms. "You'll want to present those broken down by month, Major. The Ministry prefers seasonal analysis."

"Of course. Underhill maintains our figures."

"Underhill," Harrowgate said again, tasting the name without interest. "Efficient fellow?"

"Indispensable," Vane said warmly. "You find one man in ten years who can look at suffering all day and still write a straight column. I'd be lost without him."

Every word true. Every word a nail.

In the great hall they stopped, and I stopped with them, because Hazel was there.

She shouldn't have been. Marisol kept her in the kitchen when soldiers were about; that arrangement had held for weeks. But there she stood at the foot of the stairs, small and mud-brown in a dress that had once been someone else's, holding her charcoal stick the way other children hold a talisman, staring up at the banners that orderlies had hung from the minstrels' gallery overnight—the regimental colors, arranged just so, so that a photograph taken from the proper angle would show a house at war and not a house full of holes.

Ashcombe saw her first. He was nearest the stairs. He looked down at her and she looked up at him, and neither of them said anything, and I watched the youngest historian's face do something the older two's faces did not: it changed.

"That's—" Pell began.

"A local child," Vane said smoothly. "The groundskeeper's daughter's charge. She's mute. Tragic case, shell-fire near her village. She stays here for her own safety." He extended his grey glove toward the stairs as if shooing a pigeon. "Marisol! Take Hazel to the kitchen, there's a good girl."

Marisol appeared as if summoned by witchcraft, which perhaps she was, and took Hazel's hand. But Hazel didn't move immediately. She kept looking at the banners, and then at the men beneath them, and then—only briefly, only for a heartbeat—at me. And in that heartbeat she did the thing that terrified me more than anything the morning had yet produced.

She put her charcoal stick back in her pocket. Deliberately. Like a woman putting away evidence.

Then she let Marisol lead her away.

"Remarkably composed," Harrowgate observed. "For a mute."

"They're often calmest ones," Vane said. "Shall we see the orchard? The trees predate the war. There's something steadying about them."

* * *

The orchard tour I was not invited on. Instead I was summoned to the administrative room—a room that had been a drawing room before the army needed somewhere to deny things in writing—and handed a typed requisition with three signatures at the bottom and one at the top, and the one at the top was Vane's.

**ALL NON-REGIMENTAL RECORDS TO BE SURRENDERED FOR MINISTRY REVIEW PRIOR TO COMPILATION OF OFFICIAL ACCOUNT. LEDGERS OF DEATH TO BE SUBMITTED IN FINAL FORM. NO ANNOTATIONS. NO SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIALS.**

"No annotations," Harrowgate repeated, when I read it aloud. "We've had trouble, in other facilities, with nurses adding... commentary. Personal notes. Sentiment. It complicates the arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," I said.

"Deaths," he said comfortably. "The war produced a great many, Mr. Underhill, and the Ministry's task is to make them legible. A name in a column is legible. A name in a column with a paragraph of grief attached is—" he waved his pen—"literature. You're not a literary man, are you?"

"No," I said. "I'm a nurse."

"Precisely." He beamed. "So you'll understand that your ledger must contain names, dates, causes. Nothing else. The dead of Wrenfield will be very well served by a clean record. Better served than by sentiment."

I thought of Sergeant Brann, whose last words I had written between two dates in small careful letters because the margin allowed it. I thought of the woman from the village whose name appeared in my ledger twice—once as a casualty and once, in the second ledger, the hidden one, as a witness. I thought of every annotation Harrowgate would call sentiment and I had called the only prayer I knew how to make.

"When do you require the final form?" I asked.

"Three days," he said. "Major Vane suggests you'll need the time to ensure its tidiness." His smile was kind, entirely kind, the kindness of a man who believed sincerely that he was doing good work. That was the worst of it. Monsters are rare. Clerks are everywhere, and clerks burn more than fires do.

"Three days," I agreed.

Pell cleared his throat over his forms. "One further matter. The Ministry has received"—he checked a paper—"certain inquiries regarding the village of Harrow Bend. A garrison action, autumn of last year. Your facility admitted personnel from that engagement?"

"Some," I said carefully.

"Any civilians from the village itself?"

The room got very quiet, or I did, which amounts to the same thing. Through the window I could see the orchard, and the touring party moving among the trees like figures in someone else's dream, Vane's grey glove gesturing at the blossoms.

"One child," I said. "A girl. She doesn't speak."

"Doesn't or won't?"

"I'm a nurse, Mr. Pell, not a mind-reader. She doesn't speak."

"And her name?"

"Hazel," I said. "Just Hazel. No family name was given. She was left at our door with no papers." Which was true, and which I had verified was true, and which was therefore the safest sentence I had spoken all day.

Pell wrote it down. *Hazel. No surname. Mute.* And I watched the machinery do its work: a girl reduced to three words, filed, cross-referenced, closed. By Friday she would be a footnote. By spring she would be nothing at all.

"There were reports," Pell said mildly, not looking up, "of irregularities at Harrow Bend. Civilian losses exceeding regulation expectations. The inquiry was discontinued for insufficient evidence."

"Was it."

"You seem interested, Mr. Underhill."

"I'm interested in everything that comes through these doors," I said. "It's the occupation."

He looked up at me then, and for a moment—one thin, dangerous moment—something moved behind his clerk's eyes that might have been curiosity or might have been warning. "The Ministry discontinued the inquiry," he repeated. "It would take new evidence to reopen it. Considerable evidence. From credible witnesses." He returned to his forms. "Which rarely survive wars. As I'm sure you know."

I did not answer. There was no safe answer. But I carried that sentence out of the room like contraband, turning it over in my hands all afternoon: *it would take new evidence.* Not *there is no evidence.* A clerk does not specify the conditions of impossibility unless he has been asked to leave the door unlocked.

Or unless it is bait.

* * *

That evening I found Vane waiting for me in the corridor outside Ward Six, alone, which was itself a message. He had a bottle of something good under his arm and the expression of a host ensuring his guests are comfortable.

"They're gone to bed," he said. "Harrowgate snores, I'm told. Tomorrow they interview staff. I've prepared the list—twelve names, all reliable. Yours is on it."

"Generous."

"It's efficient." He uncorked the bottle, poured nothing for either of us, and held it anyway, a prop in search of a scene. "Underhill. Three days. I want to show you something, and then I want to say something, and then we can both go to sleep."

He led me into the ward—not to a patient's bedside but to the window—and pointed out at the dark, where the burn pile behind the orchard was still faintly visible if you knew where to look: a low mound of ash the rain hadn't yet finished with.

"Letters," he said. "Patients' letters home. Six years of them. Do you know why they burned, officially?" He didn't wait. "Because unverified correspondence compromises the historical record. That's the phrase. *Compromises the historical record.* I signed the order myself." He turned to me, and in the dark his face was genuinely, horribly friendly. "Here is what I want to say. In three days, this house becomes history. Real history. The kind with covers and indexes and a memorial plaque in the capital. What we did here will be remembered forever, Underhill—precisely, correctly, forever. All you have to do is give Harrowgate the ledger he asked for. Clean columns. Straight arithmetic."

"And everything else?"

"Everything else," Vane said, "is literature."

He clapped my shoulder with his gloved hand—the grey glove, the glove—and walked away whistling something I almost recognized, and I stood in the doorway of Ward Six listening to six men breathe in their sleep, and I thought: he's right. That's the trap. He's entirely right. Clean columns survive; annotations rot; the archive always wins.

But archives are kept by people. And people can be young, and troubled by burn piles, and given conditions of impossibility that leave doors unlocked.

I went back to my room and knelt and lifted the floorboard, and my second ledger lay where I had left it, undisturbed, fat with names. Three days. Enough time to copy it? Perhaps. Enough time to decide who could be trusted with it—that was the real question, and it had exactly one candidate, a silent child who put away her charcoal like a woman hiding evidence.

Tomorrow they interview staff. Twelve reliable names.

Mine was on the list. So, I noticed, checking Vane's neat handwriting by candlelight, was Marisol's.

And so, in a different ink, added later in a different hand—

was Hazel's.
