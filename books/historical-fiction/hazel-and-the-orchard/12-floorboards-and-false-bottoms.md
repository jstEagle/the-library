## 12. Floorboards and False Bottoms

The flour bin was where I kept the pages, folded flat between the muslin and the wall, because no soldier in the history of soldiers had ever searched a flour bin properly. They'd plunge a hand in, come up ghost-white to the wrist, curse, and move on. I'd watched three of them do it. It was almost entertainment.

So when I lifted the lid that morning and found the muslin shifted—folded wrong, creased where I never creased it—I stood very still for a long moment with my hands in the cold grain and listened to the kitchen around me.

Everything sounded normal. The kettle ticking. Hazel at the table, drawing. The boy Tibbs hauling water in from the pump, singing to himself off-key the way he always did, bless him.

But someone had been in my bin. Someone had put their hands into my flour and lifted my pages and read them, or nearly read them, and put them back badly, the way a man does who doesn't cook and doesn't care how things sit.

I took the pages out. Counted them. Eleven depositions, all there, all intact. Whoever it was had either been interrupted or hadn't known what he was holding—or hadn't found what he was looking for. Because these weren't the ledger. These were only copies, the ones Jonah and I had agreed to keep in the kitchen, duplicates of the depositions we'd taken in Ward Six. The real book, the one with every name going back two years, lived somewhere only Jonah knew.

Except now somebody wanted to know where.

I dried my hands and went out through the scullery, past the copper and the dripping linens, and crossed the yard to the orchard wall where Jonah was supposed to be checking the cold frames but was instead standing under the bare apple trees with his hands behind his back, staring at nothing, which was his way of thinking.

"They've been in my flour," I said.

He didn't startle. He never startled; it was one of the first things I'd noticed about him, back before I knew why a man learns not to startle. "When?"

"Sometime yesterday. Between the midday bread and the evening dishing. The pages are fine. But they were moved."

"Who has kitchen access?"

"You know who has kitchen access. Half the garrison wanders through wanting something hot." I lowered my voice as an orderly trundled past with a barrow of kindling. "But it wasn't wandering. Wandering men take food. This one took measurements."

Jonah turned a leaf over with his boot. Above us, rooks argued in the elms along the wall. "Vane's aide," he said. "Lieutenant Fenwick. He's been asking after me."

"I know. He asked Mrs. Hobb if the nurse writes letters home. He asked Tibbs if the nurse keeps accounts. He asked—" I stopped.

"He asked what?"

"He asked Marisol Quill whether her father's cider press still works." I watched that land on him. "A press, Jonah. In October. With half the orchard rotting on the ground and nobody pressing anything for anyone."

Jonah was quiet a moment. Then he did the thing I both admired and dreaded about him: he smiled, faintly, like a man finding a coin in the street. "Then we should make sure it works," he said. "Thoroughly. Before anyone checks it for themselves."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about the cider press at Wrenfield: it was older than the manor's present owner, older than the war, possibly older than most of the people fighting in it. A great oak screw-press under a slatted timber shelter at the orchard's far end, with a trough of weathered stone and a mash chest you could have buried a man in. My father had kept it oiled and true because his father had before him, and because in this country you do not let a working thing rot just because the times are against working things.

It also had a false bottom already.

Not a secret one—my father called it the straining bed, a loose slatted panel that sat above the true bottom of the trough so the pomace could be raked out. Every press had one. Which meant, Jonah said, eyes bright in that quiet ferocious way of his, that nobody would ever look for another.

We chose a grey Tuesday for it, wind coming up cold from the east, the kind of day when everyone with rank stayed indoors near a fire and everyone without rank found reasons to be elsewhere. Jonah brought the tools wrapped in a sack that looked, to any casual eye, like a nurse carrying laundry. I brought the kitchen knife I used for splitting apples and a lump of beeswax and my father's old oil can.

The plan was simple, which is the only kind of plan that survives contact with a garrison.

The false bed would be rebuilt a hand's width higher than the original, leaving a sealed space beneath—deep enough for the ledger, the depositions, the chaplain's letter if it came to that, everything we couldn't burn and couldn't be found holding. The gap would be closed with a new board cut to match the others, planed down, waxed, and rubbed with pomace leavings until it looked exactly as old and filthy as its neighbors. Then the whole thing would be topped with a layer of actual crushed apples, because a press full of pulp is a press nobody wants to inspect closely.

"The genius," Jonah said, kneeling in the trough with the pry bar, "is that it's filthy."

"I'm glad my family's press offends you."

"It doesn't offend me. It protects us. Nobody searches inside something that smells like this." He worked the old straining bed loose and lifted it out, and we looked together at the clean stone bottom, pale where the light fell through the slats, untouched by anything in years.

It looked like a grave waiting to be filled. I didn't say so. He was thinking it too—I could tell by the way he didn't say it either.

He measured. I marked. We'd brought a board salvaged from a broken crate behind the stables, and while I planed it at the shelter's edge with the spokeshave, keeping my strokes slow and even the way my father taught me—let the tool do the work, Mari, rushing is how fingers get lost—Jonah fitted wax and oilcloth along the seams of the hidden space so damp wouldn't touch whatever we laid in there.

"You've done this before," I said.

"Fitted boards?"

"Hidden things in places that smell bad enough to be safe."

His spokeshave paused, then resumed. "My mother hid letters from my father during the last trouble. In the butter crock." A small breath of a laugh. "He never once checked the butter. Twenty years of marriage and he believed whatever was in front of him."

"And your mother?"

"Read them by candlelight after he slept." He set the plane down and blew shavings off the board, and for a moment he looked younger, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "She said hiding a thing isn't lying, Mari. It's keeping. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

I ran my thumb along the edge I'd cut. Sharp, true, flush. "Your mother sounds like she'd have made a decent cook."

"She'd have liked you." He said it simply, and then we both got back to work, because there is only so much tenderness two people can stand on a cold morning when soldiers might walk past at any moment.

* * *

Which, of course, one did.

I heard him before I saw him—the particular jingle of a man wearing good boots he wasn't used to, leather creaking, steps too deliberate for the mud. I put two fingers in my mouth and gave the low whistle Hazel and I had agreed on, three notes falling, like a blackbird with opinions. Somewhere up the row, I heard the whistle answered.

Then Lieutenant Fenwick came around the corner of the shelter with his clipboard soul and his captain's ambitions, and I was alone in view, elbow-deep in a barrel of windfall apples, and Hazel was gone.

She was magnificent. I want that recorded somewhere other than Jonah's ledger, since his is spoken for: that girl, who had not spoken a word to anyone for months, who had learned invisibility the hard way that children learn terrible things, understood exactly what was being asked of her. She came up the orchard row toward the shelter leading Tibbs by the hand, pointing urgently at the branches, and Tibbs—good, confused, loyal Tibbs—caught on within seconds and started exclaiming over the apples left high in the crown of the oldest tree, the ones nobody could reach.

"Miss," Hazel insisted, tugging Fenwick's sleeve when he slowed. Not words, precisely—she didn't waste them—but gestures, urgent ones, a child with a crisis only a soldier could solve.

Fenwick had the look of a man who resented every second away from his clipboard. "What is it?"

Hazel pointed up. Tibbs pointed up. Both of them wore expressions of such profound apple-related distress that Fenwick, who I judged to be roughly nineteen and recently promoted out of some county regiment, actually sighed and said, "You want those?"

They nodded. In unison. It was beautiful.

"There's a ladder in the stable yard," I called out pleasantly from my barrel, giving him a reason to walk in the opposite direction from the press. "Though I wouldn't, sir—the last officer who climbed for windfalls tore his trousers on the way down, and there's no tailor nearer than Ashcombe."

That decided him. Nineteen-year-old lieutenants fear torn trousers more than bullets; I've seen it proven. He muttered something about urchins and fruit and walked on toward the house, and Hazel and Tibbs watched him go with faces of perfect innocence, and the moment he rounded the wall Hazel looked back at me and grinned.

Grinned. Wide and crooked and alive. First one I'd ever seen on her.

I had to turn back to my apples so she wouldn't see what it did to my face.

* * *

By late afternoon the false bed was down.

We worked the last of it in silence, communicating in the shorthand of long acquaintance and short hours: a tilt of the head meant *look*, a flat palm meant *wait*, Jonah touching his ear meant *someone coming* and once meant *no, just a pheasant*, which nearly undid me.

The new board sat flush. You truly could not tell it apart from its brothers unless you knew—which we did, and which we proved to each other twice, pressing the corner nearest the screw, where the secret catch lived: a shallow notch cut into the underside of the third slat, hooked to a wooden lever Jonah had shaped from an old tent peg. Push the lever, the bed tilted. Rake the pomace over it, and even the lever disappeared.

Then the apples. We crushed two barrels' worth in the honest part of the press, the sweet-sharp reek of it filling the shelter, pulp heaped over the false bed until the whole trough looked like what it was—a working press caught mid-season by a war that had better things to do. Jonah's sleeves were rolled to the elbow, juice to the shoulder, hair stuck down at his temple, and I thought he looked, for the first time since I'd known him, like a man doing exactly what he was meant to do rather than a man hiding from what he'd done.

"We should press for real," I said. "Make cider. Offer Vane a glass with his own hands."

"Why?"

"Because a man watches what he drinks. And while he watches his glass, he isn't watching us." I wiped my hands on my apron. "My mother's trick. She said the best place to hide a thing is in plain sight of the person looking for it."

Jonah considered that. "Your family," he said, "has an entire philosophy built out of kitchens."

"Somebody has to fight the war properly."

He laughed then—short, rusty, real—and pressed the lever, and the false bed tilted, and we looked down together at the empty dark where everything that mattered would live. He laid the depositions in first, wrapped in oilcloth, then waited, watching me.

"What?"

"You should put something in," he said. "Something of yours. So it's not only mine."

I thought about it. Then I took the pencil stub from my apron pocket and the scrap of paper I'd written that morning before dawn, and I added it to the bundle: a list of my own. Names of the village boys who'd come through Wrenfield's kitchen and never made it back to their regiments' rolls, boys I'd fed porridge to and never seen again, erased the same way Harrow Bend was erased, quietly, by clerks who never smelled the wards.

Jonah read it. Read it again. Then he folded it with the rest, gentle as handling bread dough, and closed the bed, and raked the pulp over, and we stood back and let the press be a press.

"Three days," he said quietly. "Maybe four. The historians arrive, the orders come down, and everything on this estate that isn't nailed down gets burned. When that happens—" he touched the press's beam, once, like a blessing, "—this comes out."

"And if they search before then?"

"Then we'll smell them coming." He almost smiled. "Nobody searches inside something that smells like this."

* * *

We were wrong about the three days.

It was that same evening, dark early, rain starting to tick against the kitchen windows, that Tibbs came in white as the flour and shut the door behind him with his back against it.

"Begging your pardon, miss," he said to me, and his voice cracked on it, sixteen years old and trying to be a soldier's messenger and failing at both. "Lieutenant Fenwick. He's been round the quarters. Asking again. Only this time—" Tibbs swallowed. "This time he's not asking. He's telling. He's telling them whoever's writing at night had best stop, and there's a search coming tomorrow, ward to cellar, and the Major's ordered it himself, personal."

The kettle ticked. Hazel, at the table, had gone very still over her charcoal, her hand flat on the page, protecting whatever she'd drawn.

I looked at Jonah across the kitchen, at the flour bin that had already been visited once, at the window and the black shape of the orchard beyond it, rain silvering down the glass, our press sitting wet and secret in the middle of it all.

Tomorrow, ward to cellar.

"Well," I said, reaching for my apron, "then we'd best make sure the cider's ready before they come."
