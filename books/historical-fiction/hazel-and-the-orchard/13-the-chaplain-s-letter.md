## 13. The Chaplain's Letter

Tobias Fenn died on a Tuesday, in the hour before dawn, when the ward was quiet enough that his breathing could be heard all the way to the windows.

Jonah had been sitting with him for three nights. Not because the surgeons expected anything—not because anyone had asked him to—but because Tobias had something left to say and only the dark hours in which to say it. During the day he was too weak to speak above a whisper, and the ward was never quiet enough by day. By night, with the lamps turned low and Marisol's broth cooling untouched on the stove, Tobias Fenn held onto the edge of life like a man holding a door against wind, and he talked.

He was twenty-three. Chaplain's assistant to the 4th Column, which meant he had carried the prayer book and written the letters home, and when the letters ran out of honest things to say, he had kept writing them anyway, because the mothers deserved sentences even if the sentences were kind fictions. His hands were beautiful hands, ink-stained at the knuckles even now. He had been wounded at a place the reports called a skirmish and the survivors called Harrow Bend.

"I wrote it all down," he told Jonah on the second night, in a voice sanded down to almost nothing. "You understand why I couldn't send it. You of all people."

"I understand," Jonah said.

"They'd have opened it. They open everything going through the post." His eyes found Jonah's in the lamplight, and there was no fever-glaze in them, only a terrible clarity. "I've seen you writing, Mr. Underhill. Nights. When you think nobody's watching."

Jonah kept his hands still on the blanket's edge. "You should sleep."

"Everyone tells me that." A ghost of a smile. "As if I'll waste it." Then, more quietly: "It's under the third stone from the chapel step. The loose one. I put it there the day they carried me in, before I knew if I'd live long enough to tell it out loud. I didn't trust my own memory to hold it. Memory bends. Paper doesn't."

* * *

Jonah did not go that night. He made himself wait until morning rounds were done, until the ward was fed and bandaged and the dead man two beds down had been carried out under a sheet, until everything was ordinary. Ordinary was cover. Ordinary was the craft he had spent three years perfecting: be the man who is always there and never noticed, whose footsteps the guards stop hearing, whose face the officers can't describe afterward.

At noon he walked to the ruin of the chapel at the orchard's far edge. It had been a folly even before the war—some ancestor's romantic gesture, a stub of grey stonework with ivy eating the arch—and the regiment had stripped it for firewood months ago. The step remained. The stones around it remained, half-sunk in frost-hardened mud.

The third stone from the step lifted on its frozen lip easier than it should have. Someone had loosened it carefully, packed the earth beneath so it wouldn't shift, thought about how it would look to a casual eye. Tobias had been thorough. Chaplain's assistants were thorough; their whole work was making sure nothing was lost.

Wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed thread, was a packet the size of a prayer book.

Jonah did not open it standing in the open orchard. He walked back the long way, past the cider press, past the row of graves that was getting longer every week, and read it only when he was shut in the linen room with the door barred and a candle lit, though it was barely mid-afternoon.

* * *

The letter was eleven pages. Jonah read all of them without moving.

Tobias Fenn had been at Harrow Bend on the fourteenth of October. He had stood in the square while Major Aldous Vane—Major Aldous Vane, decorated twice over, beloved of the Ministry, convalescing thirty feet from this linen room with his wounded hand and his easy smile—read the terms of occupation to a village that had already surrendered its arms. Tobias described it precisely, because precision was the only weapon left to him: the grey gloves Vane wore, the way he took them off before giving the order, laying them folded on the well's rim as if setting aside something he didn't want bloodied.

There had been a refusal. A Captain Merrick—Ivo Merrick, 4th Column—had said the words *they laid down their arms, sir*, and Vane had relieved him of command on the spot, quietly, without raising his voice, and ordered the sergeant to take Merrick into custody. And then, when the firing began, Captain Merrick had stepped into the line anyway. He had not survived the afternoon.

Tobias named them. That was what broke Jonah, reading it alone in the cold room—that the boy had gone name by name, forty-one souls of Harrow Bend, ages and occupations where he knew them. The miller's wife. The schoolteacher who had once taught Tobias himself, in another county, another life. Two boys of twelve who had been carrying water when it started. He had gathered these names afterward, from the parish register he found half-burned, from a survivor's sister in the next valley, from the mouths of men too broken to refuse him.

And at the end, on the eleventh page, in handwriting that had begun to fail:

*I give this to Mr. Underhill, nurse, whom I believe to be trustworthy, because I have watched him write down what others will not say. If I die, let it go wherever names go to be remembered. I have carried it long enough. God forgive us all, and especially me, for I stood in the square and wrote nothing down and said nothing and lived.*

Jonah sat with the letter until the candle burned itself out.

* * *

He thought first of the floorboard. It was where the ledger lived; it was habit; habit was safety. But three nights ago he had come back from Ward Six and found the board sitting a hair proud of its neighbors, dust disturbed along its seam in a straight clean line, the kind of line a blade makes. Someone had been close. Maybe searching. Maybe just lucky. He could no longer afford maybe.

He rewrapped the letter in its oilcloth and sat in the dark doing the arithmetic that had become his life. Where does a thing go that must survive a search? Not the mattress—the mattresses were flipped weekly for lice. Not the walls—Vane's aide had taken to walking the corridors with a knuckle, tapping plaster like a man buying a horse. Not on his person—if they took him, they would take whatever he carried, and the letter would die in the same drawer as him.

It had to go somewhere no searcher would ever look, guarded not by hiding but by assumption. Somewhere protected by the one blind spot every officer shared.

He thought of Hazel's doll.

She had come to Wrenfield with nothing, mute and mud-caked, clutching a rag doll stitched from feed-sacking cloth, its face embroidered by some unskilled loving hand—a crooked mouth, mismatched button eyes. She carried it everywhere. She slept with it pressed to her chest. She had drawn it once in charcoal, careful and exact, among her pictures of soldiers and smoke: the one soft thing in a gallery of hard ones.

No searcher in the world would search a traumatized child's doll. Worse men than Vane's aide had looked straight at Hazel and seen only wreckage, only an inconvenient silence to be managed. Her smallness was her armor. Her grief was her camouflage. It shamed Jonah to use it, and he knew she would rather be used than be useless—he had learned to read the fierce attention in her eyes, the way she memorized faces, the way she had drawn the grey glove from memory with a child's merciless accuracy. She was not a witness who had stayed silent out of emptiness. She was a witness keeping herself intact.

But he would not do it without asking. That was the line. He had watched men make decisions *for* Hazel since she arrived—for her protection, for her silence, for her own good—and every one of those decisions had been a violence dressed as care. If the letter went into the doll, it went with her knowledge and her yes.

* * *

He found her in the kitchen at dusk, perched on a flour barrel beside Marisol Quill, watching bread being shaped with the total absorption she gave to everything Marisol did. The girl had appointed herself Hazel's keeper weeks ago without asking anyone's permission, and Jonah had stopped pretending to mind.

"Hazel," he said quietly. "Could you come with me a moment? Only you."

Marisol wiped her hands and gave him a look—she read him too easily, that was going to be a problem someday—but she said nothing, only touched Hazel's shoulder, and Hazel slid off the barrel and came.

He took her to the linen room. He lit the lamp and sat on the floor so they were eye level, and he set the oilcloth packet between them, and then he did the hardest thing he had done in three years of hard things: he told a child the truth.

"This is a letter from a soldier named Tobias. He was at Harrow Bend, the day your village—" He stopped. Started again. "He wrote down the names of everyone who was lost there. All of them. Every single person. So that no one could pretend it didn't happen."

Hazel's hands had gone still in her lap. Her eyes, dark and unblinking, fixed on the packet.

"The bad men are going to burn all the papers here soon. Everything written. And this letter tells the truth about the officer with the grey glove—the one you drew." Her breath caught; he saw it, and hated himself for the flinch that crossed her face, and went on anyway, because she deserved the whole truth and not a softened version of it. "If they find this letter, they will burn it, and then no one will ever know the names. But there is a place they will never look. No searcher, no soldier, no officer. Not ever."

Slowly, understanding arriving ahead of his words, Hazel's arms came up and closed around the doll in her lap.

"It would be safe inside her," Jonah said. "Inside the stuffing. Where she keeps her heart. But I won't do it unless you say yes. It would be yours to carry, and yours to keep secret, and that is a heavy thing to ask of anyone, and heavier still to ask of you. You can say no. Nothing bad happens if you say no. Do you understand?"

For a long moment Hazel looked at the packet. Then at Jonah. Then out the little window, toward the orchard, toward the rows of graves she visited every Sunday with wildflowers pulled up roots and all.

When she looked back, she nodded once. Then she set the doll flat on the floor between them, and—deliberately, ceremonially, the way children conduct the serious business of play—she worked her small fingers into the split seam at the doll's back and held it open.

An offering. An enlistment.

* * *

They did it together, by lamplight, with the door barred. Jonah unpicked the stitching along the seam with a borrowed needle, working slow so the cloth wouldn't tear, and slid the oilcloth packet deep into the body of the doll, against the old straw-and-rag stuffing that smelled faintly of some farm that no longer existed. He restitched the seam with thread as close to the original color as Marisol's basket could offer, in stitches smaller and tighter than the original maker's, so that any eye—even a suspicious one—would see only an old mended toy, loved to pieces and patched, like ten thousand dolls in ten thousand houses after three years of war.

Then he took Hazel's hands in his, both of them, small and cold and steady.

"No one can ever know," he said. "Not Marisol. Not me, if they ask. Not anyone. If someone holds the doll, or wants to wash her, or looks at her too long—you smile at them. Can you do that? You smile, and you keep her close, and you never let her out of your sight. Not at night. Not at meals. Never."

Hazel nodded. And then she did something he had not seen her do since she arrived: she leaned forward and pressed her forehead briefly against his chest, the way she pressed the doll to hers at night, and just as quickly drew back, gathering the doll up, tucking it under her arm with its mended heart full of forty-one names.

At the door she paused. Turned. Looked at him with an expression he would carry the rest of his life—gravity and trust in equal measure, a child appointing herself a courier in a war that had already taken everything else from her.

Then she was gone, down the corridor, small footsteps fading, carrying the truth of Harrow Bend against her chest like a heartbeat.

* * *

Jonah barred the door again and sat down on the floor amid the linens and put his face in his hands and let himself shake, once, briefly, the way a wall shakes when something heavy passes over a bridge.

He had spent three years believing that recording was enough. That a man who wrote the names down in the dark was doing the work, that paper was a kind of courage. Now a dying boy had handed him eleven pages of testimony and a child was carrying them in her ribs, and Jonah understood that he had crossed a line he could not uncross. He was no longer a clerk of the erased. He was a conspirator. If they caught Hazel, they would not send her to an institution—they would simply ask her questions until the doll came apart, and then there would be no record at all, because records required someone alive to have made them.

Which meant the ledger had to move too. Soon. Before the burn pile, before the historians, before Vane's aide found whatever he was looking for behind that disturbed floorboard.

A knock at the door made him rise fast, heart slamming. Too light for a soldier. Two knocks, pause, one—Marisol's signal.

He opened it. She stood in the corridor with a lamp and a face drained of color, and behind her, at the far end of the hall, torchlight moved where no torchlight should be at this hour: two figures in the east wing, going room to room. Vane's aide, and a man Jonah didn't recognize, and in the unrecognized man's hand, unmistakable in the torch-glow, a long iron probe.

"They started with the chaplain's quarters," Marisol whispered. "Ten minutes ago. Jonah—they're working toward the wards."
