## 15. Stripped of the Ward

The order came on a Tuesday, folded twice and delivered by a corporal who wouldn't meet my eyes. That was the first thing I noticed—the boy's gaze skittering off mine like water off wax—and the second thing was the wax seal, which bore Vane's signet rather than the Ministry's. A reassignment order is an administrative document. It should smell of ink and bureaucracy. This one smelled of cologne.

*Re: Orderly J. Underhill, Ward Six. Effective dawn, 14th instant, reassigned to Fennwick Depot, plague and pestilential cases. Travel under escort. Acknowledge receipt.*

Fennwick. Three days' march east, past the flooded lowlands, into a compound nobody visited unless they were carried in or carried out. Plague depot was the polite term. The impolite term, among the orderlies, was *the last ward*. Men went there when the army had decided their deaths would inconvenience no one.

I stood in the corridor outside the matron's office for a long moment with the paper in my hand, listening to the manor breathe around me. Somewhere below, Marisol was arguing with a cook's assistant about the state of the barley. Somewhere above, Hazel was sitting in the linen room where she'd made herself a nest between the shelves, drawing faces on the backs of discarded bandage wrappers—every patient in Ward Six, rendered in charcoal from memory, each one named to herself though not aloud, never aloud, not yet. And thirty feet down the east wing, behind a door that smelled of tobacco and laudanum, Major Aldous Vane was convalescing toward his medal ceremony with a wounded hand he showed off like a bouquet.

He hadn't threatened me. That was what I kept returning to, walking back along the corridor with my heels clicking on stone. Two nights ago he had sat me down in the empty solar, poured two glasses of sherry with his good hand, and offered me the capital—a post at the Chelsea annex, a pension, a future in which men like us didn't trouble ourselves with rumors. He had spoken about Harrow Bend the way other men spoke about weather. Unfortunate season. Regrettable necessity. The war required hard arithmetic, Jonah, and the men who do the sum are never thanked.

And then, when I refused him—not loudly, not dramatically, just a silence held long enough to be an answer—he had smiled, and turned his glass, and said: *Of course. I understand. Loyalty to one's patients. It's why they trust you.* As if my refusal were a charming eccentricity rather than the thing he intended to bury me for.

The order arrived forty-eight hours later. Paperwork and rank. He hadn't needed to threaten me at all. He had only needed a pen.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what I always did, because the alternative was standing still and letting the fear catch up with me. I changed dressings in Ward Six. I spooned beef tea into Corporal Enys, whose jaw had been wired after Kestrel Field and who could now take nothing thicker than steam. I held the bowl for old Sergeant Bray while he coughed, and listened to him talk about his mother's kitchen in a village whose name appeared in no report I had ever seen, and when he dozed off I wrote the village name inside my cuff with a stub of pencil lead, the way I'd done a hundred times, the way I would not be able to do at Fennwick, where every orderly was searched at the gate and every man's papers were read by a captain whose entire duty was reading them.

That was the true cruelty of it, I realized as I washed my hands in the basin at the end of the ward. Not the distance. Not even the plague depot itself, though I gave it its honest weight. It was this: he was taking me away from the names. Three years I had been Wrenfield's invisible man, the nurse nobody looked at twice, and in three years I had built something here that existed nowhere else in the country—a ledger of the erased, sixty-one pages of men and women whom the official record had decided out of existence. At Fennwick I would be a stranger with a file. A new face under a lamp. Everything about me that had made the work possible would be stripped away, and everything I'd left behind would be within Vane's reach.

Except it wasn't all behind me. I had moved most of it after the floorboard incident—after I'd come back from the burn pile and found the board sitting a half-inch proud of its neighbors, dust disturbed in a fan shape, someone's knee-print pressed faintly into the grit beside it. I'd rebuilt the hiding place twice since then, moving the ledger in pieces, a few pages at a time, into places no searcher thinks to look because they are too humble or too foul. But pages remained in the manor. Pages remained in the flour bin, wrapped in oilcloth. Pages remained under the loose hearthstone in the orderlies' washroom. And one letter—the chaplain's assistant's letter, the only document in England naming Vane's order in his own superior's handwriting—remained sewn into the body of a rag doll that slept every night in the arms of a nine-year-old girl.

I dried my hands and looked down the ward at her. She was there, of course. She was always there now, on her stool at the foot of Bray's bed, watching me work with those dark unblinking eyes that took in everything and surrendered nothing. When she saw me looking, she lifted her charcoal pencil an inch, the way she did—her little salute, her question: *anything?*

I shook my head. *Nothing today.* Then, because I couldn't help myself, I crossed to her and rested my hand a moment on her head, on the coarse pale hair that Marisol had begun braiding to keep the cinders out of it.

She leaned into my palm, briefly, like a cat deciding whether to trust you. Then she went back to her drawing, and I went back to dying men, and neither of us knew yet that we had less than a day.

* * *

I told Marisol at dusk, in the scullery, with the pump running so the words couldn't carry through the wall into Vane's wing.

She didn't stop working. She was rendering fat, or pretending to, and her hands kept moving through the motion—ladle, pot, ladle—while I read her the order word for word. But her shoulders changed. They drew up under her shawl the way they did when Hazel flinched at raised voices, a whole body bracing around someone smaller than itself.

"Fennwick," she said flatly. "They're sending you to Fennwick."

"Effective at dawn. Escort at the gate."

"That's not a transfer. That's a sentence." She set the ladle down harder than necessary. "Men don't come back from Fennwick, Jonah. Even the ones who don't get sick. The fever takes the weak ones and the boredom takes everyone else."

"I know what it is."

"Do you." She turned and looked at me properly then, and I saw that she wasn't angry at me; she was frightened, and her fright had nowhere to go except sideways into sharpness. "I know what it is. I've known since the day you started sleeping four hours and checking the floorboards the other eight. What I want to know is what happens to us when you're gone."

*Us.* She said it without ceremony, the way she said everything, and it landed in my chest like a warm stone dropped through cold water. There was no *us* on any roster. There was a groundskeeper's daughter who cooked because the cooks were dead, an orphan no ministry had claimed, and a nurse who wrote at night. Three people the paperwork had misfiled, holding each other up in a hospital's margins. Of course it was an *us*. It had been an *us* for months. I suppose I'd just been too careful a man to say so.

"You stay," I said. "Both of you stay. That's the whole point of what I'm about to ask you."

I told her then. Not everything—I have never told anyone everything—but enough. Where the pages were. Which ones mattered and which were decoys. The rhythm of the aides' searches, how they swept the wards on pay days when the soldiers' tempers gave them an excuse. How to move a page from the flour bin to the hearthstone without being seen from the orchard windows, and how the orchard itself would hide you if you moved at the change of sentries, in the eleven minutes when the eastern path went unwalked.

She listened without interrupting, which I had learned was how Marisol did everything that mattered. When I finished she was quiet a moment, stirring fat that didn't need stirring.

"And if they search her?" she said finally. "If they take the doll?"

"They won't search a child."

"You don't know that. You've seen what they did to a *village*." Her voice cracked on the word and she mastered it, jaw tight. "Swear to me you've thought about this. Swear to me it isn't just the safest place you could think of—it's that nobody looks at children. Because those aren't the same thing, Jonah, and one of them puts her at the front of everything."

I had thought about it. God help me, I had thought about little else for six days. The truth was uglier than strategy: the doll was the safest place I knew, and Hazel was safest near Marisol, and Marisol was safest if the letter stayed findable by her alone, and every safe arrangement ended with a child carrying the most dangerous paper in three counties tucked against her ribs while she slept. I had simply decided, in the wordless way men decide things they're ashamed of, that a danger shared across our small family was better than a certainty of erasure.

"It isn't only that no one looks at children," I said. "It's that no one will believe she matters. Vane's whole architecture depends on Harrow Bend having no witnesses. He can't imagine the witness is a girl who doesn't speak. His imagination has a floor, and she's beneath it."

Marisol studied me for a long time. Steam rose between us. Outside, the light was failing over the orchard, the apple trees going black row by row.

"She talks to me now," she said quietly. "Did you know that?"

I hadn't. Something moved through me that had no clean name—gladness, grief, a nurse's clinical alarm at how much a voice could cost.

"What does she say?"

"Names, mostly." Marisol turned back to her pot. "Names of people. She says them at night, before she sleeps, like prayers. I count them sometimes. Forty-one." She looked over her shoulder at me. "She's not forgetting the face, Jonah. She's keeping all of them. Whatever you've got hidden in flour bins and dolls—you should understand it's the smallest part of what she's carrying."

* * *

I meant to say goodbye properly. I had it planned through the middle watch of the night, lying awake in my cot: I would tell her I was going, tell her why in some form she could hold, tell her I would come back. All lies require construction, and I am a careful builder. But when the moment came, at first light, with my kit already packed and the escort's lanterns visible through the frost on the courtyard flags—all my careful sentences fell down.

She was waiting at the top of the stairs. She must have heard the packing; she heard everything. She stood in her too-big coat with the doll clutched against her chest, and her eyes went from my face to the kit bag and back again, and she understood immediately, completely, the way children understand departures faster than adults do because they haven't yet learned to dress them up.

"Hazel," I said.

I knelt on the cold stone so we were level. I had rehearsed: *I'm being sent away for a while, it's work, it's important work, Marisol will keep you safe, I'll write when I can.* Every word of it was defensible and every word of it was false. If I explained, she would hope. Hope was a thing that could be taken away and used against you, and I had watched it happen to stronger people than her all war long. So instead I put my hands on her shoulders and I told her the only true things I could carry in my mouth at that hour.

"Listen to Marisol," I said. "Everything she tells you. Keep your names." Her chin trembled once and steadied; her eyes never left mine. "Keep them all. However many nights it takes, however far away anybody goes—keep them. That's your work now, and it's the most important work in this house, and I'm—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let a breath pass and finished. "I'm proud to be in it with you."

She stared at me. Then she pulled one hand free of the doll, reached out, and touched my cheek—the way I had touched her head on the ward, the small solemn salute, *anything?*—and her fingers were cold and certain, and I understood that she was answering a question I hadn't asked: *yes. Always yes.*

Then she opened the doll's coat, quick and deliberate, showed me the lump of the letter beneath the stitched seam, and closed it again. *Kept,* she was telling me. *I keep it.*

Marisol appeared at the stair's foot with bread and cheese wrapped in cloth, and pressed it into my kit without a word, and then, in front of God and the porter and whoever might have been watching from Vane's window, she kissed me on the mouth—briefly, fiercely, a signature on a document we'd never discussed—and stepped back.

"Eleven minutes," she said. "Remember the eastern path goes unwalked for eleven minutes. And Jonah—" she held my eyes, and hers were dry and bright and terrible, "—come back before spring, or I'm coming to Fennwick after you, and I'm a worse guest than the fever."

The escort sergeant cleared his throat at the door. The frost was turning gold. I shouldered the kit that held almost none of what mattered, and I walked out into the courtyard, and I did not look back at the stairs, because I am a coward in exactly one direction.

But at the gate, at the turn in the lane where the orchard begins, I allowed myself one glance. Two figures stood in the doorway, one small and one taller, unmoving, framed in the dark of the hall like the last two names on a page.

Three days to Fennwick, they'd told me. It took five, because the flooded lowlands were worse than the maps admitted, and by the time we limped into the depot on the sixth evening, frozen through and half-starved, the escort sergeant was cursing the mud and I was cursing nothing at all. I had counted the minutes of my own departure like a man counting coins he'd been robbed of.

It was the depot's receiving clerk who straightened the ledger in front of him and asked, with the bored cruelty of paperwork, for my name and previous station—and then looked up sharply, pen stopped mid-stroke.

"Wrenfield?" he said. "Manor in the orchard country?" He glanced at a notice pinned crookedly to the board behind him, a single sheet, Ministry seal, dated six days prior. "You'd best read this before you unload. They burned the records at Wrenfield yesterday. Whole archive, they're saying. And there's a girl—" he squinted at the notice, frowning, "—says here a child walked into the historians' session with a drawing. They're holding everyone at the manor under guard. Nobody in or out."

Hazel. The great hall. Alone in a room full of soldiers and historians, with Vane somewhere in that house and my ledger scattered through it like shrapnel.

I set down my kit.
