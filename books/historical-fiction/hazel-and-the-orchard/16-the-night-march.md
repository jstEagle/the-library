## 16. The Night March

The flour bin told me before anything else did.

I'd gone out to the scullery an hour past midnight, meaning to start the morning's bread early because the ovens had been cold all week and the patients noticed. Cold bread, they'd say, as if the war itself weren't enough. I lifted the lid, slid my hand into the flour the way I always did—past the first warm depth of it, down to where the oilcloth bundle lay—and my fingers closed on nothing but flour.

I dug. Both hands, up to the wrists, then the elbows, flour billowing up into my face and settling on my sleeves like snow that had no business falling indoors. The bottom of the bin was bare wood, scored and flour-ghosted, empty as a liar's promise.

Nine pages. Nine pages of testimony from Corporal Reyes and the chaplain's assistant Whitlock and the woman Edith Crane, who'd taken shrapnel in a village whose name the reports spelled wrong on purpose. Nine pages in Marisol Quill's own careful hand because Jonah's handwriting slanted too badly for safe reading, and we'd agreed—Jonah and I, in one of our midnight conferences by the cider press—that mine looked more like kitchen inventory.

*Flour: 40 lbs short. Sugar: 12 lbs.* And beneath it, folded small: *At Harrow Bend, on the fourteenth day of the last month of fighting, the order was given—*

Gone.

I stood there with flour in my hair and my heart going like a rabbit's, and I did not scream, though God knows I wanted to. Screaming wakes houses. Waking houses gets people asked questions, and questions at Wrenfield these days got answered with firing squads or paperwork, whichever was quieter.

Instead I did what my mother taught me when the foxes came for the hens: count what's missing before you cry over it. I counted. Nine pages. The doll still under Hazel's blanket—I checked, pressing my palm flat over the little lump of rag and calico, feeling the letter inside stiff as a swallowed bone. The ledger still under Jonah's floorboard, presumably, though he was three wards away and I couldn't check without crossing half the manor. But the depositions—the copies, the backup, the *just in case*—those were gone, and there was only one person who'd been prowling the kitchens these last two days asking after stores with a clipboard and a smile like a wet boot.

Vane's aide. Lieutenant something-or-other, all polished buttons and soft questions. *And how much flour do you go through weekly, miss? And where do you keep the overflow? Clever, keeping it near the door.*

Clever. Yes. Clever as a mouse storing grain inside the cat.

* * *

I found Hazel awake, which didn't surprise me. That child slept the way soldiers sleep—one ear open, one hand near whatever mattered. She was sitting up in our narrow room with her doll in her lap and her eyes fixed on the door, and when I came in she read my face the way she read everything, whole and fast, no words wasted.

"The flour bin," I said quietly. "It's been emptied."

She understood. She'd watched us hide those pages; she'd sat vigil while old Whitlock dictated his dying words; she knew exactly what a missing bundle meant the way other children know thunder. Her hand tightened on the doll.

"Is Jonah gone yet?" I asked, though I hated myself for asking a child such a thing.

Hazel shook her head. Then she held up her fingers—one, two—and pointed toward the window, toward the dark orchard beyond, toward the lane that ran past the manor gates and down toward the road. Two hours. Maybe less. Dawn escort, Vane had decreed, because plague depots apparently couldn't wait for decent hours, and because Major Aldous Vane never did anything so honest as wait where he could be seen.

Two hours, and me standing here with flour on my sleeves and nine pages of dead men's words walking around in an aide's coat pocket.

Here is what I want recorded somewhere, since recording things seems to be the business we're all in now: I am not a brave woman. My mother was brave—she stood between the fox and the henhouse with a burning brand and lost two fingers to it and called it a fair trade. I inherited none of that. I inherited recipes, and a talent for reading people's hungers, and the particular cowardice of someone who has survived by being useful and unremarkable. When Jonah first showed me his ledger, six months ago, I told him he was mad and that I wanted no part of it, and then I transcribed the second page anyway, because that is the kind of coward I am—the kind that can't look away.

But there are moments, I've learned, when cowardice turns inside out. When you've been quiet so long that the quiet itself becomes a kind of pressure, like water behind a dam, and something has to give, and it might as well be you.

"Hazel," I said, "get your shoes."

* * *

We went out through the kitchen garden, because the kitchen garden gate doesn't creak if you lift it as you swing it, and everyone knows the front doors of Wrenfield have eyes on them now. The night was the cold, close kind, late autumn pressing its thumb down on the world, no moon to speak of. Good for hiding. Bad for finding your way. We took both.

The orchard began where the garden ended, and I've lived beside that orchard my whole life—my father pruned it, and his father before him, and I could walk it blindfolded, which was fortunate, because that's essentially what we were doing. Apple trees gone half-wild, rows that had drifted out of true over the war years, grass waist-high where no scythe had reached since the soldiers came. In daylight it was just an orchard, tired and tangled. In the dark it was a country of its own.

And here is the strange thing I want to remember about this night, the thing I'll tell Hazel about when she's grown, if we're ever allowed a *when she's grown*: the orchard helped us. I know how that sounds. But trees that I'd known all my life seemed to arrange themselves around us—low branches lifting as we ducked beneath them, roots lying flat where my feet needed to fall, gaps opening in the hedgerow exactly wide enough for a woman and a child carrying a doll. My father used to say the orchard kept its own accounts, that every tree remembered every hand that had tended it. I laughed at him then. I didn't laugh now.

Halfway down the third row I heard them—a patrol, two men, boots crunching somewhere off to our left, lantern light swinging pale between the trunks. I pulled Hazel down behind a windfall apple tree, its rotten bulk furred with moss, and we crouched in the wet grass while the lantern light crawled past close enough that I could hear one soldier say to the other, "—said the nurse writes at night, that's all I heard, ask me no more—"

Then they moved on, and their light went with them, and Hazel's hand found mine in the dark and gripped it, and neither of us said a word because we didn't need to. *The nurse who writes at night.* They were hunting Jonah already. Not waiting for dawn, not content with paperwork and reassignment—they wanted the ledger, and they wanted it tonight, which meant whatever we'd feared was already worse.

We ran then. Not all-out—we were too deep in the orchard for that, and Hazel was seven and small—but that steady loping urgency that farm girls know from chasing escaped stock before a storm. Row by row, tree by tree, my breath sawing, Hazel's smaller footsteps pattering beside me, the doll clutched to her chest with both arms like something that might fly away.

The orchard ended at the drystone wall my grandfather built, and beyond it the lane dropped away toward the crossroads, and beyond that, half a mile down, the depot road where Jonah would be marched at first light. I put Hazel over the wall first—she climbed like a cat, no hesitation, swinging her legs over and dropping—and then I followed, catching my skirt on a stone and tearing it and not caring, not caring about any of it, the torn skirt and the flour still in my hair and the nine pages loose in the world.

At the top of the lane I stopped, because there was light below. Lanterns at the crossroads, a wagon, figures moving. Too many figures for a supply run, too organized for deserters. The escort, come early. They'd moved the departure up—or they'd never intended dawn at all, and the dawn order was the story told to the man while the trap was set for the dark hours.

"They're taking him now," I whispered, and my voice cracked on it, and Hazel looked up at me with her terrible calm eyes and then did something I will carry until I die.

She pointed—not at the wagon, not at the guards. At the orchard wall behind us. Then she mimed, with her small floured hands, climbing. Going back. Around.

The orchard ran parallel to the lane, down the whole slope of the hill, and came out near the depot road through a gap in a hedge that my father had cut years ago for carting apples and had never quite closed. It was longer. It was darker. It meant going back into the trees we'd just escaped. But it also meant arriving behind the lanterns instead of walking into them down an open lane with nowhere to hide and a child at my side.

Hazel was already moving. She'd turned, she had the wall in her hands, and she looked back at me over her shoulder with the expression of someone who has decided and cannot imagine why anyone else hasn't.

"All right," I told her, to nobody, to God, to my mother's memory. "All right, then."

We went back over the wall, and the orchard took us in again.

* * *

The last stretch I will remember in pieces for the rest of my life, because that's how fear files things: in fragments, in flashes, in the spaces between heartbeats.

The hedge gap, torn wire at its edge that caught my sleeve and held me for one endless second while Hazel slipped through clean ahead of me. The slope beyond, steep and rough with nettle beds gone brown and dormant, Hazel tumbling down it with the sure-footedness of a child who grew up among furrows. The sound of voices ahead—many voices, orders being given, the creak of wagon wheels—and the moment I grabbed Hazel and pressed us both flat against the bole of the last great pear tree at the orchard's edge, a tree older than the manor itself, planted by some dead hand in some forgotten year, its bark rough against my cheek like an old man's beard.

Beyond the tree, thirty yards, the depot road. Lanterns. A wagon with a canvas tilt. Six soldiers, perhaps seven. An officer with a clipboard—the aide, the same aide, his wet-boot smile visible even from here whenever the lantern light caught him. And at the wagon's tail, hands bound before him in the manner of a man being transported rather than reassigned, stood Jonah Underhill, thin and straight and uncomplaining, watching the ground as though the answer to everything were written in the frost there.

They were loading him. Two minutes, maybe three, and the wagon would roll.

What happened next happened fast, and I can't claim I planned any of it. Hazel moved out from behind the pear tree before I could catch her. She walked straight across the open ground toward the wagon—straight through lantern light, straight through the sightline of seven armed men—as calm and small and deliberate as if she were crossing the yard to feed hens. And the strangest thing, the thing that made my blood run cold even as it saved us, was that nobody stopped her. A mud-streaked child in a torn pinafore, wandering a depot road at night—soldiers saw that everywhere in this war. She was nobody. She was invisible. She had made unnoticeable into an art the way Jonah had, the way I had, and it carried her across thirty open yards to the wagon's wheel.

I followed because following her was the only thing left. My legs shook so badly I could hardly steer them.

By the time I reached the wagon, Hazel had done something impossible: she'd raised her arms to Jonah, wordless, the universal grammar of children, and a guard—some boy barely older than the patients we buried—had actually hesitated. Just hesitated, a half-second of human confusion in the middle of military procedure, and that half-second was the whole world. Because I stepped into it, flour-dusted and wild-haired, and said in my best kitchen voice, the voice that stops surgeons and colonels' aides alike:

"He's fevered. Plague depot, isn't it? Then somebody should know he's been febrile since Tuesday, and if you load a fevered man into a sealed wagon with six of you, you'll all know it by Friday. I'm the cook. I've fed this man every day for eight months. His symptoms are my business."

Lies, every syllable—Jonah hadn't had a day's fever in his life. But plague talk does things to soldiers. The boy-guard stepped back. The sergeant in charge frowned and looked at the aide, and the aide looked at his clipboard, and for one beautiful moment the machinery of erasure jammed on the smallest gear it had: a cook's opinion of a fever.

And in that moment Jonah looked up. He looked at Hazel, and then at me, and I saw him understand all of it in an instant—the missing pages, the flight through the orchard, why we were here—and I saw something else move across his face, something I'd never seen on him in eight months of knowing him: hope, raw and unprotected, like skin.

"Marisol," he said quietly. "The doll."

The aide's head snapped up. "What did she say? What about—" He was already reaching for Hazel, already grabbing at the doll in her arms, and Hazel twisted away, and a soldier caught her shoulder, and everything was shouting and lantern-swing and Hazel's voice—her voice, her first words in weeks, cracking out over the depot road:

"It's not yours! It's not yours, it's mine!"

And down the lane, from the direction of the manor, came hoofbeats. Fast ones. More lanterns, more riders, a carriage lamp throwing wild light across the orchard wall—and above that wall, along its whole length, the dark shapes of apple trees standing against the grey beginning of the sky, silent and full of shadows, every one of them looking, if you asked me, like witnesses.
