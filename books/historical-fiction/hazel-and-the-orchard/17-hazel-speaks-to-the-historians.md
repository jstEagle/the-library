## 17. Hazel Speaks to the Historians

The night before, Marisol braids my hair and says nothing, which is how I know something is going to happen.

Marisol is always talking when things are fine — about flour, about apples, about whether the cook at the vicarage in town over-salts her soup. When Marisol goes quiet, the quiet has weight, like snow on a roof. So I sit very still while she braids, and I hold my doll in my lap, and inside the doll's belly there is a letter that weighs more than any letter should, and neither of us says one word about it.

"Tomorrow," Marisol starts, and stops. She ties off the braid with a ribbon gone soft from washing. "Tomorrow they'll be in the great hall all day. The historians."

I nod.

"They'll ask everyone questions. Nurses, orderlies. They want numbers." Her hands are still in my hair even though the braid is done. "Numbers are easy to make wrong on purpose."

I know this. Numbers were wrong about Harrow Bend from the very first day. The number for Harrow Bend is zero. Harrow Bend does not exist anymore, not on paper, so whatever happened there happened to no one. That is what the papers say. I learned to read at the vicarage school before the war took the school away, and I can read a report fast now, because Jonah leaves them out on his desk when he wants me to see something, and I am quick, quicker than people think, because being quick is part of being invisible. You have to be quick to be invisible. Slow people get noticed.

"Hazel." Marisol turns me around on the stool. Her face does the thing where it is trying to be a kitchen face, a bread face, an everything-is-normal face, and failing. "You don't have to do anything. You hear me? Whatever you decide, you don't have to."

But I do have to. I have known it since the day I drew the square in charcoal and Jonah went white as bed linen and held the paper like it was burning him through his gloves. Nobody else was going to say it. The sergeant died saying pieces of it. The chaplain's assistant died writing it down. Everybody who carries the truth of Harrow Bend keeps dying, and passing it along like a bucket in a fire line, and now the bucket is in my hands, because I am the last one standing at the well.

Because I was at the well.

* * *

Morning comes grey and clean. The great hall of Wrenfield Manor used to be where the old family ate dinner under dead ancestors, Jonah told me once; now it has trestle tables and a smell of polish and the flags of two armies folded in a chest nobody opens. Today there are three long tables set up at the far end, and behind them sit three men.

The first historian is fat and comfortable and has a voice like a door hinge. The second is thin and writes constantly, pen moving even when nothing is happening. The third is young. Younger than Jonah. He sits slightly apart from the other two, and he keeps looking out the tall windows toward the orchard, toward the burn pile smoke that hasn't fully stopped rising in three days, and when he looks at anything else he looks like a boy caught between wanting his supper and wanting to be sick.

Guards stand at both doors. Two at the garden door, one at the corridor door. I count them the way Jonah taught me without teaching me: you always count the doors and the men and the ways out. It is the first thing he does entering any room, so quick you'd miss it, and I copied it the way I copy everything about Jonah, because Jonah is the person I would be if I were grown and could choose.

I am carrying the drawing.

It is the fourth one I made. The first three I burned in Marisol's stove myself, page by page, learning that some things you keep and some things you make again until they are right. This one is right. Charcoal on the back of an inventory sheet: the village square of Harrow Bend, the well in the middle with its little roof like a hat, the houses leaning their usual leans, the smoke coming off the Thomases' house in long smudged strokes, and the soldiers in a line, and at the front of them, one man with his arm raised, and the arm ending in a glove drawn dark, pressed hard, pressed until the charcoal shone. Grey glove. I did the glove last. I did the glove eleven times over, layer on layer, until it was the blackest thing on the page, blacker than the smoke, blacker than the well's mouth.

Everyone in the hall looks at the fat historian or the thin one. Nobody looks at a girl in a clean pinafore carrying a doll, walking along the wall by the tapestries. Being invisible is a craft. Jonah taught me that too, without meaning to. You walk like you belong to the furniture.

The nurses are lined up to give their accounts. Sister Brandon is at the table now, saying numbers. The thin historian writes them down. The numbers are wrong; I can tell by Sister Brandon's face, which does the tight thing, the thing faces do when they are saying words someone gave them to say. Jonah isn't there. I looked for Jonah first, before anything, the way you check the sun is still in the sky. He isn't in the hall. His bed was empty and stripped this morning, and Marisol's eyes when I found her were red, and she said, *eat your porridge*, and her voice cracked on *porridge*, of all the safe little words in the world, and I understood that whatever they had done to Jonah had already been done, quietly, overnight, with paperwork.

They sent Jonah away so that no one would be in the hall who knew too much.

They forgot about me. People forget about me. It is the one thing I am best at.

* * *

Sister Brandon finishes. A clerk calls, "Next," the way you'd call a hog to trough, and I detach from the wall and cross the floor.

My feet make no sound. Stone floors, wool socks, Marisol's knitting. I pass the guard at the corridor door close enough to touch his belt, and he looks straight over my head. I pass the trestle tables. I stop in front of the historians' table, and I put the drawing down on the polished wood, and I turn it to face them, and then I step back one step, because testimony needs space, Jonah says. You give the words room to land.

The fat historian glances at it first, the way you glance at a child's scribble. Then he stops glancing and looks.

The thin historian stops writing. His pen makes one last stroke, a long accidental line across his page, and he stares.

The young one leans forward. He pulls the drawing toward himself gently, by its edge, like it might go off. He looks at the square, the well, the line of soldiers, the raised arm, the glove black as a hole burned in paper.

"Where is this?" the fat historian asks me. Not unkindly. The way you'd ask a child where she got such an imagination.

"You know where," I say.

My voice comes out small and steady and it doesn't shake, and I am surprised by it, the way you're surprised by ice holding your weight. I haven't spoken aloud in front of more than two people since Harrow Bend. My voice sounds strange in my own ears, rusted, a gate after winter. But it holds.

"This is Harrow Bend," I say. "And you know it is, because there is no Harrow Bend in any of your papers, and a thing that's in no papers still happened. I was there."

The hall has gone quiet in the way halls do, a spreading quiet, ears turning. Sister Brandon's hand has come up to her mouth. Somewhere behind me a nurse whispers my name — *that's the Underhill girl* — and the whisper runs down the line of beds' worth of people faster than I could walk it.

The thin historian clears his throat. "Young lady, this session is for—"

"I know," I say. "It's for the official account. That's why I came. Because your account has a hole in it shaped exactly like my village."

I hear Marisol somewhere near the kitchen door, moving, stopped, not intervening, guarding me with her stillness. Good. If she comes for me they will send me out. I keep my eyes on the young historian, because he is the only one whose face is doing something true.

"I lived in the house with the green door, third from the smithy," I say. "On the morning it happened I was sent to the well for water. My mother sent me. She said, quick now, before the queue forms. So I was at the well when the soldiers came into the square, and the well has a stone rim, and I got behind the stone rim, and I stayed there. All day."

Nobody moves. Even the guard by the door has turned his head.

"There were forty-one people in Harrow Bend that morning," I say. "I can tell you every one. I've said them to myself every night since, like prayers, except prayers are for God and these are for the record, and the record lost them, so it can have them back."

And I begin.

"Marta Oakes, who was our midwife. Her son Tam, who was four. Widow Thomas and her daughters Ivy and Rose. The miller, Ambrose Fenn, and his wife Celia. Old Josiah Blay, who rang the church bell thirty years..."

I do not rush. Jonah takes depositions slow, lets the words settle like sediment in water, and I learned from watching him that speed insults the dead. I say each name and then a small thing attached to each name, because a name alone is a stone with nothing carved, and stones without carving get moved, get built into walls, get forgotten under walls. But a name with a thing attached — *who kept bees, who cheated at cards, who had a laugh like a cart on gravel* — that name gets a hook in the world and stays.

The fat historian's face has changed. The kindness has drained out of it and left something older and less comfortable. He reaches to slide the drawing back toward me, maybe to end this, maybe kindly, maybe not.

"That's quite enough," he says. "Child, whoever put you up to—"

"No one put me up to anyone," I say. "That's the whole point. Everyone who could have is dead."

Then I say the rest of the names. I am at the Pikes now, and the Hollis family, and the schoolmaster Mr. Redd who taught me my letters, and my throat is doing something terrible on certain names but my voice keeps going anyway, like a cart rolling downhill with the brake gone, and I let it roll.

"...and Eliza Underhill is not one of them," I say, and pause, because this part matters. "Eliza Underhill is my mother's name too, and she is dead, but she isn't on any list anywhere, because there are no lists for Harrow Bend. Until today there were no lists. Now there's me."

Silence. The kind of silence that has a crack running through it. I can feel it, the way you feel ice start to give.

The young historian speaks. His voice cracks on the first word and he steadies it. "The officer," he says, quietly. "In your drawing. The raised arm. Can you describe him?"

I can. I have had years to describe him to the inside of my skull.

"He wasn't looking at anyone," I say. "That's what I remember most. He was looking at the well. Like he was checking his work. And his hand came up, and the hand had a grey glove on it, good leather, and he didn't shout. Some officers shout. He just lifted the hand, slow, like a man signalling across a noisy room, polite. And the line fired. And he lowered the hand and checked his watch."

I look up at the young historian.

"He wore the glove on his left hand," I say. "He wears it still. It's on his left hand right now, in this manor, probably at breakfast, probably telling somebody charming something. Major Vane. I'd know that glove anywhere in the world. I drew it eleven times till I got it black enough."

The name drops into the hall like a stone into the well at Harrow Bend, and we all listen together for it to hit bottom.

The thin historian has gone grey. The fat one is on his feet saying something procedural — *this is highly irregular, the child will be removed, who is responsible for this child* — and two guards start toward me, and Marisol is finally moving too, coming fast from the kitchen door with her sleeves floured to the elbow.

But the young historian has picked up the drawing again, and he is holding it up to the light from the tall windows, and his other hand has flattened on the table like a man steadying himself on a deck. And he says, over all of it, in a voice that stops the guards mid-step:

"How many soldiers fired?"

I almost smile. It is the right question. It is the question Jonah would ask. It is the question of a man who understands that a record is made of details or it is made of nothing.

"A dozen in the square," I say. "More at the church. And one who refused. He wouldn't raise his piece, and Vane had him stand with the villagers, and he died with them, and none of you know his name either. But I do. Sergeant Eli Crane. He told the man next to him he was sorry, and the man next to him survived, because he was behind me, behind the well, wounded, and he lived nine months to die slowly in a hospital bed in this house and tell Jonah pieces of it. Pieces. I'm giving you the whole."

The young historian looks at his senior colleagues. Something passes between the three of them that has nothing to do with procedure. The fat one is calculating — I can see him calculating, cost and benefit, a man doing sums with a hole opening under his ledger. The thin one is writing again, fast, and I notice he is not writing the official account. He is writing what I said.

"Do you have proof," the fat historian says finally, "beyond a child's drawing? Testimony. Documents. Anything."

And here it is. Here is the moment Marisol and I rehearsed without rehearsing, in the language of silences we speak fluently, she and I. Inside my doll's belly is a letter naming Vane's direct order, written by a dying man. In the cider press under its false bottom is a ledger of every erased name at Wrenfield, written by Jonah. And Jonah is gone, marched off at dawn to a plague depot, because Vane wanted the record buried with the record-keeper under it.

"The proof exists," I say. "Some of it is closer than you think. Some of it you already ordered burned, and some of it you failed to, because you searched everywhere except the places where people keep the things they love."

I pick up my doll and hold her against my chest, and I let my eyes rest on the fat historian's face, calm as well water.

"Now," I say, "I believe you owe me the courtesy of taking a deposition properly. Jonah taught me how it's done. He's the head nurse here. Or he was, until six o'clock this morning, when Major Vane had him shipped to Blackfen depot to die quietly where nobody counts. If you want the proof, you'll want Jonah Underhill. And if you want him, you'll want him back before Vane's escort gets past the river crossing, because after that, even you three won't be able to find him."

The young historian is already on his feet.
