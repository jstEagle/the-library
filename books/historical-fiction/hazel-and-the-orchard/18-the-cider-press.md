## 18. The Cider Press

They came for him an hour before dawn, when the corridor lamps had burned down to their last inch of oil and the ward breathed in the shallow rhythm of the sleeping. Two soldiers. Not the garrison regulars Jonah knew by name and injury—strangers, brought with the historians' escort, their uniforms still creased from the road.

"Nurse Underhill. Major Vane requests your presence."

Requests. The word did its work. Jonah set down the cloth he had been folding, looked once down the row of cots—Corporal Enniss breathing fast in his fever, the woman from the village called Lark sleeping at last, old Whitcomb's chest rising and falling with the terrible patience of a man determined to outlast everyone's expectations—and went with them without argument. Argument was a currency he had never carried. He had survived this long by being unremarkable, by moving through rooms like a draft of cold air. But that skill had been failing him for weeks now, ever since Hazel, ever since Marisol, ever since the ledger under the floorboard grew too heavy to pretend it weighed nothing.

They marched him across the yard in the grey hour before light, past the well, past the kitchen where no smoke rose yet, toward the manor's east room—the estate office, requisitioned along with everything else, where the manor's ledgers of apples and rents had been replaced by ledgers of men.

The orchard stood black against the paling sky. He counted the trees as he passed, an old habit, the way another man might count beads on a string. Somewhere among those trunks sat the cider press, and inside the cider press, sealed in wax beneath the false bottom Marisol had built with her father's tools, lay the true record: his ledger, every name, six months of stolen memory. If they searched him and found nothing, it would be because there was nothing to find. That had been the plan. Plans, he knew by now, were things the war ate.

* * *

The east room had been prepared like a courtroom that hadn't decided yet whether it was one. The long table cleared. Three chairs behind it, two empty. A lamp turned high, so that whoever sat before the table would sit in brightness while the room behind the questioners stayed dim—a trick of architecture older than any army. Someone had thought about this. Someone had wanted the arrangement ready before the sun came up.

Vane sat in the center chair, his wounded hand resting on the table like an object of display, the grey glove folded beside it. Without the glove his hand was a ruin of knotted scar, and he kept it in the lamplight anyway, deliberately, the way other men wore medals. His face held the mild, reasonable expression Jonah had learned to distrust more than any anger.

"Nurse Underhill." Vane gestured to the single hard chair facing the table. "Thank you for coming. I know you have rounds."

"The patients—" Jonah began.

"Are being seen to. Sit, please."

Jonah sat. The chair was low; the table above him; the lamp full on his face. He kept his hands loose on his knees and made himself think of the press, of wax, of Marisol's steady hands smoothing the boards down. They cannot take what is not here. Say only what is already known. Give them nothing new.

The third man entered from the dimness: Vane's aide, Lieutenant Corvane, a narrow young officer with ink-stained fingers and the particular neatness of a man who enjoyed paperwork as an instrument. He carried a wooden box, which he set on the table between Jonah and the majors without ceremony.

"Found these," Corvane said, "in the flour bin of the manor kitchen."

He opened the lid.

Jonah had prepared himself for many things. He had rehearsed denial in the dark hours, composed his face around every possible accusation. What he had not prepared for was the sight of his own handwriting in another man's hands—the depositions, page after page, the corporal's account of the firing line, the chaplain's assistant's careful marginal notes, Marisol's transcription of the dying sergeant's words. Paper he had believed safe. Paper he had moved to the bin only days ago, believing the floorboard compromised and the kitchen unwatched.

His face, he prayed, gave back nothing but lamplight.

"You'll want to deny these are yours," Vane said comfortably. "Don't bother. We've had three men reading them since midnight. The hand is consistent. The dates are consistent. What is consistent, Underhill, is testimony. Depositions. Taken from my patients"—he corrected himself with a small smile—"*the State's* patients, I should say, though I notice you've grown proprietary about the word. Recorded without authorization. Circulated within this house. One page even appears to be in the hand of a groundskeeper's daughter, which raises questions we will come to."

"I'm a nurse," Jonah said. "I write things down. Men die in my ward and their families deserve—"

"Their families," Vane said gently, "deserve peace. This country deserves a history it can heal around, not a ledger of grievances compiled by a nurse with ambitions. You've read the Ministry's directives. Non-regimental papers are burned for a reason. The war is over, Underhill. What remains to be settled is not what happened, but what will be remembered to have happened. These pages—" he rested his good hand on the box "—are sedition dressed as sentiment."

"And Harrow Bend?" The words left Jonah before strategy could catch them. He heard his own voice, level, quiet, and understood that some part of him had stopped calculating entirely.

Vane did not move. The ruined hand lay in the light. "Harrow Bend," he said, "was a security action during active hostilities, conducted under legitimate orders, and it does not appear in any report because there was nothing there to report. No engagement. No garrison action worth the ink. A regrettable confusion involving civilians who had been armed by the enemy—or so the summary states. You've read the summary, I assume. Everyone has. It is the only document that matters."

"The summary is a lie."

"The summary," said Vane, "is history. The difference between those two words is precisely the difference between you and me." He leaned back. For a moment something moved behind the charm—not anger, exactly. Weariness, or its performance. "I offered you a future once. A post in the capital, a pension, a name that would outlive this drafty house. You declined. I respected it then. I respect it less now, because this time I am not offering. This time, Nurse, the historians are upstairs asleep, and when they wake they will be shown a clean ledger of the dead, and you will be shown a cell, or a depot, or a ditch—I haven't decided, and honestly the decision is below my rank. All I ask today is the location of the rest of your archive."

"There is no rest."

"Your ledger," Corvane said, flat. "We know it exists. An orderly saw you writing at night, months ago. Pages are missing from your quarters. The floorboard under the third cot in the nurses' alcove was disturbed—we found fresh shavings. Where is the book?"

Jonah looked at the box of confiscated depositions and felt the strangest sensation: grief and relief arriving together, twins. The pages in the box were losses. But they were losses he could survive, because they were copies, fragments, the shadow of the record and not its body. The body slept in wax under the cider press, and neither of these men would think to look in a place where apples were crushed. Let them tear up every floorboard in Wrenfield. Let them burn the whole manor. The names were outside, in the cold, in Marisol's keeping, in the orchard that had never belonged to any of them.

"I don't keep a ledger," he said.

Vane sighed, and it was almost fond. "You do. And here is what I know about you, Underhill, that you perhaps don't know about yourself. You are a man who cannot bear an unnamed dead. It is your whole character. It is why you were useless to the army and useful only here, among the dying, where naming is the entire work. So you will not have destroyed it. You will have hidden it, somewhere close, somewhere you could reach alone at night. Which means it is findable. And when it is found, it burns with everything else." He stood, buttoning his coat with his good hand. "You'll be held in the west cellar while we search. Consider it time to reflect. Men in cellars often remember where they've put things."

* * *

The west cellar smelled of apples gone soft and cider gone sharp, the whole compressed memory of the orchard's autumns seeping through the barrels. They locked him to a ring in the wall and left a single candle, and Jonah sat in the dark among the sweetness and rot and thought about traps.

Because that was what he had walked into—that was obvious now. Vane had known about the flour bin. Corvane had been in the kitchen days ago asking about the nurse who wrote at night, and the pages had gone missing, and none of it was luck. The aide had let Jonah believe the floorboard was compromised. Had perhaps disturbed it himself. Had herded the papers from hiding place to hiding place like a sheepdog working a flock, until they gathered exactly where a searcher would look first.

And yet.

Jonah pressed his head back against the cold stone and made himself count, slowly, the way he counted trees, the way he counted names.

The pages in the box were real, yes. But they were the *lesser* testimony. The chaplain's assistant's letter—the letter naming Vane's direct order at Harrow Bend, the one document that put the grey glove on the wrist of a named officer—that letter had never been in the flour bin. That letter was sewn into the body of a rag doll with mismatched buttons, a doll that slept every night in the arms of a girl who had learned better than anyone alive how to be invisible in plain sight. No soldier searching for sedition searches a child's doll. Jonah had bet a man's life on that truth, and the bet still stood.

And Marisol. Marisol, who read sleeplessness the way other women read weather. Marisol, who had watched Corvane prowling the kitchen and said nothing, and asked nothing, and the night before the historians arrived had come to Jonah's cot with flour on her sleeves and said only, "If they take the bin, they take what's in the bin. Nothing else changes." He hadn't understood her then. He understood her now. She had known the pages would fall. She had let them fall—sacrificed the lesser archive to fix the hunters' eyes on paper that proved nothing but diligence, while the letter traveled elsewhere.

Elsewhere meant the youngest historian. Pethrow—that was his name; Jonah had marked him the day the Ministry men arrived, a thin young man who had stood too long at the burn pile behind the orchard watching the patients' letters curl in the fire, with an expression Jonah recognized because he wore it himself in mirrors: the look of a man discovering that his life's work might be built on ash. Marisol had spoken to him twice, quietly, by the kitchen door. What passed between them, Jonah didn't know. But Hazel's drawing had reached the great hall somehow, and a troubled man is a door left unlatched.

The trap had sprung, all right. Both directions at once. Vane believed he held everything: the pages, the nurse, the search underway, the historians scheduled for a curated morning. But the thing that mattered had slipped the net before the net closed, and it was traveling through the house right now in the hands of a cook's daughter and a mute child and maybe—if the world had one decent seam left in it—a historian who still believed records were meant to hold the truth.

Jonah smiled in the dark, and the candle guttered, and somewhere overhead boots crossed the floor of the ward he should have been making rounds in.

* * *

They fetched him up near midmorning, when the light through the east room windows had gone honest and white and merciless. The room had changed. The two chairs behind the table were occupied now: Corvane on one side, and on the other, the senior historian—a heavy, precise man named Ollerenshaw whose published volumes Jonah had seen in the manor library, books with the smell of official approval baked into their pages. Vane stood at the window with his back half-turned, the grey glove back on his ruined hand, supervising the morning the way he supervised everything: by seeming merely to observe it.

Ollerenshaw did the talking, which told Jonah everything. When the Ministry takes the lead, the army has lost control of the stage.

"Nurse Underhill." Ollerenshaw's voice was dry as the ledgers he loved. "These documents were recovered from the kitchens. You will confirm they are yours, and you will account for each entry, and you will tell us where the remainder of your papers are held. In exchange, the Major has agreed to a lenient disposition. Administrative reassignment. Your record sealed, not destroyed. A career continued rather than ended. Do you understand the shape of what's offered?"

"I understand it's a leash," Jonah said. "You've shown me this one before."

Beside the window, Vane's expression flickered—amusement, or its mask. "Nurse," he said pleasantly, "you mistake your position. Yesterday you were an anonymous functionary in a backwater house. Today you are evidence. Evidence gets handled roughly."

"Then handle me," Jonah said, "and see what it gets you. Those pages prove nothing except that a nurse writes things down. There's no order in them. No signature. No name attached to Harrow Bend but the village's own. You're holding a mirror, Major, and calling it a weapon."

It was the closest thing to a gamble he had ever made, and he watched it land. Because it was true, and truth has a sound even liars recognize. The depositions named the dead and described the dying, but the chain of command, the direct order, the grey glove—all of that lived in one place only, and Vane's silence, the way his gloved hand tightened on the windowsill, confirmed that he knew exactly how incomplete his victory was.

"We will continue the search," Corvane said, but his voice had thinned.

"Gentlemen." Ollerenshaw raised a hand, and something in his tone had shifted too—a historian's discomfort with a case that wouldn't close. "Before we proceed further, there is a matter I would raise. Last night a document came into my possession." He paused, and Jonah's heart performed one long, slow, impossible beat. "Unofficially. Left for me, unsigned, by persons unknown. I have spent thirty years distinguishing authentic records from manufactured ones, and I will say this much: if the document I read this morning is what it appears to be—a field chaplain's assistant's letter, contemporaneous, bearing details consistent with independently recorded troop movements—it does not describe a security action. It describes an execution. With a named author of the order."

The room went very quiet. The candle-smell of the cellar seemed to follow them up into daylight.

Vane turned from the window very slowly, and for the first time since Jonah had known him, the charm was simply gone, lifted off like a cloth pulled from furniture in an empty house. What stood there was smaller and colder and much more dangerous.

"That letter," Vane said, "was burned with the rest of the non-regimental correspondence."

"So it was," said Ollerenshaw. "Yet here we are."

At which point the door opened—not the guarded door the soldiers had been holding all morning, but the far service door, the one the household used—and Marisol Quill came through it with flour still on her forearms and her chin up, flanked, absurdly, unbearably, by a girl of nine carrying a rag doll with mismatched buttons, and behind them all three of them, soldiers, historians, majors, turned as one, because Hazel walked straight across the east room as though it were the village square she had drawn in charcoal, and placed the doll on the table in front of Ollerenshaw, and said, in a voice nobody in that room had ever heard before:

"He's wearing it now. Look at his hand."

She pointed at the grey glove.
