## 20. The Names Read Aloud

The morning after the arrest, the orchard smelled of rain and ash. The burn pile behind the east wall had finally gone out in the night, drowned by a slow autumn drizzle, and what remained of it was a black scar of pulp and wet paper that no one had yet found the heart to shovel over. I stood at my window in Ward Six and watched the smoke ghosting off it, thin as breath on glass, and I thought: *that is how close it came.* All of it—every name, every dying confession, every charcoal line Hazel had drawn—nearly smoke.

Instead, Vane was in a cart under guard, rolling toward the Ministry with his grey glove wrapped in fresh bandages he did not deserve, and I was still here, and the ledger was still under the floorboard, whole.

I took it out one last time as a hidden thing. Kneeling in the grey light, prying up the board with the flat of a knife, feeling the paper's familiar weight in my hands—the weight I had carried alone for two years. Then I carried it downstairs openly, under my arm, where anyone could see it, and the strangeness of that nearly undid me before the day had properly begun.

* * *

We buried the last of the ward's dead that afternoon.

There were four graves to fill, dug that morning by orderlies who worked in silence and refused the pipes they were offered. One was Corporal Elwood Brant, who had given his deposition eleven days ago with Marisol's pen scratching beside him, and who had held on longer than any of us expected, as if he'd been waiting to hear that the letter survived. When Marisol told him it had—told him the youngest historian had read it aloud in the great hall while soldiers stood witness—he'd closed his eyes and said, "Then that's the war finished for me," and gone within the hour. The others were two privates whose names had appeared in none of Vane's tidy regimental rolls, and a woman from Harrow Bend named Sable Marsh, who had been brought to us in the spring with a wound nobody could account for and who had never once asked why we kept asking her questions. She had answered every one.

The ground behind the orchard wall was soft from the rain, and the graves went down easy, which felt like a small mercy or a small insult; I couldn't decide which.

The patients who could walk came out to stand among the apple trees. There were perhaps forty of them, leaning on crutches and on each other, their greatcoats hanging loose on bodies the war had spent. Marisol brought out chairs for the ones who couldn't stand, and she brought Hazel by the hand, and Hazel carried the ledger.

I had let her carry it. That was a decision I made without deciding, the way most true decisions happen. She held it against her chest with both arms, the way children hold bread in a bread line, and she would not give it up to me until we reached the graves, and then she placed it in my hands as carefully as if it were an egg.

"Read them," she said. It was only the fourth or fifth time I had heard her voice, and it still landed in me like something dropped from a height. "All of them."

"Hazel," I began, "some of these are very—"

"All of them." She looked up at me, mud-brown eyes steady, and behind her the whole orchard of broken men went quiet. "They didn't get said. At Harrow Bend. Nobody said them out loud. Not once."

So I opened the ledger.

* * *

I should explain what the ledger was, because the historians never quite understood, and I have been asked about it since by people who turn its pages expecting strategy or revelation. It was not evidence, mostly. It was not even argument. It was a list.

It began as names—soldiers struck from casualty reports because they'd died of dysentery rather than glory, or because their captain didn't want the number read back to him, or because a clerk somewhere preferred a round figure to a true one. Then it became civilians too: the woman shot at the Millbrook crossing, the boy of twelve the patrol log listed as "one insurgent, adolescent." Then it became what people told me in the dark of Ward Six when the morphine loosened them—not just names but last words, the name of a farm left unploughed, a wife's first name, an apology spoken to no one. Two years of nights, written in pencil I sharpened on the doorframe of the linen closet, hidden beneath a floorboard while the official record grew sleek and clean upstairs.

Four hundred and six entries. I had counted more than once, in the way of a man checking whether a wound is still bleeding.

I stood at the head of the four new graves, among the old ones—there were sixty-one patients buried behind that wall by then, each marked with an apple-wood cross Marisol's father cut in his shed—and I began to read.

* * *

"Amos Dree, private, Fourth Battalion, died of fever, eleventh of March. His mother's name is Liddy. He wanted it known he was thinking of home."

Hazel repeated it. Not all of it—she gave back the name entire and the important parts after, in her clear flat child's voice: *"Amos Dree. Thinking of home."*

That became the shape of it. I read, she returned. Name and essence, stripped down by her to what mattered, so that each entry became a kind of call and answer, a bell rung twice. I read the small shameful deaths and the large unjust ones. I read the civilians. When I reached the Harrow Bend section—I had given it its own pages by then, thirty-one names gathered from Sergeant Pike's murmuring, from Sable Marsh herself, and at the end, in a different hand, in charcoal-soft pencil, from Hazel—I stopped reading altogether and simply handed the book down to her.

She was tall enough to be heard over the rows only because Marisol lifted her onto the low wall.

She read the names of her village herself.

*"Tam Broadhurst. Widow Cetch. The miller's boys, both of them. Old Nandine who kept bees. Mrs. Pell and the baby."* On and on, thirty-one times, and she did not stumble, and she did not cry, and I understood that she had done this before in the dark of whatever room she slept in, silently, moving her lips, keeping the roll her village could no longer keep. The men in the ward stood among the trees with their caps off and their heads bowed, and some of them wept for a village most of them had never seen, and that seemed right. Grief needs somewhere to go, and the war had given theirs nowhere. We were lending it ours.

When she came to the last name she paused. I knew which one it was. I had written it myself on the night she first drew the square at Harrow Bend, after she pointed at the officer's grey glove and then, after a long stillness, at the well in the corner of her drawing, and tapped it twice.

She read it anyway. *"My papa."* And then, quieter, the way you set something heavy down gently: *"He wouldn't run. He said somebody had to stay and be witnessed."*

* * *

Afterward, the men drifted back inside by twos and threes, the way convalescents do, unhurried, reluctant to return to wards after any hour spent outdoors. The sky had cleared to a pale rinsed blue. Marisol's father appeared from somewhere with a barrel of cider that had gone hard in the press-house and started filling cups without asking anyone's opinion, and for twenty minutes the orchard sounded almost like a place where people lived rather than died.

That was when the historians came out to us.

Three of them still remained at Wrenfield; the senior two had departed for the capital the day before, laden with the official papers Vane had curated for them and, unknown to themselves, with the chaplain's letter folded into the youngest one's coat pocket, passed to him by Marisol in the doorway of the cider press with the smoothness of a woman handing over a dish of plums. The eldest had been a colonel once and wore his scholarship like a uniform. The middle one took notes on everything and believed nothing. But the youngest—Perrin, his name was, a narrow man of perhaps twenty-six with ink-stained cuffs and the hunted look of someone who had entered the profession to find truth and kept finding paperwork—he came across the grass alone, stopping twice to look at the crosses.

Mr. Quill offered him cider. He took it, held it, forgot to drink it.

"Mr. Underhill." He said it like a title. He had learned my name only yesterday, in the great hall, when the letter was being read and everything Vane had built was coming down around his excellent posture. "May I ask—where is the ledger? The one the girl read from?"

"It's in my room," I said. "Under no floorboards, as of this morning."

Something moved through his face—relief, envy, shame, I couldn't have ordered them. "I have to make you an offer," he said. "And I want you to understand I'm making it against instruction. My superiors will want the ledger deposited with the Ministry. Catalogued. Restricted access, certainly. Perhaps—" he glanced toward the black scar of the burn pile, and his throat worked— "perhaps quietly lost, in ten years, when no one remembers to object."

"And instead?"

"Publication." He said the word the way other men say *surrender*. "Full publication. Every name. Under your own name, Mr. Underhill, with your testimony attached, and the depositions, and the chaplain's letter, and—" he lowered his voice and looked to where Hazel sat on the low wall, eating an apple with total absorption— "and whatever arrangements her guardians permit regarding the child's account."

I looked at him for a long moment. The orchard was quiet behind him. Somewhere past the wall, water was running off the burn pile into the ditch.

"There's a problem with that," I said.

"Instructed access? I can fight that fight—"

"No." I shook my head. "My name. That's the problem."

* * *

He didn't understand at first, and I suppose I can't blame him; he was a young man who had grown up believing names were free, that a name was simply what you called yourself in public. He had never worked two years in a hospital where the safest men were the invisible ones, where survival meant being unrecorded, where I had made a craft—an art, Marisol would say—of passing unnoticed through corridors full of powerful people. He had never had a reason to write a list in secret, and so he had never learned what a name costs.

But standing there among the graves, I found I could explain it, and more than that, I found I didn't entirely believe my own explanation anymore. Something had shifted in me since the great hall. Since Hazel had walked past armed guards with a drawing in her fist because no one had ever taught her to be afraid of her own voice.

"The nurses kept those people alive long enough to testify," I said. "Marisol transcribed half the depositions while stirring a pot. Hazel carried testimony in her skull across a country that wanted it dead. The orderlies dug these graves and cut these crosses and never once asked me what I was hiding under the floorboard, though Tam in Ward Four knew—I've seen him know—for a year." I looked down at the ledger in my hands. "And besides that, sir, there is a soldier buried in the third row from the wall, Corporal Edran Moss, who died shielding that book during a search in February. He saw it taken from me. He put himself between me and the aide's lantern and took the questioning that was meant for me, and he never told me afterward, and he never told anyone else either. So no. It doesn't go out under my name. My name hid for two years. Let it go on hiding. It's earned its rest."

Perrin was quiet. Then he said, carefully, "Then under whose name do I print it?"

I hadn't planned the answer. It came up out of me the way the names had come up out of Hazel, whole and certain.

"Credit it to the nurses of Wrenfield. All of them. Every pair of hands that held a pen or a patient or a frightened child while the official record looked the other way." I turned the ledger over in my hands, feeling the worn boards of its cover, warm now from sitting all afternoon in the sun against Hazel's chest. "'The Nurses of Wrenfield.' Put that on the spine. In fifty years, when some student asks who they were, there won't be an answer, and that will be correct. They were everyone. That's the point of them."

He wrote it down. Standing in an orchard among sixty-one crosses, with cider going flat in his untouched cup, he took out his notebook and wrote the words down, and I watched him do it, and I confess something loosened in my chest that had been tight since the first day of the war.

"You understand," he said, not looking up, "that this makes it harder to bury. A single author can be discredited. An institution of anonymous women—" He smiled, briefly, helplessly. "There's no procedure for silencing that."

"No," I agreed. "There isn't."

* * *

He asked for the ledger that evening, and I gave it to him, but not before one thing.

After supper, when the wards were settled and Marisol had gone to argue with the quartermaster about coal, I sat with Hazel on the window seat at the end of Ward Six, the ledger open across both our knees, and I showed her the pages she had never seen—the two years before Harrow Bend, all the names that had nothing to do with her village, the fevered soldiers and the shot boys and the mothers. She studied them with terrible seriousness, her finger travelling down each column.

"Did anybody say these ones?" she asked.

"Some," I said. "In here, sometimes. To me, at night. And once, today, out loud, under the trees."

"Not enough, though." It wasn't a question.

"No," I said. "Not enough."

She thought about that for a while, chewing the inside of her cheek the way she did. Then she said: "When the book gets printed, can I have one? A real one, with a cover. So I can read them again. Somebody should keep saying them, after everybody forgets who wrote it down."

"You," I said, "are going to be a great deal of trouble when you're grown."

She leaned her head against my arm, which she had begun doing only in the last week, and which dismantled me completely every time, and together we looked out the dark window at the orchard, where the crosses stood pale in the moonlight and the ash of the burn pile was only another patch of ordinary night.

"They'll come for the ledger officially," I told her, because I had decided she would not be lied to, not about this. "Perrin's superiors. There'll be forms and demands and probably men in better coats than yesterday's. What happens next isn't finished happening."

"I know," Hazel said, with the enormous patience of the very young for the very slow. "That's why I need my own copy."

* * *

Perrin left for the capital three days later with the ledger sewn into his luggage and a receipt I made him sign, dated and witnessed by Marisol, because I had learned from the enemy the value of paperwork. The morning he rode out, the whole walking population of the ward turned out at the gate to see him off, unbidden, and stood in a ragged line as his horse picked its way down the lane, and Perrin took off his hat to us, and I thought: *there goes four hundred and six people, riding to the capital in a saddlebag.*

I did not know then—and how could I—that within the month the Ministry's reply would arrive at Wrenfield in an official envelope, and that it would concern Hazel, and that the machinery of erasure, wounded, was not finished trying to swallow her.

But that evening, I walked out alone among the graves in the last light, and I stood at the third row from the wall, at the cross of apple wood that reads only *E.M.*, and I put my hand flat on the cold top of it, and I said his name out loud, once, to no one—to the trees, to the dark, to the man himself wherever he was.

"Edran Moss," I said. "You are remembered."

And for the first time in two years, saying a name out loud cost me nothing at all.
