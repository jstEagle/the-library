## 21. What the Record Keeps

The bells in Ashcombe started at dawn, and by noon the whole town was drunk on something that wasn't cider.

Marisol could hear them from the orchard—the bells, then the fiddles, then the ragged cheer that went up every time someone remembered the war was over again. News traveled slow out here, but joy traveled slower still, arriving in waves all afternoon like weather. She stood at the kitchen window with a bowl of apples going soft in her hands and watched the road, where carts and walkers and one man on a mule all streamed toward town, toward the bonfire they said would be the biggest in the county's history.

Nobody streamed toward Wrenfield. That was the point of Wrenfield now. The hospital had emptied like a jug tipped over—last ambulatory patients gone three days ago, the walking wounded limping down the drive with their papers folded in their coats, waving at Hazel from the gate. The gravely ill had been transferred to the depot at Marle, which everyone called a mercy and no one who'd seen Marle believed was one. The matron left on Tuesday. The orderlies left Wednesday, taking the good spoons.

What remained was the manor, stripped of its function but not its furniture; the orchard, heavy with late fruit nobody would pick; and the three of them.

Marisol put down the apples and counted that fact again, the way she counted everything lately, as if arithmetic might betray her. Three. Her father had gone to town with the rest—he'd earned his fiddles and his ale, twenty years of keeping an orchard alive through a war—and he'd kissed the top of her head at the gate and said, *Don't let them decide anything while I'm gone.* Which was her father's way of saying he knew exactly what was coming, because the Ministry man had arrived that morning.

His name was Ferraby. He was not the youngest historian—that one, Tewson, had gone back to the capital with the ledger and the chaplain's letter and the whole weight of what had happened at Harrow Bend—but he wore the same grey coat and carried the same leather satchel, and satchels like that, in Marisol's experience, only ever held forms.

He sat in the great hall now, at the long table where Hazel had once laid her charcoal drawing down in front of men who thought history was something you could sweep. He had a file open. The file had Hazel's name in it. Marisol had seen it when he wasn't looking, which was a skill she had, reading things she wasn't supposed to—she'd grown up in a house where her father kept the orchard's accounts in code during the war years, and she'd cracked it at twelve out of boredom.

*Underhill, Jonah — discharge pending.*
*Quill, Marisol — civilian, no standing.*
*Hazel — minor, orphaned, status: ward of the Crown. Recommendation: institutional placement, Children's Asylum and Training School, Harrowgate.*

Institutional placement. Four words that meant a high window and a uniform and a name reduced to a case number, the same machinery that had swallowed villages whole now pointed at one small girl who had already been swallowed once and crawled out.

Ferraby had smiled at Hazel across the table and said, "You'll like Harrowgate, I think. There's a garden."

And Hazel—who spoke now, though sparingly, the way other people spent money—had looked at him and said nothing at all, and taken Marisol's hand under the table, and squeezed it once.

That squeeze was the whole argument. Everything else was paperwork.

* * *

Jonah found her in the kitchen an hour before dusk. He moved differently now—not better, exactly. Lighter in some ways, heavier in others, the way a man does when he has set down something he carried for years and discovered his body doesn't know how to stand without it. The ledger was gone. It was in the capital in Tewson's satchel, and someday it would be a book with a dead soldier's name on the spine, and Jonah still woke some nights reaching for the floorboard that held nothing.

"They're celebrating in town," Marisol said.

"I heard."

"You should go. You've earned fiddles too."

"I've earned sleep," he said, "and I don't know how to do that either anymore." He sat down across from her at the long table, and for a while neither of them spoke, because they were both people who understood that silence could be a conversation if you trusted the other person to hear it.

"He showed me the file," Marisol said finally. "Ferraby. He didn't mean to. Or maybe he did—maybe it's a test, to see if we'll fight him. Harrowgate. He wants to take her Thursday."

Jonah nodded slowly, the way he did when a patient took a turn and he was calculating what was left.

"What are her chances?" Marisol asked, and hated herself a little for using his language.

"With Ferraby? Fair. He's not cruel. He's a clerk." Jonah turned his cup on the table, a habit from the wards. "Clerks are worse than cruel men, sometimes. Cruel men know what they're doing. A clerk just moves the paper, and the paper does the damage, and his hands stay clean, and he sleeps fine." He looked up. "Has anyone asked her?"

"Asked Hazel? About Harrowgate?"

"About what she wants." He said it simply, as if it were a radical thing, which Marisol supposed it was. "Everyone in this whole affair—the Ministry, the historians, Vane, all of it—everyone decided what the record would keep. What happened to her gets written down, but nobody asks what happens next. She's not testimony. She's a girl."

"She's a girl who buried forty-one names in my father's orchard," Marisol said. "She reads. She's teaching herself letters off the chaplain's assistant's old primer. She won't eat fish. She sleeps with a doll that weighs twice what a doll should weigh because it's holding a letter that helped hang a major." She stopped, because her voice had gone somewhere she didn't like. "No. Nobody asked her."

"Then we ask," Jonah said. "Tonight. Before Thursday."

* * *

They found her in the orchard, because of course they did. The child had claimed the orchard the way other children claimed bedrooms. She was sitting against the old Russet, the tree her father—her real one, the one from Harrow Bend—had supposedly passed under once on a market trip, though Marisol suspected Hazel had invented that and knew she'd invented it and kept it anyway, the way you keep anything of a person you've lost.

Forty-one little mounds lay past the third row. The graves. Jonah had read the names there until his voice went rough, and Hazel had repeated every one, and the youngest historian had cried into his notebook and pretended it was the wind. Now the mounds were settling into grass, and by next spring they'd just be part of the orchard, which was exactly right. Fruit trees and graves both needed the same thing: someone to come back for them, year after year.

"Hazel," Jonah said, sitting down across from her with the care of a man who had learned never to loom over children, especially not this one. "Mr. Ferraby wants to take you somewhere. Have you met him? The grey coat, the satchel."

Hazel nodded.

"He says there's a school. Other children. A garden."

Hazel looked at her shoes.

"It's your choice," Marisol said, and heard how strange the words sounded in her own mouth, how rarely they got said to anyone in this country anymore, least of all to a nine-year-old. "Whatever you say, we'll do. But you have to say it."

A long silence. A wind came through the rows and shook down two apples, and Hazel watched them fall with the attention she gave everything, as if cataloguing it, as if adding it to the list she kept behind her eyes.

Then she spoke, in her flat, precise, child-logical way that always landed like a hammer wrapped in cloth:

"If I go to the school, who remembers me?"

Marisol felt it in her chest like a crack in ice.

"Who knows my name is Hazel? At the school, I'm a number. Numbers get lost." She looked up, and her eyes were the eyes of a girl who had watched a village full of people become numbers, become nothing, erased in an afternoon by a man with a grey glove. "You write names down. That's what you do. Both of you." Her chin trembled once and steadied, and Marisol thought: *there is iron in this child, and everyone keeps forgetting it because she's small.* "I want to be somewhere I'm a name."

Jonah closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he didn't hide it, because hiding was over for him now, that was the whole point of everything that had happened.

"Then you'll stay," he said. "Here. With us. If the Ministry allows it."

"And if they don't?" Marisol asked.

"Then we make them," Jonah said, and there was something new in his voice, something that hadn't been there a year ago when he'd been a nurse perfecting the art of being unnoticed. "I spent the whole war learning what erasure looks like from the inside. I'm not going to watch them do it to her in the name of care. There are procedures. Guardianship petitions. Tewson owes us—all three historians owe us more than they can say in an official report. And if the procedures fail—" he paused, and Marisol saw the old defiance surface, quiet as ever, permanent as bedrock, "—then I have spent enough of my life keeping records hidden. I can keep one person hidden instead. But it won't come to that. We'll do it properly. In the open. Where it counts."

Hazel reached out and took his hand, and then Marisol's, and held them both without looking at either of them, staring straight ahead at the graves among the trees, and the three of them sat like that while the light went gold and the bells in Ashcombe rang on.

* * *

Ferraby came at ten the next morning, and Marisol met him in the hall with tea she'd made strong enough to strip varnish, because she was raised polite but not stupid.

"I have the placement order," he began.

"I know. I read your file yesterday while you were measuring the windows."

Something flickered across his face—indignation, then, unwillingly, respect.

"She stays," Marisol said. "With us. Mr. Underhill and myself will petition for joint guardianship. We have witnesses—patients, orderlies, a groundskeeper with a reputation for honesty that even your office hasn't managed to ruin. We have a letter from Historian Tewson, if it comes to that, and I believe you know what's in Tewson's reports about this manor, and about who protected whom, and about whose testimony cracked open Harrow Bend. That girl's word opened the biggest wound in this whole rotten war's record, and you want to hand her to an institution with a number." She set down the cup. "So. You can be the clerk who moved the paper, or you can be the man who was here, at the end, when the record finally kept something good. Your choice. I understand you people like choices on paper."

Ferraby sat very still. Outside, faintly, the last celebrations drifted up from Ashcombe—a fiddle, a shout, someone singing badly and loudly.

"You'd have references, of course," he said at last, slowly, as if discovering the words. "Character statements. The historian's office would... look favorably. Given the circumstances." He cleared his throat. "There's a form."

"There's always a form," Marisol said. "Get it out. I'll make more tea."

It took him twenty minutes to unpack the satchel and an hour to fill the forms, and through all of it, Hazel sat on the stairs where she could see him, saying nothing, watching the way she watched everything, and when Ferraby finally signed the last page and blotted it, he glanced up at her and said, almost despite himself, "There's a garden here, isn't there. An orchard."

"Yes," Hazel said.

"Better than Harrowgate's, I expect."

"Yes," Hazel said, and Marisol saw the corner of her mouth move, which was more smile than most people got in a month.

* * *

Her father came home at sunset, smelling of ale and woodsmoke, and listened to the whole account leaning in the doorway with his arms crossed, nodding at intervals.

"So it's four," he said finally. "An orchard needs four hands minimum anyway. Has done since the war started. Be nice to run short of nothing again."

That night Marisol cooked in the big kitchen for the first time in months for no reason except abundance—eggs and bread and the early apples stewed with honey—and they ate at the long table in the great hall, the same table, because none of them suggested anywhere else and none of them had to. Jonah lit candles. Hazel set places for four without being asked, getting them nearly right.

Afterward, Jonah stood at the window looking out at the dark bulk of the orchard, and Marisol came to stand beside him.

"You'll stay too," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I have nowhere else," he said. "That used to feel like a sentence. It doesn't tonight." He was quiet a moment. "Tewson wrote to me. The book—it will take a year, maybe two. Censorship fights, ministry review. But it will come out. Under Corporal Edran Mott's name, per his family's consent. 'By the nurses of Wrenfield,' on the title page." He shook his head slowly. "My name won't be in it anywhere. And I find I don't mind. I minded, before. I thought being forgotten was the worst thing. Now I think—" he gestured at the table, at Hazel carefully folding her napkin, at the whole improbable room, "—now I think the record keeps what matters, and what matters was never my name."

"The record keeps this," Marisol said. "This room. Tonight. Even if no one writes it down."

"Someone will," Jonah said. "Eventually. Someone always does."

Far off down the valley, Ashcombe's last bonfire collapsed in a fountain of sparks, and the bells rang the hour, and in the orchard the wind moved through the rows over the graves and the trees alike, keeping them all.

Hazel appeared between them, tugging Marisol's sleeve, the weighted doll tucked under her arm.

"Can we go out?" she asked. "To the trees. Before bed. Papa used to say the orchard tells you things at night if you listen."

Jonah looked at Marisol. Marisol looked at Jonah. Neither of them said *she isn't yours* or *she isn't mine*, because neither of those things were true anymore in any way that counted.

"Yes," Jonah said. "Get your coat. We'll bring the lantern."

And as they stepped out under the first cold stars, three figures and one lantern moving down the rows between the living trees and the dead, Marisol heard Hazel begin, softly, in the dark, to recite names—not the forty-one tonight, but new ones, small ones, ordinary ones: her own, and Jonah's, and Marisol's, and Marisol's father's, spoken aloud into the branches like a planting, like seed going into ground, the way you say a thing so it will grow.
