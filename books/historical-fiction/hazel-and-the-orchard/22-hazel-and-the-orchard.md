## 22. Hazel and the Orchard

The apple trees have taken the manor.

That is the first thing anyone sees when they come up the old carriage road: not ruins, not a monument, just trees—rows of them, planted crooked where the floors used to be, growing straight and tall where the walls once stood. The east wing collapsed in my twelfth year, and Marisol said let it lie, and we did. The orchard keeper's daughter knew what she was about. You don't clear away a thing like Wrenfield. You cover it. You let the roots do the remembering.

I am thirty-one years old this autumn, and I keep the orchard now. My hands are brown to the wrist, there is a scar across my left thumb from a pruning saw, and I can name every tree on this hill by its parent tree, the way some people name horses. Visitors from town find that strange. They find it stranger still when they learn what I keep in the locked drawer of the press house, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp: a book with a dead soldier's name on its spine and forty thousand living names inside it.

*The Nurses' Ledger*, they called it when it was printed. By the nurses of Wrenfield, says the title page, which is true as far as it goes. It is also true that the hand that wrote most of those names belonged to a man who spent nine years pretending he had no handwriting at all, and that the book only exists because a girl who would not speak remembered a list of neighbors all the way down to the cooper's youngest son, who was six.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. I always do, in September, when the wind comes off the ridge smelling of bruised fruit and woodsmoke. This is the month everything happened. This is the month I teach.

* * *

My apprentice's name is Tam, and he is eleven, the age I was when Colonel Averill's aide set me down on Wrenfield's front step with a note pinned to my coat. Tam talks constantly, which I encourage, because for three years after Harrow Bend I didn't talk at all, and I know exactly what silence costs when it's the enforced kind. His silences are his own, earned honestly by an ordinary childhood, and he spends them like a boy with money in both pockets.

This morning I found him sitting cross-legged under the Grange tree—the big one, the first Jonah ever grafted, the one that grows out of what used to be the great hall—copying names onto slate.

"Where did you get those?" I asked him.

"Your book," he said, unashamed. "The one in the drawer. I didn't unlock it. The latch was loose."

I sat down beside him. The slate held fourteen names in a careful, wobbling hand. He'd copied them wrong—not badly, but wrong, transposed letters, a date misread—and I thought about how many times in my life someone has gotten a name almost right, and what it does to you when your own is one of them.

"You don't copy them," I said. "You write them out."

He looked at me the way Jonah looks at weather. "What's the difference?"

"The difference is that copying is for school and writing-out is for keeping." I took the slate from him, turned it over, wet my finger, and wiped one name clean. "Say this one back to me."

"Henry Oakes," he said. "Cooper. Of Harrow Bend. Age—" he frowned— "six? No. Sixty-six?"

"He was six." I handed the slate back. "His father was sixty-six. They're both in the book, father and son, because a sergeant named Brack refused an order and got shot for it, and a chaplain's assistant named Pell wrote down what happened before they shot him too, or near enough, and a nurse hid the letter in a doll because nobody searches a child's doll. Now say Henry Oakes again."

"Henry Oakes," Tam said, slower this time. "Age six."

"That's writing-out. Do it that way and you can use my pen."

He used my pen. He writes with it most mornings now, at the long table in the press house, while the cider press sits quiet except for season. Underneath its false bottom—which Marisol built with kitchen tools and Jonah holding the lantern, in a single night twenty years ago—it still holds nothing but the smell of apples and the shape of what it once carried. We never put anything else in there. Some hiding places should retire honored.

* * *

Jonah is eighty-two. He will be annoyed that I've written his age down, because he spent so many decades being nobody in particular that being somebody specific still strikes him as faintly indecent. But he has made his peace with it, mostly. These days he is impossible to miss on purpose. He wears a bright red scarf in winter—Marisol knitted it, deliberately, as a joke that stopped being a joke—and he tells stories to anyone who stands still long enough, and when people ask his name he gives it, plainly, the way other men hand over a coin.

He sits on the porch of the farmhouse we built where the stables used to be, most mornings, with a blanket over his knees even in warm weather because Marisol has decided he is fragile and he has decided that arguing with Marisol about her own household is a war he lost before I was born.

This morning I brought him cider, new-pressed, and sat on the step below him the way I have sat since I was small enough to fit sideways on that step.

"You're teaching the boy," he said.

"I'm teaching the boy."

"What do you start with?"

"You did," I said. "You started with the floorboard. I start with the tree. Everyone starts somewhere they can't be found, and then they come out."

He drank his cider and looked down the hill at the orchard, at the rows going gold at the edges, at the low stone markers we set among the trees where the graves used to be—no ranks, no regiments, just names, cut deep enough to hold their edges for two hundred years.

"I read in the gazette," he said, "that they're revising the official history again. Third time."

"They keep having to," I said. "Because the record keeps arguing with them."

He smiled at that. It was the smile I loved best and feared most as a child, the one that meant a thought had pleased him and he was going to act on it. "When I was your age—"

"When you were my age you were smuggling depositions out in flour sacks."

"—when I was *your* age," he continued, with the patience of a man repeating himself for eighty years, "I thought the whole work was the ledger. Write it down, get it out, get it printed. I thought once the names were in print, the job was done." He tapped the arm of his chair. "It isn't. Paper argues. People believe. But then people die, and the paper needs new mouths. That's you. That's the boy. That's whoever he teaches."

"And you?" I asked. "What are you, then?"

"Retired," he said serenely. "A man on a porch."

But that evening I looked out the kitchen window and saw Tam had carried his slate up to the porch, and Jonah was reading it, and correcting nothing, and telling him something that made the boy sit very straight, the way I used to sit when Jonah read the names aloud over the graves and I repeated each one after him until my voice gave out and Marisol carried me in asleep.

* * *

Marisol runs the house and, by extension, the parish, the market road, and possibly the county; she has simply never accepted any title that would make it official. Her hair went white in her forties and she kept it short out of what she calls practicality and everyone else calls style. She still cooks for harvest crews. She still reads people the way she read Jonah that first winter—accurately, kindly, without asking questions until questions were welcome.

She found me in the press house at dusk, checking the latch on the drawer, which is not loose; Tam lied, or half-lied, and I haven't decided whether to mind.

"He's ready," she said, meaning Tam.

"He's eleven."

"I was feeding a whole hospital at seventeen. Jonah was keeping a secret ledger at twenty-four. Hazel—" she paused, and I let her, because she is the only person alive allowed to construct that particular sentence— "Hazel was carrying the whole of Harrow Bend around inside her at eight, waiting for somewhere safe to put it down. Eleven is plenty. What matters isn't the age. It's whether they know why."

"Why do you think he knows why?"

"Because he wiped the slate himself before supper," she said, "and started over, and got Henry Oakes right."

* * *

We read the names twice a year now—once in spring, for the ward dead, the soldiers and the civilians who died at Wrenfield of wounds and fever while the world was busy ending; and once in September, for Harrow Bend, on the day. It takes four hours. Neighbors come up from town, and people from two valleys over, and lately a few who travel farther—a woman whose grandfather appears on page six hundred and twelve, a young officer from the reformed army who comes every year and stands at the back with his cap in his hands and has never yet managed to speak.

Jonah used to read them all himself. Then we shared it, Jonah and I, name answering name, call and response, the way it was that first year in the orchard with the graves fresh and the war barely cold. Now Marisol reads, and I answer, and Tam answers with us, and last spring a girl from the town school asked if she could try a page, and Jonah said yes before anyone else could breathe, and that is how it goes. The book gets heavier every year, and lighter too, because more voices carry it.

Afterward, when the neighbors have gone down the hill with jars of cider and the light has gone the color of the last apples, the three of us—or four of us, counting Tam, and we count him—sit on the porch. Tonight Jonah fell asleep mid-sentence, which happens now, and Marisol covered him without stopping her own story, which is love in the dialect of our family.

Tam sat on the steps with the published ledger open on his knees, though he cannot read most of it yet; the entries are terse, telegraphic, a nurse's shorthand for catastrophe. He traced one line with his finger and asked, quietly, so as not to wake Jonah:

"This one. *Underhill, J., orderly.* Why is he in his own book?"

"He isn't," I said. "Not really. He's in it because when they printed it, they asked him what name to put on the dedication, and he wouldn't give his own. Said he'd spent the whole war making sure other people got remembered, and he wasn't going to take a spot." I looked at the old man sleeping under his blanket, red scarf loose at his throat even in September, visible from the road, visible from anywhere, done forever with disappearing. "So they put him in anyway. Just one line. He pretends he doesn't know it's there."

"Did you tell him we know?"

"Many times."

"What does he say?"

I watched the orchard go dark, row by row, the way it has gone dark every September of my life, over the bones of a manor full of secrets that none of us kept, thank God, none of us, not one.

"He says," I told Tam, "that a record is only finished when it includes the person who was ashamed to be in it. And then he changes the subject, because he is a very old man who was very brave for a very long time, and bravery embarrasses him worse than anything."

Tam nodded, the way children nod when they have understood more than they can say. He closed the book carefully, both hands, the way we taught him, and set it on the table between the chairs where anyone coming up the path would see it plain—no drawer, no oilcloth, no floorboard. Just a book, in the open air, keeping watch over an orchard that grows where erasure used to live.

Above the valley the first stars came out. Jonah stirred in his sleep and murmured a name—I caught only the end of it, a woman's name, a patient from Ward Six, dead sixty years and spoken tonight and tomorrow and the September after that—and Marisol answered him softly from her chair, the way you answer in a call and response, the way the living answer the dead when the dead have been properly, permanently, lovingly written down.

"Tomorrow," Tam said, "can I start page two?"

"Tomorrow," I said, "you can start page two."

And far down the dark road, small and steady, came the sound of hooves—someone climbing toward the orchard lights, a stranger arriving late, the way strangers had once arrived at a manor door with sealed notes and terrible errands. But this rider slowed at the sight of our windows, and took off his hat, and called out that he had come a long way looking for the place where the book was kept, and that his grandmother's name was in it.

Marisol rose to light the lantern. I stood and offered him the step.

"We've been expecting you," I said, which was true, "for exactly as long as the book has been open."
