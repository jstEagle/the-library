## 1. The Weight of Glass

The light at Brindle Point had burned every night for sixty-one years, and Cyrus Thackeray had kept it alight for twenty-nine of them, and in all that time he had learned one truth above the others: that order was the only honest prayer a man could offer.

He climbed the tower at half past six, as he always did, his boots finding the iron treads by feel rather than sight. Ninety-three steps. He had counted them once, years ago, on a dare from his wife — *you count everything, Cyrus, I swear you'll count the hairs on my head when we're old* — and now the counting was simply the way his body knew the world. Ninety-three steps up. The door to the lamp room, swollen in the salt air, needing the hip-check he'd perfected over two decades. Then the room itself: circular, glazed on all sides, the great Fresnel lens rising at its center like something grown rather than built, a beehive of prisms and brass that caught even the greyest evening light and held it, multiplied, until the whole apparatus seemed lit from within.

Maren would have loved this room. She had loved it, in fact, before the sea took her brother off the *Cormorant* in '98 and something in her began to lean away from all bright things.

He did not permit himself these thoughts at the top of the tower. There was work. He checked the wicks first, running his thumb along their trimmed edges, then the oil reservoirs — three-quarters full, the kerosene thickening slightly with the cold, which meant the season was turning whether the calendar admitted it or not. The barometer in his sitting room below had been falling since Tuesday, slow and steady as a confession: 29.6, 29.4, 29.2. His father's barometer, brass-cased, brought from the light at Whitley Head when his father's hands could no longer manage the stairs. It had never lied to him. Storm coming, it said. Two days, maybe three.

Outside, autumn was closing over the point like a hand over a candle. The gorse had gone rust-brown along the cliff path. Down in the harbor the fishing fleet rode at moorings, hulls knocking softly against one another in the swell, and beyond the breakwater the North Sea lay flat and pewter-colored to the horizon, deceptively calm, the way a man can look calm the morning before his heart fails him.

Cyrus wound the clockwork that rotated the lens. The weight descended through the tower's core, geared and counterweighted, turning the great lens with a patience no human arm could sustain — one revolution every forty seconds, producing the flash character that mariners' books recorded as *Brindle Point: flashing white, every ten seconds.* Ten seconds of darkness, five of light. He had timed it against his pocket watch so many times in twenty-nine years that if the mechanism ever drifted he felt it in his chest before he saw it on the dial, the way a musician hears a wrong note.

Tonight it ran true. He gave the winding crank forty turns — never thirty-eight, never forty-two; forty was what the manual prescribed and what his hands had agreed to — and stood a moment listening to the gears take up the load, that deep, almost subterranean purr beneath the floor.

Then down the stairs again, ninety-three steps, to the kitchen, to the ledger.

* * *

The ledgers were kept in the bureau in his sitting room, and there were more of them than any visitor would expect. The official log, of course — weather, wind, lamp hours, oil consumed, vessels sighted, the dry bones of the service. But beside it lived the others. A ledger of maintenance, going back to 1887, recording every wick trimmed, every brass fitting polished, every pane of the lantern washed — and each pane numbered, because his father had numbered them, 1 through 24, beginning at due north and running clockwise, so that an entry might read *Pane 9, salt haze interior, vinegar and newspaper, minor scratching noted, monitor.* A ledger of supplies, cross-referenced with invoices. And one more, which he had never shown to anyone and which had no official standing whatsoever: a plain notebook in which, for twenty years, Cyrus Thackeray had kept account of debts.

Not money. Debts of kindness, of obligation, of things owed between neighbors — though in twenty years he had written few names in it besides one family's.

The idea had come to him from his father, or rather from watching his father die. The old man, near the end, had kept repeating a list — not possessions, not last words in any grand sense, just names and small facts, muttered like a man taking inventory: *Whitley Head, the barometer, your mother's ring to the eldest, Tom Ferrell owes me a book, I owe Tom Ferrell an apology.* As if dying were an audit, and one must present clean accounts. Cyrus had understood then that a life was a ledger whether a man wrote it down or not, and that most men's trouble came from keeping the accounts in their heads, where entries blurred and grudges gathered interest. Better on paper. Better in ink.

So: the notebook. *Henri Marchand, mended roof ridge, Aug. 1901 — repaid in kind, shingled my south slope after the gale, Oct. 1901. Account clear.* *Elise Marchand, bread during my illness, Feb. 1904 — repaid: lamp oil delivered during their coal shortage, Dec. 1905.* *Colette Marchand, born March 1899 — no entry possible. Carried forward indefinitely.*

That last line he had written the night the girl was born, half as a joke to himself, and had never revised it. Some accounts could not be cleared. They could only be carried.

It was nearly eight when a knock came at the cottage door — two knocks, a pause, a third, which was Henri Marchand's knock and no other's, a rhythm the man had used for fifteen years as if announcing himself by Morse code: *here, here… still here.*

"Come in, it's open," Cyrus called, though it wasn't, and then rose to open it himself, because that was also ritual.

Henri came in carrying the cold with him, smelling of woodsmoke and the tarred netting he mended in his shed. He was a small man, quick-eyed, French-born these thirty years but Brindle Point to the bone by now, his accent worn smooth as beach glass. In his hands, wrapped in cloth, a covered dish.

"Elise says you eat like a hermit when the weather turns."

"Elise is not wrong."

"She made cassoulet. She made too much. These are unrelated facts." Henri set the dish on the stove top and looked around the kitchen with the proprietary ease of long habit — at the ledgers stacked on the bureau, the barometer on the wall, the single chair pulled up to the table. "Your glass is falling."

"It's been falling since Tuesday."

"Storm?"

"Two days. Three."

Henri nodded and sat without being asked, which was the privilege of fifteen years, and Cyrus poured tea without asking, which was the answering privilege, and for a while they talked about nothing — the price of pitch, the harbor board's endless quarrel over the new winch, Colette's conquest of her sums ("She beat me at figures, Cyrus. My own daughter. She adds faster than I do and she lets me know it"). It was the kind of conversation two men could hold for hours while saying almost nothing, and it was, Cyrus understood, one of the true pleasures remaining to him: speech that required no accounting afterward.

But tonight Henri stayed a beat too long when the talk ran out. He turned his teacup in its saucer, once, twice.

"The registry officer comes next month," he said, to the window rather than to Cyrus. "They're making lists now. Of the foreign-born. You've heard."

"I've heard." Cyrus had heard, distantly, the way one hears weather reports for far-off seas. War was a thing that happened to other coastlines. "You've been here thirty years, Henri. You fish out of a Brindle Point boat. What list matters to a man like you?"

"That's what Elise says." Henri smiled, but the smile sat oddly on him, like a borrowed coat. "Almost word for word." He drank, set the cup down, stood. "Well. The dish stays. Wash it when you're done; she counts her dishes the way you count your steps."

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and for a moment seemed about to say something else — his eyes went to the bureau, to the stack of ledgers, then away again. "Goodnight, Cyrus. Keep the light."

"Every night," Cyrus said. "God willing and the oil holding."

The door closed. The cold came in behind it and settled.

* * *

At nine he climbed again. Ninety-three steps. He lit the mantle with the taper, adjusted the flame to the height marked on the brass gauge — a hair under two inches, always — and watched the lens gather the new light and send it out across the water, one white flash every ten seconds, sweeping the dark from north to north again.

From the gallery rail he could see the village strung along the shore road, a scatter of lamps and chimney smoke. Third house past the fish cellars, one lamp burning in an upstairs window: the Marchands'. He could tell which room by the height of the light. Kitchen, then. Elise sitting up late with her mending or her accounts — she kept accounts too, he knew; Henri joked that the two of them, Cyrus and Elise, were the only people on the point who believed the universe could be reasoned with if you wrote things down properly.

Colette would be asleep. Eleven years old now, and last spring she had climbed the tower path alone for the first time, marched into the lamp room, and asked to see how the light worked, and Cyrus had spent an hour explaining lenses and clockwork and the arithmetic of flash characters, and she had listened the way he himself listened — completely, silently, filing everything. *When I'm big,* she had said, *I will keep a lighthouse in France, where Papa was born, and then the light will go between your country and mine like a rope.* He had thought about that sentence more times since than he cared to admit. A rope of light between two countries. It sounded like a child's wish. Lately, in the newspapers, it had begun to sound like a prophecy of the thing that wished otherwise.

The wind was rising from the southeast, as the barometer promised. He logged it: *8 p.m., wind SE, fresh, glass falling, sky clearing ahead of system.* His handwriting in the log was the same hand as everywhere else in his life — upright, even, each figure closed — a hand that had been praised by three Inspectors of Lights and once described, in an official report he kept folded in the supply ledger, as *exemplary in its regularity.*

Regularity. That was the word the service used for virtue, and Cyrus had made it the word for grief too. Because the truth, which he set down nowhere, not even in the private notebook — especially not there — was that Maren had died eleven years ago this month, and that on the night of her funeral he had come back to this tower and lit the lamp and trimmed the wick and wound the clockwork forty turns, and that the doing of it, the terrible ordinary doing of it, had been the only thing that kept him from walking down the cliff path and into the sea after her. Grief was weather. It could not be argued with or outwaited. But it could be scheduled around. One could give it its allotted hour — his hour was the walk down from the lamp room, the dark stretch of stair between step sixty and step eighty, where the windows faced west and he was alone — and one could keep the rest of the day in order, and order, repeated faithfully enough, became a kind of bearing. The light flashed every ten seconds. The sea struck the rocks every seven. The widower climbed his stairs at six and nine and midnight. Between those fixed points, a life could be suspended like a lens, turning.

Below, the first gusts rattled the gallery glass. Pane 14 sometimes loosened in a southeast blow; tomorrow he would check the glazing points. He noted it in the maintenance ledger before he forgot — *Pane 14, check putty and pins prior to SE gale* — and dated it, and initialed it, C.T., and closed the book.

Down in the village the kitchen lamp across the shore road went out. A moment later, or perhaps a minute, an upstairs lamp bloomed in its place. He watched it a moment longer than the task required, then turned back to his instruments, mildly reproving himself, the way he reproached himself for every imprecision: attention paid beyond need was waste, and waste was disorder.

He went down. He ate the cassoulet — Elise cooked the way he kept ledgers, exactly and generously at once — and washed the dish, and set it inside his door for returning, and at midnight he climbed again, ninety-three steps, and wound the mechanism forty turns, and stood in the rotating beam's long silence between flashes.

The storm arrived on Thursday, as forecast. It was a bad one but an honest one, coming straight at the point with no feints, and Cyrus rode it out in the familiar rhythm — up, trim, wind, log; down, tea, log; up again — while the sea broke white over the low rocks and the tower hummed like a struck string. By Friday dawn the glass had steadied, the wind backed to the northwest, and Brindle Point emerged rinsed and glittering, gulls wheeling over the wrack line. Nothing damaged. Nothing lost. He entered it all in the ledger, balanced, complete.

It was on Saturday, with the air scrubbed clean and the sun out, that the first grey thing appeared on the horizon — not a cloud this time, but a ship, a naval patrol vessel standing in toward the harbor, uninvited and unhurried, flying a flag that needed no reading. And behind Cyrus, down the shore road, engines: lorries, army-grey, crawling up from the coast road in single file, trailing dust despite the week's rain, as if they carried something heavier than men.

Cyrus stood on the gallery with his logbook under his arm, twenty-nine years of regularity in his hand, and watched them come, and thought — with the strange clarity that precedes all catastrophe — *the glass is steady, the light is sound, and none of my accounts are ready for this.*

Down in the third house past the fish cellars, someone had stepped out into the road, small and still, watching the lorries too.
