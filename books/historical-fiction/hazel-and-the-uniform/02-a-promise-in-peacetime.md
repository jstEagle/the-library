## 2. A Promise in Peacetime

The lamp turned above him, steady as a heartbeat, and Cyrus Thackeray counted its rotations the way other men counted prayers.

Fourteen seconds to sweep the full circle. Fourteen seconds of light, four seconds of dark where the land interrupted, then light again over the water. He had timed it so often that he could stand in the gallery on a windless night and know, without the watch in his pocket, whether the mechanism was running true — the way a mother knows a child's breathing from the next room, not by listening but by the absence of anything wrong.

It was autumn now, two years past another autumn he did not let himself think about, and the mackerel boats had come back to Brindle Point harbor with their holds smelling of salt and silver. From the gallery he could see the Marchands' kitchen window burning yellow through the dark, a small domestic star on the hillside below the tower. Henri would be at the table this hour, working at his ledgers — for Henri Marchand had been a keeper of things too, though his ledger held accounts of canvas and rope and the small debts of fishermen rather than the great debt of ships' lives. Elise would be mending. Colette, if she had not yet escaped her bed, would be sitting under the table with a pencil, drawing ships on the backs of her father's invoices.

Cyrus knew all this without seeing it, and knowing it was a kind of company.

He went down the iron stairs, hand trailing the rail worn smooth by three generations of Thackerays, and in the watch room he opened his own ledger out of habit rather than need. Wind: southwest, freshening. Barometer: falling, slowly. Lamp: trimmed at nine, no irregularity. The entries marched down the page in his upright hand, each one a small assertion that the world was still orderly, that a man could meet chaos every night and defeat it with ink.

There was a knock at the door below — soft, twice, apologetic even in its knuckles.

He knew who it would be before he opened it. Nobody else on the point knocked that way, as though asking pardon for existing.

* * *

Henri Marchand stood in the lamplight of the doorway looking like a man who had walked a long way in bad weather, though he had crossed only forty yards of garden path. He was fifty-one that year and had been a strong man all his life — shoulders like a capstan, hands that could splice wire cable — but tonight he seemed assembled hastily, as if God had put him together from smaller pieces than usual. His color was wrong. There was grey in his lips that was not age.

"Cyrus." His accent wrapped the name, made it *Syrus*, soft at the end. "You are busy."

"The lamp is lit and turning itself. Come up out of the cold."

But Henri didn't want the watch room, and Cyrus understood why without being told. A dying man does not want to sit beneath the instrument of his friend's duty, in the smell of brass and oil and the patient machinery of rescue. They ended in the kitchen of the cottage instead, because that was nearest, and Cyrus put the kettle on because neither of them could think of anything else to do with their hands.

"Elise has gone over to Mrs. Penhale's," Henri said. "For the sewing circle." He said it carefully, the way you lay a card face down. "Colette is asleep. I told her I would sit up for the light."

"You should be sitting up for your own rest."

"I have rested." Henri lowered himself into the chair by the stove, and Cyrus saw how much the lowering cost him — the slow negotiation of a body no longer trustworthy, testing each angle before committing weight. "Three days in bed. I am tired of resting. It is like being buried politely, in advance."

The kettle began its low gathering noise. Cyrus took down two cups, then hesitated, and took down the third — the good one with the painted forget-me-nots that had been Maren's, that he never used, that he washed once a year in spring. He set it in front of Henri without comment. Henri looked at it a long moment.

"Your wife's cup," he said.

"It pours tea either way."

They sat with that. Outside, the wind had begun to find the chimney and try small notes in it. The lamp above them swept its fourteen-second circle over the sea, invisible from here except as a faint brightening along the horizon's hem, like the thought of dawn that never arrived.

"I have been to Doctor Kellow in Falmouth," Henri said at last. "I told Elise I was going for new boots."

"And are the boots satisfactory?"

"He says six months. Perhaps eight. He uses words like *growth* and does not meet my eyes when he says them, which is how I know." Henri turned the cup slowly on the table, not drinking. "In French medicine they would tell me everything and weep. In English medicine they tell me nothing and look at my boots. I prefer the boots."

"Henri—"

"Let me say it all, Cyrus, because I will lose my courage otherwise, and I have only tonight. Tomorrow I go home and tell Elise a different story, with better numbers in it, and after tomorrow I cannot speak of this again. You understand? Tonight is the only evening this is allowed to exist."

Cyrus sat down across from him. The stove ticked. Somewhere far off, a ship's bell buoy clanked its dull note against the swell.

"When I was sick in the winter — the cough you thought was the weather — I had time to think," Henri said. "Lying awake at three, four o'clock, watching the beam go round through the curtain. Every fourteen seconds, a stroke of light across the ceiling. Do you know what I thought? I thought: there is a man up there whose whole work is to make sure that strangers do not die. All night. Every night. Fifteen years. And I thought, I have been lucky enough in my life to live beside such a man, and call him friend, and borrow his tools." He smiled — the crooked, self-deprecating smile that had made fifteen years of Brindle Point trust a Frenchman entirely. "So you see, I am not coming to you as a stranger asking charity. I am coming to you as an auditor. I have examined the accounts."

"Henri, whatever you need—"

"What I need is to die well. That part is mine, and I have made my arrangements with God, and we understand each other, He and I. What I need *from you*" — and here his voice changed, became something older and more formal; Cyrus realized he was hearing the voice Henri had used in some other life, in some office in Brest or Marseille, before the boats and the rope — "is a promise. If you will give it."

The wind rose half a tone in the chimney. Above them the light passed, and passed again.

"My wife is French," Henri said. "You know this. You know also what she fled, or you guess — she has never spoken of it fully, even to me, and I have not pressed her. She came here to be invisible, Cyrus. That was the whole of her ambition. To raise our daughter on a rock at the edge of England where nobody would ask questions about the family she left behind, the name she carries, the papers she does not have. She has lived fifteen years among your people and made them love her, and still she keeps her suitcase packed in her mind. Always ready. Always packed." He stopped. His thumb had whitened on the cup. "And Colette is seven years old, and speaks English in the street and French at home, and believes her papa is the strongest man on the coast because I carried her on my shoulders to the top of your tower once and told her I could see France."

"Could you?"

"No. But a father may lie about geography." The smile flickered and went out. "I am not afraid for myself. I am afraid of the ordinary thing, Cyrus. Not fire, not shipwreck. I am afraid of paperwork. Of the town deciding, one day, that French is foreign. Of men in offices with lists. My grandfather saw such lists in his time. Lists are very efficient. That is what frightens me — not cruelty, efficiency. Cruelty requires a reason. Efficiency requires only a form."

He pushed back from the table, and Cyrus watched him gather himself, and understood suddenly that Henri had rehearsed this — had stood in his own kitchen practicing the words, so that his friend would not have to watch him break.

"If anything happens to me — when it happens, I mean, since we are honest tonight — I am asking you to keep them. Not their roof; they will have the house, I have arranged it. Not their bread; Elise has her needle and the shop accounts. I ask for something harder than money." Henri leaned forward, and the lamplight fell into the hollows of his face. "Keep them *counted*. When the day comes that someone stands with a list and asks who belongs here — be standing beside them. Be the man who says, these two are mine to answer for. I have watched you, Cyrus Thackeray. You are a man who answers for things. It is your whole nature. The light goes out, you answer for it. So I am putting my wife and child into your keeping, the way the Trinity House puts a lamp into yours. Formally. With witnesses." He glanced upward, toward the ceiling, toward the turning light. "Him, if no one else."

The kettle had long since boiled itself quiet. The tea sat untouched, going dark and bitter in the pot. Cyrus found that his throat had closed, and that grief had arrived ahead of schedule, sitting in the room with them like a third guest, patient and certain.

"You're speaking as though it's already decided," Cyrus managed.

"For me it is decided. For you it is only a question. I am asking the question while I still can hear the answer." Henri's composure slipped then — just a hairline crack, quickly sealed, but Cyrus saw the sea behind the seawall. "I know what I ask. I know it is not a small thing, though I am saying it in a small kitchen. I would not ask it of a small man. But you will say yes, because you are you, and then—" his voice caught for the first time, "—then I can stop being afraid, and spend what time remains learning how to leave them properly. Teaching Colette her scales. Repainting the shop shutters. Dying like a workman, cleanly, instead of like a drowning man clutching at everything. You would be giving me that, do you understand? You would be giving me a good death, in advance."

There was a silence in which the light swept the horizon three times, and Cyrus understood — with the strange clarity that comes once or twice in a life, unasked — that everything afterward would proceed from the next sentence. He did not know, could not know, the exact shape of what would follow: the grey lorries, the registry, the ink on his own trembling hands. He knew only what he knew that night, in his dead wife's kitchen, facing a dying friend.

That some vows arrive dressed as conversation, and you accept them before you feel the weight settle onto you — the way a man accepts an anchor over the side, and only afterwards understands what it will cost to bring it up again.

"Yes," Cyrus said. "You have my word. Whatever comes — they are mine to answer for."

Henri closed his eyes. For a moment he was so still that Cyrus feared the worst right there at the table; then he breathed, a long breath out, like a man setting down freight.

"*Merci,*" he whispered, and then, absurdly, formally, extending his hand across the table to be shaken like a contract: "*Merci*, Keeper."

Cyrus took the hand. It was thinner than he remembered. They shook once, gravely, two men sealing the most important document either would ever sign, with no paper between them and no witness but a lamp.

Henri stayed until nearly midnight. They drank the cold tea because it seemed wasteful otherwise, and talked of rope and weather and whether the new Fresnel lens at Longships was superior to theirs (it was not; they agreed it was not), and for an hour it was simply two friends in a kitchen, and the promise lay quietly among them like a sleeping dog.

At the door Henri paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the tower black against the stars, at the beam swinging out over the dark water.

"Fourteen seconds," he said. "I have counted it a thousand times from my bed. You know what I like best? Even when the fog comes and you cannot see it — it is still going round. All night. Whether anyone sees or not." He nodded slowly. "That is the whole of it, I think. That is what I want for them. Someone going round. Whether anyone sees or not."

Then he went down the garden path into the dark, walking carefully, and Cyrus stood in the doorway until the sound of the Marchands' gate reached him across the grass.

* * *

Two years later, almost to the week, Cyrus climbed the tower in the last hour of the ebb and found that his hands remembered everything.

The lamp needed no instruction from him. It turned, and flashed, and turned, indifferent to anniversaries, and he stood in the watch room with his ledger open before him and could not make his pen move. Because the page he ought to be writing — *Wind SW moderate, barometer steady* — kept being crossed by another entry, unwritten, unwriteable: the memory of a handshake in a kitchen, and the word *formally*, and the knowledge that had come to him lately, slowly, the way rot comes to pilings, from below the waterline where no one inspects.

That the world had changed. That there were now men in offices with lists, exactly as Henri had said, and that the lists were being drawn up closer every day — that the requisition orders were rumoured at the harbourmaster's, that Captain Bell's survey party had been seen measuring the headland, that names were wanted. All names. Written down. Accounted for.

Below him, through the dark, the Marchands' kitchen window — Colette's window now, Elise's window now — burned its small yellow light. Two years since Henri had died like a workman, cleanly, in his sleep, with the promise already signed and witnessed by a lamp. Two years since Cyrus had understood, standing at that same graveside, that a vow made in peacetime is the cheapest thing in the world to give and the dearest to keep, because peacetime does not collect its debts.

Peacetime was ending. He could feel it the way he felt weather three days out — not in evidence but in pressure, in the ache behind the ears, in the gulls going strangely quiet.

He wrote his entry at last, in his upright hand: *All regular.* Then he blotted it, closed the book, and stood at the window watching the yellow window below, and asked himself the first question of many:

When they come for the names — and they will come — whose name will I be able to cross out, and whose must I answer for?

Far below, at the foot of the tower, the road from the village lay empty in the beam's slow sweep. But down the coast, faint as a smudge, headlights were crawling the cliff road in the wrong direction — inland vehicles, army-slow, where at this hour no civilian cart had any business being.

Cyrus reached for his coat.
