## 3. The Orders Come

The lorries came on a Tuesday, which struck Cyrus Thackeray as wrong in some fundamental way he could not name. Disasters, in his experience, belonged to the night — to storms, to wrecks, to the sudden flare of distress rockets over dark water. He had built his whole life around the idea that the sea announced its intentions before it took anything, and that a man who kept watch would never be wholly unprepared. But these lorries arrived at half past nine in the morning, under a sky of washed wool and thin sun, grinding up the coast road in second gear with all the patience of men who knew they had already won.

He heard them before he saw them. The sound came up through the rock of the point itself, through the soles of his boots as he stood on the gallery wiping salt haze from the lantern glass — a low, insistent droning, like a swarm of iron bees, growing until the gulls lifted off the cliff grass in a single aggrieved sheet. Cyrus set down his cloth. He folded it once, twice, laid it on the sill of the lamp room where it always went, and only then did he look down the length of the causeway toward the town.

There were six of them, canvas-topped, grey as the winter sea they had come to look upon, and behind them a staff car flying a pennant that snapped and fluttered like something alive trying to escape. They rolled through Brindle Point's one street past the chandler's, past the baker's with its smell of yesterday's bread still hanging in the cold air, past the schoolhouse where children had been called to the windows and stood pressed against the glass like fish in a tank. The lorries stopped in the square by the war memorial, which was new and bore no names yet, only the promise of them.

Cyrus counted the seconds between the engines cutting and the first door opening. Fourteen. He was a man who counted things; it was the habit of a lifetime spent measuring oil and time and the interval between flashes of distant lights. Fourteen seconds of silence in which the town held its breath, and then boots on cobbles, and the ordinary world of Brindle Point ended without ever announcing that it had been living.

* * *

By noon the harbor master's office had been requisitioned, along with the customs shed, the fish cellars, and the two best rooms of the Ship Inn. Soldiers unloaded crates of signal equipment and coils of wire and began, with unnerving cheerfulness, to string telephone lines along the seawall. Cyrus watched from the foot of the tower, arms crossed against the wind, while a young officer with a clipboard consulted with the harbor master, Mr. Tredwell, whose face had gone the color of the lorries.

The staff car came up the causeway at two o'clock.

It stopped at the base of the lighthouse, and the man who stepped out of it was not what Cyrus expected. He had imagined requisition as a kind of piracy — loud men, rough hands, the arrogance of force. Instead the officer who removed his cap and looked up at the tower was perhaps forty, lean, with spectacles and a neat mustache going silver at the edges, and he carried himself with the unhurried air of an accountant arriving at a firm he already understood better than its owners did.

"Keeper Thackeray?" His voice carried without being raised. "Captain Bell. I'm instructed to inform you that Brindle Point, its harbor, its headland, and this lighthouse are now under the administration of the Admiralty and the War Office jointly. Here is your copy." He produced a paper, typed, folded once, and offered it with the precision of a man handing over change.

Cyrus took it. He read it standing there in the wind, the corners fluttering against his fingers, and the words swam and settled: *requisition*, *military necessity*, *coastal defense*, *effective immediately*. At the bottom, a signature that might have been anyone's and a stamp that had been applied with more force than care.

"The light stays lit," Cyrus said. It was not a question, but it came out like one.

"The light stays lit," Bell agreed. "That is precisely why we're here rather than elsewhere. You'll continue as keeper under military instruction. Your wages will be honored." A pause, small as a held breath. "There is also the matter of records."

"Records."

"All municipal documents. Registers, shipping logs, tide tables, correspondence files. And the parish ledger." Bell said the word *ledger* the way another man might say *ammunition* — as though it were the item around which everything else organized itself. "Every soul in the district, Keeper, entered by name, age, birthplace, and allegiance. The tribunal sitting at Falmouth requires a complete accounting of the population. Particularly the foreign-born."

The wind came off the sea and pushed against Cyrus's chest as if it meant to turn him. Behind him, above him, the great lens turned in its bath of mercury, steady as a heartbeat, throwing its beam out over water the color of pewter. Fifteen years he had kept that ledger current — births, deaths, arrivals, departures, every hand raised at church, every family that had ever moored a boat or buried a child in the little graveyard behind St. Brendan's. He had written Elise Marchand's name in it himself, eleven years ago, in his own careful copperplate, when she and Henri had come off the packet boat from Roscoff with a baby wrapped in a shawl and nothing else in the world.

*Marchand, Elise. b. Dieppe, France. Age 24.*

And beneath it now, in memory if not yet in ink, the word *enemy*, waiting to be written by other hands.

"You'll have the ledger," Cyrus said. "Tomorrow morning. Tonight it needs to be brought current — there are entries outstanding since the herring fleet came in."

Bell studied him for a moment. Through the spectacles his eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who had learned that people told you everything eventually, and that hurry only spoiled the telling. "Very well. Nine o'clock. I'll send a corporal." He replaced his cap, inclined his head — courteous, exact — and paused halfway back to the car as though remembering something incidental. "You've lived here long?"

"My whole life."

"And your neighbors? You know them well?"

"I know everyone here well," Cyrus said. "That's what the ledger is for."

Something moved behind Bell's face — not quite a smile, not quite its opposite. "Yes," he said. "I imagine it is." He got into the car, and the car went away down the causeway, and Cyrus stood alone at the foot of his lighthouse with a piece of paper he had not asked for and the taste of salt and iron on his tongue.

* * *

He walked home by the long way, along the cliff path, because the long way passed the Marchand cottage.

It sat low against the hillside with its back to the west wind, whitewashed walls gone grey at the seams, smoke rising thin and blue from the chimney. In the garden, despite the season and despite everything, Elise had leeks and kale standing in rows, and Colette — eleven now, all elbows and her father's dark quick eyes — was pulling a bucket up from the well with the grim efficiency of a dockhand. Cyrus raised a hand. Colette raised one back, then pointed urgently toward the sea, toward the lorries visible even from here as a line of grey beetles in the square below, and made a face that asked the question her mother never would.

Cyrus shook his head slowly. *I don't know yet.* She seemed to accept this, or at least to file it among the many things adults did not know, and went back to her bucket.

Elise appeared in the doorway. She did not come out. She stood with one hand on the frame and looked at him across forty feet of winter garden, and the distance felt enormous and entirely artificial, the way distances always feel when two people are used to speaking without words. Her hair was pinned up carelessly; there was flour on her wrist. She had been baking. On a day when the army arrived to take their town, she had been making bread, because her children — her child — would need eating tomorrow regardless of who administered the harbor, and Cyrus thought there was more courage in the flour on her wrist than in anything the soldiers were unloading down in the square.

She tilted her head slightly. *Well?*

"They want the records," he said. "The parish ledger. All the names."

He watched the understanding arrive in her, not suddenly but steadily, like water finding its level. She did not go pale. She did not step back into the house. She simply became very still, the stillness of a deer that has heard something in the brush and has not yet decided what it was, and then she nodded once and said, in English she had never quite lost the shape of Normandy from:

"Then it is just paper, yes? Names are only paper."

"No," Cyrus said quietly. "Paper is never only paper. Paper is what they build everything else out of."

She considered this. Down in the garden, Colette banged the bucket against the well stones, singing to herself something tuneless and French. "Henri's name is in that book too," Elise said. "Dead three years, and still they will count him."

"He'll be counted kindly," Cyrus said. "I wrote his entries myself."

"Yes." Something crossed her face then, brief as a shadow of a gull over water — gratitude, or grief, or the place where those two had worn together into a single smooth thing over the years. "Goodnight, Monsieur Thackeray."

"Goodnight, Mrs. Marchand."

He walked on. Behind him the door closed softly, and the light in the kitchen window came on, and he did not let himself think about what he was thinking about, not yet, not on the cliff path where anyone might see his face. There would be time tonight, alone, with the ledger open under the lamp. There would be time for thinking then.

* * *

The lighthouse cottage had been his father's before it was his, and his grandfather's before that, and it contained, after three generations of coastal life, almost nothing that could not be packed in an hour. This was not poverty exactly, though it resembled it. It was philosophy. His grandfather had a saying, carved into the beam over the hearth in letters gone black with age and smoke: *Keep only what can be carried.* The old man had been wrecked twice in his youth, had lost a boat, a house in Penzance, and a first wife to the sea in various forms, and had concluded that ownership was a wager against fortune that fortune always collected on eventually. So the Thackerays traveled light. Three chairs, a table, tools enough for the machinery, books enough for the winters, his mother's good china saved for occasions that had stopped coming. Everything in the house answered the question: can it be carried?

Only two things in it were heavy. The first was his father's barometer, brass-cased, which had hung by the door since before Cyrus could reach it, and which he tapped each morning the way other men said their prayers. The second was the ledger.

It lay on the shelf in the lamp room, not the cottage, because it belonged to the station and not to the man — a fat volume bound in green cloth, its spine cracked into white seams, its pages soft as linen from years of thumbing. Cyrus climbed the stairs that evening after supper, carrying a candle he did not need because the electric light was working, and set the ledger on the chart table, and opened it, and looked at what he was supposed to surrender in the morning.

The names ran down the pages in his own hand, decade upon decade of them. Births in blue-black ink. Deaths in the same ink, because he had never seen the sense of different colors for different sorrows. Fishing licenses, marriages, the arrival of the Kellys from County Clare in '04, the departure of the Pentreath boys to the mines, the influenza winter of '09 recorded in a column of small crosses that he had drawn with a nib worn nearly flat. Here was the whole town reduced to what could fit in a canvas satchel — and that, he understood now, was the point of it. That was why Captain Bell wanted the book. Not because names were dangerous in themselves, but because a government cannot intern a person; it can only intern a name. Find the name, and the rest follows, obedient as shadow follows body.

He turned the pages with a finger that had begun, very faintly, to tremble.

*Marchand, Henri. b. Le Havre, France. Fisherman. Naturalized 1908.* Dead now, the word *deceased* entered in Cyrus's own hand three winters ago, in weather much like this weather. *Marchand, Elise. b. Dieppe.* *Marchand, Colette. b. at sea aboard the SS Saint-Malo, 1905.* Three names. One of them dead, one of them a child born on the water with no country claiming her, and one of them a woman who had lived in Brindle Point for eleven years, who baked bread and grew kale and pulled weeds from between the cobbles of her path because she could not abide disorder, who had sat beside Henri's bed in his last week and translated his muttering French into calm English for the doctor, who owed Cyrus nothing and to whom Cyrus owed — 

Owed what? He had promised a dying man. That was all. A casual thing, really, said in a room that smelled of camphor and tide, with Henri's hand light as a bird's on his sleeve: *If anything happens, keep an eye on them. Elise pretends she doesn't need anyone. She does. They both do.* And Cyrus had said *of course*, the way men say such things, easily, meaning it, never imagining that *of course* could grow teeth. Two years of quiet watching since — coal delivered before the frost, the roof mended, Colette taught the names of the constellations, a friendship conducted in favors small enough to be deniable — and none of it, none of it had prepared him for a Tuesday morning and six grey lorries and a polite man with spectacles asking for every name in town.

Somewhere below, the machinery turned. The light swept out over the black water, four seconds bright, three dark, four bright, the rhythm Cyrus had fallen asleep to for forty years and expected to fall asleep to for forty more. He listened to it the way a man listens to his own heart when something has frightened him.

They would register Elise. That was certain; the orders were posted in the square by suppertime, he'd seen the soldiers paste them up, and the wording left no shelter for interpretation: enemy aliens resident within coastal districts to present themselves for registration, subsequent internment as directed by tribunal. *As directed.* Not *if*. A woman born in Dieppe, married to a man born in Le Havre, mother of a girl born on a French ship — they would take her, and they would take her gently, with receipts signed in triplicate and a welfare officer assigned to see that Colette was *placed appropriately*, and the town would stand aside and call it regrettable, and the light would go on turning over their empty cottage, illuminating nothing.

Unless the ledger said otherwise.

Cyrus sat down at the chart table. He set the candle stub beside the book, though the electric bulb burned steady above him. His hand lay flat on the open page for a long moment, covering the name as though shielding a flame from wind.

In the morning he was to hand this book to a corporal. In the afternoon it would sit before Captain Bell, who would run a pale forefinger down the columns and mark what interested him. Every fact of Elise's life was in here, honestly recorded, in the hand of a man whose honesty was the only currency the town had ever needed from him. And somewhere in the back pages, in the burial register, in the sad alphabet of the departed, were names of women born across the Channel who had died and been buried and were beyond the reach of any tribunal —

He stopped the thought. He stood up and walked once around the lamp room, around the great lens turning in its slow silver patience, and came back to the table, and sat down again.

*Keep only what can be carried.* But his grandfather had never said what to do when what you carried was not a thing at all. When it was a promise. When it weighed nothing on any scale and could not be left in a storeroom or sold or sunk, when it walked with you into every room including the one where a captain sits with a ledger open before him and a pencil sharpened for marking.

The clock in the stairwell struck eleven. Below in the town, the lorries were parked and silent, the sentries stamped their feet at the harbor mouth, and in a whitewashed cottage on the hillside a woman slept badly or not at all with her daughter breathing beside her, and neither of them knew — how could they know? — that the keeper of the light was sitting alone above the sea, holding a pen he had not yet decided to lift.

Cyrus dipped the nib. The ink caught the lamplight, black and gleaming, patient as everything else that waited in the dark.

Outside, far out where the water deepened, the first long swell of a coming storm moved landward, unseen.
