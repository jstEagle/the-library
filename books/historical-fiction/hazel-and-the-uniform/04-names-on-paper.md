## 4. Names on Paper

The notice was nailed to the door of the post office on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the whole of Brindle Point had read it, and by Thursday the whole of Brindle Point had decided not to speak of it.

Elise Marchand read it last of anyone, because she had learned in fifteen years of living among the English that things posted in public were not meant for her eyes even when they were. She stood beneath the eave while a thin rain came off the sea and silvered her shoulders, and she read the printed lines twice through, because the first time she had understood them only as shapes.

*All persons of enemy origin resident within the district of Brindle Point are required to register their names, places of abode, and occupations with the military authority at the harbour office. Failure to register will be treated as an offence against the Defence of the Realm. Internment orders are anticipated for certain categories.*

Certain categories. The phrase sat on the page like something dead and small — a moth, a drowned fly — and yet it had been printed in London, set in type by hands that had never seen her face, never smelled the bread she baked, never heard Colette recite the names of the constellations in a French so soft it was nearly singing. *Certain categories.* She was one now. She had become a category the way other women became widows: without ceremony, without being asked.

She did not touch the notice. She turned up her collar instead, and walked home along the harbour road with her basket empty because she had forgotten, in the end, why she had come into town at all.

* * *

The house next to the lighthouse path had been Henri's before it was hers — his mother's, truly, an old Frenchwoman who had crossed the Channel in another century's optimism and planted wisteria against the south wall as though England owed her France. The wisteria still came every May. Elise had long ago stopped wondering whether it bloomed out of loyalty or habit.

Inside, she set the empty basket on the table and stood a moment in the middle of the kitchen, listening. This was what her days had come to, this listening — for boots on the lane, for voices raised in the shop, for the particular quality of silence that meant people had been talking about you and had stopped. The stove ticked. The kettle, left from morning, had gone cold with a skin across its surface like the ice on a pond. Outside, the gulls argued over something neither would surrender.

Colette was at school. That was one mercy of the army's arrival that no one had thought to arrange: children were still expected at their desks, war or no war, and the schoolmistress, Miss Prewitt, had declared in front of the assembled village council that arithmetic would not be suspended for the duration, and if the Kaiser wished to dispute it he could present himself at four o'clock sharp. Elise had laughed then — three weeks ago, another country ago.

She took down the tin from the high shelf where Henri's papers lived. It had been a biscuit tin once; now it held a marriage certificate in careful copperplate, Colette's birth paper, the photographs — Henri in his uniform, absurdly young, the moustache he grew for the photograph alone already doubtful — and the letters, the good ones, the ones she reread in winter when the wind made the windows talk.

Henri had been French. That was the whole of it, and none of it. French-born, like her; but he had taken British papers in 1909, paid his shillings, sworn his oaths to a crown that was not his own because Colette was coming and Colette would be born here and here would therefore have to be enough. He had served — there was a paper for that too, somewhere, folded smaller than all the others, the regimental record of a man who had volunteered in the first foolish autumn and come home coughing blood from a gas cloud at a place whose name Elise refused to learn properly, because learning it would make it real, and it was real enough already in the sound of his breathing at night.

He had died in the spring. In peacetime terms — that is, before the lorries came grey over the headland — dying of what the war had put in your lungs counted as a private grief. Now the categories had shifted again. She was a widow of an alien, which was a different animal entirely from a widow of a soldier, though the man in the ground was precisely the same man.

She read the registration notice's words again from memory. Places of abode. Occupations. She had an occupation; she had always been quietly proud of it. She mended sails, and she kept the accounts for three of the fishing families who trusted a foreigner with their sums because she had never once got them wrong. Her name was written in their books. Her name was written everywhere in Brindle Point, in fifteen years' worth of ledgers and parish rolls and school registers, and now some clerk in a borrowed office at the harbour would draw a line under it and move it from the column of neighbours to the column of categories.

She put the tin back. Then she took it down again, and took out Henri's discharge paper, and unfolded it on the kitchen table with the care of a woman handling a relic, which is what it was.

*Henri Étienne Marchand. Private. 12th Battalion. Served with distinction. Discharged medically unfit, February 1915.*

Distinction. She touched the word. Paper held words so easily, gave them up so cheaply, and yet everything now depended on them — on ink, on filing, on whether a document had survived the journey from a battalion office in France to a registry in London to whatever grey room in the harbour master's house now decided the fates of sail-menders and mothers. Henri had told her once, near the end, when his voice had gone to a whisper and his jokes had gone with it: *"Keep everything, Elise. Paper is how they will remember us. Make sure the paper tells the truth."*

She smoothed the page flat with both palms, as though it might feel the gesture and hold still in the world's memory.

* * *

In the afternoon she walked to the point.

It was not a decision so much as a tide going out of her, pulling her along the path she had walked a thousand times, past the black rocks where Colette gathered periwinkles, past the gorse that held the light of the low sun the way a cup holds water. The lighthouse stood against the sky doing what it had always done, which was simply to be taller than everything and older in its purpose. Soldiers had dug into the turf below it now; sandbags lay in rows like sleeping animals; a machine gun under oilcloth pointed at nothing, at the whole grey ocean at once.

Cyrus Thackeray was outside the tower, cleaning the glass of the storm panes. He worked the way he did everything — slowly, thoroughly, with the patience of a man who understood that light passed through glass and that any flaw would eventually show. He had a cloth in one hand and vinegar smell rose faintly even from ten feet away, cutting through the salt.

Elise slowed. She had not intended to stop. Stopping would mean speaking, and speaking would mean either lying or telling the truth, and she had prepared herself for neither.

But Cyrus looked up, and there was no helping it then. Fifteen years of looking up at him across fences and gravesides and market stalls had built between them an ease that needed no explanation, and explanation was exactly what she could not afford.

"Mrs. Marchand." He nodded, the way he always did, formal as a deacon and about as warm as one, though the warmth was underneath, where warmth keeps longest.

"Monsieur Thackeray." Her accent thickened when she was tired or frightened, and she heard it thicken now, and hated it, because the accent was the category, the accent was the thing on the notice, the accent was what they would hear behind her dignity.

"The glass wants doing before the equinox," he said. "Storms come early this year. I feel it in my knee."

"You feel everything in that knee."

"It's an honest knee. It has never once lied to me." A ghost of a smile. His hands kept moving on the glass, circling, patient.

She stood in the lee of the tower, out of the wind, and looked at him, and he looked back, and the whole unsaid weight of the last fortnight stood between them like a third person. He had carried Henri's coffin. He had signed things for her at the solicitor's. He knew what was in the tin on the shelf. He knew, surely — everyone knew now — about the registry, the notices, the lists.

And she would not ask him. She understood this about herself with sudden clarity, standing there in the vinegar-and-salt smell: she would not ask. To ask would be to make him complicit before he chose it, to hand him her fear like a debt presented, and she had spent fifteen years in this country refusing to present anyone a bill. She had buried a husband with her own money. She had raised a daughter on her own sums. Whatever was coming, it would find her upright.

So she said, "How goes the light?"

"Wants a new burner. Requisitioned everything else — lamp oil, boats, the lot — but the light itself they need working, so I'm left alone with my own machinery, which suits me." He wrung out the cloth. His eyes went past her shoulder, briefly, to the sandbags and the soldiers, and came back. "Colette well?"

"Well. She has opinions about arithmetic."

"She gets those honestly."

They spoke of nothing for another minute — the weather, the herring, the price of paraffin, which had gone criminal — and it was the most honest conversation Elise had had in a week, because it was conducted entirely in code, and code, she thought, walking home in the failing light, was the language this war had given them. Every ordinary word now stood in for an extraordinary one. *How goes the light* meant *are we safe*. *Colette well* meant *I am watching over you both*. *Good evening, Monsieur Thackeray* meant — she did not let herself finish that sentence, even in the privacy of her own skull, where the censor was less reliable than she needed him to be.

* * *

That night the fear came, as it always came, after dark, when the tasks ran out.

She banked the fire and sat beside it with mending in her lap that she did not do. Upstairs Colette slept with one arm flung over the edge of the bed, utterly certain of the world, the way only children and the very foolish can afford to be. The wind had risen in the evening, working itself up from the southwest, and the lighthouse beam swung through the gap in the curtains at its appointed interval — white, dark, white, dark — steady as a heartbeat that was not hers.

They will come with a list, she thought. They will come to the door with a list, and my name will be on it, and Colette will ask questions, and I will pack one case, and the case will not hold a life, because a life does not fold to the size of a case. She thought of her mother's hands, in Dieppe, flour to the wrists, thirty years gone; she thought of the sea crossing she barely remembered; she thought of internment — the word had begun arriving in the town's mouth lately, dropped carelessly in the shop, in the lane, the way you'd drop a stone in a well just to hear how deep it went. An island somewhere. Barbed wire, someone said, though no one had seen it. Women too, someone else said, and then everyone looked at her, and then no one did.

The worst of it was not the wire. She found, searching herself honestly by firelight, that she could bear wire, could bear the island, could bear the indignity, because she had borne worse and her bones knew the technique. The worst of it was Colette at the window asking why, the worst of it was becoming in her daughter's eyes a person whom a country could take away, the worst of it was the lesson it would teach: that belonging is provisional, that home is a lease, that a girl born under this sky can be reclassified out of it by men with stamps.

*I will tell her,* Elise promised the fire. *Whatever happens, I will tell her the truth, in pieces she can carry. Papa served. Papa's name is on paper that says he served. We are not a category. Categories are what they write. We are what we carry.*

White, dark, white, dark. The beam swept the ceiling and passed on, indifferent and faithful at once, and she thought — not for the first time since the lorries came — of Cyrus Thackeray alone up there in the tower with his brass and his glass and his ledger, keeping watch over a coast that had recently begun keeping watch over itself.

There had been something in his face today, when his eyes had gone past her shoulder to the soldiers and come back. Not pity — she would have refused pity as she'd refuse spoiled fish. Concern, then. Concern with a shape to it, an intention, the way a man looks at weather he intends to work in rather than hide from.

She asked him nothing. She would ask him nothing. But she saw that he had seen her fear — seen it and taken note of it, the way he took note of everything, in that methodical keeper's mind of his, where apparently there was room amid the lamp hours and the tide tables to file away the face of a frightened widow.

She did not know, folding away the mending at last and pinning out the candle, that upstairs in the lighthouse, under lamplight steadier than her own, that same methodical mind had opened the parish ledger to the M's, and was reading her entry over and over — name, age, nation, abode — as a man reads a thing he has already decided to change.
