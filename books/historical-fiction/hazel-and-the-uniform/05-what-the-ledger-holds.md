## 5. What the Ledger Holds

The lamp was trimmed and turning, the great lens drinking its oil and throwing its measured beam across the water, and Cyrus Thackeray climbed down out of the lantern room with the smell of brass polish and paraffin on his hands and the particular emptiness of a man who has run out of small tasks and must now face the large one waiting below.

It sat on the kitchen table where he had left it that morning, squared to the grain of the wood with the fussiness of long habit: the parish ledger of Brindle Point, bound in green calf, its corners worn round as sea stones. Forty years of names in his own hand and his father's before him — baptisms at the mission chapel, marriages, deaths by sea and otherwise, the annual registry of souls resident upon the point as required by statute and, latterly, by the men in grey who had come with their lorries and their clipboards and taken possession of his lighthouse as casually as gulls taking a jetty.

Captain Bell wanted it on his desk by eight o'clock tomorrow morning.

Cyrus lit the storm lamp, turned the wick low, and sat down across from the book without touching it. The window above the sink showed a slice of black sky and, far out, the stuttering light of the naval patrol feeling its way along the shipping lane. Wind had come up with the dark. It moved through the marram grass with a sound like sand poured over paper, and beneath it the sea kept its old argument with the rocks, patient, inexhaustible, indifferent to empires.

He had told himself all day that there was nothing to decide. That the ledger was a record and records were records, that he was a keeper of lights and ledgers and neither kind of keeping entitled him to edit what he kept. That Elise Marchand's name stood in that book because she had been born in Dieppe and married a Breton fisherman who had brought her to Brindle Point fifteen years ago, and no amount of wishing would make her anything but French-born, and France and Germany being at war, and the regulations being what they were—

But the regulations did not say what they said. That was the whole trouble. He had read the internment order three times, by lamplight, while the soldiers unloaded timber for their gun platform on the headland. *Enemy aliens resident within the defended coastal zone are liable to detention.* Liable. A soft word for a hard thing, and behind it the barbed wire already going up around the disused herring station at Wold Harbour, and behind that the trains, and behind the trains — he would not think of what came behind the trains. He had seen enough photographs in the papers to know, and enough of the world to know that photographs were mercifully incomplete.

Elise. Colette, who was eleven and collected limpets off the low rocks and said her prayers in two languages.

Henri's voice came back to him then, the way it did most nights now, thinner than it had been in life, thinned further by memory's erosion. *You will look after them, mon ami. You will keep the light on them, yes?* Henri had said it laughing, or half-laughing, because the morphine made everything either very funny or very solemn and he had chosen funny, and Cyrus — forty-one years old, widowed six years, alone in a tower with a book of names — had said *aye*, the way you say aye to a dying man, meaning it entirely and understanding nothing. Keep the light on them. He had thought it a metaphor. He had not known he would be asked to make it literal, to stand between a widow and the state with nothing in his hands but ink and nerve.

The clock on the mantel — his father's, brass-cased, the only object besides the barometer he had carried out of his boyhood house — ticked through the quarter hour. Ten past eleven. He should sleep; the light wanted trimming again at two. Instead he reached out and drew the ledger toward him, and the leather was cool under his palms, and it seemed suddenly heavier than any book had a right to be, as though every name inside it had settled like ballast at the bottom.

He opened it to the current year.

* * *

He read the way a man reads scripture in a crisis — slowly, hunting for a loophole, dreading one.

The March page. The April page. His pen had been steady in those months; he could see the steadiness, the confident downstrokes, and he hated that steadiness a little, the way you hate your own face in a photograph taken before bad news. *Marchand, Henri Émile. Fisherman, Roselea Cottage.* Dead in June of the growth in his belly, entered in the ledger with the date and the words *departed this life* because Cyrus had never learned to write *died* without feeling he was signing something.

*Marchand, Elise Jeanne. Widow, Roselea Cottage. National origin: French.*

*Marchand, Colette Marie. Child, Roselea Cottage. National origin: French.*

Two lines. Fifty-odd characters of iron gall ink. He counted them twice, as if the number might change, as if the book might take pity.

Colette was safe, or safer. The order used the word *adults*. Children went where their mothers went, of course, but the order did not name children, and Bell was a precise man — he had seen that within five minutes of meeting him, in the way the captain's eyes had gone down the requisition list ticking items with his finger, in the way he had thanked Cyrus for the tea and meant it. Perhaps precision could be appealed to. Perhaps a child was an irregularity too far even for a regulation.

But Elise. Adult, female, national origin French, resident within the defended coastal zone. Every box ticked in the government's own hand. She would be given — what, ten days' notice? A week? She would pack what she could carry, because that was all anyone was allowed, and Colette would carry the little tin with the limpet shells, and they would be put on the morning boat or the afternoon train, and Cyrus Thackeray, keeper of the Brindle Point light, would stand at his rail and watch them go smaller and smaller into a country of paperwork, and the light would turn above him as though nothing had happened, because nothing would have happened. Nothing ever happened to lighthouses. That was the arrangement. They stood still and the world broke against them.

He got up and paced. Six steps one way, six the other, the length of a keeper's kitchen, the length of a keeper's life. The wind had strengthened; somewhere overhead the vane creaked its slow circle. He thought: I will write to Bell. I will explain. She is the widow of a man who fished these waters for fifteen years, whose brother died at Verdun — wait, no, that was the cousin, the cousin died at Verdun, Henri himself had been too old for the French army and had sailed out of a Breton port carrying supplies under whatever flag let him keep sailing. There were things in Henri's past that would sound well set down. There were always things that sounded well set down.

And he imagined Captain Bell receiving such a letter, reading it with those patient grey eyes, and writing back in his neat clerk's hand: *I sympathize profoundly. The regulations do not provide for sympathy.* Because Bell would mean it. That was the horror of the decent ones. The cruel ones you could hate and be done with. But Bell would file Elise Marchand away with genuine regret, between a requisition for barbed wire and a stores inventory, and regret would accomplish precisely nothing, regret was not a category the forms allowed.

There was only the ledger. The ledger was the town, reduced to what could be carried — that was what the lorries had come for, in the end. Names on paper. And if the paper was all the army truly held of Brindle Point, then the paper was also all it could lose.

The thought arrived the way weather arrives off the Atlantic: not decided upon, simply present, and then upon him.

His heart went hard and quick. He sat back down and put both hands flat on the table as if to steady a deck, and he said aloud, to the empty kitchen, in his father's voice because it was convenient: "No, lad." His father had said *no, lad* to smugglers and to revenue men alike, to any man who asked him to bend the truth of a chart. Honest to the last fathom, his father. Drowned honest, too, forty years of honesty ending in thirty feet of North Sea off the Skerries, because honesty does not keep lights burning, oil keeps lights burning, and judgment keeps them aimed, and judgment is not the same as obedience, is it, Father? Is it?

The clock ticked. The beam swung its white arm across the window, once, twice, the metronome he had lived inside for twenty-two years, and under that rhythm his breathing slowed, and the thing in him that had said *no, lad* grew quiet, and the thing that had said it grew quiet, and what remained was simpler and older than either: a promise, made in peacetime, to a dying man who had earned it.

He turned to the registry section and found the dead.

* * *

This was the part he had not let himself think about all day, and now he made himself do it properly, the way he made himself service the fog apparatus in fair weather so it would work in foul. If he was going to damn himself he would do it with craft, and craft meant knowing the book better than anyone who read it.

The ledger recorded residents, and residents departed, and departed residents were ruled through — a single line struck, dated, annotated — but never removed, because a ledger that forgot its dead was no ledger at all. So the dead of Brindle Point lay all through these pages in their struck-through rows, and among them, four pages back, in the entries copied over from the old mission register when the parish records were consolidated in 1909:

*Merrick, Agnes Louisa. Widow. National origin: English. Departed this life 14th February 1911.*

Agnes Merrick. He closed his eyes and saw her exactly: small, sharp-faced, forever in a coat too heavy for the season, keeper of the hens behind the rope-walk until her lungs went bad with the feather dust. No family. No friends who would speak of her now; the ones who might have were scattered or dead themselves. A woman who had arrived from inland, lived nine years, and left no wake. If ever a soul had been permitted to dissolve quietly back into the chalk and the grass, it was Agnes Merrick.

And here was the shape of it, laid bare and terrible in its simplicity: the registry of enemy aliens was being compiled *from this ledger*. Bell had said so. The army had no other source; Brindle Point kept no duplicate books. What the ledger said, the lists would say. If the ledger held no French-born adult woman at Roselea Cottage, then no such woman existed for the purpose of the internment order, and the barbed wire at Wold Harbour would go on waiting for other names.

It would require more than blotting a line. Blotted lines invited questions; questions invited comparison against the mission registers, the school rolls, the census returns of 1901 — which the army had also requisitioned, God help them all, God help the century. No. It required substitution. A living woman erased and a dead one resurrected into her place: *Merrick, Agnes Louisa. Widow, Roselea Cottage,* written in the same hand, the same ink, occupying the line where Elise Marchand had lived on paper for seven years. Let the clerks cross-check their censuses. In 1901 there had indeed been a French-born woman at Brindle Point — old Madame Follain, dead herself since 1908 — and Cyrus would wager his certificate that no clerk in London could prove which Frenchwoman had lived where, in a village the post office served twice a week. Paper fog. He would give them fog. He knew how to keep a light burning through fog; perhaps he could learn the reverse trade.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to another man — a criminal, presumably, since that was now the word, and the word sat in his chest like swallowed stone. Twenty-two years of service. His father's name and his grandfather's before that, keepers back to the oil-lamp days, a family that had put three generations into the saving of strangers. All of it reducible, if he were caught, to a line in someone else's report: *keeper falsified official records, charges pending.* Prison, likely. Certainly disgrace, which for a Thackeray amounted to the same thing at slower speed.

And against it: Elise hanging washing on the line at Roselea with the wind fighting her for the sheets, laughing when they escaped. Colette running the shingle with her skirts full of shells. Bread on his table on Sundays, bread he had not baked, offered by a woman who had buried a husband and still set two places out of habit and then, catching herself, deliberately set three.

*Aye,* he had said. He had said aye to a dying man with his hand in Henri's cold claw of a hand, and there were perhaps four sentences in a life that a man could not take back, and that had been one of them, and he had known it even then, in the marrow, however little he had understood it in the head.

"Forgive me, Agnes," he said softly, to the dead woman whose good name he was about to spend. "I'll not forget you for it. That's worth something, or ought to be."

He prepared his materials the way he prepared the lamp: with ritual, because ritual steadied the hands. Fresh ink, the good bottle, mixed to the same violet-black as the original batch — he knew his inks the way a painter knows pigments, and the 1909 consolidation had been written with the iron-gall stock from the blue tin, of which a dried crust yet remained at the bottom, and he shaved a knife-point of it into the fresh solution until the color matched under the lamp, matched under daylight-grey, matched to his own satisfied and horrified eye. The correct nib. A scrap of paper for practice, on which he wrote *Merrick, Agnes Louisa* six times, adjusting pressure, matching the slight forward lean of his younger hand, the way he had crossed his capitals in those days — he had stopped crossing them sometime around 1913, why had he stopped, some notion of efficiency, and now efficiency nearly undid him and he crossed them again like a young man.

Then he turned to the page, and he did not hesitate, because hesitation was the enemy of good work and this, whatever else it was, would be good work.

First the erasure. He had thought about ink-removers, chemicals, blades — rejected them all; paper remembers violence. Instead: the paste-pot. Every keeper kept flour paste for repairs to chart and notice, and a damp cloth and patience would lift the top fibers of old writing if you coaxed rather than fought, thinning the ink to ghost. He worked with the cloth in small circles, breath held, the lamp turned up high, and line by line Elise Marchand faded from the page like foam pulled back off sand — *faded, not vanished*; under a strong glass a trained eye would find the grave of her there, the pale fossil of strokes — and Cyrus felt each pass of the cloth as a physical wrongness, a small tearing somewhere behind his sternum, because he was erasing a woman, he was unmaking a person who existed, who had eaten breakfast that morning twelve hundred yards from where he sat, and the book in front of him was the only document in the Empire that insisted she was real.

"That's the trick of it," he murmured to the empty room, and his voice came out cracked. "They don't need to find you. They only need to stop finding you on the page."

He waited for the paper to dry. He wiped the nib. He took up the pen.

*Merrick, Agnes Louisa. Widow, Roselea Cottage. National origin: English.*

The false line went down clean, the letters sitting obediently in the ghost-tracks of the true ones, and when it was done he sat back and looked at what he had made and found that his hands had gone still at last — stiller than they had been all day, stiller than they'd been at his wife's bedside, a deep terrible calm like the calm that comes before the third day of a gale. On the page, where a French widow had lived in ink for seven years, an Englishwoman long dead now kept her cottage and her safety, and no power on earth short of a confessor could restore the truth except the man holding the pen.

Except the man holding the pen.

He looked at that sentence inside himself for a long moment. Then he closed the ledger, and buckled its strap, and set it by the door where it would watch him all night with its green leather eye, ready for eight o'clock, ready for Captain Bell's tidy desk and tidy eyes.

* * *

He slept badly and woke at half one to trim the lamp, and while his hands did the familiar work — wick, oil, the lens turning its slow silver — his mind kept returning to one detail, one splinter working its way toward the surface of him.

Bell had asked, that first afternoon, standing among the crates and clipboards: *"Keeper, how long have you maintained this registry yourself?"* An innocent question. Clerks asked it everywhere. And Cyrus had answered honestly, because honesty was a habit twenty-two years deep: *"Since the consolidation. Nine years, sir."*

Nine years. The consolidation. 1909. The exact year the mission register had been folded into the ledger, the year Agnes Merrick had been copied in, the year any alteration would have to be dated from if it were to escape notice —

— and Captain Bell, who noticed ink inconsistencies the way other men noticed rain coming, had been auditing requisitioned records all week, alone in the customs house, with nothing but time.

Cyrus stood in the lantern room with the wind moaning at the glass and the beam sweeping out and sweeping back, and understood that he had committed his crime beautifully and then handed the investigating officer the one sentence that would lead him straight to it. Nine years, keeper. Everything in that book written since 1909 was written by you, and everything written by you can be compared with everything else written by you, and one day soon a meticulous man would lay the pages side by side under a strong light.

Below him, past the dark shape of the headland, one window still glimmered at Roselea Cottage. Elise, awake or asleep, unaware that her existence now hung on the difference between two shades of violet-black.

Cyrus turned back to the lamp and began, with steady hands, to trim the wick for the rest of the night.
