## 6. The Officer Who Counts

Captain Edmund Bell had a habit, acquired in India and never surrendered, of counting things when he entered a room. Chairs. Windows. The distance between the door and the farthest wall. It was not nervousness; he had long ago stopped being nervous about anything smaller than artillery fire. It was simply the way his mind took hold of a place — by its numbers, its measures, its small verifiable truths — before it trusted the place to hold anything larger.

So when he entered the requisitioned customs house at Brindle Point on the third morning of the occupation, he counted, without meaning to: fourteen crates of municipal records, one broken windowpane boarded with a fish-crate lid, two soldiers playing cards at the far table who stood up too quickly, and forty-one gulls on the roof, though the gulls he did not write down.

The customs house smelled of salt-soaked oak and old ink, that particular smell of coastal offices where paper lived its whole life in damp air and grew soft as bread. Rain had come in the night and sat against the windows in a fine grey curtain. Beyond it, the harbour lay low and iron-coloured, the fishing boats pulled up in rows like sleeping animals, and past the mole the sea ran white all the way to the point, where the lighthouse stood with its fresh military sentry posted at the base like a nail driven through the town's oldest finger.

Bell removed his coat, hung it precisely over the back of the chair marked for him by Sergeant Doyle — who had learned within two days that the Captain noticed crooked things — and sat down at the table where the parish ledger waited.

It was a handsome book. That was his first observation, and he made it professionally, because Bell's father had been a stationer in Cheltenham and Bell had spent his boyhood among ledgers the way other boys spent theirs among horses. He knew vellum from rag paper at a touch, knew the weight of a well-bound spine, knew that a book this size, this sound, this carefully kept, represented years of a single man's hand. *Thackeray*, the requisition manifest said. *C. Thackeray, Keeper of Light, custodian of parish records.* There was no clerk in Brindle Point; there hadn't been for a generation; the keeper kept the names the way he kept the light, out of some old arrangement between the town and the sea.

Bell opened the cover.

He worked the way he did everything: slowly, completely, without interruption. The first hour was births. He read every entry, copied salient details into his own notebook in a hand so regular it looked printed — name, date, parents, parish of the parents if noted — and he did this not because the War Office required it but because he had found, in eighteen years of soldiering, that the only way to know a place was to know its dead and its born, its marriages and its debts. Armies moved through towns like weather. Bell preferred to be something else. He had never been able to say precisely what, but he suspected it was nearer to an accountant than a storm.

The entries themselves were lovely, he had to admit. A fine italic hand, consistent slant, iron-gall ink gone brown at the edges of the older pages the way good ink does when it ages honestly. Baptisms mostly, and burials — a lighthouse parish is heavy with burials — and here and there a marriage written in with what looked like genuine ceremony, flourishes on the capital letters, a small cross where the date met the year.

*Marchand, Henri Luc, of Brindle Point, m. Elise Fauvel, of Dieppe, France.* 1899. Bell paused over that only as long as he paused over anything: three seconds, the time it takes to file a fact. French-born wife. Registered alien, then, presumably, since '14. Married to a local man, naturalized or not, living — the census annexes were in crate nine — somewhere near the light. He wrote the name down. He wrote everyone's name down. That was the whole point of him.

By noon the rain had settled in for the day and Doyle brought tea, and Bell drank it while reading deaths, which he found fitting, though he'd have been hard pressed to explain why. Deaths told you more about a town than births did. Births were hope, and hope was uniform everywhere. Deaths showed you what the place was afraid of: drowned, drowned, drowned, fever, drowned, lost off the collier *Resolute*, drowned. And always, at the bottom of each terrible winter cluster, the quiet signature of the light itself — ships *not* lost, implied in every year the drownings thinned.

*Marchand, Henri Luc, d. 4 October 1914, consumption, aged 41. Survived by wife Elise and daughter Colette Marguerite.*

Bell filed that too. Three seconds.

* * *

It was at four o'clock in the afternoon, in the marriages, going back — he worked backwards through books, always, on the theory that errors accumulate toward the present the way silt accumulates toward the mouth of a river — that his pen stopped moving.

It did not stop dramatically. Bell's pen never did anything dramatically. It simply lifted a half-inch from the paper and stayed there, and Bell sat very still in the grey afternoon light with his head tilted slightly, the way a man tilts his head at a clock that has stopped ticking.

The entry was dated 1913. A fisherman named Aldous Pryce, married to a local girl, Thomasin Crew. Ordinary in every respect. The ink was right. The slant was right. The little cross beside the date, present in every marriage Thackeray had recorded for twenty years, was right.

But the ink was wrong underneath.

Not visibly — not to anyone who had not spent a boyhood running his thumb across ledger pages in his father's shop while his father scolded him for touching the stock. But iron-gall ink does something peculiar when it is laid over older iron-gall ink rather than blank paper: it bites differently. It feathers along the old strokes, follows them like water finding a dry channel beneath wet sand. On the reverse side of the page, held up to the window light, the ghost of the under-stroke shows through — a faint shadow-map of whatever had been written there first, blotted and scraped, but not entirely conquered.

There had been another entry under the Pryce marriage. Recently. Within weeks, certainly. The scrape marks were fresh enough that the paper still held a shine where the blade had passed.

Bell held the page to the window for a long moment. The rain ticked against the glass. Behind him, one of the card-playing soldiers coughed and was shushed by the other.

He did not feel what a less careful man would have felt, which is triumph. Bell had learned to distrust the hot feelings; they made soldiers brave and investigators stupid. What he felt was closer to disappointment, and beneath the disappointment, something uncomfortably like recognition. Someone had altered this book. Someone had done it recently, skilfully, and in haste — and had done it, judging by the care taken with matching the hand, the slant, the little cross, with love. Or with terror. In Bell's experience the two produced nearly identical penmanship.

He turned the page back and forth twice more, then set the ledger flat on the table and opened his notebook.

*Pryce–Crew marriage, 1913,* he wrote. *Under-entry removed. Compare verso shadows with register of resident aliens, crate nine.*

Then he went and fetched crate nine.

* * *

The registry of enemy aliens for the Brindle Point district was a thinner document than the newspapers made such registries sound. Forty-three names in the district entire. Most were German — trawlermen, a clockmaker in the village, two elderly sisters who kept bees and whose loyalty to the Kaiser was, by every local account Bell had collected, considerably weaker than their loyalty to their hives. Nine were French or Belgian. Of the French, the Marchand widow and her daughter were the only ones resident at the point itself.

Her registration card was in order, he noted. Photograph affixed, thumbprint taken by the constable from Harbury who came out monthly, address given as *the cottage adjacent the lighthouse, Brindle Point*. Occupation: none stated. Dependents: one daughter, aged eleven.

And there it was, the arithmetic that made the rest of the afternoon quiet. An enemy alien registered at the point. A scraped page in a ledger kept by a man who lived at the point. A war on, and tribunals announced, and internment orders expected within the month for Category B and C aliens all down the coast. Bell had seen the lists. He knew what was coming for the bee-keeping sisters and the clockmaker, and he knew, with the same certainty, what was coming for Elise Marchand unless someone stood between her name and the state.

Someone, it appeared, had already tried to.

Bell sat with the two documents side by side for a long time. Outside, the rain thickened; the gulls abandoned the roof one by one, and he counted them go — twelve, nineteen, thirty-one — the way he counted everything, because counting was how he kept himself from feeling, and feeling, just now, was inconvenient.

Because here was the trouble, and he was honest enough to look at it straight. If he reported the alteration tonight, the machinery would do what machinery does. The ledger would be seized as evidence. The keeper would be arrested — falsification of official records in wartime, a serious matter, a court-martial matter. The widow would be questioned, and questioned again, and her daughter taken into some district office's care while the questions happened, and at the end of it she would be interned anyway, only now with a scandal attached, and a man who had spent his life keeping ships off the rocks would go to a cell in Harbury with his name ruined.

And the alteration would be found regardless. That was the other truth Bell had to hold. He was not the only man who would eventually work backwards through this book; there were tribunals ahead, and clerks, and clerks' clerks. What he could do alone, he could not undo. The page was already scraped. The decision had already been made, by hands other than his, weeks ago.

Or almost alone.

Bell closed the ledger, aligned its edges with the table edge — a habit, purely a habit — and considered the matter the way he considered all matters: as a question of what could actually be carried.

He thought of the keeper, whom he had met exactly once, on the day of requisition. A tall, spare man with a burn scar on his left forearm visible below the rolled sleeve, who had handed over the keys of the light with both hands, like a man giving up a child, and who had said only, "She needs winding at midnight and turning by hand if the fog engine fails," and had looked at Bell with eyes that asked for nothing and dared nothing and somehow, in their perfect stillness, accused everything. Bell had put the man down in his mental catalogue as *competent, proud, grieving, dangerous only in the way that honest men are dangerous* — and honest men, Bell had learned in India during the last bad year, were the most dangerous of all, because they could not be bought and therefore could not be predicted.

He thought of the widow. He had not met her yet. He intended to, soon, under some pretext gentler than a summons — a welfare check, perhaps, the kind the Army performed badly and meant well by. He wanted to see her hands. He wanted to hear whether her English carried fifteen years of the coast in it or still broke against Dieppe. He wanted, though he did not fully admit this even to himself, to understand what kind of woman inspired a lighthouse keeper to take a knife to a parish book he had tended for twenty years like a garden.

Doyle appeared at the end of the room and cleared his throat. "Begging pardon, sir. Mess, sir."

"In a moment, Sergeant."

Bell took out a clean sheet of paper, and beneath his notes on the Pryce marriage he wrote one line more:

*Irregularity observed. Not raised.*

Then, after a moment, in his smallest and most private hand, at the bottom corner where no clerk's eye would travel first:

*Why?*

He blotted it, folded his notes into the inside pocket of his tunic — not the file, not yet, never yet — and buttoned the pocket shut over his heart, which was where he kept the things he had decided not to know immediately.

"Coming, Sergeant," he said, and rose, and counted the chairs on his way to the door: fourteen crates, one ledger, two soldiers, one secret. The rain had eased at last. Out past the harbour wall the sea was getting ready, in its slow grey way, to make the whole question of paper seem very small indeed.

That night the barometer at the customs house began to fall, steadily, and did not stop falling for three days.
