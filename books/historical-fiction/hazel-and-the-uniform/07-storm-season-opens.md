## 7. Storm Season Opens

The barometer began falling at noon on the last day of October, and by three o'clock it had dropped so far, so fast, that Cyrus Thackeray tapped its glass face twice with his knuckle, as though the instrument might be persuaded to reconsider. It did not. The needle sat wedged against the left of the dial like a bird pressed into a corner, and Cyrus stood in the lamp room reading it the way other men read scripture — with a kind of grim and total attention.

Below him the town of Brindle Point went about its afternoon oblivious, or pretending to be. The requisition had been in force six weeks now, long enough that the strangeness had worn grooves of habit: the grey lorries grinding up the coast road each morning, the sentry at the harbor mouth who had learned to nod instead of challenge him, the sound of entrenching tools biting the turf of the headland where no trench had any business being dug. The army had come to the point to defend it, and in defending it had occupied it, and Cyrus had discovered that a man could live inside his own life the way a gull lives inside a storm — battered but upright, going about the business of staying alive.

He wound the clockwork that turned the lens, checked the level of the oil reservoir, and looked south along the horizon, where the sky had gone the color of wet slate and was stacking itself into mountains. Forty years on this coast had taught him to smell weather before he saw it. This one would arrive with the dark. It would be the first real blow of the season, and the first since the soldiers came.

At four o'clock Private Willis, the young signalman assigned as liaison to the light station, clattered up the tower stairs two at a time and appeared in the doorway with rain already spotting his collar.

"Captain's compliments, Mr. Thackeray. They're doubling the watch on the headland tonight. Says you'll want hands if you need them."

"Tell the captain I've kept this light through thirty years of Octobers," Cyrus said. "I'll want nothing but oil and quiet."

Willis grinned, saluted wrong, and clattered back down. Cyrus watched him go, then turned south again. Far out, past the last of the visible water, something vast was clearing its throat.

* * *

Elise Marchand felt the storm coming in her teeth first — an old filling from Avranches, put in when she was seventeen, that ached before every hard blow since. She was taking in the washing when the wind arrived ahead of itself, a single long gust that tore the pillowcase from her line and carried it over the garden wall like a fleeing spirit. Colette shrieked with laughter and gave chase, and Elise let her run the length of the lane before calling her back, because there were soldiers on the headland now and a mother learned to keep her daughter within the radius of her own voice.

The cottage she shared with Colette sat below the lighthouse, close enough that at night the revolving beam passed across their ceiling once every seven and a half seconds. She had timed it in the early months of widowhood, lying awake, counting. Seven and a half seconds of light, then the dark, then the light again. She had come to think of it as breathing — the coast breathing, Henri's friend the keeper breathing for all of them, patient and inexhaustible. Some nights it was the only thing that made sleep possible.

She knew what was written on the registry now. A letter had come in September, official paper, an eagle stamped in blue, informing her that as an enemy alien resident within a protected coastal zone she was required to report monthly to the provost's office, observe a curfew of nine o'clock, and surrender any wireless apparatus or carrier pigeons. Carrier pigeons. She had laughed at that alone in the kitchen, and then stopped laughing, because laughing felt dangerous, and because Colette had looked up from her slate with those grave grey eyes — Henri's eyes — and asked why Mama was making that noise.

They did not know yet. Not officially. But Elise could count the way anyone could count, and she had watched the lists posted in the village square grow longer each week, names of families from Whitby, from Hull, from fishing villages down the coast, taken inland to camps whose locations were spoken of only in lowered voices. Her name was on the registry. The registry fed the lists. It was arithmetic, and she had done the sum, and she told no one, not even — especially not — Cyrus Thackeray, who already carried too much in his big careful hands.

She banked the fire, laid bread and cold mutton on the table, and pinned her hair for the night. Through the wall she could hear the wind organizing itself, finding its voice, testing the chimney. Nine o'clock, the curfew said. She was a lawful woman. She would be behind a locked door by nine.

The storm had other ideas about her evening.

* * *

It came ashore just after ten, and it came not gradually but all at once, as though the whole Atlantic had leaned its shoulder into the coast. Cyrus was in the lamp room when the first gust struck the tower, a blow he felt through his boots, up through his knees, into the roots of his teeth. The great lens turned steadily above him, throwing its beam out into chaos, and beyond the glass the world had ceased to exist — no horizon, no headland, no town, only a black roaring medium shot through with horizontal rain.

At half past eleven, the electric supply failed.

This had never happened before the requisition. The army had tapped the coastal generator for its searchlights and its wireless hut, and whatever the storm had done — felled a line, flooded the dynamo shed — the current to the lamp room simply died, leaving the reserve wick burning in the great lens like a match in a cathedral. The light dimmed to a fraction of itself. Out on that sea, somewhere, ships existed. Cyrus did not stop to think. He took the stairs in the dark, counted them by feel, and came out into the yard where the wind picked him up and threw him against the coal shed.

He needed hands. There were hands forty yards away, in the cottage below, and the knowledge of it went through him like a fishhook.

He knocked, and the door opened on its chain, and one grey eye appeared, and then the chain was drawn and Elise Marchand stood before him in a shawl with a candle, and behind her Colette slept sitting up on the kitchen chair, head tipped against the wall, having refused her bed in the excitement.

"The light's failing," Cyrus said. The wind ate half the words but she heard them. "The army's taken the power and the army's lost the power. I have to keep the wick trimmed and the mechanism turning by hand if it jams, and I can't do both alone."

She did not ask whether it was permitted. That was the thing he remembered afterward, the thing he would carry: that she did not ask permission, did not hesitate, did not point out the curfew printed in her own handwriting in her own registration book. She set the candle down, woke Colette enough to move her to the bed, wrapped her in blankets, kissed her hair, and said, "Madame Bessant lives across the lane. Her light burns. She will sit with her."

Then she took her oilskins from their nail — Henri's oilskins, let down at the cuffs, patched at the elbows — and followed him out into the night.

* * *

The path between the cottages and the tower was eighty steps in fair weather. That night it was a war. The wind came off the sea in fists; rain flew sideways, hard as thrown gravel; twice the air filled entirely with spray torn off the harbor rocks a quarter mile away, so that they walked blind inside the ocean itself. Cyrus went ahead with the lantern, turned, and found Elise right behind him with her head down and one fist knotted in his coat belt like a mountaineer on a rope, and there was no time to think about how that felt, because the tower door was banging in the wind like artillery and they had to reach it before it tore loose.

Inside, the stairs spiraled up into the drum of the tower, and the wind found the ventilators and played them like organ pipes, a sound so enormous and continuous that speech became pointless. They climbed. In the lamp room, the reserve flame guttered in its glass, small and stubborn, and the great lens turned its slow wheel around it, and through the curved panes the darkness raged.

Cyrus showed her the work. He showed her the trimming of the wick — a quarter turn, no more, the little brass key between finger and thumb — and the polishing of the condensing panes with a rag kept for that purpose alone, and the gauge on the oil reservoir that must be watched, and above all the clockwork, which was already laboring, its escapement clicking unevenly, threatening at any minute to bind against grit or cold and leave the lens stationary. A stationary light was worse than none. A fixed light confused a master into reading it as a star, or worse, as another vessel's lantern.

"If it stops," he shouted over the wind, "you turn this." His hand closed over the auxiliary crank, iron, cold as a gravestone. "Clockwise. Steady as you can. I'll mind the flame."

She nodded once and took off her shawl and folded it, precisely, over the rail, and rolled her sleeves, and he understood that he was not looking at a frightened widow doing him a favor. He was looking at a woman who had crossed an ocean once already, who had nursed a dying man through a winter, who had kept a child fed and unafraid through a war that called her its enemy. The wind could shout all it liked. It was shouting at the wrong person.

* * *

The hours that followed had no shape except rhythm. Trim the wick. Watch the gauge. Polish the pane where salt spray crept even through sealed glass, laying its pale film. And when the clockwork seized — which it did, twice before two o'clock and three times after — take the crank and turn, and turn, and turn.

The second seizure came at some black hour past midnight. Cyrus was bent over the burner when he heard the mechanism's click falter, stutter, die. Elise was already at the crank before he could speak. He watched her set both hands on the iron and lean, and the great lens resisted, and then yielded, and began its revolution under her arms' steady pressure, and the beam swung out over the void, once every eight seconds now, nine, slower than regulation but alive.

She turned it for eleven minutes without stopping. He timed her against his pocket watch, meaning to spell her sooner, and found he could not look away. The lamplight lay along her jaw and throat; her breath came hard through parted lips; her hands, red-knuckled, worked the crank in long patient strokes, and the light swept and swept and swept out over a sea full of men who would never know that a Frenchwoman they would never meet had turned it toward them by hand.

"You can rest," he said at last. "I'll take it."

"In a moment." Her voice was roughened but even. "There is a rhythm to it. If we change hands the rhythm breaks."

So he minded the flame and she held the rhythm, and neither of them spoke again for a long time, and Cyrus Thackeray, who had kept silence easily for twenty years, discovered that silence shared was an entirely different country from silence kept alone. The wind screamed; the tower swayed on its foundation stone by increments no instrument could measure but every body aboard could feel; the lens turned; and in the small gold world at the top of the tower there was exactly this: her breathing, his breathing, the work between them.

Once, near three o'clock, she said, without turning her head, "Henri stood this watch with you. He told me. He said you argued about the proper trimming of a wick like two priests arguing about God."

"He was wrong about wicks," Cyrus said. "I've had thirty years to be sure."

She laughed — a short, low laugh, startled out of her, swallowed at once by the wind — and he heard in it a sound he had not heard from her since before Henri died, and he turned back to the burner and gave the flame his whole attention, because it required very little and he had suddenly very little to give it.

* * *

Toward dawn the wind began to lose its argument with the land. It came in longer intervals now, gusts with pauses between, and in the pauses they could hear the sea itself, a continuous mountainous grinding along the point where the colliers and fishing fleet rode at double anchors. The eastern sky did not brighten so much as surrender — a slow dilution of black to bruise to the color of dishwater.

At the change of the watch the clockwork caught and held, freed of grit, ticking evenly again, and Elise released the crank for the last time and stood flexing her fingers, and Cyrus saw for the first time the state of her hands: blistered along the pads, split at two knuckles, the nails broken nearly to the quick. She saw him seeing and folded her hands into her sleeves, an old reflex, a woman hiding evidence.

"Madame Bessant will have breakfast," she said. "Colette will have interrogated her thoroughly by now. She will want a full account of the storm."

"Colette," Cyrus said, "should hear the full account. It was a historic blow."

"And what part of the account do I give?" Elise asked, and there was something in the question lighter than its words, almost playful, but underneath it, unmistakably, the true question — the one about the registry, and the lists, and the curfew she had broken, and what it would cost, and who would pay. *What part of this night may I keep?*

Cyrus took the rag and cleaned the last film of salt from the condensing pane, slowly, buying the moment.

"The truth," he said finally. "That the light needed keeping, and you kept it. Let Bell make of that what he likes."

Her eyes came up to his, sharp and grey in the dying lamplight. "You said his name," she observed quietly. "Not *the captain*. You said *Bell*, as if you knew him."

"I know his type," Cyrus said, and it was mostly true, and they both let it be true.

But as they came down the tower stair together, stiff-legged, into the grey wash of morning, Cyrus saw through the vent slit what the storm had uncovered on the headland: the soldiers had not sheltered in their tents through the night. They had worked — all night, by searchlight and hurricane lamp, digging, sandbagging, hauling a gun carriage up the slope — and now, silhouetted on the ridge against the wreck of the weather, stood a slim figure in a greatcoat with binoculars raised, not toward the sea but toward the tower. Toward the lamp room. Toward the two figures he had perhaps been watching there all night.

Captain Bell lowered the glasses. Even at two hundred yards, even through streaming rain, Cyrus would have sworn the man smiled.

And in Elise's pocket, folded small, was the letter she had not yet shown him — the one that had come with the morning post before the storm, bearing the blue eagle stamp and a single line beneath her name: *Notice of removal to be served.*
