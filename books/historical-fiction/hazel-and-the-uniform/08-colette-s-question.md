## 8. Colette's Question

The soldiers came to the point in the grey half-light of morning, and after that they were always there.

Elise learned their rhythms before she learned their faces. There was one who paced the headland path at the change of tide, boots crunching the frozen shingle in a pattern she could set a clock by. Another stood longer at the bend where the coast road curved past the Marchand cottage, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring the sea, though his eyes never rested on the water. They were young, most of them. Younger than Henri would have been now. Their greatcoats were too large in the shoulders, and their breath hung about them in little clouds that the wind took and scattered, and Elise thought, some mornings, that she could warm her hands at the sight of them if she let herself be foolish.

She did not let herself be foolish.

Colette noticed everything. That was the trouble with children — they missed nothing and understood less, or understood differently, which amounted to the same danger. She was seven years old and sharp as a gull after fish guts, and she had begun to count the soldiers the way other children counted gulls or waves.

"Maman," she said one morning, standing on the stool by the window with her elbows on the sill, "that one is there again."

"Which one?"

"The one with the red nose. He stands by the wall and he looks at our house and then he writes something in his book."

Elise kept her hands moving in the wash basin, wringing the sheet she had been scrubbing, letting the water run pink-raw over her knuckles. The cold of it was useful. Cold kept a face still when stillness mattered.

"Perhaps he likes the view," she said.

"He isn't looking at the view. He's looking at *us*." Colette turned, and her dark hair — Henri's hair, that black that never held light so much as swallowed it — swung across her thin shoulders. "Why do soldiers watch our house?"

Elise lifted the sheet from the water. Steam did not rise; it was too cold even for steam. She wrung it again, methodically, and gave herself those few seconds to find an answer that was neither a lie nor the truth, because Colette was old enough to smell either one alone.

"Because the army has taken Brindle Point for its own," she said. "They watch everything now. The harbour, the church, Mrs. Penhale's goat. You've seen them writing about the goat."

Colette considered this. The goat was a good lever; it always had been. But her daughter's eyes came back to her with the terrible directness of childhood, and Elise knew the lever had not held.

"Mr. Thackeray doesn't like them in the tower," Colette said. "I saw his face when they carried their boxes up the stairs. He looked like Grand-père did when the priest moved his tools."

"Mr. Thackeray is very particular about his lighthouse."

"Is it still his lighthouse if the soldiers are inside it?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Elise hung the sheet across the line by the stove, smoothing it flat with both palms, and turned to face her daughter fully. In the small room the light came grey through sea-fogged glass, and Colette stood in it like something painted — her father's colouring, her mother's stubbornness, and nothing at all of the country whose uniforms now ringed the town.

"It is his to keep the light in," Elise said. "Whatever else happens. Do you remember what I told you about keeping a thing? About Papa's watch?"

"Keeping isn't owning." Colette recited it dutifully, then ruined the recitation by thinking. "But the soldiers don't keep anything. They only write things down."

*From the mouths of babes,* her mother used to say, back in the old country, wiping flour from her hands. *God puts truth there because no one will punish them for it.*

"Come down from the window," Elise said. "Bring me your cup."

* * *

The questions did not stop. They never stopped now; they simply changed shape, the way weather changes shape without ever quite leaving the coast. At supper — barley, salt fish, a scrap of cabbage that had seen better weeks — Colette arranged her spoon parallel to her knife with great precision and asked, conversationally, as though asking whether it might rain:

"Papa isn't coming back, is he?"

Elise's spoon stopped halfway between bowl and mouth.

It had been four years since the burial on the hill above the church, four years since Father Anselm's Latin had thinned out over the winter grass while she stood with a seven-week-old Colette wrapped against her chest, and still the question could arrive without warning and strike her somewhere below the ribs. She had answered it before, in pieces, in the gradual way one explains death to a child who grows into understanding year by year. But this was not that question. This question wore the other one's clothes.

"No, my heart," she said carefully. "Papa died. We talked about this. He was ill, and the doctors couldn't make him well, and he went to God."

"I know he died." Colette's voice was patient, almost pitying, the tone of a child humoring a parent who has misunderstood. "I mean coming *back*. Like a ghost. Or like the men who come home from France with only one arm. Mrs. Penhale says everyone is coming back from the war except the ones who don't."

The fire ticked in the grate. Outside, along the headland, the soldier with the regular footsteps passed on his tide-change round, and Elise timed her breathing to the fading crunch of his boots.

"Papa was not a soldier in this war," she said. "He was a fisherman and a boat-builder. He built the Maréchal twins' lugger, do you remember, the summer before—"

"I know." Colette pushed a piece of cabbage to the rim of her bowl and contemplated it. "But the soldiers think Papa was French, and France is fighting Germany, and we're not supposed to be French anymore, are we? Tommy Trevanion says we're the enemy now. He said French people go away in trains and don't come back."

The cold went through Elise then, a cold that had nothing to do with the season. She set down her spoon. Her hands, she observed distantly, were perfectly steady, and she was grateful to them the way one is grateful to a dog that does not bark at the wrong moment.

"What do you say to Tommy Trevanion?" she asked.

"I said Papa is dead so he can't be sent anywhere." Colette looked up. "And then I hit him. Because he laughed."

"*Colette.*"

"He laughed at Papa."

There was nothing to be done about the hitting; part of Elise, a part she would admit to no priest, had wanted to hit him herself. She rose, gathered her daughter's bowl, and stood with her back to the child, at the basin, looking out the dark window where the lighthouse beam swung its slow white arm across the water and across the glass and across her own ghost-reflected face.

"We do not laugh at people," she said to the reflection. "And we do not strike them. Both rules. Even for that."

"Yes, Maman."

"And you are not to speak of trains. Not to Tommy Trevanion, not to anyone. If people ask about us, we are the Marchands. Papa fished these waters fifteen years. We are from here now. That is all anyone needs to know."

There was a silence behind her. When she turned, Colette was watching her with an expression far too old for seven — an expression Elise recognised, with a lurch, as her own: the look of a person doing sums and deciding not to say the total aloud.

"That's not all true, though," Colette said quietly. "Is it."

Elise crossed the room and knelt beside her chair, and took her daughter's small cold hands in both of hers, and told her the largest lie of her life, or the smallest truth, she could not determine which.

"You are my girl," she said. "You are safe. Whatever else happens in this bad, frightened world, you are safe, and I am here, and Mr. Thackeray keeps the light. Now bed."

* * *

Bed was a fiction they shared, mother and daughter, in the narrow room under the eaves. Colette slept — children sleep the way stones sink — but Elise lay listening to the house.

It talked at night, the cottage. Timbers contracting, slate ticking, the wind working at the doorframe with patient fingers. Fifteen years and she knew every voice it had. So when, past midnight, she heard the soft scrape at the kitchen door — not the wind; the wind rattled, it did not scrape — she was out of bed and down the ladder-steps with the candle unlit, moving in the dark by memory.

A folded paper lay on the stone just inside the threshold. No knock. No footstep retreating that she could hear, but the night outside had a quality of held breath that told her someone had stood there not ten seconds gone and chosen not to be seen.

She lit the candle with fingers that were not steady now, alone now, permitted to be unsteady now, and unfolded the paper.

It was a page torn from a printed notice — the kind posted on the customs-house board, the kind read aloud in church now instead of banns. Registry of Aliens. Internment orders expected within the fortnight for registered enemy persons residing in coastal districts. And beneath the printed text, in pencil, in a hurried hand she did not recognise, a single line:

*Mme Marchand — your name is on the list. The keeper knows.*

She read it three times. The candle guttered and steadied. Out the window the light swept past, once, twice, metronomic, indifferent, and Elise stood in the dark kitchen with her daughter asleep above her and the promise of a train in her hand.

So it had come. Some part of her had known since the lorries came growling over the downs in October; some part of her had known longer than that, known it in the way one knows a storm is coming before the barometer speaks — through the skin, through the behaviour of birds, through some animal arithmetic older than instruments. She was French-born. The war made arithmetic of such things. It reduced a woman to her birthplace and filed the rest away.

But — *the keeper knows.* She thought of Cyrus Thackeray's face at church last Sunday, how he had watched Captain Bell during the notices, his jaw set like mortar curing. She thought of the ledger — Henri had told her once, laughing, that Cyrus kept accounts of everything in Brindle Point as though God Himself might call for an audit. She thought of the way he now looked at her when he brought milk or mended the pump — the look of a man carrying something breakable across ice.

He was doing something. He was risking something. She did not know what, precisely, and she understood that she must not know, that ignorance between them was not innocence but armour — if she did not know, she could not betray it; if she could not betray it, then whatever he had done might hold.

Mothers learn this arithmetic early. You do not tell the child how afraid you are. You do not tell the friend how much you know. You carry the total silently and pay it out in small coin — a steady voice, a mended stocking, bread shared at the right moment — and you pray the reserve holds until the war ends or you do.

She burned the paper in the stove, feeding it in bit by bit so the flame wouldn't flare and show through the window. The pencil line glowed last, the words swelling with heat, going brown, going black, going to nothing. Then she raked the ash fine and stood a long moment with the poker in her hand like a weapon she didn't know how to use.

Above her, in the narrow bed, Colette sighed in her sleep and murmured something — a name, maybe, or half a dream. Elise climbed the steps and lay down beside her and pulled the quilt over both of them, and listened to the light turn, and turn, and turn.

*Papa isn't coming back, is he.* Not a question about ghosts at all. A question about the shape of their family now — two names where three should be, a household that existed on the tolerance of a coast at war. And Elise lying awake composing answers the way she composed everything now: carefully, in halves, each word weighed like ballast, because the wrong word spoken in daylight could follow a girl down a street, could follow a woman onto a train.

Tomorrow she would send Colette to Mrs. Penhale's with the good butter, a small kindness banked against future need. Tomorrow she would mend the shutter hinge that faced the road, because a house that looks tended is a house that looks intended — intended to stay. Tomorrow she would find reason to carry eggs up to the lighthouse, not to ask anything, not to know anything, only to stand in Cyrus Thackeray's kitchen for five minutes and see with her own eyes whether the man holding her life in his ledgered hands still looked like a man holding it well.

And tomorrow, or the day after, or however many days the fortnight spared her, the soldiers' pencils would finish whatever they were writing in their little books by the wall.

The light swept the window. In its white pass the room showed itself plain for one instant — the two of them, mother and daughter, one sleeping, one counting — and then the dark came back, and the beam moved on, faithful and blind, to show the same brief mercy to the water, and the rocks, and the waiting sea.
