## 9. Requisitioned

The order came down on a Tuesday, in the voice of a corporal too young to shave properly, delivered while Cyrus was trimming the wicks for the dusk watch. The boy stood at the foot of the tower stairs with a clipboard clutched to his chest like a breviary and read out the billeting notice as though it were scripture he did not himself believe: effective immediately, the keeper's cottage attached to Brindle Point Light would house six men of the coastal defence detachment. The keeper would be relocated to accommodate His Majesty's forces.

*Relocated*, Cyrus thought. As if he were a chair. As if forty-one years of Thackerays had simply been shifted two feet to the left.

"Where, exactly," Cyrus asked, "am I to be relocated to?"

The corporal consulted his clipboard. "The oil store has been vacated pending reassessment of storage protocols. It's dry. It's got a stove." He said this last with the tone of a man offering a consolation prize in a raffle, and seemed genuinely puzzled when Cyrus did not thank him.

So that was how it went. Forty-one years of Thackerays at the Point — his father before him, his mother's rose cuttings still blooming stubbornly against the south wall, the kitchen where Alice had died with her hand in his — all of it folded up like a map and put away so that six boys in khaki could sleep where his daughter had been born.

He packed in the hour before dusk. There was not much, because Cyrus Thackeray had long ago learned the discipline of measure. His clothes. His razors. His father's barometer, wrapped in a flour sack and set gently in the bottom of a canvas bag, face up, because you did not lay a barometer on its side any more than you laid a sleeping child on its face. And then the tin.

The tin sat at the back of the wardrobe's lowest drawer, beneath a false bottom his father had built for storing spare lamp parts during the scarcity years of the eighties. Inside: his own few papers, such as they were — Alice's death notice, Colette's christening card, which he had no right to keep and could not make himself surrender — and Henri Marchand's letters. Eleven of them, written over fifteen years in a small, upright, careful hand, the ink gone brown at the folds. Letters about mackerel runs and roof slates and, once, a long and unexpectedly beautiful passage about watching Colette learn to whistle. Not one word in any of them that could hang a man. And yet every one of them, now, in the winter of 1916, was evidence: correspondence between the keeper of an imperial light station and a French-born national whose name was or was not — depending on which version of the ledger Captain Bell happened to be consulting on a given afternoon — resident at Brindle Point.

He weighed the tin in his hands. It was heavier than tin and paper had any right to be.

In the end he did not bury it, though he considered the coal shed and even, briefly, the high-water rocks below the tower. Burying a thing made it hidden; hidden things looked like guilt when found. Instead he wrapped it in oilcloth, tied it with tarred twine, and wrote across the top in his plain carpenter's hand: *SPARE LAMP PARTS — DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT SUPERVISION*. Then he carried it out through the scullery, past the soldiers already tracking grey mud across Alice's kitchen flags, and along the covered way to the oil store, where he slid it onto the highest shelf behind a row of empty four-gallon cans, in the shadow, within reach of a man standing on a stool.

Within reach. That was the whole philosophy of it. A thing buried was lost; a thing within reach could be protected.

* * *

They moved in the way weather moves — gradually, then all at once. First their kit bags, stacked in his hall like sandbags. Then their boots, six pairs drying by his hearth, smelling of lime and wet wool. By Friday the cottage had stopped being his in every particular except the legal one: his name was still on the requisition papers as keeper, but Private Dunn had claimed the chair by the window, Lance-Corporal Meeks had driven a nail into Alice's pantry door from which to hang a haversack, and someone — he never learned who — had used the good teapot to hold rifle grease.

Cyrus told himself he did not mind. He had slept in the storeroom of the low light during the great gale of '93, when he was nineteen; he had slept upright in the lamp room twice since; a man who kept a lighthouse learned early that comfort was a rumour the land told. But there is a difference between hardship chosen and hardship imposed, and the difference had a smell — boot polish and bully beef and the chemical tang of signal flares — and it had a sound, which was worse: the sound of other men's breathing in your rooms at night, other men's laughter bouncing off your mother's ceiling, other men calling your kitchen *the mess*.

The oil store, at least, was his. Nine feet by twelve, stone-floored, with the old stove that had heated oil drums in another era and now, with a grate improvised from a biscuit tin, heated Cyrus instead. He hung his coat on the original hook. He set the barometer on the shelf where he could see it from his cot, because his father had taught him to read falling glass the way other men read faces, and he did not intend to lose the habit just because the army owned his windows now. Each evening he climbed the tower and worked the light, and each night he came down to nine feet by twelve of occupied country that was, at least, occupied only by himself.

It was in the second week that the inspections began.

* * *

They called them kit checks, which was a joke in poor taste, since Cyrus's kit consisted of a cot, a stove, and a barometer. But the young lieutenant who conducted them — a pale, thorough boy named Waverley with ink-stained fingers and a manner of profound apology — went through the oil store the way a customs man goes through a tramp steamer, lifting the folded blanket, peering into the coal scuttle, running a finger along shelf edges for dust.

"The storeroom's subject to weekly inspection, Mr. Thackeray. Regulations. Nothing personal."

"Nothing in it is personal," Cyrus said, and was pleased at how evenly it came out.

Except that it was, all of it, personal, and the lie lived thirty inches above the floor on the highest shelf, behind four empty oil cans, wearing a label in his own handwriting. He thought about it the way a man with a bad tooth thinks about the tooth: constantly, involuntarily, mapping the geography of it in the dark. *Spare lamp parts.* Would Waverley know lamp parts? Probably not. Would he open the tin anyway, because opening things was his entire employment? Almost certainly, eventually. The tarred twine would buy him ten seconds. Ten seconds in which a lieutenant might decide that a rusted tin of lamp fittings in a lighthouse oil store was exactly what the label said it was — or might not.

That night, after the light was lit and the soldiers' card game in his old kitchen had subsided into grumbling, Cyrus took the tin down and reconsidered it. The letters he could not destroy. He had told Elise once, on the cliff path, that some promises were architecture — you did not see them holding anything up, but remove them and everything came down. Henri's letters were that. Proof of a friendship. Proof, if it ever came to it, that a French fisherman and an English keeper had trusted each other across fifteen winters, which was either innocence or conspiracy depending entirely on who held the paper.

He unwrapped the oilcloth, counted the letters — eleven, all present, the whistling letter third from the top — and then did something he had been avoiding for a fortnight. He separated them. Five went back into the tin, beneath a genuine layer of lamp fittings he fetched from the tower stores: brass collars, a spare burner, wick keys, honest metal with honest grease on it. Six he sewed into the ticking of his cot mattress, flat against the frame side, where a hand feeling for lumps would find only what every old mattress owns. He redistributed Alice's death notice and Colette's christening card into the lining of his coat, one in each breast pocket' seam, over his heart, because sentiment deserved its own hiding place.

Then he sat on the cot, which now contained six letters and one secret, and listened to the wind come round to the northeast, and understood that he had crossed some line whose location he had never actually seen. He had falsified a ledger — that had been a single act, terrible and complete, done in one shaking hour of lamplight. This was different. This was a life arranged around concealment, a house of hidden seams. You could live in such a house, apparently. You could sleep in it. You could wake in it and make tea.

The barometer's needle stood at 29.1 and edging down. He wrote the reading in the log with steady fingers, and marveled a little at the fingers.

* * *

The near-discovery, when it came, came on a Thursday, and it came not because of suspicion but because of boredom, which Cyrus would afterwards consider the most dangerous force on the entire coast.

Private Dunn had found the tin. Not the tin — the *shelf* — while hunting for somewhere to stash a bottle of stout procured, via channels best not examined, from the Crown and Anchor in the village. Dunn was a garrulous boy from Leeds with a laugh like a door hinge, and he had decided over the preceding weeks that the keeper was "sound," principally because Cyrus fixed things without being asked: the stove door, Dunn's boot heel, the jammed bolt on the covered-way door. So it was in a spirit of confederacy rather than larceny that Dunn let himself into the oil store that Thursday afternoon while Cyrus was aloft, hoisted himself onto the stool, and began shifting oil cans to make a hiding place for his bottle.

Cyrus came down the tower stair at half-past three into the sound of scraping metal.

He crossed the yard at a pace he had not used since the collier year. The oil store door stood open, cold light pouring in, and there was Dunn on the stool, tin in hands, twine half-unwound, saying with enormous cheerfulness, "Beg pardon, Mr. Thackeray — just wanted a corner for my bottle, only your label says—"

"Spare lamp parts," Cyrus said. "Which is what they are. Which is why the label says do not open without supervision, meaning me. Put it down."

Dunn put it down. But he had already worked the lid loose, and inside, on top of five years' worth of brass and grease, lay the corner of a letter, cream-coloured against the dark metal, one small upright line of brown ink visible like a vein under skin.

For three full seconds neither of them moved. The wind filled the silence with the sound of the sea working the rocks below, patient, professional.

"That's correspondence," Dunn said slowly, and Cyrus watched the arithmetic move behind the boy's face — keeper, letters, foreign-sounding handwriting, the notices posted on the village board that everyone had read and nobody discussed in front of the keeper. "Only — only we had a talk, didn't we, about foreign post. Lieutenant Waverley said all correspondence going to non-British persons has to be—" He stopped. He looked at the letter again, and then, mercifully, at Cyrus, and his voice dropped as though the walls might inform on him. "This isn't going to France, is it."

"No," Cyrus said. "It came from France. Fifteen years ago. They're the letters of a dead friend, Private, and they are the only thing of him I have, and if you take your hands off that lid we will never need to discuss it again."

He had not meant to say so much. He heard his own voice and recognized, distantly, the tone of a man stepping off a known height. But Dunn was twenty and lonely and hundreds of miles from a mother who presumably missed him, and whatever moved in his face then was older than regulations.

"Dead friend," Dunn repeated. He set the lid down. He rewound the twine, clumsily, twice around, and patted the tin as though settling a dog. "Right. Lamp parts." He climbed down, retrieved his bottle, and then paused at the door with it under his arm, not looking at Cyrus. "There's a kit check Monday. Waverley. He does shelves." A beat. "Might be an idea to put heavier stuff on top. Tools, like. Nobody looks twice at a shelf what's all tools."

Then he was gone across the yard, whistling, and Cyrus stood alone in the cold store with his heart going like a auxiliary pump and the smell of stout hanging in the air.

* * *

That night he rebuilt the hiding place according to Dunn's doctrine. Spanners on top of the letters. A coil of rope. The kind of clutter that reads, to an inspecting eye, as industry. He sewed the fifth and sixth letters deeper into the mattress. He ate his supper standing at the stove and realized, halfway through the tin mug of tea, that he had just accepted help from one of the occupying garrison — that the walls he was building had a boy from Leeds mixed into their mortar now, and that this was either his ruin or his salvation depending on whether Dunn could keep a secret smaller than a bottle of stout.

He thought of Elise, in her rooms above the net loft, doing whatever quiet arithmetic she did each day to stay ahead of the registries. He thought of Colette asking her unanswerable questions. He thought of Captain Bell, whom he had seen only twice since the requisition, both times at a distance, both times with that unhurried, courteous attention that made Cyrus feel like a page being turned.

And he thought of Henri, dying, in the narrow bed by the window, gripping his wrist with a strength that surprised them both: *You will look after them, Thackeray. Swear it. Swear it on the light.*

*I swear it on the light,* he had said, two years ago, in a warm room, in peacetime — and here was the invoice arriving, itemized: a falsified ledger, a tin of letters, a mattress full of confession, a home gone over to strangers, and now the friendship of a homesick soldier as collateral.

The barometer stood at 28.8. Outside, the northeast wind had begun to organize itself into something with intent, dragging spray off the headland in long white smears. The old-timers would have called it a storm-breeder. Three days yet, maybe four, and then the sea would come up over the lower rocks and test every joint and seam on the point, as it did every year, indifferent to ledgers and tribunals and the paperwork of empires.

Cyrus banked the stove, lay down on his mattress of secrets, and listened to the garrison breathing in his house across the yard — and beneath all of it, far off but strengthening, the long first note of the coming storm, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Monday, he remembered. Kit check Monday. And beyond Monday, according to the gossip that moved faster than dispatches through the garrison, the military tribunal at Fallowcross was expected to sit before the month was out — and lists were already being drawn.
