## 10. The Second Storm

The barometer fell all Tuesday like a man sinking slowly into deep water, and by Wednesday morning it had stopped falling altogether — which was worse. Cyrus Thackeray had kept his father's instrument for thirty years, tapping its glass face each dawn the way other men consulted scripture, and he knew what that flat, breathless stillness meant. The sea had drawn back its fist.

He stood in the lamp room at first light, checking the wicks though they needed nothing, and watched the swell come in long and green out of the southeast. It was the wrong direction for a gale and the right one for trouble. Brindle Point's rocks ran out like crooked fingers beneath the water there, and any vessel driven before such a sea would find them whether she wanted to or not.

Below, on the headland, the soldiers were still working their pickaxes into the turf, throwing up emplacements for the guns that had arrived last week. Their voices carried thin and sharp through the cold air. Cyrus had learned to tune them out the way one tunes out gulls — until one of them shouted something about weather, and another laughed, and the laugh was swallowed whole by a sound from the east: not thunder, but the deep, grinding groan of the sea rearranging itself.

By noon the wind had come. By two o'clock the fog signal was useless beneath it.

* * *

Elise Marchand felt the storm arrive in her kitchen before she heard it — a change of pressure that made the window panes tick against their putty and set the kettle singing without fire. Colette stood at the glass with her nose pressed flat, watching the soldiers below bend into the wind like grass stems.

"Maman, they can't stand up."

"Then you will stay away from that window." She said it gently enough, and drew her daughter back by the collar, and poured the tea that had gone cold anyway. Fifteen years on this coast had taught her the grammar of storms the way it had taught her French and English both: this one spoke with an accent she didn't like. The house seemed to lean east, listening.

She thought of the lighthouse — she thought of it more than she allowed herself to, these days, now that thinking of it meant thinking of the man inside it, and of what he had done with ink and blotting paper for her sake, and of what would happen if anyone ever counted twice. The storm was almost a mercy. A storm gave everyone something honest to fear.

At four o'clock, Private Dunn came to the door with the army's ration allocation and stood dripping on her step, nineteen years old, apologetic as a wet dog. "Orders are you keep indoors tonight, ma'am. There's a blow coming off the point like nothing the sergeant's seen."

"We have seen many," Elise said, and could not keep the small pride from her voice. "But thank you, Private. Will you be warm?"

He looked startled, as though no one had asked him that since his mother. "Warm enough, ma'am."

She gave him a heel of bread spread with dripping because it seemed cruel not to, and when he had gone she stood in the doorway a moment watching the sea climb the horizon in walls. Somewhere out in all of that were ships. There were always ships.

* * *

The collier *Ellen May* broke from the murk at seven minutes past six, and Cyrus saw her before the lookouts did — a black wedge standing out of the grey, her funnel bent at an angle no funnel should take, her decks swept clean. He had the light burning, the great lens turning its slow wheel above him, and he threw his shoulder against the fog signal lever even knowing the wind shredded the sound before it traveled a cable's length.

They saw him anyway. Perhaps they had been steering on the light itself for hours, blind and trusting, the way sailors have always steered on lights they will never meet the keeper of. The ship turned her head seaward, fighting, and Cyrus understood in one glance at the set of her masts that she had lost her steering gear, or nearly, and that the sea was walking her west along the coast like a drunk being shown the door.

Straight onto the Fingers.

He took the stairs three at a time, dropped down the ladder into the watch room, seized the telephone. The line to the garrison had been installed in October; it crackled and went dead and crackled again.

"*Ellen May*, collier, dismasted steering, driving on the rocks south of the point!" he shouted into the mouthpiece. "You have minutes, not hours. Get your men to the water line with lines and lanterns — the breeches buoy will never be rigged in time. Boat launch is useless. You'll need hands on the rocks and you'll need them NOW."

A pause. Then Captain Bell's voice, level and clear even through the wire: "Understood, Mr. Thackeray. We are coming down."

The generator room below the tower held the army's new dynamo, which fed the electric searchlight the military had mounted beside his lamp — their light, bright and graceless, that swept the sea in arrogant circles where his turned with patience. And then, at half past six, at exactly the wrong moment, it made a noise like a struck bell and died.

Darkness took the headland whole.

* * *

In the storeroom that had once been his home, lit by one guttering oil lamp, Cyrus heard the generators fail and heard, a heartbeat later, the sound he had been dreading since noon: timber meeting rock, a long tearing crunch carried over the wind like furniture breaking in a house one street away.

He moved. There was no thought in it, only thirty years of ritual converted instantly to motion — storm shutters, hand lanterns, the old signal flags from the chest under the stairs, and above all the paraffin. Eight cases of it stacked in the oil store, army-sealed but his by every right of habit. If the electric eye of the place was dead, he would give them flame instead.

He got the emergency lamp lit in the gallery in four minutes — a task he had drilled himself on in peacetime out of boredom and now blessed himself for — a fixed red light, steady, marking the point. It was not enough. A fixed red said *here is danger*. What the *Ellen May*'s boats needed was something to steer *for*.

He was dragging the third case of paraffin toward the winch platform when hands took the other end of the case, and he looked up into Elise Marchand's face, rain streaming off her bonnet, her eyes furious with fear and purpose in equal measure.

"I told you to stay inside."

"You told Colette to stay inside. She is inside. I am here. Tell me where."

For one absurd second the storm vanished for him entirely. Then the ship screamed again on the rocks, and he pointed. "The flares. In the iron box. Strike them and hold them high and do not let the wind take them. Can your hands stand it?"

"My hands raised you a barn once in a gale. Hold your light, Cyrus."

* * *

What happened on the Fingers between seven o'clock and midnight, no single account ever held entire. The collier had broken her back on the outermost rock, and her eleven men — eleven, they counted afterward, the twelfth having been washed off with the deckhouse in the first impact — had gotten into two boats that the sea wanted to kill them for using.

Private Dunn, who survived the night and later told the story to anyone who would listen for the rest of his life, swore the flares came first: a woman on the gallery platform holding red fire above her head in the teeth of the wind, arm steady as a fence post, so that each boat had a mark to run for. Then the keeper's light, sweeping low along the water where no chart showed light had any business being — Thackeray up in the tower with the great lens unclutched from its clockwork, hauling the apparatus around by hand, throwing the beam deliberately onto the calmer water behind the innermost rock. Making, with two thousand tons of glass and brass, a corridor.

"He aimed it," Dunn said, telling it years later. "That's what nobody believed. He aimed the light like a lantern in a doorway and he walked them in."

Captain Bell stood waist-deep in the surf with a line around his chest, taking men off the second boat as it broke. He did not know until morning that the first man he pulled gasping from the water had been hauled the last ten yards by Thackeray himself, who had come down from the tower leaving his light unmanned for ninety seconds because a human hand reached for a human hand and no keeper alive could refuse it.

Eleven men came ashore. Not one was lost. At some hour of the dark, the army's generators coughed back to life and the electric searchlight blazed up uselessly over an empty sea, and everyone on the rocks ignored it completely.

* * *

Near dawn the wind dropped as suddenly as it had risen, and the storm walked away eastward trailing rain. The survivors lay in blankets in the barracks, and the dead deckhouse lay strewn along the tide line, and Elise sat on the gallery steps with her hands burned pink from flare-paint and phosphorus, shaking now that it was permitted to shake.

Cyrus came down the tower stair and sat beside her without speaking. Below, soldiers moved among the wreckage with lanterns, salvaging, and their voices were different this morning — quieter, and directed upward at the lighthouse more than once.

"You held the light," she said at last. "All night. With your arms."

"The machinery does it usually." He flexed his fingers, and she saw they would not work properly for days. "It seemed a poor night to trust machinery."

A sound escaped her that was half laugh, half sob. Fifteen years she had known this man, and he made jokes the way other men bled — rarely, and only when gravely wounded.

"Cyrus." She looked out at the wreck-strewn water rather than at him, because looking at him had become dangerous in ways unrelated to storms and ledgers. "What you did for us. On paper. I never asked. I never thanked—"

"Don't." His voice was very quiet. "There is nothing on paper. There is only you and Colette, whom I promised Henri, and whom I'd have promised the storm itself if it asked nicely."

Overhead the great lens turned its slow wheel, wound down now, ticking as it cooled. Eleven men slept below who owed their breath to paraffin and stubbornness. For one morning the war receded, and the point belonged to its keeper again.

Then Private Dunn appeared at the foot of the stair, saluting awkwardly, rain-soaked and reverent. "Beg pardon, Mr. Thackeray. Captain Bell's compliments. He asks — he says to tell you the men would count it an honor if you'd take breakfast with the garrison, sir. And ma'am." A pause; the boy shifted. "And the Captain says further he'd like a word after. About the records, sir. Says there's no hurry. Says he'll come round for tea tomorrow instead."

The wind had gone out of the world, and in the enormous quiet that followed, Cyrus Thackeray looked at Elise, and Elise looked at Cyrus, and neither of them said what both were thinking: that no officer requested tea without already knowing what he intended to find.
