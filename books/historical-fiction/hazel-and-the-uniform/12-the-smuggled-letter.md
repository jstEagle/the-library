## 12. The Smuggled Letter

The letter came on a Thursday, which Cyrus Thackeray would remember afterward because Thursdays were the days he wound the clockwork that turned the lens, and he had been kneeling inside the machine with an oil can in his hand when the knock came — three raps, soft, almost apologetic, the way Private Dunn knocked at everything as though the war might be hiding behind each door and would rather not be disturbed.

"Post for you, sir. Well — post for the station, technically." Dunn held out the envelope with both hands, the way one might return a wounded bird. "It's been through the censor's office. They opened it. I expect you'll want to know they read it."

"I'd assumed they had," Cyrus said. The envelope bore the marks: a slit along the top edge done cleanly with a blade, a stamp in faded violet — *PASSED BY CENSOR* — and beneath that, in smaller type, the number of some office in some distant town where a man or woman of ordinary conscience sat all day reading other people's words for treason.

He waited until Dunn's boots had faded down the iron stair before he broke the seal. His hands were still greasy from the clockwork; he left two faint thumbprints on the paper, and he would think later that this was fitting — that a letter about Henri Marchand should carry his fingerprints on it, evidence of handling, of contact, of a life that had touched another life and left its mark.

The handwriting was unfamiliar. Small, upright, careful — a scholar's hand, cramped by habit into the margins. *Reverend Alistair Penhale, Chaplain to the Forces (Retired), St. Jude's Vicarage, Truro.* Cyrus did not know the name. For one foolish moment, standing in the cold store room that had lately become his bedroom, he thought it might be a summons, a trap, Bell's polite fingers reaching for him through the post. Then he saw the second line, and everything else in the room went quiet.

*I write regarding Captain Henri Marchand, late of the 2nd Battalion, whose family I understand still resides at Brindle Point.*

Cyrus sat down on the crate that served him for a chair and read it twice, then a third time, slowly, the way one drinks something warm on a freezing day — deliberately, so as not to waste a drop.

Henri had written to the chaplain in the spring of 1914. That much Cyrus had never known. It was there in the chaplain's account: a letter from a Frenchman living in Cornwall, asking — in English apologised over twice for its accent — whether his old commanding officer could be induced to write down what Henri had done in '70 and again in the reserve musters after, because, the chaplain quoted, *my wife is French and my daughter was born here, and I find I want the paper of my life to be in order, in case the paper should ever need to speak for them.*

In case the paper should ever need to speak for them.

Cyrus set the letter down on his knee. Below the storeroom, the sea worked at the rocks with its patient hammers. Above, in the lamp room, the great lens stood idle in the grey afternoon light, waiting for dark the way it had waited every dark of his life, patient as a held breath.

He read on.

The chaplain, it seemed, had not answered Henri's letter until it was too late — the reply returned marked *deceased*, and the matter had lain on his conscience since. But the inquiry itself had gone forward. There existed, Reverend Penhale wrote, a service record for Henri Marchand in the regimental archive at Exeter: commissions, commendations, the Croix de guerre with star from the earlier war, twenty-two years' cumulative service between the French army and the British auxiliary transport corps in which Henri had served during the first winter of this one, ferrying munitions from the quay at Brindle Point before his heart failed him. *A record of honourable and distinguished service,* the chaplain wrote, *such as would reflect credit upon any man and confer consideration upon any widow.*

But — and here the small upright hand grew smaller, tighter, pressed hard into the page as though the writer were leaning his whole body against the sentence — *the file is misfiled. Some clerk's error of years past has lodged it under a variant spelling, Marchant, and the registry index does not show it. I have petitioned the archive formally. They answer that such corrections take time. In wartime, reverend though I am, I know what is meant by that. Time is the one commodity the war does not issue.*

Cyrus looked up. The single window of the storeroom framed a slice of grey sea and a strip of sky the colour of dishwater, and pinned against the sky, the headland guns that the garrison had emplaced in October, their barrels shrouded against salt air. Somewhere beyond the ridge, in the requisitioned schoolhouse, Elise would be getting Colette's supper. He pictured her hands — quick, capable hands that had learned gutting fish in this harbour and had once, in another country, learned to bind a soldier's wounds — moving through the small rituals of the evening meal. He pictured her face when the soldiers in the lane stopped talking as she passed. He had watched that happen now too many times. The silence that followed her down the street had a shape, and the shape was a door closing.

The tribunal had a date now. Everyone on the point knew it, the way everyone knows the tide. Monday. The requisitioned customs house, ten o'clock. Three officers and a stipendiary magistrate flown in from Plymouth, empowered to examine the register of enemy aliens and order removals to the internment camp at Knockaloe or, in cases argued worthy, grant exemptions. Elise's name stood on the registry. Not Elise Marchand — the ledger Cyrus had surrendered no longer contained that name, and he had lived every day since with the falsification folded under his ribs like a broken bone — but the central registry in London, compiled months before from parish returns, held her true. Bell would have it. Of course Bell would have it. Bell noticed ink inconsistencies in ledgers; Bell noticed everything, filed it away with those pale courteous eyes, and said nothing, which was worse than saying anything at all.

And if she stood before the board as an undocumented enemy alien, a French widow with no proof of anything but her foreignness, the board would do what boards do. They would remove her. And Colette — Colette would go into some home, some institution with a charitable name, and Cyrus would stand his watch over a coast emptied of the only family he had left, keeping a light for strangers.

He read the letter a fourth time. Then he did what he had trained himself to do in thirty-one years of tending a light: he took the thing apart to see how it worked.

The record existed. Good. Records could be copied, certified, carried. The chaplain had power of a sort — a clergyman's letterhead, a colonel's old friendship — but the archive had already answered him with the war's universal solvent: *wait.* The tribunal was Monday. Today was Thursday. Four days, minus whatever the post took, and the post took what it liked now, censored and delayed and rerouted through offices of men with violet stamps.

No. The letter alone would not do it. A chaplain's testimony, however honourable, was hearsay to a tribunal. What they needed was the record itself, or a certified extract of it, under seal, in his hand, by Sunday night.

Cyrus rose and paced the storeroom — six steps, turn, six steps. The room still smelled of hemp and tar from its former life; his cot was made with military squareness because the soldiers who shared the tower below inspected everything, jokingly, the way boys inspect, and he had found that order was a language even occupation understood.

Options. He laid them out like tools on a bench.

First: reply to Penhale, beg him to travel to Exeter himself, extract in hand, onward to Brindle Point. Distance: Truro to Exeter, forty miles and two railways; Exeter to here, a full day's journey in peacetime, longer now with troop movements fouling the timetables. Could a retired chaplain reach Exeter, argue a misfiled record out of a wartime archive, obtain certification, and arrive by Sunday? Men had done more improbable things. Men had also died waiting for less improbable things.

Second: go himself. Cyrus closed his eyes and saw the impossibility plainly. The lighthouse was requisitioned; keepers did not leave their posts without relief, and no relief would come for a personal errand. If he applied for leave, the application would cross Captain Bell's desk, and Bell would ask why, in that soft voice, and Cyrus would have to lie to a man who audited lies for a living. Worse — the leave itself would put him away from the point on the very days before the tribunal, a fact that would look, to a meticulous mind, exactly like what it was.

Third: someone else goes. He stopped pacing. The window showed him the harbour road, and beyond the harbour wall, the cottage with the blue door where Elise had lived fifteen years, where Henri had died with his hand in Cyrus's grip. Who? The vicar, old Mr. Tresidder, was gentle and useless and terrified of the military government. The fishermen would do a great deal for Elise Marchand in their hearts and nothing at all in the open, having learned what happened to men who were helpful to registered aliens.

Then his eye fell on the letter again, and he laughed aloud — a short, rusty sound, startling in the empty room — because the answer had been sitting in his hand. Not a person to send. A person to summon. Penhale had written *I understand the family still resides at Brindle Point.* Someone had told him so. Someone had written to the chaplain after Henri's death, prompting this conscience-stricken search. Who?

He read the opening paragraphs again, more carefully, and found it in the third paragraph: *Your late husband's commanding officer, Colonel E. Vane-Rossiter, whom I had occasion to write last month regarding another matter, mentioned your residence.*

Vane-Rossiter. The colonel. Cyrus knew the name — had seen it once, years ago, at the bottom of a letter Henri kept folded in the drawer of his workbench among his tools, a letter Henri would take out sometimes on Sunday evenings and read silently with his coffee, and once, only once, had explained: *He is the man who made me an English sergeant when France was too far away to ask permission. A good man. Old-fashioned. The kind who believes a promise is a physical object.*

A promise is a physical object. Cyrus looked at his own hands, at the oil stains, at the thumbprints drying on the chaplain's letter. Henri had believed that. Had built his last request out of that belief. And now the paper of Henri's life — the physical object of his service, his honour, his right to consideration — lay misfiled in an Exeter archive under a clerk's misspelling, and the only man who believed promises were solid enough to move such mountains was a colonel somewhere in England, old-fashioned, presumably aging, who had once made a foreign fisherman into an English sergeant because it was right.

Cyrus sat down and pulled his own writing things toward him — a board across the crate, ink frozen half the winter, a pen he kept for the logbook. He began the reply to Penhale first, because courtesy cost nothing and allies were made of it: gratitude, the news of Elise's circumstances stated with terrible restraint, the date of the tribunal set down plainly. *If any means exist to place a certified copy of the record before the board by Sunday evening, you will save more than one life, though the law will only thank you for one.*

Then he took a second sheet, and hesitated over it.

What he wrote next would be seen. Everything was seen now; the censor had opened this very envelope with a blade and passed it with a stamp. Whatever he wrote to Vane-Rossiter would pass under the same violet light. He could not say: a friend's widow is about to be interned and I have falsified the parish register to hide her and I need you to dig her husband's record out of a filing error before Monday or all is lost. He could not even say most of that to himself yet.

He wrote instead:

*Sir — I have the honour to write concerning Sergeant Henri Marchand, whom you were so good as to attest into the auxiliary transport corps in the winter of 1914. I was his friend and neighbour these fifteen years, and keeper of the Brindle Point light, and I held his hand when he died in March of that year. His widow and daughter remain here. I am informed by the Reverend Penhale that the sergeant's service record exists but is misplaced in the registry under a variant spelling. Mrs. Marchand is a French national resident on a defended coast, and matters touching her status come before the authorities imminently. I do not presume to instruct you, sir; I only believe that a man who once turned a good sailor into a sergeant with a stroke of his pen might wish to know what that stroke has since amounted to, and what it might yet amount to. Respectfully, Cyrus Thackeray, Keeper, Brindle Point.*

He read it over. It said everything and admitted nothing, which he supposed was the new grammar of the age. Every honest sentence now had to be constructed like a smuggler's hull — clean lines above, concealed space below.

He sealed both letters. Then he sat a long while with them in his hands, weighing them, because he had learned that everything weighed something now. The letters weighed perhaps an ounce together. They also weighed his post, his freedom, possibly Elise's safety — for if the tribunal heard that the keeper of the light had been soliciting military records on behalf of an internee, the inference available to a mind like Bell's did not bear thinking of. And yet to do nothing was also a weight, and it was heavier. He had learned that much in the night hours with the ledger open before him: that neutrality in the presence of harm is not neutral; it is a choice with a name, and the name is cowardice.

He would post them himself, at the garrison post office, in plain sight, signed for. Nothing hidden was safe; let the letters be the most examined paper on the coast.

* * *

Saturday morning came up wild, a scythe of wind off the Atlantic with rain in it, and Cyrus walked to the village with the letters inside his coat, over his heart, like contraband after all despite his resolutions. The garrison post office occupied the old fish-curing shed, and the queue outside it was long and quiet — soldiers mostly, and a few women with telegraph forms, faces he had known all his life now arranged into the careful blankness the times required.

Private Dunn was sorting sacks inside and waved him to the front, and Cyrus signed the register, and it was while he was writing his name — the pen scratching, the wind moaning in the eaves of the shed — that he felt the particular silence fall that he had learned to dread. Conversation behind him did not stop so much as lower itself, the way water lowers before a swell.

Captain Bell stood in the doorway.

He was not in uniform quite — he wore the mackintosh and cap of a man caught out in weather, and he held a newspaper folded to keep the rain off, and he looked, as always, entirely reasonable, entirely unhurried, a man to whom urgency was a vulgar habit of lesser minds.

"Keeper Thackeray." Bell's eyes went to the register, to the two envelopes Dunn was weighing. "Correspondence?"

"Personal letters, Captain. To clergymen and an old officer, regarding a late friend."

"Regarding—" Bell's eyebrows invited completion.

"Henri Marchand," Cyrus said, and gave the name to him openly, deliberately, because he had decided on the walk down that concealment was now worth less than the appearance of candour. "His service record. It seems it exists, but has been misfiled. I'm attempting to have it recovered. For his widow's sake."

Something moved behind Bell's pale eyes — not surprise; Cyrus doubted anything surprised the man — but interest, recalculation, the click of a piece dropping into a new position on a board.

"The widow Marchand," Bell said. "Whose name appears— " and there he paused, precisely, delicately, on the edge of a sentence Cyrus knew he could finish either way, "—on certain registries."

"On some," Cyrus said. "Not, I believe, on the parish ledger you hold."

"No," Bell agreed softly. "Curiously, it does not." He unfolded his newspaper, shook it out against the wind, and Cyrus understood the gesture: *I am not detaining you; I am merely present, which is worse.* "I hope your letters reach their destinations, Keeper. Correspondence can be slow in wartime. Tribunals, on the other hand—" he glanced at the register, at the date column, and Cyrus watched him do the arithmetic, Monday, three days, "—tribunals are remarkably punctual."

He inclined his head and went out into the rain, and the newspaper tilted over his shoulders like a wing, and the queue breathed again behind Cyrus, and Dunn, sorting sacks with studied deafness, said nothing at all.

Cyrus walked back up the harbour road with the wind shouldering him sideways and the rain needling his face, and he thought about hope, which he had always mistrusted as an emotion fit for churches and not for coasts. Hope with no deadline was faith. Hope with a deadline was a fuse. Somewhere in England, an old colonel might or might not still be alive, might or might not remember a French sergeant, might or might not exert himself for a dead man's family against the inert mass of a wartime registry. And somewhere between those possibilities ran a wire burning steadily toward Monday, ten o'clock, the customs house, three officers and a magistrate.

That evening he climbed to the lamp room early. Elise was there — she came now most evenings, ostensibly to help prepare the apparatus, actually because the lamp room was the one place on the point where the army's eyes did not easily follow, and they had long ago stopped pretending to themselves why her presence steadied his hands. She was polishing the brass of the backup lamp, sleeves rolled, hair pinned up crooked, and the low sun came through the storm glass and laid a band of gold across her wrists.

"A letter came," he said. "From Henri's chaplain. There's a record, Elise. Twenty-two years of service. Commendations. The Croix de guerre. It exists — it's real — it's only misfiled. If it can be got out of Exeter and certified before Monday, no board in England can intern the widow of a man with that record."

She had gone very still, the cloth stopped in her hand. He watched her not hope — watched her refuse it, the way one refuses to look at a swell that might be the ninth wave or just the sea breathing. "And if it cannot be got out in time?"

"Then we do what we've already been doing," Cyrus said. "We keep the light turning, and we lie to the empire, and I go where you go."

"Cyrus." His name in her mouth, rare, deliberate. "They will not let you go anywhere. You are the light."

"Then the light goes where you go," he said, "because I am not the light. I never was. I'm only the man who tends it."

Below them the wind rose a note, testing the glass, and far out under the bruised horizon the third great storm of the year was gathering itself like a fist, and neither of them knew yet — though the barometer in the passage had begun, very softly, to fall — how soon it would come, or what it would demand, or that it would arrive not on Monday with the tribunal but sooner, on Sunday night, with the whole garrison awake and the wires down and the sea coming for the point like something owed.
