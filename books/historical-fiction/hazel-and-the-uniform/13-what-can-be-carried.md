## 13. What Can Be Carried

The notices went up on Thursday, pasted to the board outside the chandlery with flour paste that had not yet dried. Elise saw them from across the street, four white rectangles in a row like teeth, and she stood on the cobbles with her basket on her arm and did not cross.

She had known they were coming. Captain Bell's visit had told her that much — the careful questions, the notebook he never quite opened, the way his eyes had gone around her kitchen and counted things. And Colette had come home from the well with news that traveled faster than any official paper: that soldiers had posted lists at the harbor office too, and that Mrs. Penhale had read them aloud to half the town before remembering whose name was at the bottom of the second column.

*All enemy aliens resident within the defended zone will present themselves for registration at the drill hall, Saturday next, by ten o'clock. Those failing to register will be liable to internment. Internment orders will follow for all persons so registered.*

Enemy aliens. The words sat strangely against everything she knew of herself. Fifteen years she had lived at Brindle Point. She had learned English here, learned the smell of bladderwrack at low tide and the sound the wind made when it came down the chimney flue of the Marchand cottage — a low note, almost musical, that Henri had said was the house singing. She had buried a husband in the churchyard above the strand, under a stone she could see from her kitchen window if she stood in exactly one place, which she did most mornings without thinking about it.

And now there was a list with her name on it, and flour paste drying on the chandlery board, and she was an alien on the ground where her daughter had been born.

She crossed the street after all. It seemed important to do it in daylight, unhurried, as though the notice were nothing but weather. Her boots sounded loud on the cobbles. She read the whole thing through once, then again, because reading it twice made it ordinary, and ordinary things could be survived. The ink had smeared slightly where someone's thumb had pressed it up wet. Below her own name — *Marchand, Elise, née—* no, they hadn't troubled themselves with her maiden name; only *widow, of Brindle Point, French national* — below her name was old Mr. Kessler's, the German clockmaker from Trelow who had repaired Cyrus Thackeray's chronometers for twenty years, and who had not left his shop since the war began except to buy bread.

Elise looked up from the board and found the street looking back at her.

It was not hostility. That was the thing she would try, in later years, to explain, and never quite manage. Nobody spat. Nobody crossed to the far side. But faces turned away — quickly, politely, the way one turns from a person weeping in church — and conversations resumed a half-beat too loudly, and Mrs. Tregowan, who had sold her mackerel every Friday since Colette was in arms, suddenly found urgent business at the back of her cart. A boy she knew by name — Tommy Chenoweth, who had once broken her window with a cricket ball and been forgiven with cake — pulled his cap down and walked very fast, looking at his boots.

Fifteen years. It took perhaps ninety seconds for a street to empty itself of her.

She gathered her basket and walked home along the cliff path rather than through the village, because the path was hers in a way the street no longer was. The gorse was in its winter color, dark and salt-scoured, and the sea below ran in long grey ridges with white combed off their backs. The wind smelled of iron. Another storm gathering out west, over the horizon's shoulder — she had learned to read storms the way Henri had taught her to read tide tables, by feel more than figures, and this one would be two days coming, maybe three. Big. She could tell by how far away it already felt.

At the top of the rise she stopped and looked back at Brindle Point, at the toy roofs and the smoke standing straight up out of chimneys in the still air before weather, and at the lighthouse beyond, white tower, lantern burning its pale daytime flame. Somewhere inside that tower or the cottage beside it was a man who had blotted a line out of a book for her sake. She did not know it for certain. She suspected it the way one suspects a wound under clothing — by the way he held himself, by what he did not say. He had asked her nothing when the registry went up. She had asked him nothing either. Between them lay the promise, and the promise had its own language, and in that language silence meant: *I am doing something, and you must not know what, because knowing would endanger you.*

She stood there until the cold found her hands, and then she went home to pack.

* * *

The case was under the bed, Henri's old one, canvas with leather straps, still smelling faintly of the pipe tobacco he had carried in it on their crossing from Saint-Malo. She set it on the kitchen table and opened it flat and then stood looking at it for a long time.

This was the difficulty: not deciding what to take, but deciding what kind of person the taking would say she was.

Cyrus had a philosophy of measure. She had absorbed it over years of borrowed cups of sugar and returned plates of scones, over the slow accumulation of neighborliness that had become, after Henri died, something closer than neighborliness. *You carry what can be carried,* Cyrus said. It was a keeper's creed. In a lighthouse storeroom everything had its weight logged, its place, its purpose; nothing was kept for sentiment that could not also keep you alive. Oil, wicks, matches, fresh water, the emergency clockwork for the lens. When she had once asked him why he owned so little, he had looked surprised. *Everything I've kept has earned its place,* he'd said. *Same as a crew.*

She thought of that now, standing over the empty case, and understood that packing for internment was really a question of what she believed about herself. If she packed photographs, she was a woman preserving a past. If she packed tools, she was a woman expecting a future. If she packed needle and thread and spare stockings, she was simply practical, and practicality was a kind of armor, and armor was what she intended to wear into that drill hall on Saturday morning.

She began with the essentials because they were easiest. Two dresses, the wool ones, and the grey coat with the good lining. Stockings, three pairs. The small sewing kit that had crossed the Atlantic in 1901 and had never once failed her — needlecase of bone, threads wound on cardboard scraps. Her brush and comb. The Bible, Henri's, with the births written inside the cover in his careful schoolmaster's hand: his own, hers, and beneath them, in fresher ink, *Colette, born at Brindle Point, 4th of June, 1908, in a gale.* He had been proud of the gale.

Then the harder decisions.

Henri's letters — the bundle tied in string that she had reread so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth. She had hidden them in the flour bin when Captain Bell came to tea, and the hiding had told her something: that papers were dangerous now, that even love letters could be evidence. But she could not leave them. Whatever happened at the drill hall, whatever room they put her in at the end of it, she wanted those pages near, because Henri's handwriting was the last place his voice still lived. She wrapped them in oilcloth and laid them flat beneath the dresses, where a searching hand might mistake them for nothing.

The photograph of Henri in his uniform — taken the autumn before the war, when he had marched in the town's volunteer parade wearing a jacket borrowed from the fishmonger's son, laughing, because everyone had known it was theater then. It was not theater anymore. She put the photograph face-down between the Bible's pages, at Second Corinthians, because Henri had liked that chapter, *for we walk by faith, not by sight.* She was not certain she believed anything just now, but she believed in carrying him.

Colette's things she packed last, and here her measure broke down entirely. A doll went in, and came out, and went in again. A picture book. The red mittens. Extra socks. A tin of barley sugar against fear — Colette chewed barley sugar the way other children bit their nails, and Elise had learned that the sweet clicking of it in her daughter's teeth could hold terror at bay for whole minutes at a time. By the end the case would not close, and Elise knelt on the floor taking things back out, one at a time, testing each against the question: *can this be carried?*

What she finally removed was her own. A second dress. Her good shawl. The silver hairpins that had been her mother's, which she hesitated over longest of all, and then sewed into the hem of Colette's coat instead — not carried, but carried differently, smuggled forward inside the person she loved most.

When the straps buckled at last, the case weighed almost nothing. That frightened her more than the heavy version had. A life reduced to eleven pounds.

* * *

Colette came home at four with windburned cheeks and the particular set of her shoulders that meant she had heard something and was deciding whether to say it. She was eight going on forty, Henri's child through and through — he had talked to her as to an equal from the cradle, and the habit had made her serious in a way that both charmed and broke Elise's heart.

They ate supper of fish stew and brown bread, and Colette pushed her potatoes around and finally said, to the table rather than to her mother:

"Mrs. Penhale says we'll have to go away."

"Mrs. Penhale says many things."

"She said there's a camp." Colette's spoon stopped. "On an island. Where they send people who came from France. She said it gently, though." This last was offered as if gentleness, wherever encountered, ought to be credited. "She cried a little. After."

Elise set down her fork. Through the window behind her daughter, the lighthouse beam had begun its turn in the thickening dusk, sweeping the fields, sweeping the sea, sweeping the fields again. She watched it pass and pass.

"What did you say to Mrs. Penhale?"

"Nothing. Tommy Chenoweth asked me if I was a spy, and I said spies don't make barley sugar, and then Mrs. Penhale sent him home." Colette considered. "Mama. Is Papa coming back?"

There it was. The question arrived the way it always did — sideways, attached to some smaller matter, surfacing at supper or bedtime when the day's defenses were down. Elise had answered it many ways in the two years since the burial. Once she had answered with anger and regretted it for months. Mostly she answered with truth shaped small enough for a child to hold.

"No, my darling. Papa died. You remember."

"I remember. But Mrs. Penhale said people come back from France." Colette's voice had gone very quiet, and Elise understood suddenly that this was not confusion but logic, ruthless child-logic: France took Papa; therefore France might give him back; therefore if Mama is sent to France—

"I'm not going to France," Elise said carefully. "I don't think I will be. There are people working to be sure of it."

"Mr. Thackeray?"

The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Elise heard it fall all the way down.

"Why do you ask about Mr. Thackeray?"

Colette shrugged, eight years old again, guilty and unrepentant. "He fixed my boot. Last week. He didn't want anyone to see, so he did it behind the boat shed. And he gave me barley sugar." A pause. "He always knows when we need barley sugar."

Elise rose and began clearing the dishes, though the table was barely touched, because her hands required occupation or they would have shown something. Behind her, the light swept its patient circles. *He keeps us safe* — Colette had said something of the kind to Captain Bell, Elise remembered now with a chill that had nothing to do with the weather, that terrible innocent sentence offered across the tea table while Bell's pencil hovered. She had thought little of it then. She thought of it constantly now.

"Listen to me, Colette." She knelt beside her daughter's chair so their eyes were level. "Saturday morning we're going to walk to the drill hall, you and I, and answer some questions, and then we are coming home. Do you understand? Whatever happens after, whatever anyone says — home is here. Home is me. As long as you are with me, you are home, and I will not let anything change that. Not lists. Not camps. Not Mrs. Penhale, God love her, crying into her herring nets."

Colette studied her mother's face the way she studied tide pools, thoroughly and without blinking. Then she nodded once, satisfied, the way children are satisfied by vows adults know better than to make.

But Elise made it anyway. She kissed the windburned cheek, sent her to wash, and stood alone at the window with the case waiting on the chair like a guest who had arrived early.

* * *

Later, when Colette slept, Elise took out the case and repacked it. Not because the contents were wrong, but because her hands needed work and her mind needed the ritual. Measure, order, weight logged and place assigned — a keeper's discipline, borrowed from a man who had probably not slept properly himself in weeks. She folded each garment into a rectangle. She placed the letters where her hands would find them first upon opening, before anything else, in case opening the case ever meant arriving somewhere exhausted and afraid.

Near midnight she noticed the lamp in the tower was behaving oddly.

She had watched that light for fifteen years, first as a curiosity and then as Henri's neighbor's great machine and then, after the requisition, as something she checked nightly the way one checks a sleeping child's breathing. She knew its rhythm the way she knew her own pulse. Tonight it stuttered. Not failed — stuttered, a hesitation in the sweep, twice, three times, then steady again. Wind, perhaps, shaking the tower. Or machinery. Or —

Or a signal. The thought arrived fully formed, absurd and undeniable at once. Cyrus Thackeray did nothing by accident; she had known him twelve years and had never once seen him misplace a tool or waste a word. If his light faltered tonight, on this night, with Saturday two days off and the notices' paste barely dry —

She put on her coat and boots and stepped out into the dark.

The wind had begun to arrive ahead of the storm, long preliminary gusts that smelled of far-off rain, and the grass on the headland bent and hissed. The lighthouse door, when she reached it, stood unlocked. That alone told her everything. Cyrus Thackeray had locked that door every night of his life since the soldiers came; she had seen him check it twice.

Inside, the stairway spiraled up into darkness, and from somewhere above came the small sounds of a man working — metal, a wrench, the mutter of breath. She climbed. At the top, framed in the lamplight of the great lens, Cyrus stood with an oil can in his hand and guilt all over his face, and beside the mechanism, deliberately placed where she could not fail to see it, lay a folded paper with her name on the outside in his square keeper's hand.

"He asked me to tell you," Cyrus said quietly, "that the tribunal sits Monday. And that he's found where the record is misfiled." He swallowed. "The chaplain wrote back, Elise. Henri's service is in writing. We can prove it — before Monday, if the mail holds, if the roads hold, if—" He stopped himself, the way he always did, at the edge of promises.

Elise picked up the letter. Her hands, which had shaken all day over a packing case, were perfectly steady now.

Outside, the storm's first real gust struck the glass, and the great lens turned, and turned, and did not hesitate at all.
