## 14. The Missing Page

Captain Edmund Bell had made a career of small discrepancies.

In the mapping office at Aldershot, before the war, he had been the man they gave the impossible charts to — coastlines surveyed twice by different hands, soundings that contradicted one another by a fathom, place-names spelled three ways on a single sheet. Anyone could find a large error. A large error announced itself. But a coastline that was wrong in only one place, in only one line of ink, wrong in a way that would never be noticed unless you compared it against itself — that required patience. That required a man willing to sit with a document for six hours while the rain came down and the garrison's generators thumped below him like an uneasy heart.

He had found it on a Tuesday.

The Brindle Point parish ledger was not a beautiful thing. It was bound in salt-stiffened leather, its corners worn round as beach stones, and it smelled of lamp oil and damp and the particular mustiness of paper that had spent forty years within sight of the sea. Bell had requisitioned it along with everything else — the harbor master's logs, the rate books, the school register, the baptismal rolls — because Captain Hartley above him wanted *everything*, and Hartley's superior wanted everything catalogued, and somewhere far above them all sat men who believed a war could be won by filing cabinets if only the cabinets were full enough.

Bell did not believe this. But he was fifty-one years old, too old for France, too meticulous to be idle, and so he had become the army's memory at the edge of England: the man who knew what every boat in Brindle Point harbor was licensed to carry, how many pounds of tea Mrs. Penhale had bought in March, and which houses on the seaward lane had chimneys that smoked in wet weather, because the smoke showed on his aerial photographs and he had made a note of it out of pure habit.

He had gone through the ledger the way he went through everything — name by name, cross-referencing against the registry of enemy aliens, the ration allocations, the militia exemption lists. It was dull work, and he was suited to dull work, and he was honest enough with himself to admit there was comfort in it. Names were simpler than people. Thackeray, Cyrus, keeper, b. 1871. Marchand, Henri, fisherman-naturalized '98, d. 1914. Marchand, Elise, widow—

He stopped.

He had stopped, that first Tuesday, and set down his pen, and looked out the fogged window of the requisitioned customs house at the grey water, and thought about nothing at all for the better part of four minutes. Then he had come back to the page.

The entry read: *Marchand, Elise, widow of the above, French-born, resident Seawatch Cottage. Internment status: pending.*

It read correctly. It was entered in the correct hand — the same careful, upright script that filled the rest of the ledger, a hand Bell had already traced back through twenty years of records to a younger Cyrus Thackeray writing tidier versions of the same letters. The date was correct. The ink appeared correct.

That was what troubled him. He held the ledger's spine up to the window light, angling the page, and there it was — faintly, the way a submerged rock shows only when the tide is right. Beneath *Marchand, Elise* there was a ghost. Another word, blotted out with a skill that almost succeeded: not scratched, not scraped, but blotted, blotted while the ink was still wet and then written over with a steadiness that most men could not have managed with dry paper. And beneath the overwriting, when the light came through at the proper angle, the shadow of a different name entirely. Shorter. Older. A woman's name, he suspected, though he could not yet read it whole.

Bell set the ledger down and took off his spectacles and cleaned them very thoroughly on his handkerchief, though they did not need cleaning.

Someone had altered a government record. Someone had done it well — not the work of a panicked amateur but of a man who understood ink, who understood blotting papers and drying sand and the patience to wait until the moment between writing and setting that was narrow as a keyhole. Which meant it had been planned. Which meant whoever had done it had known, days or weeks in advance, that the ledger would be surrendered, and had sat with it open under a lamp and committed his small treason with a steady hand.

There were exactly two men in Brindle Point with access to the parish ledger. One was the vicar, Reverend Prynne, who was seventy-three and signed his name so violently he tore the paper. The other was Cyrus Thackeray.

Bell put the ledger away in the locked cabinet with the rest of the records and did not speak of it to anyone. Not to Lieutenant Dawes, who handled the internment schedules and had a habit of humming when he worked. Not to Sergeant Mowbray, whose enthusiasm for raids and sweeps Bell had already learned to leash twice. And not to Captain Hartley, who would have had Thackeray in irons by Friday and the Marchand woman on a transport by Saturday, and who would then consider the matter closed, because for Hartley matters were always closed once someone was suffering.

Bell wrote a single note in his private book — *L. folio 212, ink inconsistency, source TBD* — and closed the book, and thought about the sea.

The trouble with knowing things, Bell reflected, was that knowledge behaved like water in a bilge. It collected in the lowest places first, and it corroded quietly, and eventually you either pumped it out or it sank you.

For nine days he pumped nothing. He watched instead.

He watched Thackeray keep the light through the second storm, when the collier went onto the rocks and eleven men came off her alive because one civilian keeper had run a lantern up the tower stair by hand when the military generators drowned. Bell had stood in the rain on the headland and watched the beam sweep and sweep and sweep, and had felt something in his chest that was not quite admiration and not quite dread, because he understood by then that he was watching a man who would not stop, and men who did not stop were either heroes or problems, and often both.

He watched the Marchand woman walk the village road with her head level and her basket on her arm, past neighbors who studied their boots, past the notice board where the internment orders were posted, and he thought she carried herself like the wives of officers he had known — the ones who packed their husbands off to the front and then ran the county hospitals without complaint. Dignity, he had learned long ago, was not evidence of innocence. Guilty people could be dignified. Innocent people could be shifty. That was precisely why tribunals existed and precisely why they failed.

And he watched, on the ninth day, the way Thackeray crossed from the lighthouse to Seawatch Cottage at dusk with a covered dish, and the way the door opened before he knocked — opened before he knocked, which meant she had seen him coming, which meant she was watching for him, which meant all of it, the whole quiet architecture of it, was exactly what Bell suspected.

That was the evening Bell decided to pump.

*

He chose the tower itself, because it was the one place in Brindle Point where no soldier lingered without purpose. The lamp room above, the storerooms below, and between them the winding stair, iron and stone, where the wind made the high slit windows moan like a choir warming up. At seven in the evening the great lens was turning overhead with its soft whale-song of gearing, and the sea beyond the glass was running black and silver under a rising moon.

Bell climbed. He had sent no word ahead; a man who announced these conversations got a prepared version of the person he came to see. He found Thackeray in the watch room, halfway up the tower, in the small circular chamber where the chart table stood and the logbook lay open and a kettle sat on a spirit stove filling the room with steam that clung to the cold glass.

Thackeray looked up from the log. He was taller than Bell remembered from the requisition day, or perhaps he only stood straighter in his own domain. His sleeves were rolled to the forearm; there was oil on one wrist and brass polish on the other, and his eyes went to Bell's face with a quickness that told Bell he had already guessed the visit was not social.

"Captain Bell." No offer of a hand. "I'd ask if you've come about the light, but the light is burning."

"I can see that from the end of the world," Bell said. "May I sit?"

"There's one chair."

"One is sufficient. I'm only half a visitor."

He sat. The chair was wooden and salt-swollen and more comfortable than any chair at headquarters. Thackeray poured tea without being asked — two cups, not one, and Bell filed that away as well: the man kept two cups ready in a tower he occupied alone, and did not appear to notice he had done it, which was somehow the most damning detail of the entire evening.

They drank. The lens turned above them. Somewhere below, the sea worked at the rocks with patient criminal hands.

"You saved eleven men," Bell said at last. "Mowbray's report gives you credit. Hartley grudgingly passed it up the chain. There may be a commendation. There may not. The army dislikes owing debts to civilians."

"The men owed me nothing," Thackeray said. "The light did its work. I only kept it lit."

"That's a modest answer."

"It's a true one."

"It is." Bell turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "And here is my difficulty, Mr. Thackeray. My trade is truth of the smallest kind. Not the grand kind — I leave grand truths to chaplains and politicians. I deal in the small ones. Whether a boat's registration was renewed. Whether a signature matches the one beside it. Whether the ink on a page is the same age all the way across."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Above them the gearing murmured on, indifferent.

"Folio two hundred and twelve," Bell said quietly. "The register of residents. The Marchand entries."

He watched Thackeray's hands. They did not move. They stayed flat on the table on either side of the open logbook, and Bell thought, with something close to grief, that he had interrogated enough men to know that stillness like that was not calm. Stillness like that was a man holding a door shut with his body.

"There's an alteration," Bell went on. "Blotted while wet, overwritten dry. Competent — better than competent; I'd say practiced, if I didn't suspect it was done only once in a lifetime and never again. Under raking light there's another name beneath. A shorter entry. A woman's." He paused. "I have not reported it."

The wind rose a note in the slit windows. Thackeray did not take the offered opening, whatever it was, and Bell respected him enormously for it, and dreaded him a little too.

"You're asking why," Thackeray said.

"I'm going to tell you why, and then I'm going to make you an offer, and you're going to refuse it, and we'll both be sorry. Are you ready?"

Thackeray said nothing. Bell took it as readiness.

"I have a wife in Portsmouth," Bell said. "Thirty-one years married this spring. She writes me that the house shakes when the naval guns test across the harbor, and she has begun sleeping in the downstairs room because she cannot bear to feel the walls move above her. My son is at Ypres with the Royal Engineers. I have not seen either of them in fourteen months. I am telling you this not as friendship but as arithmetic — so you understand that I know exactly what the war costs, and I do not spend its currency lightly."

He set his cup down.

"Henri Marchand was naturalized in 1898. I've read the file. Twelve years a resident, married to an English-born mother's daughter—" he caught himself; the record said otherwise, and Thackeray's eyes flickered, and Bell adjusted without breaking stride— "a resident of this parish fifteen years. He fished these waters, paid his rates, raised his child. When war was declared he volunteered information to the coast guard about French channel markings — there's a letter in his file from your harbormaster praising his cooperation. And then he died. Before he could be asked to prove anything, before anyone thought to doubt him, he died of his illness like a gentleman, and left behind a widow and a daughter who woke up one morning as enemies of the crown because a piece of paper in Berlin said so."

Thackeray's jaw worked. Bell pressed on, gently, the way a man presses on a wound to find whether it bleeds inward or out.

"And someone in this town, holding the book that contained every name the army now owns, sat down under a lamp and decided that one widow and one child would simply — not be there. Blotted out. Substituted with a dead woman's name, God rest her, pulled from some earlier page. Do you know what I thought when I found it? I thought, *here is a man who will hang.* Because that's what it is, Thackeray. Falsifying a crown record in wartime. Not a fine. Not dismissal. Penal servitude, if Hartley reads that page the way I read it. And he will, if I put it in front of him, because Hartley reads everything the way a butcher reads a carcass."

The fire in the little stove guttered and recovered. Thackeray spoke for the first time in minutes, and his voice was low and even as ballast stone.

"Why haven't you put it in front of him?"

"Because," Bell said, "the page is not the whole ledger."

He leaned forward now, and laid it out, and hated himself with a distant professional detachment even as he did it, because the offer was genuine and mercy and a trap all at once and he could not separate the strands himself.

"There are irregularities in these records beyond yours. Small ones. The harbormaster's tonnage figures don't reconcile with the customs receipts — someone's landing goods unregistered, and it isn't smuggling so much as hunger, but it's still a crime. Two exemptions in the militia list were granted on medical certificates from a doctor who died last year and cannot verify them. The school register shows three children attending who were never formally enrolled. None of it is your doing. All of it is discoverable. And a tribunal is sitting Monday week to review the alien registrations of this district, and if I bring them a clean report — everything reconciled, every loose thread tied — then Hartley is satisfied, the district is signed off, and folio two hundred and twelve goes into the archive as one clerk's ink error among hundreds, unworthy of comment."

He paused.

"But if the tribunal finds the irregularities themselves — if some other officer with less patience than I have starts pulling threads and finds your thread knotted in among them — then I can't protect that page, and I can't protect you, and I certainly can't protect the woman you blotted out. So here is the offer, plainly. Help me reconcile the records. Sit with me, name by name, and tell me what you know of this town's small evasions — the boats, the certificates, the children — so that I can close them cleanly before Monday. Do that, and the missing page disappears. Not forgiven. Disappeared. Nobody suffers for it, including her." He held the man's eyes. "That is the price, Thackeray. Your neighbors' small sins, traded for your own large one. I'm offering you the chance to buy mercy with other people's coin. Most men take it."

Above them the great lens wheeled its slow circuit, sweeping the dark, sweeping the dark, sweeping the dark.

Thackeray rose. Not quickly. He crossed to the window and stood looking out at the black water for a long time, and Bell watched his back and saw in it the posture of a man at the edge of a wall deciding which direction to fall.

"My father kept this light thirty-one years," Thackeray said, finally, without turning. "He taught me the ledger before he taught me to swim. Every name in this parish, in his hand and then mine. You know what a ledger is, Captain? It isn't the crown's memory. It's the town's. It says: these people lived here. These people mattered enough to write down. Henri Marchand mattered. His wife matters. His daughter—" his voice caught once, and steadied— "his daughter matters, and I will not sell one line of this town to save another, not to you, not to the tribunal, not to the King himself."

"Even to save her," Bell said quietly. "Even knowing what refusal costs."

Thackeray turned. In the lamplight his face was terrible and calm, a man who had already fallen and discovered there had been ground beneath him all along.

"Especially that. If I give you the fishermen's boats and the doctor's certificates, you'll have me forever — a man who buys his safety piecemeal. And the day comes when you need one more name, and then one more, and I'll have taught you that I can be emptied. No, Captain. Take the page to Hartley if you must. Take me too. But the ledger stands as written, and I stand where I wrote it."

Silence, except for the mechanism and the sea.

Bell stood also, slowly, joints announcing themselves the way they did after fifty. He took out his handkerchief and folded it, unnecessarily, and put it away again.

"Do you know," he said, "in thirty years of auditing other men's honesty, you are the fourth man who has refused me. The first two took the offer after a night's sleep. The third informed on his brother." He gathered his cap from the chair. "You are the first to refuse for someone who isn't your blood."

"She is everyone's blood," Thackeray said. "She's just the only one keeping count."

At the stair door Bell paused, one hand on the cold iron rail, and looked back at the keeper standing in his small yellow circle of lamplight with the sea roaring behind the glass.

"The tribunal sits Monday week," Bell said. "Nine o'clock, the customs house. Hartley will preside. He will ask about the registry, and he will ask about you, and I cannot silence him — only myself." He settled the cap on his head. "Whatever you're going to do, Mr. Thackeray, do it before Monday."

And he went down the iron stairs into the dark, counting the steps as he went, as he counted everything, while above him the light swept on over a town that did not know how narrowly it kept its names.

*

The rain began before he reached the customs house.

It came in off the channel in long grey sheets, the vanguard of something larger — Barometer falling since noon, he thought, noting without meaning to the heaviness of the air, the wrong-colored light on the water, the gulls sitting sullen and grounded along the seawall like a jury refusing to deliberate. Thirty years of coastal paperwork had taught him the alphabet of weather, and the sky tonight was spelling out a word he didn't want to finish reading.

Inside, the requisitioned office smelled of wet wool and cold tea. Dawes was asleep over the internment schedules, pen still inked. Bell hung his coat, sat, unlocked the cabinet, and drew out the parish ledger.

Folio two hundred and twelve. He looked at it a long while under the desk lamp, tilting the page, watching the drowned name rise and sink beneath the living one like something breathing under ice.

Then he took out his private notebook and turned past *ink inconsistency, source TBD*, and beneath it wrote a new line:

*Refused. Won't trade. Will confess rather than comply.*

He read it back, and found that his hand had added, without his instructing it, a final phrase:

*Find the husband's service record before Hartley does.*

He sat back. The rain thickened against the window, and far out over the channel the darkness deepened toward the third storm, and Bell — who had spent his whole life reconciling what men wrote against what men did — closed the notebook softly, as if shutting a door on a room where someone was praying.

Monday was five days away.
