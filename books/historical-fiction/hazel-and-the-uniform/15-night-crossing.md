## 15. Night Crossing

The barometer had been falling since Tuesday. Cyrus Thackeray checked it the way other men checked their pulses — first thing in the morning, last thing at night, and once in the small hours when sleep abandoned him to the sound of the sea working against the headland like a jaw worrying a bone. The needle had dropped past *Change* without pausing and now sat trembling at the edge of *Stormy*, and when he tapped the glass with one knuckle it sank a fraction further, as if offended by his doubt.

He knew storms the way he knew hymns: by their openings. This one began with a wind from the southeast that carried the smell of warm rot off the marshes, then swung hard through south to southwest in the space of an afternoon, and by Friday's dusk the gulls had gone inland in great ragged skeins, wheeling over the village as though fleeing a fire only they could see coming. The soldiers on the point had doubled their sandbags and staked their canvas. The wireless mast at the signal station hummed and groaned like a man in pain.

And in the signal room, under a lamp with a cracked chimney, Cyrus had read the order that changed the shape of the weekend.

It lay now folded inside his coat, against his chest, where he could feel its edges with every breath. *Sweep of coastal district, Saturday 0600. All registered enemy aliens to be presented for removal.* The handwriting was not Bell's. It was some clerk's hand, round and indifferent as a schoolboy's, and that frightened Cyrus more than malice would have. Malice you could reason with. Indifference was a tide; you could not argue with a tide.

The tribunal sat Monday. He had two days — no, one night — and a storm coming that would swallow the coast whole.

He stood in the doorway of the lighthouse cottage, which was no longer his cottage except by the fiction of habit, and looked down the lane toward the Marchands' window. A candle burned there. It always burned late; Elise sewed for the garrison now, mending what the army could not be troubled to mend, and her silhouette moved behind the curtain with the patience of a woman who had learned that stillness was its own kind of speech.

*Saturday 0600.* The sweep would come up from the harbor road, cordons at both ends of Front Street, soldiers going door to door with lists. Her name would be on the list. Not the ledger — he had seen to that, God forgive him — but the registry, the one kept in the district office, the one he could not reach with ink and blotting paper.

There was only one place on the Brindle Point peninsula the sweep would not look.

Above them.

* * *

He knocked at her door the way he always knocked — twice, softly, with the flat of his fingers, a code they had never spoken aloud but both understood. The candle guttered as the door opened, and Elise stood in the light-wobble with a mended sleeve still in her hands, needle pinned to her collar like a small silver brooch.

"Monsieur Thackeray." Always formal, always in front of Colette. "You look like a man carrying weather."

"I am," he said. "May I come in?"

She read him in an instant — she had Henri's trick of reading, or perhaps he'd taught it to her, the soldier's habit of taking measure of a face before the mouth could arrange itself into lies — and stepped aside without another word.

Colette was asleep in the box bed behind the curtain, nine years old and curled like a comma, one arm flung out over the blankets toward the wall as if reaching for something just beyond the plaster. Cyrus lowered himself onto the chair across from Elise and told her everything in a voice pitched under the wind: the order, the hour, the cordon, the trucks already parked in the harbor yard with their canvases lashed down.

When he finished, Elise did not weep and did not argue. She set down her sewing, smoothed the mended sleeve flat on her knee as if it required her full attention, and said, "You have thought of something. I can tell because you are sitting like a man about to stand."

"The lamp room," he said.

Her eyes went up — reflexively, toward the ceiling, as though she might see through timber and plaster and forty feet of painted iron into the glass cage above the light. "The sweep will search the tower."

"They'll search the ground floor. The oil store, the keeper's quarters, the stores shed. Bell's men are thorough downward and lazy upward." He heard how it sounded and pressed on before shame could slow him. "The stairs above the watch room are secured behind my own lock. There is a landing halfway where the draft comes through, but the lamp room itself—" He stopped, recalibrated, made himself say the plain truth of it. "It will be cold. It will be loud. The glass sings in a storm like this; you feel the whole tower lean. But there is water, and bread, and a bench, and no list in the kingdom has a column for *above the light*."

Elise was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind found the gap under the door and played a low note across it, like breath across a bottle.

"And Monday?"

"Monday the tribunal sits. I go before them. I have the chaplain's letter." His hand rose unconsciously to his chest, to the folded order, and beneath it the thinner fold of the chaplain's confirmation — Henri Marchand, sergeant, wounded at Verdun sector, service honorable, record misfiled but existing, *existing*, if anyone would trouble to look. "I put Henri's name in front of them, and yours follows out of his. That is the plan. All of it. Everything between now and Monday is only keeping you off the trucks long enough for me to say it out loud."

"You make it sound simple."

"It isn't simple. It's the most complicated thing I've ever done, and I have kept a light burning through thirty winters." He looked at his own hands, sailor's hands, keeper's hands, blunt and scarred and suddenly untrustworthy. "But it is the plan I have."

Elise studied him. In the candlelight her face was all planes and shadow, French bones under English weather, fifteen years of this coastline written into the squint she got against the east wind. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"Cyrus. If they find us up there, it is not deportation for me. It is the charge for you. Falsifying records, harboring — they will take your light for certain, and perhaps more than your light."

"I know what it costs."

"Do you?" And there it was, the question she had never asked and he had never answered, hanging between them with all its freight. She let it sit exactly as long as it needed to, then gathered it up again herself, the way she gathered up everything painful and put it away in some drawer he had never been shown. "Very well. Tonight, then. Before dawn makes the streets unsafe."

"Not tonight." He leaned forward. "Tonight the wind is bad but honest. Tomorrow it turns. There's a window in the early hours — between three and four, when the sentries change and the rain comes in off the water hard enough to blur anything ten feet away. We cross then."

"*We*," she repeated, and almost smiled. "You use the plural as if you are coming too."

"I am coming too. Someone has to carry the child."

At the name, Colette stirred behind the curtain. A small voice came through the dark, thick with sleep: "Maman? Is it the storm?"

"Only the storm, ma puce. Sleep."

But the child did not sleep, not entirely; Cyrus heard her turning, and heard Elise rise and go to her, and heard the low murmur of French that was half lullaby and half lie — *c'est rien, dors, je suis là* — and he sat in the other room with his hat in his hands and understood that he had just arranged for a nine-year-old to spend two nights hiding in a glass lantern at the top of a tower in the worst storm in living memory, and that he would do it again, and again, and that this was what the promise had always meant and he had simply been too young in his grief to read it properly.

* * *

The hours between eleven and three were the longest of his life.

He spent them preparing, because preparation was the only prayer he knew. He laid a mattress pad from the stores bench in the lee of the lens pedestal, wedged behind the iron column where the wind's voice went from shriek to murmur. He filled two stone jars with water and stoppered them with cloth. Bread, a tin of condensed milk, a pot of jam from a jar Elise had given him at Christmas and that he had hoarded like a relic. His father's pocket barometer, which he wrapped in flannel and set on the pad — not for use, but because a Thackeray did not go into weather without one, and because the small brass weight of it felt like company.

He checked the clockwork. The rotation mechanism drove the lens around its bath of mercury, steady as a heartbeat, and in a storm like the coming one the weights would need winding every ninety minutes instead of the usual three hours, the cold thickening the oil in the gear train until the whole machine dragged. Which meant that whoever sheltered in the lamp room would share it with a man climbing down and up the stairs all night, over and over, wind and work, work and wind.

Which meant, he realized somewhere around one o'clock, standing in the watch room with the wind trying the shutters like a burglar, that Elise would hear him coming and going all night and would know, by the rhythm of his boots on the iron stairs, exactly how the rest of his weekend was shaped. There was no privacy left between them after this. Perhaps there never had been.

At twenty past two he went down the lane.

The rain arrived precisely as promised, a grey wall of it sweeping in off the water, and he walked through it hatless because a hood turned the face anonymous but bare hair and streaming skin made a man look merely mad, and mad men were waved along. The sentry at the lane's end had his collar up and his shoulders around his ears and wanted nothing so much as for the world to leave him alone; Cyrus raised a hand — *light check, routine* — and the boy waved him through without a word.

Elise was dressed and waiting. Colette, woken and warned, stood beside her in a coat too big for her — Henri's old ulster, sleeves rolled three times, hem to her ankles — with her nightgown stuffed into a satchel and her arms around a bundle that Cyrus, with a lurch, recognized as the wooden horse he had carved for her fifth birthday.

"Papa's horse is coming," Colette announced, in the matter-of-fact voice of children stating terms.

"Yes," Cyrus said. "Papa's horse is coming."

They went up the lane single file, Elise in the middle, Cyrus ahead with a shaded lantern turned to its lowest coin of light. The rain hit sideways. Somewhere below, the sea was making a sound he had heard only four times in thirty winters, a sound less like waves than like the whole Atlantic drawing breath to speak. The tower appeared out of the dark in sections — base, then nothing, then the gallery rail catching his lantern-glint like a wire of stars.

Inside, the stairs spiraled up into blackness. Cyrus went first, Colette second with her hands on his coat tails, Elise last, and the iron rang under their feet in a hollow music that the wind swallowed note by note. Halfway up Colette began to cry — silently, he could tell by the shake of the small hand gripping his, tears without sound, a child who had already learned that fear should be quiet — and he stopped on the landing and knelt and put her hand flat against the wall.

"Feel that?" The tower shivered under his palm, the old stones transmitting the storm like a struck bell. "That's the wind asking permission to get in. And that"—he pointed upward, to where the light wheeled overhead, its beam sweeping the fog in long golden blades—"is us telling it no. Your papa helped keep this light. Did you know that? He stood where you're going to stand. Nothing gets past a Marchand and a Thackeray in the same tower."

Colette sniffed, considered this, and nodded with terrible seriousness. They climbed on.

* * *

The lamp room took them in like a held breath.

Ninety feet up, the storm was not weather but presence — the glass walls flexed and boomed, the lantern sang in a hundred registers, and beyond it all the world was a chaos of black water and white ruin, the combers coming in from the southwest in ranks you could count by their phosphorescence, each one detonating against the headland with a shock he felt in his teeth. But inside the lens's orbit there was a strange calm, a cylinder of gold light turning slowly, endlessly, and Colette stood transfixed at the center of it with her face lit like a saint in a window.

"It's a lighthouse *inside* the lighthouse," she whispered.

"That's the lens. Fresnel. Augustin Fresnel built the first one before your maman was born. Two hundred and fifty pieces of glass, all holding hands." He showed her the bench, the pad, the jars, the little nest behind the column, and she arranged herself in it with the horse tucked under her chin and was asleep, astonishingly, within minutes — children can sleep through the end of the world if the world is sufficiently explained to them first.

Then it was only the two of them and the light.

Elise stood at the glass with her palms flat against the frame, watching the sea explode below, and Cyrus climbed down to begin the first of the night's windings. Ninety minutes later he climbed back up with the ache already setting into his shoulders, and she had made tea somehow — there was a spirit stove he hadn't known she'd brought, smuggled up in the satchel — and handed him a mug without a word, and they sat together behind the column while the beam wheeled above them, painting the little space gold, then dark, then gold.

"Thirty winters of this," she said at last. "Alone."

"Not alone. With the work."

"The work is not company, Cyrus."

"No," he admitted. "It isn't."

The wind slammed the lantern glass and the whole tower leaned — a slow, sickening tilt and recover, the old iron singing — and her hand found his forearm in the dark and stayed there. Neither of them mentioned it. Some things, once named, become smaller; this did not.

"Henri used to say you were the most stubborn man on the coast," she said quietly. "He said it as the highest compliment. He said, *if ever I cannot be there, find the stubbornest man in England and give him my family.*" She turned her head, and in the swinging gold light her eyes were wet and level. "He did not tell you to do this much. Whatever happens Monday — whatever they take from you — I want you to know that I know the size of what you were not asked to do."

Cyrus looked at his hands around the mug. There were a dozen things in his chest, and all of them were true, and none of them belonged in a lamp room two days before a tribunal with a sleeping child six feet away. So he said the truest one in the smallest words he owned:

"You were never a debt to me, Elise. Not one hour of it. I want that written somewhere better than a ledger."

The light turned. The dark came and went. Her hand tightened on his arm, and she did not answer, and the not-answering was itself an answer, and he filed it away the way he filed everything — carefully, in order, against the day he would need it.

Below them, faint through ninety feet of iron and stone and storm, the telephone bell in the watch room began to ring.

He counted the rings. Three long, two short. The garrison's emergency signal — and at three in the morning, with the greatest storm in a generation making landfall, it could mean only one thing.

Something was on the rocks.
