## 16. The Great Storm

**Cyrus**

The barometer had been falling since Thursday, and by Saturday evening it fell the way a man falls from a height — not gradually but all at once, the needle sagging past numbers Cyrus's father had pencilled on the dial forty years ago with the annotation *never again, God willing*. The glass itself seemed to sweat. He stood in the lamp room with his thumb on the instrument case and felt the storm arrive before it made a sound: a pressure in his back teeth, a heaviness in the sea that turned it the colour of gunmetal poured from a great height.

By eight o'clock the wind came. Not the gusting, theatrical wind of ordinary gales, which announces itself and spends itself in fits, but a single sustained note, low as an organ's bottom pipe, as though the whole coast had been fitted with a mouth and given one thing to say. The tower trembled down its length. Cyrus had climbed it through hurricanes before; he knew the difference between a tower complaining and a tower pleading. This was neither. This was the tower holding its breath.

Below him the garrison had battened down. The soldiers who had spent three weeks fortifying the headland — sandbags now gone to sodden grey sludge, the searchlight platform lashed and groaning — had retreated into the barracks wing of the cottage, and through the floorboards Cyrus could hear them: cards, laughter pitched too high, the particular bravado of young men pretending not to listen to the weather.

He heard, too, the smaller sounds he had been listening for since nightfall. A footstep in the oil store. The soft arithmetic of a woman settling a child among blankets that smelled of kerosene and hemp.

He did not go down. Going down would have been a kind of announcement, and announcements were dangerous. Instead he checked the clockwork that rotated the lens — four hundredweight of brass and crystal riding a bath of mercury, turning all night on a friction so fine it could be started with one finger — and he listened to it the way a physician listens to a heart, and he thought: *not yet*.

The machinery flooded at half past eleven.

It did not flood dramatically. There was no explosion, no catastrophe of metal. There was simply a sound like a long sigh beneath the floor, and then the clockwork's rhythm faltered — a hesitation, another — and stopped, the way a watch stops when it is dropped in water. The sea had come up over the base of the tower in quantities no drain built in 1861 was designed for, and the storm cellar where the weight-channels ran had filled, and the weights hung dead in their shafts, and the great lens stood still, throwing its beam out over the water toward a fixed point on the horizon as if transfixed by something it saw there.

A fixed light, Cyrus knew, was worse than none. A fixed light told a helmsman exactly where the rocks were and left him to guess everything else. Every ship's master for twenty miles knew Brindle Point flashed red every ten seconds; take away the flash and you took away the arithmetic they were doing in their wheelhouses tonight, in the dark, in this.

He put his shoulder to the manual crank first, out of habit, and the crank refused him — seized, or the gears below drowned past redemption. Which left the yoke. Which left hands.

The hand-turning gear was a relic from before the clockwork, a capstan arrangement bolted to the rotating bedplate itself, meant for emergencies measured in hours, not minutes. It took a strong man working steadily to keep four hundredweight of glass and brass in motion, ten seconds to the revolution, hour after hour. Cyrus had done it once, in 1909, for six hours, and had not been able to button his own cuffs for two days.

He looked at the crank handles, and he looked at the floor beneath which Colette Marchand slept her child's sleep among the oil drums, and he began to turn.

The lens came alive under his palms with a reluctance he felt through his whole body, a grinding negotiation of mercury and gearing, and then it moved, and then it was moving, and the beam went out over the water and swept and swept and swept.

Ten seconds. Again. Ten seconds. Again.

Somewhere around the fourth hundred revolutions he understood the arithmetic of what he had undertaken, and understood also that he could not stop, and that understanding changed nothing.

* * *

**Elise**

She had not meant to be found. That was the whole design of the thing — she and Colette folded into the oil store like two letters slipped between the pages of someone else's book, present but unrecorded, breathing quietly through the longest night of the winter while soldiers played cards twelve feet below. She had brought bread and a candle stub and Henri's photograph, though she could not have said why the photograph; perhaps because a promise should be witnessed by the one who made it.

But she heard the machinery die. Everyone in the tower heard it — that sigh, that stopping — and then she heard something else, something the soldiers below were too far away and too loud to catch: the sound of one man beginning a labour intended for two, or three, or four.

She had watched Cyrus Thackeray work for fifteen years. She knew the economy of his movements, the way he never lifted more than a thing weighed, the way he kept his griefs the way he kept his lamps — trimmed, shadowed, burning. And she knew, sitting in the dark with her daughter's head heavy on her knee, that he was going to destroy himself tonight turn by turn, politely, without ever once asking, because asking was not in him; because he had promised a dying man something in peacetime and had decided, in the manner of men of his generation and his coast, that the promise included everything down to the marrow.

There are moments when dignity and love point in opposite directions. Elise had lived long enough to know the difference between them, and long enough to choose love, and to do it without ceremony.

She settled Colette deeper among the blankets — the child slept as Henri had slept, completely, absolutely, as though sleep were a country she held citizenship in — and she covered the candle, and she climbed the ladder to the lamp room.

Cyrus did not see her at first. He was bent to the yoke, sleeves rolled, breath coming in the white ragged pulls of a man rowing through his own last reserves, and the beam wheeled above him and lit his face and took it away again, lit and took, lit and took, so that he seemed to flicker, to be many men, all of them exhausted.

"You will ruin your back," she said, "and then who will repair my clocks?"

He straightened slowly, the way old masts come upright. For a moment she thought he might argue — she saw the refusal assemble itself behind his eyes, the whole architecture of *you must not be seen here, you must not risk* — and then he looked at her properly, rain-dark hair coming loose from its pins, sleeves already pushed up, and whatever speech he had prepared failed him, and what remained was only a man looking at a woman in a room full of light.

"The stroke wants your whole body," he said at last. "Not the arms. Lean into it, like drawing water."

"I have been drawing water," she said, "since before you were keeping this light."

They took the two handles opposite one another, and the lens turned between them like a slow planet.

* * *

**Cyrus**

There is a labour that erases thought. He had known it all his life — hauling gear, winding weights, the long repetitive mercies of work — but he had never known it like this. The rhythm took them both within twenty minutes: push, sweep, transfer, push, the beam going out over the black Atlantic and returning across their faces, and in the rhythm there was no room for Captain Bell, or tribunals, or ledgers, or the war, or anything at all except the count.

"Seven," she said, marking the seconds under her breath, because he had taught her years ago, casually, on some summer visit, that the flash must fall on the ten and drift meant death. "Eight. Nine. Ten."

The flash. Around. Again.

The wind found the tower and tried to lift it. At some point after midnight a sea came over the headland itself — a green wall of it, absurd, impossible, carrying stones the size of skulls — and struck the north face, and the whole structure rang like a struck bell, and dust sifted down through the beam, and neither of them stopped. Stopping had become unthinkable. The count had become the world.

Once, between the third hour and the fourth, her hand failed on the return stroke — only for an instant, a slip of wet palm on wet iron — and he caught the handle she lost without breaking the motion, taking the whole weight through his own shoulders, and she saw it in his face, the flinch, and she said nothing, and took the handle back, and they continued. That was the whole of their conversation about what they were to each other. He would carry what she dropped; she would take it up again; the light would not fail.

At some hour of the morning he became aware that his hands had gone somewhere beyond pain. He looked down at them almost with curiosity — split at the heel of the palm, the blood diluted pink by spray, the fingers refusing entirely to close — and Elise followed his gaze, and reached into her apron, and produced strips of linen torn beforehand, wound one round each of his palms without a word, tied them with her teeth, and set her hands again beside his.

It occurred to him, pushing into the fifth hour, watching the beam sweep across the drowning dark, that he had imagined keeping his promise to Henri as a single act — some large thing, done once, finished. A hiding. A name blotted. But promises, it turned out, were not objects. They were motions. They were this: ten seconds, again, until the arms learned the meaning of the vow better than the mouth had ever spoken it.

"Six," said Elise. "Seven. Eight."

The collier's rocket went up at ten minutes past four.

* * *

**Elise**

She saw it first — a thread of white fire climbing out of the southeast, hanging, falling. A distress signal, small as a struck match against all that blackness.

"There," she said, and Cyrus leaned to the glass, and she watched his face do the terrible arithmetic: a ship out there, in this, steering by them.

"Collier, by the flare's colour," he said. "Low in the water already." He was silent one full revolution of the lens. Then: "If we stop, they die confused. If we keep turning, they die tired but they die knowing where the rocks are." He set his jaw. "We keep turning."

So they kept turning, and the rockets came at intervals — five of them, then six — and each one Elise counted aloud like a heartbeat, like a name, and with each one she thought of Henri in his last week, how near the end he had gripped Cyrus's wrist and said, *the light, at least, will not lie to anyone*, and how strange and enormous that small faith seemed now, spinning above a drowning crew.

Dawn, when it finally came, was not dawn so much as a gradual admission of failure by the darkness — a bruised greying, the sea resolving out of it in white rags and hills. And through the thinning murk they saw it: a steam collier lying broadside a half-mile off the point, funnels gone, deck awash, and along the cliff road the garrison's searchlight swinging wildly, uselessly, its operators fighting a mechanism never built for such wind.

And she saw, too, the small figures already gathering at the foot of the tower — soldiers, a dozen of them, staring upward at the lamp room, at the beam that had burned unbroken all night, at the two shapes moving inside the glass.

One of the figures detached itself from the rest and began, deliberately, wearily, to climb.

* * *

**Cyrus**

He knew the tread before he saw the face. Bell climbed the way he did everything — precisely, without hurry, as though the storm were a filing error he intended to correct. Water ran off his greatcoat in ropes. His cap was ruined. He looked, Cyrus thought, like a man who had been awake deciding something for most of the night.

The captain stepped into the lamp room, and the beam swept over him — once, twice — and he took in the scene with those pale, patient eyes: the stripped yoke, the bloody bandages, the wreck of Cyrus's hands, and Elise Marchand standing at the other handle with her chin up, daring him, in her stillness, to make her into something furtive.

Bell looked out through the glass at the collier, at the eleven heads visible on her fo'c'sle even at that distance, small and dark and waiting for the rocket line the soldiers below were even now rigging.

"Eleven men," Bell said at last, and his voice was hoarse, and he did not look away from the sea. "My sergeant counted them from the cliff. Eleven." He was quiet a moment. "They'll live to be counted elsewhere. That will be noted."

Then he turned, and for the first time since the requisition he looked directly at Elise, and Cyrus saw the question form and hang between them — *what I saw tonight, and what I am obliged by regulation to do about it* — and saw the captain fold the question, carefully, the way he filed everything, and put it away.

"The tribunal convenes Monday at nine," Bell said. "Six hours from now, Mr. Thackeray, you will stand before the board. What you tell them—" and here his gaze moved, briefly, to the bloody linen, to the turning they had kept alive through the worst night in living memory, "—is yours to decide."

He paused at the ladder's head, one hand on the rail, rain still dripping from him onto the clean steel plate.

"For what it is worth," he said, not turning around, "the light never went dark. My report will say so."

And he went down the stairs, and below, faintly, came the crack and arc of the rescue rocket reaching across the water — and the tribunal, and everything it would cost, was six hours away.
