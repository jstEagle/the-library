## 18. Tribunal

They came for him at half past six, while the gulls were still arguing over the tide line and the barracks yard smelled of wet coal and gun oil. A private with a boy's chin and a rifle too tall for him stood at the storeroom door and said, sir, the board is ready, as though Cyrus were the one keeping them waiting.

He had dressed carefully, the way he did for everything now — ritual against ruin. His good coat, the dark grey one Elise had once brushed clean of salt spray without being asked, on an evening so far back it belonged to another country. His collar was starched. His boots were blacked. Whatever they took from him today, they would not take his neatness. A keeper's discipline lived in the hands and the spine long after the uniform was stripped away.

The hearing was held not in the town hall but in the requisitioned customs house, where the ledgers of shipping fees still stood in their cases like saints in niches, and the Union Jack hung behind the bench at an angle no one had thought to correct. Three officers sat at a table draped in army blankets — a colonel from the coast defence staff, a major of engineers who kept glancing at his watch, and a thin captain of the Judge Advocate's department whose pen never seemed to stop moving even when nothing was being said. Captain Bell stood apart, near the window, hands clasped behind him, watching the harbour the way he watched everything: politely, thoroughly, without blinking.

Cyrus was shown to a chair that had been placed facing the bench, alone, with no table before it. He understood the arrangement. It was furniture designed for confession.

"Keeper Thackeray." The colonel consulted a file. "You are here concerning the parish ledger surrendered into military custody on the fourteenth of October last. You are aware of the nature of this inquiry?"

"I am, sir."

"Then let us be efficient. The war does not wait upon procedure." The colonel looked up, and his eyes were tired in the way of men who had signed too many papers that sent too many people somewhere cold. "An examination of records has disclosed certain irregularities. Entries altered. Ink inconsistent with the period of recording. A name struck through and overwritten in what our analysts describe as a hasty hand." He paused. "Do you understand what is alleged?"

Cyrus had rehearsed this in the storeroom through two sleepless nights, pacing the length of oil crates, speaking aloud to the dark. He had rehearsed denial too, just to feel its weight, and found it heavier than truth by half. Truth, at least, could be carried.

"Yes, sir. And I will save the board its time." He rose — no one told him to sit again — and took from inside his coat the packet he had guarded since the chaplain's letter reached him wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of a fish crate. "I falsified the ledger. I alone. No one assisted me, no one knew of it, and I will account for every mark of ink in that book if the board requires it."

The major of engineers stopped looking at his watch.

The colonel's face did not change, but something behind it shifted, the way water shifts beneath ice. "You confess freely?"

"Freely, sir. It is why I asked to speak first."

"Then state your offence plainly. In your own words."

Cyrus unfolded the packet as he spoke, laying each document on his knee like cards dealt to himself alone. "In November last, when the registry of enemy aliens was compiled from the parish ledger, the name of Elise Marchand appeared in it. Widow of Henri Marchand of Brindle Point, born in Normandy, resident of this parish for fifteen years. Under the tribunal's standing order she was liable to internment." His voice held. He was surprised by that; he had expected it to break. "On the night before the ledger was surrendered, I obliterated her entry and substituted the name of a woman dead these six years. I did it to prevent her removal. I have kept the light burning every night since, and every night I have known what I did."

"And you knew," the Judge Advocate's captain said, pen finally stilled, "that falsifying official records under wartime requisition carries — "

"I know what it carries, sir. I have carried it since the moment my pen left the page."

The word *carried* sat in the room. Bell's head turned a fraction, the first motion he had made, and Cyrus felt the look pass over him — not triumphant, not pitying. Weighing. That was always what Bell did: weighed things, filed them, decided later whether they were evidence or simply weight.

"Sentiment," the engineer major said at last, dismissively. "The man admits forgery out of sentiment."

"Out of promise, sir." Cyrus turned to face him. "If the board will permit it, that is precisely what I have come to put on record. Not sentiment. A debt."

The colonel gestured: proceed, and be brief about it.

"Henri Marchand came to this coast in 1901. He married in this parish, fished out of this harbour, raised a daughter in this town. When war was declared he presented himself at the recruiting office in Falmouth within the week — a French national, forty-one years old, flat-footed and seasick on land, and they laughed at him, so he went to the French consulate and enlisted with the foreign service of his own country's army." Cyrus drew out the first letter. The paper had gone soft at the folds from handling. "He served two years in the Argonne before a shell took his lung and sent him home to die. I sat with him through the last of it. What he asked of me in that room is between him, me, and God — but part of it was this: that his family should not suffer for the country of his birth while his blood was spent for the country of his enlistment. I promised. That promise is the whole of my crime, and I would commit it again tonight."

He laid the letters on the table before the bench, one by one, squaring the edges as though order could argue for him.

"The regimental chaplain of the 4th Mixed, Père Aubert, has confirmed in writing that Henri Marchand served under arms from August 1914 until his medical discharge in May of this year. Citations included. The second letter is from the consulate registrar. The third — " his hand hesitated, only a fraction — "is in Henri's own writing, dictated in his last week, naming his service and requesting that any debt owed to a soldier be paid to his widow and child."

Silence. Outside the window a transport lorrie changed gear on the hill road, grinding down toward the quay. The Judge Advocate's captain reached forward and read the chaplain's letter twice, slowly, his lips moving.

"This correspondence," the colonel said, "was in your possession how long?"

"Nine days, sir."

"Nine days." The colonel set down his pen. "And the tribunal sitting on the Marchand woman convenes Thursday. You have been in possession of exculpatory evidence for nine days and produced it only now, in the hour of your own arraignment. Why?"

Because nine days ago there was still a chance the ledger page would go unnoticed. Because hope makes cowards of careful men. Because he had wanted, desperately, to save Elise and keep his secret both, and only when Bell had stood in the lamp room doorway at dawn with the storm still dripping off his coat and said *the board sits Monday* had Cyrus understood that the hour of choosing had come due with interest.

"Because I hoped, sir, to spare the board the trouble," he said instead. "And because a man should not trade another's name to buy mercy for himself. I am not here to bargain. I am here to confess and to present the evidence that clears her. Both can be true. I ask for nothing."

It was not entirely true, and he felt the lie small and hot in his chest — he asked for everything, for her freedom, for Colette's mornings on the shingle, for the right to have done one clean thing in a dirty season — but the shape of it was honest enough to stand on.

The colonel studied him for a long moment. Then: "Captain Bell. You conducted the audit of requisitioned records and the subsequent inquiry. The board will hear you."

Bell came forward unhurriedly. Up close he looked as though he had slept no better than Cyrus; there was a grey shadow under each eye, and his immaculate collar sat a little less perfectly than it had in October. He saluted, was acknowledged, and stood with his fingertips resting on the table's edge.

"Captain, do you corroborate the accused's account of the ledger alteration?"

Here it was. The whole morning balanced inside that question like a keel. Bell knew about the lamp room. Knew about Elise's hands on the winding crank at three in the morning while Colette slept in the oil store and the sea came over the gallery glass in sheets. Knew, and had climbed down the tower stairs and said nothing but *six hours*. He could open his mouth now and end all three of them with two sentences delivered in that mild, courteous voice.

"I corroborate," Bell said, "the finding of the ink inconsistency as recorded in my report of the twelfth, and the accused's admission of it this morning." He paused. "As to the service record: I have independently verified the chaplain's letter through naval intelligence at Plymouth, who confirm the French foreign-service enlistment of Henri Marchand and the citations as genuine. I attach my verification to the record." He laid a single sheet beside Cyrus's packet, aligned it with the edge of the table, and stepped back.

"And the circumstances of discovery?" the Judge Advocate pressed. "Your report notes irregularities beyond the ledger. Movement of persons. Anomalies during the storm of the ninth. Do you wish to add anything?"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, in the customs house cellar, a soldier dropped something metal and swore softly, and the sound seemed enormous.

Bell looked at the window. The harbour lay out there grey and glittering, the collier still on the rocks at low water with her back broken, eleven men alive in the world because a lighthouse keeper had wired a truck lamp to a signal battery in a howling gale. Then he looked at Cyrus, and something passed between them that had no words in any regulation — the look of two men who had both stood in high places in bad weather and understood exactly what the other one had seen.

"Nothing further, sir," Bell said. "The rescue of the eleventh of December was conducted under conditions that would account for any anomalies noted. I consider them explained."

"Considered explained," the colonel repeated, and wrote it down, and Cyrus felt the sentence lift off his shoulders and Elise's and Colette's and settle, entire, onto his own back where it belonged. That was the arithmetic he had come to do. Let them take one man. One man was bearable. One man had already done the sums.

The board deliberated for eleven minutes — the engineer major timed it, Cyrus could tell by the way he kept his hand over his watch — while outside the window the tide turned and the gulls regrouped and somewhere in the village a church bell rang the quarter hour as it had rung it before there was a war or a requisition or a ledger with anyone's name in it at all.

"Keeper Thackeray." The colonel's voice had changed; it was quieter now, and somehow worse. "This board finds as follows. The documentary evidence establishes that the late Henri Marchand rendered active military service to an allied power, and accordingly the exemption of his widow from the standing internment order is recommended to take immediate effect. She and the child will be so notified in writing today." A pause. "Separately, you stand convicted of falsifying official records under requisition authority. Sentence: dismissal from the Corporation of Trinity House service, forfeiture of keeper's appointment at Brindle Point, effective upon the arrival of your relief. Further prosecution is — " the faintest hesitation — "not recommended, given your cooperation and the circumstances of record. Do you have anything to say before the board closes?"

Cyrus thought of many things. He thought of the first night he had lit the Brindle Point lamp at nineteen years old, his father's hand on his shoulder saying *mind the mantle, mind the weather, mind the ones out there in the dark*. He thought of the ledger's ruled lines, and how a man's whole soul could hide between two of them. He thought of Elise's hands, competent and salt-cracked, turning the crank beside his in the great storm, and how she had not asked him why, not once, not ever.

Only one thing needed saying.

"I would say thank you, sir, but not to the board." He straightened. "To whoever reads this into the record: the light at Brindle Point is sound. The machinery wants care in the north bearing — it has wanted it since September, and I have nursed it along, but my relief should see to it properly. There are eleven families on this coast and more besides who owe their men to that lamp. Whoever keeps it next, tell him the ones out there in the dark don't know his name and don't need to. They only need the light. Mind the mantle. Mind the weather."

The colonel looked at him for a moment longer than procedure required. Then the pen moved, the file closed, and it was done — twenty-two years ended in eleven minutes, his name struck from the light the way he had struck hers from the book, and the symmetry of it was so exact it almost made him smile.

He was escorted out through the customs house corridor, past the niche-cased shipping ledgers, past the private with the boy's chin who now looked at him differently, the way one looks at a thing one cannot yet classify. At the door, where the wind came raw off the water, Bell was waiting with his cap in his hands.

They stood a moment without speaking. Down the hill, past the fish sheds, a figure waited at the bend of the road with a shawl pulled close and a child's mittened hand in hers — waiting, as she must have waited since dawn, not allowed near the door, reading the verdict in the way a man walks.

"You knew what I'd seen," Cyrus said quietly. "In the tower. You could have buried me with a breath."

Bell put his cap on, settled it with two fingers, and for the first time since the lorries had come to Brindle Point he looked briefly, genuinely tired. "I spent three weeks auditing this town, Keeper. Every boat, every barn, every heart I could get a look into. Yours was the only record that came out consistent." He glanced down the hill toward the figure at the bend, then back, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "The board's recommendation reaches the district commandant tonight. But exemptions travel slower than sentences — there will be paperwork, signatures, the usual cruelty of clerks. She isn't safe in her own house until the order is in her hand, and there are men in the garrison who read regulations the way other men read scripture."

He stepped aside and opened the door, and the wind came through cold and smelling of the sea that had tried to drown eleven men and failed.

"Go and tell her," Bell said. "Quickly. Before someone less tired than I am counts the hours."

* * *

Cyrus came down the hill road with his cap in his hands and his appointment in ruins and his step lighter than it had been in fourteen months, and Elise read all of it at once — the loss, the freedom, the strange peace in his face — the way she read weather off the water, at a glance and completely.

"Well?" she said, in French, though there was no one left to hide from.

"You'll have the paper in a day or two. Until then — " he looked up at the point, at the tower standing white against the scud, at the lamp room where Colette had slept through the century's worst storm wrapped in an army blanket — "until then, we keep the light."
