## 20. Relieving Keeper

The new keeper arrived on the Tuesday, in a grey army lorry that ground up the coast road in second gear, throwing salt spray and gravel. Cyrus watched him come from the gallery of the tower, where he had gone ostensibly to check the ventilators but in truth because he had spent thirty years watching the road for arriving boats and could not stop now. Habit outlives title. That was the thought that came to him as the lorry halted below and a young man unfolded himself from the passenger seat — tall, fair, with the pink, wind-scoured face of someone who had never worked indoors. He wore his Trinity House uniform with the collar buttoned to the throat even in this weather, and Cyrus understood at once: this was a man who believed the uniform was the job.

Cyrus had believed that once too.

He went down the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, counting the steps as he always did — sixty-eight, worn smooth in the middle by three generations of Thackeray soles — and opened the door before the stranger could knock.

"Thackeray," the young man said, extending a gloved hand. "I'm Ellery. Relieving keeper." He said it without irony, without any awareness that the phrase had been turned inside out these past weeks. *Relieving keeper* — once a rotation, a brotherhood, men who passed the light between them like a bucket passed down a line. Now it meant something else entirely. It meant *the man sent to take your place after your disgrace.*

"Cyrus Thackeray," Cyrus said, though the boy obviously knew. "Come up, then. I'll show you the works while there's still light."

They climbed together, and Cyrus gave him the tower the way a father gives away a daughter — naming every dowry item as they went. The clockwork that drove the rotation, and its temperament: it wanted winding every four hours exactly, not four hours and ten minutes, or it would sulk through the small watches and lose a half-second of arc, and over a night those half-seconds accumulated into darkness where there should have been a blade of light. The mercury float beneath the lens, which must be kept clean as a communion chalice. The oil store, and the rule about never carrying a naked flame past its threshold — a rule young Ellery nodded at, but Cyrus saw him file it under regulations rather than under memory. There is a difference. Regulations are what you follow when someone is watching. Memory is what holds when the generator floods and the wind is screaming at two hundred tons and there is no one watching at all.

"The generators," Cyrus said, standing in the engine room where the flood-marks from the great storm still showed as a dirty tideline along the wall, "will fail you once a year. Usually in winter. Usually during the worst night God sends."

"I'm sure the army will see them maintained properly now."

Cyrus looked at him. The boy flushed.

"Yes," Cyrus said mildly. "Well. When they fail — and they will fail on my birthday, if the sea has a sense of humour, which it does — the hand crank is behind you. Two men can hold the light by hand-turning the pedestal. Four is better. You'll want to know which of the garrison can be trusted to turn a wheel for six hours without complaint, and you'll want to know it *before* the night you need them."

Ellery wrote this in a small notebook. He wrote everything in the notebook. Cyrus did not tell him that the important things would never fit in a notebook; that knowledge lived in the wrists and the ears — in the particular knock an engine makes eight hours before it dies, in the way the glass frosts a quarter-hour before fog closes the point. He did not tell him because no one had told him either, forty years ago, and he had learned it the only way it could be learned, which was badly, alone, at sea's expense.

But he did tell him one more thing, at the top of the tower, with the whole grey world spread out below — the town huddled under smoke, the harbor with its requisitioned boats, the headland stitched with sandbags and barbed wire.

"There's a family in the village," Cyrus said. "Marchand. Widow and a daughter. They were friends of mine. If ever they come to the door needing anything — oil, bread, a word — you'll give it to them."

Ellery glanced up. He was not stupid, whatever else he was. "They were saying in the mess — the tribunal, and all that—"

"The tribunal is finished," Cyrus said. "What remains is a woman who lost her husband and a girl who lost her father, living in a country that has forgotten the difference between an enemy and a foreigner. That's all I'll say about it. But you asked nothing, and I've told you everything, so we understand each other."

The boy looked out at the sea for a long moment. "My father kept the light at Flamborough," he said finally. "Twenty-two years. He said a keeper's first duty is to people he'll never meet."

"He said true," said Cyrus. "Now you know why they took the tower from me and left me my name."

* * *

He packed on Wednesday, in the cottage that was no longer his, while Ellery's kit cases stood in the hall like strangers at a funeral.

There was not much. He had known there would not be much — he had told Elise once that a keeper learns early to measure his life against what can be carried down sixty-eight steps, and he had lived by it — but knowing a thing and feeling it are different weathers altogether. He laid everything out on the kitchen table first, the way he inventoried the oil store, because order steadied him.

Henri's letters. Forty-one of them, tied in packets with the twine from the fish crates, spanning eleven years of a friendship conducted partly in person and mostly on paper, because Henri wrote the way other men breathed — constantly, copiously, about weather and politics and Colette's teeth and whether English ale was an insult or an achievement. The last letter was dated nine days before Henri died, and Cyrus did not open that one. He had read it once. Once was enough for any lifetime. He wrapped the packets in oilcloth, which repels damp better than anything the Post Office ever invented, and set them in the bottom of his canvas bag where the barometer would not crush them.

His father's barometer. Mahogany case, brass bezel gone green at the edges, the dial face yellowed like old teeth. *Stormy — Rain — Changeable — Fair — Set Fair*, and the needle that his father had trusted over the Admiralty forecasts and been right more often than not. Cyrus tapped the glass gently — the old ritual, tapping to see which way the needle would jump, reading its hesitation like a mood. It sat just short of *Rain*, trembling. His father's voice came back to him across forty years: *It's not the number, lad, it's the direction. A falling glass forgives nothing.* He wrapped it in his spare shirt and carried it separately, like a child.

Then the rest, which took five minutes: two suits, one good. Books — six of them, the Ramage navigation manual his father had annotated, the Bible his mother had annotated, three novels Elise had pressed on him over the years with instructions to actually read them this time, Monsieur Thackeray, and he had, actually read them, and loved two of them and pretended otherwise out of some stubbornness he could no longer explain. His razor. His mother's photograph, the only one taken of her, blurred because she had laughed when the photographer said don't. The tribunal's written verdict, folded twice, which he considered leaving and then did not, because a man should carry his own verdict; the weight of it was the least of what he owed.

And that was the table. Thirty years of keeping the Brindle Point light, and it fit into one canvas bag and a wrapped instrument, with room left over. He stood looking at it and felt — not grief, exactly. Grief was for things torn away. This was something quieter and harder to name: the discovery that the tower had never really been his, that he had only been its tenant the way he was a tenant of his own hands. The post was the Crown's. The work had been his, and the work went with him, folded up small, into oilcloth and canvas.

The logbook was the hardest. Not taking it — he had no right to it, and knew it — but writing his final entry. He sat at the kitchen table with the official form before him, the one the army supplied now instead of the old ledger-bound logs, and he wrote:

*Relieved this day, 14th December 1916, light in good order, lens clean, oil stock full, clockwork wound. All matters regular.*

*All matters regular.* He looked at the sentence a long time. Somewhere under it lay the blotted entry in a parish ledger, a Frenchwoman's name surrendered to a dead woman's, sixty nights of hidden lamps and shared labor, a promise made to a dying man in a peacetime kitchen. None of it regular. All of it, he judged in the balance he had built himself over sixty years, right. He signed the form, blotted it, and added one line more, knowing the censors would pass it because it was true and meaningless to anyone but him:

*The light may be trusted.*

* * *

They came for him Friday morning, though *came for him* overstated it. Nobody marched him anywhere; the war had no more use for humiliating an old keeper than it had spare men for the task. A corporal appeared at the door with a hand-cart for his trunk — the one luxury he'd allowed, a sea chest his grandfather had brought back from Halifax — and said the lorry would be round by ten, sir, if that suited.

It suited. Nothing suited and everything suited; the words had stopped meaning much either way.

He spent the last morning doing what he had done every morning for thirty years: he climbed the tower and put the light to bed for the day. Ellery walked up with him, unasked but welcome, and together they cleaned the lens, trimmed no wicks — the incandescent mantle needed no trimming, another change too far for Cyrus's affections — checked the mercury, wound the clockwork full. Cyrus ran his thumb along the brass rail of the lantern one last time, the rail his father's hand had polished and his grandfather's before, worn silver-bright in one place where three generations of thumbs had found the same resting spot without ever discussing it.

"That spot," he told Ellery, tapping it. "You'll find your thumb goes there eventually. Don't fight it. It knows something you don't."

"What does it know?"

"That you live here now. Whatever they call you on paper."

At noon the sun made its brief winter appearance, thin as a communion wafer, and Cyrus carried his bag and his barometer down the sixty-eight steps and out the door. The sea chest went ahead on the cart, jolting over the cobbles of the yard.

And then a strange thing happened, and it happened without orders, which is the only reason it mattered.

The garrison was drawn up in the yard — not all of them, perhaps twenty men, the ones off watch, formed loosely by the lorries with their breath smoking in the cold. Cyrus had assumed they were parading for some inspection, and he kept his head down and made for the vehicle. But as he passed, the sergeant — Sergeant Doyle, who had come ashore with Bell's original landing party in October and had spent one long bad night in November hauling crank-handles beside Cyrus while a collier full of coal tried to become a reef — stepped forward out of the line.

Doyle came to attention. Then he saluted.

Not the casual flick a man gives a superior he's humoring. The full thing, arm steady, held, the salute given to rank of a kind the army has no insignia for. And after him, one by one, in no order anyone had commanded, the others came to it — the signaller, the gunners, the two boys from the anti-aircraft detachment who could not have been nineteen, the cook still in his apron with a spoon in his fist, saluting a disgraced keeper with a canvas bag and a wrapped barometer, off the point he had kept alight for thirty years and through the worst storm season the coast had recorded in living memory.

Cyrus stood still. His throat did something complicated. He had imagined this departure a hundred ways in the past week — escorted, ignored, whispered about — but never this, and certainly never *unprompted*, because he understood what unprompted meant. It meant no officer had ordered it. It meant Bell had not arranged it, or if Bell had known of it, had chosen — deliberately, in his meticulous, not-unkind way — to look elsewhere while it assembled itself. It meant the men had decided among themselves what the tribunal's verdict could not undo: that some titles are conferred by towers and storms and not by boards of officers.

He returned the salute. Held it as long as Doyle held his. Then he got into the lorry, because a man who tarries at his own honouring makes everyone uncomfortable, and the driver started the engine.

As they pulled out of the yard, he looked back once. The tower stood against a sky the colour of dishwater, and high up in the lantern, small with distance, Ellery's pale head moved along the gallery — already checking the ventilators, already learning the rail. The light would go on. That was the whole point of a light; it did not belong to anyone, it simply burned, and the burning was the duty, and the duty passed from hand to hand down the years like the bucket down the line.

Sergeant Doyle rode partway down the coast road with the trunk cart and got off at the village crossroads, where the road forked — one way to the railway station and the world, the other way into the village, past the church, toward the cottages where rooms had been taken and clocks waited repairing and a woman named Marchand had said, the evening before, in her plain unhurried French-inflected voice, *you will come to supper the first night, monsieur, and bring the barometer, I wish to see if it agrees with my own bones.*

The lorry slowed at the fork. Cyrus gathered his bag.

"One thing, sir," Doyle said, at the ground, holding the door. "Captain Bell — he didn't order the parade, if that's what you're thinking. But he knew. He stood at his window the whole time. And afterward he wrote something in his book." Doyle hesitated, choosing. "Small book. The one he writes everything in. I couldn't read it from where I was. But it wasn't a report."

Cyrus climbed down. The village lay ahead in the blue dusk, first lamps being lit, smoke rising straight in the cold. Somewhere down that lane, supper was being laid for three, and a girl called Colette had been asking, all week, whether Mr. Thackeray would be bringing the clock parts he'd promised, and whether he was staying *near* now, properly near, the way Papa used to be near.

He thanked the sergeant. He shouldered the bag, took up the barometer, and set his feet toward the lights of the village, the needle in its mahogany case trembling at his back — falling, falling, toward *Changeable*, his father murmuring across forty years, *it's not the number, lad, it's the direction* — while behind him the great lens began its first slow sweep of the night, sending its blade of gold out over the water, over the town, over all of them equally.
