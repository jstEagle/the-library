## 21. The Light Still Turns

Spring came to Brindle Point the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and then all at once. One morning in April Elise opened her window above the harbour and found the blackthorn in the lane had broken into white overnight, as if a fleet of small ships had come to anchor outside her glass. The wind off the water had lost its iron edge. It smelled now of wet earth and salt and, faintly, of the gorse beginning to flower on the headland where the soldiers had been.

The soldiers were gone. The lorries had rumbled away through the village in March, leaving ruts in the road that filled with rainwater and reflected the sky, so that for a week the lane seemed to run with pieces of heaven. The barbed wire on the point was being taken down coil by coil. And the lighthouse stood as it had always stood, white against the grey water, its lamp lit each evening by a keeper whose name Elise did not care to learn, because it was not the name that mattered.

She had taken rooms above the draper's shop on Fore Street — two rooms and a lean-to kitchen that caught the morning sun and none of the afternoon. Mrs. Pentreath, the draper's widow who let them, had been one of the neighbours who would not meet her eyes during the worst months. Now she brought up milk each morning and lingered to talk about her son in Flanders, and Elise understood this for what it was: an apology too large for words, delivered daily in a jug. She accepted it as such. Fifteen years in England had taught her that the English apologized in gestures the way the French apologized in speech, and that both nations found the other's method insufficient.

Colette was at the village school now, walking down the hill each morning with her satchel and her hair in two plaits tied with ribbon saved from before. She had learned to skip. Elise watched her go some mornings from the window and thought: *Henri, look. Look what she is becoming.* Colette spoke French at home and English everywhere else, and slid between the two languages the way she slid between the gateposts on the lane — sideways, laughing, without effort. Whatever else the war had taken, it had not taken that.

* * *

Cyrus Thackeray came to tea on Thursdays.

He came at four o'clock precisely, because he was a man who had spent thirty years keeping a light on schedule and could no more arrive at half past than the tide could refuse the moon. He came up Fore Street from the cottage he rented near the churchyard, carrying his tools in a leather satchel and usually something small for Colette — once a compass with a cracked face, once a brass key that fit nothing, which she wore on a string around her neck like a medal.

His hands were different now. That was what Elise noticed first, those early Thursdays. For thirty years his hands had been oiler's hands and wick-trimmer's hands, hands that knew glass and brass and the weight of a storm shutter. Now they were clockmaker's hands. He repaired them — clocks, watches, the great regulator in the hotel lobby that no one had been able to set right since old Mr. Tregaskin died. Mr. Colenso the butcher had a carriage clock Cyrus had restored; the schoolhouse bell ran on a mechanism he had rebuilt from scrap. He had a workbench under the eaves of his cottage, and when the light failed in any clock in Brindle Point — as lights will fail — it came to Cyrus Thackeray, who coaxed it back to life with tweezers and patience.

"It is a good trade," he said to her once, his cup halfway to his mouth. "Repairing time instead of marking it."

"You were always repairing things," Elise said. "You only called it something else."

He looked at her then — that long, careful look of his, the look of a man reading a ledger — and said nothing, and drank his tea.

That was how they were, that first spring. There was between them everything they had lived through: the ledger with its blotted line, the storms, the nights in the lamp room with the machinery flooded and the light turning by hand while Colette slept in the oil store wrapped in sailcloth. There was the tribunal, and his confession given standing, in a clear voice, like a man reciting the names of constellations. There was the verdict — Elise exempted by her husband's service, Cyrus stripped of his post for falsifying records — and there was the strange, unprompted salute of the garrison as he walked off the point for the last time, carrying Henri's letters and his father's barometer and very little else.

All of this lay between them like a sea between shores, and neither of them crossed it. They spoke of clocks. They spoke of Colette's schooling, of Mrs. Pentreath's milk, of the herring season and whether the church roof would hold another winter. On clear evenings, after he had gone, Elise would stand at her window and watch the light sweep out over the bay, and she would think about how it fell on all of them equally — on Cyrus in his cottage, on herself above the draper's, on Captain Bell's grave in the far corner of the churchyard where he had been laid the previous autumn, taken by his heart before the war could take anything more from him. The light did not distinguish. It never had. That had been the whole argument of Cyrus Thackeray's life, made nightly against the dark.

* * *

It was Colette who broke the pattern, because it is always the children who say the thing the adults have agreed not to say.

One Thursday in May, when Cyrus had finished his tea and was showing Colette how the escapement of a carriage clock worked — holding the small wheel up to the window light, explaining the tick and the tock as though they were the heartbeat of something living — Colette looked up at him with her father's exact eyes and asked:

"Will you always come on Thursdays?"

The room went very quiet. Down in the shop, Mrs. Pentreath's till rang once and went still.

"I expect so," Cyrus said. "As long as I'm wanted."

"Always?" Colette pressed, with the relentless logic of nine years old. "Even when we don't need anything fixed?"

"Especially then," said Cyrus.

Colette nodded, satisfied, and returned to the escapement. But Elise sat with her hands folded in her lap and felt the words go through her like the beam of the light itself, sweeping over ground that had been dark a long while.

* * *

After Colette was abed, Elise walked Cyrus down as far as the corner where Fore Street met the harbour road. It was more steps than courtesy required, and both of them knew it, and neither of them turned back.

The evening was one of those spring evenings that cannot decide whether to be night or day — the sky a pale washed gold at the western edge, the water calm enough to hold the colours doubled. Gulls settled on the masts below. Somewhere in the village a door closed, and a dog answered it.

Above the point, the new keeper lit the light. They both watched the flash appear — the long slow sweep, white to white.

"Four seconds' eclipse," Cyrus said, out of thirty years of habit, and then stopped himself, as if he'd spoken out of turn in church.

"Tell me," Elise said.

"Tell you what?"

"About the years ahead." She kept her eyes on the light, because it was easier to speak to a horizon than to a face. "You have repaired every clock in this town, Mr. Thackeray. You have told me about the weather, and the herring, and Mrs. Chegwin's chimney. You have been to my table forty times and you have never once said what you came to say. I am French. I am blunt. Say it."

He was quiet long enough that she finally turned to look at him. He stood with his cap in his hands — he had taken it off, she realized, turning it slowly by the brim, the way men do when their hands need employment and their courage needs company.

"When Henri was dying," he said at last, "I promised him I would look after you. I made that promise lightly. Not because I didn't mean it — but because I didn't know what it would cost, or what it would become." He looked down at his hands, the clockmaker's hands. "For two years I told myself everything I did was duty. Keeping you off the ledger was duty. Turning the light by hand in the great storm was duty. Standing before that tribunal was duty. And then—" he made a small helpless gesture toward the hill behind them, toward the rooms above the draper's, "—then they took my post, and my duty ended, and I found I still came on Thursdays. Every Thursday. And I could not call it duty anymore, because there was nothing left to be dutiful *to*. So I have been coming to your table these three months with no excuse at all, drinking tea I did not want, talking about herrings—"

"—you do talk about herrings a great deal," Elise said, and her voice was not quite steady.

"—because I love you," Cyrus said. "And I have loved you since before I had permission to, and I am done pretending otherwise. But I'll not presume on a promise made to a dying man. If you'll have me, it must be freely. Chosen. Not owed." He put the cap back on his head, straightened it, and stood very square, the way he had stood before the tribunal board. "So. What are the years ahead, Elise? Tell me, and I'll shape myself to them."

The light swept past them again, laying its brief white hand on the harbour water, on the blackthorn, on his face.

Elise thought of Henri — not with grief, for the grief had worn smooth as beach glass over the long winter, but with something like gratitude. He had known. Of course he had known. Men who are dying see clearly across distances the living cannot; he had looked from his bed at the keeper next door and seen exactly this Thursday evening, exactly this question, and he had set it in motion the way one sets a mechanism moving, trusting the escapement to keep the time.

"You ask what the years ahead are," she said. "I will tell you. They are Thursdays. They are teaching Colette that a promise is a heavy thing but not a chain. They are watching the light together on clear nights and knowing it shines on us the same as everyone — no better, no worse, and free." She reached out and took his hand, the clockmaker's hand, and felt it close around hers with the care of a man handling something older and finer than himself. "I choose it, Cyrus. Not because you promised Henri. Because you are the man who keeps promises, and I have watched you pay for it, and there is no braver sight in this world."

He breathed out — a long breath, held, she suspected, since the tribunal, perhaps since the ledger, perhaps since a deathbed two winters gone.

"There is a word," he said, "for a keeper relieved of his post who stays anyway."

"There is a word in French for it also," Elise said. "We use it often. Come to dinner on Sunday. Not Thursday. Sunday — a different day entirely, so that you will know this is a different thing."

"A different thing," he repeated, as though committing it to the logbook of his heart.

They stood a moment longer while the light turned above them, four seconds' eclipse, then white again, patient and equal and sure. Then Elise turned back up the hill toward her rooms, toward her sleeping daughter, toward Sunday — and behind her she heard Cyrus's boots on the harbour road, unhurried, steady as a well-kept clock, walking into the years ahead.

At her window, before she banked the fire, she saw the sweep come round once more. And she found she was counting the eclipses the way he did — not as loss, but as measure. Four seconds of dark, then light. Four seconds, then light.

It was, she thought, a fair accounting of any life worth the name.
