## 1. The Letters I Should Not Read

There were three hundred and eleven letters in Lady Edith Vane's correspondence case that October, and Rosalind Ravensworth had read every one of them.

Officially, she had read none.

Officially, she was a chauffeuse—a woman in a long motoring coat with goggles pushed up into her hair and grease permanently under the left thumbnail—and her duties ended at the bonnet of the Vane motorcar. She drove. She maintained. She kept her mouth shut and her eyes forward, and if a lady wished to conduct her correspondence from the back seat while Rosalind's hands occupied themselves with the wheel, then that was between the lady and her letters, and none of Rosalind's business whatsoever.

In practice, she had developed a system.

The trick was the mirror. The driving mirror, small and round and angled just so, gave her a slice of the back seat: the corner of an envelope, the slant of a hand, the flicker of Lady Vane's eyes as she read. Rosalind could hold a page in that mirror for four seconds at a time—longer at traffic stops, longer still when a coal lorry blocked the road outside the Corn Exchange, which happened most Tuesdays—and she had trained her memory the way other women trained their figures, daily and without mercy. Four seconds per glance, six glances per letter, and by the time they reached their destination she could recite the whole of it to herself in the dark of the garage like a prayer she wasn't sure she believed in.

She did not keep copies. Copies were evidence, and evidence was the one thing Rosalind Ravensworth could not afford. What she kept instead was the thing no constable could confiscate: a mind arranged like a filing cabinet, cross-referenced, indexed, and locked.

*Mrs. Aldous, Harrogate. Third letter this month begging for the subscription money back; Lady Vane will send double, anonymously, through the WSPU office.*

*Rev. Whitmore complaining again about the word "revolt" in the pamphlets. He spells it "revolte." Lady Vane finds this delightful and will not correct him.*

*The Home Office man, initials only, no address. Asking whether the Leeds branch intends to escalate before spring. Lady Vane's reply drafted but unsent for two days now—she is weighing something.*

Rosalind weighed things too, in her own quiet way. Weighed them, catalogued them, and filed them behind the door she never opened.

* * *

Tuesday began, as all her Tuesdays began, at half past five in the morning, with the radiator.

The Vane motorcar was a 1911 Daimler, black as a beetle and twice as temperamental, and it hated cold mornings the way Lady Vane hated the Daily Mail. Rosalind was under the bonnet in the grey dark of the mews, sleeves pinned to the elbow, when the kitchen door opened and Mrs. Beddoe put her head out.

"She's asking for you."

"Tell her ten minutes."

"She knows. She wants you to know she knows."

Which was Lady Vane entire: a woman who managed people by letting them believe they'd fooled her, when in fact she'd simply decided the illusion amused her. Rosalind wiped her hands, lowered the bonnet, and came around to the steps where Lady Edith stood wrapped in a coat too fine for the weather, holding a cup of tea she would not drink.

"Cold," Lady Vane said. It was not a complaint. It was an observation offered to the universe, and the universe was expected to take notes.

"The radiator agrees with you, my lady."

"Nothing agrees with me before eight o'clock." But she took Rosalind's arm getting into the car, which she did for no one else, and settled herself against the cushions with the correspondence case on her lap like a sleeping cat. "Committee at nine. The Harrogates at eleven. And we are to collect Miss Pankhurst's parcel from the station at two, assuming the railway has not lost it, which the railway will have done."

"Assuming, my lady."

"You assume beautifully, Ravensworth. It's why I keep you."

It was, Rosalind suspected, only about forty percent of the reason. The other sixty percent involved an incident in Scarborough the previous spring that neither of them ever mentioned, involving a locked cellar, a plainclothes inspector, and Rosalind's talent for looking like exactly the sort of dull, competent woman whom nothing interesting had ever happened to. Lady Vane had watched her lie to that inspector with the serene blankness of a Sunday schoolteacher, and something in the old lady's sharp face had changed—not warmth, exactly. Recognition.

Two women who understood the value of being underestimated. It made for a working arrangement of unusual honesty, provided the honesty stayed on Lady Vane's side of the driving mirror.

Rosalind's own side remained, as always, sealed.

* * *

Leeds at seven in the morning was a city clearing its throat. Smoke stood over the rooftops in columns so straight they looked drawn on with a rule, and the streets ran brown with the first tide of mill workers, shawled heads bowed against the drizzle. Rosalind drove through them slowly, partly for safety, mostly because she liked to look at them—these hundreds of women who would never drive a motorcar or read a Cabinet minister's letters or stand in a room where history was decided, and who were history anyway, every last bent-backed one of them, though nobody had thought to file them under anything.

She had been nobody once. Was nobody still, officially. There was a comfort in it she never quite explained to anyone: invisibility as armour, anonymity as a kind of wealth. A woman no one could describe accurately was a woman no one could find. She had spent four years and one changed name purchasing that condition, and she paid the premiums daily, in loneliness, and considered it a bargain.

Mostly she considered it a bargain.

At the traffic stop by the Corn Exchange—the coal lorry was there, right on schedule, God preserve the order of Tuesdays—Lady Vane opened the correspondence case and began, without preamble, to read aloud.

Not aloud, precisely. Lady Vane read the way she did everything: at herself, in a mutter, half-audible, trusting no one and confirming everything. "…disgraceful…the Home Secretary has no intention…and here, this one is threatening, Ravensworth, listen—" and she would not listen, because listening was service and reading was privacy, and the wall between those two things was load-bearing in the Vane household. But the muttering carried, as it was meant to. Lady Vane performed her mail the way other ladies performed pianoforte: for the benefit of anyone qualified to appreciate it.

And Rosalind, eyes forward, hands at ten and two, appreciated.

"—signed *Ashcombe*, of all people. Ashcombe! Two years dead and still writing to annoy me. His secretary has no idea, presumably. Or his secretary knows precisely, which would be far more interesting—"

The coal lorry moved. The car moved. The moment folded itself away into the cabinet, filed under *Ashcombe, Lord — posthumous correspondence — verify*.

She would not think about it further. That was the discipline. Curiosity, in Rosalind's experience, was the first crack in any seal, and she maintained her seals the way she maintained the Daimler: ruthlessly, on schedule, whatever the weather.

* * *

The committee meeting consumed the morning. Rosalind waited outside the Mechanics' Institute in the drizzle, hat brim down, watching the suffragists arrive in twos and threes—young ones with bright defiant hats, old ones with faces like folded letters. A constable loitered across the street making a poor job of pretending to inspect a lamppost. Rosalind gave him a nod so bland it was nearly an apology, and he looked straight through her, which was the correct response, the desired response, the response she had engineered with four years of deliberate dullness.

It worked. It always worked. And every time it worked, something small and unreasonable in her chest registered the success as a failure.

At eleven, the Harrogate widow. At half past twelve, an argument in the car over whether Lady Vane would or would not eat the sandwich Mrs. Beddoe had prepared (she would not; she ate half of it while insisting she couldn't). At two, the station, and Miss Pankhurst's parcel intact after all, which put Lady Vane into such a good humour that she talked the whole way home—about the committee, about the Government, about a shipping dispute she'd heard of at luncheon, dockworkers out on strike at Southampton of all places, and certain gentlemen of her acquaintance muttering that the insurance on some of those vessels was worth more than the cargo.

"Moral hazard," said Lady Vane, savouring the phrase like a boiled sweet. "That is what the actuaries call it, Ravensworth. When a man profits too conveniently from disaster, sensible people begin to ask questions. Are you listening?"

"I am always listening, my lady."

"Yes," said Lady Vane, looking out the window at the smoke. "That is rather what worries me sometimes."

Rosalind's hands did not tighten on the wheel. She had trained them not to. But somewhere deep in the cabinet, a drawer that had been shut since 1909 shifted half an inch against its lock, and she pretended not to hear it, the way she had been pretending for four years.

* * *

Evening found the motorcar put away, the day's letters sorted into the case, and Rosalind in the garage with a lamp and the Daimler's brake linkage, which wanted attention she was happy to give it, because brakes asked no questions.

She was lying on the creeper with her head under the axle when the memory ambushed her, as memories did when her hands were busy and her guard was down.

Her father's study. The smell of pipe smoke and sealing wax. Herself at nineteen, standing in the doorway with a handful of papers she had stolen from his desk, saying words she had rehearsed for three days: *I know what the Meridian Line is, Papa. I know what you signed.* And the look on his face—not guilt, not rage, but something worse: arrangement. Her father had looked at her the way a man looks at an equation that has produced the wrong answer. Something to be solved.

Four days later, the Yorkshire paper carried the notice: *Killed in a carriage accident while travelling on the Continent.* A daughter named Rosalind Ravensworth, deceased. A grave in a churchyard she had never once visited, holding a coffin full of stones, while the living girl went north with a new name and instructions to stay dead.

He had been thorough. He was always thorough. It was the quality she had inherited from him along with the colour of her eyes, and she hated it, and relied on it, and both of those facts sat in her chest like swallowed stones.

She rolled out from under the car and sat up, wiping her hands on a rag that had stopped being clean sometime in August.

The point of being invisible was that it worked. The point of the cabinet, the mirror, the memorized letters, the whole apparatus of careful silence, was that it kept her alive. She had chosen it. She chose it again every morning when she pinned her sleeves and lowered the bonnet and became, once more, the dull competent woman whom nothing interesting had ever happened to.

From the house came the faint ring of the telephone, and then Mrs. Beddoe's footsteps hurrying along the hall, and then—unusually—hurrying back.

"Miss Ravensworth!" Through the door, slightly breathless. "There's someone at the service entrance. A woman. Soaked through, and won't give a name, and—" a pause, and when Mrs. Beddoe spoke again her voice had dropped, "—she's asking for the lady who fights."

Rosalind sat very still in the lamplight, rag in her hands, and felt the evening tilt beneath her, gently, the way the ground tilts just before it gives.

She should have sent the woman away. Every instinct she owned, every rule she had written for her own survival, said the same thing: strangers at the service door, strangers asking for Lady Vane by reputation, strangers soaked in rain and secrets—that way lay everything she had spent four years burying.

She folded the rag. Set it down. Stood.

"Inform her," she heard herself say, "that I am coming."
