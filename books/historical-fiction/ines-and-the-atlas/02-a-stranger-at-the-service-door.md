## 2. A Stranger at the Service Door

The rain had followed me from Liverpool like a debt collector.

It came down on Leeds in sheets that Tuesday evening, hammering the cobbles of the mews behind the Vane house until the whole world sounded like applause for something terrible. I stood under the dripping eave across the lane and watched the kitchen door with the patience of a woman who had nothing left to lose except a packet of oilskin and the truth folded inside it.

Three days I had sat in chapels and public houses and the waiting rooms of men who did not write back, asking after *the lady who fights*. They all knew her name—every woman in the North knew Lady Edith Vane's name—but names were not addresses. Names got you watched. It was a chauffeuse I finally paid two shillings to break her silence, a sharp-faced girl in oilskins who looked me over outside the post office and said, "If it's the mistress you want, you'll be disappointed. If it's Miss Ravensworth you want, God help you, but she sees everything."

I didn't know then what that meant. I know now.

The kitchen windows glowed amber through the rain. Inside, someone was working late—a silhouette bent over a table, unhurried, precise. Steam rose from a copper in the corner. The cook had gone up an hour ago; I'd watched her leave by the side gate with an umbrella and a loaf under her arm. Whatever household this was, it ran on rails, and the figure at that table was the hand that kept them oiled.

My boots were ruined. My coat had given up its pretence of waterproofness somewhere past Huddersfield. The oilskin packet rode inside my shirt, against my breastbone, warm as a second heart. Two years I had carried it or things like it—first the telegram, then the papers the company sent with their condolences and their careful lies, and finally *this*, which I had taken from a dead man's desk in Hull while his murderers' colleagues slept one floor below.

I crossed the lane.

The door was oak, scarred, unglamorous—the kind of door that existed so grand ones elsewhere could stay clean. I raised my fist to knock and discovered my hand wouldn't close properly. Cold does that. Fear does that too, though I'd have denied fear if you'd asked me. I hammered instead with the flat of my palm, three dull thuds swallowed by the weather.

The silhouette at the table went still.

I waited. Rain found the back of my collar and ran a cold finger down my spine. Then a lamp moved, a bolt slid, and the door opened the width of a Bible's spine, and I was looking at a woman I would spend the rest of my life failing to describe adequately to anyone who hadn't met her.

She was not beautiful in the way that stops conversations. She was beautiful in the way that ends them. Dark hair pinned with mechanical efficiency, a grey day dress with sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink on the side of one hand like a tattoo she'd forgotten to wash off. Her eyes did something no eyes had done to me in two years: they took inventory. Collar, hands, boots, the bulge under my coat where a pistol would sit if I were the sort of woman who carried pistols. She completed the audit in perhaps a second and a half, and I watched her arrive at a verdict.

"No canvassing," she said. "No begging. The collection is Thursdays."

"I'm not—" My voice came out wrong, scraped thin. I started again. "I need the lady who fights."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not softening. Recalculation.

"Lady Vane receives petitions at her Monday surgeries. Apply in writing." She began to close the door.

"*Miss Ravensworth.*"

The door stopped. An inch of lamplight lay along her cheekbone.

"The girl at the post office," I said. "She said you see everything. She said you'd know whether I was lying." I peeled my coat open just enough to show the corner of the packet—oilskin, waxed, tied with tarred twine, the knots swollen with salt. "Two years ago the steamship *Atlas* went down off Flamborough Head with forty-one men aboard. The newspapers called it stress of weather. I'm calling it murder, and I've been calling it murder to every editor and magistrate and Member of Parliament between here and Holyhead, and every one of them has told me to go home. So I've come to the only house in England where going home isn't an answer."

She didn't move. The rain applauded.

"Forty-one men," she said at last, quietly. "And you're related to how many of them?"

"One." My throat closed around the number. "The clerk. Thomas Ferro. Third manifest clerk. He was twenty-four." I heard my own voice drop into a register I hated, the flat one, the one that made landladies nervous. "He wrote letters home every fortnight. Right up until the last voyage. And the last letter wasn't about weather at all."

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—I believed she would shut the door. I could see the whole argument happening behind her face: the risk arithmetic, the household asleep upstairs, the stranger bleeding rainwater onto the step with madness in her voice and contraband in her shirt. I had seen that same calculation on better-dressed faces a hundred times. It always ended the same way. Doors close. That is what doors are *for*.

"Inside," she said.

* * *

The kitchen was warm the way only working kitchens are warm, heat soaked into the stone floor itself, into the hanging copper pans, into the big scrubbed table where she'd clearly been sitting for hours with ledgers and correspondence arranged in ranks as neat as soldiers. A kettle hung within reach of the fire without needing to be fetched. Everything about the room said: *this is a machine, and it is running well.* Everything about the way she moved through it said she was the mechanism.

"Sit." She nodded at a stool by the fire. "Don't touch anything."

"Wasn't planning to."

"You'd be surprised what people touch." She poured hot water over leaves in a brown pot without measuring—she owned the measurements by memory—and set a cup in front of me, then, after a visible pause in which hospitality fought policy and won narrowly, poured one for herself. She stayed standing on the far side of the table. Deliberate. The table between us like a frontier.

"Name," she said.

"Ines Ferro."

"Ferro." She turned the word over. "Italian?"

"Father was. Mother was a Hull fisherman's daughter with no interest in being asked about it." I wrapped both hands around the cup because they needed a job. "I've been asked about it a great deal anyway."

"And the packet. You said murder. You said letters. People bring Lady Vane grievances every week—wages, husbands, landlords. She's good at grievances. Why bring this one to the service door at nine o'clock at night in the pouring rain instead of Monday surgery like everyone else?"

"Because Monday surgery gets you sympathy." I set the cup down. My hands had started doing something embarrassing. "Sympathy is what I've had for two years. Sympathy and closed files. What I brought can't wait for Monday, and shouldn't be shown to a room full of strangers, and—" I stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because I watched your house for three days before I knocked. I saw the letters go out and come in. I saw the motor leave at half seven and return at six. I saw you burn papers in the copper's grate on Sunday night and stand over the ashes until they were powder. Whoever you are, Miss Ravensworth, you already live like the truth is dangerous. I'm asking you to believe mine is too."

She studied me the way I imagine a surgeon studies an X-ray—not the person, the structure. Looking for the fracture.

Then she said, "Show me."

I drew out the packet. My fingers had gone stiff enough that the tarred twine defeated me twice; she reached across, produced a small knife from somewhere about her person—I never did learn everywhere she kept things—and cut it clean. The oilskin unwrapped with a smell of bilge and brine, the smell of the inside of Thomas's desk drawer, and I had to look at the ceiling beams for a moment and count them. Eleven beams. There had been eleven beams in our mother's kitchen too. Some coincidences are just cruelties wearing costumes.

"There's a covering note," I said when I could speak. "Read that first. Read the hand first. Then read the rest."

She unfolded the single sheet with two fingers, cautious as if it might bite, angled it to the lamp—

—and stopped.

I have thought often since about exactly what happened in her face in that first second. Most people, reading, wear comprehension like a garment, put it on piece by piece. Rosalind Ravensworth went white all at once, the colour draining from cheek to jaw to throat as if a tap had been opened at her collar, and her thumb pressed hard against the corner of the page, and she said, in a voice stripped down to bare wire:

"Where did you get this?"

"A desk in Hull. The Harrow Shipping offices. Second floor, third room, locked, and I had the key because the man whose key it was doesn't need it any more." I leaned forward. "You know the hand."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. I've watched eleven editors read that sheet and not one of them went the colour of the tablecloth." I stood up. Policy be damned; the frontier of the table meant nothing to me any more. "Look at the date, Miss Ravensworth. Look at the signature. And then tell me—who taught you to recognise the handwriting of a drowned man?"

She didn't answer. She was still holding the page, and now I saw the tremor start in her thumb and work inward, and I understood, with the particular lurch of a woman who has bet her life on a stranger and just watched the stranger flinch, that I had guessed right about more than the handwriting.

"Lord Ashcombe," she said. Almost to herself. "That is Lord Ashcombe's hand. Every loop of it. I've handled four hundred of his letters and I could draw those loops blindfolded."

"Four hundred—" I gripped the edge of the table. "*How?*"

"Never mind how." But her voice had gone distant, a woman speaking from partway inside a memory, and she smoothed the page flat on the scrubbed wood and read, and I watched her lips move faintly over the words I knew by heart:

*"…the matter concludes Thursday next. The vessel will be written off at Lloyd's within the quarter, and the blame will fall where it has been arranged to fall—you may assure the gentlemen at the Board that the strikers will hang for it in print if not in court. Burn this, as ever. A."*

"Burn this, as ever," I repeated. "He writes that to all of them. He thinks he's clever. My brother thought so too, apparently—he copied it out before he posted it. Thomas kept a fair copy of everything. Company rule, he said." My laugh came out broken. "Company rule saved the evidence that proves the company killed him."

Rosalind Ravensworth looked up at me across the kitchen, and whatever competent loneliness had lived in her face when she opened the door was gone, burned off like fog in sun, and what replaced it frightened me far more than the bowler-hatted man who had followed me from Hull. Because it was certainty. She believed me. One glance at a dead man's loops and she believed me, and belief like that, arriving that fast, could only mean she had her own reasons to find this letter plausible.

"This can't wait until morning," she said.

"No."

"It can't wait for Lady Vane's Monday anything. If the date in that letter means what it appears to mean—" She stopped herself. Pressed her palms flat on the table, and I watched a woman visibly rebuild herself into someone decisive. "You'll stay tonight. There's a truckle bed in the linen room; the cook won't come down till six. You'll eat something. And at seven o'clock you'll tell Lady Edith Vane what you told me, and God help us both, Miss Ferro, because if that letter is genuine, then the man who wrote it is alive, rich, and respectable—and we are the only two women in Yorkshire who know it."

She gathered the oilskin packet, folded it with the reverence due to something explosive, and crossed to the copper's grate—then paused, twine in hand, and glanced back at me with something that was almost, almost a smile.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the girl at the post office undersold me. I don't just see everything." She nodded at the letter in her hands. "I remember it."

She fed the tarred twine to the flames, and it went up green and hissing, and upstairs somewhere a clock struck ten into the rain.
