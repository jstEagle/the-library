## 3. The Hand of a Drowned Man

The girl was still in the kitchen when I came down at half past five the next morning, which meant Lady Vane had not turned her out, which meant—against every rule I had built for myself in three years of service—that she intended for me to deal with her.

I had not slept. That was not unusual. What was unusual was that I had spent the night not composing letters in my head or rehearsing tomorrow's routes but turning over a single image: an oilskin packet on my mistress's kitchen table, and a line of handwriting visible at its mouth like a snake's tongue.

*Ashcombe.*

I did not believe in ghosts. I believed in ledgers, timetables, tire pressures, and the exact weight of silence a woman must maintain to survive. Ghosts were for novelists and curates. But dead men do not write letters, and so either the letter was forged or Lord Ashcombe was not dead, and both possibilities required more thinking than I could do while a stranger slept ten feet from the larder.

She was awake when I entered. Sitting upright on the kitchen chair where she'd been left, hands folded in her lap as though she had been raised by someone strict. Her clothes had dried overnight into shapes that suggested they had once been respectable. Her eyes tracked me across the room with the flat patience of a cat that has decided whether it likes you and is waiting for you to catch up.

"There's bread," I said, because it was the only civil thing available at that hour.

"I'm not hungry."

"Everyone's hungry eventually." I set the kettle on. "Rosalind Ravensworth. I drive for Lady Vane."

"Ines Marchetti." A pause. "My mother was English."

Which explained the accent that was mostly Yorkshire with salt in it. I sat down opposite her, and we regarded each other the way two dogs regard each other through a fence.

"The handwriting," I said. "You said last night it was Lord Ashcombe's."

"He wrote to my brother. Four times before the *Atlas* went down. This one came after."

"After," I repeated. "After the ship sank."

"Yes."

"And your brother?"

Her jaw moved once. "He was a clerk aboard her. They told us it was an accident. Boiler fault, or cargo shifted, depending on which newspaper you bought." She looked at her hands. "They didn't even agree on the story between themselves."

The kettle began its low climb toward boiling. I let it climb. In three years of reading other people's post I had developed a sense for lies—not the clumsy kind, but the kind that arrives dressed in truth and pays its fare honestly. Everything this woman said had that texture. Which was precisely what made it dangerous.

"Lady Vane will want to see you after breakfast," I said. "Until then, I'd ask you not to discuss the contents of that packet with anyone. Including me."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. "You haven't asked to see it."

"I read enough letters for a living."

"What do you do with them? The ones you shouldn't have read?"

"That depends," I said, and took the kettle off the heat, "on how much trouble they'd cause."

* * *

Lady Vane took her breakfast at eight, standing, in the morning room, with the *Yorkshire Post* propped against the toast rack. She was sixty-one years old and had been thrown off platforms in Manchester, heckled in London, and imprisoned in Holloway, and none of it had succeeded in making her hurry. When I brought her the oilskin packet she looked at it for a long moment over the top of her spectacles.

"Well," she said. "Fetch the others."

"The others?"

"The letters from the archive. The Ashcombe correspondence. Third cabinet, blue box, the year 1911." She set down her knife. "And send Mrs. Pilling up with more toast. It seems we are in for a long morning."

The Vane household kept an archive. Most people found this eccentric; I found it inevitable. Lady Edith Vane did not trust memory—memory could be denied, discredited, made to seem hysterical—and so anything that passed through her hands worth keeping went into the cabinets in the back study: depositions, clippings, parliamentary questions, and eleven years of correspondence with men who would have been horrified to learn their letters survived them.

I carried the blue box to the morning room and laid it open on the table beside the packet. Lady Vane washed her hands first, as if preparing for surgery. Then she drew out the letter inside the oilskin—a single sheet, folded twice, wrapped around two smaller enclosures—and unfolded it beneath the window light.

I stood at her shoulder. I am not proud of what I did next, but I will be accurate about it: I read over her arm, and I read fast.

*My dear Mrs. Marchetti,*

*Your discretion is valued and shall be rewarded. You will understand that certain arrangements cannot be conducted through ordinary channels. The matter of the vessel proceeds as discussed. The timing is fixed and will not be altered, whatever noise the unions make. Your brother's position gives him access; see that he uses it and says nothing.*

*Regarding compensation—the sum agreed upon delivery. I remain, madam, your obedient servant,*

*Ashcombe.*

Beneath it were two pages in a different hand, cramped and blotted, unsigned. I caught only fragments before Lady Vane turned the sheet: dates. Tonnage figures. A phrase underlined twice—*insurance valuation exceeds cargo fourfold*—and a name I did not recognize: *Harrowgate & Voss.*

Lady Vane read it all the way through, twice, without moving. Then she set it down on the table, smoothed it flat with one finger, and picked up the archived letters one by one, holding each beside the new sheet as a jeweller holds a stone against a known standard.

She worked in silence for perhaps six minutes. I counted them by the clock on the mantel because counting was better than breathing.

At the fourth letter she stopped.

"Come here," she said. "Tell me what you see."

I leaned in. Two lines of writing side by side. The archived letter—an invitation to a fund-raising dinner dated March 1911—and the new one, dated eleven days ago.

"The hand," I said. "It's the same."

"Describe it."

"The *A*s are peaked rather than round. He breaks his *t*'s leftward at the top. There's a downward drag on every terminal stroke, as if—" I stopped.

"As if?" Lady Vane prompted.

"As if the writer is tired. Or old. Or hurried." I pointed to the date line on the new letter. "But here the peaks are sharper than in 1911. Two years older. Hands change."

Lady Vane looked up at me, and there was something in her face I had rarely seen: approval, arriving late but arriving whole. "Three years of driving me about," she said, "and it turns out I've been paying a chauffeuse and getting a graphologist."

"My father collected autographs," I said, which was true, and was also the smallest possible true thing I could say about my father.

"Mm." She turned back to the table. "Then you'll confirm it plainly. Is this Lord Ashcombe's hand?"

I compared the letters again, all five of them now, the dead man's voice spread across the table in ink. Peaked *A*s. Left-broken *t*'s. The tired terminal drag, deepened by age.

"Yes," I said. "It's his."

"Lord Ashcombe," Lady Vane said, to the room in general, "died on the ninth of September, 1911. His yacht was lost off the Skerries with four crew. There was an inquest. There was a memorial service at St. Margaret's which I attended, out of courtesy, and where the Bishop of Ripon spoke for twenty minutes about the transience of worldly achievement." She removed her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve with terrible calm. "Either the Bishop was misinformed, or this letter was written by a corpse with excellent penmanship."

* * *

Ines Marchetti was brought up from the kitchen at nine and stood in front of Lady Vane's desk like a woman before a magistrate. I stayed by the door, officially invisible, unofficially unable to leave.

"You wrote to my brother," Ines said, before Lady Vane could open proceedings. "In 1912. After the sinking. My sister-in-law showed me the reply. You wrote that the truth would out, and that grief was a poor fuel but the only one that burned hot enough."

"I write a great many letters," Lady Vane said. "Most of them come to nothing."

"This one didn't. I've spent two years coming to nothing because of it." Ines lifted the oilskin packet from the desk and held it out. "Read it. All of it. Including what he sent underneath."

Lady Vane read. I watched her the way I watch weather. First the stillness of concentration; then, crossing the second enclosure, a different stillness altogether—the stillness of a woman who has stepped onto ice and heard it speak.

She finished. She folded the sheets along their existing creases, precisely, and set them down, and put both hands flat on the desk, and did not move.

I had seen Lady Vane receive bad news before. Bad news made her quicker. It made her dictation faster and her appointments rearranged and her enemies' names added to lists. This was not that. This was a woman going very still the way water goes very still just before it freezes, and I understood, with a cold spreading feeling in my own chest, that whatever was in those enclosures had passed some threshold she had not expected to cross in her morning room over the toast.

"Mrs. Marchetti," she said at last. "These unsigned pages. Where did your brother obtain them?"

"He copied them. He was clerk aboard the *Atlas* on her last three runs, and then he was transferred to the shore office at Hull, and in the office he saw the manifests. He saw what was listed and what was actually loaded, and the difference between the two." Ines's voice stayed level, but her hands had curled into fists at her sides. "He started copying things. Dates. Valuations. He wasn't a political man—he didn't know what to do with it. Then he got the letter from Ashcombe, telling him to keep quiet and he'd be paid, and he understood what he'd stumbled into, and he panicked. He gave the copies to me. He said if anything happened to him—"

The sentence ended. It did not finish itself.

"—then it happened," Ines said instead.

"And the *Atlas* sailed from Hull on the eleventh of October, 1911," Lady Vane said quietly, "and foundered with the loss of nineteen hands, and the inquiry attributed it to shifting cargo in heavy weather, and the owners collected their insurance in January of 1912." She paused. "Nineteen men."

"Nineteen," Ines said. "One of them mine."

For a moment nobody spoke. Downstairs a door banged; the ordinary machinery of the house went on grinding. I stood by the door with my heart doing something unprofessional and thought: *nineteen men, blamed on God and the weather, while the men who arranged it collected the money and attended the funerals.* I had read blackmail and adultery and fraud in three years of intercepted correspondence. I had never before held, even at second hand, the paper record of nineteen deaths.

"The names," Lady Vane said. She tapped the second enclosure. "*Harrowgate & Voss*. That is a shipping concern. And here—" her finger moved down the page "—is initials only. *H.V.M.* Who is H.V.M.?"

"I don't know," Ines said. "Tommy didn't know. He thought it might be someone at the Board of Trade. Someone who could smooth the inquiry."

"*H.V.M.*" Lady Vane repeated it as though tasting it. She did not look at me. I noticed, afterwards, that she did not look at me, and understood only much later why that absence had frightened her.

She rose and walked to the window. Rain had started again, silvering the glass. Below, a tram ground past, and a boy sold newspapers, and Leeds went about its business with the whole rotten architecture of the world sitting on top of it undisturbed.

"Do you know what happens," she said, without turning, "if this letter leaves this house and proves to be a forgery? Do you know what they will do to the movement? Every speech I have given, every woman who chained herself to a railing—they will say we are forgers now, hysterics and forgers, and half the country will believe them gladly. It would set us back ten years. Perhaps twenty."

"I know," Ines said.

"And if it is genuine and we bury it, then nineteen men stay drowned in a lie, and the men who drowned them go on arranging ships and inquiries and bishops until the day they die comfortably in bed." She turned around. "I find I cannot do that either. I am too old to start choosing my comforts over my principles; it gets easier each time, they tell me, and I don't care to learn."

"Then what?" I asked. I hadn't meant to speak.

Lady Vane looked at me for a long moment.

"Then we establish which it is," she said. "Beyond argument. Beyond denial. And quickly, because a man who has been dead for two years does not write letters unless he believes himself safe, and a man who believes himself safe makes mistakes—but a man who learns that his letters are being read stops making mistakes and starts making examples." She came back to the desk and rested her fingertips on the oilskin packet, lightly, as though it might go off. "This letter was posted from where, Mrs. Marchetti?"

"Hull. The postmark."

"And who else knows you have it?"

"No one living."

"Then we have perhaps days. Perhaps less. If Ashcombe is alive, he is alive because powerful men want him hidden, and the arrangement holds only so long as everyone keeps quiet. Letters get posted in moments of vanity. Sooner or later the vanity passes and the caution returns, and then someone comes looking for every person who ever received one." She drew a breath. "Rosalind."

"Yes, my lady."

"Sit down."

I sat. It was the first time in three years she had invited me to sit in her presence, and I felt the strangeness of it like a change of altitude.

"You read this letter as well as I did," she said. "Better, if we're honest about the penmanship. I want you to tell me plainly: can this be got to someone who can use it, before whoever wrote it decides to tidy up?"

"Got where?" I said. "To whom?"

"To a newspaper that isn't owned by the men in question. To a lawyer with courage. To a Member who owes me a favour and hates Harrowgate & Voss." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "There are more options than you'd credit, Rosalind. The difficulty is not the destination. The difficulty is the road. Because I promise you that somewhere between this kitchen and any printing press in England, there will be men whose job it is to stop exactly this letter, and they are better paid than we are."

Outside, the rain thickened. Ines Marchetti stood in the middle of the morning room in her ruined coat with her dead brother's evidence in her fists, watching us both like a woman counting her remaining coins.

"The *Atlas*," I said slowly, and then stopped, because saying it made it real. "The new *Atlas*. The replacement ship. She's fitting out at Southampton. There were placards at the station—*sailing Thursday's tide, cargo and passengers for the Channel run.*"

"Yes," Lady Vane said.

"If the same men own her, and the same arrangement is proceeding—as the letter says, *the matter of the vessel proceeds*—then whatever proof exists of the first sinking may exist aboard the second. Manifests. Correspondence. The pattern repeats because patterns are cheaper than inventions." I heard my own voice as though it belonged to someone slightly braver. "We wouldn't need to prove the letter is genuine. We'd need corroboration. From the source."

Silence. The mantel clock ticked through four full seconds.

"It is two hundred and forty miles to Southampton," Lady Vane said, "and the tide is on Thursday morning, and today is Monday." She took off her spectacles, folded them, and set them down with a small decisive click. "Pack light, drive carefully, and neither of you mentions this house or this business to a living soul. Mrs. Marchetti—you will travel as my guest, since a lone woman on the road invites questions and two women invite fewer."

"And me?" I asked.

Lady Vane looked at me, and behind the iron of her expression something moved that might, in another life, have been apology.

"You, Rosalind, will drive," she said. "And somewhere on that road, you are going to tell me who *H.V.M.* is, because when I read that name, you went white to the lips, and I have won elections by noticing less."

The floor tilted—or I did. I kept my hands on my knees and my face composed, three years of practice holding me upright, while the ordinary world I had built out of timetables and tyre pressures and well-read letters slid quietly sideways into something with teeth.

"I don't know anyone called H.V.M.," I said.

It was the first lie I had ever told her.

Lady Vane held my gaze, unhurried, and let me know by the mildness of her smile that she knew it too.

"We leave at six," she said.
