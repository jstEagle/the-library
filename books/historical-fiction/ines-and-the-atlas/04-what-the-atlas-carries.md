## 4. What the Atlas Carries

The kitchen was warm in the way only a servant's kitchen knows how to be—heat pressed from the stove, the kettle ticking as it cooled, the smell of yesterday's bread and today's boiling linen layered into the walls like sediment. Ines sat where they had put her, on a stool too low for her knees, with a cup of tea she had not touched going lukewarm at her elbow. Her coat hung dripping by the door. She could hear it drip.

The tall woman—the driver, Rosalind—had gone out and come back twice: once with dry towels, once with the letter held flat between two fingers as though it might bite. Now Rosalind stood at the far end of the scrubbed table with Lady Vane's housekeeper, both of them looking at Ines the way people look at weather coming in off the moors.

"You said," Rosalind began, "that you were told to give this to the lady who fights."

"I was told." Ines's voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat. Two days of walking and third-class carriages and sleeping rough in a station yard had sanded it down to gravel.

"Told by whom?"

"By my brother. Or—" She stopped. Started again, because there was no way to say it that didn't sound mad, so she said it plainly, which was the only way left to her. "By what my brother left behind. Tomás was a clerk aboard the *Atlas*. Third manifest office. He kept copies of things he wasn't meant to keep copies of, even when he was a boy copying hymns out of the missal he'd already memorised. He never trusted anything he couldn't hold in his own hand."

The housekeeper's mouth thinned. Rosalind hadn't moved at all. That was the thing about this woman that Ines had noticed within a minute of the door opening: she did not move, or blink, or breathe in any way she didn't choose to. A face like a locked drawer.

"Your brother died," Rosalind said. Not unkindly. Just laying the fact on the table like a knife.

"Eleven months ago. The *Atlas* went down off Ushant with thirty-one souls. The papers called it an accident. Stress of weather, cargo shifted, hull failed—each paper had its own story and none of them agreed, which I noticed, because grief makes you read everything four times." Ines looked down at her hands. The nails were broken; she had torn one clean across climbing a wall outside Southampton two nights ago, running from nothing, running from everything. "I read everything. Four times. And then a fifth time, because none of it made sense, and then I stopped reading the papers and started reading his letters instead, and that's when I found the first one."

She reached into the inner pocket of her coat, past the oilskin packet's empty shape now that the letter was gone from it, and drew out a much-folded sheet of cheap paper, soft as cloth at its creases.

"Tomás wrote me every month. Sailors' letters, mostly—what he ate, how much the purser cheated at cards, whether the second engineer's wife had written again. Ordinary man's ordinary life. Then, in March of last year, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Faster. Like a man writing against time." She unfolded it carefully, the way you handle something already broken once. "He didn't tell me anything directly. He knew better. But he wrote that if anything happened to him, I should go to a certain address in Bristol and ask for a parcel. He gave me the address in the form of a shipping order, so anyone reading over his shoulder would see nothing but freight numbers."

"And you went," Rosalind said.

"I went. There was no parcel. There was a man who looked at me for a long time and asked what my brother drank." Ines smiled without any humour in it. "I said gin, which was wrong—he drank nothing but coffee—and the man relaxed and told me the parcel had been sent ahead to Leeds. Which was a lie, but a lie with instructions folded inside it, and I found them because I am also my father's daughter and I checked the paper twice."

Lady Vane's kitchen door opened and closed behind her. Ines didn't turn; she had heard enough footsteps in enough houses to know a lady of consequence by the weight of her tread alone, and this tread had authority in it, and impatience.

"Mrs. Pym," said a voice like a bell rung once, "why is there a drowned rat sitting in my kitchen telling stories about the *Atlas*?"

Ines turned then.

Lady Edith Vane was smaller than the photographs and straighter than the caricatures. The newspapers drew her as a harridan, all chin and fury; in person she was neat, silver-haired, with eyes the pale grey of a winter sea, and she looked at Ines the way a magistrate looks at a witness—not hostile, simply determined to get at the truth underneath whatever was offered.

"She claims," Mrs. Pym said carefully, "to carry proof about the sinking of the *Atlas*, my lady. From her brother, who was clerk aboard her."

"The *Atlas* is a wreck," Lady Vane said. "Her owners collected on her. Thirty-one men are dead. What proof can anyone carry of a thing two years cold?"

"This kind." Rosalind spoke from the end of the table. She still held the letter, and now she set it down on the scrubbed pine between the teapot and the salt, and turned it so Lady Vane could read it. "You'll want to sit down first, my lady, I think."

"I have been sitting down all morning," said Lady Vane, and read the letter standing.

* * *

Ines watched her read it. Watched the grey eyes go down the page once, fast, the way a person reads when they don't believe what they're reading and are checking for forgery. Then again, slowly, lips moving on the figures—the tonnage, the insurance valuation, the date of the policy renewal, the date of the proposed sailing.

When Lady Vane finally looked up, her colour was high and her voice was very quiet, which Ines would learn, later, was worse than her shouting.

"Who else has seen this?"

"No one living," Ines said. "My brother saw it written. The men who wrote it don't know a copy exists."

"Explain that."

"Tomás copied it out himself. The original passed between two offices—Halliwell & Voss, the brokers, and the underwriters' room. My brother carried manifests and correspondence between decks and desks for six years. He was invisible. Men like that"—she nodded at the letter—"never look at clerks. They talk over their heads as though clerks were furniture. Tomás was furniture that could write shorthand."

Rosalind made a small sound. Ines glanced up and caught something cross the other woman's face—recognition, almost pain—gone before she could name it.

"He copied it out in fair hand," Ines went on, "in case the original was destroyed, and posted it to himself care of a cousin in Hull, which is the oldest trick in the world for keeping a letter safe—you post it, you receive it, and no one can prove whose hand it passed through. It reached me three weeks after the Admiralty inquiry closed. Three weeks after they ruled it stress of weather and praised the owners for their loss." Her voice caught on the word *praised*, and she let it catch; she had decided long ago she would not be polite about her brother's death. "Thirty-one men. Six of them boys under nineteen. My brother was twenty-four and could swim, my lady, and drowned anyway, in a hull that was sold to the insurance company before it ever cleared Southampton Water."

The kitchen was silent except for the drip of her coat.

"Read it aloud," Lady Vane said to Rosalind. "I want to hear it in a voice that isn't shaking."

So Rosalind read it. Her voice was level and clear and cold as a sluice-gate, and the words came out into the warm kitchen and sat there like iron:

*Dear Halliwell — Further to our conversation of Tuesday last. You may assure your principals that the matter of the vessel's disposal will be concluded before the renewal quarter-day, and that the regrettable incident need reflect upon no party concerned. The timing relative to the labour difficulties at the port is fortuitous and will, we agree, serve to direct public attention where it is wanted. The surveyor has been accommodated. The manifest adjustments will be handled by our man aboard — he has been promised consideration for his family, which consideration we shall honour through the usual channels. Burn this.*

"'Our man aboard,'" Lady Vane repeated, when the silence had settled again. "A clerk in the manifest office. Promised consideration for his family." She looked at Ines, and for the first time her magistrate's stare faltered into something human. "They paid him to help sink his own ship."

"They paid him to arrange the papers so the wrong cargo showed in the holds and the right cargo showed in the books," Ines said. "And then they sank the ship with him aboard it, because dead men collect no consideration, and because a clerk who can adjust a manifest is a clerk who can testify. The 'usual channels' sent me eleven pounds and a printed card expressing sympathy of the Company." She heard her own voice go flat and strange. "Eleven pounds. I worked out once what that comes to per drowned man. I stopped working it out."

* * *

Rosalind had not moved from the end of the table. Ines could feel her watching—had felt it since the doorway, that locked-drawer attention, taking inventory of her the way she herself took inventory of rooms: exits, witnesses, weight of the cutlery.

"Why come to Leeds?" Rosalind asked. "Why come here, to this house? Why not the newspapers, the Board of Trade, the police?"

"Because I did try the newspapers." Ines counted it off on her fingers, tired now, the tea cold, the fire drawing her closer though she refused to move toward it and show weakness. "Two of them. The first reporter listened beautifully and then excused himself and did not return; two days later a man stood outside my lodgings for four hours. The second didn't listen at all—he looked at the letter and went white and told me to leave Southampton and never come back, and gave me the name of the only man in England who might touch it. Nathaniel Quill. Late of the *Chronicle*, now writing shipping intelligence out of Southampton under a name that isn't quite his own, because he was ruined for asking questions about—"

"—the *Atlas*," Rosalind finished.

"You know him."

"I know of him. Lady Vane knows of him. Continue."

"And Quill, I'm told, won't print without corroboration and won't survive another ruin, so the letter must go to him hand to hand, with testimony behind it, before—" She stopped. Drew breath. Said the rest quickly, the way you tear off a plaster. "Before Thursday. Because the *Atlas* was insured, sunk, and paid for, and a ship that has been paid for once can be bought cheap, patched, renamed, and put back on the North Atlantic run. She's in dry dock at Southampton now. Refitting. New name painted and ready—*the Aldwyn*. And on Thursday's tide she sails, and the same men, the same surveyor, the same arrangement of books versus holds goes with her, and my brother's death becomes not one murder but a template."

Lady Vane had gone to the window. Beyond the glass, the wet garden of Leeds lay grey and dripping, and somewhere beyond it the city moved, unaware. When she turned around, her face had settled into the expression Ines had seen in newsprint a hundred times—the one that preceded deputations, arrests, hunger strikes, and trouble.

"How did you find this house?" she asked.

"A shipping order." Ines allowed herself, at last, the smallest smile. "My brother taught me that freight numbers are just words wearing uniforms. The address in Bristol gave me a second address. The second address gave me a name—Vane—and the instruction that the lady who fights does not stop fighting when the fight leaves the newspapers. It said—" and here her voice did break, briefly, and she let it, because some things deserved breaking, "—it said, *she takes in strays.*"

Mrs. Pym made a noise that might have been a cough.

"I take in no such thing," said Lady Vane. Then: "Sit down properly, girl, and drink that tea before it's stone cold. Mrs. Pym, toast, and the good butter. Miss—?" She raised an eyebrow at Ines.

"Ines. Ines Ferro."

"Ferro." Rosalind said it at last, from the end of the table, and Ines realised she hadn't heard the tall woman speak a voluntary word until now. "Iron. Your brother chose well."

"He didn't choose it. It's our father's name. Portuguese, on my mother's side. It means—" 

"I know what it means," Rosalind said quietly, and looked away, and Ines stored that away too, in the growing ledger of oddities about this house: the driver who read Latin, the locked-drawer face, the way she'd said *iron* as though the word had teeth marks on it.

Lady Vane crossed to the table and stood over the letter, hands flat on the wood, reading it a third time through half-lowered lids, and when she spoke it was to nobody—to the letter itself, or perhaps to thirty-one men at the bottom of the Bay of Biscay.

"Stress of weather," she said softly. "They blamed the weather, and they blamed the strike—said agitators among the dockers had interfered with the loading, that careless hands stowed her badly. I remember the inquiry. I remember reading it at breakfast and thinking, *there lies a tidy story, and tidy stories are always bought.*" She straightened. Her jaw, Ines saw, was a weapon in itself. "They used my people as their scapegoat. Striking men wanting decent hours and a living wage, and these gentlemen pinned a drowned crew to their backs like a medal of shame. And now they mean to do it again. On Thursday."

"Yes, my lady," Ines said.

"And you propose to stop it with one letter."

"One letter," Ines said, "and one witness who can swear to her brother's hand, and one journalist who owes the truth a debt, and—" she hesitated, and then spent it, "—and whatever the lady who fights is willing to spend."

For a long moment nobody moved. The kettle ticked. The coat dripped its final drop onto the flagstones.

Then Lady Vane pulled out a chair, sat down like a judge taking the bench, and looked at Rosalind.

"Miss Ravensworth," she said. "How soon could the motor be ready for Southampton?"

* * *

Later—much later, after midnight, after Mrs. Pym had given up pretending not to listen and gone to bed, after Lady Vane had retired to her study with the letter and a magnifying glass and a face like a closing door—Ines lay awake in a narrow guest bed that smelled of lavender and starch, and listened to the strange music of a house where she was, for the moment, safe.

She thought about Tomás. Not the drowned version, the newspaper version, the statistic. The real one: fourteen years old, teaching her to copy documents in fair hand by lamplight in their aunt's kitchen in Hull, saying, *if you can write a thing clearly, little sister, no one can make it untrue.* She thought about him standing on the deck of the *Atlas* adjusting a manifest for men who had already signed his death warrant, and whether he had known—Illusion or certainty, she would never know—which way it would go. She hoped he had known. Hoped he had posted his copy to Hull knowing exactly what it was: not a confession, not a plea, but a loaded gun, waiting eleven months in a cousin's cupboard for a hand steady enough to fire it.

Grief, she had discovered, was not a feeling. It was a fuel. It burned without light for a long time, and then one day someone handed you a match.

She had walked two hundred miles on hers. Tomorrow, if the tall locked-drawer driver and the silver-haired lady had their way, she would ride.

Somewhere below her, a floorboard creaked. Ines slid out of bed and to the window, and looked down through the rain-blurred glass into the yard, where a motor car stood dark and gleaming like a patient animal, and beside it, in the lamplight from the stable door, stood Rosalind Ravensworth—with the oilskin packet in her hands, turning it over slowly, checking the seals, the stitching, the folds, as though it were a thing she already knew how to carry.

And as Ines watched, Rosalind lifted her head—sharp as a creature scenting wind—and looked directly up at the window, straight into her eyes, across forty feet of darkness and rain.

Ines did not move. Neither did Rosalind. And Ines understood, with a cold settling in her stomach that had nothing to do with the weather, that whatever this woman was carrying besides the letter, it weighed more—and that before Thursday's tide, one of them was going to have to ask about it.
