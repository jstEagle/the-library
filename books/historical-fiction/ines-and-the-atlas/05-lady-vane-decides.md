## 5. Lady Vane Decides

Lady Vane did not shout. That was the first thing Rosalind had learned about her, three years ago, and it was the thing that frightened people who didn't know her. Men who had shouted their whole lives—clerics, magistrates, the occasional cabinet minister—expected shouting in return, expected a woman to spend her anger in noise so it could be dismissed as noise. Lady Vane never obliged them. She went quiet, and she went still, and she looked at you the way an auditor looks at a ledger, and somewhere in the silence you realized you had already been weighed.

She was silent now.

She sat in the morning room with the letter open on her knee, the fire burned down to embers because she had not allowed anyone to tend it, and she read Lord Ashcombe's handwriting for the third time from beginning to end. The rain had stopped sometime before dawn but the windows were beaded still, and the light coming through them was grey and watery, and it fell across the page without warming anything.

Rosalind stood by the door. She had learned to stand by doors. It was a chauffeuse's whole education: how to be present and unnoticed, how to hold yourself at the edge of a room like furniture that might, if required, become useful. But this morning the discipline of it chafed. This morning every minute of silence felt like a minute in which the letter grew more dangerous, and Ines sat downstairs in the kitchen drinking tea that Mrs. Harker kept refilling without being asked, and somewhere south of them the *Atlas* rode at anchor with its holds full of lies.

Lady Vane turned the page.

"You've confirmed the hand," she said at last. Not looking up.

"Against the 1911 correspondence. The charity letters." Rosalind kept her voice level. "The loop he puts in the terminal *e* is identical. So is the way he crosses his sevens—he uses two strokes, always has. And the watermark on this paper matches stock we have examples of from his own estate correspondence."

"Which means it's either him or someone who studied him very closely for a very long time."

"Yes, my lady."

"And which do you believe?"

Rosalind hesitated. It was a test, probably. Everything Lady Vane said was a test or a kindness, and after three years Rosalind could usually tell the difference, though the line between them was thinner than people assumed.

"I believe it's him," Rosalind said. "A forger works from samples. Samples give you the hand but not the habits. Whoever wrote this broke his sentences where he breaks them—mid-thought, then a dash, then a fresh start. That's not imitation. That's temperament."

Something moved at the corner of Lady Vane's mouth. Approval, perhaps. She folded the letter along its original creases, carefully, as though it were a living thing that might object to rough handling, and set it on the table beside her, weighted with the cold tea glass.

"Then a man who has been dead for two years wrote a letter arranging the sinking of a second ship," she said, "and blamed it in advance on men whose only crime is asking to be paid a living wage. And he sent it to a business associate who keeps it in an oilskin packet and loses his nerve."

"He didn't lose his nerve," Rosalind said. "He died."

"Conveniently."

"Very. Ines thinks so too. She says men in this conspiracy have developed a habit of dying since the *Atlas* went down."

Lady Vane looked at her then, fully, and Rosalind felt the familiar sensation of being seen straight through—the discomfort of it, and the strange shelter in it, like standing behind a wall that knows you're there.

"You've questioned her."

"As much as I could without making her feel like a suspect."

"And?"

"And her story holds. Her brother Thomas was a purser's clerk. She has his last letter, his effects list, the official finding of accidental death with its tidy little verdict. She has spent two years being told she imagined things. She isn't inventing this now, my lady. No one invents grief into this shape. It's too expensive."

* * *

Lady Vane rose and walked to the window. She did this when she was thinking—stood where the light could get at her face and let it show everything, because she had decided long ago that hiding her mind was a waste of a mind. Rosalind watched her reflection in the wet glass: the strong jaw, the silver hair pinned without vanity, the hands clasped behind her back in the attitude of a woman reviewing troops she intended to lead somewhere unpleasant.

"There is a movement," Lady Vane said. "You will have noticed."

Rosalind almost smiled. "I drive for it four days a week."

"A movement with a name, a membership, a funds account, and a reputation under active siege. There are men in this city and in London who would pay good money to see us destroyed, and I don't mean financially. Two women arrested outside a mill gate last month—one of them nineteen years old—and the papers wrote six hundred words about hysterical females and not one word about the wage figures we were protesting with." She turned from the window. "Now. Consider what happens if this letter goes wrong. Consider what happens if two of my household are found carrying forged documents toward Southampton in possession of accusations against a peer of the realm, a shipping magnate, and—by implication—two departments of His Majesty's government. What do the papers write then?"

"That suffragists fabricate atrocity for publicity."

"That suffragists fabricate atrocity for publicity," Lady Vane agreed. "Every petition we have filed this year becomes suspect. Every witness we have produced. Every woman in a cell in Armley who refused food this week—her sacrifice becomes theatre. They will say we invented drowned sailors to make dockers look martyred." She came back to her chair but did not sit. "I am not permitted the luxury of being right, Miss Ravensworth. I am required to be unimpeachable, which is a great deal more work."

It was the closest thing to fear Rosalind had ever heard from her. She held very still and waited, because she understood that the argument wasn't finished—it had merely been stated from the losing side first, which was Lady Vane's habit. State the case against your own conviction until it stands up or falls down. Never send out an argument you haven't tried to kill.

"The case against acting," Lady Vane continued, "is complete. It is rational and it is sound and any committee in England would sustain it. Now." She picked the letter up again. "The case for."

* * *

She laid it out the way she laid out everything—in numbered steps, unhurried, each one placed like a stone in a wall.

"One. The letter proves the *Ophelia*'s sinking was arranged. Not 'may have been'—arranged, insured through a nominee, timed against the strike, and attributed in advance to sabotage by dockworkers. Two. The same method will be used again; Ashcombe writes here of a 'second application' pending, and the *Atlas* sails Thursday. Three. Thomas Merren died in the first application, along with eleven other men whose families were paid off with verdicts of misadventure. Four. The evidence currently exists in exactly one copy, in this house, in the hands of a dead man's associate who will be missed by whoever inherits his correspondence. Five." She paused. "Five. If it does not leave this house by tonight, it will be worth nothing, because by Friday everyone who might have confirmed it will have conveniently stopped breathing."

She set the letter down.

"Against which: the movement's reputation. Which I am asked to weigh against twelve dead men and however many go into the water on Thursday." Her voice did not rise; it lowered, if anything, until it was almost gentle. "There is no weighing, Miss Ravensworth. That is the trick they rely on—that respectable people can always be persuaded to trade other people's lives for their own standing. I decline to learn the trick."

Rosalind let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

"But," Lady Vane said, "declining the trick is not the same as surviving it. So we do not carry this letter carelessly and we do not carry it bravely. We carry it well. Sit down, please."

Rosalind sat. It occurred to her, distantly, that she had never once been invited to sit in this room. She had stood by doors for three years.

"Who receives it?" Lady Vane asked.

"Nathaniel Quill. The *Southern Evening Dispatch*. Ines's brother corresponded with him—there are letters among Thomas's effects. Quill printed the dock strike coverage the government called inflammatory. He lost his column for a month over it. He's compromised already, which makes him safe; a man who has already paid has less reason to sell you."

"Is he honest or merely ruined?"

"Ines says honest. She says he answered every letter Thomas ever sent, even the angry ones." Rosalind hesitated. "That's not proof, my lady."

"It is better evidence than most marriages rest on." Lady Vane took a small notebook from the drawer—actually Rosalind's notebook, taken from her coat pocket weeks ago and never returned—and uncapped her own pen. "Timescale. The *Atlas* sails Thursday's tide. Today is Monday. That allows us two days of travel and one day of margin, which is not margin, it is decoration. You will leave tomorrow morning."

"Both of us?"

"Do you trust her?"

The question landed harder than its size suggested. Rosalind thought of Ines at the kitchen table, hands wrapped around the teacup, the oilskin packet never more than arm's reach away even while she drank. A woman who had carried a dead man's letter across half of Yorkshire alone.

"Yes," Rosalind said. And then, because invisibility had begun to feel like a coat that no longer fit: "Yes. Completely."

"Then both of you. One woman alone is suspicious; two sisters delivering motor parts for their employer are tedious, and tedium is our best armour. You'll travel as the Thornbury sisters—I'll have documents drawn this afternoon. My cousin's firm supplies marine parts in Leeds; there will be genuine manifests, genuine invoices, a genuine delivery to a genuine chandler in Southampton. Nothing forged except the names, and names are the easiest forgery in England."

"And the letter?"

"Not in the motor. Not in luggage. Somewhere a customs man has no procedure for." Lady Vane's eyes glinted. "You've read enough intercepted correspondence to know how searches think, Miss Ravensworth. Tell me where you'd look."

"Trunk linings. Spare-tyre casings. Door panels. Under floorboards. Inside a woman's person, eventually, if the inspector were thorough and shameless."

"Exactly. So it goes nowhere a search reaches. We will construct something. Mrs. Harker sews better than she cooks and asks fewer questions than anyone in Yorkshire." She made her notes, quick and precise. "Money—you'll draw fifty pounds from the account, in mixed notes. Telegram codes: you know mine. When you reach Southampton, find Quill at the *Dispatch* offices. Do not approach him in public, do not telephone, do not write ahead. Hand it to him and watch his face."

"And if he refuses?"

Lady Vane capped the pen.

"Then you come home and we think of something worse," she said. "But he won't refuse. Men like Quill have been waiting two years for a piece of paper with this shape." She stood, and crossed to Rosalind, and for a moment she did something Rosalind had no precedent for: she put one hand briefly on Rosalind's shoulder. It weighed almost nothing and it changed everything. "Three years you have driven me and read my post and pretended to be furniture. I have known the whole time, you know. About the reading. You have a gift for other people's words, and it has been wasted on my invitations. Spend it on this instead."

Rosalind's throat tightened. She managed, "Yes, my lady."

"Go and tell her. Tell her we're going to Southampton, and that her brother's letter is going to cost some very respectable men their sleep." The glint returned, hard and bright. "And tell her from me: the movement will survive this. Movements survive because once in a generation they stop defending themselves and go on the attack. Thursday, Miss Ravensworth. Beat the tide."

* * *

Down in the kitchen, Ines was asleep in the chair by the stove, the packet under her folded arms like a child's doll, and Mrs. Harker was pretending loudly to scrub a pot that needed nothing. Rosalind stopped in the doorway and watched her—the rain-drowned stranger of yesterday already less a stranger, her face slack with the first real sleep she'd had in days.

Mrs. Harker lowered her voice to a whisper that would have carried across a parade ground. "Shall I wake her?"

"No. Let her sleep. We sail against the tide on Thursday."

Ines opened her eyes anyway—at the word *tide*, or at Rosalind's voice, or at whatever instinct had kept her alive this long—and looked up without moving, without blinking.

"Tell me," she said.

So Rosalind pulled out the second chair, sat down at the kitchen table like a woman with a place at it, and began.
