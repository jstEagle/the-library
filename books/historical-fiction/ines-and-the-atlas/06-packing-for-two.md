## 6. Packing for Two

The letter sat on the kitchen table all night, and by morning it had changed the house.

Not visibly—Lady Vane's townhouse on Park Square kept its composure the way a swan keeps its feet—but I felt it in the way the maids moved, quieter, quicker, as if the very walls had been told something. Rosalind had locked the oilskin packet in her room. Lady Vane had gone upstairs at eleven and not come down again until six, and when she did, she said only: "Thursday's tide. We leave tomorrow."

Which left today for the impossible business of becoming someone else.

I had never packed for a lie before. My whole life, I realized, I had packed for grief—a valise of black things, my brother's photograph, his death certificate folded soft as cloth from handling. Packing for a fiction was different work. It required imagination, and mine had rusted from disuse.

"Sit still," Rosalind said, "or I'll pin your ear."

She was standing behind me at the dressing mirror in the spare room—the good room, third floor, where the morning light came in honest and grey—and she was doing something merciless with hairpins. In the glass, a woman I half-recognized was being assembled out of me: hair coiled under a plain hat, collar high, a woollen coat two shades too sensible. The effect was not disguise exactly. It was subtraction. Everything particular about me was being tucked away.

"Sisters," Rosalind said to the mirror. "That's the story. You're my sister, come along to Southampton because our mother's poorly and I'm the only one who can drive."

"Our mother's poorly," I repeated. "Is she alive?"

"She is now. She lives in Harrogate, she takes her tea at four, and she disapproves of motorcars." Rosalind set the last pin and stepped back, considering me the way she'd consider an engine that had either fixed itself or developed a new noise. "If anyone asks, you complain about the roads. Complaining about roads is what sisters do on long drives. It makes you forgettable."

"I don't want to be forgettable."

"You want very much to be forgettable," she said, "for three days. After that you can be as memorable as you please."

There was something in her voice when she said it—not sharpness, but its shadow—that made me look at her properly instead of at her reflection. She caught me looking and turned away to the wardrobe, pulling out driving coats, and I let her turn, because we had known each other four days and I was learning her habit of presenting her back when her face had too much in it.

* * *

By ten o'clock the morning had organized itself into a campaign, and I understood that this was how Lady Vane fought: not with speeches but with lists.

The dining room table had been cleared of everything except paper, a road atlas, and a tin of shortbread nobody touched. Lady Vane sat at the head like a general who had personally invented logistics. Rosalind stood at the map. I was given a pencil and told to write, which I took to mean they wanted my hands busy and my memory recording everything.

"The motor goes south as a delivery," Lady Vane said. "Rosalind is conveying engine parts to a garage in Southampton—a Mr. Hartley's firm, which has been paid to expect her. Manifests, invoices, all correct, all boring. Boring, girls, is armour. What isn't boring?"

"The letter," I said.

"The letter does not exist." Lady Vane looked at me over her spectacles, and I saw for the first time why men twice her size went pale in meetings with her. "It is not in the house, it is not in the car, it is not on your persons. Where is it, Miss Ferro?"

I hesitated. She waited. Rosalind, at the map, went very still.

"It's sewn into the lining of my coat," I said. "Under the left arm. You taught us—no one searches a woman's armpit, they search her bag, her boot heels, her hat."

A silence. Then Lady Vane smiled, and it transformed her face into something almost girlish, dangerous in an entirely different way.

"Who taught you that?"

"A smuggler in Bilbao. I was nineteen. He was eighty if he was a day, and he was the most honest man I ever met, which tells you something about everyone else I've met."

"Bilbao," Rosalind said quietly, to the map. Her pencil had stopped moving.

"My father shipped ore out of there," I said. "My brother worked the manifests. That's how he came to the Atlas at all—he knew shipping, he trusted shipping, and shipping—" I stopped, because the sentence ended in a place I couldn't go in daylight, in a dining room, next to shortbread. "He trusted it," I finished.

Lady Vane reached across the table and turned my pencil the right way round in my fingers, a small absurd maternal gesture, and said: "Write this down. Southampton. Garage of Hartley & Sons, Blue Anchor Lane. Delivery of parts for a twelve-horse Wolseley. You are Miss Ravensworth and Miss Ferro, sisters, of Leeds. Your mother is unwell in Harrogate; you are combining the delivery with a visit to an aunt in Bournemouth. Aunt's name—"

"Pemberton," said Rosalind.

"Aunt Pemberton," I wrote, and my handwriting came out steady, which surprised me.

* * *

In the afternoon we built the car into a lie, and I learned that Rosalind Ravensworth could swear fluently, comprehensively, and with real artistry when a crankcase refused to cooperate.

The Daimler stood in the mews with its bonnet up and its rear seat stripped, and around it Rosalind had arranged a theatre of mechanical honesty: crates stencilled with a parts firm's name, straw packing, a toolbox bolted down, grease where grease would plausibly be. She worked in a man's canvas apron over her dress with her sleeves rolled, and there was something in watching her—capable, absorbed, entirely unperformed—that loosened something in my chest I'd thought was knotted for good.

"Hold that," she said. I held it. "Tighter. It's a spare tyre well, not a suggestion." I held it tighter. She fed the strap through, cinched it, tested it with both hands, and nodded. "Now the tyre goes back on top, ordinary as anything, and underneath—" she patted the casing "—our aunt Pemberton travels first class."

"The letter rides in a tyre," I said.

"The letter rides in the most boring object in England. Nobody in the history of motoring has ever searched a spare tyre." She wiped her hands on the apron and looked at the car with the expression of an artist stepping back from a canvas. "Customs will open the crates. Let them. Genuine parts, genuine invoices. They'll feel clever, find nothing, and wave us through annoyed with themselves for wasting an hour."

"And if they don't feel clever?"

"Then we improvise." She said it lightly, but I had seen her face last night, in the lamplight, when Lady Vane read Lord Ashcombe's handwriting aloud—the dead man's hand describing, in the blandest commercial prose, the arrangement of a ship's drowning. Improvise meant something heavier coming from her.

"Rosalind." I picked up a spanner just to have something in my hands. "Why are you doing this? Truly. Lady Vane I understand—it's her life's war. But you drive a car. You could say no."

She was quiet long enough that I heard a horse stamp two streets away.

"Because I read the letters," she said finally. "All of them, every week, though I'm not supposed to. Petitions from widows. Men begging for their jobs back. Women writing about things no one else will hear." She pulled the apron string loose with a single tug. "I've spent years being the person who knows everything and can do nothing about any of it. You arrived with a letter I could actually do something about." A pause. "Also, whoever killed your brother forged documents that passed through offices like the ones Ashcombe writes from, and I would like very much to watch them burn for it."

"That's not nothing," I said.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

We ate supper in the kitchen, which Lady Vane pronounced scandalous and then joined anyway, and it was there—over cold ham and a pot of tea kept going by a maid pretending not to listen—that the day stopped being strategy and started being something else.

Lady Vane told a story about 1908, being thrown into a police van in Manchester with five other women and one very indignant hat, and she did impressions, and I laughed—actually laughed, the sound surprising me out loud like a cough in church. Rosalind watched me laugh with an expression I couldn't read, and when I caught her at it she didn't look away the way she usually did. She held it a moment, then passed me the bread.

Afterwards she walked me up to the third floor, carrying a candle because she claimed electric light made her think badly, and at my door she stopped.

"Fittings," she said. "Try the coat."

I put on the grey travelling coat, and she checked the seam under my left arm with two fingers, professional as a tailor, then suddenly wasn't professional at all—her hand rested there a second longer than seams require, over the place where her brother-in-grief's last proof lay flat against my ribs.

"He was twenty-six," I said. "Tomás. You never asked his name."

"I didn't want to make you carry it out loud."

"It's lighter out loud."

Rosalind nodded slowly. "Then tell me about him on the road. All of it. We'll have forty hours of driving and I only know how to talk about engines." She stepped back, candle guttering, her face half gold and half dark. "Goodnight, Miss Ferro."

"Ines," I said. "If we're sisters, you may as well start now."

Something crossed her face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning—and she said, "Ines," testing it, and my name sounded different in her mouth than it had in anyone's for two years. Warmer. Like it belonged to a living woman.

"Goodnight, Rosalind."

She went down the stairs. I closed the door, took off the coat, and laid it across the chair with the letter sleeping in its arm. Through the window I could see the mews, and the Daimler waiting in the dark with its patient cargo, ready to carry a dead man's handwriting two hundred miles to the sea.

Sleep came slow. When it came, it brought the dream it always brought—the water, the manifest, Tomás's name in print—and then, for the first time, altered it: someone stood beside me in the dream, in a driving coat, holding a lantern against the dark water.

I woke at five with the light still off and the house silent, and dressed quickly, and that was when I heard it: voices below, low and urgent, in the hall. Rosalind's, tight with alarm. And another voice answering hers—one I didn't know, a man's voice, smooth as oiled leather, saying words that stopped my blood mid-turn:

"—asked after the girl, not you, Miss Ravensworth. The dark girl. The one from Bilbao."
