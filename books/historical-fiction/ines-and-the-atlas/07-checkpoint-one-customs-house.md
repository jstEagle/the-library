## 7. Checkpoint One: Customs House

The forged manifests were, in my professional opinion, rather good.

I had written them myself, at the kitchen table of Vane House at half past one in the morning, with Ines asleep in a chair beside me and her mouth slightly open and her hands still curled around an empty teacup as though someone might take it from her. I had copied the letterhead from a genuine consignment note I'd found in the estate files—Hartley & Sons, Motor Components, Bradford—and I had aged the paper over the stove and smudged the ink where a clerk's thumb would naturally fall. Lady Vane had looked over my shoulder and said nothing, which from her was applause.

Two years of reading other people's correspondence teaches you how documents breathe. You learn that official paper lies in a hundred small ways—the too-careful signature, the date that falls on a Sunday, the ink that's uniform when honest ink pools and fades. My forgeries breathed. I had made them tired. I had made them bored.

What I could not make them was true.

"Drive forward," said the customs man.

Leeds Central goods yard, seven-forty in the morning, a sky the colour of dishwater. The Hupmobile stood third in a queue of lorries, and the inspector who had waved the first two through now stood at my wing with a clipboard and the particular stillness of a man who has found something he doesn't yet know how to name. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with a moustache trimmed so precisely it looked drawn on each morning by another person. His name badge read MR. PRITCHARD, and beneath the badge his eyes were doing arithmetic.

"Morning," I said, and made my voice the flat, faintly bored instrument I'd spent two years perfecting. Chauffeuses are furniture with gloves. Nobody remembers furniture.

"Morning." He didn't look at me. He looked at the motor—mud on the wings, oil on the running board, a woman's driving, and he filed that away too. "Consignment?"

"Motor parts. Magneto assemblies and axle casings, Hartley and Sons, Bradford, down to a garage in Southampton." I handed him the manifest through the window, and my glove did not tremble, because I had instructed it not to, and my hands are obedient. "They're wanting it before the weekend."

He took the paper. And here is what nobody tells you about terror: it is not the pounding heart or the cold hands. It is time. Terror stretches three seconds into three hours, and you must live in every one of those hours while a stranger reads your lies in his own good time.

Pritchard read. His eye went down the columns—item, quantity, weight, value—and stopped.

"You've written eight magneto assemblies here."

"Yes."

"And eleven axle casings."

"That's right."

"Mm." He lowered the paper and looked at me for the first time. "Odd numbers."

The world narrowed to the width of a clipboard.

"Is it?" I said.

"It is if you know anything about crating." He tapped the manifest. "Magnetos ship in fours to a crate. Always have. Axles in pairs. Whoever wrote this list doesn't pack motor parts for a living." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "So who does pack them for a living, miss?"

And this is the moment I want to be honest about, because everyone else in this account will tell you I was calm. My mind went white. Not blank—white, like a page held up to a lamp, and behind it, very faintly, the shadow of everything I had ever read.

Eighteen thousand letters. Give or take. Petitions and poison-pens, love notes and ledgers, ministers' confessions and merchants' boasts, all of it passing through my hands on its way to Lady Vane's desk, all of it mine first. I had sworn never to use it. I had built my whole quiet life on never using it.

I used it.

"Hartley's don't pack them at all anymore," I said. "Not since the strike shut the Leeds depot in March. They sub-contract out now—there's a firm does their crating on the cheap, and they can't count. That's why the manifest looks like that." I let one degree of irritation into my voice, chauffeur-to-chauffeur, man-of-the-world to furniture-with-gloves. "My employer's garage raised it with them twice. Nothing came of it. Nothing ever does."

Pritchard considered this. The arithmetic behind his eyes carried on, columns adding and cancelling.

"March," he said. "You'd know the firm's name."

"Brannigan's," I said, which was the name on a shipping complaint I had read in February, addressed to no one important, about damaged crates at the Leeds depot—a complaint signed, as it happened, by a foreman named Brannigan whose handwriting suggested a man who counted badly. "Ask anyone on the yard."

"I might just do that."

"Do," I said. "Though we're late already, and Mr. Hartley will dock my wage before he docks yours."

Beside me, Ines sat motionless under the borrowed cap and coat, her sister-costume abandoned at the checkpoint's edge because two women travelling alone invited questions and a mechanic did not. She was looking straight ahead at the customs shed with the fixed attention of a woman memorizing every exit. Her breathing was even. Her hands, folded in her lap, were not. I could see the pulse jumping in her wrists, quick as birds.

Pritchard stepped back from the wing and walked slowly along the side of the motor, and I understood that we had passed one examination only to be entered for another. He looked at the mud. He looked at the rear spare mounted on the back—the fat, bandaged bulk of it, inside which, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with bicycle tape, Lord Ashcombe's letter lay against the inner casing like a heart inside a ribcage. He looked at the tool roll. He lifted the tarpaulin over the rear seat, regarded the crates we had genuinely bought from a Bradford scrapyard to fill the weight, and set it back down.

"Open the spare," he said.

There is a version of this story where I argue, and arguing kills us. There is a version where I hesitate, and hesitation kills us. What I actually did was laugh—short, genuine, exhausted, because some animal part of my brain had decided the situation was funny and overridden the rest of me.

"If you want to change a tyre on this yard in those trousers," I said, "you're a braver man than Mr. Hartley's ever employed. It's been patched twice. Smell it—you'll get a lungful of vulcanized rubber and a story for the public house."

He didn't smell it. But he put his hand flat against the spare's casing, the way men do, testing, and the letter sat six inches from his palm wrapped in oilcloth inside the casing's old puncture, and I stood there in the dishwater morning and thought, with perfect clarity: *this is where it ends, then. Here, at the beginning.*

His hand came away.

"Right," Pritchard said. He stamped the manifest—two strokes, hard, the second harder than the first, as though the paper had personally offended him. "Southampton, you said."

"Southampton."

"Mind you get there." He handed the paper back and his eyes came up to mine, and for one moment something moved behind the precision of him—not suspicion, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has played cards all his life and has just decided, on insufficient evidence, not to call a hand he suspects is bluffing. Or perhaps I invented it. Perhaps I needed to invent it. "Yard gate's to your left. Don't run anybody down."

I did not thank God, because I had stopped believing in Him somewhere around my fourteenth year, but I performed the secular equivalent: I released the brake with exaggerated care, engaged the gear, and drove out of Leeds Central goods yard at a pace no fleeing woman has ever managed—fifteen miles an hour, both hands on the wheel, spine straight as a hymnal—while Ines sat beside me not moving and not speaking until we had cleared the gate, turned onto the road, gone a quarter mile, a half, a full mile, until the yard and its clipboards and its drawn-on moustache were swallowed by the morning behind us.

Then she spoke.

"How," she said, "did you know about Brannigan's?"

"I read a letter."

"What letter?"

"A complaint about damaged crates. February. A man named Brannigan signed it, and his figures didn't add up, and I remember thinking at the time that here was a man who should never be trusted with arithmetic." I kept my eyes on the road. "It seemed useless information. Most of it seems useless. Then one day it isn't."

Ines was quiet for a long moment. When I glanced across, she was studying me with an expression I would come to know very well in the years after—the look of a woman recalculating what she was sitting next to.

"You read all of them," she said. It wasn't a question. "Every letter you carry. Even the ones you're not supposed to."

"The ones I'm *especially* not supposed to," I said. "Those are the interesting ones."

"And you keep it all. Everything. In your head."

"There's no room anywhere else."

Another mile. The road unspooled south between hedges still wet from overnight rain, and the engine ticked beneath the bonnet like something cooling from a fire.

"He knew," Ines said finally. "That man at the gate. Pritchard. He knew the numbers were wrong, and he let us go anyway."

"Perhaps he was stupid."

"He wasn't stupid."

"No," I said. "He wasn't stupid."

We drove on through Wetherby, through Ferrybridge, the sun making a weak attempt at the horizon and giving up. At Ackworth I stopped for petrol, and while the boy worked the pump Ines unwrapped a corner of the spare and checked the letter with fingers that moved faster than her face, and found it dry, and rewrapped it, and said nothing, and I watched her do it and understood that I had acquired something in the last twenty-four hours that I had not planned for and did not know how to store: a person.

It was at Doncaster, at the railway hotel where we took tea in the lounge because Ines refused to sit with her back to a window, that the day turned again.

She saw him first. She has better eyes than I do, and older reasons for using them. A man in a bowler hat, reading a newspaper he wasn't reading, seated where he could watch both doors of the lounge. Grey coat, brown boots, a face you forgot while looking at it—which is itself memorable, once you know what forgetting looks like.

"Don't turn round," Ines said, very quietly, into her teacup.

I turned round, because I am imperfect.

The newspaper lowered an inch. The face above it was ordinary and patient and wrong, and it was fixed not on Ines, not on the spare tyre outside with the motor, not on the letter.

It was fixed on me.

And there was a moment—I want to be precise about this—there was a moment of perhaps two seconds in which the man in the bowler hat and I looked directly at one another across a hotel lounge in Doncaster, and in his face there was no curiosity at all. Only satisfaction. The look of a man who has found precisely what he was sent to find, and is thinking, quite calmly, about telegrams.

He raised his newspaper. I set down my tea.

"Ines," I said. "We need to leave. Now. And I need to tell you something on the way about why I think that man knows my face—and why I'm afraid I know his."
