## 8. Night Train South

The train pulled out of Leeds at eleven minutes past nine, and by the time we cleared the last signal gantry I had learned three things about Rosalind Ravensworth: she sat with her back never quite touching the seat, she counted the stations as we passed them under her breath, and she lied beautifully about small things.

That last one I knew already. I'd known it since the customs house.

We had a first-class compartment to ourselves—Lady Vane's money bought that much, and her name on the booking bought the rest, because nobody looked twice at a reservation made by a woman whose photograph hung in every newspaper office between here and Land's End. Rosalind had changed at the station into travelling clothes that made her look like what she was pretending to be: a chauffeuse going south to collect a motor car. Sensible tweed. Sensible hat. A leather case of forged manifests in the rack above her head and an oilskin packet sewn into the lining of her coat, where it sat over her ribs like a second heart.

I wore her sister's face. That was the joke of it—we weren't sisters, we weren't anything, we'd known each other four days—and still, when the guard asked *Miss Ravensworth, and your companion?*, Rosalind said "my sister" without a flicker, and something in my chest turned over and would not settle.

The lamp above the seat threw everything amber. Rain worked at the window in long diagonal strokes. Somewhere behind us, Leeds was shrinking into its own smoke.

"Say it," Rosalind said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been composing since we boarded. You've got a speech in you. I can see it sitting there like a stone."

I hadn't meant to say it yet. I'd meant to wait until Southampton, until the letter was delivered, until it no longer mattered. But the train did something to me—the dark rushing past, the compartment sealed like a confession box—and out it came before I could weigh it.

"You know my brother's name," I said. "You said so at the kitchen table. You said you'd read his file. But that's not how you knew him, is it."

She didn't move. The lamp caught the edge of her cheekbone and left the rest of her in shadow.

"How did you know him?" I said. "Because he knew *you*. There's a paper in his things—a list of names, written in his own hand, names of people who might help if ever he needed helping. And yours is on it. *R. Ravensworth, care of Lady E. Vane, Leeds.* Underlined twice. Why would a clerk on a cargo steamer two hundred miles away have your name underlined twice?"

For a long moment the only sound was the wheels. *Da-da-dun. Da-da-dun.* The rhythm of a thing being decided.

"May I ask you something first?" she said.

"No. You've had four days of asking."

A ghost of a smile. "Fair." She unclasped her hands, folded them again, the way people do when they're buying time with their fingers. Then: "Emiliano sent me a letter. In March of 1912. Six weeks before the Atlas went down."

The compartment seemed to tilt with the rails. "*Emiliano*? You called him—" I stopped. My brother's name, spoken in another woman's voice, in a first-class carriage smelling of other people's cigars. I gripped my own hands together hard enough to hurt.

"He wrote to Lady Vane," Rosalind said. "Care of her. He'd read about her in the papers—the speeches, the deputations. Half the country had. He wrote because he'd found something aboard his ship and didn't know where else to take it. I opened the post, as I do. Every letter, every day. I read it standing in the hall with the rain coming down outside, and I thought, this poor frightened man has written to the wrong address. So I answered him myself. Four letters, over five weeks. I told him to copy the manifest pages. I told him who at the Board of Trade was safe to approach and who wasn't. I told him—" Her voice caught, very slightly, and smoothed itself flat again. "I told him not to sail on the April run. I begged him."

The world rearranged itself around those words. Five years of grief, and someone had been holding the other end of it the whole time.

"He didn't tell me any of this," I said. "He wrote to me every week. Chatty letters. The weather aboard. A man in the stokehold who sang hymns off-key. Not one word of danger."

"Because he was protecting you."

"Because he didn't trust me!" It came out louder than I meant. The corridor door rattled somewhere down the train; footsteps passed; faded. I lowered my voice. "He thought I was careless. He used to say I'd lose my own head if it weren't screwed on. So he tells a *stranger*—"

"He tells a stranger because a stranger had nothing to lose." Rosalind's eyes were steady on mine. "That's not carelessness, Ines. That's arithmetic. I was invisible. No husband, no father anyone could threaten him with, no name in any register that mattered. You were his sister. You were the soft place where a knife goes."

I turned to the window. My own face floated there against the rushing dark, ghostly, superimposed on miles of black country. I thought of his letters, all of them, reread so many times the folds had gone furry. All that weather. All those hymns. And underneath it, apparently, a whole war I'd never been allowed to see.

"The letters," I said finally. "Do you still have them?"

"Yes."

"I want them."

"They're in Leeds. In a biscuit tin behind the third-floor water tank." Almost, almost a smile. "I hide things too. You're not the only one."

* *

The guard came through at Doncaster checking tickets, and we performed our sisterhood for him—I let my shoulder rest against hers, she called me *poppet* with a straight face—and after he'd gone we sat in the new silence of two women recalibrating.

It was I who broke it, because there was a larger question and I was too tired to leave it overnight.

"If you were writing to Emil," I said, "then you knew the Atlas was dangerous before it sank. Did you warn anyone? Did Lady Vane know?"

"Lady Vane knew nothing. She knows now—that's why we're on this train." Rosalind looked at her hands. "Did I warn anyone? I wrote back to Emil. I wrote to a man at the Board of Trade whose name I'd seen in three separate letters and judged honest—he never replied. I drafted something for the newspapers and Lady Vane's solicitor talked me out of posting it. No proof, he said. A dead clerk's suspicions, he said. Libel, he said." Her jaw set. "And then the Atlas sank in April, exactly where and how Emil feared, and eleven men drowned, and every newspaper in England printed *tragic accident*, and I sat in the motor outside a memorial service and understood that I had helped kill them."

"That's not true."

"It's what I've believed for two years. It's why I memorize every letter instead of merely reading them. I thought if I became perfectly useful, perfectly invisible, perhaps the ledger would balance." She said it without drama, the way you'd report the time of day, and somehow that made it worse. "So when you walked into the kitchen with his handwriting on that envelope, Miss Cardoso, you were carrying the answer to a question I have asked myself every night since April 1912. You were carrying my acquittal or my sentence. Of course I recognized the stakes. I'd been living inside them."

The lamp hummed. Outside, Peterborough lights slid past like something drowning.

"And the underlining," I said. "Twice. On your name. What does twice mean?"

"It means he trusted me more than he wanted to." She was quiet a moment. "Or that he was afraid of how much."

I believed her. That was the trouble. I wanted to disbelieve her—to find the seam in the story and pull—but there was no seam. Every fact locked into the facts I already carried. My brother, the careful one, the protector, choosing the one correspondent in England who couldn't be reached through him. It was exactly the sort of thing Emil would do. It was so exactly the sort of thing Emil would do that my eyes filled, unexpectedly, furiously, and I had to look at the ceiling and count the rivets until they went away.

Rosalind pretended not to notice. It was the kindest thing anyone had done for me in two years.

* *

Somewhere past Grantham the carriage grew cold and we spread our coats across both our knees, hers over mine because hers was thicker, and the oilskin packet crackled faintly between us like a sleeping animal neither of us dared wake.

"Tell me about the hiding," she said, into the dark. "Yours. You know mine now—or part of it. A woman doesn't cross half of Portugal and then half of England on her own unless she's running from something specific."

"Who says I crossed half of Portugal?"

"Your accent does. It goes northern when you're tired—you've been living up north long enough to catch it, but it comes home when you're worn through. Your brother's letters mentioned Lisbon. And you checked the corridor twice before we boarded and once since, which means somebody taught you to check corridors, and it wasn't a convent."

I laughed despite myself—a short, surprised sound. "You really do read everything."

"It's the only skill I have."

So I told her. Not all of it—not the worst of it—but some. My father's printing press in Lisbon, closed by the police in '09 for printing things the government disliked. My flight north on an English freighter, working passage, then Leeds, then mill work, then worse mill work. The name I'd shed like a coat at the border. I watched her listen the way she must have listened to Emil's letters: completely, cataloguing, missing nothing, judging nothing aloud.

"And the man in the bowler," she said quietly. "On the platform at Leeds. You saw him too."

I hadn't, but I nodded anyway, because I saw her face, and her face told me everything: he was real, and she knew him, and knowing him frightened her in a way that had nothing to do with the letter.

"Rosalind." I said her name deliberately, for the first time, and watched it land. "If this person is hunting *you* and not the letter—if we're walking into Southampton trailing your past behind us like a scent—you need to tell me before we get there. Not after. I can't protect you from a thing I don't know exists."

She was silent so long the train stopped at a station and started again, and I never learned its name.

"There are things about me," she said at last, slowly, feeling each word before releasing it, "that I have not said aloud in eleven years. And one of them is currently sitting in first class on this very train, or was at King's Cross, and will be aboard the Atlas by Thursday if I don't stop him. And when I tell you what it is—and I will, Ines, before Southampton, I swear it—you may decide you want nothing further to do with me. I want you to hear it from me rather than from him."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It's a promise. It's all I have tonight."

The lamp guttered once and steadied. She pulled her coat higher over our knees. And because I was tired, and grieving, and because she had begged my brother not to sail in April 1912 and had sat alone with that failure for two years, I found—to my considerable irritation—that I believed this promise too.

"Sleep," she said. "I'll take first watch."

"You don't trust me to watch?"

"I don't trust *anyone* to watch," she said, "which is different. Sleep, poppet."

I closed my eyes. The wheels kept their rhythm. *Da-da-dun.* And it was nearly morning when her voice came again, low, awake, urgent, her hand suddenly hard on my wrist:

"Ines. Wake up. The man in the bowler hat just walked past our corridor door."
