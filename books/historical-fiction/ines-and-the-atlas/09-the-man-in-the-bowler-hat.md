## 9. The Man in the Bowler Hat

London arrived in stages: first the smell of coal and wet cinders seeping under the carriage door, then the grey light of a February dawn sliding across Ines's sleeping face, and finally the long shriek of the brakes as King's Cross swallowed the train whole.

I had not slept. I had spent the night watching the corridor through the gap in the compartment door, cataloguing every man who walked it—the commercial traveller with his sample case, the two soldiers home on leave, the elderly clergyman who read his paper upside down and therefore was only tired, not dangerous. By three o'clock I had memorized the timetable of our fellow passengers' snoring. By four I had composed, in my head, three separate explanations for who we were and why we carried what we carried, and discarded all three as insufficient.

By five I had made peace with the fact that I would not sleep until this was over, or until I was dead, whichever came first.

"Rosalind." Ines surfaced from sleep the way she did everything—abruptly, without apology. Her hand went at once to her breast pocket, where the oilskin packet rode against her heart. Finding it there, she exhaled and looked out the window at the iron ribs of the station roof sliding past. "London."

"London," I agreed.

"You didn't sleep."

"How could you tell?"

"Because you have the look of a woman who has been rehearsing arguments with people who aren't there." She pulled her hat lower over her brow. It was one of Lady Vane's, a good grey felt that suited her better than it had ever suited Lady Vane, and beneath its brim her eyes had that flat, settled quality I was beginning to know—the look she wore when grief put on work clothes and went out to labour. "Is anyone there? People who aren't but also are?"

"One or two." I gathered our cases. "Stay close to me at the barrier. Don't speak unless someone speaks first. And if I take your arm suddenly—in any direction—you go. No questions."

"No questions," she repeated, and for once she didn't smile when she said it.

* * *

King's Cross at half past seven in the morning was a river in flood. Porters surged like pike; soldiers and sailors moved in clumps, loud with the particular laughter of men about to be separated from England; a woman with six crates of live chickens created her own weather near the ticket office. Through it all ran the steam, thick and white and sulphurous, pooling under the great clock and bleeding upward into the glass roof so that the whole station seemed to be exhaling.

I had been in this station perhaps forty times in two years of driving Lady Vane to and from her various campaigns, and I knew its geography the way I knew the geography of my own face—intimately, uncritically, without ever quite looking at it fresh. There were four exits worth using. The main concourse onto Euston Road, which was where everyone went and therefore where no one intelligent should go. The side gate onto York Way used by railway staff, guarded by a porter whose bribe rate I happened to know. The taxi rank on Pancras Road. And the long tunnel through to St Pancras next door, where another station's crowds might absorb us like a drop of ink into milk.

We came off the platform with the crowd, heads down, Ines a step behind me carrying the second case. The oilskin packet was not in either case. It was in a plain brown paper wrapper, tied with butcher's string, inside a newspaper folded under my arm. A letter travels best dressed as something worthless.

I did not look for him. That is the first rule. Looking is a kind of advertisement; the eye finds what it seeks and betrays the seeking. Instead I let my attention move the way it had learned to move during years of sitting in motor cars outside hotels and offices and private houses, watching without appearing to watch—peripheral, patient, taking in the whole room as texture rather than reading any single figure as text.

He was at the barrier. Third position from the left, beneath the departures board, wearing a bowler hat and an overcoat too good for his shoulders and an expression of magnificent, weaponized boredom. He held no newspaper. No case. Men who stand in stations holding nothing are either waiting for lovers or hunting strangers, and lovers do not stand so still.

He was perhaps fifty, heavy through the chest, greying at the temples, with the ruddy, well-fed complexion of a man who lunched well. Nothing about him said police. Police announce themselves even in plain clothes—it is a professional affliction, the posture of authority leaking through the seams. This man's stillness was different. Softer. Older. It was the stillness of someone doing a job he had done many times, for reasons of his own.

He scanned faces as they passed the barrier. He was not scanning them randomly. His eyes moved in a pattern—left to right, hat line to chin line—and every fourth or fifth face he paused on, half a second, and dismissed.

I turned us sideways, toward the bookstall, and bought a penny paper we did not need.

"We're changing stations," I murmured to Ines over the rack of periodicals.

"I gathered. Which one?"

"St Pancras. Through the tunnel. But not directly."

"Not directly," she repeated. She had gone very quiet, the way she did when fear arrived and she made room for it without letting it drive. "You've seen him."

"At the barrier. Bowler hat. He'll be looking for a woman travelling alone or in pairs, young enough to matter, dressed like clerks or companions." I tucked the wrapped packet more securely under my arm. "Which describes approximately four hundred women passing through this station before nine o'clock."

"Then we simply become two of the four hundred."

"That is precisely the plan."

The trouble with plans executed in crowds is that crowds are made of individuals, and individuals dawdle, stop, reverse, and block. We left the bookstall and moved with the flow toward the main concourse—not because we meant to leave by it, but because a fish that flees is caught, while a fish that swims calmly with the shoal past the net and then turns aside is merely water. Behind us, I felt rather than saw the bowler hat peel away from the barrier and follow.

There is a sensation known, I imagine, only to the hunted and the driver: the awareness of another vehicle in your mirror holding your exact speed, keeping your exact distance, matching every choice you make. It is not sight. Sight would be easier. It is inference, cold and continuous, a calculation running underneath your heartbeat: *still there. Still there. Still there.*

"He's following," Ines said. She had not turned her head. Clever girl.

"Yes."

"Through the main concourse?"

"Through everything, if we let him. So we don't let him." I took her arm, as promised, and turned us sharply left through a gap between a luggage trolley and a party of Welsh choristers, into the mouth of the side passage that led to the parcels office.

The passage was dim and smelled of tallow and rope. Halfway along it stood the parcels counter, and at the counter stood a queue of eight people arguing about a crate of oysters. Beyond them, a door stood open to the cab road, where a line of growlers and taxicabs idled in the drizzle.

Here is what I knew, from two years of letters read silently and filed away in the vault behind my eyes: that the Metropolitan Police kept a list of known anarchists and suffragists and photographed both indiscriminately, and that photographs circulate. Here is what else I knew: that whoever hunts a woman coming off a night train from Leeds does not know which woman, or he would be at the platform gate itself and not at the barrier playing at chance. He had a description. Descriptions fail. Faces do not—but only faces *he has seen*.

That was the thought that should have warned me. It brushed past me in the dark of the passage like a stranger's coat, and I let it go, because I was busy.

"Ines. The parcels queue. Stand in it. Hold my glove." I stripped off my right glove and pressed it into her hand. "You are furious with me. You missed the train at Peterborough because I was buying magazines. When you reach the counter, demand to send this case forward to Paris, insist it must travel today, and be unreasonable about it."

"What are you going to do?"

"Be water." I gave her the case. "Two minutes. Then walk out the cab-road door and take the third cab. I will find you at St Pancras, under the Barlow roof, by the departure board. If I am not there in twenty minutes, take the packet and go to Southampton alone. You know the rest of the plan. You can finish it."

Her jaw set. For a moment I saw her weigh refusing—she had lost everyone she had ever trusted, and here was I, instructing her to trust me with the last hour of it. Then she looked at the glove in her hand, absurd and small, and something in her face decided.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "And Rosalind—" She stopped. Whatever it was, she swallowed it, turned, and joined the oyster argument like a woman with a grievance and a case and no other business in the world.

* * *

A station, properly understood, is a machine for making people interchangeable. Every crowd is a costume. The trick is not to hide but to change—to pass through one identity and come out the far side wearing another.

I crossed the concourse openly. Let him mark me, if he marked anyone: a tall woman in a serviceable coat, walking briskly, carrying nothing, a woman with somewhere to be. I passed within fifteen feet of him. He glanced at me the way one glances at furniture. I felt the glance slide off, and something in my chest unclenched by a fraction, and then—

Then it slid back.

His head turned. Not fully—a correction, the small involuntary adjustment of a man who has seen something at the edge of vision and is not yet sure what. His eyes followed me past him, and though I kept my stride even and my face arranged into mild municipal purpose, I knew with a certainty that dropped through me like cold water down a well:

*He knows my face.*

Not a description. Not *tall, dark-haired, mid-thirties, travelling with a companion*. My face. The specific architecture of it, the nose I got from my mother, the set of the jaw I got from the man I have spent eleven years pretending I do not share blood with. Somewhere in some drawer or album or private memory, that face had been shown to him, studied by him, priced by him—and he had found it here, walking past him out of a Leeds train, and the world had tilted three degrees off its axis.

I did not run. Running is confession. I walked to the ladies' waiting room, paid my penny, went in, and stood a moment with my back against the door, breathing.

Eleven years ago I had died. Officially, tidily, with a coroner's verdict and a grave in a churchyard in Kent that bore my name and someone else's bones—or no bones at all; I never asked, because asking felt like opening a door I had nailed shut from the inside. I had been nineteen years old and I had threatened to expose my father, and my father had done what such men do: he had erased me. Not killed me—killing leaves questions, and questions leave papers. He had simply unwritten me, and I had crawled out of my own erasure into service uniforms and motor goggles and the blessed anonymity of being nobody's daughter, nobody's wife, nobody at all.

Nobody is invisible, and invisibility had been the safest country I knew.

Until now. Until a drowned man wrote letters and a grieving stranger knocked on the service door, and I drove out of my safe country with a dead lord's handwriting under my arm—straight back across the border into the country I had fled, where somebody, evidently, kept my photograph.

*Someone from her hidden past is hunting.* The words assembled themselves in my head with the terrible tidiness of a headline. And they led, by a road I refused yet to walk, to one name. There was only ever going to be one name. My father's partners were sinking ships and blaming dead dockworkers; their reach had already touched the Atlas, the customs men, God knew what else. Of course it could touch a bowler hat. Of course it could hunt a ghost. My father had taught me himself that the erased can be re-found, if you keep looking, if you keep paying, if you never believe the story you told the world.

I looked at myself in the waiting-room mirror: a pale woman with shadows under her eyes and rain-flecked shoulders, entirely ordinary, entirely see-through. Eleven years of careful unwriting, undone in half a second at a ticket barrier.

Then I did what I do. I fixed my hair differently, pinning it higher. I buttoned my coat to the throat and swapped my scarf for the shawl knotted around my case handle. I turned my collar up, changed my gait—shorter steps, weight on the heels, the walk of a woman with bad ankles—and walked out of the waiting room a slightly different person, and let the crowd drink me.

* * *

The tunnel to St Pancras runs long and curved, lit badly, busy with porters and trolleys. I went through it twice—once halfway, doubling back behind a tea trolley, once all the way through, watching the mouths of the tunnel behind me. Nobody in a bowler hat. Nobody holding nothing. Either I had washed clean of him in the crowd, or he was better than I was, and of the two possibilities I did not care to decide.

Under the great ribs of the Barlow shed, Ines stood exactly where I had told her, beneath the departure board, studying it with the fierce concentration of a woman who has never in her life taken a train for pleasure. When she saw me she did not wave. She simply moved her shoulder a quarter-inch, acknowledging, and waited until I was beside her before speaking.

"The parcel clerk thinks I'm sending linens to my aunt in Boulogne. Also I may have made him late for his supper." She handed back my glove. "Twenty-two minutes. I was starting to count in French."

"Habit of yours?"

"Habit of his. My brother counted everything. Seconds, fares, waves against the sea wall." Something crossed her face and was gone. "You lost him?"

"I think so."

"'Think' is not a word I love this week."

"It's the only honest one available." I checked the board. The Dover boat train left from Platform 3 in fifty minutes; we would take the twelve-twenty south instead, arriving Southampton with hours to spare, approaching from the coast rather than straight down the direct line where anyone clever would wait. "Listen to me, Ines. Before anything else happens, before we take another step of this—I have to tell you something, and it changes things, and you deserve to hear it from me rather than from a man in a bowler hat."

She turned. Under the iron sky of that impossible glass roof, with steam drifting through the shafts of light like weather in another world, she looked at me and waited, and I understood that she had been waiting since Leeds—since the kitchen door, maybe—for exactly this sentence.

"The man at King's Cross wasn't looking for you," I said. "He knew my face. Not my description. My face. Which means someone gave it to him, and there is exactly one person alive who has reason to have my face in his pocket, and that person is the reason I died eleven years ago." My voice stayed level. Some part of me observed this with detached admiration, as though watching a colleague perform. "My name is not Ravensworth. Or rather—it is, but it isn't only that. Rosalind Ravensworth is the name I built on top of the one I was born with, and the man who signed the drowning of that first name owns shares in the same shipping lines as Lord Ashcombe's correspondents, and if he finds me, he will not write me a letter."

Ines was silent for a long moment. Around us the station roared and hissed, indifferent.

"And your father," she said at last, quietly, getting there ahead of me the way she always somehow did, "is one of them."

It was not a question. I answered it anyway.

* * *

Behind us, at the mouth of the tunnel from King's Cross, a man in a bowler hat stepped out of the crowd, bought a newspaper from the barrow, and did not read it.
