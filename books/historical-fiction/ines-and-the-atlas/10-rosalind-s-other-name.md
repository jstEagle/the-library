## 10. Rosalind's Other Name

The boarding house on Beaufort Street had the smell of boiled cabbage and old carpet, and by half past nine it had stopped taking guests. Which meant that when the knock came at ten—soft, deliberate, twice—we both knew it wasn't the landlady.

Ines had the letter in her chemise, against her heart. Rosalind had the poker in her hand.

"Blow out the candle," she whispered.

I did. The room went from amber to grey, lit only by the gas lamp from the street below, which came through the curtain gap in a thin blade and lay across the floorboards like something spilled. Through the wall came the snores of the commercial traveller in the next room, blissful and enormous.

The knock came again. Then a voice, low and pleasant, the kind of voice a man uses to reassure horses.

"Miss Ravensworth. I know you're awake. The whole street heard the motor come in."

Rosalind went very still beside me. In the dark I could feel that stillness the way you feel a change in weather—not a sound or movement but a pressure, a dropping of temperature.

"Miss *Thornbury*," the voice corrected itself, gently, as if offering a gift. "I beg your pardon. Old habits."

The poker did not waver in her hands, but her breath did—one small catch, quickly mastered. I had driven with this woman for three days and slept in the next bed for one night, and I had never once heard her breath catch.

We listened to his footsteps descend the stairs. Not leaving—I understood that even then. He would sit in the parlour, or stand at the door, patient as tide. They were all patient, these men. Patience was what their money bought them.

Rosalind set the poker down against the bedframe with exaggerated care, the way you set down a thing you might need again very soon. Then she crossed to the window and looked through the gap in the curtain for a long moment.

"He's alone," she said.

"You know him."

"Yes."

"Rosalind." I kept my voice low because the walls were paper, but I made it a question anyway. "Who is he?"

She didn't answer immediately. She stood with her hand flat against the window glass, and the blade of lamplight cut across her face, and I watched her make some decision the way I had once watched my brother make the decision to sign things he shouldn't have: you could see the exact moment the cost was accepted.

"Sit down," she said. "It's a long story, and I'd rather tell it sitting."

* * *

She told it facing away from me at first, toward the window, as if the story were written on the glass and she needed to read it back to get the details right.

"My name is not Thornbury," she began. "Or rather—it is, but it is not the one I was born with. I was born Rosalind Ashworth Ravensworth. My father is Edmund Ravensworth."

I knew the name. Everyone who had ever read a shipping page knew the name. Ravensworth Line. Half the hulls in the northern trade. And one name on a letter written by a drowned man—

"Your father is one of them," I said.

"One of the architects of it," she said. "Not a hanger-on. Not a man who signed a document he didn't read. My father designed the scheme the way other men design houses. Room by room. The Atlas was his room."

The snoring continued through the wall, obscene in its ordinariness. A man slept eight feet from us while Rosalind Ravensworth said the word *Atlas* like a woman naming her own grave.

"I have to go back to the beginning," she said. "Will you bear with me? Because there are pieces you'll want to fit together later, and I'd rather you have all of them."

"I'm not going anywhere," I said. "Neither, I think, is he."

That almost made her smile. Almost.

"I was nineteen," she said. "Six years ago. My mother had died the winter before, and I had been sent home from finishing school in Lausanne because I wept at the wrong moments and read too much, and my father did not know what to do with a daughter except exhibit her. So he exhibited me. Dinners. House parties. And at those dinners, I learned to do the thing I do now—I listened. Nobody watches a quiet girl. That is the whole secret of invisibility, Ines. It is not hiding. It is being beneath notice, and staying there willingly, and keeping your eyes open."

"And you noticed something."

"I noticed arithmetic." She turned from the window then, and sat down on the edge of the bed opposite mine, and in the half-dark her face was younger than I had ever seen it and older at the same time. "My father's accounts never balanced, and they never balanced in a way that was too neat to be error. Ships that reported damage just under the insurance threshold. Cargoes written down before they sailed. And clerks—young men, always young men—who appeared in the ledgers one month and were gone the next, transferred, reassigned, no forwarding address. I asked about one of them. A boy called Whitmore who had been second clerk on the *Cordelia*. My father told me he'd gone to South America. The housekeeper told me he'd gone into the river. Only one of them was crying when she said it."

"What did you do?"

"What any fool girl with a conscience and no sense does." Her voice was flat, self-directed. "I wrote it down. Everything. Dates, figures, names—and my father's name at the top of the page, because I had not yet learned that evidence without protection is not evidence. It is bait. I wrote it all out fair and clean, twenty pages in my best hand, and I put it in an envelope addressed to the *Leeds Mercury*, and I told him. I told my father what I had done, because some part of me—the part that still wanted to be his daughter—wanted him to stop me. Wanted him to say, you're right, I'll end it."

"And instead?"

The pause lasted so long I thought she wouldn't answer.

"He didn't shout," she said finally. "That's the thing nobody understands about men like him. He never shouted in his life. He poured me a sherry, and he sat down across from me, and he explained to me—as patiently as a schoolmaster—that the envelope would not reach the newspaper. That the boy at the corner postbox was in his employ, as was the man who walked me to the tram, as was, he suspected though he couldn't prove it, my own maid. And then he explained what happens to girls who embarrass their fathers. Not as a threat. As information. He mentioned Whitmore. He mentioned the river. And then he said the sentence I have carried in my mouth for six years like a stone."

She looked at her hands.

"He said: *Rosalind died of fever at school last spring. It would be a kindness to everyone if the body were found.*"

The fire in the grate downstairs must have settled, because somewhere below us there was a long metallic tick, tick, tick of cooling pipe, and in the silence of the room it sounded like a clock running down.

"They held a memorial service," she went on, quieter. "I was not invited, being dead. My father wept beautifully; I'm told. And six weeks later a chauffeuse named Tamsin Thornbury answered an advertisement in Leeds—a lady wanted, licensed driver, references essential, discretion absolute. Lady Edith Vane interviewed me herself. She looked at me for a long time, and she said, 'You have the look of someone reading everything and admitting nothing,' and she hired me on the spot. Whether she knew who I was—" she lifted one shoulder "—I have never asked. Some mercies you don't inspect too closely."

I sat with all of it. The motor humming in the stable yard at Cavendish Row. The letters she read and never admitted to reading. Six years of driving a woman through Leeds, invisible by choice now, not by force—but choice built on the foundation of a grave that had no one in it.

"Why didn't you ever tell her?" I asked. "Lady Vane. Why not use her, all this time? She has power. You could have—"

"Because the moment I used it, I became visible," Rosalind said simply. "And the moment I am visible, he knows where I am. Every plan I might have made, every letter I might have intercepted—he has had six years to prepare for exactly that. My survival depends on being a rumour, not a person. Do you see? I let the movement fight without me because fighting requires a face, and my face is a death warrant."

"It's more than that," I said.

She waited.

"You're ashamed."

The word landed. I watched it land—watched her take it like a blow she had been expecting from someone else.

"Yes," she said.

"Of him?"

"Of myself." And now the words came faster, six years of them, tumbling. "Because I didn't fight. Because I let them bury a name I hated and I wore a new one like a coat and I told myself it was strategy, it was patience, it was waiting for the right moment—and Ines, the right moment never comes for people like me. People like me accumulate. We file things away. I have six years of letters in my head that could hang forty men and I did nothing, I drove my mistress through Leeds and read the world through a windscreen and called it prudence. Your brother is dead because men like my father sink ships, yes—but also because women like me watch and call it wisdom. I am not ashamed of my blood. I am ashamed that it took *you*—a stranger with a packet in her fist and nothing left to lose—walking up to the service door to make me pick up the weapon I've been carrying since I was nineteen years old."

The room was very quiet. Even the traveller next door had rolled over; the snoring had stopped.

And here is the thing I want set down properly, because it matters: I did not feel betrayed. I had expected betrayal on that train—I had lain awake at Derby station listening to her breathe and wondering whether the name Ravensworth in my brother's papers meant she was one of them, whether the letter was already gone from my chemise, whether I had delivered my brother's murderer's confession straight into the murderer's household. I had rehearsed accusations in the dark for two hundred miles.

But sitting across from her now, watching her hold her shame out in both hands like something broken she couldn't put down, I found there was no room in me for accusation. Only recognition.

"My brother kept a list too," I said.

She looked up.

"Tomas. He was a clerk, you know that much. What you don't know is that he knew what the Atlas was carrying, and he copied things—pages, figures, whatever he could reach—and he mailed them to me piece by piece over four months because he trusted the post more than he trusted anyone aboard. The last envelope came the week before she sank. I was saving them, Rosalind. Waiting until I had enough to be certain. Being prudent." I heard my own voice crack on the word and didn't care. "He is dead because I was accumulating too. So you can stop apologising for six years of watching. I've got four months of it myself, and a longer fall."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Down in the street a cab passed, wheels hissing on wet stone, and the lamplight blade slid slowly along the floor as its source moved past, and then the room was darker than before.

"We are quite a pair," Rosalind said at last. "Two cowards with one letter."

"Former cowards," I said. "We've got a deadline now. Deadlines are wonderful for courage."

That earned a real smile—small, exhausted, but real. She rose and went back to the window, and I saw her shoulders settle into a different geometry, some load redistributed.

"There's one more thing," she said. "You should hear it before morning, because it changes what tomorrow costs."

"Tell me."

"The man downstairs. The pleasant voice. His name is Halloran." She said it the way you'd spit out a seed. "He was my father's man from the beginning. When I was a child he carried my luggage to school. When I 'died' he arranged the paperwork—death certificate, school records, the whole fiction. He is the keeper of my father's graves, and there are several of them, and most are occupied." She turned. "He didn't follow us because of the letter, Ines. He followed us because of me. He recognized my face at King's Cross and wired Southampton within the hour, and that means my father knows his dead daughter is alive, and riding south with proof of what he did. Which means that from tonight onward, this is no longer only your fight, or Lady Vane's cause, or Quill's story."

She came away from the window and stood between the beds, and the darkness behind her seemed briefly to arrange itself around her like a cloak.

"Now it's a family matter," she said. "And my father has never lost one of those."

Below us, faintly, the parlour clock struck eleven—eleven measured strokes rising through the floorboards like something counting down. Thursday's tide was thirty-one hours away, and somewhere beneath us in the dark parlour, Halloran sat waiting, patient as tide himself.

Rosalind picked the poker back up.

"Get some sleep if you can," she said. "Tomorrow I introduce you to my father."
