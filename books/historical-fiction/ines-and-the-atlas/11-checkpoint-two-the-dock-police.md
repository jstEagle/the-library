## 11. Checkpoint Two: The Dock Police

The rain in Southampton came down like a verdict.

I had driven through Leeds fog, Pennine sleet, and one memorable hailstorm outside Sheffield that had sounded like applause from the dead, but I had never driven through anything that felt as deliberate as this. The drops struck the windscreen in fat, accusing pulses, and beyond them the docks rose out of the grey like something half-drawn and abandoned—cranes stooped over the water, crates shrouded in tarpaulin, the masts of a dozen ships scratching at a sky that couldn't be bothered to hold them up.

Ines sat beside me with her hands folded in her lap, which was how she sat when she was frightened. She had told me once that her mother had taught her to fold her hands in church so God would not see her nerves, and she had never unlearned it. Now she folded them over an oilskin packet's absence, because for the first time since Leeds the packet was not on her body. It was rolled tight inside the spare tyre strapped to the back of the Vane motor, wrapped in rubber sheeting, wedged where no constable would look unless he intended to change a wheel in the rain.

"You're gripping the wheel as though it owes you money," Ines said.

"It does."

"Rosalind."

"I know." I loosened my fingers. They ached. "It's fine. It's all going to be fine."

Neither of us believed it. We had left London before dawn with the bowler-hat man's face burned into both our memories, and we had made better time than any honest travellers should have, because I had pushed the Vane motor along the Portsmouth road at speeds that would have scandalised Lady Edith into one of her sermons about mechanical restraint. The engine had loved me for it. My nerves had not.

And now, five hundred yards ahead, where the road narrowed to funnel through the dock gates, I could see the checkpoint.

Not a checkpoint, officially. Officially it was nothing at all—just two police vans drawn up at the mouth of Western Road, a wooden barrier across the lane, and a cluster of constables in oilskins who were doing the thing I most feared: searching every vehicle. Not sampling. Not singling out the suspicious. Every cart, every lorry, every private motor that turned toward the water was being pulled aside, its driver made to stand in the rain while officers went through luggage, pried at crates, ran their batons along floorboards listening for hollows.

"They've been tipped off," Ines said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Quill's editor," I said. "Or whoever leaned on Quill's editor. Someone knows a letter exists. Someone knows it has to be moving tonight or not at all, and they've cast a net over the only road to the ship."

"Then we turn around."

"And deliver what? Our apologies? The Atlas sails on Thursday's tide, Ines. There is no second attempt. Whatever we carry crosses that barrier now, or it never crosses at all."

She unfolded her hands and refolded them. Ahead of us the queue crept forward—a fish cart waved through after a cursory glance, then a furniture van whose driver stood smoking while two constables emptied it drawer by drawer onto the wet cobbles. The van's owner watched his household possessions being handled by strangers' hands with an expression I recognised from mirrors: the careful blankness of a person memorising everything so they can survive it.

"Tell me the plan again," Ines said. "Tell me there is a plan."

"There is a plan," I said, "and it is a terrible plan, and it consists entirely of hoping."

"Wonderful. I've come four hundred miles on hope and a spare tyre."

"That's more support than most causes get."

But my joke landed in the mud, because we were forty yards out and a constable was already looking at us, and there is a particular quality to a policeman's attention when it has been briefed. He wasn't curious. Curiosity wanders; it gets distracted by pretty women and unusual luggage. This man's gaze had the flat patience of a paid errand. He looked at the motor, then turned and said something to a sergeant under the awning of the gatehouse, and the sergeant looked too, and neither of them smiled.

"Rosalind," Ines said.

"I see them."

"What do we do?"

What we did was what we had agreed to do, which was nothing, because turning around now would be the loudest confession available to us short of unwrapping the tyre ourselves and handing it over with a bow. So I drove forward at exactly the speed a woman delivering motor parts would drive—unhurried, faintly bored, the speed of someone thinking about her tea—and stopped at the barrier, and wound down the window, and gave Sergeant What-Might-Your-Business-Be the mild, slightly put-upon face of the working woman whose employer's schedule is not her own.

"Miss Ravensworth," I said. "Chauffeuse, in the employ of Mr. Hartwell of Berkeley Square. Delivering parts to the *Atlas* before she sails—engine spares, gaskets, the like. Mr. Hartwell's man will lose his situation if they're not aboard by midnight."

The sergeant took his time. Rain dripped off the peak of his helmet between us like a metronome counting down to something.

"Papers for the cargo?"

"Manifest's in the case behind my sister." Ines, to her eternal credit, did not flinch at the word *sister*, merely reached back and lifted the leather case from the seat and passed it forward with the weary competence of a woman who had done this nine hundred times. Inside the case, beneath a genuine delivery docket we had bought in London from a man who owed Lady Vane several favours of varying legality, lay our real papers, our real money, and nothing whatsoever that could hang us. The letter itself rode outside in the rain, spinning quietly in a tyre.

The sergeant flipped through the docket. His eyes came up.

"Hartwell," he said. "Berkeley Square."

"That's right."

"Only I'd have thought," he said, mildly, terribly, "that a firm like that would send a man."

I felt Ines go still beside me, the particular stillness of a held breath. And I understood, in the space of a heartbeat, that we had perhaps thirty seconds before this became a search, and that in thirty seconds a constable with a baton would walk around the back of the motor, and that batons have a way of finding tyres when a man is told beforehand what he is looking for.

So I did the thing that years of reading other people's letters had trained me to do. I got angry—but carefully, precisely, in the exact register of a working woman whose competence has been insulted, which is a grievance every policeman in England has heard his own mother make.

"If you'd like to drive her up to Leeds yourself, Sergeant," I said, "you're welcome to explain to Mr. Hartwell why his shipment sat at your barrier while you debated whether a woman understands an engine. Miss Vane's household uses female drivers because we're cheaper and twice as reliable, and if you'll look at the docket you'll see the parts are checked against the *Atlas*'s own chandlery order, stamp and all, which I'd be happy to wait here while you wire them to confirm, only I expect the tide won't wait with me."

I said it all without drawing breath, which was the trick of it—the flood of it, the sheer administrative weariness, a woman too busy to be afraid. The sergeant's moustache worked. Behind him, the constable who had marked us shifted his weight, and I saw him begin to move toward the rear of the motor anyway, because whatever he had been told outweighed even irritation.

And that was when the second motor arrived.

* * *

I heard it before I saw it, because there is only one machine in England that announces itself with quite such imperial indifference to weather, traffic, and common sense. The horn sounded three times, imperious as a church organ, and a long dark Napier swung past the queue of waiting carts as though queues were for other people, and drew up alongside the barrier with its near-side wheels in a puddle the size of a parish.

The window came down.

"Good evening, Sergeant," said Lady Edith Vane. "You may tell your men to stop molesting my chauffeuse."

I have seen Lady Vane address a hostile crowd of two thousand millworkers. I have seen her talk a magistrate out of a contempt charge, talk a Home Office undersecretary into admitting, on a staircase, with only me for witness, that he knew perfectly well the licensing act was unenforceable. But I had never seen her deployed like this—deliberately, at full power, in the rain, for us.

The sergeant's whole posture changed. Men spend their lives learning how to stand in front of important people, and his body forgot all of it at once. "My lady—I wasn't aware—that is, we have instructions to search—"

"Instructions. How industrious." She let her gaze travel down the line of constables, the overturned furniture van, the poor fishmonger standing cap in hand, with the leisurely appraisal of a woman pricing a bad painting. "Do you know, Sergeant, I gave a speech in this town eleven months ago. Guildhall. Fourteen hundred women, and the mayor in the chair pretending he hadn't wanted to be there. Your own superintendent wrote to me afterward—his letter sits in my correspondence case at home, between a note from Mrs. Fawcett and one from a rather confused bishop. Shall I describe it? No. Better—" she smiled, and it was a beautiful smile, and it contained absolutely no mercy whatsoever— "I shall simply mention, next time I'm interviewed, that the Southampton constabulary delayed Lady Vane's motor at the gates while her secretary's delivery was searched piece by piece in the pouring rain, on the eve of sailing, for reasons no one could articulate. The press do enjoy a small story, don't they? Something to fill a column between the shipping news and the weather."

"My lady, I only—"

"Of course you only. You're a policeman, not a fool, whatever your superiors believe." She turned her head, and for half a second her eyes found mine through the rain-streaked glass, and what passed between us had nothing to do with sergeants or newspapers. It said: *I know exactly what you are carrying and I am standing in front of it now.* Then, aloud, crisp as a ledger line: "My secretary is delivering documents to the *Atlas* on behalf of persons I am not at liberty to name, which means, Sergeant, that the sooner you finish admiring my headlights, the sooner everyone's evening improves. Myla—" this to the grim woman beside her, who had materialised out of the back of the Napier holding an umbrella like a halberd— "give the Sergeant my card. The good one."

There is a species of surrender that looks exactly like efficiency. The sergeant took the card, glanced at the constable—who had, blessedly, stopped three feet short of the spare tyre, deterred by the sudden arrival of a countess's opinion—and waved us through with the stiff ceremony of a man opening a drawbridge for a army he couldn't afford to fight.

"Mind the barrier, miss," he said.

"I always do," I said, and drove through.

I wish I could tell you my hands were steady. They were not. The wheel shivered under them the whole length of the quay, and beside me Ines let out a breath she seemed to have been holding since London, a long unspooling sound like a rope paying out.

"She came," Ines said. "She actually came. You didn't—did you wire her?"

"I wired her from London asking her to stay away. Explicitly. In capital letters."

Ines laughed—a small, disbelieving burst of sound. "Then she read your letter and did precisely what she liked with it."

"Yes," I said. "That is generally what happens."

We found the chandlery office shuttered and dark, as arranged, and in its shadow I unstrapped the spare tyre with fingers that had finally stopped shaking, and peeled back the rubber, and there it was: the oilskin packet, dry, intact, its corners sharp as the day it left Ines's hands in a Leeds doorway. She took it and held it against her chest the way she must once have held her brother, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because some things you carry for so long that setting them down makes a noise.

"Quill's lodgings are ten minutes' walk," I said. "If the tide holds, if the courier keeps the appointment, if no one else has been tipped—"

"If," said Ines.

"If," I agreed. "It's the strongest word in the language. Ask any woman."

We were halfway to the motor again, rain streaming, the *Atlas* looming black and enormous at her berth with her port lights burning like a lit skull, when I heard footsteps behind us—not hurried, not furtive, but deliberate, the tread of someone who wanted to be heard.

I turned.

Lady Vane stood under Myla's umbrella, hatless, rain silvering her grey hair, looking up at the ship with an expression I had never once seen on her face in six years of service.

It was fear.

"Rosalind," she said quietly, without taking her eyes off the water. "Before you go another step, there is something you both need to hear. The man who signed the orders for the *Atlas*—he isn't aboard yet. He arrives tomorrow." Her jaw tightened. "And I would know, because thirty years ago, before either of you was born, I nearly married him."
