## 12. Quill Refuses

The *Southampton Echo* occupied the ground floor of a building that smelled of ink and cold pipe smoke, and Nathaniel Quill looked exactly like the kind of man who had earned that smell through years of stubborn service. He was perhaps fifty, with a grey beard trimmed short and spectacles pushed up into hair that had given up on order. His shirtsleeves were rolled to the elbow. His hands, when he took them from his pockets to shake ours, were stained silver at the fingertips.

I had imagined this moment for three days. On the train, in the boarding house, in every crowded minute between Rosalind and danger, I had carried the picture of it like a coal in my chest: a journalist's face going pale as he read, his pen already moving, the machinery of the press grinding into motion behind him. My brother's name spoken aloud in print, where no one could take it back.

The reality was a bare office with two desks and a photograph of a woman I guessed was his late wife, and Quill reading Lord Ashcombe's letter twice without changing his expression at all.

"You got this past customs," he said finally. "Leeds and London both."

"We did," Rosalind said.

"And the dock police today, I assume, since you're standing here smelling of engine grease and lying about motor parts." He set the letter flat on his desk, squared its edges with one stained finger, and looked at us over his spectacles for a long moment. "Sit down. Both of you. And then tell me why I shouldn't burn this."

* * *

I had not prepared for that question. I had prepared for disbelief, for suspicion, for the weary condescension of a man who thought two women were wasting his morning. What I had not prepared was the flatness in his voice—the sound of someone who had already been warned.

"Because it's true," I said.

"Everything true is not everything printable." He leaned back until his chair creaked. "Do you know who came to see me eleven days ago? My editor. Not to talk about stories—to talk about me. About my pension. About how the *Echo* has been generous these fifteen years and generosity has its seasons. He didn't say the word Ashcombe. He didn't have to. Somebody upstairs had said it to *him*, and the saying had come down." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, unhurried, as if we had all the time in the world and none of it mattered. "So you'll understand when I tell you that a letter arriving in my office is worth precisely nothing unless it arrives with things a letter cannot supply."

"It supplies names," Rosalind said. "Dates. The route the money took—"

"It supplies claims." Quill cut her off gently, which was somehow worse than doing it roughly. "Miss—" he glanced at a card Rosalind had left, "—Ravensworth. Lady Ravensworth's household, I'm told, though I'd wager my wife's photograph that isn't the name on your papers either. You're asking me to accuse Sir Gerald Ashcombe—respected, mourning, publicly drowned and publicly grieved—of conspiracy to sink his own vessel and murder her crew. On the strength of handwriting."

"The handwriting is his," I said. "You can verify—"

"I can verify that someone wrote it." He stood and went to the window, where the harbour lay grey and busy below, cranes swinging their loads like patient herons. Somewhere out in that grey, I knew, the *Atlas* rode at anchor, fuelled and provisioned, waiting for Thursday's tide. Waiting to sail away carrying whatever proof still lived aboard her. "Here is what happens if I print this and it's wrong, or even if it's right but thin. I lose my position. That I can bear; I've buried a wife, I know how to survive a loss. But you lose more than that. The letter gets dismissed as a forgery, the forgers get hunted, and the men named in it—not accused, mind, only *named*—get to stand on platforms and call themselves victims of suffragette hysteria. And your movement, Lady Vane's movement, wears the scandal like tar. Is that what she sent you here to buy?"

"Lady Vane sent us," Rosalind said, "to buy the truth."

"The truth isn't currency," Quill said. "It's ore. It's worth nothing until it's smelted, and smelting takes corroboration." He turned from the window. "Give me any one of three things and I'll run it tomorrow above the fold, consequences be damned. A living witness who'll sign a statement. The ship's official manifest showing cargo the *Atlas* never declared—or worse, declared and never loaded. Or documents bearing more than one hand: signatures, initials, anything showing this wasn't one grieving lord scribbling confessions in the dark. Bring me one of those, and Ashcombe's own letter becomes the nail instead of the hammer."

The room was very quiet. Below us the tide was turning; I could feel it in the floor's faint shiver, or maybe only in mine.

"My brother," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended, "was a clerk aboard the *Atlas*. Thomas Ines-Marlowe. He kept the manifests. He died when she went down, and the inquiry called it an accident caused by striking dockworkers' sabotage, and everyone believed it because believing it was cheap." I stood, because sitting felt like drowning quietly. "He wrote to me every port. His last letter said the numbers didn't add up. Said the *Atlas* was insured for double her worth and loading less than her papers claimed. That letter's in Leeds. It proves suspicion, nothing more—he knew that, so he told me where aboard the real ledgers would be kept. The captain's cabin, the strongbox under the chart table. The manifest for the final voyage never went ashore, Mr. Quill. It's still on the ship."

Quill was silent for long enough that I heard the clock on his mantel ticking, patient as water dripping stone.

"If that's true," he said slowly, "then the manifest is the story. But hear me, both of you, and hear me clearly." He came around the desk, and up close his eyes were pale grey and terribly tired. "The *Atlas* sails Thursday morning. If she sails with those papers aboard, they will go overboard by Friday noon—I'd stake my soul on it, because that's what I'd do if I were guilty. After Thursday there is no story. There is only a dead clerk and a drowned ship and two women who threw away their lives on a theory." He picked up Ashcombe's letter and held it out to Rosalind, handle first, like a weapon he declined to carry. "Take it back. Guard it. And bring me pages from that manifest with a captain's signature on them, and I will burn my own career for kindling."

Rosalind took the letter. Her hands were steady; mine were not.

"Mr. Quill," she said, "do you believe us?"

He looked at her for a moment, then at me, and something in his tired face shifted—regret, or its cousin.

"Belief is the cheapest thing in journalism, Miss Ravensworth. That's rather the problem." He walked us to the door of the outer office, where clerks bent over copy without looking up, and paused with his hand on the frame. "For what it's worth—and I've learned it isn't worth much—yes. The insurance on that ship was odd, the timing was odder, and the men who profited are the same men whose lawyers visit my editor. I believe you enough to want to be wrong. Prove it properly, and I'll make them drown in print."

Then the door closed, and we were in the street with the wind off the water and nothing in our hands but a dead man's letter and a promise that wasn't one.

* * *

We walked for a while without speaking. Southampton on a Wednesday afternoon had no interest in our catastrophe: fishwives shouting prices, a boy hauling barrels, gulls conducting their eternal petty thefts above it all. At the end of the quay the *Atlas* sat in the roadstead like a fact, four funnels breathing pale smoke, preparing to erase itself from evidence.

"So that's it," I said at last. "Three checkpoints, a forged manifest, a night train, a man in a bowler hat, and it comes down to this. We carried the truth four hundred miles and it won't buy us a headline."

Rosalind stopped at the quay rail and looked out at the ship. When she spoke, her voice had the flat calm I had begun to learn was her most dangerous register—the voice she used when the alternative was shouting.

"Quill is right. That's the unbearable part. The letter alone hangs nothing. One document, one hand, deniable in a morning." She turned to me. "But the manifest—signed by the master, countersigned by the purser, listing cargo that was never aboard—that's not a claim anymore. That's arithmetic. And arithmetic doesn't drown."

"Thomas thought so too. He died being the man who counted it." I gripped the rail until the salt rust bit my palms. "You heard what he asked for. Pages from the strongbox of a ship that sails in thirty-six hours, guarded by men who killed my brother to keep those pages quiet. And now they know we're coming—they've known since King's Cross. The bowler hat wasn't following the letter, Rosalind. He was following *you*. Which means by tonight they'll have doubled the watch aboard, and the captain's cabin will be locked, and we'll be two women with a motorcar full of lies and no way through."

"Perhaps." Rosalind straightened her gloves, one finger at a time, the way she did before doing something reckless. "Or perhaps a doubled watch means they think the threat comes from outside the ship. Nobody watches the help." She nodded toward the quay, where a line of chauffeurs waited beside their idling motorcars, caps pulled low, invisible as furniture. First-class passengers were embarking early for the Thursday tide—already, gangway men checked tickets and waved through trunks and drivers alike. "First-class passengers bring their own drivers aboard. It's done all the time. A uniform opens doors that papers can't."

I followed her gaze and understood, with a sensation like stepping off a high kerb, what she was proposing. Boarding the *Atlas*. Walking the deck where Thomas had walked. Opening the cabin of the men who had signed his death.

"They'll search us," I said. "They'll search the car. They'll—"

"Tomorrow. Tonight we find a passenger willing to hire a chauffeuse and her sister, and we practice looking like furniture." She pushed off from the rail and started back along the quay, brisk as Sunday, and then paused. "Ines."

"Yes?"

"Quill asked for one thing. Let's make sure he gets more than one."

She walked on. I stood a moment longer at the rail with the wind pulling my hair toward the ship, and I thought of Thomas's last letter, and the numbers that didn't add up, and how he had trusted the sea to deliver his words home because he could trust nothing else.

Tomorrow I would walk up that gangway and open his killers' strongbox. Tonight, somewhere across the town, the man in the bowler hat was reporting to someone that the Ravensworth girl had surfaced after five years dead—and I did not yet know that by this hour tomorrow, one of us would already be locked below decks while the other hid the letter in the last place anyone would ever think to look.
