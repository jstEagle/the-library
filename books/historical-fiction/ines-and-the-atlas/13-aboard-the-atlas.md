## 13. Aboard the Atlas

The man who saved us was named Hargreaves, and he never learned that he had done it.

We found him at the Southampton shipping office on Wednesday afternoon, a cotton broker with a gout-swollen foot and a first-class ticket aboard the *Atlas* and no one to drive him to the pier but a hired cab he considered an insult. Lady Vane's network had done its work overnight: by the time Rosalind Ravensworth walked into the office in her motor coat, there was a card waiting with his name on it, a recommendation from a firm in Leeds that did not know it had recommended anyone, and a rate for two chauffeuses—driver and mechanic, sisters—that undercut every competitor in the city.

He looked us over the way a man looks over horses.

"You'll handle the luggage?"

"Everything," Rosalind said. "Motor, crates, trunks. You won't lift a finger between your door and your cabin."

"And you—" this to Ines, who stood a half-step behind with her cap pulled low—"you can keep an engine running?"

"I can keep an engine running in the dark, sir, upside down, in the rain, without a lamp."

He grunted, which from Hargreaves would prove to be the equivalent of a proposal of lifelong friendship, and signed the paper.

That was all it took. Two years of hiding, one forged letter of reference, and a gouty cotton broker's vanity, and they were going aboard.

* * *

Rosalind did not sleep that night. She lay in the boarding-house bed listening to Ines breathe in the next cot—finally asleep, exhausted from the dock police checkpoint, from the tyre full of letter, from Lady Vane arriving like weather and dispersing a police inspection with nothing but her name—and she listened to something else beneath the breathing. The sea. Even here, three streets inland, the harbor made itself heard: hooters, chains, the long groan of ships shifting against their moorings like animals dreaming.

Tomorrow she would walk up a gangway onto the ship that had killed Tomás Reyes.

She had read the letter so many times now she could recite it as scripture. *Arrangements concluded. Manifest adjusted. The men will be blamed—the strike gives us our pretext, and public feeling will do the rest. A. Ashcombe confirms the underwriters are satisfied.* Eleven lines in a dead man's hand. Eleven lines that had bought a hole in a hull somewhere below the waterline of a ship called the *Atlas*, and thirty-one drowned men, and a verdict of misadventure, and a widow in Southampton who still left a light in the window because they had never recovered the body of her son.

Except the body had come home. It had come home as a woman at a kitchen door with rain in her hair and an oilskin packet clutched to her chest like a child.

Rosalind turned over. Across the room, Ines spoke in her sleep—one word, soft, in Portuguese or Spanish or whatever language she and her brother had shared when the English around them weren't listening—and then went quiet again.

Tomorrow, Rosalind thought, we walk onto your grave, Tomás Reyes. Tomorrow we make it testify.

* * *

The *Atlas* did not look like a killer. That was the first thing, and the worst thing.

She sat at her berth in the morning light enormous and white-funnelled and almost beautiful, four hundred feet of riveted steel with her name painted at the bow in letters a man could stand inside. Gulls wheeled round her masts. Stevedores moved along the quay in a line like ants provisioning a queen, feeding her crates and coal and cable, and first-class passengers came up the gangway in bright hats, laughing, their luggage rolling ahead of them on brass-tipped trolleys.

"She's big," Ines said. She said it flatly, the way you state the size of a weapon pointed at you.

"Bigger than I imagined," Rosalind said.

"Tomás wrote me about her. First letter he ever sent from aboard—he said you could walk the length of the engine room and forget what daylight was." Ines's jaw worked. "He said she was so well built nothing could sink her."

Rosalind said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a wound.

They took the gangway at half past nine, behind Hargreaves and his mountain of luggage, and Rosalind felt the deck plates change under her feet—from the solid stone of the quay to the slight give of steel, the whole vast ship breathing with the tide. A purser's assistant checked their papers against a list. A steward directed them aft, down a stair, through a corridor painted white with brass rails, to the garage spaces amidships where the motor cars of the wealthy crossed the ocean in secured splendor.

And then, for most of a day, they were simply staff. Invisible. Rosalind had spent six years being invisible professionally—in a driver's coat and goggles she was furniture, weather, part of the machine—and she had never been grateful for it before. Now she used it deliberately, the way Ines used her lowered eyes and foreign vowels: people looked through them. People talked in front of them. By noon Rosalind knew which officer drank, which stewardess smuggled silk, and that the third engineer had been overruled twice in the loading plan by men who wore no uniform at all.

"The cargo holds forward," Ines murmured, wiping genuinely filthy hands on a rag while a second officer strolled past. "Two and Three. He says the manifest doesn't match what went in."

"Doesn't match how?"

"Weight. Crates listed as machinery coming in lighter than machinery ought to be. He thinks someone's padding the manifest to cover short-shipping. He thinks it's fraud against the cargo owners." Ines almost smiled, and it was terrible to see. "He doesn't know it's fraud against the dead."

* * *

They found the ship's library that afternoon, because first-class staff could go everywhere errands took them and no one stopped a chauffeuse carrying a case of Hargreaves's books.

It was a small oak-panelled room two decks below the boat deck, and it was empty at four in the afternoon, because first-class passengers were on deck taking the air, and that was when Ines stood in the middle of it with her hand flat against the panelled wall and began, very quietly, to shake.

"Ines."

"He walked here." Her voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "There was a library on the manifest of amenities—I memorized the whole layout from his letters, I could draw this ship blindfolded, I've been drawing her for two years in the backs of notebooks—and he wrote that he liked it here because it reminded him of the reading room in Lisbon. On Sundays, when he had the watch off, he'd sit here. He described the chair. The green leather chair by the—"

She stopped. The green leather chair sat by the porthole, worn at the arms, exactly where a clerk with a Sunday watch-off and a brother across the ocean would have wanted it.

Rosalind crossed the room and took her by both shoulders. "Look at me. You are not here to sit in his chair. You are here to finish what his letter started. Can you do that for another hour?"

"Yes."

"Then hate quietly and work loudly. We need the purser's office."

* * *

The manifest was the missing half of everything. Quill had been clear about that before they'd left him—corroboration, or nothing; a letter proves only that someone wrote a letter, but a manifest showing altered entries, in the ship's own records, dated and stamped, that was evidence a court couldn't laugh out of the room. Ines had torn pages once already, aboard during their first desperate pass—but those pages covered the old sinking. What they needed now, if it existed, was proof of the arrangement still live: the *Atlas* sailing again, insured again, loaded again under the same hands.

The purser's office was forward on C deck, and at five o'clock the purser would be at dinner preparations, and the corridor outside would be busy enough with stewards that two women walking purposefully with a case of books would be seen and not noticed.

They waited in the corridor's shadow of a bulkhead, and Rosalind felt the ship around her the way you feel a sleeping animal—not the deck anymore but the hull, the iron skin of the thing, and somewhere below it the double bottom where water had come in through a hole arranged in an office in London. Thirty-one men had died inside this animal while it lay calm on the surface of the sea, and then it had gone on sailing, patched and repainted, carrying passengers in bright hats, because money has no memory and steel does not mourn.

"All clear," Ines breathed.

They slipped into the office. It smelled of ink and varnish and cold tea. Ledgers in ranks along a shelf; the current manifest bound in blue canvas on the desk, and Rosalind's heart went into her throat because it was open, it was lying open at the cargo pages, as though the ship herself wanted to be caught.

Ines turned pages with a courier's speed. "Here. Hold Two—look at the entry dates. Loaded Tuesday, re-entered Wednesday in different ink. Someone re-wrote the weights after initial entry."

"Different hand?"

"Different hand entirely. Look at the loops—Tomás had handwriting like a printer; whoever this is writes like a doctor." She looked up, and Rosalind saw her eyes wet and furious and utterly controlled. "This is it. This is the same game, running again. They're going to do it again, Rosalind. Another ship, another crew, another thirty-one letters to mothers."

"Take the pages. Only what implicates—"

"I know what to take." And then Ines went still, head cocked, listening.

Footsteps in the corridor. Heavy ones, unhurried, the steps of a man who owns whatever floor he stands on. They grew louder. Stopped outside the door.

Rosalind had one instant to register the impossibility of escape—two women, one doorway, a dead-end office—and then the door opened, and a man walked in holding an unlit cigar and a set of keys.

He was perhaps fifty-five, silver-haired, dressed expensively in shore clothes, with the sun-reddened face of a man who played golf badly and often. He saw them. He did not shout. He simply stopped, and looked, and Rosalind watched a kind of arithmetic move behind his eyes—two female staff, purser's office, ledgers open—and watched the arithmetic arrive at the wrong answer, the answer his world had taught him: thieves, not witnesses.

"Well," he said. "Well, well. The help, going through the manifests." He smiled without warmth. "You've made my evening considerably more interesting, ladies."

And behind him, in the corridor, Rosalind heard a second set of footsteps stop—a lean, deliberate tread she had heard once before, two years ago, through a door she had been standing too near, in a house she had spent six years pretending she had never entered.

A voice said, mildly, "Those two. I'd know them anywhere."

Rosalind did not need to turn around. She knew it too.
