## 14. The Captain's Cabin

The Atlas smelled of new paint laid thick over old rot.

Ines noticed it the moment she stepped aboard—under the lemon and linseed, under the polish the stewards worked into every brass rail, there was the smell of the sea that had taken her brother. Salt gone sour. Water that had sat too long in places water should never reach. She had expected to feel fear walking these decks. What she felt instead was a cold, precise anger, the kind that sharpened rather than clouded.

She kept her head down and her hands busy. The cover held: she was Miss Ines Carrow, lady's maid to Mrs. Halliwell, a widowed first-class passenger with more trunks than sense. The trunks were Lady Vane's contribution—heavy, ridiculous, and useful, because no one questioned a maid who spent her day hauling luggage about. Ines hauled. And while she hauled, she learned the ship.

The purser's office was aft on C deck, behind a door with frosted glass. The captain's quarters were one deck above, port side, connected by a private stair to the chart room. The manifest—the true manifest, the one that would show what the Atlas had been carrying the night it went down, and what it had *claimed* to be carrying—would live in one of those two places. Ship's papers always did. Her brother Elias had explained it to her once, laughing, when he was nineteen and proud of his clerk's livery: *Everything on a ship is written twice, Ines. Once for the truth and once for the owners. The trick is finding where they keep the second version.*

They had found where. Now they needed the thing itself.

Rosalind was above, playing her part in the saloon, being invisible in plain sight the way only Rosalind could—a chauffeuse in borrowed gloves, pouring nothing, noticing everything. The bowler-hat man had not yet shown his face aboard. Every hour he didn't appear was an hour borrowed against their luck.

Tonight the captain dined ashore. Ines had heard the steward say so twice, once to the purser and once to a bosun, with the particular relish crew reserved for a night without the old man's eye. The cabin would be empty. The papers would be there.

*Go tonight,* Rosalind had said, low and quick in the shadow of the baggage room. *And if anything goes wrong—if you hear boots, if you see light—get out. The letter is enough, do you understand? Get out.*

Ines had said yes. She was lying when she said it.

* * *

She went up the service stair at half past eleven, when the ship had settled into its nighttime murmur—engines ticking, somewhere below a man singing quietly to himself, the creak and shift of eleven hundred tons of steel pretending to be asleep. The corridor outside the captain's quarters was carpeted, which was a mercy, and lit by a single lamp, which was not.

The lock was older than the door. A hairpin and four minutes of sweating patience solved it, and then she was inside, and easing the door shut behind her until the tongue found the plate with a click no louder than a knuckle cracking.

A desk. A swivel chair. A green-shaded lamp she did not dare light. A safe built into the bulkhead, its dial a dull circle in the dark like a blind eye.

Moonlight came through the porthole in thin, grey ribbons. By it she could see the desk's surface: a logbook, closed; a tray of pens; an ashtray with one dead cigar; and a ledger, open, weighted at the corners with two brass engine parts that had no business being paperweights.

She went to the ledger first.

It was the cargo manifest. Not the passenger-friendly version posted for customs—the working copy, columns dense with tonnage, crate numbers, declared values. Her finger moved down the entries. Machinery, machinery, cotton, machinery, tin plate—

Her breath stopped.

*Manifest declared: machine tools, 40 crates, consigned Hargreaves & Voss, Liverpool.*
*Manifest actual: bullion crates (empty), 40, consigned Hargreaves & Voss.*

Empty. Forty crates declared as machine tools, actually listed as *empty bullion crates*—crates that had supposedly carried gold out and come back hollow, insured to the teeth on both journeys. And beneath it, in the margin, a date. The eighth of November. Two years ago. Three days before the Atlas went down in calm seas with her brother's name on the crew list.

Elias had been a clerk. Elias had copied manifests. Elias had seen this column—*actual*—and done something reckless and honest with what he saw.

There was a sound in the passage.

Not boots. A key, turning softly in the outer door.

Ines's body understood before her mind did. There was nowhere to go. The porthole was nine inches across and forty feet above water. The connecting door to the chart room stood ajar, but beyond it lay the stair, lit, and she'd heard no one on it—which meant whoever entered had come from the chart room already, quiet as she had been.

The inner door opened.

He carried a small electric torch and did not switch it on. He stood in the doorway in evening dress, white shirt front gleaming faintly in the moonlight, and he looked at the desk, and at the ledger with its brass weights, and then he looked at her.

For a moment neither of them moved. Then he said, in a voice like a well-bred door closing: "You're the maid."

And Ines knew him.

Not from life—from paper. From a photograph she had carried folded in her coat for two years, cut from the society pages, captioned in newsprint: *Mr. Julius Ravensworth attends the shipping inquiry.* Round, respectable face. Chain across the waistcoat. The hand that had signed, at the bottom of the letter Rosalind carried next to her heart, the arrangement for forty empty crates and a drowned crew.

Ravensworth. The name had meant nothing to her the first time she heard Rosalind say it. It had begun to mean everything since.

"You're supposed to be ashore," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect.

"And you're supposed to be polishing silver." He set the torch on the desk, unhurried. "Close the ledger."

She didn't move. "You signed my brother's death."

Something shifted in his face—not guilt. Calculation. He was placing her, filing her the way he filed cargo. "The clerk," he said. "The Carrow boy. You have his look around the eyes." He sighed, and it was almost genuine. "That was never meant to be a large loss. The sea was supposed to be kinder than it was, or crueler—I forget which way round the insurers prefer it. Your brother was a detail. Details are regrettable."

The rage arrived all at once, fully formed, the way it always did when she let it off its chain—two years of it, the nights in lodging houses with her coat for a blanket, the letter from the shipping company expressing sympathy and enclosing three pounds, the grave in Leeds with no body in it. It arrived and it put weight in her arms and she crossed the cabin before she knew her legs had decided, and her hand was on the letter knife from the desk tray, and the point of it was at his throat, and he had gone very still.

"Details," she said. "Say it again."

He swallowed, carefully, the blade dimpling his collar. His eyes, this close, were pale and entirely unafraid in a way that frightened her more than any struggle would have. Men who feared death flinched. This man looked at her the way he must have looked at ledgers: assessing cost.

"If you cut my throat," he said quietly, "you will hang, and the letter will be worth nothing without me alive to deny it properly. Think. You've thought this far. Think further."

*Think further.* As if she were a junior clerk. As if Elias had been an entry misposted.

Her arm was shaking. Not from weakness—from the effort of not moving it forward one inch. It would take one inch. One inch and the world would be rid of him, and she would be rid of the dream she still had, some nights, of the water closing over the deck where Elias had stood, and whether he had called anyone's name, and whether anyone had answered.

"Ines." His voice had changed. Softer. Almost kind. "You imagine I don't know what loss is. I lost a daughter, years ago. A bright girl. The world took her and I—" He stopped himself, smoothly, as though editing a sentence. "I understand grief. Put it down. I can make things right for you. Money. Passage. A life anywhere you like, under any name you like—you seem a woman who understands names."

*Any name.* Rosalind's face rose in her mind unbidden—Rosalind saying *my father is dead to me*, Rosalind's hands steady on the wheel, Rosalind on the train, saying *you could have told me anything and I'd have chosen you anyway*. This man had made that face. This voice had raised that woman, and broken her, and buried a daughter who was not dead and never had been.

The knife trembled.

And in the trembling she saw the rest of it, clearly, the way you see the bottom of a pool when the water goes still: if she killed him here, the letter died with him. The conspiracy would close over his corpse like water and seal itself, and Elias's grave would stay a lie forever, and Rosalind would carry the daughter-shaped wound of having her father killed by her friend's hand. Rage was a fire and justice was a forge and they were not the same thing. Elias had known it. Elias had *copied*, instead of burned. Had gathered proof instead of fists, and died for it—but the proof had lived, and the proof had brought her here.

Here, holding a knife, deciding who she was going to be.

"No," she said.

His eyebrows lifted a fraction.

"I won't make you a martyr," she said, and her voice cracked and she let it crack. "You'll live. You'll stand in a courtroom with your chain across your waistcoat and you'll watch a jury read the word *actual* in this ledger, and my brother will be avenged by paper, which is what he trusted. Paper and the truth. That's worse for you than dying. Dying is easy. Being *seen*—"

Boots on the stair. More than one pair.

Ravensworth's eyes flicked past her shoulder and, for the first time, showed her something honest: alarm.

"That will be Mr. Selby," he said. "The man in the bowler hat. And he is not looking for me."

In the same instant she understood, and he saw her understand, and she was already moving—shoving him hard into the bulkhead, snatching the manifest page with its damning column, folding it small into her bodice, kicking the ledger shut as the outer door burst open and lamplight swung wild across the cabin walls.

Two men in the doorway. Behind them, filling the frame, the bowler hat.

"There," Selby said, pointing past Ravensworth entirely, past the knife still in her hand. "That's her. That's the Ravensworth girl."

The wrong name. The wrong girl. But Ravensworth, rubbing his throat, looking between Ines and the men in the door with dawning, terrible comprehension of exactly whose knife had been at his neck—

"—and the other one," Selby said, "is already in custody."
