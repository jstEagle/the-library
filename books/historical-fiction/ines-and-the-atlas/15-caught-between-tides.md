## 15. Caught Between Tides

The bowler hat came aboard at half past eleven.

I saw it from the shelter of the first-class promenade, where I stood pretending to admire the Southampton water while my heart conducted its own private mutiny. The man beneath the hat had not changed since King's Cross—the same grey coat, the same unhurried gait of a man paid by the day and certain of catching his quarry—but he had changed ships, changed cities, changed counties to follow us, and now he stepped over the gangway of the *Atlas* as casually as a man stepping into his own garden.

He paused at the top. He turned his head, slow as a lighthouse beam, and swept the promenade.

I did what I had trained myself to do for six years: I became furniture. I studied the horizon with the mild interest of a woman waiting for a husband she tolerated. I let my shoulders drop into boredom. Inside, I was screaming at Ines—*go, go below, go now*—but Ines was already gone, vanished down the companionway twenty minutes before with the manifest pages sewn into her coat lining, hunting the purser's office for whatever corroboration Quill would need. She was safe. That was the arithmetic I clung to. One of us could be safe.

The bowler hat finished its sweep and did not stop on me.

It stopped on the second officer, to whom it presented a card. The officer read it, straightened, and gestured toward the bridge stairs with the sudden deference men reserve for other men's employers. Whatever name was on that card, it opened doors.

I walked, unhurried, in the opposite direction, and let my mind do what it does best when the body is afraid: it made lists.

Item: the man was aboard, and aboard meant employed, because no stowaway wears a grey coat that clean. Someone had bought him a ticket, and whoever buys tickets for watchers on private errands is either my father or answers to him.

Item: he had known my face at King's Cross. Not Ines's. Mine. Which meant the photograph he carried—or the memory—was not two years old but six, taken before I died, before Ravensworth & Vane hired a chauffeuse with no references worth the paper, before Rosalind Ashworth ceased to exist in every registry but one.

Item: the tide. The tide turned at seven minutes past one, and this ship sailed with it, and if I was aboard when it did, there would be no letter, no Quill, no morning edition—only me, a sealed hold, and four days of open water with a man who knew precisely who I was.

Six years ago I had made a bargain with invisibility: keep me, and I will never reach for anything. I had kept my side. And here was the interest come due on the whole arrangement, standing at the top of a gangway in a bowler hat, asking after me by a name I had buried twice.

* *

I found our passenger's cabin and knocked twice, softly, as agreed.

Mrs. Havelock-Ellis was seventy, wealthy, delighted by conspiracy, and entirely convinced she had hired two sisters as her personal motor-drivers out of pure admiration for their competence rather than Lady Vane's careful engineering. She opened the door a hand's width, saw my face, and pulled me inside with the speed of a woman who had waited forty years for something interesting to happen to her.

"He boarded," she said before I spoke. "The grey coat. I saw him from the deck. He's been shown to B-deck, starboard, and a steward has been sent for his luggage—which arrived already aboard, mind you, which means he expected passage." She said it all in one breath, then pressed a small silver flask into my hand. "Brandy. You've gone the colour of the tablecloth."

"I don't—" I began, and found my hand shaking so badly around the flask that arguing seemed foolish. I drank. The brandy hit like a decision.

"Mrs. Havelock-Ellis." I gave the flask back. "In an hour, perhaps less, a man is going to describe me to the captain, and the captain is going to have me detained. When that happens, everything depends on what you do not know."

"I don't know anything," she said happily.

"That's the spirit. But listen. My sister is below, about her own business, and she must not be connected to me. If anyone asks, we met in Leeds, we are not close, you hired me through an agency and regret it—my references were thin and my manner is cold. Can you say that?"

"My dear girl, I can say it weeping if required. I was in amateur theatricals before my marriage." Her eyes, which had been twinkling, went suddenly steady and kind. "You're frightened properly, aren't you? Not adventure-frightened. The real kind."

"Yes."

"Then go and finish it before the tide does something we'll all regret." She patted my cheek with a hand like a warm sparrow. "I knew your Lady Vane in Geneva, whatever she pretends. Women like us collect each other."

I kissed her cheek, which surprised us both, and left.

* *

What followed was ninety minutes of the most disciplined walking of my life.

I could not leave the ship. I understood that now with the cold clarity of a locked door: the gangway was being watched—I had seen the second officer post a man there—and the moment Rosalind Thornbury set foot ashore, the net would close on the pier instead of the promenade, in full view of customs officers and dock police who could be relied upon to assist any gentleman with a card and a grievance. No. If I was to be caught, I would not make it easy, and I would not make Ines part of it. The plan needed the ship to sail without me if it must; the letter was already beyond my reach, sewn into a dead clerk's sister's coat, headed for a journalist's desk by way of Lady Vane's courier the moment Ines touched dry land. I was, in the strictest sense, expendable to the plan now.

It is a strange freedom, discovering you are no longer necessary. It made me reckless in exactly the useful way.

So I used the time. I walked B-deck past the bowler hat's cabin—he was within, door shut, a shadow moving behind the frosted glass, speaking to someone or writing—and I memorized the cabin number the way I once memorized cabinet ministers' correspondence: absolutely, involuntarily, forever. B-14. I noted that the purser's office was two doors along, that a fire hatch stood unlocked at the corridor's end, that the stewards' stair ran down to the lower decks without passing any checkpoint at all. Six years of driving powerful people through hostile streets teaches you to read a building the way other people read a face.

At twelve-forty I allowed myself one indulgence. I went down to the second-class promenade, where the steerage passengers' luggage was being winched aboard in dripping nets, and I looked at the sea.

It was the colour of hammered pewter, and it moved the way nothing in Leeds ever moved—vastly, indifferently, with the patience of something that has swallowed better ships than this. Somewhere under that grey water lay the bones of the *Pembroke Castle*, the vessel they had arranged to kill, and with it my brother clerk—no, *Ines's* brother, Elias, a boy who filed manifests and asked too many questions and was answered with the sea. Two conspiracies, two drownings, six years apart. My father had been perfecting the method for a long time before he applied it to his own daughter.

Because that was the truth I had been circling since King's Cross, wasn't it. The bowler hat had known my face not from a recent photograph passed among detectives, but from the old ones—from the papers my father had distributed when I vanished. *Missing daughter. Distressed mind. Any information kindly relayed.* He had hunted me with the same machinery he used for shipping schedules: efficiently, quietly, and with full confidence that the world would help him, because the world always helps men like him.

He had faked my death to bury a threat. And now his own methods had surfaced, wearing a different woman's grief, carrying a letter in a drowned man's hand. There is a justice in symmetry, even when it arrives pointing a gun at you.

The engine note changed. Deepened. A shudder went up through the deck plates like the ship clearing its throat.

One o'clock. Twenty minutes to the tide.

I turned from the rail and walked straight into the bowler hat.

* *

He had been waiting near the second-class entrance, and I understood at once, with the sickening lurch of a floor tilting, that everything I had done for ninety minutes had been watched. He had not been in his cabin conferring. He had been letting me run, letting me show him what mattered to me, letting me lead him to anything—or anyone—I couldn't bear to lose.

"You walk very well," he said, conversational, falling into step beside me as though we were old acquaintances on a morning constitutional. "Most people, when they're trying not to be seen, they hurry. Or they freeze. You do neither. I wondered at King's Cross whether I'd made an error. I haven't, have I, Miss Ashworth?"

The name went through me like cold water through a crack.

"I'm afraid you've mistaken me for someone else," I said. "My name is Thornbury. I'm a motor-driver, employed by Mrs. Havelock-Ellis, and if you continue to accost me I shall speak to the captain."

"Do," he said warmly. "Please do. I'll come along and tell him about the daughter of Henry Ashworth, who was declared dead by misadventure in Harrogate in 1907, and we can compare photographs. I have one in my breast pocket. It's a good likeness, though you've thinned since. Worry, I expect." He glanced at the sea. "Or the sea. It runs in the family, doesn't it—the sea?"

We had stopped by the rail. Around us, the business of departure went on: hatches battened, mooring lines coiled, a bosun's whistle shrieking orders into the wind. Nobody looked at a well-dressed man and a pale woman talking by the rail. That is the whole secret of the world, I thought. Everything happens beside people who have decided not to look.

"What does he want?" I asked. There was no further use in pretence, and pretence costs energy I needed elsewhere.

"To know who else has seen the letter. To know where the woman is—the little dark one, your 'sister.'" His eyes flicked over my face, reading it, and I slammed every shutter I owned. "And to convey Mr. Ashworth's regrets. He's sentimental about you, in his fashion. He talks about you. Did you know that? At dinner, to men who ought to be minding their own affairs. *My daughter would have understood the necessity,* he says. As if it were mathematics."

"He murdered Elias Vane's—" I stopped. Careful. "He killed people. For money."

"He adjusted losses," the man corrected mildly, as though correcting my grammar. "The world runs on adjusted losses, Miss Ashworth. You'd know that if you'd stayed. You might have been at the dinner table yourself."

Somewhere below us the engines turned over fully, and the deck began to tremble in earnest. A gull screamed once and gave up on the ship entirely, wheeling away toward the shore.

"Am I under arrest?" I asked.

"There's no arresting at sea. There's only guests and accidents." He touched his hat brim—a courteous, horrible little salute. "You'll be comfortable in the forward hold until Liverpool. Mr. Ashworth's instructions are explicit on the point of comfort. He'd take it very ill if you were mishandled." A pause, and something almost human crossed his face, some professional distaste quickly folded away. "The other one, there are no such instructions. So it's kindest all round if you tell me where she's got to."

The tide was turning. I could feel it in the ship's slow rotation against the dock, in the creak of the great hull taking the water's new opinion. Minutes. Perhaps seconds.

"Ines," I said—and it was the first time I had ever spoken her name aloud to an enemy, and saying it felt like handing over a hostage, and like a vow, both at once. "Is somewhere you will never find her."

"No," he agreed placidly. "Probably not. They rarely are, at first."

Two sailors came up behind him—not summoned, merely passing, and yet he stepped aside with the ease of a man who owns the deck, and I felt hands on my arms that were not his, grey-uniformed hands, and heard a voice say something about *the gentleman's party* and *this way, miss, no fuss*, and I understood that there had never been a choice to fight, not really. Not here, in the open, with witnesses who would remember only a hysterical woman being helped below by kind hands.

So I did not fight. I walked.

Down the companionway, down again, past the galley smells and the boiler heat, along a corridor lit by caged bulbs that swung with the ship's first movement, to a steel door with a hasp and a padlock that a sailor produced from his pocket as unremarkably as a key to a linen room. The bowler hat did not follow. He had other appointments—Ines, perhaps, or the purser, or simply the rail and the receding shore.

"One thing," I said, as the door swung open on darkness stacked with crates. The sailor looked at me with the flat courtesy of a man following instructions, and I spent my last coin. "Tell the gentleman his daughter says the Pembroke Castle's manifest is already off this ship. Tell him to ask himself who signed it out. He'll understand."

He wouldn't—it was half bluff, a stone thrown at a window in the dark—but doubt travels fast between conspirators, and a doubting man checks his papers, and a man checking papers is a man not watching gangways.

The door closed. The padlock spoke, twice, small and final.

* *

Here is what I learned in the first hour of the hold: darkness is not empty. Darkness is full of your own voice, waiting for company.

The crates around me smelled of tar and tea and lamp oil; I mapped them by touch, built a geography of the dark so I would not have to inhabit the other map, the one where my father sat at a long dinner table telling strangers I would have understood the necessity. I found a coil of sacking and sat on it. I found that a gap between two crates admitted a thread of grey light and, with it, air, and the distant thunder of the engines gathering themselves.

Through the hull I felt the moment we took the tide. A long, low groan ran through the steel, keel to deckhead, the sound of a vast animal deciding to move, and then the floor leaned, gently, terribly, and the shore let go of us. I heard the docks fall away—the shouts thinning, the gulls giving up, the last line of land-noise dissolving into water-noise—and I put my forehead against the cold crate in front of me and let myself, for exactly sixty seconds by my counting, be afraid.

Then I stood up and took inventory, because Rosalind Ashworth had been locked in worse rooms by a more frightening man and had walked out of that one at nineteen with nothing but a forged certificate and a plan, and she was not going to lie down on a coil of sacking at twenty-five for the convenience of a bowler hat.

Assets: one candle stub in my coat pocket, matches, a hatpin, the knowledge of B-14, the knowledge that the stewards' stair ran somewhere above me, and the certainty—faint, stubborn, load-bearing—that Ines would not sail away from me. She had come across half of England on the strength of a letter and a stranger's word. She had looked at my worst secret in a Southampton boarding house and answered it with grace. Such women do not abandon their people in holds. I had been hers for three days now, whether I had consented to the arrangement or not.

Liabilities: the tide, the distance, the water widening every minute between this steel box and everything that could save me.

And then, faintly, through the gap between the crates, threaded with engine-thunder and the hiss of water against steel, I heard another sound. Footsteps. Quick ones. Descending the iron stair that led, if my map of the dark held true, directly to this deck.

A voice followed them, low, breathless, swearing in French under its breath with a Leeds accent—and I have never in my life heard anything so beautiful.

Someone was coming down into the hold. Someone small and dark, who should have been ashore with a coat full of evidence, and who had turned around instead.

The footsteps stopped outside my door. Something metal scraped against the padlock.

"Ines," I said to the darkness, and my voice cracked clean down the middle. "The lock. Try the lock."
