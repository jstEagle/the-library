## 16. Ines Alone

The letter was burning a hole through her coat, and Ines let it.

She stood in the shadow of a cargo crane on B Deck, the Atlantic stinking of salt and diesel below her, and did the arithmetic that would decide everything. Rosalind had been gone eleven minutes. Taken below by two men who moved like men who had done this before—quietly, without fuss, without drawing a single eye from the first-class passengers still toasting themselves in the smoking room above. Eleven minutes, and no one had shouted, because no one had seen, because Rosalind Ravensworth knew how to disappear better than anyone Ines had ever met, and she had disappeared anyway.

Dawn was coming. She could feel it in the way the dock lights were losing their argument with the sky, the black over Southampton softening to the colour of wet slate. The tide would turn within the hour. The Atlas would sail, and every soul aboard would be carried out beyond the reach of English law, into international water where a woman could be quietly lost overboard and logged as an accident, the way Tomás had been logged as an accident, the way the whole rotten business had been logged from beginning to end.

*Run*, said the part of her that had kept her alive from Lisbon to Leeds. *You have the letter. You have the manifest pages. Quill is waiting on shore. One woman against all of it is not courage, it is arithmetic.*

But arithmetic had never buried anyone she loved.

She thought of Rosalind's hands on the wheel of the motor, sure and light, the way she'd talked her way past the customs inspector at Leeds with nothing but other people's stolen secrets arranged neatly behind her teeth. She thought of her on the night train, lamplight on her face, saying *my name isn't mine* as though she were handing over something still bleeding. She thought of her going down those stairs without a sound so that Ines wouldn't have to hear it, because that was what Rosalind did—she made herself invisible so that other people could be seen.

Eleven minutes. Twelve.

"Right," Ines said aloud, to nobody, to Tomás. "Not tonight."

* * *

The problem was immediate and practical: if they took her, they would search her. And if they searched her, they would find the letter, and then everything—Tomás, the manifest, the two women who had crossed half of England carrying it—would be for nothing. The letter could not come with her. That was the brutal shape of it. Love meant leaving the thing behind.

So: where does a woman hide a piece of paper on a ship about to sail, in the last minutes before dawn, when every corner might hold a man in a bowler hat?

Not in her cabin—the steward would strip it by eight o'clock. Not in the luggage—the holds would be rummaged at the first whisper of trouble. Not anywhere clever, because clever places were the first places searching men looked. Her brother had taught her that, in another life, when he was a clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a taste for proverbs: *the best hiding place is the one that exists in plain sight and beneath contempt.*

Ines walked, unhurried, a tired third-class passenger looking for the ladies' water closet, and she found what she was looking for on her second pass along C Deck: the ship's post room. A brass-lettered door, unlocked at this hour because the mail bags for the crossing had been brought aboard overnight and the sorter would not come until the bell rang at sailing. Inside, racks of canvas sacks, hundreds of them, bound for New York, Cherbourg, everywhere—sealed with lead seals and destined to sit untouched in a locked mail room for five days of open ocean.

No one searches the post. It is sacrosanct, boring, and legally untouchable. Even conspirators trust the mail; it is the one superstition of the powerful.

She slipped inside. The letter, in its oilskin fold, she pushed deep into the third sack from the door—one addressed to the Americas, ordinary, anonymous—and she whispered something over it in Portuguese, the blessing her mother used to say over bread going into the oven. Then, from the same sack, she pulled out a handful of ordinary letters and tore them small out of pure spite, letting the confetti of strangers' correspondence flutter into the corner, so that if anyone opened this sack they would find it disturbed and assume children, or rats, or nothing.

Then she stood a moment with her hand flat against the canvas.

"If anything happens to me," she told it, "get yourself delivered."

* * *

Going back was easier than leaving had been, which she should have expected. Doors only guard the way out; no one guards the way in, because no one imagines anyone would be fool enough to return.

She came down the crew stairway behind the galley, moving with the loose-ankled walk of someone who belonged there, and the smell of yesterday's soup covered her like a coat. Two stewards passed her with a nod—one of them actually stepped aside for her, God help her, mistaking her for new crew—and she counted doors until she found the one that mattered: a hatch marked in faded paint, *STORES — NO ADMITTANCE*, with a seaman's cap hanging on the hook beside it, recently arrived, still damp with fog.

Fresh guard. Fresh prisoner.

Through the seam of the hatch she could hear voices, low and ugly with the confidence of men who had done this work before and been paid well for it.

"—says keep her till after we clear the Needles. Captain's been paid to look away from the storeroom, not from the passenger list."

"And her? The other one?"

"Gone, near as anyone can tell. Which means she's run, which means she's got the paper, which means you'll want to find her before the pilot steps off, or none of us will see the money."

Ines closed her eyes. *She's run.* They thought Rosalind's capture had frightened Ines off. They thought she was a woman who saved herself.

Good. Let them think it. Fear makes men slow, and slow men make mistakes, and she intended to be gone from this deck before either of them finished his cigarette.

But first—first she put her mouth close to the hatch seam, and she spoke in English, low and even, the way Rosalind spoke when she was most dangerous: quietly.

"She's not alone," Ines said. "And neither am I."

Silence from inside. A chair scraping. Then footsteps toward the hatch, quick and heavy—

And Ines was already moving, up the stairwell, through the galley, out into the grey beginning of the morning, where the gulls were waking over Southampton Water and the tide had begun, at long last, to turn.

Behind her, a shout. Ahead of her, the gangway. Between them, forty feet of deck and every man's eyes just starting to turn her way.

She ran.

* * *

She was three steps onto the gangway when the first hand caught her sleeve.

She twisted—old instinct, Lisbon alleys, brothers' fights—and the sleeve tore instead of holding, and she was moving again, down the sloping plank toward the shore with the whole grey harbour swinging beneath her. Behind her boots thundered on steel. Ahead, the quay, the crates, the cranes standing against the brightening sky like gallows that hadn't been needed yet.

Someone blew a whistle. Someone else swore in a voice cracked with sleep. The tide groaned under the hull, the whole ship leaning its enormous weight toward departure, and Ines understood with terrible clarity that she had perhaps ninety seconds between the letter hidden safe in the mail sack and the sister-shaped hole in her chest walking free—or not walking free—off this ship, and that she would spend those seconds running, because running was the only prayer she knew.

She hit the quay at full speed, ducked between stacked crates, and heard the pursuit falter behind her—sailors don't leave their ship, not with sailing bell already ringing—and then, cutting across the gull-cry and the engine-throat noise of the waking port, came a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat.

A motor horn. Three short blasts. Lady Vane's signal.

Around the end of the crate-stack swung the great dark motor with Rosalind's seat empty and Lady Vane upright in the rear like a general who had arrived precisely when the battle required it, and Ines flung herself at the running board as the car gathered speed along the quay.

"They have her," Ines gasped. "Stores deck. Two men, maybe more. Sailing bell's already gone."

"Then we are not leaving," Lady Vane said, in the tone of a woman who had chained herself to railings for lesser causes, "until that ship gives her back."

The Atlas leaned toward the sea. The tide was turning.

And somewhere below her waterline, in a locked storeroom smelling of rope and rust, Rosalind Ravensworth was listening to the engines wake, counting the same tide, and waiting—for whatever came next—to make itself known.
