## 17. Checkpoint Three: The Gangway

They held me four hours in a cabin with no porthole, and the whole of that time I could hear the ship breathing.

That is the thing about a vessel like the Atlas—everyone imagines her silent, a mountain of steel at anchor, but she isn't. She groans. She ticks as the boilers heat. Water slaps against plates somewhere below, patient as a clock, and every sound says the same sentence over and over: *the tide turns before dawn, the tide turns before dawn.* I sat on a bare bunk with my wrists still stinging from the grip of the man in the bowler hat and I listened to that sentence and understood it was being said to me alone. They did not need to kill me. They only needed to keep me until Thursday's tide took the Atlas—and me aboard her—out past the jurisdiction of anyone who might ask inconvenient questions.

A body lost at sea requires no explanation. I had read enough letters to know how often that sentence had been made true.

The lock turned once, early on—a steward with a tray of tea he clearly had not chosen to bring, a man who would not meet my eyes and who left the cup untouched on the sill because I might have done anything to it, poison or proof. Then nothing. Hours of nothing. I rehearsed what I knew: Quill had refused us; the manifest pages were sewn somewhere Ines would not tell me even under duress, which I respected and resented in equal measure; my father's colleagues were one deck above me, sleeping soundly behind first-class doors, dreaming their tidy dreams of sunken ships and blameless ledgers.

And Ines was out there alone, in the dark, in Southampton, believing I had failed her.

I got up and put my ear to the door and listened for footsteps that never came, and I thought—not for the first time, but with new and terrible clarity—that invisibility had been my craft for eleven years, and now I was invisible where it did the least good. Anyone can hide in an empty room. The trick had always been to hide in plain sight, among letters and luggage and the ordinary machinery of other people's lives. Well then. If they had taken away my plain sight, I would have to borrow someone else's.

* * *

What Ines did, I learned afterward, in pieces, and I will set it down now as though it happened all at once, because that is how it felt from inside the hold: chaos arriving everywhere simultaneously, with no centre and no author, so that even later I could not quite believe one rain-soaked woman from Leeds had manufactured it between midnight and dawn.

She began with the cargo. The Atlas was taking on last freight through the night—crates, bales, mail sacks going aboard by the hundred—and along the dockside each item passed across a gangway watched by two constables and a purser's clerk checking names against lists. Every leaving passenger would be checked just as carefully. The bowler-hat man had seen to that; his instructions, overheard by a stewardess Ines had pressed into service with nothing but truth and desperation, were that *every soul* leaving the ship be verified against the passenger manifest, photographs compared, luggage opened. A net drawn tight around a ship about to sail.

So Ines did not go near the net. She went beneath it.

The cargo hold's forward hatch stood open to the night, and the derricks swung crates over it in long slow arcs, and beside each crate on the dock stood a stevedore with a hook and a foreman with a list. Ines had spent three years making herself useful in houses where no one looked at servants, and she looked at the foreman now—looked at the way the winch cable ran through its fairlead, at the way the men stepped back when the load came over, at the one iron pin that held the whole arrangement honest. She is not a large woman. But she grew up hauling nets, and she knows what a pin looks like when it has been half withdrawn, and she knows how to finish withdrawing it while appearing to steady the crate beside it.

The crate came off the dock at 2:40 by the customs house clock. Halfway through its arc the cable jumped the fairlead, and two tons of machine parts dropped twelve feet onto the steel deck of the hold, and the sound—I heard it myself, four decks away and behind a locked door—was the sound of a church falling down inside a drum.

Every whistle on the dock went at once. Men came running from three directions. The foremen began shouting contradictory orders into the confusion, and the constables on the gangway, whose whole training told them that accidents mean crowds and crowds mean thieves, abandoned their post for thirty seconds to see whether anyone was crushed. Into those thirty seconds walked a small grey person in a steward's coat, carrying a first-class breakfast tray, moving aft along the deck with the unhurried gait of someone going somewhere dull.

That was me. That was Ines wearing me—the coat she had taken from a laundry rack, the cap low, the tray stolen from the pantry alley where trays stand stacked by the dozen. Under the coat, against her heart, flat and thin and sewn into the lining where no search would think to look because searches look in linings first: nothing. The letter was not with her at all. The letter was where she had hidden it hours earlier, in the one place on this entire vessel no man would ever search.

But she was carrying me, you understand. Or the belief of me. Her plan, as she confessed it later with her hands shaking around a cup of tea, had been simple and mad: if the guards were watching every face leaving the ship, then give them the face they wanted somewhere else, at the far end of the deck, at the very moment the real Rosalind Ravensworth walked down the gangway.

First she needed me out of the hold.

* * *

The lock turned again at ten minutes past three, and this time it was not a steward.

It was a stewardess—a real one, mid-forties, capable hands, eyes red-rimmed with something between fear and fury—and she said six words: "Your friend says you're to come." Then she had my wrist and was pulling me into the alleyway before I could find my voice, and we were moving, fast, through the smell of soap and steam, past cabins where passengers slept the sleep of the innocent, down a stair, along a passage, until she stopped in the third-class washroom and shoved a bundle into my arms.

Steward's coat. Cap. Tray.

"She went back for you," the woman said. "After everything. She said—" and here her voice cracked, and I understood that whatever bargain Ines had struck with this stranger, it had cost the stranger more than a coat. "She said to tell you the tide waits for no one, and neither does she. Forehold stair, port side. Go as crew, go slow, and God forgive us all."

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Mrs. Adeyemi. My husband drove coal lorries for your Lady Vane before the war took his lungs." She was already turning away. "My brother didn't drown either, miss. He just stopped writing. So don't waste it."

I have been given many instructions in my life and obeyed nearly all of them. Those were the only ones I have ever followed as though my life depended on them, because it did, and because somewhere above me a woman who owed me nothing had decided it didn't.

* * *

The deck at ten to four was bedlam wearing livery. The cargo accident had rippled outward: the forward hatches closed while they sorted the wreckage, the dock police doubled at the gangway, and every departing crew member and passenger funnelled through a single checkpoint where three constables and a ship's officer worked down a list with a lantern in one hand and suspicion in the other. I saw it from behind a ventilator casing, tray balanced, heart hammering against the tin.

And I saw Ines.

She stood at the rail twenty feet beyond the checkpoint, in a grey travelling dress I did not recognize, looking exactly like what she was pretending to be: a wife waiting on a husband coming ashore on final leave. Except that her face was the wrong colour entirely, white as the painted deck, and she kept one hand pressed flat to her stomach, and when I caught her eye across that distance she gave me the smallest nod I have ever seen and then—deliberately, unmistakably—let her knees buckle.

She went down like folded linen. The scream she produced was not theatrical; it was the raw, awful sound of a woman whose body has betrayed her, and it cut through the dock noise like a knife through canvas. The nearest constable dropped his list. The ship's officer shouted for the ship's doctor. Two stewards came running, and the crowd at the gangway compressed and shifted toward the drama the way crowds always do, magnetized by distress, and in the gap that opened—

I walked.

Down the deck, past the checkpoint's flank, into the stream of departing crew signing off at the muster book. A hand took my tray—somebody helpful—and I murmured "thank you, that's mine at the rail," and kept walking, and a bored clerk glanced at my cap and my collar and my face, and here is the miracle, the small stupid human miracle: he looked at the cap and not the face. Eleven years of being unseen had taught me that people see uniforms, statuses, expectations. Nobody sees a woman carrying a tray unless she gives them reason to.

"One of the galley staff?" the clerk asked.

"Relieved at Southampton, sir. Going home to my mum."

He waved me through. I descended the gangway one step at a time, slowly enough to be boring, and the harbour water rose around me black and glittering, and behind me a woman was being carried to the infirmary on a stretcher, moaning convincingly, whispering through clenched teeth to the constable supporting her head that she was *perfectly well, thank you, it was the baby,* and would he help her to a cab.

At the bottom of the gangway I turned, once. The Atlas towered above me, lit along her length like a street, breathing her iron breath, and somewhere on board her slept the men who had drowned Ines's brother and signed my death in all but name. Let them sail, I thought. Let them sail on Thursday's tide into a morning they cannot imagine.

The tide was turning. I felt it before I saw it—the water below the pier shifting, the moored lighters swinging lazily to face the new current. Somewhere ashore, Mrs. Adeyemi's brother-in-law waited with a coal lorry, and somewhere beyond him Nathaniel Quill sat awake in an office he believed was finished with us, and somewhere in the lining of a life neither of us had chosen, the letter was already halfway to him.

Ines found me eleven minutes later, grey-faced, triumphant, holding her side where she had thrown herself against the stretcher poles for realism.

"You fainted," I said.

"I committed," she said. "There's a difference."

Then she laughed, and I laughed, and we clung to each other beside a stack of rope coils like two survivors of a wreck, which is precisely what we were, while behind us the Atlas sounded her whistle—long, deep, certain—and began, with the turning tide, to take up her moorings.

We watched her swing her great bows seaward, carrying our enemies out toward a justice they had already lost, and it was only when the ship's lights were small and sliding that Ines went suddenly rigid against my arm and said, in a voice emptied of all triumph:

"Rosalind. The courier. Lady Vane's courier—he was supposed to meet us at the Pier Head at half past four."

The customs clock on the quay read twenty-five to five.

"He isn't here," she said. "And I never told anyone where the letter is hidden."
