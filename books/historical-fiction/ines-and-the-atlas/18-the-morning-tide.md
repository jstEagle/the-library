## 18. The Morning Tide

The sea did not care.

That was the first thing I understood, standing on the dock with the wind tearing at my collar, watching the *Atlas* lean into the grey water like a wounded animal returning to the hunt. The gulls screamed. The tugs coughed and grumbled. A thousand small ordinary things went on around the enormous criminal thing, and none of them paused to mark it. Two years ago this ship had swallowed my brother and called it an accident, and now it was sailing again with its secrets tucked in its hold, and the sea simply lifted it, the way it lifts everything, without judgment, without memory.

I had memory enough for both of us.

My hands were still shaking. Not from cold, though there was cold enough in that Southampton morning to bite through wool. From the gangway—from Rosalind's hand slipping out of mine as the guards closed in, from the fifteen minutes I had spent afterwards believing I would never see her again, from the loosed cargo and the staged faint and the whole desperate theatre of it, and underneath all of it, from the torn pages pressing against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

I had hidden the letter where no one would think to look. That part had worked. Most of me had worked. But some part of me was still back on that deck, at the moment I turned away from the lifeboat deck and went *back*, down into the belly of a ship that had already killed one person I loved, because the other person I loved was locked below it.

Love outweighing survival. I had done it without thinking. That frightened me more than anything the bowler-hat man could have done.

* * *

Rosalind found me before I found her.

I was scanning the crowd on the pier—hats and crates and stevedores and one journalist-shaped absence where Nathaniel Quill should have been—and then a hand closed on my elbow, and I nearly drove that hand's owner into the water before I saw who it was. Grey-faced, hatless, one sleeve of her driving coat torn at the seam where they'd handled her roughly. Her hair had come loose entirely. She looked like a woman who had been taken apart and reassembled by someone in a hurry.

"You're bleeding," she said, which was absurd, because so was I, at the hairline, and she reached up and touched it with two fingers as if confirming a fact in a ledger.

"They didn't hurt you?" My voice came out wrong. Small.

"I've been locked in a cabin by men with more ambition than sense." She said it flatly, but her fingers stayed at my temple a moment longer than triage required. "They took my watch. I intend to invoice someone."

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, half sob, and I turned my face toward the water so she wouldn't see what it did to me. The *Atlas* was smaller now, a dark shape against the lightening east, its funnel dragging a banner of smoke across the pink sky. Sailing clean. Sailing without the evidence.

Or so it believed.

"Quill," I said.

"Courier," she said. "Lady Vane."

* * *

Here is what I learned about Lady Edith Vane in the weeks I would come to live under her roof, though I learned the shape of it that morning on the pier: she treated fame the way other women treated hatpins. Something kept close, sharp, deployed only when required—and devastating when drawn.

She had arrived in Southampton the night before like weather. Nobody had arranged it; nobody had questioned it; a woman of her stature turning up at the Grand Hotel with two maids and a motor car was not an event, it was an assumption. And somewhere between midnight and dawn she had done whatever she did—written notes, made calls, summoned a favour from a woman whose name I was never told—who agreed to carry a sealed oilskin packet north to London on the early boat train, hand to hand, no post, no telegraph office, no trail.

"She asked no questions," Rosalind told me later, "which is either loyalty or excellent training."

The courier was already gone. By the time Rosalind and I stood dripping on the pier watching the ship escape, the letter was riding south-east through the Hampshire countryside at forty miles an hour, wrapped in a shawl, in the company of a woman feeding it sandwiches as though it were a child. It would reach London by noon. It would reach Quill's lodging by afternoon. Whether Quill printed—that remained the cliff we were all hanging from.

But I had given Quill something else. That was the part I kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

The manifest pages.

I had torn them free in the captain's cabin with hands that wanted, more than anything in the world, to tear something else—to tear at the man himself, the one who signed my brother's name onto a death warrant with a fountain pen and then slept soundly ashore. I had stood three feet from him with his own blood practically singing in my ears. And instead I had opened a drawer.

Paper against paper. That was my revenge: four pages of an official manifest, listing cargo that was never loaded, insurance that was tripled eleven days before sailing, and a signature I would know anywhere—the same elegant hand as the letter, Lord Ashcombe's hand, the hand of a drowned man walking.

"The letter proves intent," Rosalind had said on the night train, in the dark, in a voice meant only for me. "The manifest proves method. Together they prove conspiracy. One without the other is a rumour. Rumours die. Conspiracies go to trial."

So the packet contained both. And I had carried them out past every checkpoint in England wedged inside the spare tyre of a motor car, and then inside my bodice, and then inside the false bottom of Lady Vane's hatbox, and at the last, folded flat against my sternum where my brother's photograph used to sit, in the pocket I had sewn there myself two years ago for exactly this purpose, though I hadn't known it then. Grief is a good tailor. It plans ahead.

* * *

We waited.

There is no prose grand enough for waiting. We waited in a tea room near the docks that smelled of steam and burnt toast, and Rosalind drank three cups of coffee without tasting any of them, and I ate nothing, and neither of us spoke much because speaking risked saying the wrong thing in front of the wrong waitress, and because we were too tired to lie well even to each other.

At half past ten a boy came in with the morning papers and the room filled with rustling, and I read the shipping news over a stranger's shoulder. *ATLAS DEPARTS SOUTHAMPTON, FULL MANIFEST, FINE WEATHER EXPECTED.* Full manifest. I put down my cup so I wouldn't throw it.

"He'll print," Rosalind said, not looking up from her untouched toast.

"He refused before."

"He refused because he had half a weapon. He has the whole blade now." She finally raised her eyes to me, and they were red-rimmed and furious and certain. "Men like Quill don't want to print. They need to be unable *not* to. We've made it impossible to look away. That's the only kind of journalism that survives an editor."

"And if his editor kills it anyway?"

"Then Lady Vane goes to his editor," Rosalind said, "and after his editor, his editor's wife, and after her, the Archbishop of Canterbury if necessary. She has been fighting men with money for twenty years, Ines. This is Tuesday to her."

It was the closest thing to comfort I got all day, and it wasn't comfort, really. It was arithmetic. But arithmetic held me together better than sympathy would have.

* * *

The telegram came at six o'clock in the evening, delivered to the Grand Hotel in a boy's grimy fist, and Lady Vane read it standing in the lobby with her gloves still on.

I watched her face. I had learned, in those few days, to read Lady Vane the way sailors read sky. There was nothing extravagant about her—she was not a woman who gave her face away—but I had seen her go very still once before, over Ashcombe's letter, and I knew the difference between her stillnesses. This one had weight shifting underneath it. This one was an earthquake being filed as a footnote.

She passed the flimsy yellow slip to Rosalind without a word. Rosalind read it aloud, quietly, for my benefit alone:

HAVE DOCUMENTS. AUTHENTICATE TONIGHT. IF TRUE THIS ENDS CAREERS AND OPENS INQUEST. SEND EVERYTHING YOU HAVE. STAY AWAY FROM DOCKS. — QUILL

"*If* true," I repeated.

"He'll find it true," Rosalind said. "Ashcombe's hand, the manifest numbers, the insurance filings—it will cross-check by midnight. He knows it. That last line is him protecting us, not doubting us."

"Stay away from the docks," I said. "How considerate. Two days late."

"Two days late and precisely correct." Rosalind folded the telegram along its crease, once, twice, precise as everything she did. Then she did something I had never seen her do. She sat down abruptly, in the middle of the hotel lobby, on a chair meant for waiting visitors, and she put her face in her hands, and she laughed—a low, cracked, exhausted sound—or cried; I honestly could not tell which, and I suspect neither could she.

I sat down beside her. I didn't touch her. Some collapses you have to be allowed to witness, and touching makes people assemble themselves too fast. So I just sat, shoulder an inch from hers, and let her do it, and after a while her breathing evened out and she straightened and became Rosalind Ravensworth again, chauffeuse, bluffer, woman of no visible history, except that now I knew the other name, and knew what it cost her to wear either one.

"We should eat," she said.

"Yes," I said, though I couldn't imagine ever wanting food again.

"It's what she'd instruct." She tipped her head towards Lady Vane, who had already ascended, having said only, on her way to the stairs: "Sixty years I've fought this empire, girls, and I have learned one thing above all others. Victory is a meal, a bath, and eight hours' sleep. Everything else is vanity."

* * *

I did not sleep. Eight hours were offered and I took perhaps two.

What I did instead, in the small hours in a strange bed in a strange city, was take out my brother's photograph and set it on the windowsill where the street lamp could reach it, and talk to him. I don't make apologies for this. He was the only congregation I had for most of my life.

"It's done," I told him. "Or it will be done, tomorrow, or the day after, when the presses run. They sailed thinking they'd won. They always sail thinking they've won. That's how you catch them, Tom—you let them sail."

He didn't answer, of course. He was twenty-six in that photograph forever, squinting into a sun that existed only in 1911, with ink on his fingers because he was always, always writing something—manifests, letters, lists, marginalia. A clerk's whole world in paper. They thought paper was harmless. They built their entire crime out of paper. And it was paper, in the end, that walked out past their checkpoints in a chauffeuse's spare tyre and a dead clerk's sister's bodice.

I thought about what I'd say to the inquest. I thought about whether Quill would use my name or protect it. I thought about whether I wanted my name used, and found, to my surprise, that I did—not the name the conspirators feared, but my own, plain, Ines, sister of Thomas, clerk of the *Atlas*, who noticed things and wrote them down and died for it. Let it be in the record. Records were his religion. Let this be his scripture.

And then, because exhaustion does what solitude cannot, I thought about Leeds. The kitchen with the black range. The way Rosalind had nearly shut the service door in my face and then hadn't. The smell of Lady Vane's tobacco and her terrible handwriting. The strange domestic gravity of that house, pulling at me even now, three hundred miles south, saying *there is a place, there is a place, there is a place.*

I had come south for vengeance. I was beginning to suspect I would go north for something else entirely.

* * *

The second telegram arrived with breakfast, and it was three words long, and Lady Vane read it and allowed herself—one degree, no more—the smallest smile I have ever seen on a human face.

"Read it, Miss Carrow," she said, and handed it to me. She had started calling me by my surname two days ago, and every time it landed like a knighthood.

**PRINTING TOMORROW. FRONT PAGE. GOD HELP THEM. — Q**

I read it twice. I handed it back. I felt nothing, and then everything, in the space of a breath—the way a wave comes ashore, all undertow first and then the whole grey weight of it arriving at once. Front page. The conspiracy, the sinking, the names, the forged accident, my brother reduced to a line item and now un-reduced, restored, *witnessed*. The *Atlas* was somewhere off Ushant by then, full steam for Lisbon, carrying nothing but its own guilt, and it did not know yet that the tide had turned against it on land while it posed on the open sea.

Rosalind was watching me across the breakfast table. "Say something," she said gently.

"I'm trying to find the word."

"Take your time. We appear to have won."

"No." I shook my head, and the strangest thing happened—I started laughing, helplessly, into my cold toast, and couldn't stop, and Rosalind started too, and even Lady Vane's mouth twitched at the corner like a curtain moving in wind. "No, we haven't won. Not yet. There'll be an inquest, and lawyers, and they'll fight every line. Ashcombe is alive and rich and dangerous, and my brother is still dead, and—" I wiped my eyes with the back of my wrist. "And I tore pages out of a captain's drawer and I keep expecting to feel noble and I just feel *tired*."

"Tired is what winning feels like at first," Lady Vane said. "Nobility arrives later, if at all. Eat your toast."

* * *

It cost something. I want to be honest about that, because triumphs are always written up as if they were free.

It cost Rosalind her invisibility, which she had worn for years like armour, and which is now gone—burned off her in a boarding house confession and a bowler-hatted man's recognition, and she cannot get it back, and I have seen her checking windows since. It cost me the clean simple story I had carried for two years, in which my grief had one villain and one remedy; the truth is bigger and messier, a web of signatures and insurers and polite men who never touched a rope or a bilge pump. It cost Lady Vane exposure of the movement she has spent her life building—because when this breaks, and it will break loudly, every newspaper will ask whether the suffragists conspired with muckraking journalists, and she will stand in that storm and hold the line with her bare hands.

And it cost a bullet. Not mine. Hers-to-be, dodged, or deferred—I don't know yet which, and that is a debt this story hasn't finished collecting. Because the *Atlas* is still out there on the grey water, and Lord Ashcombe is alive aboard her or awaiting her, and men who forge drownings do not forgive witnesses. Quill's third telegram, which came at dusk, was longer:

STORY SECURED STOP NAMES VERIFIED STOP ADVISE BOTH OF YOU DISAPPEAR IMMEDIATELY STOP ASHCOMBE NOT ON PASSENGER LIST STOP WHERE IS HE STOP

Rosalind read it and went very still, the bad stillness, the one I'd seen in her only once before.

"He isn't aboard," she said slowly. "Which means he never intended to sail with his own evidence. Which means he's coming ashore to find whoever took it."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere outside, a motor car drew up in the street below our window—too slow, too deliberate, engine idling—and did not move on.

Rosalind crossed to the curtain and looked down through the gap, and I watched her face change as she read the number plate, and heard her say, in a voice I had never heard her use, in a voice that belonged to the other name, the buried one:

"Ines. My father knows I'm here."
