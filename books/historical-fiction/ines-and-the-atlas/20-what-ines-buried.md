## 20. What Ines Buried

The cemetery at Millbrook sat above the water, which I had always thought was a cruelty and now decided was a kindness. From the low iron gate you could see the harbour—the grey stretch of it, the cranes standing like patient herons, the container ships easing out on the tide. Tomás had spent his whole short life wanting to be on that water. They had buried him within sight of it, and for two years I had come here whenever I could afford the train fare and stood at the foot of his stone with nothing to tell him but *I'm trying*.

Today I came with something better than trying.

I had bought flowers from a woman outside the station who called them *sweet Williams* as if she were introducing me to relatives. They were slightly bruised from the journey and so was I—three days since the Atlas sailed without us, two nights of sleep that kept breaking open on the sound of that shot, and one morning spent reading myself in a newspaper that cost a penny and had cost other men their liberty.

THE ATLAS SCANDAL: SHIPPING CHIEFS HELD, said the headline. Below it, in smaller type: *Documents obtained by this newspaper indicate that the sinking of the cargo vessel Atlas in 1911—attributed at the time to structural failure during storm conditions—was arranged by its owners for the insurance. The manifest pages reproduced here appear to bear the authorisation of—*

They hadn't printed my name. They hadn't printed Rosalind's. Quill had kept his word about that, though he'd looked at us across his desk like a man dividing a pudding he couldn't quite believe he'd been served. *Sources close to the matter*, he wrote. *A lady of courage whose identity we protect*. Lady Vane had laughed at that over breakfast—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind that made the parlourmaid drop a spoon. *A lady of courage*, she repeated. *There are two of them, Quill, and they are sitting in your kitchen.*

But none of that was why I'd come. I'd come because there was a stone with my brother's name on it, and beneath that name a lie that had held for two years, and today the lie was dead even if the boy under the stone still was.

TOMÁS INES REY, 1889–1911. BELOVED SON AND BROTHER. HE SERVED FAITHFULLY.

He had served faithfully. That was the bitter joke of it, the thing I'd carried like a stone in my shoe for two years—that he'd been faithful to the very men who signed him off the books. A clerk, twenty-two years old, careful with figures, proud of his beautiful copperplate hand. He'd copied out an order he wasn't meant to understand and hidden it in the lining of his coat instead of taking it to the captain or burning it or doing any of the sensible things, because Tomás believed evidence mattered. He believed the truth was a thing you could hold on to until someone strong enough came to lift it.

Then the Atlas went down in the Channel with nine men lost, and the newspapers said *structural failure*, and the owners collected their insurance, and the strikers at Southampton got blamed for unrest that nobody could prove had anything to do with anything, and my brother's careful copperplate went into the ground with him, sewn into a coat they nearly buried too.

I found the letter because grief makes you unreasonable. The family wanted to bury him quickly; I insisted on dressing him myself. The undertaker thought I was being devout. Portuguese girls from Southampton are often devout. He left me alone with my brother's body and I searched his coat seam by seam because the week before he died he had written to me—*if anything happens, Neta, look where you'd least want to look*—and I had thought he meant his savings tin.

* * *

The grass was wet and I didn't care. I knelt at the stone and put the sweet Williams in the built-in vase, which some previous mourner had half filled with rainwater, and I talked to him the way I always did, quietly, in Portuguese first and then in English, because Tomás had loved English, had practised it on the dockworkers until they stopped laughing at his vowels.

"They're arrested," I told him. "Do you hear me? All of them. Halloran first—he cried, they say, when they came for him. Cried like a man who never expected the day to arrive, though he must have known it would, he must have lain awake every night for two years knowing it." I pulled a weed from the base of the stone. "Mr Quill printed your page. Your handwriting, Tomás. In a national paper. It's not beautiful anymore the way it used to be, it's been torn and folded and rained on and there's a hole in one corner—" my voice did something I didn't authorise, and I waited it out, "—there's a hole where a bullet went through, and it's the most important piece of paper in England this fortnight. They're calling it evidence. They're calling it the Atlas letter."

The tide turned below the cemetery wall. I watched it, because Tomás would have wanted someone to watch it.

"You'd have liked how we did it," I went on. "It was ridiculous. There was a woman pretending to faint on the gangway and a chauffeuse who is now famous in three newspapers under a false name, and a spare tyre with a hollow centre that Lady Vane produced from somewhere as though she keeps hollow tyres about her person at all times, which honestly she might." I smiled at the stone. "You'd have liked Rosalind. You'd have liked her enormously and then you'd have been terrified of her, which is the correct response. She read every letter she ever carried and remembered all of them, and last Thursday that habit saved both our lives, because a customs man asked her about jute shipments and she answered him in the voice of a woman who has personally inventoried the British Empire."

The wind came up off the water and moved the sweet Williams. Somewhere behind me a sexton's barrow squeaked on the path and I ignored it. These were my minutes. I had bought them with a bullet hole and I intended to spend every one.

"And here is the thing I came to say," I said, "which is harder than the rest, so don't interrupt."

* * *

Here is what I had not told anyone, not even Rosalind—not properly, not in words arranged on purpose.

After the funeral I had a plan, and the plan was simple, and the plan was fire. I knew the name on the order—I'd read it a hundred times in that coat lining, *authorised, J. Ravensworth*—and I knew where Ravensworth kept offices in London, and I was nineteen years old and had nothing left to lose except the coat, which I slept with for a month like a madwoman. I went down to Southampton docks and got taken on as a steward's assistant on coastal runs, because ships were where the men were, and ships went where the men went, and I learned to carry trays and smile and listen. For two years I followed that conspiracy around the coast of England like a dog following a killer, waiting for one clear moment with one guilty throat.

And then, eight months ago, I heard a name in a first-class saloon off Lisbon. Not Ravensworth. A woman's name. *Rosalind Ravensworth*, said a gossipy dowager to her companion, *the Vane woman's driver—no relation, surely, to the shipping Ravensworths? What a coincidence. Or is it?*

I nearly went to Leeds then. I'll be honest with you, Tomás, I nearly went with the coat in my bag and something worse than questions in my heart. His daughter. His blood. If the father signed the order, the daughter owed me something, and I was angry enough to collect.

But I took a job in a Leeds hotel instead, and I watched the house on the hill for three weeks before I walked up to its service door, and what I saw in those three weeks changed my mind, though I fought it the whole way.

"I watched her drive your murderers' daughter through the city," I told the stone. "I watched Lady Vane get out at mills and factories and speak to women who worked fourteen hours a day, and I watched Rosalind stand beside the motor and watch those women the way you watch weather—like she was measuring something, like she was keeping accounts. And I thought: that woman keeps accounts too. Like you. Like your copperplate." I swallowed. "And I thought, maybe the account can be settled without another body. Maybe that's what you actually wanted, you ridiculous careful boy. Not vengeance. Evidence."

So I knocked at the service door with the oilskin packet under my coat, and I asked for the lady who fights, and I meant Lady Vane, and God has a sense of humour, because I got two of them.

* * *

"That's the confession," I said to Tomás. "That I stayed. Everyone thinks I stayed for you—for justice, for vengeance, pick your newspaper word. And I did come for that. But I stayed—" the weed-pulling had become useless work, so I made myself stop and put my hands flat on the cold grass, "—I stayed because on the second night, after the checkpoint, on the night train south, Rosalind woke me up at some terrible hour and asked if I was afraid, and when I said yes she didn't say *be brave* or *we're nearly there*. She said, 'Good. Fear means you've understood the situation correctly.' And then she sat with me until the fear was just information." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "Nobody had ever done that. Mam used to pray at me. You used to joke at me. This woman sat in a rattling dark carriage and treated my terror like a respectable guest.

"And then at King's Cross she lied to a man with a bowler hat so well that I believed her, and she did it for me, and I realised somewhere between Southampton and that gangway that I had stopped counting the days since you died. Do you understand? Two years of waking up and the first thought was always *Tomás is dead*, the way the tongue goes back to a broken tooth. And on the morning the Atlas sailed I woke up and my first thought was *the tide is at six*." My hands pressed the wet grass. "I'm sorry. I know how that sounds. But you told me once—you were fifteen and full of nonsense—you said, *Neta, promise me you'll belong to somebody. Being free is only worth it if there's somewhere to be free in.* I thought you were quoting a pamphlet. You probably were.

"So I'm telling you they can rest easy now, the men who did this, because they're in Wandsworth and Wormwood Scrubs and one of them is reportedly giving evidence against the others in exchange for consideration, which is the most satisfying sentence I have ever typed for Mr Quill. The strike leaders have been cleared—all nine names in the paper, publicly cleared, and two of them sent flowers to Lady Vane's house, which she pretended annoyed her. The Atlas is under arrest herself, impounded at Southampton, and they say she'll be sold for scrap, and I hope they break her up rivet by rivet and I hope it takes years.

"Your truth held, Tomás. You held it for two years in a coat lining, and it held. It's the strongest thing I ever carried."

* * *

I stayed until my knees complained. The light went long and gold over the harbour, and a tug went out, and I told him about the small things because the dead deserve small things: how Lady Vane had begun correcting people who called me "the Portuguese girl" with the flatness of a duellist; how Mrs Pym in the kitchen had taught me to make Yorkshire puddings and declared my first attempt "a hate crime against the cow"; how Rosalind had started leaving the letters unlocked on the hall table, which doesn't sound like much, but for a woman who built herself out of secrets it's practically a proposal.

Then, because the sexton was locking the gate soon, I stood, and brushed my skirt, and did the thing I'd been circling all afternoon.

"There's a stone going up in the churchyard at Headingley," I said. "Not yet—it's been ordered, it takes weeks—but it's ordered. RAVENSWORTH, it will say, and underneath it a date that isn't true, 1895, because the girl she used to be is legally dead and has decided to stay that way. She says the old name is a coat that never fit. She asked me—" I stopped. Started again. "She asked me if I would stand next to her when it's placed. Family, she said. If you'll have it."

The wind moved the flowers. The tide ran gold below the wall.

"I told her yes," I said. "Obviously I told her yes."

I touched the top of the stone, cold and certain under my palm, and I said goodbye in the old language and then in his favourite new one, and I walked down the path past the sexton and out the iron gate without looking back, because looking back is for people who are leaving something behind, and I had finally figured out that I wasn't.

The train north was crowded and I stood the whole way, wedged among sailors and salesmen, and I didn't mind. Somewhere past Sheffield the carriage lamp flickered and a woman opposite offered me a pear from a paper bag and we talked about nothing all the way to Leeds, and it was the most ordinary hour I'd spent in two years, and I hoarded it.

The house on the hill had one window lit. I came up the drive in the dark, let myself in the service door the way I now had a right to, and hung my wet coat—and there, on the hall table, deliberately, unmistakably placed where I would pass it, lay a letter.

Addressed in a hand I knew by heart. Unopened.

Rosalind stood at the foot of the stairs in her dressing gown, arms crossed, watching me read the envelope with the expression of a woman who has jumped and is waiting to find out if the water is deep.

"It came this morning," she said. "It's addressed to both of us. I haven't opened it. I thought—" and here her voice did something rare and human, something I'd only heard from her once before, on a night train— "I thought we should read it together."

My name was on it. Hers too. And above both, stamped in red, the seal of the Director of Public Prosecutions.
