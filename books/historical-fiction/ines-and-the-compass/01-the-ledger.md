## 1. The Ledger

The trouble with history, Astrid Crane had decided by her second winter at the hospital, was that it happened somewhere else.

It happened in ministries with marble floors and men whose boots were polished by other men. It happened at staff meetings she would never attend, on battlefields she could not see from the ward windows except as a dull orange flicker on the horizon at night, like a neighbor's bonfire that never went out. It happened to statesmen and generals and, occasionally, to the sorts of soldiers who ended up in the newspapers with their photographs printed slightly askew. History had a way of choosing its participants, and it had not chosen Astrid Crane, twenty-nine years old, assistant quartermaster-nurse of No. 7 Field Hospital, Channel Coast, keeper of linen stores and ledgers and the only functioning kettle in Ward C.

What happened to Astrid was inventory.

She counted things. She counted bandage rolls and splints, morphine ampoules and tins of condensed milk, blankets and bedpans and the forty-three spoons remaining of an original sixty (soldiers stole spoons the way magpies stole silver; she had long ago stopped taking it personally). She counted the men who came through the doors of the receiving tent and she counted them again when they left, one way or the other, and she wrote both numbers down in her careful, upright hand, because numbers that are not written down are merely opinions.

It was on a grey Tuesday in March, with rain coming off the Channel sideways and the stove in Ward C sulking smokelessly, that Private Dennis Whitlock died of a chest wound at four minutes past two in the afternoon.

Astrid knew it was four minutes past two because she was holding his wrist, counting his pulse the way she counted everything, when it simply declined to be counted anymore. Dr. Merrick looked up from the next cot, saw her face, and nodded once. Matron was elsewhere. The orderlies came for the body. And Astrid did what she always did, which was to find the private's file and make the entry.

The file was thin. They were all thin lately. *Whitlock, D., Pte., 2/9th Btn.* Admission noted. Treatment noted — or the abbreviation of treatment, since paper was scarce and ink scarcer. And then, in the column where the outcome belonged, a line drawn through his name in an officer's hand, and beneath it the notation:

*Deceased. No record.*

She sat with that for a moment. *No record.* As though he had arrived without a name. As though the man who had told her, hoarsely, three days earlier, that his mother kept bees and sent him honey when the post allowed it (it rarely allowed) had been nobody at all. As though the spoon she had slipped into his hand at supper last week — his last full meal — had been stolen rather than lent.

"Well," Astrid said to the empty file, in the tone she reserved for misbehaving stoves and short-changing quartermasters, "that won't do."

She took out her own notebook.

It was an ordinary notebook, purchased before the war from W.H. Smith & Son for sixpence, its cover softened now by handling. In it, on earlier pages, she had recorded such vital intelligence as *stove C eats coal twice as fast as stove B — investigate* and *Orderly Pike cannot tell lavender water from carbolic, DO NOT let him near the linens,* and, on one memorable occasion, *Dr. M. sang all of 'Pack Up Your Troubles' during an amputation and got every word wrong; the patient laughed, which I count a success.*

Now she turned to a fresh page and ruled it neatly with her thumbnail and pencil, three columns, and wrote at the top, after a moment's thought:

*Names. Properly.*

Then: *Whitlock, Dennis. Pte., 2/9th. Died 4 mins past 2, Tuesday, of a chest wound. Mother keeps bees. Liked honey.*

It was not much. It was, she supposed, barely anything. But it was more than *no record*, and she had learned long ago that more-than-nothing, done consistently, in a careful hand, has a way of accumulating into something no one can take apart.

*

"You've got your thinking face on."

Sister Edith Lowndes said this while extracting a fresh bandage roll from the supply shelf with the precision of a bank teller, and without looking around, because Edith had eyes distributed about her person in a manner science could not explain. She was sixty if she was a day, had nursed in some corner of the Empire since the age of seventeen, and held the firm conviction that Astrid was far too clever for her own good — a conviction Astrid had never successfully argued against, largely because it was true.

"I haven't got a thinking face," said Astrid. "I have one face. It does everything."

"It does everything badly, then, because it's doing your thinking right now where anyone can see it." Edith snapped the roll into her kit. "Whatever it is, write it down in your little book and stop frowning at the shelves. You'll put a crease in the calico."

Edith knew about the little book. Not its contents — Astrid was not fool enough — but of its existence, having caught Astrid writing in it within the first month of her posting. Edith had looked at the notebook, looked at Astrid, said, "Mind it doesn't fall into the fire," and returned to her work, and the matter had never been raised since. This was Edith's method of loyalty: total, unspoken, and delivered alongside criticism of one's posture.

"Edith," Astrid said, lowering her voice even though the ward corridor was empty, "when you were at Netley — the files there, the ones marked *no record*. Was that common?"

Edith paused. Only someone who had known her for years would have seen it — a half-second's stillness in the hands.

"Common enough," she said. "Paperwork's the first casualty of any war. Everyone knows that."

"And the second?"

"The men." Edith picked up her kit. "Though nobody likes to say so out loud. Come along, there's a convoy due at five and you haven't checked the morphine since Thursday."

That was the thing about Edith. She answered questions honestly, and then made certain you understood that honesty was exhausting and you should be ashamed of yourself for requiring it.

Astrid checked the morphine.

*

The convoy came at seven instead of five, because convoys, like history, went where they pleased. Twenty-two stretchers carried in from the ambulances, twenty-two men, and Astrid stood at the receiving tent with her clipboard and her lamp, taking names, ranks, wounds, regiments, in her careful upright hand, while the rain tried to get into everything including, apparently, her collar from below.

"Name?"

"Owen, sir. Corporal. Owen."

"There we are. Wound?"

"Leg. Shrapnel. It's not so bad."

"Everyone says it's not so bad," Astrid said, making the note. "If it were actually not so bad, you'd say it was terrible, out of disappointment. Where were you hit?"

"Near Ypres."

"Doing what?"

He blinked at her. Most of them blinked at her. Nobody asked the wounded what they'd been *doing*; the wound was considered sufficient information. But Astrid had learned that the doing mattered — not for the file, particularly, but because men who had just come out of hell liked to be asked questions that presumed they had been people beforehand.

"Rations detail," he said slowly. "Supply convoy. Bringing up stores from the coast road."

Something ticked at the back of Astrid's mind, small, like the stove deciding whether to draw. Supply convoy. There had been others — she remembered the entries now, scattered through the autumn pages of the official register. Men off supply details, off rations convoys, off the unglamorous business of moving boxes from one place to another, turning up in her ward and then turning up struck through.

"Nineteenth of October," she said. "Were you out then? On the coast road?"

His face closed like a shop shutter. It was quick, and it was practiced, and Astrid had catalogued enough frightened men to know what practice looked like.

"That was before my time," he said.

"You just said—"

"I said near Ypres." His voice had gone flat and pleasant, the voice of a man reciting. "I'm tired, miss. Is there a bed?"

There was a bed. There were eleven beds, and hot water, and a clean nightshirt, and Astrid gave him all of these, and smiled at him, and wrote in her clipboard *Cpl. Owen, shrapnel, leg, convoy duty* with the same steady hand she always used, and hated how steady it was.

Later, in the linen store, among the smells of soap and lavender (clearly labelled now, in Pike's defense), she opened the sixpenny notebook and added to the page:

*Cpl. R. Owen, supply detail, admitted tonight, leg. Went white at mention of Oct. 19th. Lied to me, and lied well. Somebody taught him that lie.*

And beneath it, after drawing the line across:

*N.B. Third one this month off a supply convoy. Counting is my job. Someone ought to have noticed I can still do it.*

*

The officers' dispatches came through on Fridays, carried over from headquarters in a leather wallet by a dispatch rider who never removed his goggles indoors, giving him the permanent appearance of a large startled insect. The dispatches listed casualties, confirmed admissions, requested supplies, and generally treated the hospital as a slow-witted relative whose correspondence must be answered but not read closely.

It was Astrid who reconciled them against the registers — another of her unglamorous duties, and one she performed with genuine pleasure, because reconciliation was arithmetic, and arithmetic never lied, unlike nearly everyone else in France.

This Friday, reconciling the February dispatches against her own admission book, she found it again.

*Pvt. T. Ainsley, 2/9th Btn.* Admitted to No. 7 on the ninth of January, gunshot, shoulder. Discharged to base hospital on the twenty-second, walking, complaining about the food — she remembered him, a wiry Yorkshireman who had taught the whole ward a song unfit for print. In the February dispatch, under casualties: *Ainsley, T., Pvt., died of wounds.*

Astrid set down her pen.

She got up, walked to the window, watched the grey sea perform its grey tricks for a full minute, and came back and sat down again, because this was her method: verify, verify, verify. Perhaps an error. Errors happened. Perhaps a different Ainsley. Common enough name, Ainsley.

But she had discharged him herself. Walking. Insulting the porridge. And the file — she pulled it, and there it was, recently stamped, an officer's correction laid over the original discharge like a hand pressed over a mouth. *Deceased. No record.*

Two names now on the secret page, plus Owen's observation, plus the pattern forming itself quietly underneath, patient as tide. Men off the supply convoys. Men who passed through her ward alive and left it as words in a ledger. *No record*, written by somebody whose job, like hers, was keeping records — which meant somebody, somewhere, was keeping two sets of books, and hers was the honest one.

"Right," Astrid said softly, to the lamp, to the lavender-scented dark, to nobody. "Let's see how many of you there are."

She began going backwards through the registers. January. December. November. Her lamp burned low and she trimmed it and worked on, lips moving, ticking names, striking nothing out, finding them one by one the way one finds loose stitches in a seam — and each one she found she copied onto the secret page in full, name, regiment, date, wound, the small human details she could recall: the man who hummed hymns, the boy who'd cried for his brother, the one who'd traded his cigarettes for her pencils because, he'd said, a person can't write letters without pencils, miss, and a person's got to write letters.

By two in the morning she had nine names.

Nine men recorded dead who had walked out of her ward under their own power, most of them, waving goodbye. Nine *no records*. All of them touched, directly or indirectly, by the supply convoys on the coast road. All of them struck through in the same hand — she was nearly sure it was the same hand, the capital letters with that particular hard serif, the numbers made with a flat-topped 1 like a nail.

Astrid closed the notebook and sat holding it in both hands, feeling its small weight change character entirely. Sixpence, it had cost. It felt heavier than any parcel she'd ever signed for.

History happened elsewhere, she had believed. To other people. To men in marble rooms. She had been content — genuinely, comfortably content — to stand at the edge of it with her clipboard, catching the drips.

But somebody was editing. Somebody with a nail-headed 1 was reaching into her ward, her registers, her careful arithmetic, and subtracting living men from the record — and if there was one thing Astrid Crane could not abide, professionally, personally, down to the marrow of her tidy soul, it was bad bookkeeping.

"They'll want a reason for the discrepancy," she murmured. "Someone will come asking."

Outside, the rain had stopped. The sea worked at the shingle below the sea-wall, patient, rhythmic, indifferent — the sound, she thought suddenly, of a very large page being turned.

She blew out the lamp and went to bed, and did not sleep.

Three days later, at half past midnight, with the ward asleep and the stove finally behaving, a sentry's whistle split the dark by the north gate — and Astrid, sitting up with the lamp still warm beside her, heard the sound of running feet in the wet gravel, coming toward the hospital, and only one pair of them.
