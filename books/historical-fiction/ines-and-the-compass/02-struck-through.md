## 2. Struck Through

The rain came in off the Channel that October like a bill collector — patient, inevitable, and personally offended by the state of things. It found every gap in the hospital's corrugated roof and dripped with great precision into the enamel basin I had placed beneath the worst of them three days earlier, as if it had studied the arrangement and approved.

"You're missing," I told the leak.

The leak did not answer. Very little at Number Four Field Hospital answered me directly, which was one of the reasons I had come to love my ledger so desperately. Paper, at least, listened.

I should explain about the ledger, though I confess even now that explaining makes it sound smaller than it was. Officially, I kept the admission book — the tidy, sanctioned record of who arrived at our door and in what condition. Name, rank, regiment, wound, disposition. The army is fond of dispositions; the word suggests a gentleness the practice does not deliver. And then there was the other book, the one I kept in the bottom drawer of my desk beneath a false bottom made from the lid of a biscuit tin and a certain amount of moral flexibility. In that book I wrote the names of men who appeared in my ward and then declined, thereafter, to appear anywhere at all.

It had started, as most sins do, with tidiness.

In June a corporal named Whitlock had passed through my ward with a shrapnel wound to the shoulder, cheerful as a church bell, and two weeks later I saw his name in the casualty list — killed in action, they said, on a date when I had personally held his hair back while he was sick from the anaesthetic. I wrote to the records office. I received back a correction slip so bland it could have been used as a pillow: *Entry amended. Disregard prior.* And something in me — the same something that cannot walk past an unswept floor or an unbalanced column — had taken up a pen and begun a second set of books.

I told myself it was bookkeeping. I was good at bookkeeping. It was, in the absence of any other talent, my contribution to the war: while other women rolled bandages and drove ambulances, I noticed things and wrote them down in a small hand that Matron once described as "suspiciously legible."

I did not think of myself as defying anyone. Defiance required courage, and courage required believing that what you did mattered. I believed, in those days, that history happened elsewhere — in ministers' offices, in headquarters far behind the lines, in places with carpets. Here at the edge of the Channel we had only weather, wounds, and paperwork, and of the three, paperwork was the only one that behaved.

*

Private Daniel Foss arrived on a Tuesday, though the ambulance that brought him had lost its way twice and swore afterward it was a Friday. He was twenty-three years old, a grocer's son from somewhere flat and fen-like where the churches lean, and he had a fragment of shell lodged against his spine that Surgeon-Lieutenant Pryce examined once and then discussed with me in the corridor, in the particular tone doctors reserve for conversations they wish the walls to overhear incorrectly.

"How long?" I asked, when the tone had finished.

Pryce took off his spectacles, cleaned them on his sleeve, put them back on, and looked at me over the top of them, which for Pryce constituted weeping.

"Days," he said. "Perhaps a week if he's stubborn. He'll have feeling in his legs until nearly the end, which is the cruel part. Write him up comfortably, Sister Crane."

*Write him up comfortably.* As though comfort were a kind of ink.

Foss himself had no intention of dying quietly, and certainly no intention of doing it in a bed. He wanted to be propped up where he could see the window, and he wanted to talk, and since half my ward was sleeping off surgery and the other half was pretending to, I let him talk at me while I worked. He told me about his father's shop, and the particular dishonesty of customers who taste six kinds of cheese and buy Cheddar. He told me he had been saving to marry a girl named Ada who worked the telegraph office and laughed at everything, "even things that aren't funny, Sister, which is either a virtue or a warning."

"Both," I said. "Marry her anyway."

"I mean to." He shifted against the pillows and winced in a way he tried to disguise as stretching. "That's the trouble with wars. They interrupt a fellow at the interesting bit."

I liked him enormously within an hour, which is a professional hazard I never learned to guard against. Matron says — said — that a nurse who loves her patients is a nurse preparing to be hurt, and that the trick is to care in bulk, generically, the way one cares about the weather. I have never managed it. I care specifically. It is my worst quality and the only one I would defend under oath.

*

The file arrived on Thursday morning, carried by a dispatch rider who left it on my desk without dismounting, in the manner of a man delivering bad news to as many places as possible before lunch.

I opened it expecting the usual folder of duplications. Instead I found a single card, pre-printed, pre-stamped, and pre-concluded:

**FOSS, D., PTE., 2/7th — DECEASED. NO RECORD.**

I read it four times. The rain dripped into its basin. Somewhere down the ward a man moaned in his sleep and another man told him kindly to shut up, and the ordinary machinery of the hospital went on grinding, and I stood at my desk holding a piece of cardboard that had decided, before I ever met him, that Daniel Foss was already finished.

*No record.* Not *killed in action*, which at least had the decency to be a story. Not *missing*, which left a door open. No record. As if he had been not merely struck from the list but unlisted — removed retroactively, unwritten.

I want to be honest about my first feeling, because my first feeling was not righteous anger. My first feeling was clerical. Somewhere in my chest, in the small dry chamber where I keep my professional instincts, a red pencil hovered. *Improper form,* it said. *A death requires a date, a place, a cause. This is not a record. This is an absence wearing a stamp.*

And then the second feeling arrived, slower and heavier, and I understood that the red pencil was beside the point. A card like this did not contain an error. Errors are lonely things; this card had brothers. I thought of Whitlock, amended and disregarded. I thought of the other names that had drifted through my ledger and out of the army's memory — not many, a handful across the summer, but I had written them all in my second book precisely because they shared a family resemblance, the resemblance of things tidied away.

I took out the biscuit-tin drawer and laid the cards side by side in my mind, and then in fact, which was riskier. Seven names. Whitlock. The two gunners from August whose ambulance had come in from the coast road at odd hours. A lance-corporal whose file I had never seen at all, only his dog tags, sent along later like an apology. And others. Men who had passed through my ward breathing and complaining and asking after their girls, and who had thereafter ceased, officially, ever to have existed.

I am not a clever woman, whatever else may be said of me, but I can balance a column, and seven entries in a column will tell you what they have in common if you look long enough.

They had all come in on the same days of the week — Tuesdays and Fridays, the convoy days, when lorries ran the coast road from the supply depot at Harcourt. They had all belonged, loosely, to the great unmoving river of men and machines that kept the front fed: drivers, stevedores, escorts, clerks. Not infantry. Not men who died gloriously at the sharp end. Men who *drove things* and *counted things* and *moved things* — men, in other words, who might have seen where the things went.

I sat with that thought for a long moment and then put it away carefully, the way you put away a knife you are not yet ready to need.

*

On Friday evening Foss sent for me.

I knew before I reached his bed. There is a change in the air around a dying man in his last days, a stillness, as though the ward has agreed to speak more quietly around him forever. His color had gone the grey of the sea that morning, and his hands — a grocer's hands, blunt and careful — lay on the blanket making small precise movements, tallying invisible stock.

"Sister Crane." He smiled when he saw me, and the smile cost him something and he paid it anyway. "You're the one who writes things down."

"I keep the books," I admitted. "Among other crimes."

"That's what I heard." He wet his lips. "The other fellows say you're particular. That you get the spellings right and all."

"Foss," I said. "One S, two... no. Two S's. One F."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and I held the cup for him and hated how familiar the gesture had become in thirty-six hours.

"They sent a man round yesterday," he said, when he had breath again. "From the records chaps. While you were off your supper. Asked me questions in a whisper, like we were in church. Whether I'd driven out to Harcourt in September. What I'd seen on the road." His eyes came up to mine, and they were clear — clearer than they had been in days, with that terrible lucidity the body sometimes grants as a farewell gift. "I told him I mostly seen mud and other lorries. Which was true. Mostly."

"Mostly," I repeated gently.

"Sister." He reached out and caught my sleeve, and his grip was still strong, a grip built for sacks of flour. "When I go — and don't you dare argue with me about when, you've that look Pryce gets — I want it done proper. Written down. Date, place, the whole thing. Ada's got to have a paper that says I existed, or she'll wait for me her whole life, and she's got a laugh to spend on someone worth it." His voice cracked and he pressed on through the crack like a man crossing ice. "Someone sent me a card today. Or a card come for me. Says deceased, no record. No record! Twenty-three years and a girl waiting and a dad with a shop full of cheeses nobody appreciates, and some clerk stamps *no record* like I'm a lost parcel."

The basin caught a drop of rain. Down the ward, someone's radio, contraband and beloved, murmured a dance tune with the volume of a secret.

"I'll write it properly," I said. "I promise you, Private Foss. Everything I know, written plainly, signed with my name."

"With your name?" He frowned, puzzled, as if I had offered to pay for his funeral. "Sister, it's my name that wants writing. Yours is yours."

"Yes," I said, and found, to my surprise, that my throat needed clearing. "But it seems to me lately that somebody ought to stand behind the ink."

He studied me for a moment, and then nodded slowly, the nod of a man closing a bargain, and settled back into his pillows.

"Then write it big," he said. "I was always told my hand was too small to read."

*

He lasted until Sunday, being, as predicted, stubborn. I sat with him through the last afternoon because there was nothing else medically useful to do, and he talked about Ada in loops, circling her name the way a moth circles a lamp, and near the end he asked me whether telegraph girls were allowed to marry grocers and I said absolutely not, it was forbidden, and he said *good*, satisfied, as if prohibition were the surest proof of love, and perhaps it is.

At ten minutes past six he stopped, between one sentence and the next, mid-loop, and the ward went on around us because wards must.

I did my duties. I did them all, in order, competently, and then I went to my desk and I did one more.

In the official register I entered him as the card demanded: *FOSS, D., PTE. Deceased. No record.* My hand shook on the first letter and steadied by the third, and I despised the sentence even as my fingers produced it, neat as treason.

Then I unlocked the biscuit-tin drawer, and in my own book, the true one, I gave him what he had asked for. The date. The place — this ward, this leaking roof, this basin catching rain. The cause, stated honestly. And beneath it, in my best and largest hand, the words he had earned: *Daniel Foss, of Crane & Son Grocers — no. Of Foss & Son. He was going to be a partner. He was loved by a girl named Ada, who laughs at everything. He existed.*

I signed it. My own name, full and plain: Astrid Mary Crane, Sister, QAIMNS. Standing behind the ink, as promised.

It was only when I had blotted the page and sat looking at it that I felt the first real crack — not in my detachment, which I had always privately suspected was mostly posture, but in the story I had been telling myself about my position in the world. For four years I had believed that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in carpeted rooms, and that my business was bandages and arithmetic. But Daniel Foss had died at ten minutes past six on a Sunday with his hand in mine, and some clerk in a carpeted room had already decided, days in advance, that it hadn't happened. History was not happening elsewhere. History was happening *here*, and it was being written by whoever held the pen, and the pen, at present, was held by men who stamped *no record* on living boys.

Well. I had a pen too. Mine leaked less.

I copied the seven names onto a fresh page, arranged by convoy date, and stared at them until the pattern stood up out of the paper like a figure from a photograph developing: Tuesday convoys, Friday convoys, the coast road, Harcourt depot. Every omitted man had run that road. Something moved along it that the army did not want counted, and the men who might have counted it were being counted out instead.

I was still sitting there, cold to the elbows with understanding, when Matron's bell rang twice — her signal for *come at once, and bring discretion*. And when I reached her office, slightly breathless, she was standing at the window with her arms folded, looking down at the gate below, where a lamp swung in the wind and two sentries were arguing with the dark.

"There's a man at the wire," Matron said, without turning. "Mud to the eyebrows, no papers, and he won't give a name. He's carrying something wrapped in oilskin, and he holds it" — she paused, choosing the word with care — "*possessively*. The sergeant wants to fetch the military police."

"And you?" I asked.

Matron turned then, and her face wore the expression of a woman about to make a great deal of trouble for exactly the right person.

"I thought," she said, "that you might know something about men whom the records have misplaced."
