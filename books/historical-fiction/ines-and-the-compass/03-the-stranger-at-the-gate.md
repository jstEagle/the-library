## 3. The Stranger at the Gate

I had been walking for nine days when I decided that the sea would be the death of me.

Not the war. Not the military police with their whistles and their lanterns and their tedious conviction that every man in France was either a hero or a criminal, with no room in between. The sea. Because the closer I came to it, the flatter the land grew, and the more the sky opened up like a great grey eye, and the fewer places there were to hide. A man can vanish into a wood. A man cannot vanish into a beet field that has been harvested down to its stubble and lies under moonlight like a shaved head.

So it was that on the ninth night, cold through, hungry past the point where hunger complains and into the region where it simply goes quiet, I crested a low dune and saw — spread below me like something out of a child's drawing of salvation — lights. Rows of them. Tents, huts, a long low building with a red cross painted on its roof so large you could probably read it from heaven, assuming heaven was paying attention, which by then I had my doubts about.

A field hospital. On the Channel coast. Which meant orderlies, and nurses, and wounded men who had no interest whatsoever in chasing deserters, and — this was the part I said aloud to myself, because nine days alone will make a chatterbox of anyone — *food*.

It also meant sentries. Two of them, as it turned out, posted at the gate with the particular slouch of men who had been standing in the same cold wind for four hours and had concluded, reasonably enough, that no one in their right mind would try to sneak into a hospital. People sneaked *out*. The whole architecture of the place faced the wrong way, if you take my meaning. The wire, the lamps, the watching eyes — all of it arranged against escape, not arrival.

Which is how a man as honest as I once was comes to learn the habits of dishonesty. You do not creep toward what people are guarding. You limp away from it.

I got myself filthy — not difficult, I regret to say; I'd been rehearsing that particular effect all week — tore my sleeve, muddied my face, and came staggering out of the dunes along the wire's flank, weaving like a man who has forgotten which way is down. Then I fell over. This required no acting whatsoever. My knees had been threatening resignation for two days and chose that moment to accept.

"Sentry!" I croaked, from the ground. "Sentry — water —"

Boots came running. There is nothing in the world that gets a soldier moving faster than another soldier face-down in the dirt; we are all haunted by the fear that someday the man on the ground will be us, and no one will run.

"Here, what's this — Christ, he's in a state." Hands under my arms, hauling me upright. A young face, freckled, deeply worried. "Where did you come from? There's nothing out that way but dunes and dead rabbits."

"Dunes," I agreed, and let my eyes roll back a little, which is easier than it sounds when you haven't eaten since Tuesday. "Convoy. Got separated. Days ago. Please —"

He didn't ask which convoy. That should have told me something, even through the fog in my head: he didn't ask, because asking was above his pay, and because a hospital gate at night collects strays the way a lamp collects moths, and the moths are somebody else's paperwork. He whistled up his mate, and between them they half-carried me through the gate I could never have passed upright and honest, and I remember thinking, as the smell of carbolic and coal smoke washed over me, that I had just committed my second crime in three weeks and hadn't even broken stride.

The first crime, in case you're wondering, was running. Though whether running from a thing is a crime depends entirely on what the thing turns out to be, and I would have defended myself on that point at any tribunal in the land — a statement of intent, you might call it, given that I eventually had cause to make one.

They dumped me, gently enough, on a stretcher in a receiving tent, and a porter with a candle looked me over and pronounced me "more dirt than damage," which I took as a compliment to my endurance and an insult to my dramatics.

"He says convoy," the freckled sentry offered. "Separated."

"Mm," said the porter, who had the gift of saying *mm* in a way that meant *I have heard eleven stories tonight and yours is the fourth-worst lie among them.* But he was tired, and I was nobody's emergency, and so I was left on the stretcher with a blanket that smelled of other men's fevers, and I lay there listening to the tent breathe around me — coughing, muttering, someone far off weeping without any sound at all, which is the worst kind — and I held the oilskin packet against my belly with both hands the way you hold a wound.

You should understand something about that packet. For nine days it had been the only fixed point in my universe. Everything else — the hedgerows, the barns, the culvert I slept in outside Abbeville with rats who had no respect for rank — all of it shifted and blurred. But the packet stayed. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Some things weigh more than they are; grief works that way too.

And then she appeared.

I didn't know her name yet. All I knew was that a woman came down the row of stretchers with a lamp in one hand and a ledger under her arm, and she stopped at mine, and she looked at me the way no one had looked at me in nine days — which is to say, *at* me, rather than through me or past me or around me in search of someone more important.

She was not beautiful in the way that makes a man forget his own name; she was beautiful in the way that makes him remember it, and wonder what he's been doing with it. Dark hair coming loose from its cap. Eyes that had done a great deal of arithmetic.

"You're not on my list," she said.

Now, a sensible deserter would have produced his convoy story again, polished and ready. But I was tired, and hungry, and there is something about being looked at properly after a week of being invisible that loosens the tongue.

"No," I said.

"Convoy?"

"Of a kind."

She considered this. The lamp hissed. Somewhere behind her, a man called out for his mother in a voice like tearing calico, and she didn't flinch, and I noticed that — the not-flinching. You can tell everything about a person by what they've stopped flinching at.

"You're not wounded," she said. It wasn't an accusation. It was almost gentle. "You're starving, you're frozen half-through, and your hands won't stop gripping that packet, but you're not wounded."

"How can you tell?"

"Because wounded men hold still." She set the lamp on a crate and crouched to my level, and I caught the smell of soap under the carbolic, clean and ordinary, like a church vestry or my mother's kitchen, and I am not ashamed to say my eyes stung. "Who are you?"

"Ines," I said. Just the one name. It seemed safer.

"That's not a soldier's name."

"It's my mother's. She had opinions about what to call children and no one with the authority to stop her."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The *ancestor* of a smile, the fossil record of one. "Well, Ines-with-one-name. You have until the medical officer comes round, which is twenty minutes, perhaps thirty if he stops for the port he thinks I don't know about. In twenty minutes you are going to be asked questions by men whose questions have consequences. So decide quickly which sort of story you want to be standing in when they arrive."

"I don't —"

"You're holding a letter," she said, "in oilskin. Oilskin is for keeping paper dry, and paper worth keeping dry is paper worth drowning for. And you keep looking at the tent flap. Every ten seconds. Like a fox at a henhouse door. I've been keeping records in this hospital for eight months, Private Nobody, and reading faces longer than that, and yours is the face of a man who is not merely lost but *hiding*, and I would like to know, before the officers get here and start writing things down about you, whether hiding is the right thing for you to be doing. Because sometimes it is. That's the part they never put in the sermons."

I stared at her. Nine days of ditches, and the first person to see me clearly was a nurse with a ledger.

"If I tell you," I said slowly, "you'll have to decide something too. And I wouldn't wish my decisions on anyone."

"Deciding is my profession," she said. "Triage. It means choosing who gets help first. Go on."

And God forgive me, I nearly told her everything, right there among the stretcher handles and the fevered breathing — the convoy, the diversion, the captain, the names read aloud and then struck away as though a pen could unmake a man. But the tent flap moved, and Matron came through it.

You know the type. Spine like a flagpole, voice like a slammed drawer, eyes that had seen every trick invented by soldiers since the Flood and were not impressed by any of them, including mine. She took one look at me — mud, no wounds, oilskin packet, eyes at the tent flap — and arrived at the correct conclusion before I'd finished arranging my face.

"A stray," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Found at the wire," the freckled sentry said helpfully. "Claims convoy."

"They all claim convoy." She looked down at me the way you look at weather coming in off the sea. "Regimental number."

I gave her one. It belonged to a man I had buried a piece of my heart next to, and I used it without hesitation, which is the sort of thing I had become, and I felt the nurse's eyes on me while I did it, and knew she'd heard the hesitation I thought I'd hidden.

Matron wrote it down. "Desertion is a capital matter," she said, conversationally, to the tent in general. "Strays go to the provost in the morning. He keeps a cell for them. Warm, at least." She turned to go, and paused, and I have replayed that pause a thousand times since and still cannot tell you whether it was mercy or theatre. "No one leaves this tent tonight. Understood?"

Understood, said everyone, meaning none of us.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic of that night, laid out plainly, because I have learned that plainness is braver than poetry:

If I stayed, I was taken at dawn by the provost, and the packet went into an evidence locker, and whatever Captain Vane had wanted done with it died in a drawer. If I ran, there was wire, and lamps, and a coastline flattening out to nothing, and I was nine days empty, and my knees had already handed in their notice.

There was a third option. I saw it only because I was watching her.

The nurse worked her way down the row and back, changing dressings, adjusting pillows, writing in her ledger — always the ledger, a small black book she touched more often than most men touch their own hearts. And twice, passing my stretcher, she did something small and deliberate: she set a mug of something hot where my hand could find it, and later she draped an extra blanket over my legs with a tuck at the end so neat it was practically a signature.

And between those two kindnesses, she knelt by a private three stretchers down — a boy dying by inches, quietly, the way some of them did, ashamed of the fuss — and I heard her say, "Can you tell me your name again? Properly? All of it?" And the boy told her, and she wrote it down in the little black book with the care of a woman signing a treaty, and I understood that I was in the presence of someone else who believed that names mattered, which is a dangerous belief in wartime, and possibly the only belief worth anything at all.

When the medical officer made his round — smelling, as predicted, faintly and guiltily of port — she stood close by and answered his questions before he could think to ask mine. *Stray, likely shell-shocked, half-starved, no wounds, Matron's orders, morning.* He grunted, shone his torch in my eyes, found nothing there he recognized as a threat, and moved on. Port is a merciful officer.

Afterward the tent settled. The lamps were turned low. The weeping man stopped, one way or another; I choose to believe he slept.

And then she came back, and sat on the edge of my stretcher as casually as if visiting a friend, and said, without preamble: "The linen store has a window high on the north side, and a lock older than the war. The key lives on a hook in the corridor, third from the left, beside the ones nobody uses. If a person were foolish enough to be in that corridor around midnight, and foolish enough to take that key, and foolish enough to hide in that store until the guard rotation changed at two — well. I couldn't say what became of him. I'm very busy at night. I see very little."

I looked at her for a long moment. "Why?" It came out rougher than I meant. "You don't know me. You don't know what I've done or what I'm carrying. Turning me over would be the correct thing. The safe thing. Nobody would ever —"

"Nobody would ever know," she finished for me. "Yes. I've noticed that about this war. Most of what happens in it, nobody ever knows." She looked at her hands, then at me, and her voice dropped to something meant only for the space between us. "Three months ago I started writing down names. Names that go missing from the dispatches. Men who pass through this ward alive and appear nowhere afterward except struck through in someone else's book. Eleven of them now. I don't know why they're struck through. But I know that a hospital is supposed to be where the truth about a war gets written down last, by whoever survives to write it — and lately the pens are being taken away from us." She stood, smoothed her apron, and was once again entirely a nurse, brisk and unreadable. "Midnight, Private Nobody. Third hook from the left. And eat the rest of that bread — you'll need your legs."

She was gone before I found my voice.

* * *

I did not go to the linen store because I trusted her. Let the record show that. I went because the alternative was the provost's warm cell, and because a man carrying another man's last words develops a superstition about chances: you do not refuse them, whatever shape they come in, however much they look like trouble wearing a nurse's cap.

But somewhere between midnight and the third hook from the left, moving down that dark corridor with the key cold in my fist and my heart going like a drum corps, I realized the fear had changed. For nine days it had been the dumb animal fear of pursuit — of boots behind me, of names called out over parade grounds, of rope. This was different. This was smaller and hotter and pointed forward instead of backward. Fear, it turns out, is directional. Hers had aimed mine at the door of the linen store instead of away from it.

The lock was older than the war, as promised; it complained, then surrendered. Inside: shelves of folded sheets, pillowcases stacked like snowdrifts, and air that smelled of starch and lavender and something underneath both — dust, and patience. I wedged myself into the gap behind the tallest shelf with the packet against my chest and waited for two o'clock, and in the waiting I heard, distantly, the tide turning, and a bugle somewhere, and once — clear across the yard, through two walls of canvas — her voice, low and steady, giving a name back to some boy who had begun to forget his own.

I pressed the packet tighter and thought: *she writes them down. Whoever strikes them through, she writes them down.*

And I thought: *maybe that's what the captain meant me to find. Not a safe pair of hands. A stubborn pair.*

At two o'clock the guard changed. Boots crunched gravel, voices swapped complaints, and a door somewhere opened onto darkness and the smell of the sea.

And then, soft as a confession, footsteps stopped outside the linen store door — and a key turned in the lock from the *outside*, and her voice came through the wood, barely louder than the tide:

"The sentries are doubled tonight. Something's spooked them. Stay where you are — and Ines?" A pause, in which I heard her decide something. "In the morning, you're going to have to tell me whose handwriting that is. Because I've seen it before. Three times. Struck through in black ink. And the man who wrote it is supposed to be dead."

The footsteps moved away. I sat in the dark among the clean sheets with my heart hammering out questions faster than I could silence them, and above me, crossing the ceiling of the world, boots went by — too many boots, moving with purpose, toward the wards.

Toward, I would learn soon enough, her ledgers.

But that night I knew only this: I had walked nine days to deliver a dead man's letter, and delivered it instead to the one living soul in France who already missed him.
