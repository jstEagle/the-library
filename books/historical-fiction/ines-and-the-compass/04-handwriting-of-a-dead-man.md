## 4. Handwriting of a Dead Man

Ines had never been much of a judge of women, or men, or dogs, or horses — his father had said so often enough — but he judged the nurse who came down into the linen store at dawn with a candle and a heel of bread to be the finest creature he had ever seen.

She set the bread on a crate beside him without a word, as though feeding a fugitive in a hospital linen store were a service she performed nightly, like turning mattresses. Then she sat down on an upturned bucket, folded her hands, and looked at him with the particular patience of a woman who counted things for a living.

"You've been staring at that packet all night," she said.

"I haven't been staring."

"You have. The way a man stares at a dog that's bitten him once and might again." She nodded at the oilskin bundle in his lap, which he was indeed holding at arm's length, the way one holds something both precious and possibly explosive. "You carried it through half of France and past two sentries who'd have shot you for the buttons on your coat. And now you won't open it."

"It's not mine to open," Ines said. "Not really. I mean — it is. He gave it to me. That's rather the whole point of me."

"Who?"

Ines looked at her. She had flour or ash or some third thing smudged along one jaw, and her eyes were the grey of the Channel in November, which is to say the colour of everything important. He had made a rule, somewhere in the mud between Arras and here, that he would tell no one anything ever again. Rules, he had learned, were made of paper, and paper burned.

"Captain Elias Vane," he said.

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Or there was, and Ines simply felt it — the peculiar sensation of having said a word in a room where that word had weight.

"Elias Vane," the nurse repeated. Not a question. Something worse than a question: a recitation.

"You know him?" His heart did something unhelpful. "Is he here? Was he here?"

"He's dead," she said, gently, the way one tells a man his house has burned down. "Killed in the spring. April, I think. Convoy near—" She stopped herself, and Ines watched her stop herself, watched the sentence go back down her throat like a swallowed key. "It was in the dispatches."

"It would be," Ines said bitterly. "He writes very good dispatches. He'd write a good one about his own death, if anyone let him."

"You were close."

"I was his batman. Three years. I shaved him, I mended him, I carried his letters and his gin and, twice, his conscience." Ines turned the oilskin packet over in his hands. "I know his handwriting better than my own mother's face. And this—"

He stopped. Because the thing he had been circling all night, the thing that had kept him awake among the smelling salts and the pillowslips, finally came out into the candlelight where it could be looked at.

"The letter inside," he said. "It isn't old. It isn't six months old. It isn't even six *weeks* old."

* * *

Astrid Crane had believed, until roughly four o'clock that morning, that she understood the world. It was a modest understanding and she had never expected more of it. History happened elsewhere — in ministries, in staff cars, in rooms with maps on the walls — and its residue drifted down to people like her as casualty lists, which she copied out, because someone ought.

Then a deserter with kind, frightened eyes had said *Elias Vane* in the voice of a man naming a living friend, and the floor of her understanding had tilted the way a ship tilts.

She went about her morning round in a curious double state: her hands moving through their familiar work — pulse, dressing, water jug, pulse — while her mind stood apart, holding up three memories to the light.

The first: March. A private from a supply company, delirious with fever, whispering a name over and over. *Vane. Tell Vane. Vane.* She had assumed a brother. She had written *brother?* in the margin and no one had ever answered.

The second: April. The dispatch naming Captain E. Vane among the dead of a convoy ambush. She had remembered it only because of March — a small coincidence, the sort the war produced by the thousand. Two men sharing a name; one dead, one calling for him. She had felt a brief, useless sorrow for the caller, wherever he was, and turned the page.

The third: June. The ledger entry. The name *Vane, E., Capt.* — struck through in red pencil in the adjutant's hand, the way eleven other names had been struck through, the way names were struck when they were not permitted to exist.

Three times. The name had crossed her life three times, each time thinner, each time less alive, and now it had walked in through the gate wearing mud and stolen boots and the face of a man who loved its owner.

At noon she went down to the linen store.

* * *

"Show me," Astrid said.

Ines hesitated — she could see the hesitation cost him something, a whole argument conducted behind his eyes and lost — and then he unwound the oilcloth. Inside were papers: a few loose sheets, folded twice, and beneath them a longer letter, its folds gone soft as cloth from handling.

He handed her the letter. His hands were shaking. She noticed she had stopped breathing and made herself start again.

The paper was good stock, army-issue but the better grade, the kind officers requisitioned for things that mattered. The handwriting slanted rightward, quick and certain, the loops of its capitals open like doors left unlatched. At the bottom, a signature: *Elias Vane.*

Astrid knew handwriting the way some women knew faces. She kept the ward records, the dietaries, the admission books; she had seen ten thousand signatures, the brave scrawls and the schoolroom copperplates, the dying scratches that trailed off mid-name. This hand she had never seen before, and yet she knew it instantly for what it was: the hand of a man accustomed to being believed.

And it was fresh. The ink had not faded. The creases were weeks old, not months. She held the page closer to the candle.

"Dated the ninth of October," she said quietly.

"Yes."

"Captain Vane died in April."

"Yes," Ines said again, and now his voice cracked down the middle like a plate dropped on stone. "That's what I keep telling myself, matron. Only the letter keeps disagreeing."

* * *

They read it together, or rather she read it aloud while he sat with his fists closed on his knees, wincing at certain sentences like a man hearing a doctor describe his own X-ray.

*"To whoever Ines trusts — and God help you, it will not be many, because he trusts no one, and I taught him that and I apologize for it—"*

"I don't trust anyone," Ines muttered.

"You trusted me with this," Astrid observed.

"That was exhaustion."

"Naturally."

*"—he will have told you nothing, so I must. On the fourth of April a supply convoy was diverted from the coastal road. Not lost. Diverted — by order of an officer whose name is in this letter, and whose name I will not put at the top of a page that may be searched. What was in those lorries was not tinned beef. Eleven men knew. Ten of them are now recorded as killed in action. You may verify this; it is even true, in the sense that the paperwork says so. It is true in no other sense whatsoever. I survived because I was meant to die convincingly and did it badly, which is the most useful failure of my career."*

Astrid's voice stopped working. She read the next part silently, her lips still moving, and Ines watched the colour leave her face in orderly stages, like a tide going out.

*"I am alive. I am hiding, and I am writing to strangers because everyone I might have written to is either dead-on-paper or dead-in-fact. If you hold this letter, then Ines found you, which means Ines ran, which means they came for him too. I want him to know — make sure he knows, whoever you are — that running was not cowardice. I ordered him to forget, and he disobeyed me, and disobedience of that particular order is the bravest thing any man under my command has done. He carried the truth out when every instinct of self-preservation told him to swallow it. Tell him his captain is proud of him. Tell him I am alive, and that being alive is now his job too."*

Silence. Somewhere above them a patient coughed, twice, and a trolley wheel squeaked along the corridor, the ordinary machinery of mercy going about its morning.

"He's alive," Ines said at last. It came out very small. "All this time. I mourned him. I did it properly, Matron — I'll have you know I'm thorough even at grief — and he was sitting somewhere writing letters in his ridiculous looping hand."

"Why didn't you open it sooner?" she asked. "On the road?"

"Because if I didn't open it, it hadn't been read, and if it hadn't been read, none of it had started yet." He gave a laugh with no floor under it. "I know how that sounds."

"It doesn't sound mad," Astrid said. "It sounds like a man trying to hold a door shut with his whole body." She smoothed the page on her knee. "There's more. Listen."

*"The eleventh witness lives. He does not know yet that he is the last. Find him before they do, or they will tidy him away like the rest, and then there will be no one left who saw the lorries except me, and I am, officially, a dead man, and dead men make poor witnesses. Names follow. Burn nothing. Trust the person who keeps records — in any army, the ones who write things down are the last to be lied to successfully."*

Astrid lowered the letter.

"He wrote that in October," she said slowly. "Months ago. Months before you ever reached this gate. He didn't know who would read it. He just — described me."

"You?"

"*The person who keeps records.*" She heard her own voice go strange. "Private Hollis, in March, asking for Vane. The dispatch in April. The struck-through name in June. I thought these were coincidences. Little grains of sand. And he—" she pressed her hand flat against the page as though she could feel warmth coming up through it, "—he has been standing on the *other side* of the sand, watching the same grains, for a year."

* * *

Ines watched her come apart and reassemble, and understood that he was watching something he himself had done in a ditch outside Étaples, alone, in the rain. It was oddly consoling to see it done properly, indoors, by someone with a candle.

"What is it?" he asked. "What did I say?"

"You didn't say anything. You handed me a letter." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and slightly terrible. "Mr. Ines. In my ledger — the private one, the real one — there is a name struck through three times in six months. Elias Vane. Once as a fevered man's last word. Once as a dispatch. Once in red pencil, in the same hand that struck through ten other names."

"Ten other—"

"Ten," she said. "Eleven counting yours, if your file exists, which I suspect it does, marked *deserter, no record.*" She stood, pacing the narrow aisle between the linen shelves, and Ines had the impression of a clock that had been wound after years of standing idle. "I have spent eight months believing I was keeping a record of small cruelties. Omissions. Sloppiness. I told myself the war eats paperwork faster than it produces it. But it wasn't sloppiness. It was *editing*. Someone is revising the war, Mr. Ines, and I have been filing his drafts without knowing it."

"Ines," he said.

"What?"

"Not *Mr. Ines.* Ines is the whole of it — surname abandoned somewhere around Amiens, along with my razor and my principles. Just Ines." He smiled despite everything; she had that effect, apparently by accident, the way weather has effects. "And you're not a filing clerk anymore, are you?"

"No," Astrid said. She stopped pacing. "No, I don't suppose I am."

The candle guttered. Down the corridor, the lunch trolley began its rounds, clinking. Ordinary sounds. But Ines found he was listening past them now, the way a man listens past a quiet dog — and sure enough, overhead, across the ceiling of the linen store, came the measured tread of boots. Officer's boots, unhurried, pausing at intervals as though reading the corridor the way one reads a page.

Astrid blew out the candle in one motion and stood utterly still in the dark. When she spoke, her whisper was level and very close.

"That will be the adjutant doing his rounds. He does them at noon and midnight, always the same route, always the same pace. I know this hospital the way you know your captain's handwriting." A breath. "Which means, Ines, that we now both know things the other needs — and I think we should compare our ledgers before whoever those boots belong to compares them for us."

In the dark, Ines held out the letter like a man offering evidence at his own trial.

"Then start with this," he whispered. "Because if Vane's alive — then the man who signed his death warrant is walking around with a pencil, and I'd very much like to know why his red strikes match your black ink."

Above them, the boots stopped — directly over the linen store — and did not move on.
