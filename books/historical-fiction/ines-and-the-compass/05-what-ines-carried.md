## 5. What Ines Carried

There is a particular kind of silence a man learns in the army, and it is not the silence before battle, which everyone writes songs about. It is the silence of a batman standing outside his officer's door at four in the morning, holding tea he has made too weak because the ration is thin and the captain takes it black and strong, which means the tea is an apology rather than a drink.

I had been Captain Elias Vane's batman for fourteen months, and I had gotten very good at silences. Polishing boots in silence. Standing at attention in silence. Listening through canvas walls in silence, because a batman who hears nothing hears everything, if you follow me, and I followed him everywhere.

His name was Vane, which is a word that means weathervane — the thing on the church spire that tells you which way the wind blows. His mother had a sense of humor, or God did. He was tall and quiet and he wrote letters the way other men swore: constantly, furiously, and with more feeling than was strictly regulation. I used to post six or seven of them a week. To whom, I never asked. A batman doesn't ask. But I noticed they all went to the same address in Kent, and I noticed that once, when one came back marked *return to sender*, he sat down on his camp bed and didn't speak for a day, and I made the tea stronger for a week without being asked to.

You will want to know why a grown man — twenty-six years old, a cooper's son from Rotherhithe, handy with barrels and better with horses — became so devoted to another man's correspondence. And the honest answer is that before the war I had never mattered to anyone in a way that required me to be somewhere at a particular time. The army gave me a uniform and a number. Elias Vane gave me a name he actually used. He'd say "Ines, the bay is off his feed," as if informing me were a courtesy between colleagues, and I would take the bay's temperature and think: *here is a man telling me things because my knowing them matters.*

It is extraordinary, the things a person will do for that.

* * *

The convoy left Rouen on the ninth of March, and it should not have gone where it went.

I know this now in the way you know a hymn — by heart, in order, verse upon verse — but on the ninth of March I knew only that something was wrong with the morning. Captain Vane stood at the crossroads outside the depot holding two sets of orders, and I watched him read the second one three times, and I thought: someone has made a mistake, and he is deciding whether to be the sort of man who mentions it.

He mentioned it.

"Private Merriweather," he said — that was me, though my real surname is Merrilees and the enlistment clerk couldn't spell it, so I have answered to a kind of weather ever since — "how are you at remembering things?"

"Fair to middling, sir," I said. "My mother says I remember what I ought to forget and forget what I ought to remember."

"Useful," said the captain, in a voice like a door closing. "Try to reverse it."

What I remembered, thereafter, was this. That the convoy — forty lorries, marked medical, loaded under my own eyes with crates stamped *field dressings* — was diverted by a supplementary order onto the coast road north, to a depot that did not officially exist. That at this depot the crates were opened, and were not full of dressings. That Major Grell — and I learned his name the way you learn the name of a dog that has bitten you — supervised the unloading personally, wearing gloves, in March, and thanked the drivers for their discretion as though he were handing out medals.

That there were eleven men besides myself who saw what was in those crates, and that Captain Vane wrote all our names in a little green notebook he kept inside his tunic, over his heart, which is where sensible men keep such things.

"What's in the crates, sir?" I asked him that night, because a batman may ask one question per day and no more; it is in the regulations, or ought to be.

"Paperwork," he said. "Expensive paperwork. The kind that gets bought twice and delivered nowhere." He looked at me for a long moment. "You didn't see anything, Private."

"No, sir," I agreed, having seen everything.

* * *

Here is what I want you to understand about the weeks that followed, because people always imagine scandal as a great crash, china hitting the floor. It is not. It is a slow drip behind a wall. You hear it at night. You mention it to someone and they look at you strangely. You begin to doubt your own ears.

The drips went like this. Driver Cobb, who had joked about the gloves, was reported killed at Ypres — except I had drunk tea with him on the Tuesday after his death, which even for the army seemed careless. Sergeant Aylward, transferred, then dead. Then the strange thing, the thing that turned my bowels to water when I finally understood it: their files began arriving pre-marked. *Deceased, no record.* As if some clerk in a warm office had decided in advance how these men would end, and history had simply been polite enough to comply.

Captain Vane stopped sleeping. I knew because I brought the tea at four and he was always awake behind the door, writing. Not letters now. Lists. Names crossed out in red — struck through, the way a schoolmaster kills a misspelled word — and beside each one, in his small fierce hand, a date and a place and the words *not true.*

"Sir," I said, one night, "those men aren't dead."

"No," he said. "They're just inconvenient. Death is very convenient, Merriweather. No pension, no inquiry, no witness." He closed the notebook. "Dead men can't testify."

"And living ones?"

"Living ones," he said, "are a problem I am trying to solve before somebody solves me."

I laughed. I am ashamed of it now — I thought he was making a joke, because officers made jokes and I laughed at them the way a horse flicks its ears at flies, automatically, harmlessly. He did not laugh back. He put his hand on my shoulder, which he had never done, and said, "If anything happens to me, there's a letter. You'll know it when you see it. Don't lose it, and don't read it until you have to."

"When would I have to, sir?"

"I don't know," he said. "That's rather the trouble with letters."

* * *

They came for him on the twenty-second of April, and they did not come politely.

Three officers and a military policeman, arriving at dusk with papers, saying the word *escort* in the tone men use for *coffin.* I stood outside the tent with the tea going cold in my hands — the four o'clock ritual inverted, useless, absurd — and heard the conversation through canvas the way I had heard so many conversations, except that this time hearing everything helped nothing.

At dawn a dispatch crossed my desk — well, it crossed the sergeant-major's desk, but I saw it, because seeing things was my whole education — announcing that Captain E. Vane had been killed in action, date of death the twenty-third of April, and I stood in the mess tent holding a ladle of porridge and understood, with the clarity of a man falling, that the drip behind the wall had become the flood, and that I was next, or nearly, or already.

They gave me the order three days later. Not an arrest — nothing so honest. A transfer, and a quiet interview with a major whose gloves I recognized even though his face belonged to no one, and the sentence that has followed me across every mile since:

"You were present at the northern depot on the ninth of March. You saw nothing. Sign here."

And I signed.

God help me, I signed. My hand shook so badly the signature looked like a sick man's pulse, but I signed, because the alternative was a name in a green notebook and a file marked *deceased,* and I was twenty-six and a coward in the ordinary, sensible, forgivable way that most men are cowards, which is to say completely.

Then, that same evening — because the universe is not subtle, merely thorough — I found the letter.

It was in my kit bag. Inside the spare socks, rolled tight, oilskin-wrapped, and I knew before I unwrapped it what it was, the way you know a knock on the door before you open it. His handwriting. Even through the paper I could tell — the capital E like a ship's anchor, the crossings-out that were themselves elegant. On the front, in that hand: *For Merriweather, who sees nothing.*

I sat down on my bunk and I laughed until I cried, which took some doing in a hut with nine sleeping men, and then I ran.

I want to be exact about this, because whatever else happens — whatever tribunal, whatever verdict — the record should say that I ran. Not that I slipped away cleverly, not that I conducted a strategic withdrawal. I ran like a rabbit, at night, with the letter against my chest and my boots in my hand so the sentries wouldn't hear, and I did not think once about duty or justice or the eleven men whose deaths had been invented by clerks. I thought about staying alive, and I did it.

The guilt of the survivor is a funny thing. People expect it to feel like grief. It doesn't. It feels like hunger — constant, undignified, waking you at four o'clock (always four; the tea hour; the body keeps its own ledgers) demanding to be fed. For five months I fed it. I walked at night and slept in hedges. I worked a fortnight on a barge under a false name and lost the job when I flinched at a whistle. I ate from bins and once, memorably, from a church jumble sale, on the theory that the Church of England would rather I stole a pork pie than starved, a theology I still half believe.

All that time, the letter stayed wrapped. Because — and here is where I must be honest past the point of comfort — I was afraid not of what opening it would cost me, but of what it would ask. A dead man's handwriting has authority no living officer can match. I knew that if I read it, I would do what it said. I had spent fourteen months learning to obey that hand.

So I carried it the way you carry a splinter too deep to dig out: painfully, successfully, for months.

* * *

Until the field hospital by the Channel. Until the linen store, and the nurse with the steady eyes — Crane, her badge said, Miss A. Crane — who hid me among the sheets when every instinct she owned must have been screaming to fetch the sentries.

Which brings me, at last, to tonight: to this store-room smelling of soap and carbolic, lit by one stolen candle, sitting on a crate of bandages opposite a woman who has just told me, in a voice flat as a ledger line, that she has seen the name *Vane* struck through three times in three different files.

Three times. I set the letter on my knee and felt it weigh what it had always weighed, which is nothing, which is everything.

"You said there's a letter," she said. "In that packet. Whose handwriting?"

"It's his," I said. "Captain Vane's. I'd know it upside down in the dark, ma'am, and I've seen it upside down in the dark, near enough, these past months, checking it wasn't a dream." I turned the oilskin over. My fingers had gone stupid. "Five months I've carried it and never opened it. There's cowardice for you. I was afraid of orders from a dead man."

"Or afraid they'd be good orders," she said quietly. "Ones worth obeying." She nodded at the packet. "Open it."

I worked the knots loose — my hands remembered knots from barrels, thank God — and unfolded the pages in the candlelight, and there it was, the anchor-E, alive on the page after all this time, and I heard myself make a sound that was not quite laughter.

I read the first line silently. Then, because some things are too large to hold alone, and because she had hidden me among her linens and written down dead men's names and deserved to share the weight, I read aloud.

"*Merriweather — if you are reading this, then either I am dead or it has become expedient that I be considered so, and I confess I no longer see much difference in how this army treats the two conditions.*"

Miss Crane sat down slowly on the crate opposite, as if her legs had filed a complaint.

"*By now you know what was in the convoy,*" I read. "*You knew it within a week, though you were too loyal to say so and too decent to run. I am counting on the first quality to bring you this letter and the second to make you act on it. The enclosed list contains eleven names — the eleven men who saw the diversion of 9 March and who have since been entered into the record as dead. They are not dead. They are scattered, frightened, and silenced, and Major Grell's correction bureau is finding them one at a time. When it finds the last one, the paper trail dies, and the convoys go on diverting forever, and boys in wards all along this coast will bleed into dressings that were sold before they were shipped.*"

My voice cracked on that last clause. Miss Crane did not look away, and I was grateful, because being looked at steadily while you break is a kind of bandaging.

"*I have sent copies where I hope they will do good. But copies can be denied. What cannot be denied is testimony, taken properly, on the record, from witnesses who are alive and willing and named. Find them, Merriweather. All of them if you can; one of them if you must. Get a statement signed and witnessed and beyond the reach of Grell's pen. You were the best listener I ever had. Now be the one who speaks.*"

I stopped. There was a postscript. Of course there was a postscript; the man could not write a laundry list without a postscript. I read it, and my eyes blurred, and I read it again to be sure.

"*P.S. — Everyone will tell you that you deserted, and technically they will be right, and it does not matter in the least. Running toward a thing is indistinguishable from running away from it, except at the finish. I have watched you finish things for fourteen months. Finish this. — E.V.*"

The candle guttered. Somewhere down the ward a man coughed in his sleep, and the sea worked at the shingle below the hospital, patient as a clerk filing papers.

"He thinks I'm brave," I said. My voice sounded borrowed. "He wrote it down. In his own hand. That makes it official."

Miss Crane reached across and took the list of eleven names from my lap, held it to the light, and studied it with the expression of a woman balancing accounts. Then she went very still.

"Ines," she said — my name, which nobody in uniform had ever used, and which I had not told her — "this third name. Aylward, S., sergeant. He's not scattered and he's not frightened." She looked up, and the candlelight did something complicated in her eyes. "He's lying in convalescent ward two, thirty beds from here. I changed his dressing yesterday."
