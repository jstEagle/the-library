## 6. Matron's Warning

Matron Everidge had a way of entering a room that made the room feel it ought to have been tidier.

Astrid felt it the moment the linen store door opened behind her — a draft of corridor air, the faint smell of carbolic and cigarette smoke, and then that particular silence which meant Matron was standing in the doorway taking inventory. Astrid did not turn around. She went on folding a sheet she had already folded twice, because her hands needed something lawful to do while her mind raced through every plausible explanation for why a nurse should be spending her off-duty hour alone with the bandages.

There were no plausible explanations. There was only Ines, asleep behind the third shelf of blankets, hidden by the simple expedient of a stacked pallet and a hospital's infinite talent for not looking at things it did not wish to see.

"Crane," said Matron.

"Matron."

"Close the door."

Astrid closed it. The linen store was narrow, lit by one bulb with a shade of tin that had been soldered by somebody who loved the hospital more than he respected electricity. In that light Matron's face looked like a map drawn in pencil — all fine lines, nothing wasted. She was sixty if she was a day, though no one had ever been brave enough to confirm it, and she had run this converted seaside hotel of shattered men through two years of war with the serenity of a woman who had long ago decided that panic was a luxury item and she could not afford the duty on it.

"I'm doing an inventory," Astrid said. "The sheets."

"At nine o'clock at night."

"We're short forty-two."

"We're short sixty," said Matron, "because the orderlies use them to carry the coal. Sit down."

There was only one stool, and sitting felt like surrendering, so Astrid stayed standing. Matron did not press the point. She moved along the shelves instead, trailing one finger across the folded linen as though checking it, which she was not, until she came to the third shelf of blankets and stopped.

Behind it, Astrid heard the smallest sound in the world: a man holding his breath.

"You've put him behind my good army blankets," Matron observed. "Those are the warm ones."

"He was cold," Astrid said. "He'd been in a culvert for two days."

"In a culvert." Matron pronounced the word the way another woman might say *syphilis*. "And you brought him into my hospital."

"He brought himself. I opened the gate."

"Ah. Well. That's very different, morally speaking." Matron turned. The tin-shaded light caught her eyes, which were grey and unhurried and missed absolutely nothing. "Do you know what they do to deserters, Crane? Because I do. I've watched two of them shot from the courtyard of a place not unlike this one, and both times the chaplain cried, and neither time did the firing party, and afterward everyone had sherry. It's considered bad form to shoot a man and then not offer sherry."

Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "He isn't a deserter in the way you mean."

"There's only one way I mean."

"He saw something. Something they want buried. He's carrying proof of it — a letter, names —"

"Stop." Matron raised one hand, not sharply, but with the finality of a woman who had spent twenty years stopping junior nurses mid-sentence before they said something that would end up in someone's file. "Don't tell me. Do you understand? Whatever it is, I don't want to know it. Knowing things is how people hang. You can hang for what you know just as dead as for what you did, and you look worse at the trial."

"They'll hang him either way if we hand him over."

"They may hang us if we don't." Matron let that sit in the air between them, plain and heavy as a sandbag. "Harboring a deserter in a military hospital during wartime. They won't need a long rope for that. They'll use the short one, the efficient one. The one they keep coiled by the paperwork."

And here was the thing Astrid could not reconcile — the thing that kept her upright on legs that wanted to fold: Matron was not reporting them. Every word out of her mouth was a reason to march straight to the telephone, and yet there she stood, arms crossed over her apron, making conversation with the third shelf of blankets as though it owed her rent.

"Then why," Astrid asked carefully, "haven't you called the guard?"

Matron was quiet for long enough that the bulb above them seemed to buzz louder. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of its dryness, or perhaps just revealing that the dryness had always been a coat worn over something else.

"Because in March of 1915," she said, "I signed a ledger."

Astrid blinked. "Matron?"

"A casualty ledger. Not unlike yours, though mine was in Boulogne and much worse smelling. We were overrun for three days — you'll not remember it, it never made the papers, the papers that spring were full of gas and glory. During those three days I wrote down the names of forty-one men who died in my ward. Properly. Full names, next of kin, regimental numbers, the lot. When the line was restored and the officers returned, an adjutant came through with a pencil and struck out nineteen of my entries." Her finger traced a line through the air, quick and contemptuous. "*Deceased prior to admission. No record.* Nineteen men I had held while they died, erased between my ink and his pencil. As if I'd hallucinated them. As if they'd been weather."

Astrid felt something turn over in her chest, slow and cold, like a ship shifting at anchor. "What happened to them? The nineteen?"

"That is precisely the question I have been asking for two years," said Matron, "in a tone of voice that does not invite answers, because I am a coward in some respects and a survivor in all of them. But I will tell you what I noticed, Crane, since you seem to be the noticing sort." She stepped closer. From the third shelf came the soft creak of a man trying not to exist; Matron ignored it with dignity. "The nineteen weren't random. They were all convoy men. All from the same supply route. And when I mentioned that observation to the adjutant — politely, as one does — I found myself transferred to this posting within the fortnight. Channel coast. Quiet. Out of the way." Her mouth curved without warmth. "They promoted me to get rid of me. It's the English way. We bury our embarrassments under honors."

"The convoy," Astrid repeated. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears. "Ours too. The omissions in my ward — they follow the same supply line. Convoy seven."

"I know," said Matron.

"You *know*?"

"I read your ledger."

The floor tilted, very slightly, and righted itself. "My ledger is kept in a locked drawer under four other documents."

"Yes," said Matron. "Terrible lock. I could open it with a hairpin and mild disapproval. Did you think a woman who has run hospitals for thirty years couldn't find where her own night nurse hides her diary?" She permitted herself, briefly, the ghost of a smile — the first Astrid had ever seen on her, and it transformed her face the way a match transforms a dark room, illuminating everything for one second and then leaving it darker than before. "Eleven entries in two months, Crane. Eleven men marked *returned to unit* in your book and *no record* in theirs. Eleven. That's not clerical error. That's policy."

Behind the blankets, Ines made a sound — half cough, half word — and Astrid's whole body turned toward the shelf like iron filings toward a magnet. Matron watched her do it, and something in her expression shifted, some assessment completed.

"You care about him," she said. It was not quite a question and not quite an accusation. "Three days you've had him, and you care about him. Which is inconvenient of you. Caring about people in wartime is like carrying glassware across a battlefield. Eventually you drop something and cut yourself, and everyone acts surprised, as though glass were meant to survive artillery."

"He'll be moved tonight," Astrid said. "I have a plan."

"You have a plan," Matron echoed. "Nurses always have plans. Your plan involves crossing a dockyard controlled by the military police with a fugitive whose face is on three notice boards, I don't doubt, and your plan will fail, because plans made by tired women at midnight generally do." She paused. "It needs to fail more slowly, that's all. You need time, documents, and information. Two of those three I can help you with."

"*Why?*" The question escaped Astrid before she could dress it up in manners. "Forgive me, Matron, but — why risk it? You said yourself knowing things is how people hang."

For a moment Matron looked past her, at the shelves, at the tin lamp, at thirty years of other people's linens folded under her supervision. When she answered, it was almost gently.

"Because I have spent two years keeping secrets for men who didn't deserve the courtesy," she said. "I have filed their corrections and burned their memoranda and taught myself to sleep, most nights, by telling myself that a nurse's duty ends at the ward door. And then a girl in my employ starts writing dead men's names in the margins of an inventory book, in a hand so careful it looks ashamed of itself, and I think: there is one person in this building who still believes the record matters." She met Astrid's eyes. "I would rather hang beside that person than dine beside the adjutants."

The silence that followed was not comfortable, but it was honest, which Astrid was learning was rarer and better.

Then Matron sniffed, briskly, and the moment was folded away as neatly as her linens. "Right. Practicalities. He stays where he is for now — the linen store is the one room in this hospital nobody enters voluntarily, they think the damp gives them lumbago. He eats what the orderlies leave. He does *not* smoke back there, whatever he tells you; the smell carries down the corridor and Private Dunmore's nose is for sale to whoever's bidding. How long since he ate properly?"

"Two days. I've been bringing him my supper."

"And you've been eating what, exactly? Air?" Matron clicked her tongue. "Tomorrow there will be an extra tray logged as a special diet for a patient in Convalescent Four who has, as it happens, been dead since Tuesday and cannot complain of the error. See that he gets it. Growing boys and fugitives, same appetite."

From behind the blankets came a muffled, hoarse voice: "Thank you, ma'am."

"Don't thank me," said Matron crisply, in the direction of the shelf. "Thanking me means admitting you exist, and officially you don't, which is the entire point. Keep it that way. And if you must exist, exist quietly."

There was a pause, and then, softer, the sound of a man who had not been spoken to kindly in weeks saying nothing at all, loudly.

* * *

They walked the corridor together, Matron first, her keys silent in her pocket where lesser women's keys jangled. The hospital breathed around them — the sigh of the sea against the sea wall below, a man in Ward Two talking in his sleep to his mother, the eternal small percussion of a big building settling into night. At the junction by the dispensary Matron stopped, and Astrid nearly walked into her, and understood from the stillness of her shoulders that something was coming.

"Crane. One further matter." Matron did not turn around. "You'll want to check the official record against yours. Confirm your eleven. I know you will; you have the face of a woman who counts everything twice."

"I —"

"Spare me the denial, it's insulting to us both." Matron drew something from her apron pocket, small and cold, and held it out behind her without ceremony. A key. Brass, old, unremarkable — except that Astrid knew exactly what it opened, because she had stood outside that door twice in her life and felt the same pull a swimmer feels toward deep water. "Records room. Second corridor, end of the block. The duty clerk sleeps from eleven until four; he sleeps the sleep of a man who has discovered that diligence in wartime leads only to more work. You have a window."

Astrid took the key. It weighed nothing and everything. "Matron, if anyone sees me —"

"No one will see you, because you will go at ten past eleven, and you will sign yourself out of the building in the night book as attending a patient in Convalescent One, and I will have already entered that patient in the log myself, because I enter logs at nine each evening and no one checks a matron's entries. That is the trick of this whole war, Crane." Now she did turn, and the corridor lamp gave her face the look of an old coin — worn smooth, value intact. "No one checks a matron's entries. They check the nurses' and they check the orderlies' and they check each other's, greedily. But a woman of my age with my expression? They assume I am beyond interest. Age and a disapproving face are the best camouflage the army issues, and they issue it free."

"Why give me this?" Astrid closed her hand around the key. Its teeth pressed into her palm. "Truly. You could know nothing. You told me so yourself."

"I did. And I meant it, and I intend to go on meaning it right up until the moment I don't." Matron smoothed her apron, a gesture Astrid had seen a thousand times and never once thought about, and would now think about forever. "But understand me, girl, and understand me completely. I am not helping you save that man behind my blankets, worthy as he may be. Men are saved and lost by the hundred and the war doesn't notice. I am helping you save the *record*. Those eleven names. Because when this business is over — and it will end, they all end, that is the one promise war keeps — there will be a history written. There always is. And it will be written by the victors' clerks, in offices with good lighting, and it will say what the pencils tell it to say unless somebody, somewhere, kept a truer book." Her chin lifted a fraction. "I kept a truer book once. Forty-one names. Nineteen of them were taken from me. I have regretted many things in my life, Crane, but I have never once regretted the ink."

Somewhere below, the tide turned against the sea wall with a sound like a great door being slowly, reluctantly pulled shut.

"Ten past eleven," Matron said. "Take nothing but paper. Write nothing but truth. And Crane—" she was already moving away down the dark corridor, keys silent, voice floating back over her shoulder with the last of its dry humor reassembled like armor— "do wear your sensible shoes. The records room floor hasn't been swept since Christmas, and I'll not have you sneezing your way into a court-martial."

Astrid stood alone at the junction, listening to the sea and the sleeping ward and her own heart going like a courier's boots.

In her closed fist, the key had warmed to the temperature of her body, as though it had always belonged there.

Ten past eleven. She had ninety minutes, a forged entry standing in her name, and — for the first time since the war began — one other person in the world who knew exactly what she was, and had handed her the key anyway.

She went to change her shoes.
