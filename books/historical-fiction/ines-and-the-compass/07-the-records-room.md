## 7. The Records Room

The key Matron had left me sat in my apron pocket all evening like a stone I'd swallowed and hadn't yet digested. It was not an ordinary key — nothing in this hospital was ordinary once you looked at it properly — but a heavy brass thing, older than the war, the sort that opened church vestries or the private cupboards of women who died rich. It did not look like it belonged to the records room. Then again, Matron did not look like a woman who broke rules. She looked like a woman who *enforced* them, which is a far better disguise.

I told myself, while I spooned barley broth into Private Dunning and rebandaged Corporal Fenn's leg, that I had not yet decided to use it. This was a lie, and I knew it was a lie, because I had already chosen my shoes. One does not choose soft-soled shoes for a night of honest sleeping.

At half past eleven the ward went quiet in that particular way wards do — not silent, never silent, what with the breathing and the moaning and young Dunning narrating his dream about a cheese shop in a voice like a foghorn — but settled. Sister Wray made her final round, clucked over Fenn's dressing, and went to her cot. The lamp in the corridor burned low. Outside, the sea worked at the shingle the way it always did, patiently, as if it had all the time in the world and intended to prove it.

I waited until midnight by mending socks. I am aware this sounds domestic to the point of farce, but there is a long tradition of women doing dangerous things with their hands occupied by wool, and I saw no reason to break with tradition. Besides, if anyone came upon me, I intended to be found doing something so blameless that suspicion would die of embarrassment.

Then I put down the socks, took the lamp with its flame turned nearly out, and went into the corridor.

* * *

The records room stood at the end of the administrative wing, below the offices where Major Whoever-Happened-To-Be-Visiting drank tea and rearranged the war into columns. To reach it I had to pass the matron's office, the adjutant's cubbyhole, and a notice board bearing a poster of a pointing man and the words YOUR COMRADE NEEDS YOU — SUPPLY LINES DON'T RUN THEMSELVES, which had always struck me as the most unintentionally honest piece of propaganda on the coast.

My heart behaved disgracefully the whole way. It did not pound — pounding would have been too simple. It performed small irregular gymnastics, stopping entirely when the floorboards creaked under my left heel and then galloping to catch up, so that by the time I reached the records room door I felt less like a heroine of history than a woman in urgent need of a chair.

The brass key fit. Of course it fit. Matron did not leave keys that didn't fit; she considered it untidy.

Inside, the room smelled of paper, dust, and the faint ghost of somebody's pipe, smoked here against regulations some months ago. Moonlight came through a high window in a thin grey wedge and lay across a filing cabinet like a sheet over a body. Shelves ran along two walls, sagging under regimental histories nobody read, blank forms nobody wanted, and ledgers — row upon row of them, spines cracked, corners bloomed with salt damp. The whole war's paperwork, filed within reach of the tide that kept taking our wounded and giving back wreckage.

I set the lamp on the desk, shielded its light with a folder, and stood a moment simply listening. Nothing. The building ticked and sighed around me. Somewhere a door latch lifted and dropped in the wind, once, twice, like a patient knocking.

"Right," I said to myself, very quietly, because saying it aloud made the thing real, and I have always needed things to be real before I can be brave about them.

* * *

I knew which ledgers I wanted. I had thought about little else for three days, turning the problem over the way one turns a hard sweet in the mouth. Our own ward ledger lived in my desk upstairs — the official copy, the one open to inspection — and I knew every name in it the way a curate knows his psalms. What I needed were the casualty returns: the lists wired up the chain each week, the numbers that became dispatches, the dispatches that became history.

They were in the second cabinet, third drawer, under a folder labelled MISCELLANEOUS CORRECTIONS. Even the label had a whiff of the firing squad about it.

I carried the returns to the desk and set my own ledger beside them — I had brought a fair copy of the relevant pages, transcribed in my best hand, because the original did not leave my sight. Then I began.

It was slow work, and I will not pretend it was anything but grim. The returns recorded men by number, unit, and disposition, and dispositions came in only three flavours: returned to duty, evacuated, deceased. I ran my finger down October's list and cross-checked each name against mine.

Private Aldous Reeve, 2nd/Devons. Deceased, eleventh of October. I had dressed his shoulder myself on the twelfth. He had complained about the porridge. Dead men do not complain about porridge; it is practically the definition of death.

Gunner Thomas Quill. Deceased, fourteenth of October. Passed through my ward on the fifteenth, lucid, hungry, and convinced — wrongly, as it turned out — that Nurse Pemberton was fond of him.

Lance-Corporal Emlyn Satt. Deceased, sixteenth. I had held his hand while the morphine took hold, and he had woken the next morning asking for tea, which I counted among the small miracles of my trade.

On and on. The lamp flame trembled in its glass, or perhaps my hand trembled and blamed the lamp, we were close enough by then. Page after page, name after name, and the pattern assembling itself out of the ink like a photograph developing in solution: eleven men. Eleven men marked dead in the returns who had drawn breath in my ward, eaten our bread, complained about our porridge, and been shipped onward to convalescence — alive, officially nonexistent, and never once mentioned again in any document that mattered.

Eleven. And every one of them from the same fortnight. Every one of them attached, however loosely, to Convoy 114 — the convoy whose wagons I had watched roll past the hospital windows empty going *out*, which I had privately considered odd at the time and then, in the great human tradition, stopped considering at all.

I sat back. My chair scraped, and I froze, absurdly, as though the chair might report me.

Here is what I had believed about history before that night: that it happened elsewhere, to other people, and that my job was to sweep up afterward. Sitting in that stolen room with two ledgers open before me, I understood — with the plain, cold clarity of a woman reading her own handwriting — that history had been happening *here*. In my ward. Under my nose, wearing a clerk's uniform, signed and countersigned. Somebody had taken eleven living men and rubbed them out of existence with a pen, and they had done it in triplicate.

One does not erase eleven men in triplicate unless one is afraid of being caught. And one is only afraid of being caught if the erasure matters. Therefore it mattered. Q.E.D., as my father used to say before adding something wrong.

* * *

I copied.

Not everything — there was no time for everything, and my candle of courage burned shorter than I'd planned. But the first pages, the October returns complete, and alongside them my own ward entries for the same dates, the living names set against the dead ones, line upon line, the truth laid out in parallel columns so plainly that even a major could not mistake it. Even a major would mistake it, actually; I had met majors. But a tribunal might not.

My hand, I confess, was beautiful. There is something in me — I expect there is something in most of us who keep records — that rises to the occasion when it ought to be terrified. The letters came out even and upright, the figures neat, the little crosses beside each discrepancy precise as stitches. Methodical defiance, disguised as bookkeeping. I had been practicing it for a year without knowing why. Now I knew.

Aldous Reeve, alive on the twelfth. Gunner Quill, alive and lovesick on the fifteenth. Emlyn Satt, resurrected by tea on the seventeenth. Name after name, my pen moving in the small hours, the sea keeping its patient time outside, and I thought: *this is what it feels like to matter.* Not grandly. Not the way generals matter, with maps and flags. But the way a witness matters. The way a ledger matters when everyone else has agreed to forget.

I was, I will admit, rather pleased with myself. Smug, even. I remember thinking that I should very much like to see the face of whoever had written *deceased* across Aldous Reeve when confronted with the man's actual pulse.

That was when I heard the boots.

* * *

They came from above — the offices, the tea-drinking rooms — unhurried, evenly spaced, the tread of someone with authority and a full night's sleep behind him. Two sets, maybe three. In this building, at this hour, boots overhead meant one thing: officers who did not need to sneak, which is infinitely worse than officers who do. A sneaking man can be reasoned with. A man walking openly at one in the morning believes he owns the room you are stealing from, because he does.

I did the arithmetic of terror quickly and badly. The stair to the administrative wing came down somewhere near the notice board, forty feet of corridor away. The records room had no other door. The window was high, narrow, and opened onto a four-foot drop to the yard — survivable, but noisy, and I was carrying a lit lamp, two ledgers' worth of guilt, and a conscience that had recently begun making noise of its own.

The boots crossed the ceiling above me and stopped. Directly overhead. A chair scraped. Someone had sat down in the middle of the night to work, which meant he might stay an hour, or might, at any moment, decide he needed a file.

I turned the lamp down until the flame was a blue bead, and stood in the dark among the shelves with my hand pressed flat against my own ledger as though it were a frightened animal needing steadying. Which, in fairness, it was.

And in that darkness, with my heart performing its gymnastics again, a thought arrived that had no business arriving, given the circumstances: *eleven names.* Eleven men walked out of the war by clerical decree. Captain Elias Vane, struck through three times in my margin notes. A deserter hidden in my linen store with a letter from a dead man. The pattern had edges now, and the edges pointed somewhere, and whoever owned those boots upstairs was very likely the hand that held the pen.

The chair above moved again. Boots resumed, crossing toward the stairs.

Toward my stairs.

I blew out the lamp — no ceremony, just breath and panic — and stood blind in the dark, listening to my own pulse and the sea and, closer now, closer still, the measured fall of boots coming down through the building toward the corridor, toward the notice board, toward the door of the records room where a brass key hung in a lock that had, until tonight, opened for exactly one woman.

The handle turned.
