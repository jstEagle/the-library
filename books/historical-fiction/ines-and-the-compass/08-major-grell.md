## 8. Major Grell

Major Ambrose Grell believed in the moral utility of paperwork the way other men believed in prayer. Paperwork, properly administered, forgave nothing. It remembered everything. It was the only honest witness a war could produce, which was precisely why it required such diligent editing.

He dressed that morning with particular care. Not vanity — he had never been vain — but respect. One owed one's work the courtesy of appearing equal to it. His tunic was brushed, his boots gleamed without ostentation, and his cap sat at the exact angle prescribed by regulation, neither jaunty nor funereal. A man's angle of cap told you everything about his relationship to authority. Grell's said: *I am authority, and I have read the manual twice.*

The drive down to the Channel coast took three hours, along roads lined with poplars that had lost their argument with the wind. His driver, a corporal with a face like a worried potato, kept glancing at him in the mirror as though hoping to be given something to do. Grell obliged him with silence. Silence, well deployed, was the most economical form of instruction.

"You'll wait at the crossroads," Grell said when they reached the lane to the hospital. "If I'm not back by four o'clock, drive to Boulogne and telephone the number in my folio."

"Back from where, sir?"

Grell looked at him. The corporal blushed to the ears.

"The hospital, Corporal. Where we are currently parked. Beside the large building with the red crosses."

"Yes sir. Sorry sir."

*Everyone is sorry,* Grell thought, stepping out into the salt wind. *It is the century's great reflex.* Nobody, anymore, was ever simply mistaken, or negligent, or criminally careless in a manner requiring documentation. They were sorry. It saved everyone a great deal of time if one simply did not require their sorrow.

The field hospital had begun life as a seaside hotel, and it had not entirely surrendered its former character. There was still a wrought-iron gate, though it now hung crooked on its hinges, and a gravel drive gone soft with ambulance wheels, and geraniums — God help them — persisting in a window box above the ward marked *SURGICAL*. Somebody watered them. That was the thing about hospitals, Grell had found: they were full of people whose instinct, in the teeth of catastrophe, was to water things. He found it touching. He also found it made them terrible liars, because they were people who believed that tending to something would keep it alive.

A medical orderly met him at the door, took one look at his major's crown, and stood up so straight his spine audibly reconsidered its position.

"Major Grell, Fourth Directorate," Grell said, presenting his papers with the small ceremonial pause such documents deserved. "I'm here to conduct an inspection of records and administrative practice. You will inform your commanding officer. You will not announce it to the staff at large."

"No, sir. Er — administrative practice, sir?"

"Paperwork, son. How it's done, by whom, and whether anyone has been doing it creatively."

The orderly nodded several times more than the exchange warranted and departed at a pace just short of running. Good. Word would travel anyway; it always did; but it would travel in the garbled way word travels, arriving at its destination wrong in every particular, and a frightened imagination invents far worse than the truth. Let them imagine. Frightened people put things away.

* * *

Matron received him in what had once been the hotel's writing room — a detail Grell noted and filed. Writing rooms bred inkwells, and inkwells bred habits.

She was a tall woman somewhere past fifty, with the unimpressed composure of a church warden, and she shook his hand as though confirming it contained no bones of interest.

"Matron Ellery," she said. "We weren't expecting an inspection."

"That is generally considered to improve them, Matron."

"I dare say." She poured tea without asking whether he wanted it, which he respected. Hospitality as a form of crowd control. "You'll want to see the admission books, the discharge ledgers, the mortuary registers."

"I'll want to see everything that takes a pen," Grell agreed pleasantly. "And then I'll want to talk to whoever does the writing."

Something crossed her face — not fear exactly; more the look of a woman recalculating a bill. "Our clerical work is shared out. Wounded hands make poor secretaries, and the officers write their own dispatches. The day-to-day entries fall mostly to one of my nurses. Miss Crane."

"Miss Crane," Grell repeated, making a small show of noting the name in a pocketbook he had already filled with her name two days ago. "Efficient, is she?"

"She is thorough."

There was a hesitation on the second word, small enough to fit inside a teacup. Grell smiled and drank his tea.

Thorough. Yes. He had seen her hand on the duplicated returns sent up to Base, those tidy columns of admissions that arrived monthly like clockwork and matched, almost always, almost exactly, the figures his own office maintained for her hospital. *Almost.* The discrepancies were beautiful in their modesty. A name present in her ledger absent from the official roll. Another present in the roll with a date of death that her records showed as a transfer. Eleven such, over six months, all traceable — if one knew where to look — to a single supply convoy in the spring and the regrettable publicity it had briefly attracted before being, with considerable effort, unpublished.

Eleven corrections. He thought of them privately as his eleven punctuation marks: small marks that changed the meaning of a sentence. Without them the sentence said something seditious. With them, it said what it had always officially said.

It was not, he wanted to be clear about this, murder. Grell wished people would understand this. It was *grammar*. Men who had seen something they should not have seen were moved — transferred, reassigned, posted to units where their testimony would be buried under ordinary noise, or in two or three regrettable cases posted to units that subsequently experienced ordinary noise of the shellfire variety. War was a machine for producing casualties; he had merely ensured that certain men became the casualties war intended them to be, rather than witnesses nobody had ordered. The distinction seemed to him enormous. It seemed to him, in fact, to be the whole of civilization.

What it was not, was something a nurse in a seaside hotel should be noticing with her careful hand.

"Show me the records room," he said, rising.

* * *

The records room had been the hotel's luggage store, and it retained a faint smell of other people's holidays beneath the smell of paper and carbolic. Grell went through the shelves the way he imagined other men went through a lover's letters: slowly, reverentially, checking dates.

The admission book lay open on the desk — someone had left it open, which meant either sloth or recent use — and there it was. Her hand.

He had expected neatness. What he had not expected was this: a script so even it might have been set in type, each entry aligned to an invisible rule, the numbers formed with a draftsman's precision. No flourishes, no haste-marks, none of the little dishonesties of ordinary handwriting. And yet it was not a cold hand. There was warmth in it, somewhere in the spacing, in the way the descenders reached down generously, as if the writer gave every letter a little more room than it strictly needed. A hand that made space for things.

Grell disliked it immediately and completely.

"Miss Crane writes the daily entries?" he called through the door, where Matron had stationed herself like a piece of furniture of unusual quality.

"When she's able. She also nurses, Major. We're stretched."

"Naturally." He turned pages. Dates marched past, immaculate. Here a private, gunshot wound, thigh, transferred. Here a sergeant, gas, recovered. Here—

He stopped.

At the bottom of one page, in the margin, in the same flawless hand but smaller, pressed close against the printed edge as though seeking shelter: a name. *Pvt. T. Aldous.* No wound listed. No disposition. Just the name, recorded the way one records a bird one has glimpsed — *it was here, I saw it, I am not mad.*

Aldous. Number seven of his eleven. Officially dead of wounds in May. Officially buried, according to a return Grell himself had countersigned, in a plot that Grell happened to know contained a different man entirely, because Grell had written that return and had chosen the plot from a map.

And here the man was, alive as ink, sheltering in a margin.

"You keep marginal notes, Matron," he said lightly.

"We keep everything, Major. It's a hospital. Later on, someone always wants to know."

"Later on," Grell agreed, "someone always does." He closed the book gently, the way one closes a door on a room where something private is happening. "And where might I find Miss Crane?"

"Ward Two. She's on dressings until noon." Matron paused, and then, with the exquisite timing of her profession, added: "She's very devoted to the men, Major. It makes her careful with their names. Nurses get sentimental about names. It's how we keep the patients straight from the wounds."

"A defect I shall bear in mind," said Grell, "charitably."

* * *

He watched her for twenty minutes before she saw him.

Ward Two held thirty beds, most of them occupied by men in various stages of being repaired, and she moved among them with the unhurried economy of a woman who had learned exactly how fast she could go without frightening anybody. She spoke to each one. Not the empty murmuring he'd heard from lesser nurses — actual words, individual words, a joke here, a question about a mother there. She was perhaps thirty, brown-haired, unremarkable in the way that competent women in uniform are rendered unremarkable by design, and her hands were sure.

And she wrote things down. Constantly. A pencil stub appeared from her apron; a fold of paper from her cuff; a temperature, a pulse, a request, a name. She wrote the way other people breathed.

When at last the ward orderly murmured something to her and she turned and saw the major's crown standing among the bedframes, she did not startle. Grell paid attention to that. Most people startled. She simply finished the dressing she was applying — completed the turn, tucked the edge, patted it once — washed her hands, dried them, and came toward him at a walk.

"Major. Matron said you'd be inspecting."

"Not inspecting the wounded, Miss Crane. Inspecting the paperwork. Though I see you keep some of it in your cuffs."

"Some of it keeps itself," she said. "I just hold the pencil."

He smiled — his inspection smile, the one that had unnerved two quartermasters and a chaplain into voluntary confessions — and it slid off her as though she had assessed it, priced it, and declined to purchase.

"Your hand," he said, "is remarkable. Very few people write so clearly under field conditions."

"Thank you."

"It's not entirely a compliment. Clear writing is memorable writing. Memorable writing gets read. In wartime, Miss Crane, the best documents are the dull ones — the ones no one reads twice." He let that sit between them like a dropped instrument. "Tell me about your admission book. Who authorizes your entries?"

"The admitting officer signs. I record."

"And do you ever record... more than you're given?"

The question hung there, courteous as a drawn curtain. Behind her, down the ward, a man moaned softly in his sleep and another coughed, and the sounds gave her a moment to arrange whatever needed arranging.

"I record what happens, Major," she said. "It's the same thing."

"It is rarely the same thing anywhere," said Grell, "and never in an army."

* * *

He lunched in the former dining room on excellent fish — the sea's one reliable gift to this coast — and reviewed his notes, and found his thoughts returning, with the persistence of a tongue to a sore tooth, to that margin. *Pvt. T. Aldous*, pressed flat against the printed line, hiding in plain sight on a page destined for his own office every month.

Six months of returns. Six months in which she had transcribed her hospital honestly and his office had corrected her hospital quietly, and at no point — he was nearly sure of this — had she flagged the discrepancy upward. No protest memos. No queries to Base. She had simply *kept* them. Filed them in margins. Built, entry by meticulous entry, a parallel record in the negative spaces of the official one.

It was not treachery. Treachery sought an outcome. This was something he had no clean word for — a woman keeping faith with a version of events that existed nowhere but in her own fair hand. Sentimental, as Matron said. Dangerous precisely because it was sincere. A fanatic of accuracy.

Fanatics of accuracy could not be threatened. Threatened, they hardened. They had to be *shown* — shown that the record they worshipped had an author, that behind every official sentence stood a man with a pen, and that the pen answered to the Directorate. Once she understood whose hand corrected hers, her own hand would begin to tremble, and trembling ruined the kind of precision she lived by. Or it could be redirected. An offer, even. Accuracy of her quality was wasted on temperatures and pulses.

He would need to know, first, how much she had actually assembled. Whether the margins were habit or architecture. Whether anything had left this building in her hand — and if it had, in whose pocket.

There was also, his notes reminded him in his own compact script, the matter of the deserter reported near the coast road ten days ago — a report his office had received, assessed, and filed with unusual speed, given that deserters in this sector were generally of interest to no one but the provosts. Unless the deserter had been of a unit connected to a certain convoy. Unless he had been carrying something. Files, in Grell's experience, traveled. Letters especially. A letter was the one document no Directorate could fully police, because it chose its own moment to be read.

He finished his fish, dabbed his mouth, and stood.

"One more visit before I go," he told the orderly who fetched his cap. "Ward Two again. And then I should like a word with Miss Crane — alone, and at leisure. Arrange it, would you? Tell her the Major would value her assistance with some transcription."

The orderly blinked. "Transcription, sir?"

"Yes." Grell settled his cap at its regulation angle and smiled at nothing in particular. "Her hand is so very clear, after all. It would be a shame for it to go to waste — or astray."

* * *

He found her at dusk, at the desk in the records room, finishing the day's entries in the last of the light. She rose when he entered, pencil at rest, and folded her hands — the gesture of a woman closing a drawer.

"Miss Crane," said Grell warmly, drawing out the chair opposite hers and sitting down uninvited. "I wonder if you'd help me with a small clerical task. It concerns eleven names."
