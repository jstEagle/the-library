## 9. Two Kinds of Duty

The linen store smelled of carbolic and lavender, which was an odd combination, Astrid thought — the hospital's two attempts at mercy, one chemical and one sentimental, meeting somewhere in the middle of a shelf. She slipped inside with a covered tray and closed the door softly behind her.

Ines was asleep sitting up, wedged between a stack of folded sheets and the wall, his chin on his chest, the oilskin packet still clutched against his ribs like a child holds a stolen loaf of bread. Even asleep, he held it. She had noticed that about him in the three days he'd been hidden there: he let go of nothing.

She set the tray down harder than strictly necessary.

He woke the way soldiers wake — all at once, silently, one hand already moving — and then saw her and came back into his body by degrees. "Nurse Crane."

"Supper," she said. "Barley soup. The cook thinks I've developed an appetite for eating alone in store cupboards, which is only half a lie."

He took the bowl with both hands, the way they all did, the starving ones. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." She sat down on an upturned crate across from him, knees nearly touching his. "I need to talk to you, and I need you not to interrupt until I'm finished, because if you interrupt I'll lose my nerve, and I haven't got much to spare."

He put the spoon down. That surprised her a little. Most men would have kept eating.

"Grell is here," she said. "Major Grell. He arrived yesterday. He's inspecting the hospital — paperwork, he says. He stood over my desk for twenty minutes this morning while I entered dressings into the requisition book, and he said, *You have a very careful hand, Nurse Crane,* in the tone a man uses when he's deciding whether to buy a horse or shoot it."

Ines had gone very still.

"He knows something is in this hospital that shouldn't be. I don't know how much he knows, or whether he's connected to your convoy, but eleven names have been struck from the record and you are carrying a dead man's handwriting, and I have concluded — after considerable reflection, and some poor sleep — that we cannot stay here waiting for him to find us like mushrooms under a blanket."

"We?" Ines said quietly.

"Don't make me regret the pronoun. What I mean to say is: you have to go. Tonight, before the tide if possible. There's a fisherman at the east jetty — Matron's cousin, or her cousin's friend, or some relation whose exact nature she declined to specify — who owes the hospital a favor for reasons also unspecified. He can take you down the coast. From there you go inland, you disappear, you let someone else carry this." She nodded at the packet. "You've carried it far enough. Further than anyone could have asked."

* * *

Ines looked at her for a long moment, and what he felt first was not gratitude but something closer to grief — the specific grief of being offered a door and knowing it opened onto a smaller room than the one you were standing in.

"You don't understand what's in the letter," he said.

"I know it's signed by a dead man."

"That's not the half of it." He set the bowl aside, carefully, as if it were evidence. "Captain Vane didn't just write down names. He wrote where the names came from. The convoy in March — the one they listed as lost to shellfire, all eleven of them — it never came under fire. It was diverted. Three lorries of medical stores, quinine, morphine, surgical steel, redirected north under requisition papers signed by Grell himself. Sold, Nurse. Not lost. *Sold.* And the men who saw the diversion orders — drivers, escorts, a quartermaster-sergeant — were sent forward into a gap in the line that had no business having men in it. Casualties of war. Very tidy. Except Vane kept a copy of everything, and when they came for him he gave it to me and told me to run, and I ran, and he—" Ines stopped. His jaw worked. "They say he's dead. The letter says otherwise. But I can't prove either thing sitting in a linen cupboard smelling of old ladies' soap."

"Lavender," Astrid said automatically. Then, because humor was the only tool she'd never dropped in a crisis: "It's French. It came in a Red Cross parcel. We're fighting a war over soap, apparently, among other things."

He almost smiled. It was the almost that undid her a little — the way it started and then remembered itself.

"I can't run," he said. "Not anymore. I ran once already. Do you know what it's like, running with something like this? Every checkpoint, every uniform, every kind face asking if you've eaten — you start thinking everyone is Grell. You start trusting no one, and then you meet a woman who hides you in a cupboard and brings you barley soup and tells you her plan for saving your life, and you realize—" he looked at her directly now, and Astrid found she couldn't look away — "you realize you stopped being able to tell the difference between surviving and living, and this letter is the only living thing left in your hands."

"The letter isn't alive, Ines. It's paper. It's brave paper, but it can't testify. Men can. Which is why you need to take it somewhere it can be *used*, not buried in a store cupboard with us."

"And leave you here? With him?" He shook his head. "Grell noticed your handwriting. That means he'll notice more. A man like that collects details the way I collected shrapnel — slowly, and then all at once. If he finds the ledger—"

"He won't."

"If he finds the ledger, what does he do to the woman who wrote it?"

Astrid was quiet. Outside, the tide was turning; she could hear the long suck of water pulling back from the shingle, the sound she had come to think of as the sea clearing its throat before speaking.

"I don't know," she admitted. "Something bureaucratic, probably. Grell strikes me as a man who would file my execution correctly and in triplicate."

"This isn't funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It isn't." But his mouth betrayed him again, briefly, and then he leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the smile went out of him entirely. "Listen to me. I was a batman for two years. I laid out a captain's kit every morning and pressed his trousers and knew where he kept his letters, and in two years I learned one thing about officers like Grell: they win by making other people carry the risk. He has never once been in danger himself. He sends drivers into gaps and clerks into archives and nurses into tribunals, and he stays clean. You want to fight him by staying? Staying is exactly the shape of the trap he builds."

"And running is exactly the shape of the hole he digs for people like you," Astrid said, and heard her own voice sharpen. "Eleven men ran, Ines. They ran where he told them to run, and now they're lines through ink. I write down the names of men nobody else will write down. That's the whole of what I do in this world, and it's small, and it's bookkeeping, and I used to think history happened somewhere else — in parliaments, in staff conferences, in places with carpets. But it doesn't. It happens here, in wards, to private soldiers whose files arrive pre-marked *deceased, no record*, and if I run, then I confirm everything I ever believed about myself being a bystander, and I would rather be shot than confirmed in that particular belief."

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere above them a patient coughed twice and was quiet.

"I'm not asking you to stop writing names," Ines said at last, slowly, as if picking his way through wire. "I'm asking you to be alive to keep doing it."

"And I'm asking you to consider that some things are worth more than being alive to do them." She paused. "That came out more heroic than I intended. What I actually mean is: my patients are upstairs. Forty-one of them, six of whom will die this week regardless of anything Grell or God does, and thirty-five of whom will not, if the nursing continues, which requires the nurse. You don't abandon thirty-five people so that one woman can feel safe. Nobody who had ever worked a ward would suggest it."

Ines looked at her — really looked — and something shifted in his chest, some load redistributing its weight.

Because he understood her suddenly. Not her argument; her. He had spent the war watching men talk about duty as though it were a coat you put on, official and buttoned, issued by the Army. Astrid's duty wasn't issued. It was made, stitch by stitch, out of barley soup and careful hands and names written in pencil at the back of a ledger, and she would no more drop it mid-stitch than she'd walk out on a bleeding man. It was the maddest, most sensible thing he'd ever heard.

"My mother was a seamstress," he said, apropos of nothing, and then laughed at himself. "Sorry. It's just — you argue like her. Like being right is a form of housework. Something you get on with."

"My father was a customs clerk," Astrid returned. "You argue like a man who has read one very exciting pamphlet and met no one."

That startled a real laugh out of him — short, rusty, as if the mechanism needed oil — and the sound changed the room. The cupboard became, for a moment, just a place two people sat close together in the lamplight, and the war waited politely outside the door.

"I have an idea," Astrid said. "Since we're both evidently too stubborn to take each other's advice."

"Go on."

"You're right that the letter needs to reach someone who can use it. I'm right that I can't leave, and that hiding in a cupboard is a strategy with a short shelf life. So we stop choosing between your duty and mine." She counted it off on her fingers, briskly, the way she triaged. "One: you stay — not in the cupboard, I can get you into the convalescent ward as a walking wounded case; half those boys haven't washed in a month and nobody looks at faces anyway. Two: we find the eleventh name. My ledger says Private Aldous Merrick, transferred to convalescence on the fourth — which means he may still be here, and if he is, he's the only living witness on this coast. Three: when the letter and the ledger and the witness are in the same room, *then* we decide who runs and who stays, and we decide it together, like adults with a plan instead of fugitives with a soup bowl."

Ines stared at her. "You want to walk a deserter into a military ward."

"I want to walk a batman into a military ward. Desertion requires proof, and Grell keeps losing paperwork — it seems to be his whole method." She stood, brushing lavender-scented fluff from her skirt, and looked down at him with an expression he would remember for years: tired, frightened, and absolutely unbudgeable. "Well? You've been arguing with me for half an hour. Have I won yet?"

"Not yet," Ines said. But he picked up the soup and finished it, every drop, and handed her the bowl with both hands, and they both understood that something had been settled that neither of them would put into words for quite some time.

At the door she paused, hand on the latch.

"Ines. When Vane gave you the letter — did he say why you? Of all people?"

He was quiet for a moment. "He said I was the only man in the company who checked everything twice." A pause. "I thought he was mocking me."

"He wasn't," Astrid said. "Careful people save lives. It's the whole theory of my profession." And she slipped out, leaving him alone with the lavender and the turning tide and the extraordinary, terrifying discovery that he was no longer afraid of the same things he'd been afraid of that morning.

Above them, in the records room, Major Grell turned a page with one gloved finger, and smiled at what he found there.
