## 10. The Eleventh Name

There is a particular smell to a convalescent ward that a field hospital never quite achieves: less blood, more hope, and an undertone of burnt porridge that no amount of lavender water will conquer. I had not been down the long corridor to Ward C in nearly a week — my duties kept me among the acute cases, the ones whose names I wrote in pencil first and ink second — but on the morning after Major Grell's inspection, with his cologne still faintly offending the nurses' station, I found an excuse to make the walk.

The excuse was arithmetic. Ten men, struck from the record. Ten men who had breathed in my ward and vanished from the dispatches like breath from a mirror. I had copied their names into my ledger in my careful schoolroom hand, and I had lain awake doing sums against my will, because sums were what I did instead of praying.

Ten struck through. But Private Willett's file — the second name, the one Matron had surreptitiously let me see in the records room — had carried a convoy number. Convoy 44. And Convoy 44, if the requisition chits were to be believed, had carried eleven drivers.

I had been avoiding that particular sum for three days.

Eleven men assigned. Ten omitted. Either the eleventh was honestly dead — genuinely, properly dead, the way the paperwork claimed they all were — or he was alive somewhere, and every hour he breathed was an hour the command had not yet got around to correcting.

Ward C smelled of burnt porridge and carbolic, and it was full of the walking wounded, which is to say it was full of men performing wellness for each other's benefit. A sergeant with a bandaged hand was losing at cards with theatrical outrage. Two privates by the window were arguing about whether a certain film actress was superior to a certain music-hall singer, with the passionate imprecision of men who had seen neither in over a year. Nobody was dying. It was, by the standards of the last two years, almost festive.

I made my way down the row of beds with a clipboard I did not need, checking dressings I had no authority to check, and all the while I was reading the admission board at the end of the room.

Eleven beds occupied. My eye went down the names twice, because the first time my heart was making too much noise to hear anything.

*Driver T. Osei.*

I did not let myself stop walking. I had learned that much — that the body betrays you fastest when it stops. I finished the row, complimented the card sergeant on a bluff I suspected he was running, and only then, at the board, allowed myself to stand and read like a woman studying a shopping list.

Thomas Osei. Admitted nine days ago. Gunshot wound, left thigh. Stable. Transferred from Casualty Clearing Station 12.

Convoy 44.

He was twenty-three or twenty-four, brown-skinned, thin in the way of men who had been eating army food since before they could grow a proper beard, and he was sitting up in the fourth bed with a book he was not reading. I noticed this because I had spent two years learning to read soldiers, and a man holding a book upside-down-adjacent, turning pages at random intervals, eyes fixed on the door — that man was not convalescing. That man was waiting for something to come through the door.

I knew the feeling intimately. I had been that man for six months, except my door was a ledger.

* * *

Sister Bright caught me in the corridor. Beatrice Bright is the closest thing Ward C has to weather: she arrives, everyone adjusts, and afterwards nobody can quite say what happened but the beds are all made.

"You're a long way from your dying men, Crane." She said it without malice; Sister Bright regarded death as a professional inconvenience, like a delivery arriving during tea service.

"I'm checking admissions against transfers," I said, which was true in the way that a keyhole is a door.

"Mm." She fell into step beside me, which meant she wanted to talk and wished it not to look like talking. "You've had visitors asking after this ward. Twice now. Officers' aides — very clean boots, very soft voices. Asking whether any of our men had 'served with transport units recently.' Not naming names. Just... browsing."

My stomach performed a small controlled demolition. "When?"

"Tuesday. And again yesterday evening, late, when the orderlies were changing shift. The second one didn't sign the visitors' book." She glanced at me sideways. Her eyes are the pale grey of hospital linen, and about as easy to hide things from. "You know, Crane, most nurses, when officers browse, they're flattered."

"I've never been most nurses."

"No," she agreed. "That's rather what worries people about you." We walked four steps in silence, our heels keeping time on the linoleum. Then, in a voice pitched precisely for me and no one else: "The man in bed four hasn't slept properly in three nights. Jumps at doors. Won't take his morphia — says it makes him slow. I've nursed a good many frightened men, and most of them are frightened of dying." She stopped at the ward door and looked back toward bed four. "He's frightened of paperwork. I don't entirely know what to do with that."

"Neither do I," I said, which was the first lie I had told her. I knew exactly what to do with it. What I did not know was whether I was brave enough.

"Well," said Sister Bright, straightening her cuffs, "if some sensible person were to sit with him and get him talking, it might help him sleep. And if that sensible person happened to be good with records, so much the better. Some men can't rest until they think someone's written them down properly." She said it lightly, airily, the way one remarks on the weather, and swept off toward the linen cupboard as though she had not just handed me a key to something.

I stood in the doorway of Ward C and made myself do the sum out loud, under my breath, where no one could strike it through.

Ten names in my ledger. One name on the board. Eleven minus ten equals one witness, breathing, nine days stable, two visits from men with soft voices, and a nurse telling me, in the language of sensible persons, that time was shorter than porridge.

* * *

I waited until afternoon visiting lull, when the wards go drowsy and even the card sergeant naps between hands, and then I pulled a chair up to bed four with the shameless confidence of a woman who has checked a thousand pulses and never once been asked why.

"Driver Osei. I'm Nurse Crane. I'm going to look at that dressing, and you're going to pretend to be asleep, and then we're both going to be disappointed in each other."

He looked at me. He had remarkable eyes, dark and very still, the sort of eyes that had learned early to give nothing away — and they gave away everything anyway, because fear is the one thing that leaks through any training. "I haven't done anything," he said.

"Nobody's done anything," I agreed. "That's rather the whole trouble, isn't it."

His hand found the edge of the blanket and gripped it. "If you're from the convoy board, I've already given my statement. Twice. Same statement both times. You can check."

"I'm not from any board." I lifted the dressing corner, inspected a wound that was healing beautifully, and lowered my voice to the register I used for men who needed truth delivered gently, like medicine. "I'm from the ledger nobody keeps. Driver Osei — Thomas — how many men drove out with Convoy 44?"

The silence lasted long enough for the card sergeant to snore twice. When Osei finally spoke, his voice had gone flat and careful, a soldier reciting. "Eleven drivers. Official strength, eleven."

"And how many came back?"

"That depends who's asking."

"Astrid Crane," I said. "Ask anyone in this hospital. I write down names. All of them. Even ones other people prefer forgotten." I took my little notebook from my apron pocket — the working copy, the one I kept for temperatures and pulse rates and small human facts — and set it open on his blanket. "Elias Vane sent a letter out of the dark with a man called Ines. In that letter there are names. I have ten of them in my own hand. I believe yours is the eleventh, and I believe the reason you're lying here jumping at doors is that the first ten are dead, officially, on paper, while the men who killed them are filling out forms upstairs."

Something moved behind those still dark eyes — not courage exactly, but its raw material, waiting to be refined.

"You've had visitors," I went on quietly. "Twice. Clean boots, soft voices. Men who ask questions without naming names. I'd wager my next month's pay they didn't ask you to confirm anything. They asked you to *remember correctly.*"

Osei's grip on the blanket turned his knuckles the colour of bone. "The second one," he said, very low, "stood exactly where you're standing. And he smiled, and he said, 'Driver Osei, you've been very fortunate. Most men in your accident didn't survive.' And then he thanked me for my service and left, and I lay here all night understanding perfectly what fortunate means when a major's aide says it."

"Yes," I said. "I've met the major. He inspected our paperwork this week. He complimented my handwriting." I heard my own voice do something it hadn't done before — harden. "He'll be back, Thomas. Or his men will. And the third visit won't be a smile."

"They told me the war moves fast." He laughed — one dry note, no humour in it at all. "Nobody told me it moves toward you."

"Then we move faster." I tapped the notebook. "Not today. Not while you're too weak to run and too watched to speak. But when the moment comes — and it's coming, I can feel it the way you feel weather in a bad leg — I need to know that when I ask you to put your name on paper, properly, on the record, witnessed, you'll do it."

He looked at the notebook for a long time. Then at me. Then at the door — the habit of a lifetime, and who was I to begrudge him it.

"You know what I keep thinking?" he said. "Nine days in this bed. Nine days being nobody. No unit, no convoy, just 'gunshot wound, left thigh,' like the leg enlisted on its own. They took my name off me the day that convoy turned down the coast road." His jaw set, and for a moment he looked every hour of his age and several years beyond it. "If I sign. If I say it out loud, in front of officers, in ink — will it bring the others back? Will it matter?"

I thought of Private Willett, begging me with his last breath to write his name down properly. I thought of the file marked *deceased, no record*, and how a man can die twice, once in the body and once in the archive, and how the second death is the one the historians never notice.

"It won't bring them back," I said. "Nothing does. But it will mean they existed. There's a difference between dying and being erased, Thomas, and it's the entire difference, and it's the only thing left that we can give them."

The actress-and-singer debate by the window reached a crescendo and collapsed into laughter. Somewhere a trolley squeaked. Ordinary sounds, an ordinary afternoon — and underneath it, both of us listening for boots.

"All right," Osei said at last, and his voice cracked only slightly on it. "When the moment comes. On paper. Properly." He managed something that was almost a smile. "But I'm telling you now, Nurse Crane — I want to read it before I sign it. Every word. After the week I've had, I'm not signing anything else blind."

"Wise man," I said, and meant it more than he could know.

I closed the notebook and stood, and it was then that I saw, through the ward's tall window, the black motorcar at the gates — the same car, the same silhouette at the wheel, the same unhurried arrogance of a man who owns every road he drives down.

Major Grell had arrived again. And this time he was not browsing.

He was asking the adjutant, cap in hand and expression pleasant, for the file of one patient: gunshot wound, left thigh, admitted nine days prior. I watched the adjutant nod. I watched the orderly start up the corridor toward Ward C with the speed of a man who wants to be helpful and survive the war.

Thomas Osei saw my face change, and followed my eyes to the window, and went white under his brown skin.

"Nurse Crane," he whispered. "What do I do?"

And I discovered that I already knew the answer — that somewhere between a dying private's last request and a stranger's oilskin packet, I had become a woman with answers — and that the answer was going to cost me everything I had spent six months carefully not becoming.

"Nothing," I said, pulling the curtain around his bed with steady hands while my heart hammered like a telegraph key. "You do nothing, and you remember nothing, and you leave the rest to me. Because in about four minutes, a very courteous man is going to ask me some very specific questions about my ledger."

I picked up my clipboard, arranged my face into bland professional calm, and started up the corridor to meet him.

"And God help me," I murmured to no one at all, "I've become such a good liar."
