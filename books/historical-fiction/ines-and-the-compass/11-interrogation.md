## 11. Interrogation

The summons came at breakfast, delivered by a corporal with the apologetic posture of a boy asked to fetch a dead mouse. *Sister Crane. Records room. Ten o'clock. Major Grell.*

Astrid had been expecting it for three days, in the way one expects rain — watching the sky, noting the pressure change, going about her work anyway. She finished her tea. It had gone lukewarm, but she drank it all the same; you didn't know when you'd get another.

"You look pale, Sister," said Orderly Bramble, who was sixty if he was a day and had been declared unfit for everything except carrying men, which he did like a man carrying his own sons.

"The light in here is unflattering," Astrid said. "It flatters no one."

"That's true enough." He considered her porridge. "You going to eat that?"

"Take it."

She washed her cup herself, which she never did, and dried it thoroughly, which she did even less. It seemed suddenly important to leave things tidy. Not because she believed tidiness would save her, but because if this was the day her luck ran out, she wanted to be the sort of woman who left a clean cup behind.

At ten minutes to ten she checked her appearance in the ward's cracked mirror. Her face looked the same as ever: sensible, thirty-one, the kind of face matrons trusted and majors overlooked. That had always been her great advantage. She had built a whole quiet rebellion on being overlooked.

She wondered, walking the corridor toward the records room, whether it still worked now that she knew what she was doing. There was a difference, she thought, between an innocent woman and a guilty one pretending innocence, and she feared the difference lived somewhere around the eyes.

Then she corrected herself. She'd been lying since the first struck-through name. Six months of lying, small and steady as a pulse. The only new thing was doing it to a man's face.

* * *

The records room had been rearranged for the occasion. The long table where clerks normally hunched over manifests stood bare, scrubbed, its surface catching the grey Channel light through salt-fogged windows. Two chairs faced each other across it like a pair of chess players waiting for someone to declare the game begun.

Major Grell sat at the far end, reading.

He was smaller than she remembered from the parade inspection — or perhaps *assembled* was the word. A neat man. Everything about him was neat: the parting of his hair, the angle of his cap on the table beside him, the way his papers lay squared to the table's edge as though they had been issued that way. He wore spectacles with plain rims and read with the tip of one finger moving beneath each line, unhurried, thorough.

"Sister Crane." He did not stand. He gestured at the chair opposite with two fingers, as though beckoning something small. "Thank you for your punctuality."

"You said ten o'clock, sir."

"I did. And here you are at ten exactly." He smiled briefly, a movement of the mouth without any involvement of the eyes. "Punctuality is a form of honesty, don't you find? A person who says ten o'clock and means ten o'clock is a person whose other statements may be relied upon."

"I hadn't thought of it that way, sir."

"No?" He returned to his papers. She counted her own heartbeats. At twenty-two he set them down and folded his hands. "I've been reviewing the hospital's ledgers. Yours, chiefly. You have a beautiful hand, Sister Crane. Truly. There are perhaps four people on this coast who keep records the way you do — clear, consistent, complete." He paused on the last word just long enough for it to acquire a second meaning. "It makes irregularities very easy to spot."

"Irregularities, sir?"

"Mm." He drew a ledger toward him — hers, the current one — and opened it to a page she knew by heart. "Let me ask you about procedure. A wounded man comes into your ward. What do you record?"

"Name, regiment, service number, nature of wound, treatment given, disposition."

"Disposition meaning?"

"Transferred, discharged, or—" she let the pause sit naturally, "—died."

"Died." He nodded slowly, a man savouring a familiar dish. "And when a man dies, Sister Crane, what happens to his file?"

"It goes up to divisional records, sir. A copy stays with us."

"And who prepares the copy?"

"I do."

"So if there were a discrepancy between your copy and the divisional record — say, regarding a man's disposition — it would be a discrepancy *you* created."

The trap was so plainly built that she could see the join-marks, could almost smell the fresh-cut timber of it. She made herself look mildly confused rather than mildly alarmed. Confusion was safer. Confused women got explanations; alarmed women got follow-up questions.

"I'm not sure I follow, sir. Our copies have always matched."

"Have they." It was not a question so much as a place to hang his hat. He turned the ledger around and slid it across the table so she could read it. One name had been circled in pencil — Private D. Whitlock, whom she had dressed and bandaged on the fourth of March and who had walked out of her ward two days later under his own power. In the divisional record, Whitlock was deceased. "This man. Do you remember him?"

She had rehearsed this. Not Whitlock specifically, but the shape of the question. *Never hesitate,* Matron had told her once, years ago, teaching her to give bad news to families. *Hesitation is cruelty. Decide what you're going to say and say it as though it were always true.* Matron had meant kindnesses. The principle apparently held for lies.

"Whitlock," Astrid said, and frowned gently, the frown of a busy woman searching a crowded memory. "Gunshot, upper thigh. No — that might have been the other one. We had several thigh wounds that week." She shook her head. "I'm sorry, sir. We pass through two hundred men a month. Names blur."

"They blur," Grell agreed pleasantly. "And yet yours is such a careful hand."

"Careful hands can copy carelessly written reports, sir. If the intake slips were wrong—"

"If the intake slips were wrong." He took off his spectacles and polished them on a handkerchief that appeared from nowhere, giving her, for a few seconds, a face that was oddly naked and oddly young. Without the glasses he looked like a schoolmaster who had wandered into the wrong story. "Do you know what I admire most about you, Sister Crane? You anticipate. I begin a sentence and you arrive at its end before I do. Most nurses I interview spend their energy looking frightened. You spend yours thinking." He put the glasses back on. "It's refreshing. It's also, I should tell you, unwise."

"Sir, I don't understand what I've done wrong."

"Nothing. Yet." He said it with the mildness of a weather report. "That is rather the point of today's conversation. To ensure nothing goes wrong. To offer you the chance to be, as you so evidently are, a careful person, on the correct side of the paperwork."

He rose then, finally, and went to the window. Below, the tide was turning; she could hear it, that low dragging exhale the shingle made when the water went out. He watched it as though the sea were also a ledger he was auditing.

"Do you know how history is kept, Sister Crane?" he said, not turning. "People imagine it is kept by the victors. A pleasant myth, but imprecise. History is kept by *clerks*. By whoever held the pen at the moment the pen mattered. Everything else — the speeches, the monuments — arrives afterward and merely decorates what the clerk wrote down." He turned back to her. "You hold a pen. I hold a larger one. The question before us is simply whether we write in the same book."

* * *

He offered her tea, sent for it, and while they waited he talked — easily, almost warmly — about supply logistics. She understood immediately that this too was a test, or a lure. Convoy H-17, he called it, and watched her face as he said it. She gave him the blankness of a woman who has heard the phrase but never had cause to care about it. Inside, something cold turned over. So that was the name. She filed it away with the eleven names, in the vault behind her sternum that had no lock and therefore could never be searched.

When the tea came he poured it himself, which surprised her, and pushed a cup across the table.

"Now," he said, sitting again, becoming once more entirely neat, "to business. There is a position opening at divisional headquarters. Records oversight. It carries the rank of assistant commandant, a salary to match, and — I will be direct, because I suspect you prefer it — access to the entire correspondence of this sector. Every dispatch. Every manifest. A woman with your hand and your memory could, within a year, be the most informed person on this coast short of myself." He sipped. "It is offered because I believe wasted precision is a sin. Your precision is currently wasted on bandage inventories."

For one absurd moment she thought of the linen store, of a mud-caked man sleeping under sheets that smelled of carbolic, and nearly laughed. Instead she looked down at her tea.

"It's very kind, sir."

"It isn't kind. It's arithmetic." He set down his cup. "There is a condition, naturally. Cooperation. If you recall anything irregular — any officer or person who encouraged you to alter records, any stranger who passed through your wards, any document that came into your keeping which did not belong to you — you would bring it to me. Before anyone else. Discreetly." He produced, from his breast pocket, a card, and laid it on the table between them like a man laying down a low trump. "My direct line. Not through the switchboard. Ever."

She looked at the card. Thick paper. His name printed in small type, nothing wasted.

"I'll be honest with you, sir," she said, and heard her own voice do something remarkable — go slightly flat, slightly tired, the voice of a woman confessing a small professional vanity. "I have wanted out of this hospital for six months. The mud, the hours, the— we lose men here who'd live anywhere else, and nobody above us ever asks why. If there's a desk with dry floors at divisional HQ, I'd be a fool to refuse it."

Something moved behind his spectacles. Satisfaction, she thought, and underneath it, faintly, disappointment — the auditor's sorrow at finding the books balanced by ordinary greed after all. People were simpler than he feared. That was his creed. She could feel it comforting him like a hot water bottle.

"But," she went on, before the silence could curdle, "you said it yourself — I'm careful. If I hand you rumours and half-memories to make myself look useful, that's not careful. That's a nurse telling a surgeon which limb to take off based on gossip. Give me a little time. Let me go back through my ledgers properly, the way I'd do it at a proper desk, and bring you facts. Not feelings."

Grell regarded her for a long moment. Then he smiled, and this time it reached his eyes, which was somehow worse.

"There it is," he said softly. "The anticipation again. I offer you a fish and you negotiate for the rod." He tapped the card once with one neat finger. "Take it. Take a week. But Sister Crane—" the pleasantness thinned, just at the edges, like ice at the rim of a bucket, "—understand what a week means. Things are moving. Convoys, tribunals, tides. When the water changes, I will want my facts. And I will want them from you, not from whatever I dig up without you. The first version of a story is the one that survives. Ask any historian."

"I've never met a historian, sir."

"No," he said, gathering his papers, squaring them, capping his pencil with a small click. "But you're about to make some."

* * *

She stood when he stood. She shook the hand he offered — dry, cool, precise grip, calibrated to communicate that he could crush things but chose not to — and she wished him good morning in a steady voice.

At the door his voice stopped her.

"One more thing." He had not turned from the window. "Your matron. Sister Ashcombe. She's been here since the first month of the war, hasn't she. Long service. Devoted." A pause, delicately weighted. "It would be a shame if her final posting were marred by an inquiry into the conduct of her staff. These things attach themselves to whoever is nominally responsible. Matrons especially. Do you follow?"

"She keeps excellent order, sir. Her staff wouldn't dream of misconduct."

"Yes," said Grell. "So I've begun to hope."

She walked back along the corridor at her usual pace. Usual pace — that was a discipline too, like the clean cup, like the mild frown. Her legs wanted to run. Her hands, she noticed with detached interest, were perfectly steady, and she hated them for it, hated the smooth machine she had apparently become somewhere in the last six months without ever marking the date. There should have been a moment, she felt, when a person stopped being a bystander and started being the other thing. There should have been a bell, or at least a sensation. Instead there was only a card in her pocket getting heavier with every step, and the taste of her own lie still in her mouth — and it hadn't even been a lie, that was the devil of it. Half of it had been true. She did want out. She did hate the mud. Grell had interviewed her for forty minutes and come away holding a coin that she had let him believe was her whole self, heads or tails, greedor loyalty, while the real coin sat in her chest with eleven names engraved on it that he would never read.

She found Matron in the dispensary, counting morphine tablets into their paper envelopes, lips moving.

"Well?" Matron said, without looking up.

"He offered me a promotion."

Matron's counting slowed. "And?"

"And I asked for time to review the ledgers."

Now Matron looked up. Her eyes went over Astrid's face the way she went over a new admission — checking colour, checking pupils, triaging. Whatever she found there made her jaw tighten.

"A week," Astrid said. "He gave me a week."

"Then you've got six days less than you think. Men like that give time the way the tide gives sand — generously, and only until it turns." Matron sealed an envelope, set it down, and looked at the window, where the light had gone the hard silver of an afternoon that meant weather. "The movement orders came through while you were upstairs. All patients fit to travel, and the staff to accompany them, embark Thursday morning. Hospital ship *Dover Castle*, dawn tide."

Thursday. Astrid did the arithmetic and felt the floor tilt gently under her certainties. Three days. Less than three days, once you subtracted the night, and everything that mattered would happen at night.

"Grell controls the manifests aboard," Matron went on quietly. "Every name on that ship will pass across his desk before the gangplank goes down. Which means, Sister Crane, that whatever you're going to do—" she picked up the next envelope and resumed counting, voice level, "—you had best decide tonight. Because after Thursday, there is no record anyone can add to. Only the ones he writes."

Astrid stood in the doorway a moment longer, the major's card pressing its corner against her ribs like a splinter.

Tonight, then. Tonight she would find Ines, and the eleventh name, and they would count what they had — pages, courage, minutes — and see whether it came to enough.

Down at the shore, faint through the stone walls, the tide was coming in.
