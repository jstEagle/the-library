## 12. The Witness Breaks

The eleventh man's name was Corporal Thomas Fenwick, and he had a way of folding himself into corners as though the world might forget him if he made himself small enough. I knew the trick well. I had spent three months perfecting it myself.

I found him in the convalescent ward on a grey Tuesday afternoon, propped up by the window with his left arm in a sling and his right hand — his writing hand, thank God — wrapped around a cup of tea that had gone cold hours ago. He was watching the rain come off the Channel in long silver sheets, and when I sat down on the chair beside his cot, he flinched so hard the cup rattled against its saucer.

"Easy," I said. "I'm not one of them."

"There's no *them*." His voice was flat as a dropped plank. "You're imagining things, mate. Everybody here is imagining things."

"I was Vane's batman."

That landed. I watched it land — watched the flinch go out of him like a tide pulling back from a beach, leaving something rawer underneath. He looked at me properly for the first time. He had one of those faces that hadn't decided yet whether to be young or old; the war had got halfway through the argument and then stalled.

"He's dead," Fenwick said.

"Yes," I said. "So am I, officially. It's a very convenient war for some people."

He almost laughed. Almost. It came out as a sort of cough, and then he set the cold tea down on the sill and looked at his own hands for a while, and I let the silence do its work. Silence does more work than talking ever does. Captain Vane taught me that too, though he'd have phrased it better.

"Astrid says you saw the convoy," I said at last. "The third of September."

Fenwick's jaw worked. "Crane. The ledger woman."

"The ledger woman," I agreed. It was as good a title as any. She'd have hated it and secretly kept it, I suspected, filed away somewhere behind her careful handwriting.

"They told us to say it was shelled," Fenwick said, to the window rather than to me. "That's all. They didn't even tell us a good lie. The trucks went east instead of west, and the crates were marked medical but they weighed like ammunition, and when Hobbs asked about it, Hobbs got sent home on a stretcher he didn't need, and the rest of us got a lecture from a major with a voice like a filing cabinet." He swallowed. "Grell."

"Grell."

"And now you want me to put my name on paper saying so." He turned to look at me, and there it was — the fear, plain and enormous, filling up the space behind his eyes like water filling a boot. "They've been here twice already. Two nice gentlemen with very clean boots. Asking how I'm settling in. Whether I remember the third of September. Smiling the whole time." He held up his bandaged arm. "This happened falling downstairs. That's what they wrote. Falling downstairs, in a hospital, in bed."

"I know," I said quietly. "I'm sorry."

"Sorry doesn't sign statements."

"No," I said. "Courage signs statements. And courage is just being frightened and doing it anyway, and taking as long about it as you need."

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the windows.

"How long?" he said.

"Ten minutes," I said. "A name, a date, what you saw, signed at the bottom. Astrid will witness it. Then we hide it where no clean boots will ever find it, and if anything happens to any of us, it comes out anyway. That's the whole point of paper, Corporal. Paper is braver than people. Paper doesn't flinch."

He laughed then — really laughed, a short startled bark — and something in the ward seemed to loosen around us. A man two cots over snored on. Somewhere below us, a trolley rattled along the corridor, and the rain came down, and none of it, yet, was anything to be afraid of.

"All right," Fenwick said. "Paper. Fetch your paper."

* * *

Astrid brought the page folded inside a magazine, which I thought was a touch of real genius until she admitted she'd simply grabbed the nearest thing, but I let her keep the credit. We gathered at the foot of Fenwick's cot with the studied casualness of three people who had never once conspired in their lives, and Astrid smoothed the paper flat on her knee and handed Fenwick her pen — her good pen, the one she used for the ledger, the one whose nib I had watched her clean at midnight like a surgeon with a scalpel.

"Just tell it plainly," she said. "Plain is harder to argue with than clever."

"Plain," Fenwick repeated, like a prayer. He bent over the page against his knee, and his hand shook, and then — this was the thing I'll remember about Thomas Fenwick as long as I live — his hand stopped shaking. Not immediately. But somewhere in the middle of the second sentence, the letters steadied themselves, growing small and square and certain, and I realized I was watching a man become brave in real time, minute by minute, the way a fire takes hold of wet wood.

*On the night of 3 September,* he wrote, *I was ordered to assist in loading lorries of the 4th Supply Column which had been diverted contrary to standing orders. The manifest read medical stores. The weight did not. When Private Hobbs raised the discrepancy he was removed on falsified grounds. Eleven men of the loading party were later recorded deceased. I can account for ten of them alive after the date of record.*

He sat back. The ward was very quiet except for the rain.

"That's it," he said hoarsely. "That's all of it. It doesn't sound like much."

"It sounds like everything," Astrid said.

She witnessed it beneath his signature — her name, her rank, the date, written in that beautiful unarguable hand — and I folded the page twice and slipped it into the oilskin packet, and into the inside pocket of the coat Matron had procured for me, and for perhaps forty seconds we were three people who had done an enormously brave thing and gotten away with it.

Then the doors at the end of the ward opened, and two men in very clean boots came in out of the rain.

* * *

I had imagined this moment, in the way you imagine things at three in the morning: the freeze, the plunge of the stomach, the sudden deafening noise of your own blood. Imagination had prepared me for all of that. What it had not prepared me for was Fenwick's face.

He didn't go pale. He went *grey*, the exact grey of old dishwater, and his hand shot out and gripped my sleeve with a strength I wouldn't have credited in a convalescent, and he said, in a whisper with almost no air in it: "Not again. I can't do the stairs again."

"It's all right—"

"It's not all right—" His breath was coming fast and shallow now, quickening toward something worse, and I understood with terrible clarity that Thomas Fenwick had reached the absolute end of his ration of courage, and that nobody — nobody honest — could blame him. He'd spent it all on four sentences and a signature. Courage measured in minutes, Astrid had said once, and I was learning what she meant: courage isn't a quality you carry. It's a currency you spend, and Fenwick's purse was empty, and the boots were crossing the ward.

The taller of the two men — I'd seen him before, Grell's aide, the one who smiled with only the bottom half of his face — stopped at the first cot and began a low pleasant conversation with its occupant. Working his way down. Methodical. They weren't here for a social call; someone had talked, or someone had guessed, or the rain had simply decided to stop being on our side.

"The coal cellar," Astrid breathed. She said it without moving her lips, the way I imagine she recites names under her breath at night, and I nearly laughed at the absurdity of it — the ledger woman, giving directions like a sergeant-major. "Matron showed me. Through the linen store, the door behind the boilers."

"Fenwick," I said. "Look at me. You're going to stand up now, slowly, like a man going for a walk, and you're going to lean on me, because your head hurts — your head has been hurting for days, everyone knows it — and we're going toward the linen store because you feel faint. Can you do faint?"

"I've been doing faint for a week," he whispered.

"Good man. Best performance of your life."

We stood up. Astrid drifted to the other side of the ward, picked up a chart, became instantly and completely a nurse doing nurse things, and I have never loved anyone more than I loved her in that moment, pretending to fuss over a sleeping private while two of Grell's men worked their way down a row of cots twenty feet behind her.

Fenwick leaned on me. He was heavier than he looked, or perhaps fear weighs something after all. We moved down the aisle between the beds at the pace of a man escorting a wobbly friend, and the aide's voice droned on behind us — *and how are you settling in, corporal, and does anything come back to you about September* — and Fenwick's grip on my arm tightened with every step until I thought he'd leave bruises, and I welcomed every one.

The linen store smelled of soap and starch and something older, something like the inside of the building itself. Astrid caught the door before it could swing shut and slip through sideways, and there she was in the half-dark with us, breathing hard, eyes bright as coins.

"Behind the boilers," she whispered. "There's a hatch. It's supposed to be bricked up but it isn't. Matron uses it."

"For coal?"

"For whatever Matron needs it for," Astrid said, in a tone that closed the subject, and I added another item to the long and growing list of questions I intended never to ask that woman.

The cellar was black as the inside of a hat. We went down iron steps with Fenwick wheezing between us, past the great humped shapes of the boilers — warm, mercifully banked for the evening — and there, sure enough, low in the wall, a wooden hatch with a rope handle and a smear of coal dust around its edges like a signature.

"In," I said. "Quick as you can."

Fenwick got his shoulders through, then stuck. Panic does that — makes a man rigid as a fence post. I put my hands on either side of him and felt the whole length of him shaking.

"Corporal," I said, close to his ear. "Thomas. Listen to me. Four sentences. You wrote four sentences today that eleven families have been waiting six months to hear. Whatever else happens, that's done. That exists. You're on the record now, son. Nothing those boots do can unwrite you."

He let out a breath that was half sob, and the sob loosened him, and he slid through into the dark beyond like a man going through ice, and I followed, and above us — muffled, then less muffled — came the sound of the ward door opening and the bottom-half-of-the-face voice saying, pleasantly, "Is Corporal Fenwick in his bed? Only we were told he'd taken a turn..."

And then Astrid's voice, cool as well-water: "Taken a turn where? He's been in the solarium since two o'clock. I'll fetch the sister's log if you doubt me. Page forty-one. My handwriting, I'm afraid; you'll find it very legible."

God. *Page forty-one.* She'd invented a log entry the way other people blow their noses, and she'd offered them her handwriting as proof, and I lay in the coal-dark with my cheek against the earth and understood that I would follow that woman into anything — into the sea, into the tribunal, into whatever came next.

Boots overhead. Lots of boots. They searched the ward, then the corridor, then — the voices said — the linen store, and I heard drawers pulled and shelves thumped and Astrid answering question after question in that same level, unhurried voice, offering pages, citing times, being precisely and infuriatingly helpful, until at last the aide said something about checking with Major Grell, and the door closed, and the boots went away, and the three of us lay in the coal dust and listened to our own hearts.

It was Astrid who crawled through last, arriving beside me smelling of starch and lamp oil, and in the pitch dark her hand found my shoulder and squeezed once — confirmation, communion, the whole conversation conducted in five fingers.

"Well," came Fenwick's voice out of the blackness, thin and shaking and — I could hardly believe it — threaded with laughter. "How was that for faint?"

"Convincing," I said. "Though you overdid the wheeze in the middle."

"That wasn't acting, that was genuine terror."

"We couldn't tell the difference," Astrid said. "Which is the mark of good theatre."

We lay there a moment longer, three conspirators in a coal cellar, dust settling on us like the beginning of snow. Then Astrid's hand found mine again, tighter this time, and she said the thing we had all been thinking and none of us had dared to say:

"He'll come back. And next time he won't send polite men with clean boots. He'll seal the hospital." A pause, and her voice dropped lower still. "Ines — the ship. There's a ship sailing on the morning tide. If we're going to move, we move tomorrow. All of us. Everything."

Above us, far off across the rain, a whistle sounded — one long note, then two short ones, the docks signaling, the tide turning.

Tomorrow, then. Everything, tomorrow.
