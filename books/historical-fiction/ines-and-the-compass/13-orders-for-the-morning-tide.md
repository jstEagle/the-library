## 13. Orders for the Morning Tide

The orders arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were, in my experience, days on which nothing happened — the middle children of the week, dull and dependable. One did not expect one's entire world to be repossessed between breakfast and lunch.

Sister Bright brought me the paper herself. She was a small, quick woman with a face like a folded napkin and a habit of speaking in whispers even when there was nothing to whisper about, so that receiving bad news from her always felt faintly conspiratorial.

"Matron says you're to look at this," she whispered, pressing the flimsy typed sheet into my hand as though it might go off.

I looked. I have read a great many documents in my life, most of them dull, several of them lies, and this one managed to be both at once. It was headed with the usual eagle-and-crown, stamped twice, and informed us that *by order of the Deputy Director of Medical Services, all walking wounded and cot cases currently accommodated at No. 9 Casualty Clearing Station are to be prepared for evacuation by hospital ship, sailing dawn tide, Friday*. All documentation to be completed and countersigned prior to embarkation. Countersigning officer: Major A. Grell.

I read it three times. The words did not improve with familiarity.

"Dawn Friday," Sister Bright whispered. "That's the day after tomorrow."

"So it is," I said.

"There'll be such a rush. Eleven men on the dangerously ill list, and they want them *shipped*."

"They want them shipped," I agreed, "or they want them gone. It amounts to the same thing on paper."

She gave me a nervous little laugh and hurried off, and I stood in the corridor holding the order and feeling something I could not immediately name. It took me a moment to identify it, because I had not felt it before — or rather, I had felt its opposite for so long that its absence had come to seem like the natural state of things. It was clarity. Terrible, trapped, crystalline clarity. For months I had lived in a fog of small defiances: names copied into my ledger at midnight, struck-through men recorded properly in my own private book, all of it done in the comfortable belief that I was keeping a candle lit in a window somewhere no one would ever look. Now someone was looking. Now the window had a deadline attached.

*Friday. Dawn tide.*

* * *

I found Matron in her office, doing what Matron did best, which was making a hostile bureaucracy behave through sheer force of personality and a well-organized drawer of pens. She was fifty-some years old, straight-backed as a tent pole, with grey hair pinned so severely it seemed to be serving a sentence, and she had run this hospital through shelling, influenza, and the arrival of four successive commanding officers who each believed they had invented administration.

She read my face before I'd said a word.

"You've seen the orders."

"I've seen the orders."

"Then sit down, Miss Crane, and stop looming. You loom when you're frightened. You've been doing it since you were twenty minutes old."

I sat. This startled me more than the orders had, though I suppose I should explain: Matron Crane is my aunt. Not by blood — by the older and sterner kinship of having known me since I was born in the village she still, in moments of stress, referred to as *home*. She had pulled strings to get me posted here when the war started, over my protests, and had never once treated me differently from any other nurse under her command, which I had always counted among her virtues. It was only now, sitting across her desk with my hands folded like a schoolgirl, that I understood how completely I had mistaken her.

"You knew," I said.

"I suspected." She aligned a pen with the edge of her blotter, a tiny violence of precision. "Grell's office telephoned yesterday asking after our census figures. Census figures, Astrid. Nobody asks a field hospital for census figures unless somebody upstairs has noticed that the arithmetic doesn't please them."

"The arithmetic being eleven men too alive."

"The arithmetic being whatever Major Grell says it is." She looked up then, and her eyes were flat and grey as Channel weather. "You will notice the order requires every document aboard that ship to be countersigned by him personally. Every file. Every discharge paper. Every record of admission and death. Whatever leaves this station on Friday will leave as his version of events."

"And whatever doesn't leave—"

"—will be ash by Saturday, if he's thorough, which I believe he is." She said it without drama, the way she might announce a delivery of dressings. "So. The question before us is not whether he intends to bury his mistakes. He has been burying them for six months. The question is what we intend to do about it in the next forty-eight hours."

*I*, she had said. Not *you*. We.

"Matron," I began carefully, "if I didn't know better, I would say you were suggesting—"

"I am suggesting nothing. Suggestion implies uncertainty." She opened her desk drawer, removed a fresh bottle of ink, and set it on the blotter between us with a small decisive click. "I am observing that this hospital will be emptied by Friday morning, that a ship sails at dawn, and that you have always been an appallingly fast writer when sufficiently motivated. Beyond that, I am observing nothing whatsoever, because observing things in wartime is how people end up in front of tribunals."

The word *tribunal* went through me like cold water. She saw it land, and for a moment — just a moment — the tent-pole posture softened, and she was simply my aunt Agnes again, who had once fished me out of the mill pond and sworn me to secrecy with a jam tart.

"He's coming tomorrow, isn't he," I said. "Grell."

"He is. Inspection tour. He'll walk the wards, praise the cleanliness, ask you three questions that sound innocent and are not, and leave with exactly what he came for." She closed the drawer. "Which is why tonight, Astrid, you are going to do whatever it is you do in that linen room after lights-out, and you are going to do it faster than you have ever done anything in your life."

I stared at her. "How long have you known about the linen room?"

"Since March." A pause. "The key fits both doors, dear. That was not an accident. I had it cut that way."

* * *

I told Ines at dusk, behind the coal sheds where we had taken to meeting because it was the one place on the station without windows. He received the news standing very still, the way he did — stillness was his native language, learned, I think, in the worst possible school — and when I finished he was quiet for so long that I filled the silence myself, which is a fault of mine.

"Two days," I said. "Perhaps less. If Grell countersigns everything aboard, then the moment we're at sea, every scrap of paper confirming those men existed goes into his briefcase and comes out as fiction. And we'll be passengers on his fiction. Literally. He'll be able to lose us over the side if he likes, and the log will say we died of our wounds."

"No," Ines said slowly. "He won't lose you over the side. You're more useful landed somewhere quiet and charged with something." He said it without cruelty, merely as fact, the way one notes the weather. "Deserters get shot, Miss Crane. Nurses who forge records get tribunals. He's been very tidy about it so far. He'll stay tidy."

"You may call me Astrid, you know. You've hidden in my linen room for two weeks. I feel we've passed the formal stage."

"Astrid." He tried the name the way a man tests ice. Then: "There's a boat train from the port. Civilians on leave, medical staff rotated home. If we could get aboard *that* instead—"

"With no papers? With Grell's military police already watching the gate? They'd take you before you reached the platform, and they'd take me for aiding you, and the ledger would burn in the same week regardless." I shook my head. "No. The ship is terrible and it's also the only door he's opening himself. He can't search his own evacuation too hard without explaining why. Protocol is a wall, but it has to hold him too."

He looked at me with something I hadn't seen from him before — not surprise exactly, but a kind of recalibration, as though he had filed me under one heading and was now, reluctantly, amending the entry. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else since Tuesday lunchtime, which was approximately four hours ago, and I've had four cups of tea in that span, so my thinking is both rapid and vibrating."

And then — this is the part I keep returning to, the part I would write down even if no one made me — he laughed. A short laugh, surprised out of him, quickly suppressed, but real. Ines laughed the way other men coughed: something escaping that the body had been ordered to contain. And I found that I would have worked rather hard to hear it again.

"All right," he said. "Dawn Friday. Which means the copying has to happen tomorrow night, after Grell's inspection, because tonight he'll still have eyes here—" he nodded toward the road "—and because the manifest gets finalized Thursday noon. Anything we put aboard has to be aboard by then, disguised as something boring."

"Boring," I repeated. "Ines, my entire life's work is a ledger of names. There is nothing on earth more boring, thank God, than a nurse's admission book. It's the perfect camouflage. No one has ever voluntarily opened one in the history of medicine."

"Myself excepted."

"You hid inside a linen cupboard behind one for a fortnight. I rest my case."

But the jest ran out of air quickly, as jests did that season, and we stood in the coal-smelling dark while the searchlight swept the channel and the sea made its patient remarks against the wall. Two days. Forty-eight hours in which a man with neat handwriting and a briefcase would decide whether eleven lives remained facts or became rumours.

"Ines," I said at last. "The eleventh name. Private Hollis, in convalescent ward B. If we sail without him—"

"We don't sail without him." It came out flat and immediate, no discussion in it, and I confess it warmed me in a way that was probably unprofessional. "A witness who'll sign is worth more than the whole ledger. Paper accuses. Men testify." He paused. "But he's terrified, and Grell's people have been at him. If we push him wrong, he'll bolt, or worse, he'll go to them."

"Then I'll talk to him. Tomorrow. Before inspection, while the ward's busy — busy wards are blind, everyone's too occupied to notice a nurse having a long chat about the weather." I hesitated. "He trusts nurses. He doesn't trust much else."

Ines nodded slowly, and then did something unexpected: he put out his hand, and after a moment's incomprehension on my part, shook mine, formally, gravely, like a man closing a contract.

"Dawn Friday," he said. "We get the truth on the water, or we don't get it at all."

* * *

That night I could not sleep, which surprised nobody, least of all me. I sat up in the nurses' quarters with my one permitted candle and my ledger open on my knees, reading the eleven names as though they were letters from a friend. Whitcombe. Parr. Osei. The list ran down the page in my own careful hand, each name written twice over — once in the official book where it had been struck, once here where it stood whole — and I thought about what Matron had said. *His version of events.* As though events were dough, to be shaped. As though a man could die or live depending on whose pen touched the form.

Somewhere below, the tide turned. I heard it change its mind against the sea wall, that long low sigh the Channel makes when it reverses, and I thought: the water does this every six hours without being ordered. The water keeps its own timetable and answers to nobody's paperwork. Dawn Friday, the ship would go out on a tide that did not care what Major Grell countersigned, and it seemed to me suddenly, absurdly, that the tide was the only honest officer left on the coast.

Two days. One inspection to survive, one witness to convince, one ledger to duplicate, one ship to board under false colours.

I pinched out the candle and lay in the dark with my heart going like a sewing machine, and I found — this is the strange part; I record it here because it is true — that beneath the fear there was something almost like gratitude. For fourteen months I had believed that history happened elsewhere, to other people, in rooms with maps on the walls. Now the rooms had turned their attention to a field hospital on the Channel coast, and I understood, with the awful steadiness that arrives at three in the morning, that I had been in history all along. I had simply never before been asked to sign it.

Sleep took me near four. It was, all things considered, the best hour's rest of my life.

At seven, Sister Bright woke me with a cup of tea and a face like a dropped pie.

"He's early," she whispered. "Major Grell. His car's at the gate *now*."
