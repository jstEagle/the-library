## 14. Vane's Letter, Whole

The oilskin packet had been in my possession for one hundred and ninety-three days, and until that night I had never opened it all the way.

I want to be honest about why, because honesty is the whole point of what follows. It wasn't fear of discovery — though God knows there was enough of that to float a barge. It wasn't superstition, exactly, either, though soldiers are superstitious creatures and I had come to think of the packet the way a man thinks of a bullet with his name on it: better carried than examined.

It was this. The letter was the last thing Elias Vane ever gave me. As long as it stayed folded, as long as I never read past the first line — *Ines, if you are reading this then you ran, and I am glad* — then some part of me could go on believing he was still alive to have written it. Reading it whole would make it a testament. And testaments are for dead men, and I had already buried Captain Vane once, officially, in ink, in a dispatch I knew to be a lie, and I did not think I had it in me to bury him again in private.

That was the arithmetic of cowardice, and I had gotten very good at it.

But that night — the night after Matron slid the records-room key across her desk like a woman passing contraband chocolate, the night after Astrid came back from the records room pale as laundry with eleven names copied out in her careful hand — that night the arithmetic stopped working. We were going to board a ship. We were going to put our necks into a noose woven from paper, and it seemed suddenly obscene to gamble everything on a letter I hadn't the courage to finish.

So I asked Astrid to sit with me while I read it. Not to hold my hand. Just to sit. There's a difference, and she understood it without my having to explain, which is one of about four hundred things I could say about Astrid Crane and probably will before this is over.

* * *

We used the linen store, because it was the only room in the hospital with a lock that worked from the inside and a smell that didn't remind anyone of blood. Astrid brought a lamp and turned the wick down low, so the flame sat inside its glass like a held breath. Shelves of folded sheets rose around us on every side, white and orderly, the ghosts of a thousand beds waiting to be made.

I sat on an upturned crate. She sat on another crate opposite, knees nearly touching mine, and folded her hands in her lap like a congregation of one.

"You don't have to stay," I said.

"I know," she said. "Read it aloud. If it's evidence, I should hear it as evidence. If it's something else—" she paused, and in the lamplight I saw her decide to be exact, because she was always exact, "—then you shouldn't be alone with it."

The oilskin was stiff with salt and handling. My fingers had learned its folds by heart without my permission. I worked the wrapping open the way you unwrap a wound, and took out the letter — two pages, thin paper, covered front and back in that handwriting I would know anywhere, slanting rightward like a man leaning into wind.

Elias wrote the way he spoke: fast, certain, and only mostly grammatical.

"Ines, if you are reading this then you ran, and I am glad."

I stopped there. I always stopped there. Astrid waited. Down the corridor somewhere a man coughed the deep wet cough of a chest wound, and neither of us moved until it settled.

I read on.

*"You will call it desertion. You will call it cowardice for years, because you are stubborn and because calling yourself a coward is easier than admitting you did the brave thing, which is running. Listen to me. I ordered you to forget, and you refused. Do you understand what that cost? Every man in that room obeyed. Eleven men said yes sir thank you sir and went quiet as graves, and they are graves now, or paperwork pretending to be graves. You were the only one who said nothing at all and walked out, which is the only answer to an order like that that keeps your soul intact."*

My voice cracked on *soul*. I pressed on anyway, because Astrid was watching me with those grey steady eyes and I found I couldn't stop while she watched.

*"They diverted Convoy 14 in March. You saw the manifests. Shell fragments sold forward through Rotterdam, medical stores swapped for ballast, and eleven men who counted the crates and signed their names beside the counts. Grell's arrangement is elegant — that's what makes it dangerous. He doesn't steal quietly. He steals loudly, and then corrects the record, and everyone knows just enough to be afraid. The convoy goes into the books destroyed by shellfire. The men go into the books dead. I go into the books dead too, because I counted the crates as well, and because I would not sign the correction."*

"He's alive," Astrid said softly. Not a question. A kind of confirmation, spoken to the room.

"Keep listening," I said, though my mouth had gone dry as biscuit. "There's more."

*"I am hiding in the one place Grell will never look, which is under his own nose — I won't write where, because paper travels badly. I am alive, Ines. I want that written plainly so you cannot twist it later into grief, the way you twist everything. I am alive, and tired, and angry, and I intend to see this finished."*

A sound came out of me then that I'm not proud of — half laugh, half something else entirely. Alive. Six months of carrying him like a coffin, and he'd been *under Grell's nose*, being stubborn at somebody, presumably.

Astrid reached out and put her hand flat on my knee. Just that. Just weight and warmth. I took a breath that shook on the intake and kept reading.

*"But I may not get the chance. They are close, and getting closer, and if they take me they will not shoot me — shooting makes martyrs and inquiries. They will post me dead again, properly this time, somewhere verifiable. So this letter must do my work if I can't."*

Here his handwriting changed. Slowed. The letters got rounder, more careful, the way they did when he was writing something he meant.

*"To whoever holds this letter — and I know exactly who holds it, because I gave it to him with my own hands, and he took it because taking things and looking after them is what he does — here is what I ask.*"

*"Find the witnesses. Some may still live; Grell's corrections take time and paperwork and paperwork can be beaten by better paperwork. Find someone who keeps records honestly — there is always someone, in every hospital and every depot, some quiet person with a good hand and a suspicious mind, and they are worth more than a regiment.*"

Astrid made a small noise. I looked up. She had both hands pressed over her mouth.

*"Copy everything. Twice. Hide one copy where fire can't follow and carry the other against your body. Then get it off this coast, onto a ship, into a courtroom, in front of eyes that outrank Grell. Names, dates, manifests, the lot. Don't argue the case. Just lay the record down and let it testify, because records are the only witnesses Grell can't reach."*

*"And Ines — finish what I started. Not the counting. Anyone can count. Finish the part I couldn't: the running toward. You ran away once and it was the bravest thing done in my presence that entire year. Now run toward something. Take the truth and run toward the people who need it, and don't stop to feel ashamed, because you have nothing — hear me, nothing — to be ashamed of."*

The last lines were cramped into the margin, written fast, the way he wrote when the adjutant was coming:

*"You thought I chose you as my batman because you polished boots well. I chose you because you were the only man in the mess who watered the cat. That's the whole of it. That's the man you are. E.V."*

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there after the words ran out. A long time. Long enough for the lamp to gutter once when a draught came under the door, long enough for Astrid to move her hand from my knee to the crate beside her and back again, giving me my silence and keeping hers.

Here is what I could not say, and finally could: I had spent six months constructing myself as a coward, and I had built him well. Coward Ines, who deserted. Coward Ines, who left his officer. Coward Ines, who carried a sealed letter across half the coast of France like a man carrying a stone in his mouth, afraid even to chew it.

And Elias — dead Elias, living Elias, impossible Elias — had taken that careful architecture of shame in one paragraph and knocked it flat. *Watered the cat.* Of all the sentences in the world to save a man. I put my face in my hands and laughed, or wept, or did whatever it is when both arrive at once and neither will give way, and Astrid Crane, bless her forever, did not tell me to hush. There were wounded men forty feet away. She simply leaned forward and took the corner of the letter from my fingers so it wouldn't crumple, and let me come apart quietly among the folded sheets.

When I could speak again, what came out was fury, which surprised us both.

"He knew." My voice came out scraped. "He knew about the letter. He knew what it would do. He wrote it *knowing* I'd wait months to open it, knowing I'd torture myself the whole time, and he — he pre-forgave me. He absolved me in advance, in writing, like a receipt. Like he was filing papers." I wiped my face with the heel of my hand. "Who does that? Who *does* that?"

"Someone who knew you very well," Astrid said. "And loved you rather a lot."

"It's high-handed."

"Mm."

"It's *arrogant*. Deciding how I'd feel. Telling me I'm brave as though bravery were a fact on a manifest."

"Yes," she said. "It's also true, every word of it, and you know it is, and the anger is just grief wearing a uniform. I've worn that uniform. It fits badly but it's warm."

I stared at her. Lamplight moved on her face. There was, I realized, not one ounce of pity in her expression — only recognition, the fierce level look of a woman meeting a fellow survivor of the same country, which is the country of people who keep things.

"He's alive," I said, testing the words. "He's actually alive."

"He's alive," she agreed. "And hiding under Major Grell's nose, which means he's either brilliant or insane."

"Both. Always both." A wild, exhausted grin was trying to happen on my face and I let it. "He used to hide the colonel's brandy in the ammunition crate marked EMPTY, on the grounds that no one inspects a lie that obvious."

"And this is the man whose handwriting you've been carrying for six months like a relic."

"This is the man whose handwriting *she's* been striking through in ledgers," I said, and the grin died, because that was the thing, wasn't it. Two of us, keeping vigil over the same name from opposite ends of a war — me with my oilskin, her with her pen — and it had taken a deserter stumbling through a gate in the dark to join the halves up.

Astrid picked up the second page, which I had let fall, and read the final instruction silently, her lips moving. Then she looked up.

"'Finish what he started,'" she quoted. "That's not addressed to you anymore, Ines. It's addressed to whoever holds the letter. At present, that's both of us."

* * *

We came out of the linen store separately, five minutes apart, the way you learn to. But at the door she caught my sleeve.

"The copying starts tomorrow night," she said. "Matron's key, the records room, all of it. If we're doing this — if we're doing *his* errand — then I need to know you'll be there. Not because I doubt you." Precisely because she doubted herself, I understood, and wanted witnesses. "Because a record needs two hands. One to copy, one to check."

"One to run toward," I said, "and one to run with."

Something crossed her face then — quick, unguarded, gone — that I would spend a great many nights afterward trying to name correctly, and would eventually name wrong several times before naming right. She squeezed my sleeve once and was gone down the corridor, footsteps soft on the boards.

I stood a moment longer in the dark with the letter against my chest, inside the shirt, where it had lived so long it had worn smooth against my skin.

Somewhere out on the black water a ship's horn sounded, low and patient, like something waiting to be told a story.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow we start writing it down.
