## 15. What We Owe the Dead Who Aren't

The linen store after lights-out smelled of carbolic and lavender, which struck Astrid as an absurd combination — the hospital's way of fighting death on two fronts and winning at neither. She had wedged a stool against the door, hung her spare shawl over the transom window, and lit the stub of candle she'd been hoarding in her apron pocket for three weeks, saving it the way other people saved ration sugar.

Ines sat on an upturned crate between the shelving and the mangle, knees nearly to his ears, looking like a man who had been folded by hand and never quite unfolded again. He had shaved, she noticed. Someone — Matron, presumably, though neither would ever confirm it — had found him a razor and a clean collar, and the effect was startling. Under six months of mud and fear there was an actual face: young, stubborn, with a chin that argued even when his mouth didn't.

"We need to be systematic," Astrid said, laying her ledger on the wash-board like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"Systematic," Ines repeated, as though tasting it. "Right. I was in the artillery. We were systematic too. The system was: duck."

"I'm serious."

"So am I. Ducking saved my life more often than courage did." But he leaned forward, and she saw that he was paying attention — the particular attention of a man who had carried a letter through hell and wanted very much for it to have been worth carrying.

Astrid opened the ledger to the pages she had marked with pins. Eleven names, each one written twice: once in her careful hand, dated, with ward notes, temperatures, the small human details no one had thought to strike from *her* book because no one had known it existed — and once, in absentia, in the official record's cold strikethrough.

"Private Aldous Reed," she said, touching the first pin. "Admitted the fourteenth of March with a shrapnel wound to the shoulder. Discharged fit. Reported dead the twenty-second of April in official dispatch — 'killed in convoy action.'" She looked up. "I nursed him. He ate four helpings of plum duff and complained about all of them. Dead men don't complain about plum duff, Ines. It's practically their only pleasure."

That startled a laugh out of him — brief, rusty, as if the mechanism hadn't been oiled in months. "You've got a strange way of keeping records, Sister Crane."

"It's not strange. It's accurate. There's a difference, and I'm beginning to think it might get us both shot." She turned the page. "Eleven men. All of them passed through this ward alive. All of them attached, directly or indirectly, to Convoy 9. And all of them now officially deceased, no record, no grave, no next of kin notified. Which means somewhere in France there are eleven families waiting for letters that were never going to come anyway, because—" she stopped.

"Because Vane intercepted the notifications," Ines said quietly. "That was part of it. That's why he wrote the letter. He couldn't stop the men being listed, but he could make sure somebody, someday, knew they weren't dead." He tapped the oilskin packet, resting on his knee like something holy or explosive, which Astrid supposed it was both. "He wrote eleven names in it. Same eleven?"

She compared, running her finger down both columns while the candle guttered. The flame bent toward him, giving his face a warm edge. "Same eleven. Same order, even."

"He kept them in order," Ines said, and his voice cracked slightly on it. "Alphabetical by company. He was tidy about everything. His kit, his boots, his letters. I used to think if the army ever fell apart entirely, Captain Vane would just reorganize it." He swallowed. "I didn't know he was still doing it. Keeping them in order. After."

*After* meant the day Ines had run. Neither of them said so. Astrid reached over and, very deliberately, turned the conversation back to business, because she had learned that kindness sometimes looks like paperwork.

"Here is what we have," she said. "One ledger. One letter naming names. One witness — Private Ellery, bed nine, convalescent, who has agreed to sign a statement provided nobody looks at him sharply. And one hospital ship sailing at dawn on Thursday's tide."

"And Major Grell," Ines said, "who controls every document aboard that ship, and who I'd bet my last button already knows the ledger exists."

"Yes. Him." Astrid pulled a sheet of scrap paper toward her and began to write, because writing things down steadied her the way prayer steadied other women. "So we can't simply walk on board holding evidence. We have to become unremarkable. And you, Ines—"

"I'm remarkable," he said. "It's been remarked upon. Mostly by military police."

"—you are going to be a stretcher case," she finished, ignoring him.

*

The plan, as it unspooled under the candle, was simple in the way that all dangerous plans are simple — a series of small lies laid end to end like stepping stones across a river full of crocodiles.

First: the copying. The original ledger would stay hidden; a duplicate would be made in Astrid's hand, page for page, and the duplicate would travel. "If they take one, they can't take both," she said. "And if they take me—" she said it briskly, the way she read temperatures, "—then the copy goes on without me."

"No," Ines said.

"It wasn't a question."

"It doesn't have to be a question to get an answer." He stood, which in the low-ceilinged store meant hunching, and paced the length of two shelves. "You don't understand what it costs, being the one who runs. I do. I'm the world's leading expert. You carry those pages off that ship and you spend the rest of your life wondering if you should've stayed, and believe me, Sister, that wondering eats a man from the boots upward."

"And if I stay and they burn the ledger and shoot the witness and bury Convoy 9 so deep no historian ever finds it?" She kept her voice level. "Then I'll have wondered too, but I'll have wondered in the wrong direction. There's a right direction to wonder in, Ines. It's toward the thing you should have done."

He stopped pacing. For a moment the only sound was the sea, far off, working at the harbour wall with its patient patient hands.

"You're going to be difficult about this," he said at last.

"I'm going to be *systematic* about this," she said, and had the satisfaction of seeing him laugh again despite himself.

Second: transport chits. This was Ines's contribution, and he was, she had to admit, rather magnificent at it. He produced from inside his boot a collection of forms — requisitions, movement orders, a medical pass — collected over months of not-quite-being-caught, and spread them out like a card sharp showing his hand.

"Grell's office uses a particular stamp," he said. "Eagle over anchor, inked violet, pressed hard in the top right. I watched them do it forty times before I ran. The trick isn't forging the stamp — I can carve one from a cork in an hour. The trick is the handwriting. Clerks always press harder on the date. They're proud of dates." He demonstrated, tongue between his teeth, copying a clerk's cramped capitals onto a blank chit. "There. Two stretcher cases, priority transfer, authorized by Captain — hmm."

"Captain E. Vane," Astrid suggested, and then put her hand over her mouth, appalled at herself and delighted at herself in equal measure.

Ines stared at her. Then the laugh came properly this time, shaking his shoulders, and he had to muffle it in his sleeve lest it wake the ward above. "Oh, he'd hate that. He'd hate it *politely*. He'd write us both a memorandum." He bent to the work. "Vane it is. Let the dead man sign us aboard. It's the least the living owe him."

Third: Private Ellery. Bed nine, convalescent, twenty-three years old, with a tremor in his hands that had nothing to do with his wound. He would travel as a genuine casualty — which, Astrid argued, he was, just not the kind that showed on an X-ray. A man whose name had appeared on a death list without dying was wounded somewhere the army had no chart for.

"He'll be searched at the gangplank," Ines said.

"Everyone gets searched at the gangplank. But sick men get searched *gently*, and their papers get read quickly, because clerks want their suppers." She made a note. "Ellery signs his statement aboard, in front of witnesses, once we're past the point where Grell can stop the ship. Until then, he's just another poor devil going home."

"And until then," Ines said quietly, "he's bait. If Grell's looking for a runaway witness, a nervous lad on a stretcher is the easiest thing in the world to spot."

Astrid set down her pen. "Then we make him not nervous."

"With what? Courage?"

"With practice. Tomorrow I'll drill him. Name, rank, unit, wound, home town. Over and over, until he can recite it in his sleep. Fear is mostly surprise, Ines. Take away the surprise and you're left with something a person can actually carry." She thought of her mother saying something similar about grief, years ago, in a kitchen smelling of bread, and pushed the thought away before it could slow her hands.

*

The candle was burning low when the plan was done — copied ledger, forged chits, disguised witness, dawn tide — and there came one of those pauses that happens when two people have been talking about enormous things in ordinary words and suddenly hear themselves.

"Do you know what I keep thinking about?" Ines said, into the quiet. "Not Grell. Not the tribunal, not the firing squad they'll presumably line up for us with great efficiency. I keep thinking about Vane's boots. I used to black them every morning. Left, then right, then the heels. He never once checked my work. He just trusted it." His jaw worked. "I ran, and he wrote me a letter saying I was brave. I've read it forty times trying to find where he says I'm a coward, because that's how it feels from the inside. It's not in there. It's just — not in there."

"Ines." Astrid reached across the wash-board and took his hand. It was rougher than she'd expected, and warm, and it held on to hers like a man holding a rope. "Listen to me. You carried his letter through half of France with the whole machinery of the army hunting you. That's not cowardice wearing a disguise. Cowards don't deliver mail."

"To the dead," he said. "I'm delivering mail to a dead man."

"To men who aren't dead," she corrected. "That's rather the entire point."

He laughed, wetly this time, and pressed her hand, and did not let go, and she did not ask him to. The lavender and the carbolic did their strange duet. Somewhere above them a patient coughed twice and was settled.

"I'm afraid," Astrid said. It came out before she had decided to say it — one of those sentences that escapes custody. "Since you were generous enough to go first with the confession, I'll match you. I've spent my whole life believing history happened somewhere else, to braver people, and now it's happened to *me*, in my own ledger, in my own handwriting, and I am terrified. Every night I check under my mattress for my own courage and find it missing."

"Mine's under there too," Ines said. "We seem to have mislaid it jointly."

"What a pair we are. A deserter and a bookkeeper, afraid together in a linen cupboard."

"That's not a linen cupboard," he said, surveying the shelves, the mangle, the stacked sheets like a general reviewing terrain. "This is obviously a strategic command post. Any tribunal that says otherwise is just jealous."

She smiled — really smiled, the kind that ached — and realized, with the mild astonishment of someone discovering a fact long known to everyone else, that she did not want to do any of this with anyone else. Not the copying, not the forging, not the gangplank, not whatever came after. It was not a grand thing. It was not poetry. It was the plain and enormous discovery that his fear answered hers, call and response, and that she felt braver holding his hand than she had ever felt alone with her careful, correct, lonely ledgers.

She did not say it. Some things, said aloud, become orders to be obeyed or refused, and she wanted this to remain what it was: true, unwritten, and entirely off the record.

But perhaps her face said it, because Ines looked at her for a long moment and then said, very softly, "Well. Here we are, then."

"Here we are," she agreed.

He raised her hand — formally, absurdly, like a man dancing at a village hall in peacetime — and pressed it once, and let go, and became practical again, because they had hours of work left and one guttering candle between them and the whole weight of the truth to move before Thursday's tide.

*

They worked until the sky behind the transom window went from black to iron to the pale grey of unmade beds. Astrid copied pages in two hands — her own for the original entries, a plainer clerk's hand for the forged chits — while Ines carved the stamp from a cork with a penknife, swearing gently at the eagle, which came out looking first like a chicken and then like an eagle that had been through a war, which Ines declared was more authentic anyway.

At half past three, footsteps in the corridor made them freeze — then soften, when the steps paused and something was slid under the door without a knock. A key. The records-room key, on a loop of ribbon, with a scrap of paper in Matron's upright hand:

*The chapel Bible needs rebinding. See to it. — M.*

And beneath it, smaller, almost grudging, as if the pen had been embarrassed: *God keeps better records than any of you. Make use of Him.*

Ines read it twice and looked at Astrid. "Is she helping us or threatening us?"

"With Matron," Astrid said, tucking the key into her bodice, "it has never once been necessary to decide."

She snuffed the candle. Through the gap under the shawl-covered window, the first thin light was coming up over the Channel, and with it, distantly, the sound she had been listening for all night and dreading: the boom of the dock guns marking the morning tide, and beneath it, fainter, the grind of lorries turning in through the main gate — earlier than any scheduled convoy, and flying, unless her eyes deceived her in the half-dark, the pennant of the Inspectorate of Records.

Grell was here. Two days early.

Ines was already moving, gathering chits, blowing out what remained of the candle's smoke with the calm of a man who had learned that panic was a luxury billed later. "Sister Crane," he said quietly, "tell me you have a place to hide a cork shaped like a defeated eagle."

"I have," said Astrid, pulling on her apron, "an entire hospital."
