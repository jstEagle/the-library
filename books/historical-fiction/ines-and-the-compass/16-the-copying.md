## 16. The Copying

The night had the particular silence of a building that was pretending to sleep. Somewhere below me a floorboard settled; somewhere above, the wind worried at a loose slate like a tongue worrying a sore tooth. I sat at the little table in the linen store with a candle stub, two pens, an inkpot balanced on a folded towel so it wouldn't ring against the wood if my hand shook — and my ledger open before me, eleven names long and six months deep.

Ines had gone an hour ago, back to whatever corner of the coal cellar he'd made into a home, after pressing his finger to the table's surface as though he could pin the hours in place.

"Copy everything," he'd said. "Even the weather notes. Especially the weather notes."

"Why especially?"

"Because nobody forges weather, Astrid. A court will believe your rain before it believes your handwriting."

It was the sort of thing that sounded wise until you thought about it, and then it sounded wise anyway.

I had decided, somewhere between supper and the changing of the sentries, that one copy of anything important was a tragedy waiting for a match. So: two copies. One to go where no one would ever look voluntarily — the chapel Bible, that great bruised family tome Matron had inherited with the hospital and which hadn't been opened, by the dust on it, since the previous war. And one sewn into the lining of my uniform jacket, against my own ribs, where it would travel as far as my body did.

Two hands, I'd decided, for two copies. If Grell had ever seen my writing — and he *had* looked at my ledgers the way a jeweller looks at paste, hunting for the flaw — I didn't want both sets betraying me with the same slant on the *t*.

So I began.

*Crane, A., Sister. Entry for March the third.* In my own hand first, faithful as a church mouse. Then beneath it, in the other hand — a rounder, clumsier fist I invented out of whole cloth, the hand of a woman who pressed too hard and crossed nothing cleanly — the same words again.

It was slow work, copying a ledger is not dramatic work, and I will confess something here that I have confessed to no one since: around midnight, my mind wandered, and I wrote *Private Aldous Reed, shrapnel, left shoulder, cheerful about it* in the wrong hand, and had to blot it and begin again, and laughed — actually laughed, alone, at one in the morning, in a linen store, with a hanging offense spread out under my nose. Grief and fear do strange things to the humours. So does tiredness. The three together make a kind of giddy soup.

I thought about Private Reed while I worked. Cheerful about it. He'd been struck from the convoy report the way you strike through a word you've written twice. Eleven men. I copied their names until I could have recited them backwards in the dark, which, as it turned out, I would one day be asked to do.

* * *

Sometime after two, the door opened without a knock.

Only two people in France opened doors that way: thieves and Matron, and only one of them would have brought a cup of cocoa. She set it down among my papers with the unhurried precision of a woman laying mines.

"You look," she said, "like a woman copying out lines for misbehaving."

"That is very nearly what I am."

"Mm." She pulled the second chair closer — we kept a second chair in there for sitting on while folding, nothing suspicious in a chair — and lowered herself into it with the creak of joints that had spent thirty years being loyal to other people's secrets. She looked at the pages a long moment. At the Bible, already open on its spine, its gutter yawning.

"I won't ask what it says," she said. "I've found that knowing things is expensive."

"It says eleven men were alive when this hospital finished with them, and dead when the reports did."

Matron nodded slowly, as if I'd told her the tide came in twice a day. "And now you're making it say it twice."

"In case one copy drowns."

"In case one copy is *found*." She took up the cocoa, tasted it, and grimaced at it in the proprietary way of someone who had made it herself. "Sister Crane, I am going to tell you something I don't tell the young nurses, because the young nurses cry. When I was your age — near enough — I kept a private list too."

I stopped writing. "Of what?"

"Bodies. Which ones I'd washed, which ones I'd closed the eyes of, which ones had a wedding ring I'd taped to a card because there was no one to send it to." She said it in the tone other women used for describing a recipe. "Forty-one names by the end of it. I burned it when they transferred me, because I thought it was morbid." Her eyes went to my ledger. "It wasn't morbid. It was the only honest paperwork in three armies, and I'm glad somebody's finally doing it properly."

"Matron—"

"Don't thank me. It makes it solemn, and solemn things get caught." She stood, joints complaining, and took the cocoa with her as evidence that she'd never been there at all. At the door she paused. "If anyone comes asking, you've been asleep since ten. I checked on you myself. I check on everyone. It's in my duties."

"It isn't in your duties."

"No," she agreed, and shut the door softly behind her.

That, more than anything all night, nearly undid me. Not fear — kindness does that, it gets in under the ribs where fear can't reach.

* * *

By four o'clock my right hand had become a stranger to me. The invented hand was easier now, curiously — I had practiced it enough that it flowed while my own hand cramped. There is something in that, I think. The self you invent learns faster than the self you were issued.

I hid the pages as I finished them. The Bible was first: I had prised loose the mull and boards inside the back cover — a desecration I apologized for aloud, twice — and built a shallow bed of paper between the cover and the endpaper, flat as a pressed flower. Forty pages went in. Then the paste, then the weight of the great book itself clamped down on top, and there it was: scripture, apparently, all the way through.

The second set I sewed. My mother taught me hem-stitching when I was nine, on the theory that a girl who couldn't mend was a girl who'd marry badly, and I remember thinking even then that the theory was nonsense but the skill was sound. I turned my uniform jacket inside out under the lamp, laid the folded pages along the left lining where a breast pocket's shadow would disguise any bulk, and stitched them in with small, mean stitches. It took an hour. My fingers bled twice and I licked the blood away and kept going, because the thread was black and the blood wasn't, and even in treason I could not abide a visible mark.

When it was done I put the jacket on over my nightdress and sat in it, feeling the pages rest against me like a second heartbeat. Slightly stiff. Slightly warm. I have carried letters against my person before; everyone has. But this was different. This was not a message from someone to me. This was me, in duplicate.

I want to be honest about how it felt, because people imagine that courage feels like trumpets. It felt like wearing a wet coat. It felt like the certain knowledge that if I was searched — truly searched, not waved through — every stitch my mother taught me would come out under a corporal's knife, and the pages would come out with them, and the last thing I would ever write would have been *Private Aldous Reed, shrapnel, left shoulder, cheerful about it.*

And still I did not take the jacket off. That is the whole of bravery, I've decided. Doing it anyway, with wet sleeves.

* * *

Near dawn, Ines came.

He had a knock of his own, eventually — three soft taps, a rhythm we'd agreed on, though by then I'd have known his breathing through the door. He looked terrible in the way that only the frightened-rested can, slept-in and wound-up at once, eyes too bright.

"Well?" he said.

I handed him the Bible. He weighed it in both hands, frowning. "It's heavier."

"It's fuller."

He thumbed the pages, ran his palm down the spine, held it to the candlelight as if holiness might show through as a shadow. Then he looked up at me with an expression I hadn't seen on him before — something between awe and alarm, the face of a man realizing the parcel in his hands has a pulse.

"You put it *in* it."

"Where else? Nobody has faith anymore. It's the safest building in France."

"Astrid." He set it down carefully, the way you set down eggs. "If they search the ship—"

"They'll search the wounded, and the bags, and the officers twice. No one searches the Bible. Not even Grell." I heard how it sounded even as I said it — a girl insisting her luck would hold. I tried again. "It goes to the chapel in the morning with the other devotional books. Matron carries them herself. After that it's furniture, and furniture sails with the ship."

"And the second set?"

I opened the jacket just enough. He bent close, and I watched his face change as understanding arrived — the exact moment a man realizes that the woman in front of him is wearing her own death warrant, hemmed neatly, in black thread.

"No," he said.

"Yes."

"*No.* Take the Bible copy off the ship. Wear the pages or send the pages, not both. If you're taken—" He stopped, because neither of us liked finishing that sentence. He started another one instead, quieter. "You're staying behind. You told me. You said the tribunal, you said you'd testify. So the pages sail and *you* stay, and if they search you afterward—"

"Then they'll find a jacket with a heavy lining and a woman who doesn't know what they're talking about." I buttoned it up again, calmly, as if buttoning could settle the argument. "Ines. Listen to me. The whole plan rests on one simple fact: they cannot hang what they cannot find, and they cannot unwrite what they cannot seize. Two copies means two chances. One copy means none. I didn't spend a night bleeding onto black thread to halve our odds out of sentiment."

He stared at me. And then, absurdly, he smiled — tired and crooked and real.

"You've changed," he said. "When you hid me in here you were terrified of your own shadow."

"My shadow was doing something wrong then. Now it's doing something right. It's much less frightening once you've chosen it." I gathered the pens, blotted the inkpot, banked the candle. "Besides, you changed me first. You walked through that gate half-dead with a dead man's letter, and I haven't been properly bored since."

"That's not the compliment you think it is."

"It's the only one I have. Take it or leave it."

He took the Bible then, hugged it to his chest like a man carrying someone else's child, and paused at the door.

"Matron knows?" he asked.

"Matron knew before I did. She always does."

"Then God help us, we're in competent hands." He knocked his knuckles gently on the good book, once, as though asking it to witness. "Chapel at eight."

"Chapel at eight."

And he was gone, and I sat alone again with the guttering candle and my one original ledger, and I felt the strangest sensation of my life: lightness. I had spent the night duplicating myself, and now pieces of me were out in the world, walking away in other people's coats and covers, beyond my reach forever. Any sane woman would call that terror. It felt like the opposite. It felt like letting go of something so it could float.

I blew out the candle and got into my cot in my buttoned jacket and slept for ninety minutes, dreamlessly, the best rest I'd had in weeks.

At seven-forty, Matron shook me awake. Her hand was steady but her face was not.

"Sister Crane," she said, "there's an orderly at the gate selling newspapers and something worse. Get up. We've been sold."
