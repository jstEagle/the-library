## 17. Betrayal at the Gate

There is a particular smell to betrayal, I learned that evening, and it is carbolic soap and cheap tobacco.

The orderly's name was Pell. He was a small man with a stoop and a way of hovering at the edge of conversations like a moth deciding whether a lamp was worth it. For three days he had been kind to us — or what passes for kind in a place like that: he brought the eleventh man, Private Whitlock, an extra blanket; he looked the other way when Astrid kept the linen store locked longer than strictly necessary; he once said, in his mild moth-like voice, "Funny weather for this time of year," which in wartime French hospitals is practically a declaration of brotherhood.

I liked him. That is the part I keep returning to. I *liked* him.

He sold us for two things, as it turned out later: a discharge paper with a clean stamp on it, and the promise that his name would not appear anywhere near the inquiry Grell was so obviously assembling. I cannot even hate him properly. I have been the frightened man at the edge of someone else's courage. I know exactly what two things are worth when you are afraid of one thing.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and Astrid says — well. Astrid isn't here to say anything at the moment, which is rather the whole of my problem. Let me start where the evening started, which was with hope, that most dangerous of rations.

* * *

We had a plan by then, Astrid and I, worked out over three nights of lamplight and whispered arithmetic. It was a good plan. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own skull where nobody can strike it through: the plan was *good*. Copy the ledger twice, hide it twice, forge chits for three, put Whitlock between two genuine stretcher cases where a fever made him anonymous, and board the *Dover Castle* at dawn with the tide. Matron had already agreed to carry the Bible. Everything had a shape to it. Everything clicked like a well-oiled buckle.

Whitlock himself had come further than any of us expected. Three days earlier he'd shaken so badly he couldn't hold a pencil; now he could sign his own name without his teeth chattering, provided you gave him a moment first and didn't stand too close. "I keep practicing," he told me that afternoon, in the convalescent ward, holding up the page where he'd written his name eleven times down the margin like a schoolboy doing lines. "In case they want it more than once."

"They'll want it more than once," I said. "They'll want it in triplicate, knowing officers."

He almost smiled. "I was in supplies before all this," he said. "I know how much things cost. That's why they can't have me forgetting." He said it simply, the way men say the truest things, and then he folded his practice page into a neat square and put it in his breast pocket like a talisman, which is exactly what it was.

That should have been my first warning: that Whitlock talked more. That he sat nearer the window. That hope had loosened something in him that fear had kept tight, and loose things rattle, and rattling carries.

Pell was very good at carrying.

* * *

It went wrong at half past seven, though we didn't know it until quarter past eight.

Astrid came to find me behind the laundry boilers, where I had developed the habit of standing in the steam because the steam couldn't ask me questions. She was walking too fast. Astrid walks fast anyway — she has the stride of a woman who has spent years covering wards built by men who never had to walk them — but there is fast, and there is *fast*, and she was writing her will with her feet.

"Grell's aides were here at six," she said. "Not the usual visit. They took the duty roster. All of it."

"Can they do that?"

"They're doing it." She glanced over both shoulders, quick as a wren. "And Pell asked Matron for the evening off. Pell hasn't had an evening off since Arras."

I remember feeling the floor of my stomach open like a trapdoor. You would think, having run from Vane's convoy, having slept in ditches with one ear open, having survived on the particular diet of paranoia that desertion forces on you — you would think I'd be good at spotting the moment the net closes. But the net always closes from the direction you've stopped watching. I'd been watching the gates, the roads, the tide tables. I had not been watching the moths.

"Whitlock," I said.

"Whitlock," she agreed grimly. "Ward Four. If they've got the roster, they know who's in which bed. We move him tonight or not at all."

"The ship's not until dawn."

"The ship is not until dawn," Astrid said, "but the docks are being sealed at nine. Matron heard it from the quartermaster. Officially it's 'movement security.'" She made the phrase sound like something she'd found on the sole of her boot. "Unofficially, Grell wants his witnesses in a barrel with the lid nailed down before anyone can float them out to sea."

Nine o'clock. It was nearly eight. The coal cellar route — our coal cellar route, the one I'd scouted twice and memorized like scripture — came out beyond the incinerator, forty yards of shadow from the wall to the gatehouse, and the gatehouse had sentries, and the sentries after nine would have orders.

Forty yards. One witness who shook. One forged chit each. And a moon coming up like a searchlight with opinions.

"We need a third pair of hands," I said. "Somebody to make noise on the far side of the yard, pull the sentries' eyes—"

"I'll do it."

"You can't—"

"Ines." She took my sleeve, and her voice did the thing it does when she stops arguing and starts simply stating the world as she finds it. "If either of us is caught near Whitlock, we hang together, literally, and the ledger hangs with us. But if I'm caught being *me* — Sister Crane, head nurse, twelve years of service, a spotless record and a temper the command finds tiresome but useful — if I'm caught being me, I'm detained. Questioned. Annoyed. Not hanged. I am worth more to them free-floating than swinging." Her mouth twitched. "Besides, I make an excellent diversion. I've been politely insubordinate to majors for years. It's practically my profession."

"There will be guards," I said. "There will be questions. There may be a cell."

"There will be a tribunal eventually either way," she said. "This way I choose the timing." Then, softer, in a voice I hadn't heard from her before and have never stopped hearing since: "You're the one who carries it, Ines. I'm the one who stays. That's how it's always been going to end. I knew it before you arrived, honestly, I just hadn't met you yet."

I want it noted that I argued. I argued for four whole minutes, which in my experience is a heroic duration for anyone arguing with Astrid Crane. I lost, of course. I suspect I was always going to lose. There is a species of courage that looks, from the outside, exactly like stubbornness wearing its good coat, and she had it in quantities that field hospitals run on.

* * *

Eight-forty. Ward Four.

Getting Whitlock out of his bed was the easy part; he'd been dressed under his blankets for two days, sleeping like a man expecting fire drill. The hard part was the shaking, which came back the moment his boots hit the floor, and the question, which I'll credit him for asking:

"If they take me," he said, "you'll still get the names out? Even without me?"

"Without you there are no names," I said. "There's just gossip. You're the whole case, son. You're the ink."

He nodded, and stood up straighter, and I thought — foolishly, blessedly, for about ninety seconds — that we might actually manage this thing.

The coal cellar stairs are steep and black and smell of the inside of the earth, and Whitlock went down them with my hand on his elbow counting steps aloud like a nursery rhyme, and at the bottom Astrid pressed something into my palm in the dark. I thought it was a key. It was her fountain pen.

"If it comes to choosing," she said, "the pages go and I don't. Promise me properly."

"I don't promise properly things I intend to break," I said.

"That," she said, "is the most romantic thing anyone has said to me in a war," and then she turned and went up the stairs and out into the moonlight to save my life, and God forgive me, I let her.

* * *

What happened next I assembled afterward from pieces, the way you assemble anything you weren't present for but will be haunted by regardless.

Astrid walked into the middle of the parade yard at eight minutes to nine — visible, unhurried, magnificent — and informed the sentries at the gatehouse, in a voice that carried to the docks, that there had been a mix-up in the morphine stores, that Major Grell's signature on the requisition was invalid, and that unless someone fetched the duty officer *immediately* men would die of arithmetic. It was not even entirely a lie, which is the beauty of her. Half the garrison converged to watch Sister Crane dismantle a sergeant. Two lamps swung away from the wall. The sentries' eyes went where sentries' eyes go when a furious nurse is auditing a war.

And in that gap, Whitlock and I crossed forty yards of shadow, over gravel I would swear has personally offended me, past the incinerator, to the drain-grate by the gatehouse — and might have made it, might have *made it*, had the docks not already been sealed.

They were not supposed to be sealed until nine. Whoever arranged it moved early. Pell, I imagine, wanted his paper before the shops shut.

The lorry came out of the dark by the customs shed with its lights hooded, and military police unfolded from it the way they do, unhurried, certain, men who have never once in their lives had to run for anything. Whitlock made a sound I hope never to hear again, halfway between a word and a wound, and grabbed my arm, and I did the arithmetic I had promised myself I wouldn't do: one of us or both of us, the pages or the man, the drain-gap wide enough for one.

You will want me to say I hesitated nobly. I didn't hesitate at all. Whitlock put his hands flat against my chest and pushed — the trembling man, the man who practiced his signature eleven times, pushed me bodily into the dark of the drain culvert — and turned to face them standing up straight, and said, in a voice that barely shook at all, "Private T. Whitlock, supply column. I expect you have paperwork."

Because of course they did. Because Pell had seen to it. And because Whitlock understood — as I did not yet, crouched in filth with my heart trying to exit through my ears — that a witness in their hands is still a witness, and that everything now depended on what he said and didn't say, and that he had chosen, in the last minute available to him, to spend his fear on me.

From the culvert I watched them load him into the lorry. I watched Astrid, fifty yards off, still holding court over the morphine ledger, see the whole of it in a single sideways glance — the empty grate, the closing doors — and I watched her do the bravest stupidest thing I have ever witnessed: she raised her voice, insulted a captain's parentage by way of his filing system, and got herself arrested on the spot, loudly, so that every MP in the yard would be looking at her instead of sweeping the shadows for a deserter.

Guilt inverted, Astrid called it once, laughing, in another lifetime: *the rescuer rescued.* I rescued Whitlock from despair and he rescued me from the lorry and Astrid rescued us both from a distance of fifty yards by getting herself taken to wherever they were taking the rest of us, and I lay in a drainage culvert smelling of everything a drainage culvert smells of, clutching a fountain pen and a set of forged chits for three, and understood that I had never in my life been less alone or more afraid.

* * *

The sirens started at midnight.

By then I was back inside, back through the coal cellar, back in the linen store with its blessed locked door, sitting on a pile of sheets that smelled of someone else's clean future, taking inventory of what remained. The duplicate pages: safe, sewn into my jacket lining where they pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Matron's Bible: gone ahead, thank God, already sitting in a chapel on a dock somewhere, waiting for morning like a patient congregation. The witness: taken. The plan: holed below the waterline but not yet sunk. My heart: filed under *missing, presumed frantic.*

Matron came at ten past twelve. She didn't knock — Matron has never knocked in her life; doors simply admit her out of professional respect. She stood in the doorway with a candle and looked at me sitting among the linens looking, I imagine, like a large guilty ghost, and for once she didn't lecture.

"They've taken Sister Crane to the guardroom," she said. "Not a cell. The *guardroom*. Grell wants her comfortable and frightened, in that order." She set the candle down. "Sailing's still dawn tide. They'll want her questioned before she sails, which means they'll want her questioned *tired*, which means they'll start at four."

"Then I have four hours."

"You have less than that, and no plan, no papers, and one ally," Matron said, "who happens to hold keys to every room in this hospital including, as of tonight, the guardroom corridor." From her apron she produced a ring of them, iron and brass, heavy as a verdict, and held them out. Her hand was steady. Her eyes were not, quite, and I loved her for the difference. "I spent thirty years keeping other people's secrets, young man. I find I've developed a taste for letting them out."

She left me the candle and the keys and one instruction: "Whatever you're planning — plan faster."

So there I sat, in a room full of clean sheets, holding the keys to the one door I should never open, with four hours until they questioned her, three until the docks woke, and one name — hers — newly added to the list of people I intended to drag out of this war alive, whatever it cost, whatever it broke, whatever the tide thought about it.

Somewhere across the yard, a guardroom lamp was burning over Astrid Crane.

I picked up the keys and stood up.

Four hours. Plan faster.
