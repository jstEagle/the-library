## 18. The Tribunal Room

They kept me waiting for two hours and eleven minutes. I know because I counted, and counting is what I do.

The room they chose for it was almost a joke — the old customs house at the top of the harbour road, with a fireplace that hadn't been swept since before the war and a map of the Channel on the wall so faded that the coastline had begun to argue with itself. Someone had dragged in a trestle table and two chairs, arranged like an altar and a penitent's stool. There was even a jug of water and one glass, which I took as a courtesy until I realised it was set on his side only.

I stood at the window while I waited and watched the gulls quarrel over something in the tideline. The tide was going out. It does that whether or not empires are embarrassed, which I have always found restful.

When the door opened I did not turn around straight away. Let him wait for *me*, I thought, for three full seconds, which is the length of courage when you measure it out in sips.

"Sister Crane." Major Grell's voice had not changed. It was the voice of a man reading aloud terms and conditions to someone who has already signed. "Thank you for coming."

"I wasn't given a choice," I said, and turned.

He looked exactly as he had in the ward — grey suit beneath the officer's coat, grey eyes, the small courteous smile of a bank manager about to refuse your loan. He crossed to the table, considered both chairs, and took the one behind it. He gestured to mine as though offering me tea.

"Please."

I sat. My knees were doing something interesting, so I folded my hands over them and pretended they weren't.

"You're wondering why you're here."

"I'm wondering why it took you so long," I said.

Something moved behind his face — not quite surprise. Recalculation. He had come prepared for a frightened nurse, and here instead was a bookkeeper, and bookkeepers frighten men like Grell in an entirely different way.

* * *

He began, as I expected, with the ledger.

"Your records are irregular," he said. "Admissions without corresponding dispatch entries. Names in your hand that appear nowhere in official documentation." He opened a folder — his own ledger, I supposed, though his recorded only what he wished were true. "You will appreciate that in wartime, unofficial documentation constitutes a risk."

"Do you sleep well?" I asked.

"Sister Crane—"

"It's only that I've wondered. Men who strike names from the record. Whether they sleep, or whether they lie awake listening for the sound of pens."

He closed the folder with great care, the way one closes a door on a draught.

Then he changed tactics, and this was where he showed his quality. He became kind.

"You've done remarkable work here," he said. "Matron's reports speak highly of you. Your penmanship alone — do you know how rare it is, to find a woman whose hand is fit for staff work? There's a position at Base Headquarters. Records division. Warm room. A stipend. A future, Sister, which is more than most of us can promise." He leaned forward. "All it requires is precision. Bring me the ledger — the true ledger, the one you keep under the third floorboard of your cot — and bring me the deserter. Do that, and none of this ever touched you."

My heart stopped — not from fear of him knowing about the floorboard, but from the small, absurd vanity of it. All those nights, lying awake listening for boots, and he had known all along. And then, following close behind, the colder thought: if he knew where the ledger was, why was I sitting here being offered tea I hadn't poured?

Because he didn't have it yet. Because the floorboard was empty. Because Ines had it, or Matron did, and Grell's beautiful precise intelligence had arrived one day too late.

I let myself look surprised anyway. One should never waste good acting on a man who expects to see it.

"The third floorboard," I said. "Major. You flatter me. I can't even keep my stockings mended."

"We searched your quarters this morning."

"Then you found my sewing kit, my ration chocolate — which I'll thank you not to eat, I've been saving it — and a very dull diary about the weather." This was true, and it was also the finest lie I had ever told, because the diary *was* about the weather, and I had written every dull word of it for exactly this purpose. "If that constitutes espionage, you'd better arrest the Met Office."

His smile thinned by a millimetre. "Sister Crane. I want to be clear about your position. Harboutring a deserter. Removal of military documents. If pressed — and I am very good at pressing — espionage under the Defence of the Realm Act. The tribunal convenes Friday. You would be wise to think about what your future looks like from the inside of a cell in Rouen."

"I have thought about it," I said. "It has poor light and worse coffee. But then, I've seen your canteen."

* * *

Here is what I had learned in the long hours between his men taking me and this room: fear is a currency, and you may spend it or you may invest it. Grell wanted me to spend mine all at once, in this chair, in a rush of tears and ledgers and names. Every minute I kept it, it earned interest.

So I sat very still and watched him watch me, and I thought about Private Willett, who had died asking me to write his name down properly. I thought about the ten before him. I felt my heart slow to its working pace, the pace of a woman counting stock.

"May I say something, Major?" I asked.

"If it's a confession—"

"It's a correction," I said. "You've made an error in your arithmetic. You believe there are things that happened that no one knows about. You believe a name struck through is a name forgotten." I unfolded my hands. "Would you like to test that?"

He sat back. Warily now. Men like Grell are wary of exactly one thing: the moment the interview stops belonging to them.

"There was Private Alfred Willett," I said. "Of the 2nd Hampshires. Admitted to my ward on the ninth of March with shrapnel in the left thigh. Died on the eleventh, at twenty minutes past four in the morning, asking for his mother, whose address I wrote down and posted myself. Your dispatch lists no such man."

Grell's face did not move. But his thumb, resting on the folder, pressed a little whiter.

"There was Corporal James Enwright. Luggage handler in civilian life, which he told me twice, as though luggage were something to be proud of. Fever, then pneumonia. Buried behind the chapel because there wasn't time for anything grander. Struck through in your convoy report of the twelfth of March."

"There was Private Thomas Quill, who sang while they changed his dressings, badly, and everyone let him. There was Sergeant Denis Fairweather, who gave me his wedding ring to hold and was too dead to ask for it back, so I wear it on a ribbon around my neck, and you may inspect it, Major, if you doubt me. There was Lance-Corporal Peter Hossick. Private Samuel Adey. Gunner Robert Meech. Private Charles Dunwoody, called Dunny, who owed me half a crown and paid it the night before he died, which tells you everything about hope. Private Edward Lamb. Private William Ossler."

Eleven names. I had said them perhaps five hundred times in the dark, walking them like a rosary, and now I laid them out across the trestle table one by one, and I watched.

I watched Alfred Willett land, and Thomas Quill, and Dunny's half-crown. I watched each name arrive somewhere behind Grell's eyes that he had boarded up long ago, and I saw the boarding splinter. Not guilt — I don't believe he has the materials for guilt. Fear. The pure animal fear of a man discovering that memory is a record too, that ink can be burned but not a woman who has said the names aloud every night until they live in her mouth independent of any paper at all.

"You can burn my ledger," I said quietly. "You can burn every copy I ever made. I will still be able to recite them to you in the dark, Major, in order, with their regiments, and I will teach them to whoever asks. That is what a ledger actually is. The paper was only ever the least of it."

The fire cracked in the grate. Somewhere below, the harbour clock struck the quarter-hour, patient as tide.

"Who else has the names?" Grell asked. His voice had lost its varnish.

"That's not how remembering works," I said. "That's the trouble with your method. You think truth is filed. Truth is *carried*. It's walking around in a hundred heads along this coast — orderlies, matrons, chaplains, boys who carried stretchers. You struck eleven men from the record and thereby created eleven hundred witnesses. You didn't bury anything. You planted it."

He stood. He stood too quickly, and his chair scraped, and in that scrape I heard the whole edifice shift — the courteous coldness cracking along some seam I had finally, after weeks of guessing, found.

"This is treason," he said.

"No," I said. "Treason would be striking their names out. This is just arithmetic."

For a long moment neither of us moved. Then he gathered his folder, squared its edges against the table — twice, three times, a small compulsive tenderness toward paper that I confess I recognised — and looked at me with something that was nearly respect, if respect could be distilled into a wish for your destruction.

"Friday," he said. "The tribunal sits Friday. You will have a cell until then, and a great deal of time to consider whether your memory is worth what it will cost you." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, not turning. "The deserter will hang, Sister Crane. Whatever you've done with him. That much I can promise."

"He isn't a deserter," I said to his back. "And neither, when this is finished, will anyone remember that you said he was."

The door closed. The gulls went on outside the window, quarrelling over the tideline, indifferent as history.

I sat alone in the customs house for another hour while a military policeman pretended to guard me and mostly guarded his sandwich. I did not feel triumphant, which surprised me. I felt the way I imagine Ines feels after running — hollowed and lit from inside at once, every nerve spent on the spending.

But underneath the fear, steady as a pulse, was this: Grell had searched my quarters and found nothing. Grell did not have the ledger. Which meant the pages were already moving — in a Bible bound for the chapel, in a uniform lining, in the pocket of a man who had once carried a dead captain's letter through six months of dark — and every hour I sat in this room was an hour they travelled.

Friday, he had said.

I looked at the faded map, at the coastline arguing with itself, and I thought: *he has given me a date.* Men who worship documents cannot help making them, even for their enemies. And dates can be read, and dates can be missed, and somewhere between now and Friday there was a morning tide.

The tide goes out whether or not empires are embarrassed.

Mine went out at dawn.

* * *

They put me in a store-room at the end of the records corridor — not a cell, because officially I was not yet arrested, merely *detained pending clarification*, a phrase Grell's clerks had clearly enjoyed writing. A camp bed, a bucket, one barred window looking down on the yard where the transport lorries were loading for the morning evacuation.

Loading, I noted. Not loading for the ship. Loading *away* from it. He meant to move the patients inland before dawn, every witness aboard the hospital ship replaced by his own paperwork.

Through the wall, the records room hummed with lamplight and typewriters, building Friday's version of the truth.

And then, at some hour past midnight when even the typewriters had gone quiet, I heard three things in sequence: boots in the corridor, the scrape of a key that did not belong to any lock on this floor —

— and Ines's voice, low and urgent through the door, saying my name.
