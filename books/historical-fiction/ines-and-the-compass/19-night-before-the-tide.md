## 19. Night Before the Tide

The key Matron slipped into my hand was warm from her pocket, which struck me as the strangest thing about it. Keys are cold things. They live in locks and iron rings and the pockets of men who want doors shut. This one had been carried against a woman's body for years — through surgeries and shellings and the slow erosion of everything soft in the world — and it came to me still holding her heat, as if she were saying: *I cannot come with you, but part of me will.*

"Third door past the dispensary," she said. Her voice was low and even, the voice I imagined she used to tell orderlies that no, the bleeding had not stopped, and yes, they would hold the lamp steadier. "Guard changes at two. The new man is Private Dunmore, who is nineteen and frightened of Matrons more than Germans, so when he sees me coming he will find something fascinating to study on the far wall. You will have perhaps six minutes."

"Six minutes." My mouth was dry. "And if it takes seven?"

"Then you will be very glad it only took six." She adjusted her cap in the dark corridor, a small vain gesture so human it nearly undid me. "Ines. The tribunal room is at the end of the east wing. There will be a corporal outside Astrid's door."

"And the corporal?"

"Is currently drinking tea laced with rather more laudanum than his prescription allows, because his prescription allows none, and because I told him it would settle his stomach." She permitted herself the ghost of a smile. "I have been a matron for eleven years, Mr. Ashworth. Do you imagine I got through it by asking politely?"

I decided then and there that if I ever wrote anything down — and it seemed increasingly likely that someone would be writing all of this down whether we survived it or not — I would give this woman a better name than the one the Army had issued her. Something with *lion* in it.

She turned to go, then paused. "Her shoes are by the tribunal room door. They took them off her — some regulation about prisoners' laces. She'll not thank you for forgetting them. She has walked half of France in those shoes and considers them an old friend."

* * *

The sirens started as I crossed the courtyard.

They rose out of the dark like something wounded, that long rising wail the coastal stations used for air raids, and every lamp in the compound went brown, shuttered down to slivers. Which was, I reflected, pressing myself against a wall of wet brick, either the worst possible luck or the best — because a blackout meant shadows, and shadows meant I could cross open ground that at any other hour would have had me shot before my third step.

Somewhere over the Channel, engines droned. The war had decided to provide its own music tonight, as though it resented being left out of our little drama.

The corporal outside the tribunal room was exactly where Matron promised, slumped in a chair with his chin on his chest and an empty mug tipping gently in his fingers, dreaming whatever laudanum dreams come to young men with bad stomachs. I stepped over his boots. Astrid's shoes sat neatly beneath the chair beside him, laces tucked inside, and I picked them up with both hands like something holy.

Then I tried the door, and it was locked, and for one black moment the whole night tilted toward ruin.

"Astrid," I whispered. "Astrid Crane."

Silence. Then, muffled, incredulous: "Ines?" A scrape, footsteps, and her voice again, closer, right against the wood. "You absolute fool. How?"

"Matron poisoned the guard and gave me her key."

There was a pause while she absorbed this. "Poisoned is a strong word."

"He's snoring, Astrid."

"That does sound like Matron's work." The lock turned — she had a key of her own, apparently, taken from a hook while her captors weren't attending, because of course she had; I was beginning to understand that Astrid Crane committed small crimes the way other people breathed, without ceremony and without guilt. The door opened and there she was, pale in the thin light, hair coming loose from its pins, wearing the expression of a woman who had spent hours composing arguments for a tribunal and now found the tribunal unnecessary.

I held out the shoes.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you thought of the shoes."

"Matron said you'd not forgive me."

"Matron," said Astrid, sitting to pull them on, "is the finest officer in this entire army and should be running the country." She looked up at me, and something in her face broke and reassembled itself all in the same second. "You came back for me. You could have sailed with the witness. You came back."

"You'd have done the same."

"I'd have done it faster and with less sentiment." But her hand found mine in the dark and stayed there, one heartbeat longer than duty required, and neither of us mentioned it, because some things survive precisely by not being named. "The pages?"

"The linen store, where we hid them. And the Bible set is already gone — Matron took it to the chapel this morning, before Grell's men arrived. It's sitting under a hymnal on God's own shelf, waiting for us like a parcel at a post office."

"Then let's go collect our mail."

* * *

We moved through the hospital's back corridors the way I had learned to move as a batman — heel first, weight low, counting the rhythm of the sirens between steps. Astrid kept pace without instruction, and I understood that she had been doing her own version of quiet movement for months: slipping names into ledgers, sliding files beneath others, an entire clandestine career conducted between the lines of other people's paperwork.

The linen store was exactly as we'd left it, smelling of soap and camphor, the shelves stacked with sheets whiter than anything had a right to be in this century. Astrid went straight to the third shelf, pressed the false panel I'd rigged from a crate lid, and drew out the oilcloth packet — her ledger pages, copied in two hands, the record of eleven men the Army had declared dead while they went on inconveniently breathing.

She weighed it in her palm. "All of it," she murmured. "Every name. Private Whitlock, who cried for his mother and lived. Sergeant Amory, who they listed dead twice, which I consider sloppy work even by their standards."

"There's poetry in that. Dying twice and surviving both times."

"It's not poetry, it's bookkeeping." But she smiled when she said it, and I heard what the smile cost her, and what it defied.

Above us, boots. Not the shuffling of orderlies — the crisp, deliberate tread of military police doing a sweep, and beneath the siren's howl, the unmistakable cadence of more than one pair. Grell had not gone to bed. Grell did not go to bed; men like him considered sleep a form of negligence.

Astrid blew out our lamp. We stood shoulder to shoulder in the dark among the clean sheets, her breath quick against my ear, and I confess that in that moment — hunted, exhausted, sirens screaming overhead like the whole sky filing a complaint — I was happier than I had been in two years. Because I was not alone in the dark anymore, and the person beside me in the dark happened to be the bravest person I had ever met, and neither fact required saying aloud.

The boots passed. Faded. We breathed again.

"Coal cellar," she whispered. "It lets out behind the laundry, and the laundry wall has a gap in it that Corporal Bell uses to visit the girl at the pier café. If Corporal Bell can fit through it, so can we, though I'll deny knowing about it under oath."

"You've thought about testifying."

"I think about everything, Ines. It's my affliction."

* * *

The dock road was chaos in the best possible sense. The raid had scattered everyone's timetables — sentries pulled inland, lorries rerouted, a whole garrison standing in the rain staring uselessly at a sky containing nothing but clouds and rumor. We came through the laundry gap one at a time, Astrid first, tearing her sleeve on the brick and not slowing, and we joined the river of stretchers and kit bags flowing toward the quay where the hospital ship rode at anchor with her lights burning green and merciful.

And there, exactly where the plan said he would be and where I had privately bet my last shilling he would not be, stood the eleventh name.

Private Ellis Hobbes — witness, convalescent, a man who had spent a week signing nothing and shaking over everything — was leaning against a bollard with his kit on his shoulder and an expression on his face I can only describe as astonished virtue.

"Hobbes," I said. "How in God's name—"

"Released." He said it like a man reporting a miracle to his vicar. "This morning. Administrative error, the release paper said. Some clerk had filed my discharge under the wrong regiment and nobody caught it until—" he frowned, working it out even now, "—until a matron came round asking after my paperwork. Very thorough woman. Frightening, really."

Astrid made a sound that might have been a laugh swallowed whole. "Grell will tear that office apart."

"Grell," said Astrid, "will discover that his own system, which worships paperwork above truth, has been beaten by paperwork. There is a justice in that I intend to enjoy for the rest of my life, however long the tribunal permits it to be."

Hobbes looked between us, and then at the ship, and his hands began their trembling again. "They'll check papers at the gangplank. I'm supposed to be in the convalescent ward. If they ask—"

"They won't ask," I said. "Tonight the whole coast is an administrative error. We're going to walk up that plank looking bored, because nobody in the history of armies has ever stopped three bored people with correct posture." I had been a batman long enough to know that the entire British Empire ran on the assumption that anyone who looked like he knew where he was going must be going somewhere sanctioned.

"And if they do ask?" Hobbes asked.

"Then we're very lucky Astrid is carrying forged chits in her uniform lining next to a stolen ledger, and I am carrying nothing but a letter from a dead man, and Hobbes — you are carrying your discharge papers, which are real, which are stamped, and which say you are going where we are going." I clapped his shoulder. "For once in this whole rotten affair, the honest document belongs to us."

The tide was turning. I could feel it in the water slapping the pilings, that patient sideways pull, the sea keeping its own appointments regardless of empires. Somewhere behind us the sirens wound down at last, replaced by the ordinary night noises of a port pretending nothing had happened.

We joined the queue at the gangplank. Ahead of us, a clerk with a clipboard checked papers by lamplight — a thin, tired man who looked as though he had been awake since the previous war. Two ahead. One ahead.

"Ines." Astrid's voice was very quiet. She was watching the far end of the quay, where a staff car had just swung through the gates, its lamps cutting white lanes through the drizzle.

I didn't need to look to know who stepped out of it. Protocol, punctuality, the worship of documents — Major Grell would be at the foot of that plank within ninety seconds, and he would have our descriptions, and the queue moved at the speed of tired clerks.

"Astrid," I said, "when we reach that clerk, keep talking to me. About dressings, about the weather, about anything. Boredom is armor."

"And you?"

"I'll do what I did for Captain Vane for two years. I'll look like a man whose only ambition in life is to carry someone else's bag."

She laughed — actually laughed, low and hopeless and magnificent — and took my arm as the clerk beckoned, and behind us I heard the sharp, unhurried click of major's heels on wet stone, coming on like a clock that believed it owned the morning.
