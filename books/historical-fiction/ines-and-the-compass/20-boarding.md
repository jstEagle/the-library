## 20. Boarding

The docks at half past four in the morning were the colour of wet ash, and they smelled of coal smoke, tar, and the particular institutional despair of army porridge being ladled out somewhere below. Ines stood at the foot of the gangplank with a forged chit in his hand and his heart going like a drum corps, and he found himself thinking — absurdly, unforgivably — about the porridge.

It was nerves. He knew it was nerves. His mind, faced with the alternative subject matter, had simply seized on the safest available thought and was worrying it like a dog with an old slipper. *The porridge is probably why Private Weller looks green,* he told himself. *Nothing to do with the fact that in ninety seconds a clerk is going to look at papers that would not survive a determined squint.*

Weller was the eleventh name. He stood between Astrid and Ines in a borrowed greatcoat two sizes too large, carrying a stretcher he did not need and gripping its handles as though the stretcher might at any moment attempt escape without him. He had been shaking since the coal cellar. He was shaking now in a fine, steady, industrial rhythm, like a small machine committed to its work.

"Weller," Astrid murmured, without moving her lips much. "You are a dysentery case. Dysentery does not require acting. You need only look miserable."

"I can do that," Weller said, in a whisper. "I've been doing that for free since 1914."

"Good man."

The queue ahead of them was short — eleven genuine casualties on stretchers, four walking wounded, two nurses, a chaplain with a crate of hymnals, and one mule who boarded first because the mule outranked everyone by virtue of being the only party not complaining. At the top of the plank, under a hurricane lamp swinging in the wind off the Channel, sat a clerk at a folding table with a ledger of his own.

Ines looked at that ledger and felt something twist in his chest that was almost recognition. Ledgers everywhere this morning. The world, apparently, ran on ledgers, and everything depended on which clerk was holding the pen.

He knew this one. Or rather, he knew *of* him: Corporal Finch, requisitions, a thin stooped man with ink-stained cuffs and the expression of someone who had been cold since November of 1914 and had made his peace with it. The question — the question upon which, God help them, everything now turned — was whether Corporal Finch was the sort of clerk who checked papers, or the sort who checked *boxes*, and whether those two things were the same thing this morning.

Because Grell's men had sealed the docks at midnight. Everyone said so. And yet here was the boarding proceeding, here was the mule ascending, here was a hospital ship breathing steam into the dark like a patient running a fever — because somewhere above them all, orders conflicted, and conflicting orders produced exactly the gap through which a deserter, a nurse, and a dysenteric witness might squeeze if the squeeze was administered correctly.

Matron had administered it. Matron, who could not be seen anywhere near them, whose last words to Astrid had been — according to Astrid — *"The chapel is very quiet at this hour, Nurse, and I find I am not at all sorry about what is kept there."* Ines had decided some weeks ago that he would never fully understand Englishwomen, and had since stopped trying, the way one stops trying to understand the tide.

"Next," said the sergeant at the bottom of the plank, and their little procession moved.

* * *

Astrid counted her own heartbeats the way she counted names: steadily, without sentiment, because sentiment made you miscount.

Against her ribs, bound flat beneath her uniform jacket with two bandages and a quantity of pins that would have horrified Matron on purely practical grounds, lay forty-one pages. Her ledger, copied in two hands over one sleepless night — her own careful bookkeeping script, and beneath each entry, in a smaller line, the truth: *passed through this ward alive. Discharge falsified. Witness to convoy diversion of 14 March.* Forty-one pages against her ribs, rising and falling with her breath, and she had discovered in the last hour that she could feel them, individually, the way one feels a rib. As though the pages had become part of her anatomy. She supposed, in a sense, they had.

She had also, tucked into her sleeve where a sympathetic search might overlook it, a folded paper bearing eleven signatures — ten now, plus Weller's, obtained at three in the morning in the chapel with Matron standing guard at the door and the Almighty, presumably, looking on with interest.

The queue advanced. A stretcher case went up. The chaplain went up, hymnals and all, unchecked, because even the war respected the chaplain's crate.

Then they were at the table.

Corporal Finch looked up. His eyes were pale and patient behind round spectacles fogged at the edges, and they went first to Astrid's papers, then to her face, then back to the papers, with the unhurried thoroughness of a man who had spent his entire war being rushed and had concluded, on reflection, not to be.

"Nurse Crane," Astrid said. "Three orderlies, escorting. Chits attached."

Finch took the chits. He read them. He read them again. Somewhere below them the ship's engines changed pitch, a low note that Astrid felt in her teeth.

Ines, beside her, had gone very still. Astrid did not look at him. She had learned, in the interrogation room, that stillness is contagious and so is panic, and that whichever one you carry determines which one spreads.

"These transport authorisations," Finch said at last, "were issued by Base Records, Section Four." He said it the way a man says *you and I both know Section Four has not issued anything but confusion since August.*

"That's correct," said Astrid.

"Section Four's stamps," Finch continued, turning a chit over as though the reverse might confess something, "changed design twice last month. This is the older stamp."

"The newer stamp," Astrid said pleasantly, "was applied retroactively to documents already issued, per circular 61-C, which I would be happy to produce from memory, having read it four times in the course of my duties." She paused. "Most of us down here read our circulars, Corporal. It passes the time between air raids."

There was a silence with weather in it.

Then Finch set the chits down, took out his own pen, and signed the manifest — and as he signed, quite deliberately and without looking up, he turned the manifest slightly toward himself, so that the angle of the lamp fell across the page and away from their faces, and he said, in the flat voice of a man reciting regulations to nobody in particular:

"Papers in order. Casualty evacuations board amidships. Orderlies return to shore duties on disembarkation of patients only." A beat. "Mind how you go. Sea's rough past the mole."

"Thank you, Corporal," said Astrid.

"Thank *your* section," said Finch, stamping, and the stamp came down on all four chits at once with the finality of a small, bureaucratic thunderclap.

They walked up the plank. Astrid kept her eyes forward and her hand pressed flat against her ribs, where forty-one pages beat in time with her heart, and behind her she heard Weller's stretcher-handles creak and Ines exhale the longest breath of his life, so long that she feared for a moment he might faint before reaching the deck.

* * *

They made it to amidships. They made it past the embarkation officer, who glanced at the chits, glanced at the mule, and visibly decided the mule was the day's actual problem. They made Weller into a bunk among genuine dysentery cases, who welcomed him with the immediate, generous solidarity of men united by a common affliction, and within thirty seconds one of them was telling him a joke involving a general, a goat, and a pontoon bridge, and Weller was laughing — actually laughing — with tears standing in his eyes, and Astrid had to turn away and pretend to check a dressing.

And then, at ten minutes past five, with the mooring lines coming off the bollards one by one and the sky in the east turning the colour of a healing bruise, Ines looked down the length of the deck toward the gangplank and said, in a voice that had dropped several degrees:

"Astrid."

She followed his gaze.

Major Grell stood at the foot of the gangplank.

He was not running — Grell did not run; Grell arrived, the way weather arrives. He stood in his immaculate coat at the base of the lowered plank with two military police flanking him, and even at forty yards Astrid could see the situation assembling itself around him: the plank already drawn halfway up, the deckhands coiling lines, the embarkation officer saluting with the crisp defensiveness of a man who intended to hide inside protocol until the danger passed.

"He's come for us," Ines said. "He knows. Finch— no. No, someone talked. The orderly—"

"Look at him," Astrid said.

Ines looked.

Grell was arguing. That was the extraordinary thing. Major Grell, who commanded rooms the way gravity commands falling objects, was standing at the bottom of a gangplank *arguing*, and losing, and — Astrid watched it dawn slowly across Ines's face — losing precisely, exquisitely, to the thing he loved best.

The embarkation officer was pointing at the tide table. Then at the sailing order. Then at a document Grell had thrust at him, which he was examining with the pained courtesy of a specialist shown an amateur's surgery. His voice carried across the water in fragments:

"—movement authority countersigned at Base, sir, and I've no authority to countermand a countersigned—"

"—these passengers are wanted for questioning—"

"—then you'll want a detention order, sir, countersigned, and I'll want to log the reason for delay, and the delay will be logged against—" a pause, delicately calibrated, "—whoever signs it."

Astrid felt something rise in her chest that she recognised, after a moment's astonishment, as joy.

Protocol. The vast, suffocating, magnificent machinery of military procedure, the same machine that had struck eleven names from eleven files and buried eleven living men in paperwork — that machine worked in both directions. It had always worked in both directions. Grell had simply never imagined anyone would climb inside it after him.

"He can't board without a detention order," she said softly. "And he can't issue one without signing his name to the delay. And if he signs his name to the delay of a hospital ship carrying documented casualties—" she let the sentence hang, unfinished, the way one lets a trap finish closing on its own.

"He'd be writing evidence," Ines breathed. "Against himself. In his own hand."

"In triplicate," said Astrid, with enormous satisfaction. "This is the happiest I have been in four years."

Below, Grell stood motionless at the foot of the plank for one long moment — and Astrid, watching him, saw the exact instant when he understood it too. Saw his head tilt, very slightly, the way it had tilted across the interview table when she had recited the eleven names. He was a man discovering that the instrument he had sharpened all his life had an edge that cut both ways, and that he was holding the wrong end.

He looked up at the deck. Whether he found her face in the grey morning light or not, she could not say. But he raised one hand — not a salute, nothing so human as a salute; a gesture like a man marking a page he intended to return to — and then he turned, coat swinging, and walked back up the dock toward his motorcar, and the two military police followed him like punctuation.

"He'll be waiting at the other end," Ines said quietly. "At port."

"I know," said Astrid.

The last mooring line came off. The ship leaned away from the wall with a groan of timber and a churn of foam, and the gap of black water opened between the hull and the dock — six inches, a foot, three feet — and Astrid stood at the rail with the wind pulling her hair loose and watched England, such as it was, such as it had been, slide slowly astern.

Forty-one pages against her ribs. Eleven signatures in her sleeve. One witness aft, laughing at goat jokes between waves of terror. One corporal at the dockside already entering the sailing in his manifest, unremarkable, permanent, *on the record*.

"You're crying," Ines said. Not accusingly. Almost with wonder.

"It's the wind," said Astrid.

"It's a fairly emotional wind," Ines agreed, and put his arm — briefly, awkwardly, in the manner of a man handling something both precious and explosive — around her shoulders, and together they watched the coast recede.

It was Weller who broke the moment, appearing at Astrid's elbow white-faced, clutching her sleeve with a grip that had nothing to do with dysentery.

"Nurse. Nurse Crane." He swallowed. "That man on the quarterdeck. The one in civilian clothes taking the passenger roll." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "I know him. He visited me twice in the convalescent ward."

Astrid turned. Forward, near the bridge, a thin figure in a grey suit stood with a clipboard, checking names off as the passengers came up from below — and even at this distance, even in the wind, there was something in the set of his shoulders that she had seen before, across an interview table, taking notes in a careful hand.

Not Grell's man.

Grell's *other* man. The one she had never seen leave the room.

"He asked for me by name," Weller whispered. "He called me the eleventh witness."

The ship steamed on into the open Channel, carrying them all — the pages, the names, the witness, the truth — straight toward him.
