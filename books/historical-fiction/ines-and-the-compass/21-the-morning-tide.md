## 21. The Morning Tide

The tide turned at 4:40 in the morning, and the *St. Aldwyn* turned with it, the way a great sleeping animal turns toward a familiar sound.

Astrid felt the motion before she understood it — a slow lean, a shudder through the deck plates, and then the peculiar sensation of the world behind her window beginning to slide sideways. The harbor lights of the coast she had lived on for two years drifted rightward, unhurried, as though the shore itself were being drawn away like a sheet from under dishes that must not be disturbed.

She had expected weeping, or prayer, or at least some ceremonial trembling at the moment of departure. What she got instead was Private Hollis, propped against a bulkhead, complaining about the tea.

"They've boiled it twice," he said darkly. "You can smell it. Boiled tea is an abomination and I'll say so in my statement if they let me."

"You will do no such thing," said Corporal Fenwick, who had claimed the bunk above him and was now climbing down into it like a man entering church late. "Eleven names, Hollis. Eleven men struck out of existence by their own side. And your contribution to history will be *the tea was overdone*."

"History is made of small grievances," Astrid said. "Ask any wife."

It was the kind of joke she would once have swallowed. A year ago — even six months ago — Astrid Crane did not make jokes in front of patients, because Astrid Crane was furniture that could take a pulse. She was beginning to suspect that furniture had been holding its tongue for a very long time, and now that it had started talking, there would be no stopping it.

Below decks, the *St. Aldwyn* smelled of carbolic, wet wool, and engine oil — the perfume of every evacuation, but different this time. Different because wedged between the splinted legs and the bandaged chests were eleven men who were not, strictly speaking, wounded, and one batman deserter who kept touching his jacket pocket where the duplicate pages rode flat against his ribs, the way other men touched cigarettes they were trying not to smoke.

And different because in the corner near the boiler, someone had set up a table.

Matron had arranged it. Of course Matron had arranged it; Matron could arrange weather. A folding table, two chairs, a lamp borrowed from the dispensary, and — Astrid had nearly laughed when she saw it — an actual inkwell, lashed to the table's edge with surgical tape, so that the sea could not commit its own act of erasure.

"On the record," Matron had said, in the tone she used for things that were not suggestions. "Properly witnessed, properly dated, properly signed. If this goes into a courtroom looking like a smuggler's laundry list, Grell will eat it alive and pick his teeth with your pen."

So it fell to Astrid to take the statements, because her hand was careful, because she knew the ledger by heart, and because — as Ines put it with a crooked half-smile — "you've got the sort of handwriting that looks innocent even when it's committing treason."

* * *

They began at five o'clock, while the ship was still clearing the harbor mouth, because no one could sleep anyway.

Private Thomas Hollis went first, having volunteered with the eagerness of a man who had been rehearsing in his head since the coal cellar. He gave his name in full — Thomas Amos Hollis, service number recited like scripture — and his unit, and the date he had seen the convoy turned off the coast road toward a warehouse that appeared on no manifest. Astrid wrote it all down. She read it back to him. He signed beneath it, and Corporal Fenwick signed as witness, and something happened to the air in the room when the pen lifted from the paper.

It took Astrid a moment to name the thing. Then she understood: it was the sound of a name becoming permanent. Twenty months she had spent writing names in a ledger the officers pretended not to see, names that existed only in her small careful hand and nowhere else on God's earth. Now each name landed on paper that would be read, examined, argued over, entered into evidence. Each signature was a small stone laid across a grave that had never held a body — only an omission.

"There," said Hollis, staring at his own signature as though it belonged to a stranger. "That's me. That's actually me." He looked up, and his eyes were bright and slightly ridiculous. "I thought they'd rubbed me out. I've thought that since March. Like a pencil mark."

"No one rubs you out again," Astrid said. "Not while I hold the pen."

Which was a more romantic sentence than she intended, and Fenwick raised one eyebrow at her in a way that suggested she'd be hearing about it later.

They worked through the morning. Ten statements, then eleven — the eleventh belonging to young Whitcombe, the man from the convalescent ward, whose hands shook so badly on the gangplank that Ines had steered him aboard with a grip like a dock cleat. Whitcombe's statement was the longest and the hardest. Twice he stopped, and both times Astrid did not rush him. She had learned that from dying men: silence is also a form of writing. When he finished signing, he sat back and looked at his hands as if they had done something without his permission.

"I keep thinking they're still coming for me," he said quietly.

"They tried the docks last night," Astrid said. "We're at sea. Even Major Grell worships protocol, and protocol ends at the three-mile line."

"Does it?" said Whitcombe.

No one answered him, which was answer enough, and Astrid found herself looking out the porthole at the grey water and doing arithmetic she didn't want to do.

* * *

Ines found her at noon, up on deck, holding the rail with both hands as though the sea might try to take it back.

He looked better than he had any right to. Two days of sleep and ship's rations had filled out the hollows of his face a little; the wind had put color in his cheeks that fear had drained out of them. He came and stood beside her and held the rail too, and for a while they simply watched the coast diminish — a grey pencil line, then a suggestion, then a memory.

"You know what's funny?" Ines said. "I spent eight months running from the army. Sleeping in ditches, eating raw turnips, lying to everyone including myself. And now the army is behind me and all I can think is — what if they need me back there? Isn't that mad? Eight months wanting nothing but to be shut of the place, and now I'm homesick for the very ditch."

"It's not mad," Astrid said. "It's only that hatred and homesickness wear the same coat."

He glanced at her. "Where do you learn things like that?"

"In ledgers. People write the strangest confessions next to their ration counts."

The gentle humor between them had become, over these last weeks, a kind of language — the dialect of two people who had discovered that fear shared aloud becomes almost companionable. She had not planned on Ines. She had planned on being furniture forever: reliable, unnoticed, present at every event and part of none. History happened elsewhere, to other people, to men with rank and women with monuments. She had believed that the way you believe the sun rises — not as a fact you chose, but as the shape of the world.

Then a mud-caked stranger had come through the gate with a dead man's handwriting in his pocket, and somewhere between the linen store and the records room and the coal cellar and the tribunal antechamber, Astrid Crane had walked out of the margins of her own book.

"The last port is Falmouth," Ines said. "Ship coals there tomorrow morning, then straight across. Matron told me. Matron tells me everything now; I think she's decided I'm a project." He hesitated, and she heard the hesitation, and waited, because she had learned that from him too. "I've been thinking about Falmouth."

"Have you."

"When we dock. You could come with me. Or rather — stay on. Come across. The inquiry sits in London, doesn't it? You'd testify, give the ledger over, all of it, and then—" He waved a hand at the horizon, which was currently offering them nothing but cloud. "Then whatever comes after. There's supposed to be an after. People talk about it."

Astrid watched the water for a long moment.

"If I stay aboard," she said, "they'll call me a fugitive. Do you understand that? Grell has already drafted the charges — espionage, they're charmingly grand about it — and if I sail away before answering them, then every word in my ledger becomes the scribbling of a runaway. He won't have to burn the pages. He'll just have to burn me, and the pages will burn with me, quietly, in absentia, in a report nobody reads."

"And if you get off at Falmouth?"

"Then I walk into the tribunal with my head up and the original ledger under my arm and I say: here are the eleven names, and here is my hand that wrote them, and here I stand, on the record, where I should have stood twenty months ago." She turned to look at him. "Ines. They can't erase a woman who walks into the room."

"They can hang a woman who walks into the room."

"They can try," she said. "But they'll have to do it in front of witnesses. That's rather the whole point."

He was quiet. Below them, faint through the deck plates, came the sound of Hollis telling another patient, with immense satisfaction, about the tea.

"You said once," Ines said at last, "that history happens to other people. Do you remember? First night in the linen store, you said it like — like a law of nature. Like gravity."

"I remember."

"And now?"

"Now I think gravity is only a law until somebody builds a machine," Astrid said. "I intend to be a machine. An extremely well-organized one, with good penmanship."

He laughed — the real laugh, the one that started in his chest and surprised him on the way out — and then the laugh failed and what was underneath it showed through, and he reached out and took her hand where it lay on the rail. His hand was warm and chapped and shaking very slightly, and she did not pretend not to notice.

"I ran from everything my whole life," he said. "And you're going to walk straight into the thing I ran from. On purpose. With your name attached." He shook his head slowly. "Captain Vane wrote to me that I was braver than I knew. He was wrong, Astrid. He was writing about you."

"He wasn't wrong about either of us," Astrid said. "He just hadn't met us yet."

* * *

They told Matron at dusk, in her cabin, standing at attention like schoolchildren, which lasted approximately nine seconds before Matron told them to sit down and stop being theatrical.

"You're staying," Matron said to Astrid. It was not a question.

"At Falmouth. I disembark, I surrender to the port authority, I request — formally, in writing — that my tribunal be public." Astrid laid the original ledger on Matron's desk, wrapped in oilcloth. "And I want the request witnessed. By you, if you'll do it. And logged in the ship's record, so that the fact of my intention exists before my feet touch the dock. Let Grell explain why a nurse volunteering for trial needed chasing."

For a moment Astrid thought Matron would refuse — or worse, lecture. Instead Matron picked up the ledger, weighed it in her hands like a loaf at market, and set it down again.

"Twenty years I've kept other people's secrets," she said. "Doctors' mistakes. Officers' vices. One colonel's second family in Boulogne, heaven preserve us. And I always told myself it was discretion. It was really just cowardice with good posture." She uncapped her pen. "Give me the date. I'll write 'Nurse A. Crane voluntarily disembarks to face charges, in possession of documentary evidence, having declined all opportunity of flight.' Signed, E. Marchbanks, Matron. Dated. Witnessed by the ship's master, whom I'll wake for the purpose, because he owes me for the poker incident of 1915 and knows it."

"Matron," Astrid said, "you are—"

"Old enough to know that flattery from subordinates is how wars start," said Matron, already writing. But her hand paused over the page, and her voice, when it came again, was quieter. "You'll testify?"

"Everything. All of it. My ledger, my hand, my name."

"Then listen to an old coward with excellent posture." Matron looked up. "Grell will offer you a bargain. He always does; it's cheaper than trials. He'll dress it up as mercy — cooperation, leniency, a quiet transfer. And the price will be the same every time: some piece of the truth, traded away politely, one clause at a time, until there's nothing left worth trading." She signed with a flourish. "Don't trade clauses, Crane. Names. Give him names. Names are the one currency he cannot counterfeit."

* * *

At Falmouth Roads, the morning came up pewter and rose, and the anchor chain ran out with a sound like the world's longest sigh.

They stood at the rail together, she and Ines, while the coaling lighters put out from the quay. The duplicate pages rode inside his jacket, sewn there overnight by Matron's own needle — "the chapel Bible trick is spent," she'd said, "so we shall try the tailor trick" — and Astrid had the original under her arm in its oilcloth, and between them, on paper, in ink, in signatures, eleven men existed officially for the first time since March.

"London," Ines said. "The inquiry opens in — what, six weeks? Seven?"

"Whenever the historians finish arguing about the seating."

"I'll be in the gallery. Front row. Every day." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "Every single day, Astrid. And when you stand up and say your name, I'll hear it, and so will whoever writes it down, and that's—" His voice caught, and he covered it with a grin. "That's more witnesses than most miracles get."

"Miracles don't have Matrons," Astrid said. "That's how you tell them apart."

The lighter bumped alongside. A bosun called something unintelligible and eternal. Somewhere below, Hollis was explaining the tea situation to the ship's cat.

Astrid picked up her ledger.

"One last thing," Ines said quickly, before she could turn away. "When you give your testimony. When they ask you why — and Grell will ask, he'll make it sound like an accusation — *why did you keep the names?* — what will you tell them?"

Astrid considered it. She had considered it all night, in fact, between copying and not-sleeping, turning the question over like a stone.

"I'll tell them the truth," she said. "That I used to believe history was something that happened to soldiers and statesmen, in big rooms, in other people's handwriting. And then I noticed that the men in my ward were disappearing from the record — not dying, *disappearing* — and I understood that history isn't happening elsewhere after all. It was happening to my patients. It was happening in my ledger. It had been happening to me the whole time." She smiled. "I just hadn't signed my name to it yet."

She went down to the gangway. The petty officer checked her papers, frowned at Matron's notation, and asked whether she was quite certain of her intentions, and Astrid Crane — furniture no longer — said, "Quite certain. Please log the time."

Behind her, at the rail, a man who had once run from everything stood very still and watched her go, keeping her in his sightline the way you keep a flame cupped in wind — and above them both the morning tide ran strong toward England, carrying, in opposite directions, two copies of the same truth.

She did not look back. She was saving that, she told herself, for the tribunal room — where Major Grell would be waiting, courteous and cold, with his bargain already drafted.

Let him read it. She had names.
