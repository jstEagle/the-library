## 22. On the Record

*Ines*

The courtroom in Rouen had once been a granary, and it still smelled faintly of wheat and mice, which Ines found fitting. History, he had learned, was mostly stored in buildings like this — dry places where important things were kept and occasionally nibbled.

He sat in the gallery with his hands folded like a man at church who did not entirely believe in God but respected the architecture. His uniform — a borrowed sergeant's coat two sizes too big, because the inquiry's clerks had insisted he wear *something* official, and nothing in the quartermaster's inventory had been cut for a man who'd spent six months living on stolen turnips — hung off his shoulders like a curtain. He kept tugging it straight.

Beside him sat Corporal Whitlock, the eleventh name, who had been sick twice that morning into his hat and now held the hat at a diplomatic distance from his person.

"Do you think they'll ask me to speak?" Whitlock whispered.

"They'll ask you to sign," Ines said. "You've done your speaking. That was the brave part."

"I was sick in my hat."

"Bravery takes many forms."

At the front of the room, beneath a window through which October light fell in a long dusty column, eleven chairs had been arranged. They were empty. The president of the inquiry, a silver-haired major-general whose mustache seemed to have been borrowed from a larger, more serious man, had insisted on them. "The court will observe," he had said in his opening remarks, "eleven empty chairs. They will remain empty until these proceedings have given each of them back its occupant." It was theatrical, and Ines suspected someone had rehearsed it in front of a mirror, but when the clerk rose and began to read the names aloud — slowly, properly, each one spelled out for the record — the theater stopped mattering.

Private Alfred Dunn. Private Thomas Merrick. Sergeant Joel Abersley.

Ines had carried those names across half of France folded inside an oilskin packet against his chest, and hearing them spoken aloud in a warm room made something in his chest unlock that he had not known was locked. He had spent so long believing that names were things the army could erase — strike through with a neat line and a stamp — that the sound of them, read by a bored clerk in a flat voice, felt like weather changing.

Captain Elias Vane, listed among the witnesses though he could not attend, was alive and hiding in a farmhouse outside Dieppe, growing terrible onions and refusing all invitations to appear in person until the inquiry guaranteed him immunity in writing, twice, in triplicate. He had sent Ines a letter about it. The handwriting was unmistakable.

And then the doors at the back opened, and everyone turned, and Astrid Crane came up the aisle in her nursing sister's uniform with her chin level and her ledger held against her ribs the way Ines had seen her hold it a hundred times — not like a book, but like something breathing.

She did not look at him as she passed. She looked straight ahead, at the empty chairs, and Ines understood: she wasn't walking past him. She was walking toward them.

* * *

*Astrid*

Astrid had imagined this moment perhaps four hundred times, and in none of the imaginings had she tripped slightly on the third step of the witness box, or discovered that the water provided for witnesses came in a carafe with a chip in the lip, or noticed that Major-General Harcourt's mustache moved independently of his mouth, like an animal thinking its own thoughts.

She had expected to be afraid. She was, a little. But underneath the fear was something she recognized from the ward at night: the steady, methodical calm of a task that needed doing and that she, specifically, knew how to do.

"State your name and posting for the record," said the president.

"Astrid Miriam Crane. Nursing Sister, Third Field Hospital, Channel Coast. From March 1917 to February of this year."

"You kept a ledger."

"I kept *the* ledger," she said, and heard a small ripple go through the gallery, and resisted the urge to look for Ines. "The official casualty returns were compiled from dispatch summaries. My ledger was compiled from the men themselves. I asked their names, I wrote down what they told me, and I wrote down what I saw. It is not a complicated method. I recommend it."

Someone in the gallery laughed, briefly and nervously, and was shushed.

The ledger was passed forward. She had not seen the Bible copy since Matron had carried it out of the hospital under her arm one gray morning, announcing to no one in particular that the chapel wanted tidying. Now both copies lay on the evidence table side by side, and it struck Astrid — with a jolt, like a fever breaking — that they existed. Both of them. Out in the world. No longer hers to hide.

"The court observes," said Harcourt, turning the first page with surprising gentleness, "that the opening entry bears a name other than those of the eleven men in question."

"Yes, sir. It bears mine."

"You recorded yourself."

"I recorded everyone who came through that ward, sir. I came through it every day for eleven months. It seemed dishonest to make an exception."

Harcourt looked at her over the page for a long moment. The mustache twitched, possibly in approval.

"Read the entry," he said.

So she read it. Her own voice sounded strange in her ears, saying her own name into a machine that would turn it into typescript, into files, into history. *"Astrid M. Crane, present and accounted for. Witnessed the admission of Private Alfred Dunn, 14 June 1917, 0300 hours. Private Dunn was alive, conscious, and asked me to write his name down properly. I did. No one else appears to have done so since."*

The room was very quiet.

"Major Grell's counsel will wish to cross-examine," Harcourt said, "but I confess I cannot immediately imagine on what grounds."

From the defense table, a thin lawyer with a cold face rose and began asking questions about procedure, and irregularities, and whether a nursing sister was qualified to make entries of a quasi-official nature, and Astrid answered each one smoothly and hated — as she had hated standing before Grell in the tribunal room — how good she had become at this. Lying was not required anymore. But the skill remained, like a scar that ached before rain.

Halfway through the cross-examination, she allowed herself one glance at the gallery. Ines sat there in his enormous borrowed coat, and when their eyes met he did something small and ridiculous with his eyebrows that made her have to press her lips together and pretend a sudden interest in the carafe.

Eleven months ago she had believed that history happened elsewhere, to other people — to generals and ministers and men whose names went onto monuments. She had thought her job was to mop floors adjacent to it.

Now her name was page one.

* * *

*Ines*

The inquiry ran for nine days. Ines attended every one of them, partly out of duty and partly because after months of coal cellars and ditches and running, sitting still in a warm room while the truth got said out loud felt like the most luxurious experience available to a man in Europe.

On the fourth day they read Vane's letter into evidence, and the whole room learned what Ines had learned by lamplight in a linen store: that the captain had lived; that he had seen the convoy diverted and its cargo sold; that he had watched eleven men become casualties on paper so that no witnesses would survive in ink. On the fifth day, the supply requisitions were produced, and Major Grell's neat, courteous hand appeared throughout the margins, making corrections. Corrections. The word itself became a small obscenity over the course of that week, repeated so often by counsel that it lost all meaning and then regained a worse one.

Grell himself did not testify. He had been arrested in Calais attempting to board a neutral vessel with three trunks of documents and one trunk of personal effects, which the newspapers found delicious. Ines read the account to Whitlock twice, savoring especially the detail that Grell had protested his arrest on procedural grounds for a full hour, citing regulations to the military police in the manner of a man drowning who insists the water follow proper channels.

"He worshipped protocol," Astrid said, when Ines reported this to her. "It never occurred to him that protocol might someday stand between him and something he wanted. It stood between him and us at the foot of that gangplank. I've been grateful to a set of regulations exactly once in my life, and that was the time."

They had taken to taking their supper together in the small garden behind the witnesses' lodging house, because the weather held unseasonably kind and because neither of them quite knew how to stop being near the other now that no one was shooting at anyone. There was a great deal neither of them knew how to do next, Ines had realized. Running he understood. Hiding he understood. But the war had ended the way wars do — messily, officially, with bells — and the world kept insisting they walk around in it like free people, and he kept waiting for someone to check his papers.

"You're doing it again," Astrid said, on the sixth evening, watching him tug at his sleeve.

"Doing what?"

"Straightening that coat. It doesn't fit. It's never going to fit. You look like a boy wearing his father's funeral clothes."

"I look official," Ines said. "There's a difference."

"There is indeed, and you are on the wrong side of it."

He grinned, and then, because the light was golden and the fear was finally, finally draining out of him like water out of a bath, he heard himself say: "When this is finished — the inquiry, the statements, all of it — I don't entirely know what I'll do. I've been a batman, a deserter, and briefly a courier of historical documents. None of it prepares a fellow for peacetime."

"Neither does nursing, if it helps. I've been offered a position at a hospital in London. A proper one, with electricity and a matron who apparently only shouts on alternate Thursdays." She turned her cup in her hands. "I've also been offered something stranger. A publisher wrote to me. Through the inquiry solicitors, of all things. He wants — " she laughed, a short embarrassed sound — "he wants the ledger. Published. With annotations. He used the word 'memoir.' I told him I was a bookkeeper, not an author."

"What did he say?"

"He said that was precisely the qualification required. That history is mostly kept by people like us, and rarely written by them, and he'd like to correct the imbalance." She glanced up. "I haven't answered him."

"Why not?"

"Because writing it down means deciding it happened," she said simply. "To me. And I've only just gotten used to that."

Ines considered this. Then, with the courage of a man who had once escaped through a coal cellar and figured he had some to spare: "For what it's worth, I'd read it. And I know the ending."

"You do?"

"It ends with your name on page one."

* * *

*Astrid*

On the ninth day, the inquiry handed down its findings. Eleven names restored to the rolls. Eleven families to be notified, formally and correctly, some of them for the second time — the first notices having been lies. Captain Elias Vane cleared of desertion by association and of everything else by paperwork. Major Grell referred for court-martial, which everyone privately agreed would be brief, grim, and conducted with impeccable protocol.

The empty chairs stayed empty, as Harcourt had promised. But after the reading, one by one, men from the gallery — survivors, witnesses, convalescents who had traveled at their own expense to be present — came forward and laid on each chair a photograph, or a medal, or in Whitlock's case, for reasons he declined to explain, a hat.

Astrid stood at the back with Ines and watched, and found she was crying without any particular distress about it, the way rain falls on a day that needs it.

"Well," said Ines, when the room had mostly emptied and the clerks were stacking paper with the contented industry of men who had, for once, done a job worth the doing. "That's that."

"That's that," she agreed. Then she took the ledger from the evidence table — the original, returned to her custody with a receipt, because even justice requires bookkeeping — and opened it to the first page, and looked at her own name written there in her careful hand nearly a year ago, back when recording herself had been a private joke between her and the truth.

"Ines," she said. "About that publisher's letter."

"Yes?"

"I answered it this morning. Before we came here." She closed the ledger and held it against her ribs, where it belonged. "I told him yes. But I warned him the manuscript has two hands in it. Mine for the entries, and — well. Someone has to carry the pages. You've had practice."

Ines stared at her. Then he laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the first fully honest sound she'd ever heard him make — and offered her his arm with the exaggerated formality of a man who had spent six months being nobody, escorting a woman who had spent her whole life believing she was no one, out of a courtroom where both of them were now, permanently and officially, on the record.

Outside, the tide was coming in.
