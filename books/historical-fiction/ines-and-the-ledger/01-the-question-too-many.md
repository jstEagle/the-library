## 1. The Question Too Many

The census taker arrived on a Tuesday, which was the day the Hart house smelled of beeswax and boiled linen and the particular exhaustion of a woman who had spoken to four hundred people in three days. Rosalind Vasquez answered the door herself because Marta had gone to the market and because answering doors was, like everything else in her life, a thing she did without being seen doing it.

He was a narrow man with a leather satchel and a pencil worn to a stub, the kind of man whose whole authority lived in the officialness of his forms. He had a card pinned to his lapel — *U.S. Census, 1900* — though the enumeration had begun in June and it was now October, and Rosalind noticed this the way she noticed everything: quickly, silently, and with no intention of saying so.

"Mrs. Adeline Hart?" he asked.

"Miss Hart. She is resting. I keep the house." This was true in the way that half-truths are true; she kept more than the house, but he did not need the architecture of her days.

"Then perhaps you can answer for the household." He opened his book on the porch rail, balancing it against the wind coming off Fifth Avenue, and began. Names, ages, birthplaces. Adeline Hart, forty-six, born Albany, occupation — here he paused, pencil hovering — "What do I put for a lady who runs a movement?"

"You might put *reformer*," Rosalind said. "She would prefer that to *none*."

He wrote it. He wrote *servant* when he came to Marta, and *chauffeur* when he came to Rosalind, and the word went down onto the page as easily as ink goes into paper, and Rosalind watched it land there and felt nothing, or told herself she felt nothing, which by now amounted to nearly the same discipline.

It was the last question that undid her.

He had finished the household. He had counted the rooms, noted the value of the property, recorded that there were no boarders, no lodgers, no persons absent who ought to be counted present. He wetted his pencil stub between his lips, an unselfconscious little gesture, and then looked up at her with the mild, terrible attention of a man who is paid to be thorough.

"And your parents," he said. "Where were they born?"

The question was ordinary. That was what made it dangerous. She had rehearsed answers to extraordinary questions all her life — questions from magistrates' clerks, from reporters, from drunk men at rallies who wanted to know if the lady driver could really handle a motorcar — and she had no rehearsal left over for the ordinary ones.

"My mother was born in San Juan," she said. "Puerto Rico."

"And your father?"

The wind moved the maple at the curb. Somewhere up the avenue a trolley bell rang twice, impatient. Rosalind stood very still inside herself, the way one stands still when a floorboard has begun to creak under weight it was never meant to hold.

"He was born in New York," she said.

"Name?"

"Vasquez."

"Same as yours, then. Husband?"

"No husband. My mother never married." She let the sentence sit there, plain as a stone on a table, daring him to make of it what he would. Women said such things every day to census men and were written down as *widow* or *single* or, worse, nothing at all, just a dash where a life should have been.

But the taker was not a cruel man, only a curious one, and curiosity in a man with a form is more dangerous than cruelty. He tilted his head. "And he living? Your father?"

"I couldn't say."

"You couldn't say." He repeated it not mockingly but slowly, tasting the shape of the evasion, and Rosalind understood with a cold, clarifying certainty that she had made a mistake. The correct answer — the answer of a woman with nothing behind her eyes — was *he's dead*, delivered flat, already forgotten before the pencil touched paper. *I couldn't say* was a door left open six inches. Men like this pushed on doors.

"It's only for the record, miss," he said gently. "Father's name, for the family column. If you're a Vasquez and he's a Vasquez—"

"The household record is complete," Rosalind said. Her voice came out level, and she was grateful to it, grateful the way one is grateful to a horse that does not spook. "You have everyone you need."

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he closed the book, and the small sound of the cover meeting the pages was like a verdict deferred rather than dismissed. "I may have to come back," he said. "There's a supplement for — certain cases. Persons whose particulars don't fit the columns."

"Nothing about me doesn't fit the columns," Rosalind said. "Chauffeur. Twenty-four years old. Born New York. Father Vasquez, born New York. Mother, San Juan. It all fits."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly, stepping down off the porch. "It fits beautifully. That's sometimes how I know to look closer."

He tipped his hat and went down the steps, and Rosalind watched him go until he turned the corner, and then she stood on the porch of the house where she was invisible, holding the door handle, feeling for the first time in nine years the full weight of the door — its wood, its iron, its insistence on being either open or shut.

* * *

She had been fourteen when she first learned the trick of being unreadable, and she had learned it the way most people learn survival: by necessity, quickly, and from someone who loved her enough to teach her hard things.

Her mother had been a seamstress in the Vasquez house on West Twenty-Second Street — not a servant, exactly, the judge had been careful about that word even while paying her wages like one — and Rosalind had grown up in the back rooms of that house, learning French verbs through the wall from the legitimate daughter's tutor, learning which staircases creaked, learning that her father's study door opened for her only on Thursdays, between four and five, when Mrs. Vasquez took her constitutional and the household held its breath.

Judge Edmund Vasquez had been, in those Thursday hours, neither warm nor cold. He had checked her sums. He had lent her books — Blackstone abridged, then unabridged, then Burke, then Wollstonecraft, which had scandalized him so much he'd pretended it hadn't. He had called her *child* and once, memorably, catastrophically, *my dear*, and had gone gray at his own mouth and never repeated it. He had taught her, without ever meaning to, the central law of her existence: that she was real everywhere except on paper.

Her mother had taught her the rest. *You read everything and repeat nothing,* Elena Vasquez used to say, hemming dresses worth a month of her wages, the needle flashing. *A girl nobody can read is a girl nobody can use.* And when the fever took her in the winter of '93, her last instruction had been practical as always: the trunk in the cellar, the letters she'd kept, the address of Adeline Hart, whom she had met once at a reform meeting and trusted the way the poor trust the rare rich woman who means it.

Adeline had hired her within the week. Not as a maid — Adeline didn't believe in hiding capable women in kitchens — but as driver, secretary-of-the-road, keeper of the automobile that donors called Adeline's extravagance and Adeline called the future. And Rosalind had driven her to rallies in Yonkers and lectures in Boston and jailhouse visits in Albany, and somewhere across those six years she had stopped being a girl with a secret and become a secret herself: seamless, functional, unreadable.

Until a man with a pencil stub had asked the one question too many.

* * *

At four o'clock she brought Adeline her tea in the study, which was less a study than a war office — pamphlets in drifts, a telegraph wire installed at ruinous expense, three chairs that were never all empty at once. Adeline sat with her boots on the fender of the cold fireplace, reading a letter with the expression she wore for bad news from good allies.

"Your census man came," Rosalind said, setting the tray down.

"Mm. Did he count you properly? He counted me as a *reformer* — no, wait, that was you. You spoke to him?" Adeline glanced up, amused. "You never speak to officials."

"He needed the household particulars. Marta was out."

"And you gave them." Adeline returned to her letter. "Good. I hate the way they look through servants, as though counting heads of cattle. Did he ask impertinent questions?"

Rosalind poured the tea. The stream of it was steady. Her hands were steady. Nine years of practice lived in her hands.

"He asked my father's name."

The page in Adeline's hands did not turn. It was a small thing, a page not turning, and Rosalind saw it the way she saw everything, and hated that she saw it, and catalogued it anyway, because cataloguing was what she was for.

"And what did you tell him?"

"Vasquez. Born New York." A pause measured to the width of a hair. "That's all he required."

"That's all anyone requires," Adeline said lightly, and turned the page after all, and the moment passed over the room like weather.

But Rosalind carried it upstairs with her that night, to the small room under the eaves where she slept above the carriage house, and lay awake under the skylight with the city murmuring below, turning the moment over. The page that didn't turn. *That's all anyone requires.* Adeline knew something. Adeline had known something for years, probably — had known since the day Elena Vasquez's trunk arrived with a letter of introduction and a daughter attached — and had chosen, with the exquisite courtesy that was both her greatness and her flaw, never to ask.

Everyone in Rosalind's life had decided not to ask. It was the one gift they all agreed to give her: her silence, respected, preserved, entombed. And tonight, for the first time, the silence felt less like a gift than like a coffin lid, fitted carefully by loving hands, closed over a woman still breathing.

Below the window, the motorcar sat in the dark like a sleeping animal she alone could wake. On the desk lay the evening's mail she had sorted — she always sorted it first, a habit Adeline had never questioned and never examined, and why would she examine it, when examining it would mean asking what else the quiet girl noticed — and at the top of the pile, franked with a law firm's seal she did not recognize, was a thick envelope addressed in a clerk's careful hand:

*Regarding the Estate of the Hon. Edmund Vasquez, deceased.*

Rosalind looked at it for a long time.

Then she did what she had always done. She picked it up, and she read it.
