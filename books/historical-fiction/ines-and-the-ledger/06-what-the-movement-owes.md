## 6. What the Movement Owes

The ledger room of the New York Woman Suffrage Association occupied what had once been a linen closet in the headquarters building on East Twenty-Third Street, and it retained the shelves for linens, which was convenient, because Nellie Pruitt kept her ledgers folded and stacked like tablecloths, sorted by year, by committee, and by degree of embarrassment.

Nobody else knew about the third category. That was rather the point of it.

She had held the post of clerk to the Treasurer for eleven months now, having been hired on the strength of a copperplate hand, a father who kept a grocery on Ninth Avenue and had taught her sums before he taught her prayers, and a reference from a churchwarden who described her, in the phrase that got her the job, as *discreet past the point of virtue*. She was twenty-three years old, small and gray-eyed and unremarkable in the way that made men explain things in front of her as though she were a chair, and she had taken the position because she believed in the cause with the whole fierce simplicity of a girl who had watched her mother work sixteen-hour days for wages her father called *pin money* out of politeness and *theft* out of hearing of the landlord.

Eleven months later, Nellie Pruitt still believed in the cause. It was the books she had begun to doubt.

The trouble was that the books did not add up in a particular direction. A grocer's daughter learned early that error is a weather—it blows every way at random. But these errors blew one way only, steadily, like a trade wind. Money came in and its origin blurred. Money went out and its destination sharpened. And always, underneath everything, like bedrock under topsoil, there was the Judge.

* * *

She had noticed him first in February, in the dull season after the Albany convention, when she was reconciling the fourth-quarter subscriptions against the deposit slips from the Merchants' Union Bank. The Association's income came in thimbles: dues of fifty cents from schoolteachers, a dollar from widows, five dollars at Christmas from Mrs. Latham of Brooklyn, who paid every year and always in postage stamps, which the bank would not accept and which the Treasurer, Mr. Whitcomb, instructed Nellie to record as cash received and then redeem himself, at cost, which meant that somewhere in the margins of every annual report there lived a small fiction about postage stamps, harmless enough, the sort of lie a book could survive.

But that February, running the deposits against the subscription roll, Nellie found that more than half the Association's money had not come in thimbles at all. It had come in barrels. And the barrels traced back, through two committees and a building fund and something called the Press Bureau, to a single account at a single bank, drawn under a single name that appeared nowhere in the annual report.

*E. Vasquez.*

Judge Edmund Vasquez. She knew the name; everyone did. He sat on the bench of the Court of General Sessions, and he was spoken of at headquarters the way one speaks of a wealthy uncle with whom one is not entirely on terms—with gratitude, with carefulness, never with curiosity. His checks arrived quarterly, in envelopes without letters. The first time Nellie asked Mr. Whitcomb how the Judge wished his contributions acknowledged—for the Association published a donor list, tiers of it, gold and silver and bronze, down to Mrs. Latham and her stamps—Whitcomb had looked at her over his spectacles with an expression she would come to know very well.

"The Judge prefers anonymity," he said. "He has family considerations."

"What family considerations?"

"The kind," said Whitcomb, "that are none of ours. Enter it as miscellaneous."

So she entered it as miscellaneous. And then, being the sort of person she was—the sort who reconciled things—she began, in a slim notebook she bought with her own money and kept locked in the linen shelving behind the 1897 correspondence, to enter it again, properly, for herself.

* * *

By March she had traced the shape of it, and the shape astonished her.

It was not simply that Judge Vasquez gave generously. It was that Judge Vasquez *was* the Association—not its soul, which belonged to Mrs. Adeline Hart and the women who stood on platforms and endured thrown fruit with their chins lifted, but its body, its bones, its rent. The headquarters itself, the handsome narrow building with the brass plate and the assembly room on the second floor where Mrs. Hart spoke on Thursday evenings: the deed, which Nellie pulled from the city records on her lunch hour under pretense of an errand, named as owner a concern called the Twenty-Third Street Holding Company, whose sole officers, per the certificate filed at the county clerk's, were E. Vasquez and a lawyer named Hale. The Association rented the building from the Holding Company for a nominal sum—a dollar a year—which meant that the rent line in the budget, the line that made the whole enterprise appear self-sustaining, respectable, solvent, was itself a gift wearing a costume.

Then there was the newspaper. *The Citizen*, the movement's weekly, four pages and a stubborn editorial voice, which the report described as owned outright by the Publishing Committee. The Publishing Committee had purchased the press three years ago for six thousand dollars, a sum raised, per the minutes, "by special effort among friends of the cause." Nellie found the purchase check in the March file. It was drawn on the Merchants' Union. It was signed *E. Vasquez*, and attached to it, in the way of papers that have been shuffled quickly and filed anyway, was a memorandum in Hale's neat legal hand: *Per instruction, consideration to be shown on the books as subscription surplus. E.V. does not wish his connection with the paper to appear. Family considerations.*

There were those words again. Family considerations. By April Nellie had written them in her private notebook so many times that the phrase had begun to feel less like an explanation and more like a door, kept shut, with someone standing in front of it.

She was not a snooping woman by nature. She wants that understood, whoever comes after her to read this—and Nellie Pruitt, alone in the linen closet with the gas turned low, sometimes felt certain that someone would come after her, because she had read enough history to know that every concealed thing conceals a reader yet unborn. She had not set out to uncover anything. She had set out only to keep books that balanced, and the books refused to balance honestly unless she followed the money to its source, and the source, followed far enough, led always back to the same quiet man on the bench, writing checks in envelopes without letters.

What she could not decide, in the spring of 1899, was whether the Judge's money was the cause's salvation or its hostage.

* * *

The rot, when she finally put a name to it, was not in the gifts. It was in the architecture built on top of them.

Consider, as she considered, sitting at her desk on a Saturday in May when the office was empty and the street noise below had gone soft with heat: consider what would happen if the true state of affairs were examined. Not gossiped about—examined. Subpoenaed, say, or reported, or merely inquired into by some honest journalist with patience and a pencil.

The annual reports would fail first, and fail ugly. They presented the Association as funded by its members—twelve hundred women, most of them poor, all of them proud of their fifty-cent shares. In truth the members' dues covered rather less than a fifth of expenses. The rest was the Judge, laundered through the Holding Company, the Publishing Committee, the Lecture Fund (which had received, in January, four hundred dollars from an anonymous *friend of equality*, drawn on—you had only to hold the check to the window light, for the bank's watermark showed through cheap paper—Merchants' Union), and a dozen smaller channels that Hale had constructed with the delicacy of a plumber running pipes behind plaster. Each individual pipe was defensible, perhaps. Together they formed a system whose entire purpose was to let the Association say, publicly and sincerely, *we stand on our own feet*, while standing, actually, on the Judge.

And the Judge's own money—there Nellie's notebook grew darker, though she wrote in it sparingly, because some entries frightened her even in her own handwriting. There was the matter of the six thousand for the press, which was clean enough on its face. But there was also a withdrawal of nine hundred dollars in November of 1897, entered in the Merchants' Union records she had seen during her lunch-hour visit as payable to *E.V.—special*, with no payee endorsed, which was irregular in a way that made her father's ghost rise up in her chest and say *never, never do that, Nellie, a check with no payee is a check nobody can follow, and a check nobody can follow is a check somebody does not want followed.* And there was the odd relationship between the Judge's account and a certain contractor named Brannigan, whose firm had done repairs on a courthouse annex in '96, and whose wife, according to a society item Nellie clipped and then burned, had been seen driving in Judge Vasquez's carriage in circumstances the item chose not to specify, and who had received, two months after the annex was completed, a payment of eleven hundred dollars *from* the Judge rather than *to* him, entered, so far as Nellie could reconstruct it, under no description whatsoever.

She was not so naive as to think she understood what that meant. But she was not so foolish as to think it meant nothing. Money that moves backward, away from the man who owes it, is either charity or hush, and judges of the Court of General Sessions do not generally practice charity in secret installments of eleven hundred dollars.

If any of it surfaced—if a subpoena ever touched the Merchants' Union, if a rival paper ever pulled the Holding Company's certificate the way Nellie had on her lunch hour—the damage would not fall on the Judge. Judges are armored. It would fall here, on East Twenty-Third Street, on Mrs. Hart and her twelve hundred women, and the headline wrote itself in Nellie's head with horrible ease: *SUFFRAGISTS FUNDED BY MACHINE JUDGE'S Tainted MONEY.* And beneath it, in smaller type, the obituary of every argument the movement had ever made about the cleansing virtue of women's hands in public life.

That was the rot, she thought, turning the gas lower as the afternoon dimmed. Not theft. Not quite. The Judge, so far as she could tell, stole nothing; he *lent*, and lent invisibly, and demanded in return only that the Association remain ignorant of its own spine. The rot was dependence dressed as independence. A movement that could not tell the truth about its own money would someday, under sufficient pressure, find it could not tell other truths either—that was how rot worked, she had seen it in a barrel of apples her father had once been persuaded to buy at a good price: sound on the outside, one bad core at the center, and by August the whole barrel gone.

And there was no way to fix it. That was the discovery that had cost her two nights' sleep in May. She had drafted, twice, a memorandum to Mr. Whitcomb recommending full disclosure, restructuring, publication of the Judge's gifts under his own name with his permission or without it. Both times she had reached the final paragraph and stopped, because the final paragraph required her to write the sentence *and if the Judge withdraws his support, the Association closes within the quarter,* and she could not prove otherwise. Run the numbers as she might—she ran them four times—the arithmetic was merciless. Without Vasquez money: no headquarters by summer. No *Citizen* by autumn. No campaign, no conventions, no Thursday evenings with Mrs. Hart's steady voice lifting two hundred tired women back onto their feet. The cause herself, the great slow ship of it, holed below the waterline by nothing more dramatic than honesty.

Mr. Whitcomb, she suspected, had run the same numbers long ago. That was why his reports were works of gentle fiction, why his answer to every question was *enter it as miscellaneous*, why he had hired a clerk described as discreet past the point of virtue. He was not a wicked man. She had watched him weep, quietly, into his collar, reading the casualty lists from the Philippines aloud to the office one morning. He was a man keeping a patient alive by any means at hand, and the means happened to be a rich man's veins, and he had made his peace with the arrangement years before Nellie Pruitt was born.

But peace, her father liked to say, is not the same as balance. And the books did not balance. They only *appeared* to balance, which in the long run was worse, because appearance is a debt too, and debts compound.

* * *

In June the envelopes began arriving without return addresses.

The first contained a newspaper clipping about Judge Vasquez's court, nothing remarkable, and Nellie might have filed it as a courtesy had someone not circled, in red pencil, a passing reference to *the Judge's household*. The second, a week later, contained a page torn from a city directory, with an address on West Forty-Sixth Street marked. The third contained no enclosure at all—only a blank sheet, folded around a single line in pencil: *Who audits your benefactor?*

She burned that one in the stove and told no one, and then sat a long while in the linen closet with her private notebook open on her knee, at the page where she had copied out the November 1897 withdrawal, the nine hundred dollars, *E.V.—special*, no payee.

Because here was the thing she had not written anywhere, the thing she now made herself look at straight: the questions were finding the Association from outside, but they were not random. Someone was following the same pipes through the plaster that she had followed. Someone was pulling certificates and holding checks to the light. And whoever it was had begun, with those penciled lines, to knock—politely, patiently, the way one knocks on a door before opening it—to ask whether the woman who kept the books was going to be an obstacle or a witness.

She looked at the notebook. Eleven months of reconciliation, of tracing, of names and sums and dates in copperplate, all of it evidence of exactly the kind her father had taught her mattered: contemporaneous, itemized, in the hand of a person with no stake beyond the truth of the arithmetic. She thought of Mrs. Hart on the platform, chin lifted. She thought of the twelve hundred fifty-cent shares. She thought of the barrel of apples.

Then she heard Mr. Whitcomb's key in the outer door, earlier than usual, and she closed the notebook, slid it behind the 1897 correspondence, and arranged her face into the mild competence of a clerk who has spent the morning doing nothing anyone need fear.

Whitcomb came in holding a letter, and he was not mild, and he was not competent-looking; he was pale. He stood in the doorway of the linen closet a moment as though he had forgotten why he'd come.

"Miss Pruitt," he said. "Has anyone from the probate courts been here? Asking questions? About—" he hesitated, and she watched him choose the words, and she understood, with a cold settling feeling, that the door behind which someone had been standing all spring had just swung open. "About the Judge's affairs?"

"No, sir," said Nellie, in her best discreet-past-the-point-of-virtue voice. "Not yet."

Whitcomb nodded, thanked her, and withdrew, and Nellie Pruitt sat very still in the smell of old linen and lamp oil, listening to his footsteps wear a groove in the corridor, and thought: *not yet.*

Someone was coming to audit the benefactor. And when they came, they would find the books exactly as sound as appearances required, and exactly as rotten as she knew them to be—and they would find her name signed at the bottom of every page.
