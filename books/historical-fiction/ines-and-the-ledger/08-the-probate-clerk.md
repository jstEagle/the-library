## 8. The Probate Clerk

The summons came on a Tuesday, a cream-colored card with an embossed seal that Rosalind read three times in the motor's shadow before she let herself understand what it was offering her: a legitimate reason to walk into the Hall of Records and stand among strangers without explanation.

*JURY DUTY — REPORT DECEMBER 14, 9 O'CLOCK.*

She had not been summoned. She had arranged it—had written to the commissioner's office in a careful schoolroom hand, signing herself *R. Iversen*, asking whether a person of her situation might volunteer for the panel, as her employer spoke often of women's civic obligations and Miss Hart believed even those who could not vote ought to learn how the machinery turned. The reply had come within four days, which was fast enough to suggest someone upstairs had found the request amusing, or useful, or both.

It didn't matter. What mattered was that at half past eight on the fourteenth of December, Rosalind Iversen stood on the courthouse steps in her second-best coat with Adeline's blessing—"Go, learn something, and don't let them put you on a jury about horses; they always try"—and the great doors opened onto marble and echoing cold.

She had been inside courthouses before, but never like this: not as furniture, not standing against a wall holding someone's coat, not invisible in the way she had spent eleven years making herself. Today she had a card with a number on it. Today a man in uniform pointed down a corridor and said, "Jurors, that way," and looked through her exactly as he looked through everyone else, which felt so extravagant she nearly laughed.

*They can't see you unless you decide to be seen,* her mother used to say, back when the words were comfort rather than strategy. *Remember that, Rosita. It works both ways.*

She sat where the bailiff told her to sit, on a bench of varnished oak that smelled of lemon oil and wet wool, and she did not look at the signs on the doors along the gallery the way a stranger would have. She had memorized the layout from a fire-inspection diagram printed in the *Tribune* two years ago. Surrogate's Court: third door, east side. Probate filings: Room 214. She let her eyes rest on the other jurors—a fat man reading racing forms, two women who did not speak to each other, a boy hardly older than twenty picking at his cuff—and she waited with the patience of someone who had spent years waiting in motors while great matters were decided above her head.

By ten o'clock they had lost thirty of their number to a courtroom upstairs. By eleven, the rest were told there would be no more panels until afternoon, and the bailiff waved them toward lunch with the weary indifference of a man shooing pigeons.

Rosalind did not go to lunch.

She climbed the stairs to the second floor slowly, one hand on the iron rail, letting her breath find the rhythm she used for difficult deliveries—Adeline's hardest speeches, the nights when hecklers threw things. Room 214's door stood open. Inside, under a gasolier that hissed faintly, rows of ledgers rose to the ceiling in iron cages, and behind a waist-high counter a clerk was stamping documents with the metronomic boredom of a man who had done nothing else for thirty years.

He was perhaps sixty, gray at the temples, spectacles pushed up into thinning hair. His waistcoat had been good once. When he saw her he set down his stamp and looked at her properly—not the way men usually looked at a woman alone in a public building, with either appetite or suspicion, but the way a doctor looks at a patient who has walked in carrying symptoms too obvious to mention.

"Help you, miss?"

"I'm looking for—" She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it in the motor, in the bath, in the dark of her narrow room above the carriage house. And now, with the words queued and ready, she heard herself abandon all of them. "I'm looking for the estate of Judge Edmund Vasquez."

The stamp stayed where he had set it. Something moved behind the spectacles—not surprise, precisely. Recognition of a kind. As if she were the fourth such question that season, and the only one asked by a woman whose voice did not shake.

"Probate's not public session," he said carefully. "Filings are a matter of record, but the hearings—"

"I don't want a hearing." She came to the counter and laid her gloved hand flat upon it, and was distantly amazed that it lay still. "I want to know when the door closes."

The clerk took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and put them back on, and during all of this he studied her face with an attention that made her want to step backward out of the lamplight. Eleven years of being unreadable, and she could feel herself being read.

"You'd better come around the side," he said at last. "There's a stool back here, out of the traffic. You look like you've been standing in the cold since September."

*Since the census,* she almost said. Since a mild-faced man with a leather satchel had asked, in the parlor of the Hart house, with Adeline's teacup cooling three feet away: *And your relation to the head of household? Father's name? Father's occupation?*

She came around the counter. He pulled out the stool himself, an odd courtesy, and then he did something stranger: he crossed the room, glanced into the corridor, and shut the door partway, leaving them in the greenish gaslight among the ledger cages like conspirators in a novel.

"My name is Whitcomb," he said. "Thirty-one years in this room. I'll tell you what I told the lawyers, which is nothing, and then I'll tell you what I'm going to tell you anyway, because my conscience has been bothering me since October and my wife says I talk in my sleep." He folded his hands. "You're the Vasquez girl."

Not a question. Rosalind's throat closed, opened. "I'm Rosalind Iversen."

"Mm." Whitcomb nodded slowly, as though she had confirmed rather than denied. "That's what I thought. Sit, miss. It's a long story and my feet hurt."

* * *

He told it plainly, the way workingmen tell things, without ornament: In October, the firm of Hale & Priestly had filed papers on the Vasquez estate—Judge Vasquez, dead eight months in his house on Gramercy Park, mourned in the newspapers, eulogized by half the bench. Straightforward probate, except for one unusual motion filed alongside it: an order authorizing inquiry into *undocumented heirs*. Hale's junior associates had been coming in twice a week since, pulling land records, church registries, passenger manifests. Ships' lists, once. Birth records from St. Vincent's parish going back forty years.

"They're looking for somebody," Whitcomb said. "Or making sure nobody's looking for them. With lawyers it's generally both."

"Filings close—" She couldn't make her voice casual.

"January second." He said it gently, the date laid on the counter between them like a coin. "Claims of interest must be entered by close of business January second, nineteen hundred. After that, distribution proceeds as filed. The will names one heir. Claire Vasquez, daughter of the late judge and his wife, presently residing abroad." He paused. "Coming home, I hear. Booked passage for the twentieth."

The gasolier hissed. Somewhere below, a gavel knocked twice, muffled, like a knuckle on a distant door.

"And if someone else—" Rosalind began, and stopped, because finishing the sentence aloud in this room felt like stepping off a roof.

"If someone else claimed to be his daughter?" Whitcomb removed his spectacles again. He seemed to do it when he needed to say hard things; the bare eyes were kinder than the magnified ones. "Then that person had better bring paper, witness, and a lawyer, in that order, and pray the lawyer is a good one. Because Mr. Hale is very good, miss, and very thorough, and his whole task is to make the court believe no such person exists. Or that if she exists, she isn't who she says. A servant girl turns up after eight months dead claiming the judge's blood? He'll have a dozen affidavits by lunch. Character witnesses. Handwriting experts. He'll say forgery, and gold-digging, and worse, and some of it will stick no matter how true the claim is, because that's what money is *for* in a courtroom. It buys the version of events."

He let that sit. Then, quieter: "I watched him do it to a widow over a Brooklyn lot in 'ninety-three. Decent woman. True claim. Took him nine minutes to make her sound like a confidence trickster, and she had papers."

Rosalind looked at the ledgers rising in their cages—the thousands of bound volumes, each one a person reduced to entries, deeds and deaths and judgments, the whole city's memory stacked floor to ceiling. Her mother's trunk in the carriage-house loft held a ledger in that same species of binding. She had known it for eleven days now. Eleven days of carrying it the way one carries a coal: warm, heavy, burning through every layer.

"The line acknowledging her," she said. "In his own hand. Would that—"

"Would it establish paternity?" Whitcomb shook his head before she finished. "It would *begin*. It would give a lawyer something to build on. But a single line in a private book? Hale would call it sentiment, or forgery, and he'd smile while he did it. No, miss. If it were me—and mind, I'm only a clerk, I file things, I don't advise—" He looked at the door, then back. "If it were me, I wouldn't come with one document. I'd come with a hundred. Corroboration. Dates that match dates. Names that appear in three places instead of one. I'd come with so much paper the truth of it couldn't be argued, only counted." A dry almost-smile. "Courts respect volume. It's the nearest thing we have to faith."

*So much paper.* Rosalind thought of the letters. Four years of letters, read and reread and committed to memory in the blue hour before dawn—the judge's correspondence with the movement's treasurers, the sums, the dates, the little coded civilities between powerful men. She had thought her habit of reading was a sin, or at best a survival skill. Sitting on a courthouse stool under the hissing gas, she felt the ground shift under the thought. It might be neither sin nor skill. It might be evidence.

"Miss." Whitcomb's voice had dropped. "One thing more, and then we never had this conversation. There was a fellow in here last month. Census Bureau badge, but I've seen real badges. Asking after households connected to the Vasquez name. Where servants had gone, that sort of thing." He met her eyes. "Lawyers don't hire census men out of curiosity. They hire them to find people before the people find the courtroom. Whoever he's looking for—he wants to reach her before January second. Understand?"

Her skin went cold in the warm room. The mild face in the parlor. *Father's occupation.* He hadn't been filling out a form. He had been confirming a theory.

"I understand," Rosalind said.

* * *

She left by the main doors, though Whitcomb showed her a side stair, because hiding felt suddenly more dangerous than being seen. The afternoon had turned to sleet; the steps ran silver; the horses on Chambers Street stamped and steamed. She stood a long moment at the top of the stairs with her number-card still in her glove—she had never been called, had never served, had walked into the building a juror and walked out something else entirely—and did the arithmetic she could not stop doing.

December fourteenth today. Eighteen days. Claire Vasquez arriving on the twentieth. The census man somewhere in the city, paid by the estate, working backward from ship manifests and parish registers toward a woman who drove a motor car for the most famous suffragist in New York.

Eighteen days to become, from nothing, a person with standing: paper, witnesses, a lawyer. She had no lawyer. She had no money beyond her wages. She had a forged summons in her pocket and a stolen ledger in a trunk and four years of other people's secrets in her head.

And she had, as of this morning, one more thing she had not had yesterday: the knowledge that Adeline's mail contained, somewhere in its careful folds, the answer to whether the woman she loved had known all along.

Rosalind descended the courthouse steps into the sleet, and turned her face toward Ninth Avenue, where the Hart motor waited, and where tonight, when the house slept, there would be letters to read.
