## 11. The Other Daughter

Claire Vasquez-Hollis had dressed for the crossing the way other women dressed for funerals: with a precision intended to outlast grief. Her gray traveling suit was Parisian, her gloves kid leather, her hat pinned at an angle that suggested she had opinions about the wind and expected it to defer. She stood at the rail of the *City of Berlin* as the harbor opened before her—Statue of Liberty to starboard, the smoke of Manhattan smeared across a December sky—and permitted herself one long breath of American air, which tasted of coal and salt and money.

Three thousand miles she had come, from the tall shuttered house in Newport and the taller silence of her mother's sickroom, and she had rehearsed on the whole Atlantic what she would permit herself to feel.

Her father was dead eleven weeks. She had wept at the funeral exactly as much as was becoming—two handkerchiefs' worth, no more—and had spent the intervening time doing what Edmund Vasquez had raised her to do, which was to read a situation before she read a newspaper. The situation was this: the Judge's estate, vast and intricately mortgaged and threaded through with obligations he had never spoken aloud, was entering probate under lawyers who smelled of pomade and panic. And somewhere in New York, in some drawer or strongbox or lawyer's office, there existed papers that could unmake not only the estate but the family name itself.

She knew this because her father, in the last winter of his life, had told her so.

*You are my heir,* he had said, his voice already thinned by the ailment nobody would name. *And an heir is a custodian, Claire. Not a beneficiary. Custodians clean.*

She had asked what needed cleaning. He had looked at her with those hooded judicial eyes—the eyes she saw now whenever she closed hers, patient as a courtroom waiting for a verdict—and said only: *The ledger. If it surfaces, burn it. If you cannot burn it, buy it. If you cannot buy it, bury the person who holds it under so much doubt that no jury will ever lift them out again.*

He had not said what was in the ledger. He had not needed to. Claire had grown up in a house where certain doors stayed shut and certain conversations stopped when she entered, and she had learned early that the shape of a secret is legible from its outline. Whatever her father had bought, and with whose money, and to hush whose scandal—it was all written down in his small clerkly hand, in a book that had vanished along with a woman named Delphine Marchand and, presumably, with whatever child that woman had carried into the world.

The census taker's report lay in her stateroom bag, folded twice. Hale & Pruitt, the estate's attorneys, had hired the man off the books—a Mr. Elbridge Foyle, diligent, underpaid, and entirely for sale in either direction. His letter was three pages of careful observation, but one line had made Claire set down her coffee:

*The Hart household employs a driver, given name Rosalind, approximately twenty-six years of age, complexion consistent with Spanish or mixed descent, who answered questions regarding her parentage with evident evasion. I attach significance.*

Evident evasion. Claire smiled at the gulls wheeling past the rail. Somewhere ahead of her, in a city of four million strangers, there was a young woman driving an automobile full of suffragettes and carrying, quite possibly, her father's eyes.

*A sister,* Claire thought, and turned the word over like a coin of doubtful mint. She had wanted siblings once, desperately, in the years when her mother's headaches filled the Newport house with laudanum-scented quiet. She had prayed for a brother to share the weight of being Edmund Vasquez's daughter. She had never imagined the sibling would arrive as a rumor in a hired investigator's handwriting, wearing chauffeur's gloves.

Well. Family was family. And family, in Claire's experience, was the first thing a competent custodian cleaned.

* * *

The Holland House received her with the deference due to a name and a well-cut coat. She took a suite on the third floor, tipped the bellboy enough to ensure his discretion rather than his gratitude, and sent down two telegrams before she unwound her veil. The first went to Hale & Pruitt confirming her arrival and requesting a meeting Monday morning. The second went to a private address in Gramercy Park, to a Mrs. Fenwick, née Kessler, who had been her mother's maid thirty years ago and who remembered everything that had ever happened inside the Vasquez houses—including, if Claire's information was sound, the name of the woman her father had kept in a brownstone on West Thirty-Seventh Street in the year 1873.

Mrs. Fenwick came within the hour, old and upright as a churchwarden, smelling of violet cachous. She sat on the edge of the chaise with her hands folded and looked at Claire with an expression of sad appraisal.

"You have his way of sitting," she said. "Straight, like you're about to rule against somebody."

"Mrs. Fenwick. Thank you for coming."

"I didn't come for you." The old woman's mouth tightened. "I came because I'm eighty-one and I'd like to die light. Ask your questions."

Claire asked them. She asked about Delphine Marchand; Mrs. Fenwick confirmed the name without flinching, which told Claire the old woman had already been interviewed by someone—Hale's people, or perhaps the census man. She asked about the child. A girl, born in the spring of '73, christened quietly at St. Joseph's, named Rosalind. She asked whether the Judge had acknowledged the child.

"He paid for the roof over her head until her mother died," Mrs. Fenwick said. "After that—" She paused, and something moved behind her eyes, some recalculation of loyalty across forty years. "After that, I don't know. He wrote letters. Whether he signed them is another matter."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean, Miss Claire, that your father did a great deal of things with a pen he wasn't willing to do with his face."

Claire let that sit. Then, gently: "There's a ledger."

Mrs. Fenwick's hands did not move, but they became very still, the stillness of prey. "I wouldn't know anything about ledgers."

"Of course not." Claire rose, crossed to the writing desk, and returned with an envelope that she set on the chaise between them. "I'm not here to punish anyone for the past. I'm here to close it. If you remember where a woman like Delphine Marchand would keep her papers—in a trunk, with a friend, with a church—you'll send me a note. That's all. No testimony, nothing in any record. Just a note."

The old woman looked at the envelope for a long moment. "Your father offered money too," she said at last. "In '74, when your mother began to suspect. He sent me to offer Delphine five hundred dollars to go west and change her name." Her chin lifted. "Delphine sent it back. Every dollar. She said she wasn't selling the truth, she was just keeping it quiet, and there was a difference."

"There usually is," Claire said. "The difference is usually price."

Mrs. Fenwick left without taking the envelope. Claire noted it—sent it back, every dollar—and filed the fact away with the others. It meant the mother had been proud. Pride was exploitable; prideful people made predictable mistakes. But it also meant the daughter might be proud, and daughters of proud dead mothers did not sell cheaply, and might not sell at all.

She stood at the window a while after the door closed, watching dusk come down over Sixth Avenue, the gas lamps coming up one by one like a slow vote being counted. Then she rang for hot water, changed into a dinner dress the color of claret, and went down to do what she had crossed an ocean to do: begin.

* * *

She found Adeline Hart's headquarters the way she found everything—by asking the right person the right question in the right tone of sympathetic interest. The right person, in this case, was a journalist named Miss Tolliver whom Claire had known vaguely in London circles, and the question was framed as sisterly concern: *I've inherited such troubling correspondence from Papa, and I understand Mrs. Hart's organization knew him—I thought I should pay my respects, learn how his philanthropy was regarded.* By Thursday noon she had an address on East Twenty-Third and a plausible reason to call.

The headquarters occupied the second floor of a former piano warehouse, and it roared. Claire stepped out of the elevator into a storm of activity—women at folding tables addressing envelopes in fast round hands, a mimeograph clacking like a loom, a map of New York State pinned with ribbons marking counties where petitions circulated. Someone thrust a leaflet into her hand; someone else apologized for the disorder in the accent of Vassar. Claire smiled her way through all of it, a warm and slightly overwhelmed heiress, admiring the map, admiring the leaflet, admiring everything, missing nothing.

What she noted: the carpet was worn through at the entrance. What she also noted: the stationery was heavy, the postage abundant, the mimeograph new, and a framed photograph of a fine brick building on Nassau Street hung near the inner office with a small card beneath it—*Our First Press, Purchased Through the Generosity of Friends, 1893*. Claire looked at the date a moment longer than courtesy required.

1893. Her father had been at the height of his bench then, wealthy from the settlement of her grandmother's estate, generous in ways he never explained. *Purchased through the generosity of friends.* Her father had been many things to many institutions, but a friend to women's suffrage—he had ruled against their petitioners with a consistency she could recite. Unless, of course, the friendship had been purchased backward, a payment dressed as a gift, a sin laundered into a cause.

*Oh, Papa,* she thought, with something that was almost admiration. *You hid it in plain sight and called it virtue.*

"Miss Hollis?" A secretary appeared, crisp and apologetic. "Mrs. Hart can give you ten minutes. She's preparing for Tuesday."

Tuesday. Some rally, some hearing; the movement ran on Tuesdays the way mills ran on steam. Claire followed the secretary down a corridor lined with portraits of stern-faced women and was shown into an office where Adeline Hart stood behind a desk stacked with galley proofs.

Adeline Hart was sixty, silver-haired, with the kind of face that had been handsome by refusing to be pretty, and she measured Claire in one glance with the frankness of a woman who had been measuring hostile men for thirty years.

"Miss Hollis. You're Edmund Vasquez's daughter."

"I am." Claire extended her hand and let her smile carry the correct proportions of grief and courage. "I know Papa's politics weren't yours, Mrs. Hart. That's precisely why I came. I found correspondence suggesting he supported your work—quietly—and I confess it puzzled me. But it also moved me. Perhaps he was more complicated than the newspapers allowed. Perhaps—" she let her voice catch, expertly, "—perhaps I didn't know him as well as I should have."

Something flickered across Adeline Hart's face. Not sympathy. Recognition—the look of a chess player seeing a familiar opening. But she covered it, because she was Adeline Hart, and gestured to a chair.

"Judge Vasquez believed in law," she said carefully. "We disagreed about what the law ought to be. As for donations—" a pause, smooth as silk drawn over a wound, "—our contributors prefer anonymity. Many of them. It protects them from exactly the sort of scrutiny you can imagine."

"Of course." Claire sat, crossed her ankles, arranged her gloved hands. "Then I won't pry. I only hoped to see the work. Papa always said a woman should understand where money goes, even money she wishes hadn't gone there."

It was at that moment that the outer door opened and the driver came in.

Claire saw her in profile first—a young woman in a motoring coat, cheeks pink from cold, carrying a stack of mail sacks with the ease of someone who had done it a thousand times—and then the young woman turned to hang the sacks, and Claire felt the room tilt very slightly on its axis.

It was the mouth. Their father's mouth, narrow and precise even at rest, the lips that pressed together before delivering sentence. It was the brow too, the same deliberate arch, and the same watchfulness in the way the eyes moved before the head turned—as though the world were a document to be read in order. Twenty-six years old, Foyle had written. Claire counted backward without meaning to. Born spring of 1873. Conceived in the summer of '72, when her own mother had been at Saratoga taking the waters and her father had been trying a long dull fraud case on West Thirty-Seventh Street.

*Rosalind.* The name arrived in Claire's mind fully formed, and with it a sensation she had not prepared for—not hatred, not even anger, but a strange vertiginous grief, as if she had walked into a stranger's house and found her own childhood hanging on the walls, lived by someone else.

She had a moment—one breath—to decide what to be. She chose, as she always chose, to be charming.

"You must be the famous driver," Claire said warmly, rising, extending her hand. "Miss Tolliver told me the movement runs on your wheels. Claire Hollis. I'm afraid I'm descended from one of your employer's opponents—the late Judge Vasquez."

She watched the girl take her hand. Watched the name land. There was a fractional pause—no longer than politeness, but Claire had been raised at tables where a half-second's hesitation cost a fortune—and then the dark eyes came up, level and unreadable, and Rosalind said:

"Miss Hollis. My condolences. I'm Rosalind—Rosalind Reyes. I drive for Mrs. Hart."

*Reyes.* Kings. Or simply Reyes, her mother's name, worn openly like a flag of a country that no longer existed on maps. Claire held the handshake one beat longer than necessary, feeling the callus across the palm where a steering wheel lived, and smiled with perfect sisterly warmth.

"Well, Miss Reyes. I hope you'll forgive me for saying you have the most sensible occupation in this entire building. Everyone else seems to be fighting the whole century at once."

"We get there faster than you'd think," Rosalind said. Her voice was mild. Her eyes were not. They rested on Claire with an attention that had nothing to do with courtesy—an inventory, itemized and filed—and Claire recognized the exercise because she was performing it simultaneously: *strong hands, steady nerves, watches mouths when they speak, stands between the visitor and the mail sacks.*

The mail sacks. Claire laughed inwardly, delighted despite herself. *You read the letters. Of course you do. Papa would have liked you, God forgive us both.*

They spoke for three minutes about nothing—the weather, the automobile, the shocking state of Twenty-Third Street pavement—and each of them translated nothing into everything. When Rosalind mentioned that she'd heard the Judge had passed in October, her condolence was delivered in a voice of flawless neutrality, and when Claire replied that the loss had taught her how little one knows one's own family, she watched the girl's jaw tighten by a millimeter, a tension swallowed like a pill.

Two claims, one inheritance, and neither of them fooling the other for an instant. It was almost restful. Claire had spent her whole life among people who lied competently; it was a luxury to meet one who lied as well as she did.

Adeline Hart ended the audience by rising, and Claire took her leave with thanks and promises—she would send a donation, anonymously, of course—and at the door she paused, as if struck by afterthought, and turned back to the room at large.

"Miss Reyes—one small thing. My father kept meticulous records. Diaries, account books, the habit of a judge, I suppose. When his effects were inventoried, several volumes were missing. Family pieces. I'd hate to think they'd been lost or—borrowed. If you drivers hear everything in this city"—a bright, easy laugh—"do keep your ears open. There might be a reward in it. For recovery, naturally."

The room went very quiet, in the particular way rooms go quiet around Adeline Hart. Claire felt the older woman's gaze settle on her with new weight, and felt, beside the desk, the driver go still—not guilty-still, not startled-still, but the stillness of a person lowering her voice inside herself.

"New York loses everything," Rosalind said pleasantly. "If I find your father's diaries, Miss Hollis, I'll know where to reach you."

"At the Holland House. Until Christmas." Claire drew on her gloves, button by button, and gave the room her best smile—the one that had opened doors in London and Newport and, once, in a magistrate's chambers in Paris where a less careful woman would have paid a fine. "Happy hunting, all of you. On Tuesday, I mean. I do hope your rally goes beautifully."

In the elevator, descending, she let the smile fall away and stood looking at the brass doors, at her own dimmed reflection doubled in them.

The girl had the mouth. The girl had the stillness. And the girl had not denied knowledge of the ledger—had not even blinked—which meant she either knew nothing, which was fortunate, or knew everything, which was manageable, or knew enough to be dangerous, which was likely.

Claire weighed her options in the ascending order her father had prescribed: burn, buy, bury. Fire was crude and the girl clearly guarded whatever she guarded. Money—Mrs. Fenwick's story suggested money would bounce off that pride like rain off slate. Which left doubt, and doubt required groundwork: a forged receipt, a bribed witness, a whisper that the chauffeur had access and motive and a history of reading other people's mail.

Or. Her father's voice again, dry as autumn leaves: *Custodians clean, Claire. They do not always clean alone.*

Perhaps there was a third path, one the old man in his dying caution had never considered. Two daughters, one ledger, one inheritance—and the legitimate claimant, if she played it shrewdly, holding every advantage: standing, name, counsel, and the press's appetite for a servant's forgery. But also—Claire admitted it in the privacy of the elevator car, where no one could tax her for honesty—a shared enemy. Everything in that ledger implicated them both equally. Whatever sin was written in the Judge's hand stained both branches of the tree.

Back in her suite, Claire unpinned her hat, sat at the desk, and drew hotel stationery toward her. To Hale & Pruitt she wrote six lines: *Confirmed contact. Subject present and guarding something. Recommend acceleration of probate timeline. Also recommend you find me everything concerning the purchase of a printing concern on Nassau Street, 1893.* She sealed it, weighed it in her hand, and set it for the morning post.

Then she stood at the window a long while, watching snow begin over the rooftops of the city that held her father's secrets and her sister's silence, and thought about page after page in a small clerkly hand, and about the word *daughter*, which apparently could be multiplied, and about what her father would say if he could see them—the two of them circling each other in a room full of envelopes, each smiling, each counting.

*Burn it, buy it, or bury her,* the dead man whispered.

Claire smoothed her hair in the window's reflection and smiled at herself, and at the ghost, and at the snow coming down over Twenty-Third Street like a white page waiting to be written on.

"None of the above," she said softly. "Not yet. First, Papa—I'm going to read it."

Across the city, in a garage on East Twenty-Second, a young woman was doing arithmetic of her own by lamplight, and neither of them slept well, and both of them dreamed of the same book.
