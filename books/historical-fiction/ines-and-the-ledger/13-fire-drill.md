## 13. Fire Drill

Edith Wray had folded four hundred and six leaflets that October, and she could have told you the number without hesitation, because counting was how she kept herself from thinking. Counting the leaflets. Counting the stitches in her glove. Counting, now, the steps between the front door of headquarters and the records room at the back — eleven, if she cut past the coat rack; fourteen if she was honest and walked the long way around Mrs. Fenton's desk, which creaked on its left leg like an old man clearing his throat.

She was twenty-two, unpaid, and utterly certain that the cause would be won by women exactly like her: the ones who did the invisible arithmetic of a movement. Someone typed the letters. Someone folded the leaflets. Someone, on a Thursday evening in November when the radiators hissed and everyone else had gone home, stayed late to re-alphabetize the correspondence files because Miss Hart had said — not to her, exactly, but in her hearing — that a movement which could not find its own papers had no business asking a nation to find its conscience.

So Edith stayed. She stayed, and she smelled the smoke at seven minutes past nine.

* * *

The smell came first as something almost domestic — a match struck, a toast forgotten, the ghost of a kitchen. She thought for one absurd moment that the janitor had left a pipe smoldering somewhere, and she called out his name into the empty office and heard only the radiator answer.

Then she saw the light under the records room door. Not lamplight. Lamplight did not flicker in orange stripes across a floor.

Edith did not scream, though later she would describe to the fire marshal a sensation very much like screaming held in the mouth like water. She did what the drills had taught — the drills Miss Hart insisted upon, twice yearly, over the objections of the landlord: *A movement that burns down is a movement that buries itself*, Miss Hart had said, and so every volunteer knew where the sand buckets hung and where the hose spigot hid behind the stairwell's wainscoting, painted over to look like trim because insurance men asked fewer questions about trim.

Eleven steps past the coat rack. She took them running.

The fire was small. That was the first strange thing. A waste basket beside the filing cabinet marked **V**, flames no higher than her knee, chewing on paper with a soft industrious sound like mice. The second strange thing followed close on the first: the basket had been *moved*. It stood directly against the cabinet's wooden side, though the basket's usual station was under the correspondence desk ten feet away, and the scuff marks on the floorboards showed plainly where someone had dragged it there.

Paper alone did not walk. Paper alone did not position itself against the one cabinet that mattered.

Edith seized the sand bucket. Her hands knew the work even while her mind ran ahead of her, shrieking. She poured, and the flames went down hissing into gray sludge, and she stood in the dark smelling of char and river sand, looking at the cabinet marked **V** — Vasquez, though half the volunteers assumed it meant *Vermont*, where some of the petition records had come from — and understanding, slowly, the way one understands a word in a foreign language after it has been said three times, that this had not been carelessness.

Someone had tried to burn a specific thing. And the specific thing had not been in the basket when they lit it.

* * *

The fire marshal came the next morning, a comfortable man named Dooley who tapped the scorch mark with his pencil and pronounced it "a flue accident, probably" in a tone suggesting he had already billed his afternoon. Edith stood at Miss Hart's shoulder while he said it, watching Miss Hart's face do something complicated and then become smooth.

"A flue," Miss Hart repeated. "In a room with no fireplace."

"Sparks travel, ma'am. Old buildings." Mr. Dooley wrote *cause undetermined, likely accidental* in his book with the finality of a man closing a door on a drafty house, tipped his hat, and left them the smell.

The smell stayed longest in the records room — wet ash, burnt paper, the chemical tang of whatever the basket's contents had been besides paper, because Edith, kneeling afterward to salvage what she could, had found among the charred fragments a twist of waxed cloth that did not belong to any waste basket in the building. Waxed cloth was what men used to carry matches dry in a coat pocket. She wrapped it in a clean handkerchief and put it in her reticule and told no one, because telling felt like opening a door onto a dark staircase, and she wanted to stand at the top of that staircase a while longer before descending.

But she could not stop descending entirely, because the salvaging showed her more.

The fire had eaten perhaps thirty documents from the general files — suffrage pamphlets, a bundle of 1896 convention programs, nothing whose loss anyone would mourn or arrange. But around the cabinet marked **V**, the damage told a different story. The cabinet door stood open. Inside, the folders had been riffled — not searched in the frantic way of a thief, but gone through with terrible patience, every folder replaced slightly wrong, the way a book is reshelved by someone who does not know its place. And at the back of the second drawer, pressed flat against the wood, Edith found a single sheet that had been missed or dropped or abandoned: a carbon copy of a letter, dated March of 1897, from the offices of Hale & Whitcomb, attorneys, concerning "the disposition of certain instruments and writings belonging to the estate."

Estate. Singular. Unnamed. But Edith had stuffed envelopes for three years, and she knew the return addresses of power the way choir girls knew hymns. Hale & Whitcomb wrote letters on behalf of Judge Edmund Vasquez's executors, and Judge Vasquez's money, though it arrived laundered through three committees and a fictional bequest from a woman named Mrs. Eliza Prowe (who Edith privately suspected had never existed), kept the lights on in this building and the ink in these presses and Miss Hart on the platforms of five states.

The letter's last line had been circled in pencil by some anxious clerk: *until such time as all undocumented writings are secured.*

Undocumented writings.

Edith sat back on her heels in the ruined room, the carbon copy trembling very slightly in her hand, and let herself think the thought whole for the first time: *someone believes there is a ledger, and someone else wants it burned, and both someones know more about our filing system than I do.*

* * *

Miss Hart convened no meeting. That was the third strange thing, and in its way the strangest. A fire at headquarters should have produced circulars, speeches, at minimum a brisk lecture on vigilance delivered from the doorway of the records room. Instead Miss Hart closed the door, posted a notice reading *Records Room Closed for Repairs — Apply to Mrs. Fenton*, and instructed everyone, in the bright reasonable voice she used for hostile newspapermen, that the matter was settled and small and no cause for gossip.

Which is precisely how a matter becomes cause for gossip.

By Friday the volunteers had constructed four theories and dismissed two. By Saturday, Miss Prue, who typed with two fingers and listened with both ears, reported that a gentleman had come round Tuesday — *before* the fire — asking for Miss Hart, describing himself as a census official doing follow-up verification, and declining to leave a card. Mrs. Fenton remembered him. He had asked, apparently, not about the census at all but about "the household arrangements here — who keeps the books, who handles private papers, whether the organization employed anyone with connections to the Vasquez family."

Connections to the Vasquez family. Edith turned those words over all Saturday afternoon while she addressed envelopes, and the words grew heavier each hour, because there was exactly one person attached to this organization with connections to the Vasquez family, and everyone pretended otherwise, and pretending otherwise was practically a job requirement.

Rosalind Vasquez drove Miss Hart's motorcar. She had driven it for years. She was quiet as furniture and sharp as a tack, and Edith had once watched her recite, from memory and without apparent effort, the exact wording of a state senator's letter written eight months earlier — a letter Rosalind had merely carried, sealed, from the mail sack to the desk. Edith had laughed about it afterward with Miss Prue. *That girl reads everything that passes through this house*, she'd said. *Sealed or unsealed.* They had laughed. It had seemed charming, then. Evidence of a good memory idling in a life beneath it.

It did not seem charming now. It seemed like the description of a witness.

And witnesses, Edith thought, staring at the envelope in her hand addressed to a judge's wife in Albany, witnesses were precisely what the gentlemen from Hale & Whitcomb wished to see *secured*.

* * *

Sunday morning, before the others arrived, Edith came in early and did the thing she had decided to do with the mixture of terror and clarity that accompanies all truly simple decisions.

She inventoried the cabinet marked **V**.

It took her two hours. She listed every folder, every bundle, every loose sheet, comparing against the inventory card kept in Mrs. Fenton's drawer — the card was current, meticulous, in a hand she recognized as Rosalind's, because Rosalind apparently kept the secret books of the movement the way monks kept psalms. Folder by folder, item by item, the collection matched the card. Nothing missing.

Except.

At the bottom of the third drawer, beneath the petitions from Utica, there was a rectangle of cleaner wood, a pale ghost on the dark boards, the shape and size of a strong box or a document case that had lain there long enough for dust to darken around it and been lifted away recently enough for the outline to remain. Edith put her palm flat inside the shape. Cool wood. Empty.

Whatever had rested there, resting there no longer — and whoever had taken it had done so either before the fire or during the confusion after, and either way, they had known to look.

She copied her inventory in fair hand, signed and dated it, folded the carbon of the lawyer's letter inside, and addressed the packet to one person only: Miss R. Vasquez, care of the Hart residence on West Thirty-Sixth Street. Because if the movement's papers were hunted, then the movement's true archivist should know she was prey. And because — Edith admitted this to herself on the streetcar home, wind biting her ears, the waxed cloth still sitting in her reticule like a coal that refused to cool — because the fire had not been set by Rosalind. Edish knew it the way she knew her counts: the scuffs by the basket were made by a man's boot, size ten or better, and the census gentleman had asked his questions on Tuesday, and Tuesday's questions had pointed like a compass needle toward a ledger that Rosalind, whatever she carried in that careful head of hers, had evidently not surrendered to anyone.

The secret was out of the family now. Out of the drawers, out of the building, loose in the city with the year dying outside the windows. Men were willing to commit arson over it. Which meant men believed it could destroy something larger than an estate — and Edith Wray, counter of leaflets, keeper of small arithmetics, understood that she had crossed a line from which volunteers did not return. She was not folding pamphlets anymore. She was holding evidence.

On Monday morning, Mrs. Fenton handed her a note that had come by special messenger, unsigned, in a firm feminine hand:

*Miss Wray — Come to the house Wednesday at four. Bring your inventory and tell no one, including me, until we are behind a locked door. You did right. — R.V.*

Wednesday at four. Two days. Edith counted them the way she counted everything, and for the first time in her orderly life, the numbers frightened her — because the fire marshal had filed his report, and Hale & Whitcomb would read it, and if a law clerk could deduce from a scorch mark and a moved wastebasket what a volunteer had deduced in an evening, then somewhere across the city a boot was already walking toward West Thirty-Sixth Street, and the only question left was who would reach the driver first.
