## 18. December 29

The last Friday of the century arrived wearing a gray so complete it seemed the sky had been rubbed out with an eraser. Rosalind woke before dawn out of habit rather than hope, lay listening to the radiators tick in the Hart house, and counted what she had: one ledger, forty-three pages of her father's cramped upright hand; one appointment with Miss Okafor at noon; two days until filing; and no certainty that she would still possess her position, her name, or her freedom by Saturday night.

She dressed in the dark so as not to wake the household, and it was in the dark that she heard the knock.

Three raps at the kitchen door—unhurried, gloved, deliberate. Not Adeline, who never used the service entrance. Not Hale, who announced himself by letter and then by silence. Rosalind stood very still on the stairs, one hand on the banister, and thought: *she came early*. She had known, in some chamber of herself that had been working the problem while she slept, that Claire Vasquez would not let the year end without one final conversation.

She opened the door to a figure wrapped in fox fur, snow settling on the shoulders like sugar sifted over something expensive.

"Your stove is lit," Claire said, peering past her. "Thank God. May I come in before I become a memorial?"

* * *

They sat at the kitchen table because there was nowhere else. The servants' room was four feet away, and Mrs. Doyle would be down within the hour to boil oatmeal, and there was something about the domestic smallness of it—the scrubbed table, the dish of salt, the clock with its chipped face—that made the moment feel less like a negotiation between claimants and more like two sisters who had run out of ways to avoid each other.

Claire removed her gloves finger by finger. She looked tired. Rosalind noted it with a kind of grim satisfaction and then, immediately, with something closer to grief. They had the same hollows under their eyes. Their father's hollows, she supposed, though only one of them was permitted to call it a resemblance.

"You've filed nothing yet," Claire said. It was not a question.

"Monday."

"The second. Yes. I know the calendar, Rosalind." Claire smiled without warmth. "I filed Wednesday. Did Okafor tell you? No? Well. Consider yourself told."

The words landed the way she had intended them to, and Rosalind made herself breathe through the cold of them. *She filed first.* Of course she did. Claire had been raised in drawing rooms where the first word was always the winning word.

"Then why are you here?" Rosalind asked.

"Because filings can be amended. Withdrawn. Superseded." Claire leaned back and crossed her ankles under the table, and for a moment she looked sixteen again—not that Rosalind had ever seen her at sixteen, but she could imagine it, this girl learning early that charm was a currency and she'd been born with the mint. "Because I'm going to make you an offer, and you're going to want to hear it before Mrs. Doyle interrupts us with porridge."

"I don't want money from him."

"You haven't heard the number."

"It isn't the number." Rosalind got up to put the kettle on, mostly to have somewhere to put her hands. "You're paying me to disappear. That's what every offer has been. Hale's train ticket west. Your lawyers' settlement with its clauses like teeth. You're not buying my claim—you could beat my claim in court, maybe, you think, with your standing and your surname and your witnesses. You're buying my silence, and my silence is not for sale because it's already spoken for."

"To whom?" Claire asked softly. "To the record? Rosalind, the record doesn't love you. The record is a room full of paper that will outlive every person in this city. It cannot hold your hand. It cannot say thank you."

The kettle began its low gathering note. Rosalind stood with her back turned and found that her eyes were stinging, which infuriated her, because Claire was wrong but not entirely wrong, and being half-wounded by half-truths was the story of her whole life.

* * *

Claire laid it out while the tea steeped, in the fluent arithmetic of a woman who had been tutored by estate lawyers since she could read.

Half. Fifty percent of the distributable estate, negotiated quietly, no trial, no testimony, no press. Rosalind would withdraw her filing, sign a mutual release, and take the money under whatever name she liked—Vasquez, even; Claire would consent to that, would even petition for it, which was the sweetener, wasn't it, the thing Rosalind had crossed a city and a courtroom to want. Her name entered in the register. Her legitimacy acknowledged. In exchange, page forty-one stayed out of evidence, out of the newspapers, out of the mouths of every editorial cartoonist in Manhattan.

"Bury it," Claire said. "One page. Forty-one pages of our father's hand, honestly yours, honestly mine—and one page goes into the fire and we both walk out clean."

"You've read it," Rosalind said. It was not a question either. She had known, since the fire drill at headquarters, since the too-precise questions Hale had asked about *where exactly she kept her documents*, that page forty-one had a life outside the ledger now, a shadow-self circulating among people who wanted it dead. "You know what he wrote. You know what the bribe bought."

"I know what it bought." Claire's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "A newspaper. A *newspaper*, Rosalind. Do you know how many men in this city bought their newspapers worse ways than that? Half the judges on the bench. Two governors. Probably a cardinal. He took money from a man he should have jailed and he used it to print suffrage pamphlets by the ten thousand. If that's the founding sin of the movement, God help the republic, because every church and bank and university in America was founded twice as badly."

"That isn't the point."

"Then tell me the point, because I confess I'm lost." Claire set the cup down hard enough to slop. "You will destroy the funding of a movement you believe in—destroy it, the donors will scatter like pigeons, Adeline Hart will spend 1900 apologizing instead of organizing—for what? For honesty? Honesty is a private virtue. It does beautifully in parlors. It is catastrophic in public."

"My mother taught it to me anyway," Rosalind said. "In a kitchen smaller than this one. She said a lie kept well is just a debt kept longer."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The kettle clicked off. Somewhere above them a floorboard creaked—Adeline, awake, moving through the house she might lose. Rosalind felt the thought pass through her like a swallowed stone.

"And the money?" Claire pressed, quieter now, and here at last was something underneath the polish. "Half of everything. You'd refuse half of everything. You drive a woman's carriage twelve hours a day and sleep in a room the size of my closet, and you'll refuse half of everything for a page."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Rosalind came back to the table and sat, and folded her hands, and told her sister the truth because there seemed no other dignified thing left to do with it.

"Because I spent nine years reading other people's letters and keeping other people's secrets, and I watched what secrets cost. I watched Miss Hart carry one about the newspaper for six years—it changed how she stood, do you understand? She stands like a woman braced. I watched the treasurer round numbers and the donors look away and everyone agree, politely, endlessly, to be blind in the same direction. And when I finally held the proof of all of it in my hands, I understood that I could sell it, and buy a name, and spend the rest of my life standing the way she stands. Braced. Waiting for the page to surface anyway, because pages always surface. Or I could put it in the record myself, on my own terms, once, completely, and be finished." She paused. "You offered to split the estate. I'm telling you what I'd actually be splitting: the truth. Half of it hidden forever, and I'd have to live inside the hidden half."

Claire stared at her for a long moment. Outside, the snow thickened against the window glass, hissing faintly, like the century itself dissolving.

"You'll lose," Claire said finally. "You understand that. My filing stands. Your claim gets painted as a servant's forgery—I've already given the World the outline. By Monday you'll be 'the chauffeur heiress' in print, and it will stick, and no judgment in your favor will ever fully scrape it off."

"Probably."

"And the movement may not survive the scandal, and Miss Hart may not forgive you, and you will most likely end the new century exactly as you ended this one—poor, employed by someone else, and famous for all the wrong reasons."

"Also probably." Rosalind poured the tea, because if she did not do something with her hands she would reach across the table and take her sister's hand, and she suspected that would break them both. "But here is the part I think you have never had occasion to learn, Claire, because you were raised legitimate and I was raised invisible: when you have no name, no money, and no standing, the record is the only place you can exist at all. Courtrooms, registers, ledgers, the public memory—they are the only country a person like me can immigrate to. I am not going to smuggle a lie across that border on my first day as a citizen. Even a useful lie. Even for half of everything."

Claire looked down at her tea. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its drawing-room finish entirely, and what was underneath sounded young and tired and, for the first time in all their encounters, related to Rosalind by blood.

"He wrote *my daughter R.*, you know. One line. Out of forty-three pages, one line, and it wasn't even your whole name." Her mouth twisted. "He couldn't spare the letters."

"No," Rosalind said. "But he spared the ledger. He let my mother keep it. He knew she would, and he let her, and I think—" she stopped, surprised by her own thought, "—I think that was his apology. He couldn't say it, so he left a document. Our father confessed in bookkeeping."

"Our father," Claire repeated. She tested the words like a coin bitten for gold. Then she stood, gathered her gloves, and pulled the fox fur around her shoulders, becoming again entirely the creature who had knocked. At the door she paused, hand on the latch, snow waiting beyond.

"For what it's worth," she said without turning around, "if I had grown up the way you did—if I'd learned to read the world from the servants' side of it—I don't know that I'd have your nerve. I'd have taken the money. I'd have told myself a very good story about it." She opened the door onto the white morning. "Good luck Monday, sister. You'll need it. I intend to win."

And then she was gone into the falling snow, leaving a scent of bergamot and fur and the ghost of a word—*sister*—hanging in the warm kitchen air like breath.

* * *

Mrs. Doyle came down at six and found two teacups on the table and asked no questions, which was the finest courtesy the house had ever extended to Rosalind. She washed the cups slowly, letting her hands be busy while her mind walked the conversation again from end to end, looking for the place she might have chosen differently, and finding none—which was its own kind of terrible, because a choice with no better alternative available is a choice you must live inside forever.

At nine, Adeline appeared in the doorway of the stable office, dressed for no engagement, which meant she had cleared her morning, which meant she knew.

"Hale tells me Claire visited you," Adeline said.

"Adeline—"

"I'm not asking." She came in and stood by the little coal stove, and Rosalind saw that her friend's hands were not quite steady, and that she had dressed with unusual care, armor-style, for a conversation she had decided to have. "I have read every account of the last month, Rosalind, and I have drafted, in my head, seventeen statements disavowing you, and burned all seventeen, and I am done pretending I don't know what page forty-one says because I have known since 1893 and it has bent my spine ever since." She drew a breath. "Tomorrow you file. Whatever happens after—whether they laugh at you, whether the donors flee, whether they take the house—I want you to hear it from me plainly, once, before the noise starts: you are doing the right thing, and I am ashamed it took me twenty-six years and a chauffeur to show me how."

Rosalind stood very still. There was a great deal she could have said, and most of it would have broken something open in both of them that they could not afford to have broken open before a courtroom. So she said only: "Tuesday there's a rally in Newark. Shall I drive you?"

"If it still needs driving," Adeline said, with the ghost of her old smile, "yes."

After she left, Rosalind sat alone in the office with the ledger open on her knees, page forty-one facing the ceiling like a confession waiting to be heard. She read her father's figures one last time—the sum, the date, the source, the chilling neatness of it—and then she closed the book, wrapped it in oilcloth, and tied it with the plain brown string Miss Okafor had specified, because evidence should look humble going into a courthouse, Okafor said, humble and exact.

Two days. On Monday morning she would walk up the courthouse steps with everything she had: a forged-nothing, a feared-everything, a servant with forty-three pages and a memory full of other people's secrets. And Claire would already be inside, having gone first, having gone loud, having given the newspapers their story in advance so that the verdict would arrive pre-written in the public mind.

Let her, Rosalind thought, watching the snow bury the city inch by inch. Let the victors write early. History had a second draft, and she intended to be holding the pen for it.

She blew out the lamp and went upstairs to lay out her best dress—the gray one, severe and spotless—and to sleep, if sleep would come, on the last ordinary night of her invisibility.
