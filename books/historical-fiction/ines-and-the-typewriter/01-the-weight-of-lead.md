## 1. The Weight of Lead

The type had to be sorted by candlelight, because daylight belonged to the presses and the presses belonged to whoever was paying that week.

Odile Galloway sat at the sorting stone with a composing stick in her left hand and a lifetime of other people's grievances in her right, sliding letters into their compartments the way other women slid needles through linen. *A* in the upper-left tray, near the cap she could find blind. *Z* in its lonely corner where it gathered dust because no one paid for words that began with it. The lead had a smell she loved without admitting it—smoke and oil and something faintly sweet, like rain on hot cobblestones. It got under her fingernails and stayed there. Her mother used to say you could always tell a printer's daughter by her hands.

Her mother had been dead nine years. Her father was upstairs cutting matrices, and the sound came down through the floorboards like slow rain: *tink, tink, tink*, the graver finding steel, each letter he cut a letter that would outlive every man who set it.

"You're doing the *e*s wrong," Théo said from the doorway.

"I have been doing the *e*s since before you could read."

"Then you're doing them consistently wrong." He came in wiping ink from his fingers onto his apron, which was already more black than linen. He was nineteen and carried himself like a man of forty with debts, which was nearly accurate. On the table behind him lay the week's work: four hundred pamphlets for the Constitutional Unionists, still warm from the press, smelling of fresh ink and cheap patriotism. Last week it had been the Loyalist faction. The week before, a merchant's guild complaining about grain tariffs. Théo took payment from anyone whose coin didn't visibly bleed.

"The Unionists want two thousand by Friday," he said.

"They wanted one thousand yesterday."

"They increased their offer." He grinned, and it was their father's grin, quick and crooked, and Odile hated how well he wore it. "The city's getting loud, Odile. Loud is good for us."

Loud was not good for them. Loud was the opposite of what their father had spent thirty years building, which was silence—not the silence of doing nothing, but the silence of doing nothing *noticeably*. The Galloway shop printed almanacs and hymnals and bills of lading. It printed pamphlets when pamphlets paid, but always anonymously, always with the imprint page left blank, always with the type broken down and redistributed before the ink was dry on anyone's memory. Their name appeared nowhere. That was the whole architecture of their survival: be furniture. Be so ordinary that even the informants walked past.

Odile fitted another line of *e*s into place and did not say any of this, because saying it aloud would mean admitting she thought about it constantly—about the checkpoints going up at the river bridges, about the National Guard drilling in the Place with live rounds, about the way the baker now spoke in a lowered voice as though the walls had been deputized.

"Tink. Tink. Tink," said the ceiling.

* * *

The courier came on a Tuesday, in the gray hour before the lamps were lit, and he came dying, though it took Odile a moment to understand that.

She heard him first as a stumble against the doorframe—the shop door opened straight onto the street, no lobby between the world and the type—and when she looked up from the stone she saw a man folded halfway into the shop like a delivered package that had been dropped. He was young under the dirt. His coat had been good once. One hand pressed against his side, and between his fingers, black in the lamplight, something glistened.

Théo reached him first, arms out, saying the useless things people say—*sit down, sit down, I'll fetch water*—and the man shook his head with a terrible economy of motion, as if even nodding cost more than he could spend.

"Galloway," he said. "I need Galloway."

"He's upstairs—"

"No time to climb." The courier's eyes found Odile's father over Théo's shoulder, coming down the stairs already, graver still in hand, and something in the man's face unlocked, some tension held for miles, released all at once like a knot cut rather than untied. "Henri Galloway."

"You've the advantage of me," her father said quietly.

"We were at Renard's together. The year of the bread riots. You cut the matrices; I worked the lever." He swayed. Théo caught him and eased him into the chair by the stove, and the man let himself be sat the way cargo is sat, without dignity or resistance. From inside his coat he drew a letter. It was sealed twice, wax over wax, and pressed into the second seal was a device Odile didn't recognize—a hand holding a quill, severed at the wrist.

"For Aldous Renard." He said the name the way men say the names of saints or creditors. "He taught us both the trade. He must have this. No one else. Not the tribunals, not the committees, not the factions—they'd use it, and using it would bury it. Renard will know what to do." His breath whistled. "They're watching the east quarter. I couldn't get through clean. I got *mostly* through."

Blood, Odile thought. Mostly through, minus the part that leaked.

Her father took the letter but did not look at it. He looked at the courier, and the two men regarded each other across ten years and one revolution, and then her father said, "Who else knows you brought this?"

"Everyone who matters." The man almost smiled. "That's why I ran to the shop nobody's ever heard of."

* * *

He died in the back room an hour later, with Théo holding a basin and Odile holding his wrist and feeling the pulse go from a rabbit's rhythm to nothing, like a press winding down at the end of a run. Her father covered his face with an apron and stood over him for a long moment, and in the lamp shadow his shoulders looked suddenly older, as if the man's death had been delivered not to the back room but directly to Henri Galloway's spine.

"Burn the letter," Odile said.

They were in the kitchen afterward. Her father had put the sealed packet on the shelf above the flour bin, which struck her as obscene, as if it were a recipe.

"It isn't ours to burn."

"It's ours to *hang* for, apparently. Père—he named us. He died on our floor with our name in his mouth. Whoever hunted him knows where he was running. This stops being invisible the moment we touch it."

Her father sat down heavily. Up close she could see it now: her father was afraid. She had never once seen him afraid, not during the bread riots, not when the tax assessors came, not when her mother's cough turned wet and final. He had been a wall his whole life, and now the wall had a crack in it and she could see straight through to something small and human and cornered.

"Do you remember Aldous Renard?" he asked.

"I remember being seven and standing on a crate to reach the lever of his iron hand-press. I remember he paid me in licorice."

"He taught you the trade before I did. Before any of us, really." Her father turned the packet over in his hands, not opening it, just weighing it. "They declared him an enemy last spring. Enemy of the state, enemy of order, enemy of—whatever it is they think they're protecting. His press was smashed. His journeyman was taken. And he is hiding somewhere in this city with whatever he knows, and now he is to know this too." He exhaled. "There are things I saw, Odile. Years ago. Things I agreed to be quiet about, and agreeing felt like survival, and survival felt like virtue, and I let myself believe those were the same thing."

"Père—"

"Hold this for me." He pushed the packet back toward her, then stopped, drew it back. "No. Not yet. Let me think. A few days. There's a way to do this properly or there's no way at all."

She slept badly. In the morning the letter was gone from the shelf, and she assumed—hoped—he had burned it after all, and said nothing, and the lie of her hope lasted three days, during which the city grew louder and Théo printed two thousand pamphlets calling for calm and the *tink, tink, tink* from upstairs continued, steady as rain.

* * *

On the fifth day her father came down the stairs at midmorning, which was itself wrong—he never left the matrix bench before noon—and he was dressed as if for church, and he kissed the top of her head while she sorted type, and he said, "Mind the shop, my dove. I'm going to see about a delivery."

He collapsed in the street outside the cooper's stall two blocks away. The cooper's wife saw it, and the fishmonger, and six other witnesses who agreed on everything except the direction from which death had arrived. Apoplexy, said the barber-surgeon, who charged three francs to say so. But when Odile knelt in the street beside her father, his hand was closed tight around something, and she pried the fingers open, and in his palm lay a scrap of paper torn from the edge of the sealed packet, on which he had written, in the steady hand of a man who cut letters for a living:

*Renard. Promise me.*

By nightfall he was laid out in the back room where the courier had died, and the shop smelled of tallow candles, and Théo had not stopped moving since the barber-surgeon left—banking the fire, straightening chairs that were already straight, doing with his hands what he refused to do with his face.

Odile went to the false bottom of the type cabinet, where her father hid the accounts no assessor ever saw, and found the sealed packet where he had hidden it weeks ago, wax unbroken, waiting.

She held it a long moment. Then she held it longer.

Outside, down toward the river, a bell began to ring—not the hour, and not the alarm either, but the cracked old tocsin from the cathedral tower that hadn't sounded since the last revolution, ringing and ringing, and Théo came to the doorway with his face finally doing what his hands had been doing.

"Odile," he said. "Something's starting."

In her hands, the wax seals were warm from her grip, and beginning to give.
