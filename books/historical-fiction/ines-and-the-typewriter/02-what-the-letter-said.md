## 2. What the Letter Said

We buried my father on a Thursday, in rain that fell like an apology no one had asked for. The priest spoke of a good man, a quiet man, and I stood beneath my umbrella thinking that he had been right about the quiet, at least. Quiet as a locked drawer. Quiet as a seal.

By Saturday the shop was open again, because grief is a luxury and type does not set itself. Théo took over the pamphlet runs without being asked—he had always been the one who understood that survival meant moving—and I sorted cases from dawn until my fingers ached, e's into their rows, a's into theirs, the alphabet reduced to obedient little columns of lead.

The letter sat where it had sat for nine days: in the third drawer of my father's desk, beneath the spare quill knives and a tin of dried ink, wrapped in oilcloth against damp and time.

I told myself I was only dusting.

* * *

It happened at dusk, when the apprentices had gone and the press stood dark and enormous in the middle of the room like a sleeping animal. I lit one lamp, drew the shutters, and unwrapped the oilcloth on my father's desk.

The wax was stamped with his matrix-cutter's mark—the crossed gravers he used to sign proofs. His own seal, his own hand. Whatever this was, he had not been carrying someone else's secret. He had been carrying his own.

I held the letter over the lamp flame for a long moment. The paper warmed. The wax went soft at its edges. All I had to do was hold it there a breath longer.

I could not. God forgive me, I wanted to know what my father had been afraid of for eleven years, and wanting to know seemed like the last honest thing left in me.

I broke the seal.

The hand inside was his—I knew it the way I knew his face, all those sharp verticals, the g's with their peculiar flat tails, letters cut by a man who had spent his life making other people's words beautiful. It began without salutation, as if he could not bear to address even a piece of paper.

*I, Étienne Galloway, typeface cutter of the Rue des Fondeurs, do set down what I saw on the night of the seventeenth of October, in the year the printers' strike failed.*

My hands were steady. That surprised me later.

*You will know the date, though we never spoke of it. You were fourteen. Your mother sent you to your aunt's in the country that morning, and you thought it was for your health. It was so you would not see the smoke.*

He wrote that the strikers—sixty typesetters and pressmen from the quarter, men who had walked out over wages and the right to organize—had gathered in the Cour des Imprimeurs when the negotiations collapsed. He had gone among them not as a striker but as a tradesman, bringing bread, because his cousin Auguste was one of them and my father believed family obligations survived politics.

*They had drums. Someone had brought wine. It had the feeling of an ending, though none of them knew it yet.*

Then the soldiers came. Not to disperse—to finish. He wrote the general's name, and even reading it alone in a shuttered room I flinched from it, the way you flinch from a name spoken over a grave. *Colonel then, general now.* The orders had come down through channels my father did not pretend to understand. What he understood was what he saw from the archway of the Cour des Imprimeurs, where he had pressed himself when the first volley came.

*Sixty men knelt or ran or begged, and by midnight forty-one of them were dead in that courtyard, and the ones who lived were transported within the week, so that no witness remained who mattered.*

No witness who mattered.

*Auguste did not survive. I held him behind the fountain while the firing continued, and he said my name, and then he said nothing, and I stayed there until the soldiers moved on because I was too frightened to move first. Understand this: I did not stay out of loyalty or courage. I stayed because my legs would not answer. Cowardice kept me still, and stillness kept me alive, and being alive was the only thing I did that night that I can call a choice.*

I read that sentence four times. My father, who taught me that a crooked line of type was a moral failing, confessing to cowardice as if it were the worst crime in the courtyard. Perhaps for him it was. There were sixty men in that square and he was the only one the lead never touched.

*What followed, you know in your bones even if you do not know it in your mind. Within the month, the contracts came. The army's printing. The government gazette work. The commissions no small shop should have received and every small shop dreamed of. The general's adjutant visited once, drank one glass of wine, admired my matrices, and said—"Tell me if you find this phrase agreeable"—"the state prefers to reward discretion."*

*I found it agreeable. I have found it agreeable for eleven years. We ate because forty-one men died. This shop is built on their bones and I cut the type that printed the lie that buried them, and every Sunday I prayed into a God I had stopped believing in, asking Him to let me be forgotten before anyone remembered why we were spared.*

*But Aldous Renard would not forget, because Renard never forgets anything, and last spring he came to me with the muster rolls and the surviving wage lists and the names, all the names, and he asked me to testify. And I said no. I said I had children. He looked at me the way a compositor looks at a misspelled page and he said, "Étienne, neutrality is just a massacre that happens to other people," and he left, and I did not sleep for a month.*

*So this is my testimony. Late. Unwitnessed except by ink. I was there. I saw. The general ordered it and I was paid in silence and I took the payment.*

*Deliver this to Renard. If I cannot speak it while living, let it at least outlive me. Or burn it—I release you, Odile, I release you both; I have no right to bind you to a debt that is mine alone. But know that whichever you choose, you choose knowing now. There is no unreading. Forgive your father his life.*

There was no signature. Only the crossed gravers, pressed twice, hard enough nearly to cut the paper.

* * *

I do not know how long I sat there. The lamp had burned low when Théo came in, smelling of cold street and cheap tobacco, and stopped dead when he saw the open letter in my lap.

"You didn't."

"I did."

"Odile—" He said my name the way our mother used to say it before a storm drove her indoors. He crossed the room, snatched the pages, read them standing, faster and faster, and when he finished he did not rage or weep. He went very quiet, which was worse, and carried the letter to the stove.

"No," I said, and I don't remember crossing the room myself, but suddenly I had the pages back against my chest and Théo's hands were gripping my wrists and we were struggling like children over a toy, breathing hard, neither of us strong enough to win.

"Burn it," he said, close to my ear. "Listen to me. Burn it and forget you ever read it."

"You've read it too."

"Then we forget together. We're good at that. It's the family trade, isn't it?" His laugh was terrible. "A stranger came by yesterday. Asking after Papa's work, asking whether we'd ever done jobs for Renard's shop. Polite fellow. Well-dressed. Left his card." He pulled it from his waistcoat and dropped it on the desk between us like something dead.

I picked it up. No name. Only an embossed crest I recognized from the army gazettes we printed every week.

General Maraval's own device, sitting on our father's desk, in our father's shop, where I had spent my whole life believing we were too small, too careful, too neutral to matter.

"We were never invisible," I said. The understanding arrived whole, cold, complete. "We were spared. There's a difference, Théo. Eleven years of difference."

"Papa made us rich enough not to care about the difference."

"He made us bought." I smoothed the letter against my breastbone. "Forty-one men died in a courtyard and we've been eating off it ever since, and you want to burn the only apology any of them will ever get?"

"I want to live! Which is more than they can say, and more than we'll say either, if you do what I think you're going to do." He backed away from me toward the door, and in the lamplight I saw that he was crying without seeming to notice. "Renard is a hunted man. They've closed the river district. By tomorrow the barricades go up everywhere—you can feel it coming, everyone can. If you walk out that door with that letter you are walking into a war with a piece of paper in your coat, and paper doesn't stop bullets, Odile. Nothing about us ever stopped bullets. That's the whole point of what Papa wrote."

"Then that's the point of me," I said, though I did not fully understand it yet myself.

Théo stood with his hand on the latch a long moment. When he finally spoke his voice was almost gentle. "You always thought Papa's silence was safety. It wasn't. It was a leash, and someone's been holding the other end since before you could read." He opened the door onto the dark street. "Think about whose hand that is before you decide which way you're walking."

The door shut. The lamp guttered. Outside, faint but unmistakable, came the sound I would hear every night for weeks afterward: hammers, and voices, and wood being dragged across cobblestones.

The city was building its barricades. In my pocket, folded against my heart, my father's confession waited to become something else.

At dawn, I put on my thickest shawl, hid the letter in the false bottom of my type box, and went looking for the one soldier in this city who might hate General Maraval more than I feared him.
