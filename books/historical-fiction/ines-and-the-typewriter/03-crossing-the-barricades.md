## 3. Crossing the Barricades

The city broke open on a Tuesday, in the hour between the bakery queues and the first church bells, when the sky was still the color of unpolished pewter.

Odile heard it before she understood it — not the musket-crack of a single quarrel but a rolling, distributed thunder, like a press pulling sheets in every quarter of the city at once. She was standing at the stone sink with her hands in a basin of type-washing spirits when the first window up the street shattered and Théo came through the shop door white as unprinted paper.

"They've taken the Hôtel de Ville," he said. "And the Rue des Orfèvres is burning. Odile. *Odile.* The soldiers are sealing the bridges."

She dried her hands on her apron with a steadiness she did not feel. In the back room, wrapped in oilcloth inside her father's old composing stick case, the letter lay where it had lain for eleven days — the eleven days since his death, since she had broken the seal and read what he had seen and what his silence had bought. She had told herself each morning that today she would burn it. Each morning she had opened the case instead, looked at the folded pages, and closed it again, the way one looks at a debt one cannot pay.

Now the choice was being taken out of her hands by the sound of gunfire.

"Théo," she said. "Pack nothing that has your name on it."

"It's madness—"

"*Pack nothing that has your name on it.*"

He went. She took the case from its shelf, and the weight of it surprised her — not the lead, for the case held no type, only paper and oilcloth, but something else, a gravity like wet sand. Her father's promise had weighed less when it was still possible to keep.

By noon the district was sealed. She saw it from the shop's upper window: barricades of overturned carts and paving stones rising at both ends of the street, and beyond them, at the bridgehead, the gray line of the general's regulars with their bayonets fixed and their faces doing the terrible arithmetic of soldiers deciding which orders they will admit to having followed. No one crossed. A woman tried, holding up a child, and was turned back with such casual violence that Odile turned away from the window and did not look out again.

Renard's hiding place was in the Saint-Aubin quarter, across the river and two districts east. Three checkpoints. A city eating itself between here and there.

She was counting her coins — worthless now, coin always was, at moments like this — when someone knocked three times, paused, knocked twice. Not the faction knock Théo used with his buyers. Something older.

She opened the door on a soldier out of uniform: a tall woman with a healing cut above her eyebrow and a captain's greatcoat dyed the wrong black, worn like an apology. She carried herself the way men carry wounds they have decided not to mention.

"Galloway's print shop," the woman said. It was not quite a question.

"Who sent you?"

"No one living." The soldier's eyes moved past Odile's shoulder, reading the shop the way literate people read rooms — the imposing stone, the bank of cases, the smell of ink and lampblack. Something in her face loosened. "My name is Vasseur. Camille Vasseur. I served under General Marchette until March, when I was assigned to his correspondence." A pause, precise as punctuation. "I read things I should not have been able to unread. I have been a deserter since April. And yesterday I heard that Galloway's daughter might be carrying something across the river."

"Who told you that?"

"A dying man who owed your father money and paid his debts in gossip." Camille's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "You need a guide. I know which sentries sleep, which drink, and which ones still believe in something and are therefore predictable. I am offering."

Odile studied her — the officer's bearing, the deserter's hollows under the eyes, the hands of a woman who had held a rifle long enough for the rifle to hold her back. Every instinct that had kept the family invisible for twenty years said *close the door.*

But invisibility was already dead; she had read its obituary in her father's own handwriting.

"Tonight," Odile said. "After curfew."

**Interlude — the report of Camille Vasseur, never filed**

People think courage is a loud thing. It is not. Courage is the quietest thing I have ever known, quieter than snowfall on a bivouac. I learned this in March, in the commandant's tent, reading orders I had been given to file and forget — the requisition lists that were not for flour, the dates that matched the strike dates at the printing houses, the word *resolved* used about living men the way one writes it about outstanding accounts.

I deserted because I could not unknow it. That is all desertion ever is: the refusal to unknow.

And tonight, moving through my own former army's lines beside a typesetter's daughter with a letter against her ribs, I understood that she and I were doing the same act. Memory is contraband. What we remember, they must make us forget, or the whole machinery of massacre stops working. A general can survive losing a battle. He cannot survive being remembered correctly.

So when the sentry at the tannery wall lifted his lantern and I gave him the countersign I had stolen along with my life, and Odile pressed flat against the stones beside me with her breath held like a girl hiding from a thunderstorm — I felt no fear at all, or rather a fear so clean it resembled certainty. We were carrying the truest sentence in this city. Everything else out tonight was noise.

* * *

They went over the wall at the tannery because Camille knew the sentry, and through the flooded gravel pits because Camille knew the ground, and by two in the morning they stood at the edge of the burning quarter, where the fire that had started in the Rue des Orfèvres had spent six hours eating everything a print shop's neighbor owned. The heat came off the ruins in waves, and the smoke made a low ceiling they walked beneath like penitents.

"The last checkpoint is the Pont au Change," Camille murmured. "After that, ten minutes to Saint-Aubin."

They were halfway down the alley toward the river when Odile saw the torches behind them. Not a mob's ragged procession — an orderly line, four abreast, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly where to look.

"They shouldn't know this route," Camille said, very quietly. "No one knows this route."

Then Odile recognized, in the light of the flames, the set of the shoulders of the young man talking to the lieutenant — gesturing, explaining, pointing back toward the tannery wall. Théo. Trading their crossing for the shop, for his own skin, for the lie that you could pay the wolf to eat the other sheep first.

"He told them," Odile said. The words came out level, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "Camille. *He told them.*"

"Then we run."

They ran. Through a cooper's yard, over a fence hung with burning ivy, down stairs that had lost their railing into a cellar that let out onto the embankment. Behind them the torches fanned out with professional patience, cutting angles, closing boxes. A shot cracked off the stone near Odile's ear and she did not flinch; there was no time left over for flinching, only for the next step and the next.

At the foot of the Pont au Change a barrier of overturned drays barred the span, and behind it stood eight soldiers with their rifles leveled, and before them, wringing his cap, stood Théo, and Odile saw his face do what faces do when a man discovers that bargains with generals are not honored among honorable people — that the shop was never going to be spared, that his information had merely purchased him a front-row seat.

"Let the girl go," the lieutenant said, bored. "Vasseur. You're under arrest for desertion."

Camille did not turn around. She pressed the letter-case back into Odile's hands — Odile had not realized she'd been carrying it — and pushed her once, hard, toward the low arch of the coal stair beneath the bridge.

"You cross at the arches," Camille said. "Third pilaster, there's a ledge. Don't stop until Saint-Aubin. Find the cellar with the red door."

"Camille—"

"I deserted in April," the soldier said, and now she did smile, fully, for the first time. "I've been waiting since then for something worth surrendering *for*. Go."

Odile went, into the dark under the bridge, and the last sounds of the exchange followed her down: boots, orders, Camille's voice saying her name and rank without a tremor, as if reporting for duty after all. Then the river-noise swallowed everything, and there was only the ledge, the cold spray, the case held above the waterline in her two hands, and the red glow of the burning quarter sliding past above her head.

Behind her, they were taking Camille. Ahead of her, past the third pilaster, a cellar with a red door, and a dying printer, and a truth that still fit in two hands.

She did not stop until Saint-Aubin.
