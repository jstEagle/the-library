## 4. Set in Type

The cellar door was a curtain of wet canvas, and behind it the city went quiet.

Odile had followed Renard's directions through streets that smelled of smoke and spent powder—down the Rue des Fondeurs, past the fountain choked with torn proclamations, to a doorway between a shuttered chandlery and a house whose windows had been broken in some argument the night had already forgotten. She had carried the letter against her ribs for three days, wrapped in oilcloth beneath her shawl, and now, standing in the dark at the top of stone steps that breathed out cold air and the smell of ink, she found herself afraid to descend. Not of soldiers. Not of the mob. Of what she might find at the bottom.

She went down anyway. She had learned by now that dread was only grief wearing work clothes.

The press filled most of the cellar like a sleeping animal—a hand press of iron and walnut, older than her father, its platen polished by forty years of pulling. Candlelight found the galley shelves, the paper stacked in damp-proof bundles, the composing stone where a half-finished pamphlet still sat locked in its chase. And beside the stone, propped in a chair with a blanket over his legs and a pistol across his lap, sat Aldous Renard.

He was thinner than the man who had taught her to hold a stick without fear. His hair had gone entirely white since spring. But his eyes were the same—quick, amused, always reading something over your shoulder.

"Odile Galloway," he said. His voice was gravel washed in honey. "You still walk on your heels. Your father never cured you of it."

"You knew I'd come."

"I knew someone would." He coughed, and the cough went on long enough that Odile's hands curled into fists at her sides. When it passed, he wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked at the bundle she carried. "That's his hand on the wrapping. I'd know his knots anywhere. He tied everything like it was going to sea." Another smile, fainter. "Sit down, girl. I don't have the patience for standing farewells, and neither do you."

She told him then. The courier dying in the shop with rain running off the table into the cracks in the floor. Her father making her promise with a grip so hard it left marks. His death four days later, so sudden the doctor could offer nothing but a shrug and a bill. The letter opened by candlelight while Théo slept, and what was written inside—the square before the Ministry of Works, nine years ago, the striking typesetters who refused to disperse, the general's order given from horseback in a voice no louder than a man ordering dinner. Her father's signature as witness. Her father's silence, purchased with a lease renewal and thirty years of pretending not to remember.

When she finished, the cellar was silent except for the drip of water somewhere behind the press.

"Burn it," Renard said.

Odile stared at him. "You're the one it was meant for."

"And look what state I'm in to receive it." He lifted the pistol, considered it, set it aside. "One sealed letter, Odile, delivered to a dead man in hiding. What does it change? A general reads it, shrugs, burns it after me. Courts don't eat their own." His eyes fixed on her, and she saw the fever burning low behind them. "But you didn't come here to bury a letter. You came because you already know what it wants to be."

* * *

They worked through the night.

There were six apprentices in the cellar—youngest a boy of fourteen named Luc who could justify a page faster than men twice his age—and when Renard raised his voice they came out from behind the paper stores like mice who had decided, finally, to stop being mice. Odile read the testimony aloud once, all of it, her father's confession in his own careful words, and none of them spoke afterward. Then Renard said: "Set it. Octavo. Sixteen pages. We have until dawn or we have nothing."

It was the work of her childhood performed at the edge of a knife. Odile stood at the case and her fingers moved without instruction, pulling sorts by touch the way her father had taught her when she was tall enough only to reach the lower boxes—an e is easier than an a, he'd said, because you'll need ten thousand e's before you die—except that now every word was his voice, every line his ghost made solid. *I, Marcel Galloway, typeface cutter, being of sound mind and ruined conscience...* The letters were cold and heavy and they clicked into place like a lock turning.

Renard directed from his chair, too weak to stand, correcting punctuation with a voice that frayed a little more each hour. At two in the morning he called Odile to him.

"The last page," he said. "Leave space below his testimony. We'll add the names."

"The names?"

"The dead. All eleven. Typesetters have names, girl; we spend our lives setting other people's." He recited them slowly, and she set each one in capitals with a space between like graves in a churchyard, and when she pulled the proof and read them back to him, he closed his eyes and said, "Good. Now the whole city will know them better than the general."

Luc fell asleep against the paper bale around four; someone put a blanket over him and kept working. The press began its rhythm near five—Renard's oldest apprentice at the lever, another feeding sheets, ink smelling of tallow and iron—and Odile stood at the tympan pulling copies and laying them in fanning rows, and somewhere in the sixth hundred her arms stopped hurting and started feeling like her arms again, and she understood that this was the last thing her body would ever learn: how to make grief take an impression properly.

By first light there were a thousand.

* * *

The apprentices carried them up in bundles under their coats and scattered like seed.

Odile took her share to the fountain on the Rue des Fondeurs, where the crowd was thickest—bakers' boys and laundresses, deserters and widows, people queuing for bread that did not exist. She laid the sheets on the dry rim of the basin and stepped back, and watched a woman pick one up out of idle curiosity and go still, and then read aloud, and then stop reading aloud because her voice broke on the fourth name.

They moved through the crowd the way fire moves through a field. In twenty minutes a copy had reached the market square. In an hour someone had pasted one to the door of the Ministry of Works itself, on the very square where the eleven had died, and a knot of people stood before it in silence deep enough to hear the paper flutter.

Odile saw her brother across the fountain.

Théo stood at the mouth of the Rue aux Oies with a copy in both hands, still creased from the fold, his lips moving through the text he must have known was coming—he had begged her to burn it, he had sold their route to save the shop, and now the words he had feared were in every hand in the district, including his own. He looked up. Found her. His face did something complicated and then went blank, and he could not hold her gaze, and he turned away into the crowd with the sheet crushed against his chest like something he meant to keep and could not explain.

She let him go. There would be time, or there wouldn't.

* * *

The soldiers came to the print shop at noon.

Odile watched from three streets off, from a window she had chosen in advance the way you choose a seat at a funeral. They poured oil at the base of the door and stood back with the ease of men doing routine work, and the old sign—GALLOWAY & FILS, CUTTERS OF LETTERS—blackened first, then the windows bloomed orange, then the whole façade went up with a sound like tearing cloth. Théo was not among the watchers. She hoped he had been somewhere else. She hoped many things she no longer expected.

Her father's matrices were inside. Thirty years of punches and coppers, every letter he had ever cut, curling in the heat. She thought of him saying that lead remembers nothing, that only people remember, and that was why people had to be so careful.

The shop burned until midafternoon. The truth did not burn at all. By evening there were copies in two more districts; by the next night, according to a boy who ran messages for Renard's apprentices, there were copies in the garrison itself, folded into pay envelopes, tucked into ration sacks. The general's name had been set in twelve-point roman with proper leading, and no tribunal, no pardon, no proclamation could ever take the impression out of a thousand minds. Ink was a court that never adjourned.

On the third morning, Odile went back down into the cellar. Renard had lasted just long enough to see a copy of the finished pamphlet; he had died in his chair beside the press with it open on his knees, and the apprentices had buried him at dawn in a yard behind the chandlery, with a stone Luc had cut himself, badly, lovingly.

The press waited for her in the dark. On the composing stone, locked in its chase, someone had left a new forme set and ready—sixteen words in large type, and beneath them a blank space where a name should go.

Odile lit the candles, took her place at the case, and reached for the sort.
