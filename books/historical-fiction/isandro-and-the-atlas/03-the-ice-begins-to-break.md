## 3. The Ice Begins to Break

The river began to speak three days before it broke.

Quentin heard it first from the loft above the workshop, a low groan rolling up through the floorboards, as if the ice itself had learned of their intentions and objected. By dawn he was standing at the window with his coat over his nightshirt, watching the grey sheet of the Vell flex and shudder in its channel. Cracks spidered out from the eastern bank like flaws in a copperplate, black seams widening by the hour.

Behind him, his uncle slept badly, coughing in fits. His sister Brigid slept not at all; he could tell by her breathing, too even, too deliberate. A liar's stillness. She was eleven and had been lying about sleeping for a week so that he would not know she lay awake being afraid.

He dressed without lighting the lamp and took down the atlas.

It sat where it always sat now, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of the map chest, and every morning he asked himself the same question: *was it safer here, or on my body?* Every morning the answer changed. This morning, with the river talking and the soldiers' patrol boots audible even from the loft — they had taken to walking the quay at first light, counting doors — he decided the atlas would go where he went, folded into sections small enough to disappear beneath his shirt against his ribs.

He had been memorizing it for six nights now. That was the work that filled the hours when the house held its breath: not drawing, but *un*-drawing. Taking each plate apart in his mind, plate by plate, line by line, until the secret routes lived somewhere the soldiers could not search. The drover's path behind the tannery that appeared on no official survey. The ford at Kiln Bend, passable only in the last week before thaw, when the water ran shallow over the gravel bar. The culvert beneath the old millrace, wide enough for a man with a pack, too narrow for a man with a bayonet.

His master had drawn all of it forty years ago, in ink so fine it looked like thought. Quentin understood now why the old man had died face-down across this atlas rather than surrender it. It was not a book. It was a country's memory of its own back doors.

* * *

They left at dusk on the third day, when the light went blue and the patrols changed hands.

Uncle Cormac came on his own feet, refusing the cart, though each step cost him visibly and Quentin pretended not to price it. Aunt Sive carried what food there was. Brigid carried the bundle of linens that was meant to look like laundry and was instead everything the family owned that could be lifted by a child. Quentin carried the atlas against his ribs and the knowledge of the way in his head, spoken aloud to no one, because routes that exist only in one man's skull cannot be confiscated.

"The ferry berth," Cormac said again, quietly, as they slipped through the yard. "One berth, you're certain."

"One berth. Captain Ondrey holds passage for those who can pay, and the purser has taken our silver. The *Merrow* breaks ice at dawn tomorrow or not at all." He did not say the rest: that Ondrey's manifest showed four names purchased and the harbor master's ledger allowed only one amendment, and that he had spent two days understanding exactly what that arithmetic meant. He would do that sum later, alone, or never.

They went north first, away from the river, because the direct road was a trap and everyone knew it. Behind the tannery the drover's path threaded between walls of stacked hides and rendered fat, the stink so thick it made Brigid gag, and Quentin put his hand between her shoulder blades and steered her by pressure alone. *Left here. Mind the gatepost.* The path bent twice and delivered them into a birch wood where no lamp could be seen from the water, and for an hour he let himself believe the atlas had told the truth.

At Kiln Bend the ice had gone rotten along the shore, plates of it tilting on the shallowing meltwater, and they crossed on the gravel bar with the current sucking audibly at the edges, Sive white-lipped, Cormac leaning on Quentin's arm and saying nothing, which from him was a great deal.

The millrace culvert was last. Quentin went in first with a shuttered lantern, and the light came back to him off wet stone, and the dark ahead smelled of forty winters of grain dust. They were halfway through the town's underside, committed, past turning, when Brigid stopped.

"Someone's on the bank," she whispered. "Watching."

* * *

He knew the silhouette before the lantern confirmed it: Halloran, from the house with the good chimney, Halloran whose son had shared bread with Brigid at the market stall, Halloran who owed Cormac money and had lately begun standing very straight when soldiers passed.

"Quentin Kavanagh." Not a question. Halloran stood at the culvert mouth with a storm lantern raised, and behind him, far down the lane, a whistle sounded — two notes, then a third, the pattern Quentin had heard every dawn for a week. "They said the family would run north. I only had to stand where any sensible man would run."

"Halloran." Quentin kept his voice level while panic climbed his spine like water finding its level. "Whatever they've paid you—"

"They've paid me nothing." And that was the worst of it; Halloran's face was miserable and honest in the lantern light. "They've *forgiven* me things. There's a difference, and your uncle knows the difference better than any man alive." He stepped aside from the mouth, not blocking it — gesturing toward it, almost courteous. "Run if you like. It changes nothing now. I'm sorry for it, boy. Genuinely."

The whistle sounded closer. Quentin turned back to his family in the culvert's dark, and the decision arrived whole, the way the worst decisions do — not chosen but simply found waiting inside him.

"The race path," he said to Sive. "Straight down to the reed beds, then the towpath. Don't stop for anything, don't answer any hail. Get to the dock. Ondrey's ferry. Go."

Cormac understood first, because Cormac had done sums all his life. "Quentin—"

"*Go.*"

Sive took Brigid's hand. Brigid looked back once — one look, furious and terrified and eleven years old — and then the dark of the culvert swallowed them, and the sound of their footsteps faded downstream, and Quentin stood alone in the grain-smelling dark with an atlas against his ribs and a whistle blowing on both banks now.

He went out the way they'd come in, and he walked toward the whistles, and he made himself easy to find.

* * *

They caught him at the dock, which surprised him; he had expected the lane, the wood, anywhere but the open stones of the quay where the *Merrow* lay groaning against her lines with steam rising off her boiler into the freezing dark. Four soldiers, and Lieutenant Ashe in the middle of them, the officer who had come asking questions about his master's death and who had never once, Quentin now understood, believed his own questions.

"Where are they, Kavanagh?"

"Gone," Quentin said. "Hours ago. North road."

Ashe studied him with the patience of a man reading a difficult chart. Down the quay, the reed beds stood black and motionless; somewhere among them his family was moving, and every second Quentin stood here talking was a second bought with the only currency he had left.

"You'll want to search the boat anyway," Quentin said, and heard his own voice go strange and clear. "But you won't find them, because they aren't aboard. What you want is what I have." He drew the atlas from his shirt — the folded sections, the secret routes, forty years of his dead master's thought. "Every hidden crossing in the district. Every path your surveys missed. The ford at Kiln Bend. The millrace culvert. I'll recite them. All of them. Every route you need."

Ashe's eyes narrowed. "In exchange for what?"

"For whatever's in that ledger." Quentin nodded at the harbor master's office, where the manifest hung. "You've been counting berths since you got here, Lieutenant. So have I."

Out on the dark river, the ice shifted and boomed like a door being forced, and the *Merrow's* deckhand began casting off the forward line, and Quentin Kavanagh stood very still on the stones and waited to learn the exact price of everything.
