## 4. The Last Berth

The ice was going out of the river in long, groaning sheets, and the ferry *Maiden's Fortune* sat low at the berth with her rail crowded by faces Quentin would never see again.

He held his sister's hand too tightly. He knew it, and he could not stop.

"Say it back to me," he said.

Brigid rolled her eyes — fifteen years old and already tired of being treated like a fool — but she said it. "North bank to the stone weir, then the old drover's track through the birches. If the track is flooded, the ferryman knows the crossing at Miller's Bend. The atlas plate is number eleven. You drew it. You said the ink was good ink."

"The ink is the best I ever made."

"Then stop worrying about the ink." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she turned her face away toward the water, toward the far shore where the mist was lifting off the pines like breath from a mirror. "You could come. There's room. The ferryman said—"

"The ferryman said there was one berth left, and it's yours." Quentin took her shoulders and made her look at him. Her eyes were their mother's eyes, gray as slate before a storm, and he had spent his whole life drawing maps and had never once found a way to draw what those eyes did to him. "Listen to me. The soldiers want a mapmaker. They want the man who made the atlas, and they want the routes in his head. They will not follow a girl with no name worth taking."

"You have a name worth taking."

"I know," he said. "That's why I'm giving it away."

Down the dock, boots. Four soldiers in river-gray coats, moving at the unhurried pace of men who knew the exits were closed. Quentin had perhaps two minutes. He had spent his whole life learning to measure time in distances, and now he measured it in the length of a dock, in the number of strides between himself and the gangplank.

He pressed the letter into Brigid's hands — the sealed letter, the one that had named them all, folded small. "Burn it when you're across. Don't read it again. It's not ours anymore."

"And the atlas?"

Quentin touched the satchel at his hip, empty now but for a roll of blank paper and three pencils worn to stubs. "The atlas is wherever you are," he said. "Plate eleven. Every plate. I put them all in you, Brigid, whether you wanted them or not. You're the atlas now. Walk like one."

She threw her arms around him, and for a moment he was nine years old again, teaching her to hold a ruler steady, and the river was only a river, and the soldiers were only a story their uncle told to frighten children. Then she was pulling away, running up the gangplank with her shawl flying, and she did not look back, and he loved her the more for it.

The ferryman cast off. The current took the *Maiden's Fortune* sideways into the channel, into the broken ice, and Quentin turned to face the soldiers with the dock boards cold through his boots and his heart, for the first time in weeks, entirely still.

* * *

They took him to the customs house, because it had a stone floor and a table, and because the captain — a narrow man named Ossler with ink-stained fingers, which Quentin noted with some distant professional sympathy — preferred to work sitting down.

"The atlas," Ossler said. "Where is it?"

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

"Across the river. In pieces. In a girl's memory, mostly." Quentin sat when they pushed him into a chair, and he found, to his surprise, that his hands were steady. He laid them flat on the table like a man presenting finished work. "You can't seize what I've given away, Captain. But I'll tell you something better. I'll tell you every route."

Ossler's eyes narrowed. "Why would you do that?"

"Because you'll write them down." Quentin nodded at the captain's ink-stained fingers. "And because a route on paper is a dead thing, Captain. A route in a head keeps walking. You can have every mile of it, and it will still not be the atlas. The atlas was never the plates. It was the man who drew them, and the man who drew them is about to walk out of this room and keep drawing."

The interrogation lasted four hours. Quentin gave them the drover's track and the weir crossing and the smugglers' path through the birches, and he gave them the false fork he had invented three nights ago and sewn into his memory beside the true one, and he gave them the second false fork, and the depth of the ford at Miller's Bend as it had been before the spring flood — all of it delivered in the flat, patient voice of a man dictating to an apprentice. Ossler wrote it all down. Ossler's clerk wrote it down twice.

Near dawn, the captain sat back and studied him.

"You understand you can't stay in this province. The name Kavanagh is finished here. You'll be escorted to the northern road at first light, and if you're seen south of the ridge line, you'll hang."

"I understand."

"Any requests?"

Quentin thought about it. His master's face, the way the old man had held a burnisher. His uncle's hands over the candle flame, begging him to burn the letter. Brigid's shawl, flying as she ran up the gangplank. The river, going out of itself.

"Paper," he said. "And if it's not too much trouble — paper that's blank."

* * *

They gave him a dozen sheets, poor stock, and turned him out onto the northern road while the frost was still on the fences.

He walked.

The road ran through country he had never mapped — that was the whole point of it, and the whole mercy of it. Hills he had no names for. Streams that appeared on no plate, answered to no surveyor, belonged to no atlas but the one assembling itself behind his eyes with every mile. By noon the river town was a smudge of smoke on the southern horizon, and by evening it was nothing at all, and Quentin discovered that the horizon could hold a grief the way a plate held an image: entirely, and in miniature, and forever.

He camped in the lee of a stone wall and, by the light of a small fire, took out the blank paper.

His hands wanted to shake. He let them, for a while. Then he set the first sheet on his knee, took up a pencil stub, and drew the border of the page — a clean rule, north to south, the way his master had taught him, the way he had taught Brigid. Then he began.

The wall behind him. The fence posts, their spacing. The stream he had crossed at midday, its width estimated by the sound of it. The hill where the pines gave way to birch. He worked the way his master had worked, the way the dead worked through the living: patiently, precisely, loving what could be measured and honoring what could not. And when the plate was done he titled it in the old style, the style of the atlas, the style of a family that had kept its name through worse than this:

*Plate the First. The Country North of the Ridge. Surveyed by Q. Kavanagh, who is not lost.*

He sat back and looked at it for a long time.

Somewhere across the river, in a town that would soon forget him, his sister was walking north with an atlas in her bones. Somewhere south, a captain with ink-stained fingers was following routes that led nowhere. And here, on a hillside under a sky full of unnamed stars, a mapmaker with no country was making one, plate by plate, out of blank paper and the stubborn conviction that a man is not the ground he stands on.

Quentin banked the fire, wrapped the plate in oilcloth, and shouldered his satchel.

To the north, past the birches, the land rose into country no one had ever drawn — and he found, to his astonishment, that he was not afraid of it. He was hungry for it. He stood a moment longer, memorizing the shape of his own life to come, and then he walked into it, and the blank paper went with him, and it was not blank for long.
