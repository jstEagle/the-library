## 1. What Can Be Carried

The snow came down in the flat, patient way of February, as if it had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Elodie Winterbourne drove with their shoulders hunched against the cold leaking through the cab's split window, hands gloved to the second knuckle so they could feel the wheel, and watched Marguerite Ash from the corner of one eye the way a lighthouse keeper watches weather.

Marguerite sat with a portfolio on her knees and did not open it. She never opened anything in the car. That was the first lesson Elodie had learned, back in October when they'd been hired—*driver and general assistance*, said the notice pinned to the mill office door—and the second lesson had followed within a fortn: everything that mattered traveled by hand, in coats, in bread loaves, in the false bottoms of hatboxes, and nothing that mattered was ever written where a stranger might read it.

Except, of course, that Elodie read it anyway.

Not Marguerite's papers—those stayed shut. It was the letters that undid them. The correspondence that came to the meeting hall on Cordwainer Street, addressed sometimes to *Mrs. Ash, Organizing Secretary* but more often to no name at all, just the street and the town, because half the women who wrote could not risk even ink under their own roofs. Elodie collected the mail each morning in the motor's canvas bag, sorted it in the back room among the tea chests, and if a letter lay unsealed—or sealed so badly the flap lifted like a shy eyelid—Elodie read it.

They told themselves it was duty. A driver should know which letters were urgent. But duty was a thin coat for what Elodie actually did at night, which was sit at the narrow desk in the loft above the stable with a candle turned low and a ledger bound in gray cloth—the kind of blank book schoolchildren ruin by the dozen—and copy out, in a small upright hand, the facts of other people's courage.

*Marta Pell, weaver's wife. Taken Tuesday the sixth, from the spinning floor, two constables. No charge given. Her husband sent word she is held at the county gaol; her three children are with her sister in Thistle Row.*

That entry was four days old. Tonight's would be worse.

Elodie dipped the pen. Outside, the stable clock ticked like something patient and slightly malicious, and below, in the yard, the motor stood furred with snow, faithful as a horse.

*Bess Harrow, schoolteacher, dismissed this morning by the board. Cause unstated. She addressed the market crowd Saturday on equal franchise. Her landlady writes that Miss Harrow has until Friday to leave her rooms.*

There were thirty-one names now. Thirty-one lines in gray cloth, each one a woman who had spoken, or marched, or merely written to say she agreed, and had been answered with arrest, with dismissal, with the quiet administrative violence of a country at war that could not spare patience for its own citizens. Elodie knew all thirty-one by heart now, the way other people knew psalms. Some nights that knowledge felt like a weight. Most nights—Elodie examined this honestly, blowing on the wet ink—it felt like something else entirely. Like being trusted. Like being the only person in Millbrook who could see the whole shape of it, pattern after pattern, and set it down where it could not dissolve into rumor.

Nobody had asked them to keep the ledger. That was the strange, sweet center of it. Elodie had simply begun, and now the book existed, and Elodie existed alongside it, and both facts felt inevitable, like the river ice thickening toward March.

Below, the stable door scraped. Footsteps on the stairs—two steps, then a pause at the fourth stair, which creaked, which always creaked, which meant Marguerite, who had climbed these stairs forty times and still refused to memorize its treacheries.

"You're burning oil," Marguerite said from the doorway, though the candle was barely taller than a thumb. She was fifty-one years old and carried herself like a woman perpetually five minutes early. "And you're writing."

"I'm doing accounts."

"Then I'll say goodnight to your accounts." Marguerite came in anyway, warming her hands over the candle without seeming to notice the cold, and looked at the ledger upside down with the frankness of a woman who had spent twenty years being lied to by governments and had stopped pretending not to recognize it. "Marta Pell?"

"Bail hearing's Thursday. Nobody local will speak for her."

"I'll speak." Marguerite said it the way she said everything, already moving past the sentence toward the next one. "Six o'clock train. You'll drive me."

"Yes."

Marguerite lingered. In the candlelight her face looked older than it did by day, the lines around her mouth like creases in folded paper. "You know what you're keeping there," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"A record."

"It's more than that, and you know it, and you keep it anyway, alone, in a loft, by candlelight." Marguerite shook her head slowly—not disapproval; Elodie had learned the difference long ago. "One day that book will matter more than either of us. When it does, don't be sentimental about it. Books can be replaced. People who write them—" She stopped herself, patted the desk once, twice, and went down the stairs, hitting the fourth step hard.

Elodie sat a while longer, listening to the snow.

* * *

The army came on a Tuesday, and it came the way weather comes: first a rumor, then a horizon, then it was everywhere and pretending it had always been there.

Elodie saw them before anyone official did—a column of lorries on the north road, olive-dark against the white, engines growling in low gear. By the time they reached the mill square the town had come out to stand in doorways, the way towns do, arms crossed, breath steaming, performing indifference badly.

Requisition orders, the officer said. He was young for his rank and shouted unnecessarily, as though the square were a parade ground. The textile works converted to webbing production, effective immediately. The meeting hall on Cordwainer Street commandeered for garrison offices. Lodging billeted house by house. He read it from a paper he did not offer to show anyone, and when a man near the fountain asked by what authority, the officer said, "By the Army Act," in the tone one uses for a child asking why the sky.

Elodie stood with the mail bag over their shoulder and understood, in the slow cold way one understands things, that the world had tilted. Not broken—tilted, the way a table tilts, so that everything on it begins quietly sliding toward one edge.

At the hall, Marguerite received the news standing behind her own desk, which two corporals were already measuring for removal. She did not argue. Elodie watched her not argue and understood that too: argument was fuel spent on the wrong fire. Instead she gave orders in a low voice—to pack the duplicating machine's type in flour sacks, to move the minute books to the Fenwicks' cellar, to finish the circular letters tonight because the post left at seven whether the empire approved or not.

The soldiers were polite the way an avalanche is quiet. They stacked their rifles in the corner where the sewing machines usually sat and drank tea from the hall's own cups, and one of them, a boy scarcely older than Elodie, apologized when he knocked over the umbrella stand, and Elodie hated how much they wanted to forgive him.

It was nearly dusk when the captain arrived. He was not young. He had the unhurried manner of a man who had done confiscations before and found them tedious, and he walked through the hall touching things lightly—a banner here, a stack of pamphlets there—as though pricing them.

"The organizing records," he said.

"Dispersed," said Marguerite pleasantly. "We're a voluntary association. We keep very little."

"Very little," the captain agreed. "We'll see it, all the same."

And then his eye fell on Elodie, standing by the mail bags, and Elodie felt the whole loft ledger flare up inside their chest like a struck match—thirty-one names, thirty-one women, *say nothing*—but the captain's gaze slid past without catching. A driver. Staff. Furniture that could carry things.

"Your people may stay on to service the garrison," he told Marguerite. "Drivers, cooks. Someone has to keep the wheels turning." He smiled faintly at his own phrasing. "The town's part of the war effort now, Mrs. Ash. Even you."

"Especially me," Marguerite said, and smiled back, and Elodie, watching the two smiles meet like blades, picked up the mail bag and went out into the snow.

The lorries were parked along Cordwainer Street, and the snow was filling their tire tracks as fast as they made them, and by morning, Elodie knew, there would be a sentry at the hall door and a list of new regulations nailed to the post where the march notices used to hang.

But tonight the post left at seven, and the letters were in the bag, and some of them were unsealed, because fear makes careless hands of us all.

Thirty-two names, thought Elodie, walking fast, the cold biting clean. There would be thirty-two by candlelight tonight. And whatever the captain came looking for when he came looking—

He would find a gray cloth ledger full of accounts.

Elodie reached the stable, climbed to the loft, and lit the candle.
