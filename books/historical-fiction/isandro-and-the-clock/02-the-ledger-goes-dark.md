## 2. The Ledger Goes Dark

They came at eleven in the morning, when the light was flat and the mill whistle had long since gone silent, and they came in trucks.

Elodie heard them first from the kitchen of the meeting hall, where she was scrubbing teacups—Marguerite insisted on real china even now, even with sugar rationed to a spoonful, because *a movement that drinks from tin cups has already conceded it is a beggar's movement*—and the sound was wrong for the town. Too many engines. Too steady. Deliveries came rattling and cheerful, one lorry at a time. This was a convoy, and it did not slow for the ruts in Harrowgate Road.

She dried her hands on her apron and stood in the doorway of the back room where Marguerite kept her papers, and she watched Marguerite Ash, who was fifty-three years old and had been arrested twice before in two other towns, read the situation through the window with the calm of a woman checking a train timetable.

"The sideboard," Marguerite said. Not the ledger. Not *the book*, which was what they had agreed to call it, which was what Elodie had called it for eight months in her own head. The sideboard. As though the thing that mattered were the furniture.

"The sideboard is locked," Elodie said.

"Then you will want to unlock it quickly."

The knock came before Elodie's fingers found the key in the flour tin where it had lived since October. Three knocks, spaced like a metronome, and then the door simply opened anyway, because a knock from a soldier is not a request.

There were six of them. An officer in front with a face like a folded map, creased and unreadable, and behind him men with the particular blankness of men who have been told that what they are doing is procedure. They smelled of engine oil and wet wool. One of them wiped his boots on the mat, which Elodie would think about later, absurdly, for hours—the courtesy of it, the terrible ordinary courtesy.

"Major Hessler," the officer said, and did not offer a hand. "By order of the regional command, this building is requisitioned for the administration of the garrison. You will have one hour to remove personal effects. All correspondence, records, membership rolls, and printed matter are subject to seizure." His eyes moved over the room the way a man's eyes move over livestock. "Which of you keeps the books?"

"I do," said Marguerite, before Elodie could breathe.

It was true and it was not true. Marguerite kept the accounts—the shillings and sixpences, the rent, the tea. The ledger in the sideboard was something else. The ledger was Elodie's, though no one on earth knew it, though it had no owner's name in it, though it was only a school exercise book with a marbled cover that she had bought for a penny at the stationer's and ruled herself, column by column, in the lamplit hours after Marguerite went to bed.

*Name. Date. Charge. Where held. What became of them.*

Mrs. Ada Quill, weaver's wife, arrested outside the mill gates, fourteen days, released with a warning. Miss Ruth Calloway, schoolteacher, dismissed by the education board for attending a open-air meeting, three children, no income. Mrs. Brennan. The Ferrier sisters. A girl of seventeen whose name Elodie had taken from an intercepted letter and whose family had been told, when they inquired, that no such prisoner was held at any such facility—and Elodie had written in the margin, in her small upright hand, *they are lying, and I have the letter to prove it.*

"You will open that sideboard," Major Hessler said.

Marguerite looked at Elodie. It was half a second, no longer—a glance like a hand pressed flat against a door, holding it shut. Then she took the key from Elodie's stiff fingers and turned it herself, and stood aside, and let them take it.

They took everything. The correspondence files. The subscription lists of *The Dawn*, which was not even illegal, which was a newspaper. The bundle of letters from soldiers' wives that Marguerite had been answering with her own money for two years. A sergeant carried the sideboard's contents out in an ammunition crate and Elodie watched the marbled exercise book go into it between a sheaf of pamphlets and Marguerite's address book, and her heart did not pound; it stopped, entirely, a held bell, and did not start again until the trucks pulled away at noon and the silence came back wrong, too large, the way a room is too large when the clock in it has stopped.

"Elodie." Marguerite's voice, from the doorway of the back room. "Come here."

Marguerite was packing a small valise with the unhurried movements of a woman who had done this before. Two dresses. Her mother's miniature. A tin of tooth powder.

"They're taking me to the garrison headquarters for questioning," she said. "It's a formality. It may be a long formality. You will stay on as staff—the officer asked if the housekeeper would be remaining, and I said yes, that you were indispensable and entirely uninterested in politics."

"Marguerite—"

"Listen to me." She took Elodie's face in both hands, and her palms were cold from the valise handle. "They see a servant. I have spent forty years making men see exactly what I wish them to see, and you, my dear, were born with the talent. They will not watch you. They will talk in front of you. They will hand you papers to carry and forget you are holding them. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

Elodie understood. Elodie had understood for eight months, since the first night she'd read a letter not addressed to her—a soldier's letter home, left in the hall's post-basket, describing the weaver's wife being dragged by her hair—and had sat down at the kitchen table with a pencil and a penny exercise book because the names were going to be lost, because no one was writing them down, because *someone has to keep the record*.

"You are not to be brave," Marguerite said. "Bravery is a luxury for people who are noticed. You are to be dull. You are to be invisible. You are to be exactly what they think you are, until the day you are not. Do you hear me?"

"Yes," Elodie said.

Marguerite kissed her forehead, once, like a mother or a general, and was gone in the second truck.

* * *

The garrison moved in that afternoon the way water moves into a hull—through every opening at once. Cots in the meeting hall. A sentry at the door who nodded to Elodie and called her *missus* and asked, on the second day, whether she might mend a button. She mended the button. She mended buttons all that week, and cooked for twelve men who said *thank you, missus* and never once looked at her face, and every night she lay in her narrow room above the kitchen and listened to the sentry's boots below the window and did not sleep.

Because the ledger was gone, but the ledger was not lost.

It was in her. That was the thing she understood on the third night, sitting up in the dark with her heart going like a trapped bird. Eight months of entries, copied and recopied in her own memory as she'd written them—she had never needed the book to recall them. She could see the pages. She could see Mrs. Quill's chapped hands and the date of the Ferrier sisters' hearing and the exact phrasing of the letter that proved the army was lying about the girl of seventeen, whose name was Nell Pargetter, who had a mole like a comma beneath her left eye according to her mother's desperate note, which Elodie had also read, and also remembered.

She got up. She took the stub of pencil from the jar by the sink and a sheet of the cheap brown wrapping paper from the stores, and she knelt on the floor with the paper flat against the cold flagstones, because the table lamp showed under the door, and she wrote:

*Quill, Ada. 14 Nov. Disturbing the peace. 14 days. Released.*

And beneath it: *Calloway, Ruth. Dismissed 2 Dec. No hearing. Three children.*

And beneath that, in letters smaller than her natural hand, smaller than she had ever written anything: *Pargetter, Nell, 17. Held without charge since 9 Jan. Army denies custody. FALSE.*

She wrote until her knees ached and the pencil was a sliver, and then she folded the paper into eighths and sewed it—she had thread and a needle, she was the housekeeper, sewing was her alibi—into the lining of her winter coat, beneath the left arm, where a seam already bulged with mending.

Then she burned nothing, because there was nothing to burn. The evidence of the copying was a memory. The evidence of the memory was a coat.

In the morning she made porridge for twelve men and asked the sergeant, with shy concern, whether there was any news of poor Miss Ash, and the sergeant, not looking up from his plate, said the questioning was ongoing, and Major Hessler, passing through, said to the adjutant, not lowering his voice because why would he, she was staff: "The woman will give us the names herself eventually. They always do. And if she doesn't, we have her lists."

Elodie carried his plate to him and set it down and said, "Will there be anything else, sir," and he said no, and did not see her at all.

That night she added a new entry, the first of a new kind: *Ash, Marguerite. Detained 4 Feb. Charge: none given. Held: garrison HQ. Note: she told me to be dull. I intend to be the dullest woman in England until it is time not to be.*

She sewed the paper back into her coat. And as she pressed the seam flat with her thumb, she heard, through the floorboards, the sentry below shift his weight, and pause, and shift again—and she lay still in the dark and understood that the next part would not be copying names at all.

It would be moving them. Across the river, through three checkpoints, before the ice broke.

She had four weeks, one coat, and a seam.
