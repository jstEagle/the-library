## 3. Three Checkpoints

The first checkpoint was a sawhorse and a bored corporal.

Elodie drove the garrison's own cart, because nothing moved through a requisitioned town more safely than the army's property, and the army's property included its laundry. Sacks of sheets and shirts stacked behind her seat, stamped with the broad black arrows of the War Office, smelling of lye and steam. She had learned in four weeks of pretending loyalty that the surest disguise was usefulness. No one searched a woman carrying the major's linens. No one even looked at her.

"Pass," said the corporal, waving her through without rising from his brazier.

She nodded the way dull women nodded—apologetic, half-smiling—and clicked her tongue at the horse, and did not let her breath out until the sawhorse was two hundred yards behind her and the river road opened ahead, white and empty under a sky the colour of dishwater.

In the lining of her coat, against her ribs, thirty-one names lay stitched flat as a second heartbeat.

* * *

Tomas Reeve had been at the second checkpoint eleven days, and he had learned that the war, this far from France, consisted mostly of cold.

He stood at the bridge over the millrace with his rifle slung and his hands tucked in his armpits, watching carts come down the river road and reading the lists Sergeant Halloway read aloud. *No contraband. No printed matter. No persons on the wanted roster.* The roster hung inside the sentry box, pasted to the boards at eye level, and Tomas had read it so many times he could recite it in order: organisers, printers, a woman who ran a lending library, a schoolteacher dismissed for teaching unauthorised history.

That last one he knew. Not knew—*knew*. Miss Prudence Achebe, Form Three. She had caned him once for fighting and then sat with him afterward on the school steps and asked whether the fight had been worth what it cost, and he had thought about that question for twenty years, or nine years, which came to nearly the same thing at his age.

Her name was on the wanted roster between a bomb-maker nobody had ever found and a printer nobody had ever seen, and beneath it, in Halloway's careful hand: *apprehend and hold for tribunal.*

"You're staring at that list again," said Dilley, the other sentry, from the warmth of the brazier.

"I'm reading it."

"You've memorised it."

Tomas said nothing. Down the road, small against the snow, a cart was coming.

* * *

The second checkpoint stood where the road narrowed onto the old bridge, and Elodie saw it a quarter mile off and began, calmly, while still driving, to die a little.

Two sentries. A proper barrier this time, not a sawhorse but a timber gate on hinges. And a roster board visible even at distance—the pale rectangle of paper that meant names, that meant looking, that meant someone would study her face while she lied about it.

She had rehearsed. Major Corbett's laundry, due at the hospital annex by four, late already, and here was the trick Marguerite had taught her without ever meaning to: *a lie is a garment, Elodie. It fits best when you have worn it so long you've forgotten you're wearing it.* So she wore her dullness like an old coat over the new coat, over the seam, and pulled the cart up at the barrier with the mild irritation of a woman who has been delayed once too often today.

"Open it up," said the older sentry, the sergeant, not unkindly.

"The linen?"

"All of it. New orders since Tuesday." He was already lifting a sack, plunging his arm in to the shoulder. "Printed matter, miss. They think the river carries pamphlets now, like it carries fish."

The younger one—barely a man, wind-chapped, rifle slung wrong—stood by her elbow and did not help. He was looking at her. Not searching; *looking*, the way you look at a word on a list you are trying to remember.

"Hospital annex," he said. "You're the laundry woman."

"I'm whoever the major says I am, love, and the major says I'm late." She heard her own voice do it perfectly, the weary local vowels, the small contempt for soldiers that made sergeants smile instead of suspect. The older man laughed and dropped the sack.

But the young one said, quietly, "Take your coat off a minute."

The world went very narrow and very loud, the way the millrace sounded under the bridge. Elodie kept her hands on the reins. Thirty-one names against her ribs. *Ash, Marguerite. Achebe, Prudence.* A whole parish of women folded flat and sewn shut.

"Tomas," the sergeant said, warning in it.

"It's the orders, Sergeant. Coats and collars, all parties on foot or driving." He recited it like a boy reciting scripture, wanting it to be true, wanting cover. "I don't make them up."

"Then search her collar yourself and stop making a pantomime." The sergeant turned back to his sacks.

And the boy stepped close—close enough that she could smell wet wool and gun oil—and his fingers touched her collar, and she understood, in the half-second before he spoke, that he was not going to search her. That whatever he had seen in her face or she in his, they were standing inside the same narrow place now, two people alone in a crowd of two.

"Collar's clean," he said, stepping back. "Coat's heavy for March, though. Best keep it on. Cold by the water."

It was not permission. There was no such thing, with the sergeant ten feet away. But it was a sentence built like a door left unlatched, and she walked through it: gathered the reins, murmured something about the major's shirts, and waited while the gate groaned open.

She did not look back at him. Looking back was how people drowned.

* * *

Tomas watched the cart cross the bridge and told himself he had done nothing.

He had felt the seam. That was the truth he would carry to bed and turn over all night like a stone: his thumb had found the ridge of it along the coat's lining, straight and deliberate, a seam that had been opened and closed again by hand, recently, badly, by someone with no needle but plenty of reason. And under his thumb, through the cloth, paper. Many papers.

Miss Achebe's name hung inside the sentry box six feet away.

He could call out. One word—*Halt*—and the sergeant would turn, and the woman would be face-down in the snow with her coat torn open, and whatever was in that seam would go into the tribunal file next to a schoolteacher's name, and Tomas would have done his duty, and he would spend the rest of his life being the boy at the bridge.

She had not flinched. That was what undid him—not courage exactly, but the quality of it: she had stood there with her whole future in a seam-rip's distance and offered the sergeant her mildest face, and only her eyes had gone somewhere else, somewhere he couldn't follow, and he had recognised the look because he saw it every day in his own reflection when he checked the roster.

"Halt," he might say. Any second. He was a soldier. Soldiers halted things.

The cart reached the far end of the bridge. The gate creaked shut behind it.

"Tea," said Dilley. "You look grey."

Tomas went to the brazier. His hands would not stop shaking until he wrapped them around the tin cup, and even then, even warmed, they kept a fine tremble, like a needle finding its way through cloth.

* * *

The third checkpoint was not a checkpoint at all. It was Captain Voss, waiting at the ferry landing with four men and no brazier, and Elodie knew it was finished before she had fully reined in, because Voss did not ask for papers. He asked for her coat.

"The major's laundry," he said pleasantly, "was delivered this morning."

Snow fell in the silence that followed, fat and slow, flakes big enough to see the structure of. Elodie sat very still. Across the dark river, on the far bank, a lamp burned in a window—a journalist's lamp, a printing-office lamp, a lamp lit for thirty-one names that were never going to cross the water tonight.

"Off you get," said Voss.

She got down. Her legs held, which surprised her. A soldier took the coat from her shoulders with terrible gentleness, and the cold came through her blouse at once, and she thought, absurdly, of Marguerite telling her to be dull, and of how she had failed, at the end, to be anything of the kind.

Voss slit the seam with a penknife in one long pull, unhurried, a man opening an envelope. Papers slid into the snow. He picked up the topmost sheet, tilted it toward the lantern, and read aloud, in the flat voice of officialdom:

"*Achebe, Prudence. Dismissed 12 December. Charge: sedition in education.*"

He looked up at her then, and smiled the way ice sounds when it begins to crack.

"Well," he said. "You'd better come and sign something."

Behind him, out on the road, a figure in a sentry's greatcoat stood at the edge of the lamplight, not moving—too far away to be surely seen, too near not to be. Elodie met the shape of him with her eyes and gave him nothing: no plea, no name, no glance at the papers in the snow.

Then she lifted her chin, and walked past the lantern toward the boat landing and the cell beyond it, leaving thirty-one names lying in the drifts like seeds sown by the wrong hand, at the wrong season, in ground everyone believed was frozen—

—and the river, under its skin of ice, had already begun, in the deep dark below, to move.
