## 4. Before the Ice Breaks

The river did not break all at once. It groaned first—a long tectonic complaint beneath the ice that woke me in the grey hours and would not let me sleep again. I lay in the narrow bed above the print shop on the far bank and listened to the winter loosening its grip, and I thought: *it is in time. It is in time.*

Three days earlier, Tomas had come across the ice at dusk with the copy sewn against his chest where a soldier carries his paybook, and he had not stopped walking until he reached the door of the *Northern Argus*. He was seventeen. His hands shook so badly that Mr. Calloway the editor had to pour him tea before he could speak, and when he finally did, he said only: "There's a woman named Hestia Brandt on your list. She taught me to read. They dismissed her for teaching soldiers' letters back to their mothers." He looked at his tea as though it might testify for him. "I stood at that checkpoint four months. Nobody ever told me what we were guarding them from."

I had sewn that copy myself by lamplight, stitch by stitch into the lining of my coat, my fingers cramping around the needle, Marguerite's voice low at my shoulder: *Even stitches, Elodie. Even stitches. A seam betrays itself by its haste.* When the soldiers took me at the third checkpoint they found the coat, but by then there were two coats, two copies, and the second had gone out folded inside a consignment of hymnals bound for the garrison chapel—God's own smuggling, Marguerite called it, though she did not smile when she said it.

They held me eleven days. I will not write down those days except to say that the cell was cold and the questions were patient, and that I learned how long a person can say *I am only staff* before the words wear through like old cloth.

* * *

### From the Hearing Record, Assizes Hall, 14 March 1917

*THE CHAIRMAN: You are charged under the Defence of the Realm Act with the collection and transmission of documents prejudicial to military operations. Do you understand the charge?*

*A VOICE FROM THE PUBLIC GALLERY: We understand it better than you do.*

*(Disturbance. The gallery is cleared of all persons except accredited press.)*

*THE CHAIRMAN: The prosecution will present the confiscated ledger.*

*MR. HALLOWAY, FOR THE CROWN: The document before the court is a handwritten register of names, occupations, and dates of arrest or dismissal. The defence will concede its authorship is disputed.*

*MISS ASH'S COUNSEL: It is not disputed that the register exists. What is disputed, sir, is whether a list of names can be seditious when every name upon it belongs to a woman the Crown itself has already punished. We do not intend to dispute the document. We intend to enter it—and everyone named in it—as evidence for the defence.*

*THE CHAIRMAN: That is highly irregular.*

*COUNSEL: So, sir, is the war on schoolteachers.*

* * *

They brought us into the Assizes Hall through a side door because the front steps were crowded. I had imagined the hearing a hundred times in the cell, and in none of those imaginings had there been this much light—the tall windows throwing pale March sun across the benches, catching dust, catching the faces of women packed shoulder to shoulder in the gallery until the sergeant-at-arms gave up trying to count them.

Marguerite sat beside our counsel, upright as a fence post, her detained-for-questioning pallor gone and something harder in its place. She caught my eye once across the well of the court and did not nod, did not smile. She simply looked at me the way she looked at a room she was about to organize.

Then the names began.

Hestia Brandt took the stand in her good coat with the mended cuff and described, in the flat careful voice of a woman reading aloud to a slow pupil, the morning two soldiers arrived at her classroom and removed her in front of thirty children. She did weep, once, but she finished her sentence first, and I saw the chairman look away.

Marta Kessler, the weaver's wife whose husband's arrest had opened my ledger's very first page, testified with her infant asleep against her shoulder because no one in the town would take the baby and she refused to leave him with anyone who would. When the prosecutor suggested her husband had been lawfully detained under emergency provisions, she said, "Yes. And I was lawfully evicted, and my children lawfully went hungry, and Mrs. Winterbourne lawfully wrote down the date it happened. Which of these things should have been kept secret?"

The hall made a sound then—not applause, which the bailiffs would have silenced, but something lower and more terrible: a long collective exhale, like wind moving through a mill.

And so it went, name after name rising out of my small dark book and putting on flesh. The laundress. The telegraph clerk. The girl of nineteen who had been dismissed from the munitions canteen for asking why the wounded were counted before they were paid. Each one walked to the stand past the Crown's counsel, past the garrison officers in the front row, and each one became, in the telling, not evidence of sedition but a witness to everything the sedition had been about. By the second afternoon the prosecutor had stopped standing to object. He sat with his papers squared in front of him like a man waiting out weather he cannot argue with.

On the third day our counsel called me.

* * *

### From the Hearing Record

*COUNSEL: Will you state your name for the record?*

*I had rehearsed this moment in the cell. I had prepared the phrase that would keep me small: a household employee, a typist, merely staff. It rose in my throat like a stone I had swallowed weeks ago.*

*"Elodie," I said. "Elodie Winterbourne."*

*THE CHAIRMAN: You are aware you are not obliged to incriminate yourself?*

*"I am aware," I said, "that for six years I read other people's letters. Their husbands' letters from the front. Their dismissal notices. Their eviction orders. Somebody had to keep the record, sir, and nobody else was keeping it. So I wrote the names down. Every one. In my own hand. And I will sign that hand now, in the open, if the court requires it."*

*The pen scratched. It sounded very loud.*

*THE CHAIRMAN: For the record, the witness has signed the register in question and identified it as her own work.*

*"Not the register," I said. "The ledger. The army seized a ledger and called it evidence. But it was never theirs. It was theirs—" and I turned, and gestured at the gallery full of faces, "—before it was anyone's."*

* * *

The verdicts came down like thaw itself: unevenly, with cracking and grinding, but coming. Marguerite's charges were quashed on the second ground—publication having established, as the appeal judge put it with visible distaste, that the contents of the ledger were "a matter of legitimate public concern improperly suppressed rather than improperly disclosed." My own case was suspended pending review, then quietly dropped; a state that has been embarrassed in open court does not care to be embarrassed twice over a typist. Tomas was not so lucky. He lost his uniform, his pay, and his family's good opinion, in roughly that order. Marguerite found him work setting type at the *Argus*. He is clumsy at it and cheerful about being clumsy, which is more than either of us managed at seventeen.

Mr. Calloway printed the ledger complete in a special supplement—names, dates, dismissals, arrests, the whole architecture of a town quietly dismantled—and ran it under a single line of headline: **THESE WOMEN EXIST.** It sold out by noon. He printed it again. It sold out again. Somewhere in the war offices south of us, I am told, a memorandum now circulates recommending that in future such records be destroyed rather than confiscated. We framed the clipping in the meeting hall, over the mantel, next to Hestia Brandt's portrait.

On the last evening of March, the whole of us went down to the river—the meeting hall emptied onto the bank like a drawer tipped out, Marta with the baby, Hestia with her mended cuff, Tomas with ink to his wrists, Marguerite with her cane she pretended she didn't need, and me. The ice went at dusk, with a sound like a door giving way: great plates tilting up white and blue, grinding downstream in the brown flood water, carrying the winter off toward the sea piece by piece.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Marguerite said, without turning, "You know what happens now. There will be more names. There are always more names."

"I know," I said. I had a new ledger in my coat pocket. I had bought it myself, in daylight, at the stationer's on the square, and asked the price aloud like any ordinary customer. "I've started one already."

She looked at me then, and whatever she saw in my face made her nod once, the way she nodded at a room that was ready.

We stood on the bank while the river carried the ice away, all of us together, visible from either shore, our names known.
