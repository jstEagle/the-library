## 1. The Weight of Small Hands

The cold came up through the floorboards first, then through the boots, then into the bones of the feet themselves, until Rosalind Amberdale could no longer say where the warehouse ended and she began. By the second week of February in the year 1847, she had stopped thinking of her body as separate from the Corvane Trading Company's main storeroom. Her hands belonged to the tally-book. Her breath belonged to the lantern-light. The rest of her — whatever was left over, whatever still dreamed at night — belonged to nobody at all.

She stood at the long cedar counter near the river doors with a quill worn down to a stub and a ledger open before her, counting. Barrels of salted whitefish in from the ice-fishers: fourteen, fifteen, sixteen. Bolts of woolen cloth stacked to her shoulder on pallets: nine, ten, eleven. Crates stenciled with the company's mark — a black swan swallowing its own wing — containing goods she was not permitted to open or name: forty-one, forty-two. She wrote the numbers small and neat because Mr. Harrow, the factor, liked them small and neat. Small numbers took up less room in the world. Small numbers were easier to forget.

That was the whole art of her position, though no one had ever taught it to her aloud. She had learned it the way children learn the shapes of clouds, by watching until watching became knowing. Her father had drowned owing the company four hundred and twelve pounds, and debts like that did not die with a man; they sank into his family like water into wool. The company had been generous, everyone said so. They had taken his daughter off the streets at thirteen and given her honest work for honest wages, if one could call three shillings a month and a cot behind the kitchen wall honest. They had made her invisible, and invisibility was its own wage, paid out nightly in the currency of being left alone.

Rosalind had memorized the rules by her first winter, and she recited them now in her head as she counted, a rosary without comfort in it:

Don't speak first. Don't stand where you can be seen wanting something. Don't look at the director's chair as though it could ever be sat in by the likes of you. Don't want loudly.

*Don't want loudly.* That was the hardest one. Wanting was the only thing she owned outright, and she had so much of it stored up that some nights she thought it might burst through her ribs like spring ice.

"Amberdale." Harrow's voice came from the far end of the storeroom, thin as a nail drawn across slate. "The fish count again. You lost your place. I watched you lose it."

"No, sir." She had not lost it. Sixteen barrels. But she said, "Sixteen barrels," and began the line again from the top of the page with the quill trembling only slightly, because arguing with Harrow was like arguing with the cold — technically possible, practically pointless, and certain to make things worse.

He moved along the aisle between pallets, checking seals with a gloved thumb, and Rosalind let her eyes drop to the ledger while he passed. In the margin of yesterday's page, very faint, in letters so small they might have been flaws in the paper, she had written *Elias Amberdale, pearl diver, d. 1844, owed nothing that was not repaid twice over.* She had written it there the way other people scratched prayers into church pews — not to be read, only to exist somewhere outside her own skull. If Harrow ever found it he would dock her wages for the ink. She wrote it anyway. Some mornings that line in the margin was the only proof that her father had ever stood upright in the world.

Outside, Hallowmere creaked. That was the sound the town made all winter — a long wooden groaning, houses contracting, masts complaining in their rigging, and beneath everything the frozen river muttering in its sleep. The Corvane warehouse sat at the very edge of it all, last building before the docks, close enough to the water that in summer the spray came up over the loading dock and silvered the first row of crates. Rosalind knew every board of that dock by its particular complaint. She had hauled cargo across it since she was light enough not to bend the planks.

She finished the fish count, signed the page — *R.A., clerk, per instruction of F. Harrow* — and carried the ledger to his desk at the front of the room. The walk took her past the tall windows facing the river, and she permitted herself, as she always did, exactly three seconds of looking out.

The river was white to the far bank, a road of dead ice running away north until it vanished in haze. Somewhere under all that whiteness lay the beds where her father had dove for pearls, holding his breath in water dark as spilled ink, feeling along the cold mud with bare fingers. He had been good at it. He had been the best in Hallowmere, people said, able to stay down longer than any man on the river. And in the end the skill made no difference; the river took him the way rivers take everyone eventually, patiently, keeping what is given. Four hundred and twelve pounds of debt had come ashore in his place, wearing his coat.

Three seconds. She turned back to the counter. Beyond the window, gulls walked on the ice like opinions nobody had asked for.

* * *

At midday Marta sent up bread and dripping on a tin plate, as she did every day, carried by nobody — just set on the corner of the counter when Rosalind wasn't looking, appearing the way warm things appear in that building, suddenly and without explanation. Marta ran the company kitchen and the company cook's temper, and she had decided within a month of Rosalind's arrival that the girl did not eat enough, an opinion she enforced without ever once speaking of it. The bread simply arrived. Rosalind ate it simply. Neither of them acknowledged the arrangement, and this silence had long ago become its own kind of tenderness.

"You're wanted in the lamp room after rounds," said one of the porters, passing, not unkindly. "The old man asks."

Isandro asked. Rosalind felt something loosen slightly under her ribs, the way a knot loosens when you stop pulling at it. Isandro was the lamplighter, ancient and brown as walnut wood, keeper of the forty-one oil lanterns that hung through the warehouse and dock and lamp room at the end of the pier. It was his ritual, every dusk, to go the rounds with a taper and a brass can of oil, and for reasons he had never explained he had adopted Rosalind three winters ago the way a tree adopts a fence post — gradually, completely, and with total indifference to whether anyone approved.

She finished the afternoon counts. She endured Harrow's inspection, during which he ran one finger along the ledger's edge looking for dust and found none, which visibly disappointed him. Then the bell rang the end of the working day, and the storeroom exhaled its men into the blue evening, and Rosalind went first to the kitchen cellar to see Tam.

Tam was eleven, and he coughed. That was the plainest way to say it, and Rosalind tried always to think plainly about Tam because fancy thoughts made her afraid. He had their mother's fine bones and their father's habit of watching everything, and since November he had kept the cot in the cellar beside the coal bins because walking upstairs winded him. The company doctor charged five shillings a visit. Five shillings was nearly two months of Rosalind's wages. So Marta dosed him with goose fat and honey, and Rosalind read to him from a shipping catalogue because it was the only book in the building she could borrow without asking, and the winter went on being winter.

"How many today?" Tam asked from under the blanket, meaning barrels, because he kept her counts in his head the way other boys kept marbles.

"Sixteen fish, eleven cloth, forty-two sealed." She sat on the edge of the cot. His forehead was warm but not hot. Not yet hot. She held his hand and pretended she was only resting it there.

"That's low for February."

"The river's frozen solid. Nothing moves till the break-up." She said it lightly, and heard how lightly, and hated herself for it, because they both knew what the break-up meant. When the ice went out of the river, Hallowmere flooded — not catastrophically, most years, but enough that the roads south turned to porridge and the last boat upriver departed before the first crack showed. After that boat, the town was sealed for months, shut in with itself and its fevers until summer. Everyone in Hallowmere lived by that arithmetic. Every winter ended with someone standing on the dock watching the last boat go, and every winter the watchers were divided into two kinds: those going, and those staying.

"I'm fine," Tam said, which was what he always said, and he looked at her while he said it, which was what he always did, and the looking was worse than the coughing because Tam had never once in his life managed to lie with his eyes.

"You'll come up to the lamp room after," she told him. "Isandro's got a new wick he wants to show off. Says it burns twice as bright on half the oil, which I personally doubt."

"Tell him I doubt it too." A small smile, quickly swallowed by another cough. She waited it out with her hand flat on his back, counting the rise and fall the way she counted barrels, and when it passed she kissed the top of his head where the hair grew in a double crown like their father's, and went up into the cold.

* * *

Dusk in Hallowmere in February lasted approximately as long as a held breath. One moment the sky was the color of a bruise, the next it was nothing at all, and the town existed only as lamplight — little yellow islands strung along streets and docks, each one a promise somebody had kept. Rosalind met Isandro at the foot of the pier with his taper already lit, and together they began the rounds.

It was the best hour of her day and she would have died before saying so.

They worked mostly in silence, because Isandro believed silence was the proper condition for lighting lamps, the way sleep was the proper condition for dreaming. He lifted each glass chimney with two fingers, trimmed the wick, checked the oil level against a notch filed into the reservoir, touched the taper to the wick, and waited — always waited — until the flame stood up straight and sure before moving on. Forty-one lanterns. Rosalind carried the oil can and the spare chimneys wrapped in flannel, and she had long ago memorized which lanterns sulked in damp weather (numbers nine, twenty-three, and thirty) and which burned greedy (all of them, in Isandro's opinion).

"Lantern thirty," Isandro said tonight, pausing at the dock's end where the last lamp swung above the black shelf of the river. "Look at her."

Rosalind looked. The flame inside was bent sideways by wind through the glass seams, guttering, throwing shadows that leapt and collapsed and leapt again across the ice.

"What about her?"

"She fights the wind wrong." Isandro opened the housing and adjusted something invisible to Rosalind's eye. "A flame that fights dies. A flame that bends, lives. You want to remember that, girl. Comes in useful more than lamplighting does."

"For what?"

"For whatever comes." He closed the housing and the flame steadied, and his face in the new light was a landscape of creases with two sharp dark eyes set into it, missing nothing. He had been looking at her strangely all evening, she realized. Weighing something.

"You asked for me," she said. "Porter said."

"Mm." He took the oil can from her hand and hung it on his shoulder, which meant the lesson portion of the evening was concluded and the sitting portion was about to begin. There was a bench, or what had once been a bench, at the landward end of the dock, sheltered from the worst of the wind, and they sat on it as they had sat perhaps a hundred times before. Below them the frozen river lay silent and enormous. Above, the first stars were coming out hard and clean.

"You've grown," Isandro said.

"Not since autumn."

"Grown anyway. Inside, I mean." He waved a hand vaguely at his own chest. "In there. You get that look, girls your age. Like a house that's been locked all winter with the fire lit. Something's cooking and nobody's home to watch it."

"That's a terrible thing to say to a person."

"It's a true thing. Terrible's usually true." He leaned back against the rail, unhurried, a man who had made peace with time to the degree that time seemed to have made peace with him back. For a while neither spoke. The lamp overhead bent and steadied, bent and steadied.

Then Isandro said, quietly, in the tone he used for the truest things: "Ice'll break early this year. Early and loud. You mark me."

"You can know that? From here?"

"I've lit these lamps over this river for forty-one years, girl. The river talks to whoever stands over it long enough. It's talking now." He nodded downward, toward the vast pale sheet of it. "Underneath. All winter it's been muttering, but lately—" he tilted his head, listening to a sound Rosalind could not hear at all— "lately it's changed its tune. Something's coming up that water before the flood, and after the flood there'll be no coming and no going for months. Last boat leaves before the first crack shows. Same as every year."

Rosalind pulled her coat tighter, though the cold was not what had made her shiver. "There's talk of berths, then. Petitions."

"There's always talk." He looked at her sidelong, and his old eyes were kind and terrible at once. "And there's always arithmetic. One berth, girl, come the break-up. One seat on the last boat out of Hallowmere. You'd best start deciding who needs it more than anyone else alive."

She thought of Tam asleep in the cellar, breathing in shallow careful measures around the ache in his chest. She thought of herself, sixteen years old, four hundred and twelve pounds deep in a debt she hadn't made, with nothing ahead but ledgers and lanterns and the same gray days repeating until the gray days were all she was.

"I don't petition," she said. "Clerks don't. Indentured clerks especially don't. Rule number—"

"I know what the rules are." Isandro stood, joints cracking like the river's smaller cousin, and took up his taper. "I taught myself most of them too. Here's the thing nobody tells you about rules, though." He lit the next lantern on the pier, waited for the flame to stand. "Rules are just walls somebody built. Useful against weather. Useless against wanting. And you, Rosalind Amberdale, want louder than anyone I've ever lit a lamp beside. You just do it quiet."

They finished the rounds. Forty-one lanterns burning against the dark, each one a small stubborn argument that the darkness should not entirely win, and Rosalind walked back along the dock toward the warehouse with the oil can empty against her hip and Isandro's words lodged under her ribs like a splinter working its way toward the surface.

The loading dock stood empty in the lamplight, snow drifting across it in long fine feathers, the river beyond it white and waiting and whispering in a language she almost — but not quite — could hear.

One berth. One boat. Before the first crack showed.

She was halfway to the door when she stopped, because she had heard something else beneath the wind's feathering and the river's mutter — something small and thin and impossibly out of place, rising from the dark end of the dock.

It sounded, though it could not possibly be, like crying.
