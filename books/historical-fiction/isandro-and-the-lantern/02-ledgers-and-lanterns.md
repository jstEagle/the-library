## 2. Ledgers and Lanterns

The last tally of the day was always the hardest, not because the numbers grew difficult but because the light did. By four o'clock in January the windows of the Corvane warehouse had gone the color of pewter, and the ink in my ledger pot began to thicken like something dying. I would hold the pen over the page and watch my own breath ghost across the columns, blurring figures I had already written, as though even the air of Hallowmere wanted to unmake the record of what we owned.

Owed, rather. What we owned fit in a tin box under Tam's cot: our mother's shawl, a hair comb with three teeth missing, and a river stone shaped like a heart that Father had pried from the shallows the summer before he drowned. Everything else — the roof over us, the coal in our grate, the bread on our table — belonged to the Corvane Trading Company, and the Company kept its accounts of us more carefully than I kept mine of it.

I had learned the rules by then. Don't speak first. Don't want loudly. Walk close to the walls so the clerks' shoulders have room to pass you by. A girl whose father died owing the Company forty-one pounds, twelve shillings is not a person; she is an entry, and entries do well to be legible and quiet.

But there was one hour of the day when I did not have to be either.

* * *

At half past four, when the foreman Mr. Hobb rang the brass bell that released the day shift, I stayed behind. This was permitted — indeed, it was required, though no one had ever said so aloud — because the storerooms must be lit before dark settled into them fully, and the lamplighter came at half past four, and the lamplighter was Isandro.

He arrived the way winter arrives: without announcement, suddenly simply present. One moment the loading dock stood empty, snow hissing against the shutters; the next, Isandro was there with his long pole over his shoulder and his coat the color of wet bark, stamping the ice from his boots.

"You've let the cold in ahead of me again," he said, which was his greeting every evening, and I answered, as I answered every evening, "The cold doesn't wait for permission," and he grunted as though this were wisdom newly minted, though he had heard it two hundred times.

Isandro was old the way the river is old — not frail, but worn smooth, all his sharp edges carried off somewhere downstream years ago. His hands were enormous, scarred at the knuckles, and they moved among the lanterns with a gentleness that seemed borrowed from somewhere far outside him. There were thirty-one lanterns in the Corvane storerooms. I knew because I counted them the first week, out of habit, the way I counted everything: crates, barrels, bolts of cloth, days owed. Thirty-one lanterns, and Isandro could light them in the dark better than most men could light them by daylight.

"Come, then, clerk-girl," he said, and handed me the taper.

That was the ritual. He never let me merely watch. The first lantern — the one by the door of the salt storeroom, where the crystals glittered like a wall of trapped stars — he always lit himself, cupping the flame against drafts with both hands as if sheltering a small animal. But the second he passed to me, and the third, and after that we moved through the building together in a slow procession, room by room, aisle by aisle, while the darkness retreated before us like a tide going out.

There is a particular quality to lamplight in a warehouse. Daylight shows you things as they are — the dust, the rat droppings, the water stains on the ceiling. Lamplight shows you things as they might be. The bales of indigo became sleeping animals, blue-flanked and breathing. The coils of rope became sea serpents curled in their pens. The great glass demijohns of molasses caught the flame and held it, so that walking down that aisle felt like walking between two rows of small captive suns, each one trembling when I passed.

"Mind the third wick," Isandro said, every night. "It drinks fast."

"I know."

"And the one in the fur loft leans left when it's low. You'll think it's going out. It isn't. It's only thinking about it."

"I know."

He told me these things nightly anyway, and I understood, though I could not have explained it then, that the repetition was the point. It was not instruction; it was company. Some men fill silence with talk. Isandro filled it with the same sentences, worn round and comfortable as river stones, and I came to love them the way you love the creak of a familiar stair.

We worked the whole route in perhaps forty minutes, and then we ended, always, in the lamp room — a narrow closet at the building's north corner where the spare wicks and oil cans lived and where Isandro kept a stool, a kettle, and a tin of biscuits that were, without fail, slightly stale. He brewed tea black enough to stand a spoon in and poured it into two chipped cups, and we sat shoulder to shoulder beneath the single lamp that never went out, the pilot flame, the one that lit all the others.

"How's the boy?" he asked, most nights.

"Coughing."

"Worse or same?"

I turned my cup a quarter turn on my knee. "Worse."

He nodded slowly, and did not say anything foolish. That was another thing about Isandro: he knew the difference between comfort and noise.

* * *

Tam was eleven that winter, all elbows and fever, lying in the room we rented above the cooper's shop with the blanket pulled to his chin. The cough had come with the first hard frost in November and had settled into his chest like a tenant who paid nothing and intended to stay. In the daytime he was quiet about it — he had learned our family's rules faster than I ever had, poor child, wanting nothing loudly since he was six years old — but at night I heard him through the wall, that dry tearing sound, over and over, like paper being slowly ripped.

The Company doctor, Dr. Pell, kept rooms above the counting house and charged five shillings a visit. Five shillings was nearly two days of my wages, and my wages went first to the debt, second to the rent, third to bread, and whatever remained — some weeks a penny, some weeks nothing — went into a jar on the shelf that I had begun, secretly, to call *the doctor jar*, though it held perhaps ninepence by February and the coughing had not improved.

Once, in desperation, I asked Marta, the cook, whether there was anything in the kitchen stores —

She looked at me for a long moment with those flat grey eyes of hers, eyes that missed nothing and forgave little, and said, "Horehound grows along the south wall of the churchyard, girl, even under the snow. And honey's cheap if you know which farmer cheats on his weights." Then she turned back to her dough as though I had asked her nothing at all, and I understood that I had been given something, and that neither of us would ever name it.

So Tam drank horehound tea sweetened with dishonest honey, and I sat beside his cot reading to him from the almanac because it was the only book we owned, and he listened to the planting tables and the tide charts as though they were epics, and sometimes he laughed, a small cracked laugh that broke my heart precisely because it was so economical, so rationed, a boy spending joy the way he'd learned to spend everything — carefully, fearing the supply.

"Do you remember the story Mama told," he said one night, "about how Papa dove the last time?"

"I remember."

"Tell it wrong," he said. "You always tell it right. Tell it wrong so it comes out different."

I didn't understand him, but I tried. I told it with the pearl in the oyster the size of an egg, with Father surfacing laughing, with the boat waiting warm. And Tam listened with his eyes closed and his breath whistling, and afterward he said, "That's the one I'll keep," and slept, and I sat in the dark listening to him breathe and doing arithmetic I hated: five shillings a visit, ninepence in the jar, the ice on the river thickening by a finger's width every week.

* * *

In the lamp room, Isandro refilled the oil cans and I trimmed the spent wicks, and some nights he talked, in his slow way, of rivers he had known. He had walked lamp routes in four towns before Hallowmere, he said, and every town's river had a different voice. The one down south spoke all night, chattering. The northern one sulked under its ice and said nothing until spring, and then said everything at once.

"This one here," he said, tapping the floorboards, as though the Hallow lay directly beneath us, which in a sense it did, frozen and black and patient beyond the dock, "this one hums. You'll hear it come March. Like a hymn sung very far away. First time you hear it, you'll be afraid. Second time, you'll listen for it."

"Why will I be afraid?"

"Because it means the ice is breaking," he said, "and broken ice takes what it likes. Boats. Fences. People, some years." He said it without drama, the way he said everything, and then pushed the biscuit tin toward me. "Eat. You count everyone else's rations. Count your own once."

I ate the stale biscuit and it tasted, absurdly, like a feast, because warmth makes food of anything and company makes warmth of anything.

One night in late February — a night of hard clear cold, the kind where the stars seem nailed to the sky — I asked him why he did it. The route, the ritual, the tea. Why he treated a debtor's daughter like a niece.

Isandro took his time answering. He polished the glass of the pilot lamp with his sleeve, though it needed no polishing, and the flame doubled itself in the curve and trembled there.

"When I was younger than you," he said finally, "I had a family. Wife. A daughter." He set the lamp down. "The river keeps what it keeps. I won't tell you that story tonight, or maybe any night. But I'll tell you this much: after, I found that if I stopped lighting lamps, the dark got into me. Not around me. Into me. So I light them. Every night, thirty-one of them, and every night I sit with someone who needs sitting with, whether it's you or the cat or nobody at all." He shrugged his great slow shrug. "A man builds his family out of what habits survive him. You've got habits too, clerk-girl. You just haven't noticed them yet."

"What habits?"

"You show up," he said. "Every evening, you stay past the bell, past your wage, past your fear of Hobb's disapproval, and you walk the dark with an old man and learn which wicks drink fast. That's not duty. Duty ends at the bell." He tapped my hand with one scarred finger. "That's love wearing work clothes. Most love does."

I looked down at my hands, ink-stained, chapped at the knuckles, holding a chipped cup of terrible tea, and I did not cry, because crying was wanting loudly and I had trained myself out of it. But something in my chest shifted its weight, the way cargo shifts in a hold, settling into a truer position.

* * *

We finished the rounds later than usual that night because the wind had risen and blown out two lanterns near the loading doors, and Isandro made me relight them myself, standing close behind me to block the draft with his body, saying nothing, just being a wall against the weather. When the flames took and steadied, he nodded once, satisfied, and I felt the small fierce pride of it — a lit thing in a dark place, and my hand that lit it.

Outside, the snow had stopped. The river lay white and silent under the moon, and beyond it the town of Hallowmere huddled in its smoke, and beyond that, upriver, the world went on existing, though some nights I doubted it.

Walking home past the counting house, I saw a light still burning in the director's office window — rare, that, in February, months before inspection season — and a carriage standing in the yard with its horses steaming, as though someone important had arrived unannounced and unwelcome. I pulled my shawl closer and kept to the wall, invisible by design, and thought of Tam's cough behind me and the ninepence in the jar and the thirty-one lamps burning empty behind us, warming nothing but themselves.

I did not yet know why the director had come, or what the winter was about to put down on the Company's doorstep like a parcel addressed to no one.

But the lamps were lit. Whatever came next would come into the light.
