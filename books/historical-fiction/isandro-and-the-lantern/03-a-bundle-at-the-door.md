## 3. A Bundle at the Door

The whiteout came down on Hallowmere like a held breath finally released, and it did not release gently.

I had stayed late in the tally room that Thursday — the last Thursday of February, though the month had stopped meaning much by then; winter in Hallowmere was less a season than a country you emigrated to and could not leave. Mr. Hedger, the floor clerk, had left a crate of seal-oil tins miscounted from the morning delivery, and discrepancies were debts, and debts were mine. So I sat with my nib scratching while the windows went from grey to white to nothing at all, a blindness pressed against the glass, and I thought only of numbers: twelve tins this case, not eleven; forty cases, not thirty-nine; my father's debt standing at sixty-one pounds, fourteen shilling, threepence, a figure I knew better than my own birthday.

By the time I blotted the page, the warehouse was dark except for the lanterns Isandro had lit along the storeroom gallery before he went home, and they burned behind their glass like patient orange eyes. I wound my scarf to my eyebrows, took the tally book to its shelf, and let myself out through the side door into the end of the world.

* * *

I have tried, since, to describe that walk across the loading dock to Marta, and I have never managed it. Whiteout is not fog. Fog conceals; whiteout erases. There was no up and no down, no river and no sky, only a whiteness so total it seemed to make a sound of its own, a hiss like sand poured over sand, and my own boots were rumors beneath me. I found the dock rail by memory — six steps from the door, hand out, fingers remembering the cold iron before my eyes confirmed anything — and I stood there a moment because a person must stand somewhere, even in nothing.

The snow had piled against the rail in a soft crest, sculpted by wind into shapes like sleeping animals. And one of the sleeping animals was breathing.

I did not understand it at first. The sound was small and rhythmic, wrong for wind, wrong for the creak of ice on the river — a sound I knew the way Tam knew his own cough, something between a sigh and a hiccup, wet and alive. My lantern — I had taken the storm lantern from the hook by the door, good habits being all Isandro's teaching — threw its little circle onto the drift, and inside the circle the snow moved.

Not moved. *Breathed.* A shallow rise and fall under a skin of fresh powder, as though the drift itself had been quickened.

I dug with bare hands. I remember that my first thought — God forgive me, but honesty is the discipline of a ledger clerk — was *rat*. A rat frozen in the drift, still twitching. Then my fingers closed on wool, fine wool, wet through but fine, embroidered at the hem with something my thumb found before my eyes could — leaves, or feathers, or small birds, worked in blue thread — and then the wool moved and cried out against my palm, and the sound went through my hand and up my arm and into some chamber of my chest I had kept locked since the day they pulled my father's coat from the river, empty.

A child. An infant, days old, laid on the Corvane loading dock in the worst whiteout in three winters, wrapped in a blanket worth more than my year's wages.

* * *

What I should have done is printed in letters a foot high on the inside of my skull, where Company rules live: *Report all goods, persons, and property found upon Company premises to the duty clerk, who shall enter same in the Finding Book with time, date, and description.* I had copied the Finding Book myself in November. I knew its columns. Time, date, description, disposition. A child is a person; a person found is an entry; an entry is a trail; and trails, in Hallowmere, in winter, at the Corvane Trading Company, are followed by men whose footsteps I had learned to recognize and step out of.

So I stood on the dock with the baby against my chest and I conducted, in the whirling white, the most careful arithmetic of my life.

If I reported her: the Finding Book, the watchman, the magistrate, questions — *who left her, when, did you see anyone, girl, where were you standing* — and above all the description column, which would read *female infant, fine blanket, embroidery of birds*, and would sit in a public ledger like a lit candle beside a powder store, waiting for whoever had carried her here through a whiteout rather than anywhere else in the world to come back and snuff it.

Because someone had carried her here. That was the other thing my hands understood before I did. No mother sets a newborn on a company dock in a killing storm by accident. She had been brought — brought past the town, past the church with its charity basket and its warm question of *whose?*, past every softer door — to the one place in Hallowmere where things could be made to disappear into crates and manifests and bills of lading, where everything that entered could be entered wrongly or not at all. Whoever chose this door had known how Corvane worked. Had perhaps worked for Corvane. Or feared it. Or been it.

And tucked into the blanket at her throat, folded once, dry — someone had shielded it inside their coat, which meant someone had stood here in the white and the wind long enough to protect a piece of paper — was a note. Small, square, expensive paper, written in a hand so level it looked printed:

*Forget this face.*

No name. No sum. No plea. Not *care for her*, not *she is baptized Rosamund*, not *her birthday is the ninth*. Five words, an imperative, a command issued as though from a office upstairs, and the strange thing — the thing I turned over all that night and many nights after — is that it was not addressed to the finder. It was not an instruction to me. It was a wish, laid on the child herself like a second blanket. Forget this face. As if her face were a debt, and silence the only coin that could pay it.

The baby cried again, thin as a wire, and the arithmetic ended. Not because I solved it. Because she seized my finger with a grip that had no ledgers in it at all, and certain sums, once begun, cannot be balanced.

* * *

I do not remember crossing back to the door. I remember the warmth of the storeroom hitting us both like water, and the smell — tar and tallow, coffee, hemp, the sweet rot of oranges from the last autumn shipment gone soft in their straw — and I remember thinking, absurdly, *the lamp oil inventory will be short tonight,* because terror in a clerk wears the costume of her trade.

There was no fire in the tally room after hours; there was never anything in the tally room after hours but rules. But the counting room's stove was banked, and Hedger was a lazy man about banking stoves, which is to say a generous one without knowing it. I fed it the kindling we kept for the morning boy and got a blaze going, and I knelt on the hearthstones — a clerk does not kneel on hearthstones, a clerk stands at her desk with her eyes down — and unwrapped the blanket with hands that shook so badly I had to laugh at them, quietly, so as not to frighten her.

She was small. Smaller than Tam had been, I thought, though I was four when Tam was born and my memories of him small are really memories of our mother's arms seen from below. She was clean, recently fed, carefully dressed — a lawn gown under the blanket, real lace at the collar, and my stomach turned over, because lace means a household that could have kept her, which means the leaving of her was a decision with reasons, and reasons like that have owners, and owners like that have reach. Her face was furious and purple and perfect, a scrap of outrage barely longer than my thumb, and when the fire's heat reached her she stopped crying mid-wail, astonished, and stared at the flames with eyes the color of strong tea, and I understood with a lurch that she was staring at the first light she had ever truly seen, or the first in a long time, and that I — Rosalind Amberdale, invisible by design, debtor's daughter, don't-speak-first, don't-want-loudly — was the first person to look back.

Something happened then that I decline to call love, because love is a word with furniture in it, cushions and curtains, and what happened had no furniture at all. It was more primitive. It was the sound of a lock turning in a door I had believed welded shut since the river gave back nothing. Some part of me that had been holding very still for seven years — holding still the way you hold still when the ice is thin — simply sat down.

"Well," I said to her, and my voice came out wrong, half-strangled. "Well. You're in terrible trouble, little one. So am I. We'll suit."

She gripped my finger harder, as if signing.

* * *

I will not pretend I did not think of giving her up. Any honest account must include the hour I spent on the hearthstones doing battle with myself. Every argument for the Finding Book was correct. Correctness was my trade. I knew the cost of irregular entries better than anyone on that river: my father had made one — a dive off the books, a debt deferred one tide too long — and the river had collected, and the Company had collected, and my mother had died of the collecting, slow, the way grief collects, and Tam and I had been entered in the ledger as assets. I knew what happened to people who appeared where they were not written down.

And yet.

It was the note that decided me, in the end. Not the blanket, not the lace, not even her fist around my finger, though that helped. It was the five words themselves, sitting on the stove's warm brick like a summons. *Forget this face.* Because I had spent seven years practicing forgetting — forgetting my father's face, which I caught myself losing daily and grieving anew each time; forgetting my own want of anything louder than supper; making myself a margin note in the great ledger of Hallowmere. And I knew, kneeling there in borrowed firelight with a stranger's daughter asleep against my ribs, exactly what it costs to be forgotten on command. It was the only cruelty I was expert in.

They had thrown her away the way the river throws things up — no, worse; the river throws things up to be found. They had thrown her away the way the Company writes a loss: cleanly, correctly, in a hand so level it looks printed.

I picked up the note. I held it over the stove, and my hand hesitated — evidence, said the clerk in me; protection, said the same clerk a heartbeat later, evidence against whoever came looking — and in the end I did neither. I folded it small and sewed it into the hem of my own skirt with waxed thread from the packing bench, where it rode against my ankle for a long time after, a small stiff weight, a secret with a pulse. If ever I needed to prove she had been abandoned rather than stolen, there it would be. If ever I needed to prove who had abandoned her — the paper was good stock, the hand was practiced, and I was, whatever else, a tally clerk, and I knew quality when I held it — well. There it would be for that too.

Then I did the practical things, the ones that saved her life and nearly ended mine with worry. Hot water from the tap over the cistern, sugar and a drop of the milk Marta kept for the floor managers' tea — I measured that theft in my head as I did it, twopence, I'll owe it, I'll owe everything, apparently — and a warming pan from the kitchen passage, and the whole time my ears were stretched toward every sound in the walls, every settling beam, every shift of wind against the shutters, listening for boots.

Nobody came. The whiteout swallowed the town whole; even Quill, the night watchman, would be in his hut with the stove and the cards, cursing the weather like every man in Hallowmere. We had the night. We had, perhaps, only the night.

She slept in the second-best blanket from the shipping pallets, in the empty orange crate lined with sacking, drawn up next to the stove where the glow fell on her face, and I sat on the floor beside her with my back to the warm brick and watched her breathe and understood that I had just crossed a river on cracked ice and there was no walking back across it. Whatever came — the questions, the searches, the price — I had already paid it, here, in this ridiculous position, a bond-clerk asleep sitting up on a warehouse floor because a bundle in a crate mattered more than her name in a book.

Near dawn she woke and rooted, unsatisfied with milk-and-sugar, and wailed with a lung power that seemed impossible in something the size of a loaf, and I paced the storeroom gallery with her, under the lit lanterns, up and down past the coffee and the hemp and the seal oil, whispering nonsense, and it was Isandro's lamps lighting us both, burning steady in their glass against the last of the storm.

When the whiteout broke, near seven, it broke all at once — the wind dropped as if switched off, and the world came back: the dock, the rail, the river white to the far bank, the town's chimneys standing up out of the drifts smoking. Ordinary morning. Bells. Somewhere a cart.

Marta would be crossing the yard within the quarter hour, as she crossed it every morning, keys in her fist, breath in flags, her first stop the kitchen and her second the tally room to put bread and dripping where my elbow was and say nothing about it. Marta, who missed nothing that happened in that building from Michaelmas to Lent. Marta, whose eyes went over a person the way mine went over a manifest.

I looked down at the crated child, fed and drowsy and utterly incapable of explaining herself, and then out the frosted window at the yard, and felt the full size of what I had done settle over me like the snow had — softly, totally, erasing the path behind.

The kitchen door banged. Boots on the packed snow. Keys, jingling.
