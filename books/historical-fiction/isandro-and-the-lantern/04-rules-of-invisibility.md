## 4. Rules of Invisibility

Marta Kessler had cooked for the Corvane Trading Company for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned to read a room the way other women read scripture — by heart, by weight, by what was left out. She knew which clerks stole sugar and which stole time. She knew that Mr. Hobbins watered the vinegar and that the night watchman drank it. She knew the sound of every footstep on the kitchen stairs, so when she heard Rosalind Amberdale's boots pause on the third step at half past four in the morning — pause, and hold, the way a person holds their breath — Marta set down her knife and listened to the silence where a cough should have been.

Not Tam's cough. Not this time.

She had been up since three rendering fat for the puddings, the kitchen warm as a beast's belly around her, the great black stove ticking as it heated. Through the frost-furred window the whiteout still raged, the world beyond the glass erased to a whiteness so total it made her eyes ache to look at it. A night like that swallowed sound. A night like that could swallow a person whole and no one would be the wiser until spring thaw gave up the body.

And yet here was a girl on the stairs, holding her breath around something small.

Marta wiped her hands on her apron — a habit, the apron never clean long enough to matter — and said, without turning from her work, "There's bread on the board and tea in the brown pot. Sit down before you fall down."

The steps creaked. Did not descend.

"Rosalind."

Still nothing. But Marta heard it then, beneath the wind's long howl against the shutters: a thin, furious mewling, the sound of a creature newly arrived in the world and already protesting its terms. It came from under the girl's shawl, from the bundle she carried pressed against her chest like contraband, which of course it was.

Marta closed her eyes. Nineteen years of rules ran through her head in a single breath — *report all irregularities, admit no unauthorized persons, company property is company property* — and she watched them pass through her like weather, felt them go, and found herself still standing, still warm, still unmoved at her chopping block.

"I heard nothing," she said.

Rosalind came down the last steps slowly, as though the boards might revoke their permission. The girl was gray with cold and lack of sleep, her dark hair stiff with frost where the shawl had not covered it, and inside the wrapping — a man's flannel shirt, folded into a nest — was an infant. Red-faced, fists balled, mouth open on that thin wail that seemed too large for the small body producing it. Perhaps six weeks old. Perhaps less. Lips chapped blue at the edges.

"The loading dock," Rosalind said. Her voice cracked in the middle like river ice. "Left on the dock. In this." She held out, with her free hand, a scrap of paper gone soft with damp, the ink feathered but legible: *Forget this face.* "No blanket worth the name. No name at all. If I'd found her an hour later—"

"You'd have found her frozen," Marta finished. "Yes."

The baby wailed. The fire popped. Somewhere above them the warehouse beams settled and groaned under the weight of the snow, and the wind pressed at the windows like a beggar who would not leave.

Marta looked at the child for a long moment. She thought of things she had not let herself think of in years: a kitchen in another country, another lifetime, a sister's baby that hadn't lived through its first winter, and Marta at sixteen holding something so small and light it had seemed wrong, an affront, a debt the world owed back. She thought of the rules. She was fifty-three years old and tired to the bone of rules.

"Well," she said. "You can't feed her on paper and prayer. Give her here."

Rosalind stared at her. "You're not going to report it?"

"I'm going to warm milk," Marta said, "because unlike some people, I do my job." She held out her arms. "Give her here, girl, and mind her head."

* * *

The transfer of a baby from frightened arms to steady ones took longer than it should have, because Rosalind would not entirely let go — her fingers stayed curled in the flannel even after the weight left her chest, as if the child might dissolve without her touch. Marta understood. Some things, once picked up, cannot be set down again; the arms remember.

She settled the infant in the crook of one arm and with her free hand tested the bottle she'd kept for emergencies that had never come — a glass bottle, rubber nipple perished with age but serviceable, scalded and waiting in the cupboard's deepest shelf these eleven years. Cow's milk cut with water and a spoonful of sugar, warmed in a pot, tested against the inside of her wrist. All of it came back to her hands unbidden, the old knowledge waking like a limb gone to sleep, full of pins and needles.

"Sit," she told Rosalind, pushing her toward the stool by the stove. "Eat. You look like a drowned thing yourself."

The baby rooted, found the nipple, and latched with a desperation that made Marta's throat tighten. Such hunger. Such absolute, uncomplicated need. In nineteen years at Corvane she had fed a hundred hungry mouths and not one of them had ever been honest about it the way this child was honest — no pretense, no gratitude, just want, loud and unashamed.

"Don't want loudly," Rosalind murmured, watching the child feed. She said it the way a person recites a psalm, flat with repetition.

"What's that?"

"It's a rule. My rule. Don't speak first, don't want loudly, don't take up space you haven't paid for." Rosalind wrapped both hands around the teacup Marta had pushed at her. "I've been saying them to myself since Papa drowned. They keep you safe. Invisible people don't get collected."

Marta snorted softly. "Invisible people also don't get helped."

"Invisible people don't need helping. That's the point."

"Mm." Marta shifted the baby, who had begun to fuss, her small face screwing up. "And what does your rulebook say about this one, then? She wants loudly enough to wake the dead. Is she going to get collected?"

It was, Marta saw, the wrong way to have asked it, or perhaps exactly the right way, because Rosalind's face did something complicated — fear and ferocity arriving at once, elbowing each other for room. "Nobody is collecting anybody," the girl said. "She's mine now. I've decided."

*Decided.* As if a decision were a wall instead of a door. Marta let it lie. At sixteen you believed your will was mortar; at fifty-three you knew it was mostly weather-stripping. Still, she did not correct the girl. There would be time enough for hard truths, and tonight the truth that mattered was simpler: there was a baby in the kitchen, and the baby was alive, and alive meant hungry again in two hours, and wet, and needing warmth, and none of those things could be accomplished from the loading dock.

"She'll need feeding every two hours or so," Marta said instead. "More often, maybe. She's starved — feel the weight of her, there's hardly anything to her. And she'll need washing, and dry wrappings, and somewhere warm to sleep during the day, and someone has to be awake for the night feeds, and someone has to sleep sometime, and there are two of us." She paused. "Three, counting the boy, but he's not to know yet — sick children can't keep secrets, they talk in fevers. So. Two of us."

Rosalind was staring at her again, teacup forgotten halfway to her lips.

"You've done this before," she said.

"I've been alive before," Marta said. "It leaves marks." She stood, bounced the baby gently against her shoulder, and felt the small skull press into her jaw, smelled that particular smell of milk-sour skin and skin itself, and had to stand very still for a moment with her eyes closed, nineteen years collapsing into nothing.

When she opened them, Rosalind was watching her with an expression Marta decided not to name.

"Here's how it will go," Marta said briskly. "The day shift arrives at six. You'll carry her to the lamp room before five — Isandro's up at four, and his door doesn't open onto the yard. He's the only man in this building who sees nothing on principle. Tell him or don't; that's your affair. During days, she stays with me. The kitchens are my kingdom and nobody enters without reason. I've a hamper behind the flour sacks with room enough, lined with sacking, and the stove keeps the corner warm. Babies sleep more than anyone credits, if they're warm and full. Nights are yours — you sleep in the clerks' dormitory like always, but you'll hear her, won't you, across the passage?"

"I'd hear her through stone," Rosalind said quietly.

"Yes. I thought so." Marta turned back to the stove, busying her hands so her face could do whatever it wanted without witnesses. "Now wash. Both of you. There's a basin and soap, and the water's hot, and if you tell anyone I let you use the good soap I'll deny you to my grave."

* * *

They fell into it the way people fall into rivers — all at once and then swimming.

By the second morning they had a system. Rosalind brought the child down at half past four, before the kitchen maids arrived at six; Marta kept her through the long middle of the day in the hamper behind the flour sacks, which the baby — who was, Marta privately judged, far too calm a baby, the quiet kind that had learned early that crying bought nothing — endured with solemn patience, waking to feed and regarding the world with dark, unblinking eyes. At night Rosalind smuggled her up to the storeroom loft among the lantern-lit crates, and the building's noises covered whatever small sounds escaped.

Marta taught her the rest in stolen minutes. How to burp a baby against the shoulder, patting the small back with a cupped palm. How to test milk temperature on the wrist, never the finger. How to make gruel thin enough for a six-week-old who was older than six weeks in hunger-years. How to change the clouts and fold them so they didn't chafe, and how to air them behind the boiler where the steam hid everything. How to read a cry — hunger, wind, cold, or that terrible fourth cry with no cause at all, the one you answered with walking and singing and sheer endurance.

"Why do you know all this?" Rosalind asked once, folding clouts beside her at the scrubbing table, the baby asleep between them in a drawer lined with flannel. "Really."

Marta scrubbed a pot with more attention than it deserved.

"I had a sister," she said finally. "Greta. Married a bad man in the old country and died of him, eventually, the way women do. Left a baby." She rinsed the pot. Set it upside down. "The baby didn't see spring. That was a long time ago and across an ocean, and I've told you the whole of it, which is more than I've told anyone here in nineteen years, and if you repeat a word I'll sack your potatoes."

"I don't even know what that means."

"No one does. It works anyway." Marta handed her the next pot. "The point is I know what it costs, keeping something alive that the world has decided to forget. And I know what it costs not to. I paid the second price once. Never again."

Rosalind was quiet a moment. Then she said, "Thank you," and Marta grunted and put the pot away, because thank-you was a coin that didn't spend with her, and because if she spoke more she'd have said something about the girl's hands — quick, careful, already knowing — and her voice would have betrayed her, and that would never do.

* * *

Complicity, Marta discovered, was a faster road to intimacy than twenty years of neighborliness. Within a week they had signals, glances, a whole silent grammar. Two taps on the pass-through shelf meant *baby's awake*. A dishcloth hung over the kettle-handle meant *strangers in the kitchen, say nothing*. When Mr. Hobbins lingered at noon asking why the cook was boiling milk in February, Marta fixed him with a stare that had curdled stronger men and said, "Custard, Mr. Hobbins, for the director's table, unless you'd care to explain to Director Corvane why his pudding is wanting," and Hobbins retreated so fast he left a draft.

They laughed about it afterward — really laughed, both of them, helplessly, while the baby slept and the snow ticked against the glass — and Marta realized she could not remember the last time she had laughed in this kitchen. Nineteen years of competent solitude, of being necessary and unseen, of feeding a hundred people who knew her only as a source of dinners. Now there was a girl on her stool and a child in her drawer and a secret between them like a hearth-fire, and Marta, who had thought herself finished with wanting anything, found she wanted this with a fierceness that frightened her.

On the eighth night, Rosalind came down with the baby and did not leave at the usual hour. She sat by the stove, the child asleep against her chest rising and falling, and said to the fire, "What happens in spring? Boats come. People come. Someone left her with a note, Marta. Notes have authors. What happens when the author comes looking?"

Marta kept ladling dumplings into the pot. "Then we'll know more than we know now."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one." She set down the ladle. "Girl, listen to me. I've survived this place by never looking past tomorrow, because looking ahead is how you see the cliff. Right now we feed her and hide her and keep her warm, and that is tomorrow, and tomorrow is all any of us are promised. Spring will come and we will deal with spring. But—" she pointed the ladle at Rosalind, dripping, "—you will not give her up to whoever wrote that note. Not to the river, not to the company, not to charity. Whatever she is, whoever she is, she's a person now, ours, and persons get fought for. Do you understand me?"

Rosalind looked down at the sleeping child. In the lamplight her face — the baby's, then the girl's — held something Marta recognized from her own mirror: the beginning of a ferocity that would not be reasoned with.

"I understand," Rosalind said.

* * *

It was near dawn on the ninth day when Marta woke to a sound she had trained herself against — footsteps in the kitchen that were not hers, not Rosalind's, not the maids'. Heavy boots, deliberate. She rose, wrapped her shawl, and found a man standing by the cold range turning his hat in his hands: broad, weathered, with a face like a root and lamplight still clinging to his coat.

Isandro. Who had seen nothing on principle for nineteen years.

He held out a small object. A tin whistle, no longer than a thumb, polished with handling.

"She drops it," he said, in his gravel voice, "when you think no one watches. Behind the flour sacks. I found it on my rounds." His dark eyes went to the hamper, then back. "A whistle is a dangerous toy for a hamper, Mrs. Kessler."

Marta said nothing. Rules of invisibility: never confirm, never deny, never fill a silence.

But Isandro was looking at her the way he looked at lamps — measuring the wick, the oil, whether the flame would catch — and slowly he smiled, an old crack in old stone, and put the whistle into her hand and folded her fingers over it.

"I had a daughter once," he said. "The river took her. Since then I keep the lamps lit all winter, every one, every night, so nothing else gets lost in the dark." He turned to go, then paused at the door. "If the hamper ever needs a warmer corner, the lamp room is warm all night. And I hear nothing. On principle."

The door closed behind him. Marta stood alone in the gray beginning of morning, the whistle warm in her fist, and upstairs she could hear Rosalind's feet on the stairs, coming early, and the small stir of the baby waking, and outside the whiteout had finally broken — the sky clearing hard and cold, and far off, faint as a held note, the first groan of the river ice beginning to shift.

Spring was coming. Everything would come with it.
