## 5. The Director's Winter Visit

The news came down from the front office on a Tuesday, carried by the junior clerk Pell like a coal held in bare hands.

"Director Corvane himself," he whispered, though there was no one to hear but me and the mice. "Thursday. He'll inspect the winter accounts before the river locks. All ledgers out. All tallies current."

I set down my pen with a care that cost me something. Three years I had worked in this warehouse, and three years the director had been a name at the bottom of notices, a signature like a fence — *A. Corvane* — enclosing everything I did inside someone else's property. He had never come to Hallowmere in my time. The older men spoke of his visits the way sailors speak of storms: rarely, and never welcome, and you best batten down whatever you cannot afford to lose.

"Accounts," Marta said that evening in the kitchen, kneading bread with a violence the dough had not earned. "He comes to count. That's all a man like that knows how to do. Count what he owns."

"And we're counted in it," I said.

She looked at me over her shoulder, flour to her elbows, and for a moment neither of us glanced toward the ceiling, toward the lamp room above the storerooms where a small bundle of trouble slept wrapped in a shawl that no longer had embroidery worth reading. We did not look, because looking was a habit we could not afford. Lantern had been with us eleven days now. Eleven days of Marta's thinning the milk, of Isandro rigging a cradle from an old cargo net, of me learning to walk past my own hiding place with the studied blankness of a woman who has nothing in any pocket but ink-stained fingers.

"He'll be gone in two days," Marta said. "Cold men don't linger."

She was wrong about that. But she would not learn it until later, and neither would I.

* * *

Wednesday I spent preparing the ledgers, which meant Wednesday I spent lying with numbers.

Not lying exactly. The foundling cost nothing that appeared anywhere in company records — Marta smuggled milk from her own rations, Isandro bartered a coil of lamp oil for a tin of pap, and I paid for the rest out of the wage that was barely a wage at all. But there were other things. A blanket gone missing from stores in December, entered as damaged. An hour of my evenings unaccounted. Small discrepancies, invisible singly, and yet now, with the director's eye coming, they seemed to glow like embers in a dark room. I combed through the pages until midnight, smoothing every entry flat, making the books as featureless and honest-looking as a frozen pond.

Tam watched me work from his cot in our corner behind the storeroom wall. His cough had eased some since Marta started sending up broth with a clove of garlic buried in it like a secret, but his eyes were fever-bright and missed nothing.

"You're frightened," he said.

"I'm working."

"You're frightened and working." He pulled the blanket to his chin. His voice had gone thin as the ice on the window glass. "Roz. If it's about the baby—"

"Hush." The word came out sharper than I intended, and I crossed the room and pressed my hand to his forehead, cool enough, thank God. "It's about arithmetic," I said, gentler. "That's all it ever is, here. Don't fret."

He closed his eyes, but I saw his mouth set in the way it did when he decided to believe me for my sake rather than his own. My brother was nine years old and had already learned that some truths are carried quietly or not at all. Our father had taught him that, in his way, from the bottom of the river.

* * *

Director Ambrose Corvane arrived Thursday at nine o'clock precisely, in a black sledge drawn by two gray horses that steamed like kettles in the cold.

I saw him from the tally window. He stepped down without waiting for the driver, a tall man in a coat so dark it seemed to drink the snowlight, and he stood a moment looking up at the warehouse — at the whole grim face of it, timber and iron, icicles hanging from the loading dock like the teeth of something patient — before he came inside. He did not stamp the snow from his boots. I would remember that. Most men stamp. He simply walked it in with him, melting slowly, leaving dark prints down the corridor like a trail of small graves.

Mr. Harrow the factor met him with bows and a sheaf of papers, and the inspection began.

I had imagined being ignored, and mostly I was. Corvane moved through the warehouse the way cold moves through a house — under doors, along floors, arriving everywhere without visible effort. He read columns faster than Harrow could present them. He ran one gloved finger along a shelf bracket and inspected the dust or lack of it. Twice he asked a clerk a question and let the poor man stammer through half an answer before cutting him off with a nod that might have meant anything.

I kept my head down over the freight manifest and told myself I was furniture. *Don't speak first. Don't want loudly.* The rules my father left me, worn smooth as river stones from handling.

But at half past ten, Harrow's voice called my name across the floor, and furniture stopped being an option.

"The Amberdale girl. She keeps the night tallies."

Corvane turned. Up close he was older than his signature suggested — sixty perhaps, with a face built of long planes and a gray beard trimmed with geometric severity, like something measured rather than grown. His eyes were pale, almost colorless in that light, and they settled on me with the weight of an item being weighed.

"You were on duty the night of the eighth," he said. No greeting. No preamble. "The eleventh of January."

The date struck me somewhere beneath the ribs. Not the night Lantern came — that had been the twenty-second. But the eighth. The whiteout, the storm that shut the river, the night—

"Yes, sir," I said. "Storm duty. Dockside manifest."

"Were you at your post the whole of it?"

"The whole of it, sir. Though there was little to tally. Nothing moved in that weather but the wind."

Something passed across his face — not softening; a tightening, like ice forming. "Nothing came ashore? No boat? No messenger?"

"No, sir. Visibility was arm's length. I logged the watch closed at dawn and went to breakfast." Every word true. Every word arranged, the way you arrange goods in a crate, to say less than they hold. My heart was going like a drum in a parade I had no part in.

He regarded me a moment longer. Then: "You have steady handwriting. Continue."

And he moved on, coats and cold and all, down the aisle of crates, and I stood at my desk with my pen uncapped and my hand refusing, for a full minute, to be steady at all.

* * *

He broke his fast at noon in the factor's office — Marta sent up food and reported back, tight-lipped, that he had eaten four bites of everything and thanked no one — and in the afternoon he did a thing none of us could explain: he walked the dock.

The wind off the river was cruel that day, the kind that finds the gap between collar and skin and lives there. I was sent out with the afternoon manifest because Harrow wanted a clerk visible during inspection, proof of industry, and so I stood at the dock's landward end, ledger open, fingers going wooden inside my gloves, while Director Corvane walked the length of the frozen platform alone.

He walked it slowly. He stopped halfway out, where the pallets ended and the planking gave onto the ice-sheened approach, and he stood looking down at the boards as if the snow held writing. Then he went further, to the very corner beneath the eave where the storm of the twenty-second had driven its fury and where, on a night of white blindness, a basket had sat with a breath inside it.

He stood there a long time. Long enough that the cold should have driven any reasonable man back inside. Long enough that I stopped pretending to read my ledger and simply watched him, and hated myself for watching, and watched anyway.

What does a man look at, standing on the spot where his blood was abandoned? I did not know then that it was his blood. I knew only that he looked the way people look at graves — not searching, exactly. Grieving, perhaps, in a language so old and frozen that grief itself had forgotten how to thaw.

When he turned and came back, I snapped my eyes down. He stopped beside me. Snow had gathered on the shoulders of his coat and he had not brushed it away, and somehow that small neglect undid more of his terrible composure than anything else could have.

"You've worked this dock long?" he asked.

"Three winters, sir."

"In all that time — do items go missing here? Cargo, deliveries. Things left that shouldn't be, or taken that shouldn't go?"

"Rarely, sir. The watchman's rounds are regular."

"But things are left." It was not quite a question. "Packages. Parcels. People leave things on docks, in weather. It happens."

"Sometimes, sir. Mostly it's misdelivered freight, and we log it and send it back."

"And when it isn't freight."

I looked up then. I wish I could say I kept my face smooth, but his eyes were already on mine, pale and patient, and there was nothing in them I could read except cold — and beneath the cold, so faint I might have invented it, something that was not cold at all. Something drowning.

"Then it depends," I said, "on what's been lost, sir. And who's looking for it."

For a heartbeat neither of us breathed. The river groaned under its ice, far off, a sound like a door settling in a very large house. Then Ambrose Corvane nodded once, the exact nod he gave to satisfactory columns, and said, "Quite," and walked past me into the warmth, and left me on the dock with the wind and the ledger and the sudden terrible understanding that the hunt I had feared since the twenty-second was not coming.

It had arrived. It wore a black coat. And it knew how to ask questions that sounded like bookkeeping.

* * *

That evening I climbed to the lamp room with milk warmed over Marta's stove, and I sat on an upturned crate beside the cradle of netting and watched Lantern sleep with her fists curled by her ears like two small questions.

Isandro trimmed wicks at the bench, his big hands delicate as a sexton's. He didn't ask why I'd been white at supper. He had lit enough lamps in enough dark rooms to know you let the light do the asking.

"He walked the dock," I finally said. "Stood right there. Where she—" I stopped, because the baby's breathing had the small tidal rhythm that makes loud words feel like crimes. "He asked about things left behind. About what goes missing."

Isandro set down his trimmer. For a while the only sound was the tick of the cooling lamp glass and, below us, the distant complaint of the river.

"Eleven days she's been ours," he said at last. "Longer than most things stay hidden in Hallowmere."

"Ours." The word caught in my throat, sweet and awful at once. "Isandro, if he finds her—"

"Then we'll know what kind of man finds her." He took up his trimmer again, and the light steadied under his hand. "You said yourself, Rosalind-girl — depends who's looking, and what for. A man hunting to bury doesn't stand grieving on cold planks for half an hour. A man hunting to bury sends others."

But I remembered the trail of wet footprints down the corridor, and the snow he hadn't brushed from his shoulders, and the drowning thing beneath the ice of his eyes, and I thought: *grief is not mercy. Grieved men have buried more children than cruel ones ever have.*

Lantern stirred, sighed, settled. Outside, the temperature fell with the dark, and somewhere downriver the ice sang its long note, and I sat in the lamplight holding a cup of warm milk for a child too young to know she was hunted, and made myself a promise I did not yet have the power to keep:

Before that ice breaks, I will know whose face she wears. Because whoever wants it forgotten will not stop asking — and next time, they will not ask politely.
