## 6. What Tam Remembers

The fever made its own weather.

Tam Amberdale lay in the narrow bed beneath the eaves of the loft over Marta's kitchen, and the fever moved through him the way the river moved under ice — sometimes still, sometimes surging, always going somewhere he could not follow. In the good hours he could see the water stain on the ceiling beam above his head, shaped like a fish leaping, and he would count the cracks that branched from it like rivers on a map no one had ever drawn. In the bad hours there was only heat, and his mother's voice, though his mother had been two years in the churchyard now, and voices did not come back just because a boy asked them to.

He was eleven. He knew he was dying the way you know a storm is coming — not from any one thing, but from the way everyone in the house had begun to move differently around him. Softer. Slower. Marta brought broth in bowls he was too tired to finish, and she never once said *eat it all*, which Marta said about everything, about bread and salt and the price of tallow and the sins of the Corvane Trading Company. Rosalind sat with him in the evenings and read to him out of old shipping manifests when she thought he wasn't listening, her voice flat and steady as arithmetic, because the company library held no stories and Rosalind had never learned to waste anything, not candle-ends, not words, not worry.

But it was in the fever hours that his mother came to him, and told him the story again. The true one. Not the one the town told.

* * *

The town told it this way: that Pearlie Amberdale was reckless, that he dove where honest men did not dive, that greed pulled him down same as any stone, and that the debt he drowned in was the debt he deserved. Hallowmere loved a cautionary tale better than it loved a man.

His mother told it this way:

It was February. The coldest February in nine years, she said, so cold the horses' breath froze on their own muzzles and the river went quiet in a way rivers are not meant to go quiet — not sleeping but *holding*, the way a person holds their breath before pain. And the Corvane Company had lost a crate in the shallows off the eastern pier. Not just any crate. A crate of river pearls already strung, already graded, worth more than every building on Fore Street together, and Director Corvane himself had come down to the ice in his seal-fur coat to watch men try to recover what the current had taken.

They tried grappling hooks for two days. They tried a net weighted with chain. The river kept everything, gave back nothing, and the director's face got colder and colder until the men stopped looking at him at all, because a face like that made you feel your own warmth leaving your body.

And then Pearlie Amberdale came walking down onto the ice with his diving hood under his arm, and Tam's mother said the whole riverfront went silent like a congregation.

*You don't have to,* the director said to him. That part Tam's mother always repeated exactly, because she said it was the exactness of it that mattered — that Corvane had said *you don't have to*, not *please don't*, not *it's too dangerous*. An offer shaped like kindness. A door left open so that whoever walked through it closed it behind himself.

*I know,* said Pearlie Amberdale, and spat into the hole they'd cut, and started putting on his hood.

Tam knew the rest the way he knew his own hands. His father went down through the black water with a lantern lowered on a rope beside him — because that was how pearl divers worked these waters, with a light, since the sun this far north came slantwise and weak and gave up on the depths entirely. The divers called the lanterns their *wives*, Tam's mother said, because a diver without his light was a widower in the dark. And his father found the crate. Of course he found it; his father had eyes like a heron and hands that could feel a lie in a piece of driftwood. He got the rope hitched around it, and the men above hauled, and the crate came up silver and streaming into the winter light.

And then, his mother said, the current shifted.

Not a storm. Not a wave. Just the river turning over in its sleep, the way it does when the ice above presses down and the water beneath must find another way through, and it took him sideways into the underside of the ice shelf, and the last thing anyone saw of Pearlie Amberdale was his lantern, still burning, drifting up against the underside of the ice like a star fallen into a dark ceiling, moving slowly east, east, east, until the light guttered and the river closed its hand.

They found him in the spring melt, four miles downstream. The debt — the cost of the lost season, the wages advanced against pearls never gathered, the fine levied by the Company for equipment — that they found sooner. It came due the week after the funeral, laid out on the table in the Corvane clerk's copperplate hand, itemized to the halfpenny, and Tam's mother signed the indenture papers with Rosalind standing behind her chair, fourteen years old and already understanding that ink was the true deep water, that paper drowned people slower than rivers but just as thoroughly.

*Forget this debt,* his mother used to say, bitter as frost on a windowsill, *you can't forget a debt, that's the whole trick of a debt, it remembers itself.* And then she'd catch herself, seeing Tam's face, and soften, and say the other thing she said, the thing Tam carried now like a coal in his chest: *But listen to me, my lamb. Your father lit a lantern going down into the dark. Whatever else is true of us, that is also true.*

* * *

In the waking hours, Tam watched his sister.

This was easier than it sounded, because watching things was what Tam did. He was not strong and he was not quick anymore, and some days he was not even upright, but he had always been able to watch — had learned it young, flat on his back or sitting propped in doorways while other children ran, learning the faces of adults the way other boys learned the calls of birds. He knew Marta hummed when she was happy and swore in two languages when she was not. He knew Isandro's limp grew worse before snow and better before thaw, as if the old man's hip were a kind of almanac. He knew the doctor lied gently, and that gentle lies had a particular music to them, all rising tones, questions dressed as reassurances.

And he knew that something had changed in Rosalind.

It had happened in the deep of winter, around the time of the whiteout — he remembered the whiteout because the wind had screamed against the shutters for a day and a night, and Marta had baked bread just to keep the oven warm, and the whole loft had smelled of yeast and desperation. Before the whiteout, Rosalind had been tired the way she was always tired, worn thin as a coin rubbed smooth. After the whiteout, she was tired in a new way. A purposeful way. She moved through the kitchen like someone carrying water in both hands — careful, level, unwilling to spill, unwilling even to set it down.

She was smiling more. That was the strangest part. Rosalind did not smile easily; smiles on her were like sun through river ice, visible but no warmth passing through. But now, sometimes, when she thought no one was looking, a smile would arrive on her face uninvited and stay, soft and private, the way you might look at something small and sleeping.

Small and sleeping. The thought arrived one gray afternoon and would not leave.

He began, then, to watch with intent. He noted that Rosalind took her evening meal later than before, long after the kitchen emptied. He noted that she had begun making trips to the storerooms after hours — he heard the floorboards below his bed speak her name in their creaking language, a rhythm he could tell from Marta's heavy tread or Isandro's uneven gait. He noted that Marta had started setting aside milk at odd hours, and that a quantity of sugar vanished from the pantry that no grown woman's tea could account for. He noted that Rosalind's shawl, when she came up to sit with him, sometimes smelled faintly of lamp oil and something else — something warm and milky and alive, a smell he could not name but recognized somewhere below thinking, the way you recognize the smell of rain.

Once, half-dreaming in the fever's shallow end, he heard it: a sound from below, thin and unmistakable, a baby's cry, quickly hushed. He surfaced toward wakefulness, and by the time he had climbed fully into his senses it was gone, swallowed by the ordinary sounds of the kitchen — the clatter of a lid set down too hard, perhaps, or the wind finding the chimney. But his heart went on beating strangely for a long while after, quick and wondering, like a bird that has heard another bird through a wall.

He did not ask.

That was the decision he made, lying there in the dark with the fish-shaped stain swimming above him, and it surprised him how easily he made it, how completely. He would not ask. He turned the reasons over like stones on the shore and found under each one the same cold certainty: whatever Rosalind was carrying — a secret, a child, a crime, a hope — she was carrying it with both hands, carefully, and if he reached for it he might make her spill it. And Rosalind had spent six years carrying everything. Their mother's illness. The indenture. The debt that remembered itself. Tam had watched his sister grow calluses on her soul the way other girls grew them on their needle fingers, and if she had finally picked up something that made her smile at nothing in the kitchen dark, then by every saint who ever kept a ledger, he would not be the reason she had to put it down.

There was a moment, though, near the end of that week, when the decision was tested.

Rosalind came up in the evening with the lamp, and sat, and read to him from the manifests as usual — *forty barrels pitch, twelve coils rope, one case assorted hardware* — and her voice was steady as arithmetic, but her hands were not. She kept folding the corner of the page, unfolding it, folding it again, a nervous crease deepening into a little white scar in the paper. And then she stopped reading altogether, and looked at the lamp flame, and said, in a voice too casual by half, "Tam. If I ever did something you didn't understand. Something I couldn't explain right away. Would you—"

"Yes," he said.

She blinked. "I haven't asked yet."

"You don't have to ask." He pulled himself higher on the pillow, which cost him more than he let show, and looked at her straight, because some things deserved a straight look even from a boy who could not stand. "Whatever it is. Yes. You don't need my permission and you don't need my forgiveness ahead of time. You've earned both, and then some."

For a moment her face did something complicated — the coin-smooth weariness cracking along a seam he hadn't known was there — and then she laughed, short and wet, and pressed her fist briefly against her mouth, and said, "You're delirious," and he said, "Often," and she bent and kissed the top of his head, smelling of lamp oil and that warm milky livingness, and finished reading him the manifest as if forty barrels of pitch were an epic poem, and neither of them spoke of it again.

But that night, alone, Tam lay in the dark and felt something he had not felt since before his mother died: useful. He had done a thing, the smallest thing, the only thing left in his power — he had stood aside and held still, the way you hold still when a wild bird lights on your hand. And the strange thing, the thing he turned over and over like a river-worn stone as sleep came for him, was that keeping a secret felt almost the same as telling one. It warmed from the inside either way. It meant you were trusted with something that mattered.

*You don't need my forgiveness ahead of time.* He fell asleep on those words as on a raft.

* * *

On the morning the coughing turned to blood-flecked, Marta sent for the doctor, and Tam heard everything through the floorboards because hearing through floorboards was another of his talents, learned in years of being discussed in rooms he'd been carried out of.

"He needs air that isn't this air," the doctor said, and his voice had that rising music in it, the gentle lie trying to climb toward truth. "The damp off the river, the smoke, the cold — it's in his lungs like rot in a beam. If he were somewhere drier, warmer, upriver where the valley opens—" The voice paused, and Tam imagined the shrug, the spread hands. "But you know what passage costs, and you know what the ice allows."

Then Marta's voice, low and fierce, and Rosalind's, lower and fiercer still, and the word *berth* passed through the boards like a knife through cloth, and Tam lay very still and understood that his life had become a number in somebody's arithmetic, and hated it, and was ashamed of hating it, and was frightened most of all.

That night the fever took him deep, deeper than it had ever gone, and in the deep places his mother came and sat at the foot of the bed as she always did, and instead of telling the story of his father she only looked at him with her sad, patient eyes and said nothing at all, and somehow that was worse than any story.

When he woke — if it was waking — Rosalind was there, asleep in the chair with her neck bent at an angle that would ache for a day, her hand resting on the blanket over his ankle, and in the gray pre-dawn light Tam watched her and made his second decision, quieter than the first and harder.

If the berth came down to choosing — and he had heard enough through the floorboards to know it would — then his sister was going to choose him. He knew her. She would spend herself like a coin without a second thought; she had been doing it for years. And he could not allow it. He would not take the seat on that boat if taking it meant Rosalind stayed bound to the Corvane Company until the debt ate the rest of her youth. He began, in his mind, composing arguments — *I'm not well enough to travel anyway, the jostling would finish me, keep your berth, get yourself free* — rehearsing them, polishing them, laying them up like a squirrel lays up nuts against a winter it can sense coming.

He did not yet know about the third claimant. He did not yet know that his sister's careful, two-handed secret slept downstairs among the lanterns, or that the child had a face someone powerful wished forgotten, or that the ice on the river had already begun, in its slow geological patience, to compose the question that would crack all of their lives open.

He only knew that spring was coming, that the ice would break, that a boat would leave, and that his sister loved him enough to drown herself saving him.

And Tam Amberdale, eleven years old, keeper of his father's story and his sister's secret, resolved in the fever-dark that this once — this once — he would be the one who refused to be saved at her expense, even if it was the last act of strength his body ever performed.

Below, faint through the floorboards, he heard the front door open and close, and Rosalind's steps cross the yard toward the lamplight of the storerooms, and he closed his eyes and listened until the sound of her went out like a lantern lowered into deep water — and prayed, with the whole of what remained of him, that this time the light would come back up.
