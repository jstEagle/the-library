## 7. Naming the Nameless

The lamp room sat at the top of the Corvane warehouse like a lantern itself, a square of glass and iron that had watched the river freeze and thaw for forty years before Isandro Vael ever climbed its stairs. He knew every groaning tread of those stairs the way a man knows his own heartbeat — by feel, by absence, by the way silence filled the spaces between them. On the last night of February, with the wind worrying at the glass panes and the river below muttering in its sleep, he climbed them slowly, because his knees were seventy years old and because he was not alone.

Rosalind climbed ahead of him, and against her chest, wrapped in a shawl that had once been Marta's and before that belonged to no one worth remembering, the child made her small sounds. Not crying. Isandro had learned the child's vocabulary in the six weeks since she had arrived on the loading dock like a parcel addressed to nobody: the hungry cry, the wet cry, the furious cry, and this — this soft huffing, almost conversational, as though she were telling them all something important in a language they had forgotten.

"She's got opinions tonight," he said.

"She always has opinions." Rosalind shifted the bundle higher. "Tonight she's objecting to the wind."

"Then she's sensible. I object to it myself." He reached past her to open the lamp room door, and the warmth of the room breathed out to meet them — oil and brass and old smoke, the smell of forty years of his own hands.

The lamp room was not large. A central post rose through its middle, and around it wound the mechanism that fed the great harbor light above, its lenses turning slow as a patient thought. Along the walls stood the smaller lamps: the storeroom lanterns waiting for their nightly rounds, ranked on iron hooks like a congregation of round-bellied priests. Forty-one of them. Isandro could have named each one by its scratch, its dent, its particular stubbornness of wick. Number twelve sputtered when the oil ran low. Number twenty-nine had a hairline crack in its chimney that whistled on north winds. Numbers three and four had hung in this room when his daughter was alive.

He did not think of that yet. There was an order to things, even to remembering, and the order would make itself known as it always did.

Rosalind settled onto the bench by the eastern window, the one whose cushion she had begun, without asking, to keep fluffed — the way she had begun, without asking, to take over the lower rounds of his route on his bad days, the way Marta had begun setting aside a saucer of milk sweetened with a spoonful of sugar, the way a family accretes around a wound or a wonder without anyone signing anything. The company had ledgers for everything else. For this there was only habit.

"Feed her before we start," Isandro said. "A cold lamp and a hungry baby are both bad company."

"She's fed."

"Then she'll watch us work. Good. She should learn." He took down the first lantern and set it on the worktable, unhooded it, and began the ritual: trim the wick, check the oil, wipe the chimney glass until it sang clean under the cloth. Outside, the whiteout had passed but the snow still came down fine and dry as sifted flour, and the river was a black seam running through all that white, holding its breath under its lid of ice.

Six weeks. The child had been with them six weeks, and she had no name.

They had all been careful about it, in the way of people carrying something breakable across a frozen yard. Marta called her *little one* and *mouse*. Tam, from his bed by the stove, called her *the baby*, as if there were only one in the world, which in a sense there was. Rosalind called her nothing at all, Isandro had noticed. She hummed to her, fed her, changed her, held her through the small hours, and never once said a name — and Isandro understood why, because he had watched her learn the rules of invisibility so well she applied them even to love. To name a thing is to claim it. To claim it is to lose it. She had lost enough.

But a child cannot go nameless forever. Even the river gives its dead names.

* * *

They finished the trimming and began the filling, and it was somewhere around the thirtieth lantern that the jest arrived, the way jests do, sidling in sideways while everyone's hands were busy.

"You know," Isandro said, tilting the oil can with a steady wrist, "she's got the right temperament for this work. Quiet in the storerooms. Watches the flame without grabbing. Doesn't cry at the whistle of number twenty-nine, which puts her ahead of most grown men."

"Most grown men haven't spent six weeks listening to it," Rosalind said.

"That's my point. She's apprenticed herself." He set the can down and looked at the child, who was watching the nearest lit lantern with her whole face, her dark eyes full of the doubled flame. "Look at her. She follows the light. We ought to call her Lantern."

It was meant lightly. He heard it land lightly. But he saw it catch in Rosalind like a spark in dry tinder — saw her go still, and look down at the baby, and then quickly away at the window, and he understood that the joke had walked into a room inside her where nothing was joking.

"It's a foolish name," she said.

"It's a good trade name," he said. "Honest work. Keeps people from walking into walls in the dark." He picked up lantern thirty-one and hung it on its hook. "There are worse things to be named after than a lamp."

And then, because his mouth was seventy years old and had outlived his judgment, he said the other thing, the true thing, quietly, to the wick he was trimming rather than to her:

"I had a daughter."

The scissors stopped. He heard Rosalind stop breathing, and the baby made her soft conversational sound, and the wind leaned its shoulder against the glass.

"Her name was Elowen," Isandro said. "She'd be fifty-one now, if the river had let her be anything at all."

* * *

He did not often tell it. Marta knew, because Marta had cooked in Hallowmere for thirty years and the town's griefs were common property, passed along with recipes. Rosalind had not known. Rosalind was sixteen and had been in the company's keeping barely two years, and the story of Elowen Vael had sunk below the town's memory the way everything sank, into the ledger margins where history forgot people.

"She was nine," he said. "Spring flood, the year of the great break — you'll have heard the old men talk about it, they talk about it the way soldiers talk about battles they survived. The ice went out early and ugly. She went down to the bank to see it, the way children do, the way I had gone down to see it when I was her age, and the shelf gave way under her, and that was the whole of it. Four seconds. No more. The river doesn't need longer."

Rosalind had drawn the shawl closer around the baby. Her voice, when it came, was careful as a hand reaching across ice. "You were here. In this room?"

"I was on my rounds." He said it flatly, because dressing it up helped no one. "Forty-one lamps, and I lit every one of them while she was in the water. That's what I was doing. My work. The thing I do." He set down the scissors. "When they pulled her out two miles downstream, her mother asked me how I could have kept lighting lamps knowing — except I didn't know. Nobody knew until dark. And I have thought about that every night since. That the whole time my hands were doing the one thing I'm good at, my girl was going under, and the lamps burned just as bright either way. They didn't care. Light doesn't care. It just burns, or it doesn't."

The lamp room was very quiet. Above them the great lens turned, sweeping its beam across the snowfield, around and around, patient as forgiveness.

"For a long time," Isandro said, "I hated them for that. The lamps. This room. I asked the company to move me to the docks and they wouldn't — said a man who knows the route is worth three who don't, and they were right, that's the filthy truth of companies, they're usually right in ways that don't care about you. So I stayed. And one night in November, some twenty-odd years back, I was up here trimming wicks and I realized I wasn't hating anymore. I was just doing it. And I understood what had happened instead." He looked at the ranked lanterns, all forty-one, their chimneys catching the great light's turn so that each one flared gold for half a heartbeat as the beam passed, a congregation saying amen. "Grief doesn't leave, girl. It changes employment. It goes to work for you, if you let it. Every lamp I've lit since, part of me is lighting it for her. Every dark stair I've kept safe, she walks it too. The river took my daughter and I took the job of making sure the dark never got comfortable, and somewhere in forty years those two facts braided together into a life." He wiped his hands on the rag. "That's all guardianship is. Grief that found a job."

Rosalind's eyes were bright and wet in the lamplight. The baby, oblivious, had finally surrendered to sleep, her small fist curled against Rosalind's collarbone.

"Why didn't you ever tell me?" Rosalind whispered.

"Because you didn't need it yet." He smiled, and it hurt, the way true smiles do at his age. "Now you do. You're carrying a child with no name and no claimant through a building owned by men who file everything, and you're doing it alone, or you think you are. I'm telling you that you're not. That's all tonight is."

* * *

They finished the rounds after that, or rather he finished and she carried the sleeping child along the catwalks while he hung lanterns in their brackets — down the long aisle of crated tea, along the salt loft, through the rope walk where the hemp smelled of tar and patience. It was the best kind of night for the work, cold enough that the flames burned tall and steady, and by the time they came back up the stairs to the lamp room, the storm had spent itself and the sky had opened a seam of stars over the river.

And it was there, hanging the last lantern on its hook, that Isandro heard Rosalind say it.

"Lantern."

Not laughing. Testing it, the way you test thin ice with your weight, ready to pull back.

"It's foolish," she said again, but the second time the word *foolish* had gone soft.

"It's honest," he said. "Names given in fear are chains. Names given in love are lamps. Ask yourself which one you're handing her." He turned from the hook. "You don't have to decide tonight. But I'll tell you what I know about names, having buried one and kept one for forty years. A name isn't a label. It's a promise. You say it over a child and you're telling the world: this one, someone will come for. Whatever else happens, whoever comes asking — this one is not alone."

Rosalind looked down at the sleeping bundle for a long moment. Then she crossed the lamp room to the eastern window, where the river lay below in its armor of ice, black and silver under the turning beam.

"Her blanket had embroidery on it," she said, so quietly he nearly lost it to the wind. "Blue thread. Flowers. Someone loved her before whoever left her decided not to. I keep thinking about that. Whoever stitched those flowers meant to hand her a name someday, and then didn't, and now it's ours to give." She turned. "So give it properly, then. If we're going to be foolish, let's be foolish properly."

Isandro felt something move in his chest that he had thought long since boarded up.

"There's a way to do it," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Old way. Lamplighters used to do it for children born on the route, back when routes had villages on them instead of warehouses. You hold the child to the light and you speak the name into the flame, so it goes wherever the light goes. Superstition, the company would call it. The company calls a lot of things superstition that are only love wearing its work clothes."

"Do it," Rosalind said. "Show me."

He took down the oldest lantern — number three, the one that had hung in this room when Elowen was alive, its brass worn to brightness by forty years of his thumbs. He trimmed its wick with particular care and lit it, and the flame stood up straight and golden, steady as a held breath. Then he nodded to Rosalind, and she came to him, and together, his old hands under her young ones, they held the sleeping child close to the warm glass.

"Lantern," Rosalind whispered into the flame.

The fire bent at her breath and righted itself, and the name rode the light out through the glass panes, across the snow, down to the black seam of the river and out over the ice fields where the stars came down almost to touch the world.

"Lantern," Isandro said, steadier, into the flame. "Lantern, who is claimed. Lantern, who is not alone. Whoever you belong to, whoever comes looking — these hands come first."

The great lens overhead turned its slow circle. In the little room at the top of the warehouse, a child who six weeks ago had been a bundle abandoned on a dock with a note reading *forget this face* slept soundly between two people who had just, in the sight of nothing but lamplight and stars, promised the opposite.

Rosalind was crying without noise. Isandro pretended not to see, and pressed her shoulder once, and let her pretend too.

"She'll need papers someday," Rosalind said at last, scrubbing her face with her sleeve. "Real ones. A record somewhere."

"Aye, someday." He hung number three back on its hook, gently, the way you lay flowers. "Someday soon, maybe. Spring's coming earlier than usual — the ice is talking already, down at the narrows. Boats will come, notices will come, the company will start its spring arithmetic." He did not know, yet, what that arithmetic would cost them; he only felt it gathering out there beyond the glass like weather. "And whatever the spring brings, little mouse has a name now. That's armor, girl. Remember that."

Below them, far off under the starlit white, the river groaned — long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, beginning to dream of breaking free.
