## 8. The Berth Announcement

The notice went up on the company board on a morning when the light had changed.

Rosalind noticed the light before she noticed anything else. She had spent enough winters in Hallowmere to know that spring did not arrive so much as leak in — through the seams of doors, through the grey wool of February mornings, until one day you woke and found the dark had thinned at its edges like ice gone rotten. That morning, standing in the yard with her ledger under her arm, she saw the sun strike the eastern eaves of the warehouse and linger there, gold and almost warm, and something in her chest turned over the way river water turns beneath a thawing sheet of ice.

Then she saw the crowd gathered at the notice board, and the turning became a lurch.

Company notices were rarely worth a crowd. The board by the pay office usually carried nothing more than revised tariff schedules, warnings against pilferage, the occasional proclamation about dock discipline that no one read past the first line. But this morning there were twenty clerks and stevedores pressed shoulder to shoulder in the trampled snow, and Marta was already there on the fringe with flour on her hands, and Isandro stood apart as old men do, leaning on his lighting pole, watching not the paper but the faces of the people reading it.

Rosalind was small and practiced at being unseen; she slid between two tally-men and read.

BY ORDER OF THE DIRECTORATE, CORVANE TRADING COMPANY:

WHEREAS THE BREAKING OF THE ICE IS EXPECTED WITHIN THE COMING MONTHS, AND WHEREAS THE RIVER BERTH AT HALLOWMERE SHALL THEREAFTER BE CLOSED TO ALL NAVIGATION UNTIL THE FLOOD SEASON HAS PASSED AND THE WATERS SUBSIDE;

IT IS HEREBY ANNOUNCED THAT ONE (1) PASSENGER BERTH SHALL BE GRANTED ABOARD THE FINAL VESSEL TO DEPART PRIOR TO SAID CLOSURE, NAMELY THE *MERROW'S GAIN*, DUE TO SAIL UPON THE FIRST SAFE WATER.

EMPLOYEES OF THE COMPANY IN GOOD STANDING MAY PETITION FOR SAID BERTH BY WRITTEN APPLICATION, DELIVERED TO THE PAY OFFICE NO LATER THAN THE FEAST OF Saint ALBAN.

THE DIRECTORATE'S DECISION SHALL BE FINAL.

Below it all, the clerk's signature, and the wax seal — a pearl in a cupped hand, red as something freshly wounded.

One berth. Rosalind read it three times, and each time the words rearranged themselves into a different weight. One berth on the last boat out before the river closed. One passage upriver, past the shallows, past the drowned villages, to Corrow Bridge and the towns beyond where the water ran clean and the air came off the fells instead of off the fen. Where their mother's cousin Edith kept a house with a stove that worked and a door that locked and a medicine chest, if the stories were true, that held more than liniment.

Where Tam could breathe.

She felt it before she thought it — that sudden vertical drop of hope, the way a floor gives when you step somewhere you didn't know was hollow. Tam's cough had come back with March. It had never truly left; it wintered in him the way sparrows wintered in the rafters, quiet but present. But these last weeks it had deepened, gone thick and wet at the bottom, and twice in the night Rosalind had lain awake in their narrow room above the cooper's shed listening to it and doing the arithmetic she refused to finish. The doctor's fee for a consultation. The doctor's fee for the tincture he prescribed, which was laudanum and honey and cost more than a month of Tam's keep. The doctor's face when Rosalind asked what would cure him outright, and the doctor looking at the river and saying, in the flat tone of men who deliver such verdicts often: *Dry air. High ground. Away from this water.* As if prescribing a country rather than a course of treatment.

And here, pinned to a board with four nails, was a country.

* * *

She told Tam that evening, sitting on the edge of his bed with the lamp turned low because bright light made him squint, and squinting made him cough, and coughing was the enemy entire.

He was eleven and looked younger lying down, all wrists and collarbones, his hair damp at the temples from the fever that came every afternoon and left every midnight like a tide with an unkind schedule. He had their father's eyes, dark and quick, though the quickness had gone out of them lately, banked like a fire left to hold overnight.

"There's a berth," Rosalind said. "On the last boat before the flood season."

Tam said nothing. He had learned to save his breath for breathing.

"Up river. To Corrow Bridge, maybe further. Anyone can petition. Anyone in company service." She smoothed the blanket over his knees, an unnecessary gesture, a mother's gesture, and hated that her hands had learned it. "Cousin Edith would take you. She said so, last autumn, in her letter. She has the room still."

"You'd write it," Tam said. It wasn't a question. His voice had a rasp in it now that hadn't been there in the autumn, and Rosalind catalogued that rasp the way she catalogued cargo — by weight, by damage, by what it would cost to replace. "You'd write the petition."

"I'd write it tonight if you want."

Tam was quiet a while. Outside, the river spoke in its new voice — not the dead silence of deep winter but a mutter, a shifting, the sound of a thing asleep but dreaming of moving. Somewhere down the bank the ice groaned, long and low, like a door in an empty house.

"Three people," Tam said finally.

"What?"

"You, me, and—" he stopped, and Rosalind understood, and the understanding sat cold between them. He had seen the baby. Of course he had; Rosalind brought Lantern to their room on her half-days, wrapped in shawls, fed with a spoon and endless patience. Tam knew. Tam had known since January and had never once said a word about it, which was its own kind of love, the silent kind, the kind that keeps watch without being asked.

"One berth," Tam said.

"Yes."

"And Lantern can't go alone."

"No."

Tam looked at the ceiling. The fever-bright had gone out of his eyes and left something older behind, something that should not have lived in an eleven-year-old face. When he spoke again his voice was very small and very even.

"Then don't waste it on me."

"Tam—"

"I mean it." The rasp caught. He turned his head and coughed into the cloth Rosalind held ready, and she watched his whole body seize with the effort, and when it passed he lay pale and spent and continued as though he had merely paused for breath, which technically he had. "You give it to me, I go to Edith's, and you're stuck here till summer with a baby someone left to be forgotten. And whoever left her—" his eyes came to Rosalind's, dark and steady and far too knowing, "—whoever wanted her forgotten might not have stopped wanting it. You need to be here. Or you all need to go. But one of us going is just arithmetic, Roz. Bad arithmetic."

She stared at him. Sixteen years of keeping him fed and mended and alive, and here he was doing sums back at her.

"When did you get so clever?" she whispered.

"Inherited it," he said, and closed his eyes, and pretended to sleep, and let her sit there in the lamplight with her heart breaking in two directions at once.

* * *

The arithmetic did not improve over the following days. It worsened, as honest arithmetic does.

Rosalind counted the candidates the way she counted crates — precisely, unwillingly, with full knowledge of the total. There was herself, or rather herself-and-Lantern, which was one petition that could carry only one body unless the child rode in a basket counted as baggage, and the thought of Lantern listed between casks of tallow and bolts of linen made something in Rosalind's throat close. There was Tam, whose claim was the strongest on paper — a minor, an orphan, a dependent of a deceased employee, sick unto death in river air — and whose claim was weakest in every other way, because petitions were decided by the directorate, and directorates did not spend berths on dying debt-wards when living clerks were asking. There was Marta, who had served the company nineteen years and who had a sister in Corrow Bridge she mentioned only when drunk on communion wine at Christmas, and whom Rosalind had heard, once, through the kitchen wall, weeping softly over a letter that smelled of woodsmoke and distance.

Three candidates. One berth. And the boat would sail whether they wept or not.

She took to walking the dock at dusk after her rounds, when the lamps were lit and Isandro had gone on ahead, and standing at the end of the pilings looking upriver. The channel was beginning to show black water at its edges — thin leads of it, opening along the shore like eyes. In another month the whole river would go. The *Merrow's Gain* would be the last hull to leave, and after her, nothing. No boats, no barges, no post except whatever the riders carried over the fells, and they charged triple in flood season. Hallowmere would seal itself like a jar and sit on the shelf of the world until high summer.

Whatever was decided now would hold for months. Whatever was not decided would drown.

"You're doing your father's trick," Isandro said.

She hadn't heard him come up. Old men in soft boots; the river's mutter covered everything. He set his pole against the bollard and stood beside her, hands folded atop it, looking upriver too, at the black leads widening in the ice.

"What trick?"

"Standing at the end of things, looking at the water like it owes you an answer." He said it gently. "Your father used to do it. Before dives. I'd see him from the lamp room some mornings, down here in the dark before dawn, having words with the river."

"And did it ever answer him?"

Isandro was quiet a moment. The ice groaned again, nearer this time, a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

"It answered him once too many," he said. Then, briskly, before grief could settle: "The berth notice. You've seen it. Everyone's seen it. Marta's been polishing the pay office counter like it's an altar."

"I don't know what to ask for," Rosalind said. It came out smaller than she meant. "That's the truth of it, Isandro. On paper it should be Tam. He needs it most — the air upriver, the dry ground. The doctor said—" Her voice snagged. She pushed through. "But he won't go alone, and I can't leave Lantern, and Marta's served longer than I've been alive and she's got a sister waiting, and every way I turn the sum comes out cruel."

Isandro nodded slowly, as if she had described weather rather than ruin.

"Aye. That's how the company builds its kindnesses. One of everything, and three of everyone." He tapped the pole on the boards. "You know what I learned about rivers, girl? All my years lighting lamps along this bank?"

"What?"

"They don't ask permission. The ice doesn't vote on whether to break. When the water's ready, it goes, and everything in its path either moves or learns to float." He picked the pole up again, and his old eyes caught the lamplight, and for a moment Rosalind saw in them the daughter he never spoke of, the one the river had taken decades ago and kept. "Write your petition. Write it true. What happens after is river work."

He went on up the dock toward the storerooms, and Rosalind stayed at the end of the pilings a while longer, watching the black leads widen, feeling the whole frozen world begin, slowly and irreversibly, to move.

* * *

She wrote the petition that night at the kitchen table in the cook-house, after hours, by the banked coals' red glow, because the kitchen was warm and because Marta was there rolling pastry at midnight as she did when she couldn't sleep, which lately was often.

Rosalind wrote her brother's name. She wrote his age, his condition, the doctor's verdict copied out in her careful clerk's hand — *chronic affliction of the lungs, aggravated by river damp; removal to higher, drier country strongly advised* — and she wrote Cousin Edith's name and direction in Corrow Bridge, and she sealed it with a bead of Marta's sealing wax stolen without ceremony from the pantry, and then she sat looking at it, and did not sleep either.

Across the room, Marta's rolling pin had gone still.

"You gave it to the boy," Marta said. Not a question either. The kitchen had few secrets left in it.

"He needs it most."

"Everyone needs it most. That's how the notice is built." Marta set the pin down and wiped her hands on her apron, leaving pale crescents of flour. She looked at Rosalind a long moment, and her broad, kind, tired face did something complicated. "I put in a petition myself. Yesterday."

Rosalind's chest tightened. "Marta—"

"Nineteen years, girl. If sentiment counted for anything, I'd have earned that berth thrice over." Marta said it steadily, without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "But I'll tell you what I told the pay office clerk. I told them the truth: that there's a child sleeping in my store-room who has no name on any manifest, and a woman who keeps her who has no right to any berth at all, and if this company wants to decide who rides the last boat, it had best decide carefully what it's willing to look at when it looks at us."

The banked coals shifted. Somewhere upstairs in the dark of the store-room, Lantern slept on, unknowing, her small breaths lost under the sound of the river beginning to speak.

"The feast of Saint Alban," Marta said, gathering her flour and her silence around her again. "Nine days. We'll know by then who we are to them."

Rosalind carried the sealed petition to the pay office in the grey of the next morning, and set it in the box with the others, and heard it land at the bottom among the rustle of rival hopes.

Nine days, and the ice already singing.
