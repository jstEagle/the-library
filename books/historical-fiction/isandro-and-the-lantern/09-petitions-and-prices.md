## 9. Petitions and Prices

Marta Kell had worked the Corvane kitchen for nineteen years, long enough to know that bread forgave impatience more slowly than people did. She had kneaded her hands into the shape of this place — the knuckles thickened by cold water, the palms scarred by oven racks, the left thumbnail permanently ridged from a door slammed in 1839 by a factor who was now dead and unmourned. The kitchen was hers the way the river belonged to Hallowmere: not owned, exactly, but inhabited so thoroughly that ownership became an abstraction nobody bothered to argue.

She noticed things. That was the whole of her intelligence as far as the company knew — Marta noticed when the salt ran low, when the factor's clerk liked his porridge thicker on Tuesdays, when the second-floor housekeeper had been crying. What the company did not know was that noticing was not a service she performed but a discipline she kept, the way other women kept prayer. Nineteen years of watching had taught her one doctrine above all others: in a place like Corvane House, love was contraband, and anyone caught carrying it paid the tariff.

Which is why the baby frightened her more than anything had frightened her in nineteen years.

* * *

The child was nearly three months old now, hidden in the third storeroom behind the bolted canvas where the winter flax slept, and Marta still could not look at her without feeling the floor tilt. She fed her at dawn before the kitchen boys arrived, warming goat's milk over the low flame with one hand while the other kept the bread moving, two rhythms braided together until neither required thought. The baby — Lantern, they called her now, which Marta privately considered a reckless kind of tenderness, naming a hidden thing after light itself — had begun to smile. Not the wind-smile of newborns but the deliberate kind, aimed at faces, requiring witnesses.

That was the trouble. A smiling child wanted to be seen. A smiling child was a confession waiting for an audience.

On a grey Thursday in late March, with the notice board outside the factor's office still crowded with men reading the spring manifest and muttering about *berths*, Marta found Rosalind in the scullery scrubbing a copper pot that did not need scrubbing. She recognized avoidance when she saw it; she had invented half its postures.

"Sit," Marta said, and pointed the wooden spoon at the stool by the stove like a bishop pointing at a sinner.

Rosalind sat. She was sixteen and looked older in the face and younger in the hands, which were red to the wrist and cracked at every knuckle. Marta set a bowl in front of her without asking — barley broth, the good kind, with marrow — and stood over her with her arms folded until the girl began to eat.

"You've seen the notice," Marta said.

"I've seen it."

"One berth."

"One berth." Rosalind said it the way you'd repeat a sentence from a language you were trying not to learn.

"And how many souls in this building dreaming about it?"

"I don't listen to other people's dreams."

"No," Marta said. "You listen to your own, and then apologize for them. I've watched you do it for three months." She took the spoon out of the pot and laid it down, because what came next deserved her full hands. "I'm going to say something once, girl, and then I'll never say it again, so mark me. Petition for the berth yourself. Take it. Go upriver and be done with this place forever."

The scullery went very quiet. Somewhere beyond the walls the ice groaned — a long, deep note, like a church organ underwater. It had been doing that for days. The whole town pretended not to hear it, the way a household pretends not to hear a marriage failing.

Rosalind put down her spoon. "You want me gone?"

"Want?" Marta laughed once, short and hard. "Wanting is what got your father drowned, if you'll forgive my saying so. No — I don't *want*. I *count*. And here's my count: the berth goes upriver before the flood closes us in. Whoever takes it leaves before the ice breaks and doesn't come back until the thaw opens the water again — four months, maybe five. You take it, you walk off this dock a free woman, because no indenture survives distance, and Corvane won't chase a clerk worth less than the paper she's bound by. You'd have kin upriver. Your mother's people. A trade to learn. A life that isn't tallying other men's cargo in a frozen warehouse for debts you didn't run up."

"My brother—"

"Is sick," Marta finished for her. "Yes. He is. And I am sorry for it down to the marrow of me. But hear the arithmetic, Rosalind, because nobody else will say it plainly: one berth cannot carry two futures. If you give it away — and I've watched you deciding all week whom to give it to, as if mercy were a coat you could hand off your own back — then you stay. You stay, and the baby stays, and Quill keeps prowling these corridors looking for 'missing property,' and the director keeps asking his soft terrible questions about the dock. Every day that child smiles wider, and every day she becomes harder to forget. You stay, and you are betting three lives against one boat."

Rosalind's jaw tightened. Marta had raised no children of her own — the kitchen had eaten those years before they ripened — but she knew the expression. It was the same one she'd worn herself, once, at fifteen, standing in a doorway in Kettermoor with everything she owned in a pillowcase, calculating whether the road ahead or the road back would kill her quicker.

"If I go," Rosalind said slowly, "Tam stays."

"Then petition for Tam."

"Tam can barely sit up. He can't travel alone, and there's no room in one berth for a nurse, and even if there were—" Her voice caught and flattened itself, quick, like smoothing a wrinkle before anyone saw. "Even if there were, he'd be going to relatives who are strangers, sick, without me. Without anyone."

"He'd be alive."

"He'd be *alive*," Rosalind repeated, and the word came out strange, weighted, as though she were testing whether it could hold what Marta was putting on it. "And I'd be free. Is that the sum? Tam lives, I'm free, and Lantern—" She stopped on the name, hearing it too late.

Marta let the silence do its work. Then she said, quietly: "Lantern stays hidden with two aging women in a building where a man named Quill has already searched the coal cellar twice. You go, and we keep her as best we can. Maybe we manage it. Marta Kell has managed worse." She didn't say *and maybe we don't*, but the sentence lived in the pause, fat and obvious as a rat behind the flour sacks.

"You're asking me to abandon her."

"I'm asking you to survive. Abandonment is just survival with the windows curtained." Marta pulled the stool around and sat across from her, close, forearms on knees, the way she hadn't sat with another human being since her sister died. "Listen to me now. I was bound to a wool merchant in Kettermoor when I was fourteen. Fourteen. I scrubbed and counted and kept my mouth shut for six years, and the day my term ended I swore on my sister's grave I would never again let another person's debt decide the shape of my life. Then I came here, and I've spent nineteen years watching clever girls become old women in this building, one winter at a time. You are clever, Rosalind Amberdale. Cleverer than the factors, cleverer than the director himself, though he'd choke on his brandy hearing it. And you are spending your cleverness the way the poor always spend it — keeping everyone else warm while you freeze."

"That's not—"

"It is. Don't interrupt your elders when they're confessing their fears." Marta reached out and took one of the girl's ruined hands, turned it palm-up on the table. The calluses sat in rows like stitches. "This hand has already written its whole ledger here. Tally, sweep, obey, repeat. There's no line in it for you. You know that. You've known it since your father drowned and they handed you his debt like an inheritance."

Rosalind looked at her palm for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was very level, which told Marta more than tears would have.

"If I take the berth, I leave in six weeks. Before the ice breaks. And Lantern is three months old and cannot travel, so I leave her. And Tam cannot travel either, so I leave him. So the berth isn't salvation, Marta. It's a door out of a burning house that only fits one person, and both the people I—" She stopped again. Started again. "Both of them are asleep upstairs."

"Then you knock the fire down," Marta said. "You carry them. You find a way. That's what I'm telling you to do — fight for the berth, all of it, loudly, in the office, with your name on the petition. Make Corvane choose between his rules and his conscience, if he owns one. But choose *yourself* first, girl. Choose yourself first for once in your life, and then fight outward from there."

But even as she said it, Marta heard the flaw in her own counsel, and hated it. She had told the girl to save herself first the way a drowning swimmer is told to kick — correct advice, useless advice, advice that assumed the water wasn't full of the people you loved.

* * *

That evening the kitchen filled and emptied with its usual traffic — the night watchman's tea, the factor's supper tray, the boys wanting bread ends — and Marta moved through it all with her hands on autopilot while her mind turned the problem over like a stone she couldn't warm.

She had lied, in a way. Not in her facts — her facts were iron — but in her framing. She had presented the berth as arithmetic, one life weighed against three, clean as coin. But Marta had been counting for nineteen years and knew that some sums never balanced. Whatever Rosalind chose, something would die: her freedom, or her brother's chance, or the small fierce hope she carried around like a coal in a tin. There was no choice that left the girl whole. There was only the choice that left her least broken.

Marta had made such a choice once. Kettermoor, the pillowcase, the doorway. She had chosen herself, exactly as she was advising Rosalind to do, and she had survived, and she had built this kitchen into a kingdom of warm bread and low ceilings, and for nineteen years she had called it enough. Some nights, polishing the same copper pot Rosalind had polished that morning, she almost believed it.

Almost.

The thing she had not said — the thing she would never say, because it was too much like handing the girl a knife — was this: *the reason I urge you to go is that I am afraid of what staying will cost you, and I am more afraid of watching.* Because Marta had watched the baby's face change when Rosalind entered the room. She had watched Tam ask, in his feverish sideways way, whether his sister ate enough. She had watched Rosalind stand in the lamp room with the foundling against her shoulder and wear, for three unguarded seconds, an expression that no indentured clerk could afford — the expression of a woman with a future she intended to defend.

People with nothing to lose were cheap. People with everything to lose were expensive, and Corvane House bought cheap and sold dear, and Quill was already walking the halls with his lantern and his little list.

* * *

Near midnight, after the ovens cooled and the boys had gone, Marta carried the warmed milk down the corridor to the third storeroom, moving with the particular silence of the guilty. Rosalind was already there, seated on an overturned crate among the sleeping flax, the baby against her chest, both of them lit by the small hooded lamp Isandro had rigged with a shutter so its glow wouldn't leak under the door. The lamplighter's craft applied to hiding a child: contain the light, aim it, let it serve.

"She won't settle," Rosalind whispered. "Teething, maybe."

"Give her here." Marta took the baby, settled her against her shoulder with the ease of nineteen thousand repetitions, and walked the slow figure-eights that unraveled infants like knitting. The milk steamed faintly. Outside, the river sang its cracking song, longer tonight, closer.

"Did you mean it?" Rosalind asked. "About petitioning for myself."

"I never say things I don't mean. It saves time."

"And you think I should leave her. Leave her here with you and Isandro and hope Quill doesn't find her, hope the director stops asking questions, hope for five months until the water opens." Rosalind laughed, low and bitter, a sound far too old for her. "You've taught me to count, Marta. So I counted. Here's my ledger. If I take the berth, Tam loses his only advocate inside these walls — because you and Isandro can't petition for him, you're staff, not kin. His name dies on some list. And Lantern grows up hidden in a storeroom until someone finds her, and when they find her, they find her without me. So the berth buys my freedom and pays for it with both of them. That's not survival. That's escape with the price written small."

Marta kept walking her eights. The baby's eyes had closed, her fists curled under her chin like a boxer resting between rounds.

"And what's your price, then?" Marta asked. "If you stay and give the berth to Tam, what does it cost?"

Rosalind was silent long enough that Marta glanced over. In the hooded light the girl's face was all planes and shadow, and she looked suddenly like what she would be at thirty — someone weathered into authority by hard choosing.

"I don't know yet," Rosalind said. "But I found something in the shipping records last week. Something about her blanket. About who she is. And I think — I think the price might be lower than we feared. Or higher. I won't know until I read the rest."

Marta stopped walking. The baby breathed against her collarbone, small and even, each exhale a warm coin.

"Girl," she said carefully, "when you open that door, whatever's behind it — you tell me first. Before you tell Corvane, before you tell God. Promise me that."

"I promise," Rosalind said, and Marta believed her, and was afraid.

Because she had seen the girl's eyes in the lamplight — bright, certain, already halfway into whatever secret the ledgers held — and Marta Kell had lived long enough to know that certainty, in a place like Corvane House, was the most dangerous currency of all. It bought revolutions. And somebody always collected the toll.

Above them, muffled through floors and stone, the front bell of Corvane House rang once — past midnight, unscheduled — and both women went still, and the baby slept on, and somewhere in the dark of the offices Director Corvane's candle was already moving toward the shipping room, where the old records waited in their shelves like witnesses sworn to silence.
