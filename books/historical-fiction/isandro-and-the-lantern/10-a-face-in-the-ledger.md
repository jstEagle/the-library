## 10. A Face in the Ledger

The archive room at the top of the Corvane warehouse was a place where paper went to be forgotten.

Rosalind had been sent there on the first Monday of March, when thaw-water still wept down the window glass and the company's new orders demanded a full accounting of freight tariffs from the past eleven years. It was work no one wanted and no one watched, which meant it came to her. *Don't want loudly*, she reminded herself as she climbed the stairs with an oil lamp in one hand and a ledger strap over her shoulder. Wanting loudly was how you got noticed, and being noticed, her father's drowning had taught her, was how you got taken.

But the truth — the truth she kept folded small beneath her ribs like a letter never mailed — was that being unnoticed had lately become a kind of wealth. Every hour she spent among the dead records was an hour no one looked too closely at the storeroom off the lamp room, where a child slept in a crate lined with sacking, breathing in the small, astonishing way babies breathe, as if the whole world were a song only they could hear.

Lantern was four months old now. Four months of conspiracy, of Marta's goat-milk gruel warmed on the kitchen's back burner, of Isandro humming sea-shanties into the dark until the crying wore itself out. Four months of Rosalind checking over her shoulder so often that she'd begun to dream in glances.

The archive room smelled of mouse droppings and tallow and the faint sweet rot of old glue. She set her lamp on the scarred table, pulled the first ledger toward her, and began.

*Freight manifest, winter 1836.* Barley, salt pork, iron nails, bolts of undyed wool. Names of shippers she'd never heard of, most of them dead now or fled upriver. The ink had gone brown as dried blood. Outside, the river muttered under its ice — that low, restless sound Isandro called *the singing*, though it wasn't singing yet, only the throat-clearing before it. Weeks away still. Perhaps days.

She worked through the morning, tallying, copying, her pen scratching like a small animal in the walls. And it was somewhere in the second ledger — 1839, the year before her father died — that she stopped scratching.

Because there, between an entry for pickled herring and one for birch timber, was a birth record.

Birth records did not belong in shipping manifests. She knew this the way she knew the weight of a coin by feel. Someone had entered it here deliberately, wedged where no auditor would think to look, in a hand smaller and more careful than the clerks' blunt script. She read it twice.

*Born to the household, 14th of October, 1839 — a daughter. Mother: E. Vasse, laundress. Father unrecorded.*

Father unrecorded. In eleven years of ledgers, Rosalind had never once seen those words. Hallowmere recorded everything — births, deaths, debts, drownings. A father unrecorded was not an oversight. It was an erasure.

Her hands had gone cold around the pen.

She thought of nothing else for the rest of the afternoon. She finished her tallies mechanically, numbers sliding through her mind like water through fingers, and when the bell rang for the shift's end she did not go down to the counting floor. She went instead along the gallery above the storerooms, where the shadows pooled deepest, and stood outside the lamp room door listening until she heard it: the soft, wet snuffle of a sleeping child, and beneath it Isandro's voice, reading aloud from the almanac as though the weather tables were scripture.

Safe. For now.

Then she went to find Marta.

* * *

Marta received the news the way she received most things — with a wooden spoon still in her hand and her eyes doing the talking.

"A laundress," Rosalind said quietly. They stood in the pantry between sacks of flour, close enough to speak without voices carrying. "E. Vasse. Marta, do you know the name?"

The spoon went still.

"I knew an Elsie Vasse," Marta said at last, each word laid down like a stone on a grave. "Worked laundry here, winters of '38, '39. Quiet girl. Pretty in a way she tried not to be." Her jaw tightened. "Gone before the spring of '40. Sent away sudden-like. Company said she stole linens."

"Did she?"

"Company says lots of things." Marta set the spoon down with exaggerated care, the way she did when her hands wanted to shake and she would not permit it. "That girl couldn't have stolen so much as a napkin with old Mrs. Hobb watching her. No. Something happened, and someone wanted it gone, and Elsie went with it."

"And the baby?"

"Never saw a baby." But Marta's eyes flicked upward — toward the director's wing, toward the cold apartments where Ambrose Corvane stayed his two weeks each winter — and Rosalind saw the thought arrive in her face like frost forming on a window. "Never saw one," Marta repeated, softer now. "Though I remember that winter the director's son took ill. Coughing blood by Christmas. Dead by the thaw, God rest him, and half of Hallowmere wept in the streets for young Edmund Corvane. Golden boy, they called him. Kind to servants. Too kind, some said, whispering behind their hands."

The pantry was very quiet. Somewhere below, ice groaned in the riverbed, long and slow, like something turning over in its sleep.

"Marta." Rosalind's voice came out thinner than she intended. "The blanket."

She didn't have to explain. They both knew the blanket — had known it since the whiteout night in December when Rosalind had carried the bundle in from the dock with her heart hammering against the note in its folds. *Forget this face.* The blanket had been fine wool once, dove-grey, embroidered at the corner with an anchor entwined in flowering briar. Not the sort of thing a laundress could afford. Not the sort of thing anyone abandoned lightly.

"The anchor's a company mark," Rosalind said. "Every crate on this dock bears it. But the briar —"

"— is a family crest," Marta finished. "I've pressed shirts enough to know the Corvane arms when I see them. Anchor and briar. I burned my tongue on that knowledge the night you brought her in, girl, and I've been swallowing it ever since."

They looked at each other across the flour-sacks, two women holding the same terrible arithmetic.

If the child were merely some laundress's illegitimate daughter — poor, unwanted, fatherless in every way that mattered — then hiding her was dangerous but simple. Someone had left her to be forgotten, and forgotten she could safely become. But if the embroidery meant what it must mean, if the father unrecorded was a father very much recorded elsewhere, grieved publicly, mourned in marble —

Then the child was not nobody. The child was a Corvane. And the man who had ordered her face forgotten was the same man whose name was stamped on Rosalind's indenture papers, on the roof over Lantern's head, on the boat that would carry Tam upriver come the break of ice.

"We say nothing," Marta said. It was not a question.

"To whom? Who would believe us?" Rosalind laughed, a small bleak sound. "A clerk with a drowned father's debt and a cook. We'd be turned out before supper, and Quill—" she stopped. Even saying the watchman's name felt like inviting him in.

"Quill is still sniffing about," Marta said grimly. "He asked me Tuesday whether I'd noticed 'any irregular provisions.' Irregular provisions. I fed him stew heavy enough to stop his mouth and light enough to keep him stupid." She picked the spoon back up, and her spine straightened into the posture she used when the master of the house was within shouting distance. "You dig no further, Rosalind Amberdale. You hear me? Some ledgers are closed for reasons."

But Rosalind had already decided otherwise, and Marta, who had raised sons of her own before the fever took them, saw the decision settle over the girl's face like snow.

"You're going back up there," Marta said.

"After the last bell. When everyone's gone to their bunks."

Marta sighed, long and resigned, and reached past her to press a heel of brown bread into her hands. "Then eat first. Revelation on an empty stomach makes for poor decisions."

* * *

The archive room at midnight was a different country. The lamp made an island of gold in all that black, and beyond its edge the rafters creaked and settled, and far below the river sang its first true notes of the season — thin, high, splintering sounds, like glass underfoot.

Rosalind had the 1839 ledger open before her and every other volume from that year stacked in a fortress around it. She worked by lamplight with the patience of someone defusing something that might kill her.

Elsie Vasse appeared in the household wage books through January of 1840 — then stopped mid-column, her final wages marked *paid in full, discharged*. No destination listed. Rosalind ran her finger down the column of names and found what she was looking for three lines above the laundress's last entry: *Edmund T. Corvane — household expenses, physician's visits, medicines.* The payments grew heavier through December, then ceased on the 9th of February, followed by a single line in the director's own cramped, upright hand:

*Funeral. Spare nothing.*

Spare nothing. The man who counted every nail, who audited candle-stubs, had spared nothing for his son. Grief like that did not simply dissolve. It curdled. It needed somewhere to go — and Elsie Vasse, discharged within days of the death, pregnant or recently delivered, had been the direction it flowed.

Rosalind sat back. Her breath fogged faintly in the cold air.

It fit. God help her, all of it fit. The expensive blanket. The note in a hand too educated for a servant — she had studied it a hundred times in memory, the sharp confident letters of someone who signed documents daily. *Forget this face.* Not the words of a desperate mother abandoning her child. The words of a man giving an order.

And he had chosen the Corvane dock to leave her on — not the church steps, not a neighbor's door — because the company's own property was the one place his reach was absolute, where no constable would come asking, where a found infant could vanish into company paperwork as cleanly as a laundress vanished from a wage book. He had expected the child to be carried to the parish home in Marsh Hollow, three towns over, where infants given to the parish rarely saw a second spring. He had not expected a clerk with a drowned father's debt and a habit of invisibility to be the one standing on the loading dock in the whiteout.

Rosalind pressed her palms against her eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark.

Here was the thing she could not stop looking at, once she saw it: she was holding the director's greatest shame in her two cold hands. A granddaughter. Alive, thriving, laughing toothlessly at Isandro's shanties, growing fat on Marta's gruel. Ambrose Corvane had spent four months believing that face forgotten — and it was sleeping forty feet below her feet, wrapped in sacking because the original blanket was hidden in the false bottom of Marta's trunk.

Leverage. The word arrived uninvited and she flinched from it, but it would not leave. If the berth for Tam was refused, if Quill found the child, if the noose drew tight — she held the one thread that could pull the whole great house of Corvane apart. Bastard granddaughter of the director, born on the wrong side of a laundress's sheet. Whispers in Hallowmere traveled faster than floodwater.

And danger — danger in equal measure. Because men who erased faces did not hesitate to erase the people who remembered them. Elsie Vasse had learned that. Rosalind was, at this moment, sitting in the archive room where the erasure had been filed away, holding the lamp that could light it or burn it.

She copied the birth entry onto a scrap of paper, folding it small, smaller, until it disappeared into the lining of her sleeve beside the original note she had never been able to make herself destroy. Then she returned the ledgers to their shelves exactly as she'd found them — spines aligned, dust undisturbed — and stood a moment with her hand flat on the 1839 volume as if taking leave of a person.

"I'm sorry," she whispered, though whether to Elsie Vasse, or Edmund Corvane dead these eight years, or the child asleep below, she could not have said.

On the stairs down, the river sang louder. She paused at the narrow window where the moonlight lay blue-white across the ice and listened to the cracks running out from the shore like veins, like roads, like futures opening. Weeks ago the choice had seemed simple: hide the child, save the child, keep the secret until keeping it cost nothing. Now the secret had a lineage and a dead father and a living grandfather who signed manifests in an upright, unforgiving hand.

Below her, through the floorboards, she heard it again — the small snuffle, the brief stirring cry, and then Isandro's voice rising gentle through the dark, singing the little one back down.

Rosalind laid her hand over her sleeve, over the folded paper resting against her wrist like a pulse.

Tomorrow she would tell Marta what she'd found. Tomorrow they would decide together what a face was worth. But tonight, crossing the frozen gallery toward the warm spill of lantern light below, Rosalind understood that the ground beneath her had shifted as surely as the river's — and that when the ice broke, it would not only carry boats.

It was already carrying the past, and it was headed straight for Ambrose Corvane's door.
