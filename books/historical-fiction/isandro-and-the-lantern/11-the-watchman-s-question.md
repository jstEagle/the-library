## 11. The Watchman's Question

Isandro had lit the lamps of Hallowmere for thirty-one winters, and he had learned to read the weather in the wick. A flame that leaned east meant the river would groan by morning. A flame that guttered blue meant snow coming down like a debt collector. But there was no lamp yet invented that could tell a man when trouble had walked through the company gate and started counting.

It came on a Tuesday, in the shape of a small man with a small notebook.

Isandro saw him first from the top of the north stair, where the lamplighter's rounds began each dusk at the great brass fixture above the warehouse doors. The stranger stood in the yard below with his hands folded behind him — not looking at the cargo, not looking at the ice on the river, but at the building itself, the way a man looks at a house he intends to buy, or bury. He wore a gray coat too fine for a clerk and too plain for a merchant, and his boots made no sound at all on the frozen mud, which Isandro found more troubling than anything else about him. In Hallowmere, everything made noise in winter. The cold shrieked out of the timber at night. The river spoke in its sleep. A man who moved silently had practiced it.

"Evening," Isandro called down, because silence invited questions worse than words.

The man looked up. His face was unremarkable in the way of faces built for forgetting — pale eyes, a mouth that had decided long ago not to smile and had kept its resolution. "Evening," he returned. His voice matched his boots. "You'd be the lamplighter."

"I'd be."

"They said you'd be old." He said it without malice, the way one notes a fact in a ledger. Then he produced the notebook from inside his coat, wet a thumb, turned a page. "Mr. Quill. Company business. There's property gone missing from these premises, and I've been engaged to find it."

*Property.* The word dropped into Isandro's chest like a stone into a well, and he listened for the splash of it and heard none — only the long dark falling. He kept his hands easy on the lighting pole. Thirty-one winters taught a man to keep his hands easy while his heart did its arithmetic.

"Lamps," Isandro said. "Somebody's been pinching lamps?"

Quill's pale eyes came up from the page. "Lamps."

"We lose a lantern now and then off the dock line. Ice takes them, boys knock them into the water. If that's the missing property, I can tell you where every one went, near enough."

For a moment Quill only looked at him. It was not an unfriendly look, which somehow made it worse. It was the look of a man filing something away in a drawer that locked. "I expect I'll want to talk to you again," he said. "You're on the premises at all hours, aren't you? Nights especially."

"Nights are when the lamps need lighting."

"And days?"

"Days are when they need filling."

A sound escaped him that might have been a laugh in a man built differently. He closed the notebook with two fingers, gently, as though it were sleeping. "Convenient work for a man who likes to go unnoticed."

"Nobody notices the lamplighter," Isandro agreed. "That's the trade."

"Mm," said Quill. "Yes. I'm beginning to see why they hired you before me."

And he walked off toward the offices, his boots saying nothing to the frozen ground, leaving Isandro alone under the great lamp with the flame leaning hard to the east and his own heart beating like something trying to get out of a room.

* * *

He thought all night about who to tell, and settled on nobody, and hated himself for settling on nobody, and understood perfectly well why he had.

Because the thing about a secret held by four people is that it is not a secret held by four people; it is a secret held by one person at a time, whoever happens to be standing nearest the danger. Tonight that was Isandro, on his rounds, with a watchman's pale eyes somewhere behind him in the dark. Tomorrow it might be Rosalind, or Marta, or — God help them all — Tam, coughing in his cot upstairs, too honest a boy to lie well even if anyone ever thought to ask him.

The storerooms at the back of the Corvane warehouse were the oldest part of the building, timber gone black with a century of pearl oil and lamp smoke, and the company had stopped using three of them years ago because the floors sagged. That was where Rosalind kept the child. Isandro had argued against it once, early on — *a sagging floor is a loud floor* — but there was nowhere better, and the sagging rooms had one gift: no one went there. Rats went there. Drafts went there. And now a baby named for a flame, sleeping in a crate lined with sacking, guarded by the creak of condemned beams.

Isandro made his rounds that night slower than usual, listening. The wind had come around to the northeast and the whole building ticked and shuddered like a ship, which was good — a noisy building hides a quiet watcher. He filled the reservoirs in the transit room, trimmed the wicks, wiped soot from the glass. Ordinary work. Holy work, Marta called it once, and he had laughed, but she hadn't been wrong. Every lamp lit in Hallowmere was a small argument against the dark, and Isandro had spent thirty-one years making arguments.

He was on the south gallery — the iron walkway that ran along the river face of the warehouse, forty feet above the ice — when he saw the light move below him.

Not a lamp light. Lamp lights stand still or swing; this one traveled smooth and low, close to the ice, pausing. At the dock stairs. At the loading doors. At the spot — and Isandro's stomach went cold in a way the winter had never managed — at the spot where the crate had been left in the whiteout, where Rosalind had found a bundle and a note that said *forget this face*, where everything began.

Quill stood on the ice with his lantern raised, studying the loading dock the way other men studied scripture, and Isandro understood with terrible clarity that this was not a search for stolen spoons. Someone had told this man what to look for, and where to begin looking, and *begin* was the word that frightened him. Beginnings. A note. A blanket. A night in February when the snow came down so thick the whole town had agreed afterward that nobody could have seen anything.

Except somebody had seen something. Somebody always saw something.

Isandro did the bravest and stupidest thing of his sixty-three years: he knocked the nearest lamp off its bracket.

He did it carefully, mind — thirty-one winters of knowing exactly how a lantern falls — tipping it so it struck the gallery rail, burst its glass, and poured burning oil down onto the ice below in a bright, spreading, howling ribbon. The cold ate the flame fast, but for half a minute the south face of the Corvane warehouse was lit up like a festival, and every soul within a quarter mile came running with buckets and shouting, and Quill's careful examination of the dock became a man stepping backward out of the way of fire, and then a man standing aside, and then a man gone, slipped off in the confusion before anyone thought to wonder who he was.

By the time the yard emptied, the story had already set like fresh ice: the old lamplighter's hand had slipped, the rail was iced, accidents happen. Isandro let them believe it. He swept the glass himself and paid for the lamp out of his own pocket, and Marta brought him tea in the kitchen at midnight and looked at him over the rim of the cup with her flat cook's eyes that missed nothing.

"You dropped that lamp on purpose," she said.

"Dropped's a strong word."

"Why?"

He wrapped his cracked hands around the cup. Through the kitchen's inner door, faintly, he could hear the small sounds of the child waking in the next room — the little snuffle and sigh that was the truest music left in his life — and Rosalind's voice, low, singing something without words because words carried.

"There's a man going through this place," Isandro said, "with a notebook. Asking after missing property. And tonight he stood on the ice and counted the boards of the loading dock."

Marta set down her cup. Her face did not change, but her hands did — they went still in a way that cooks' hands never go still, and that stillness frightened Isandro more than Quill's boots on the frozen ground.

"He watched the dock," she repeated.

"He watched the dock."

"Then he knows where she was left."

"He suspects where she was left. There's a difference, and the difference is us." Isandro drained the tea, which had gone bitter, or perhaps he had. "Marta. Whoever hired him hasn't given up. Whatever we thought — that the winter would bury it, that the note meant forget and forgotten means safe — it isn't over. They're looking. Quietly, patiently, with notebooks. Which means we have until they find something."

Marta looked toward the inner door for a long moment. When she spoke again her voice was steady, but Isandro had known her eleven years and heard the crack in it anyway. "Then we'd best make sure there's nothing to find."

"Aye," Isandro said softly. "There's the trouble, Marta. There's things you can't burn and things you can't unsay and—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the sagging rooms, toward the small warm weight of a sleeping child — "there's things you can't hide forever no matter how dark you keep them. Some things are made to be seen. That's what a lamp's for."

"That's poetry, Isandro."

"That's lamplighting. Same trade."

She almost smiled. Then the inner door opened and Rosalind came through it with the baby against her shoulder, hair loose, eyes shadowed from weeks of broken nights, and Isandro watched the girl who had lost her father to the river hold the child someone else had thrown away, and he thought: *they will have to go through all of us, and it will not be enough, unless it becomes more than hiding.*

"The lamp outside broke," Rosalind said, seeing the bandage on his hand. "Are you hurt?"

"Only my pride, and it's been hurt before."

She smiled the tired half-smile she wore like armor, and Isandro smiled back, and lied to her, and knew she probably knew it, and knew also that some kindnesses are lies and some lies are load-bearing walls.

When he finally climbed to his room above the lamp room, past midnight, past the wind's change, he took out the stub of pencil he kept for marking wick inventory and wrote on the back of an old chit, because writing a thing down makes it survivable:

*A watchman walks silent. He asks after what is missing. He has begun at the beginning. We have until he finds the middle.*

He read it twice, then fed it to the lamp room stove and watched the curl of paper become a curl of ash become nothing, which was more than could be said for Mr. Quill's notebook, whose pages did not burn.

Below, far down, the river shifted in its sleep and gave one long groan — early, too early, the ice already dreaming of breaking — and Isandro sat in the dark listening to spring come on like an army, and thought of the notice that would soon be nailed to the company door, the berth, the boat, the arithmetic of one seat and three souls, and understood that the watchman with the silent boots was not the worst thing coming for them.

The river was. And the river did not use notebooks.

* * *

Three mornings later, Isandro came down before dawn to begin the filling, and found the lamp room door already open.

Inside, sitting on his own stool at his own bench, notebook closed upon his knee, was Mr. Quill. He had helped himself to nothing, touched nothing, disturbed nothing — the tools lay exactly as Isandro had left them — and somehow that exactness was the most menacing thing about him. He simply sat, in the gray hour, among the hanging lamps, like a man in church.

"Locked doors," Isandro said, "usually mean something."

"They mean locks," said Quill. "Locks mean time. I have time." He unfolded from the stool, unhurried, and paused at the doorway, close enough that Isandro could smell nothing on him at all — no tobacco, no drink, no sweat; a man scrubbed of habits. "One more question, lamplighter. Just one, and then I'll leave you to your wicks."

Isandro waited.

"In February, the night of the whiteout." Quill's pale eyes were very still. "You were on the premises. Everyone agrees. Did you see anyone leave anything at the loading dock — or take anything away from it?"

The lamp room was warm around them, smelling of oil and brass and thirty-one years of faithful work. Somewhere above, in the sagging rooms, a child slept who had no name on any register in Hallowmere, and Isandro felt the whole weight of the question settle onto him like the winter itself, and chose his answer the way he trimmed a wick: precisely, and knowing it would burn.

"Whiteout night," Isandro said slowly, "nobody could see their own boots. I saw the snow, and I saw it stop, and in the morning I saw it had drifted clean over the dock." He met the pale eyes. "Whatever was left there, Mr. Quill — or taken — the snow kept it first, and it keeps better than I do."

Quill regarded him for a long, silent moment. Then he opened the notebook, wrote one unhurried line — Isandro never learned what it said and suspected he would spend the rest of his life learning what it said — closed it, and put it away.

"Yes," he said. "I rather thought you'd say something like that." He stepped past Isandro into the blue morning, and paused, and did not turn around. "They pay me to find things, lamplighter. But I keep them too. Everything anyone tells me. Everything they don't." A beat, soft as snowfall. "Your name is Isandro, isn't it. I've written it down twice now."

And he walked away across the yard, silent as ever on the frozen ground, leaving Isandro standing among his lamps with the terrible new knowledge of what it is to be marked — not accused, not arrested, just *noted* — while behind him, faint and far above in the old dark storerooms, a baby woke and cried, and the river groaned again beneath the ice, closer now, much closer, like something remembering it was owed.
