## 12. Tam Worsens

The fever came back the way winter came — not all at once, but in a slow tightening, a cold that got into the joints of the days until you couldn't remember when the warmth had left.

I knew it was bad because I could smell it. That was the strange thing nobody told you about being sick this long: your nose learned the geography of your own body. There was the smell of the good days, tallow and wool and Marta's onion broth seeping up through the floorboards of the room we rented above her kitchen. And there was the smell of the bad days, which was like river ice in the dark, like something drowned and kept. When I woke on the morning after the berth notices went up all over the company yard, that second smell was sitting on my chest like a cat.

I lay still and listened to the house.

Houses talk if you let them. Ours talked in the language of other people's lives: Marta's knives against the board, two floors down, keeping their patient rhythm; the kettle finding its voice; and beneath it all, the river, which never stopped talking, even frozen. Especially frozen. The ice spoke all night in long groans and small clicks, like a giant counting coins he didn't trust anyone else to hold.

Rosalind had left for the warehouse already. I knew because she'd done the thing she always did when she thought I was sleeping — stood in the doorway too long, breathing in a way that wasn't quite steady, and then touched the doorframe with two fingers on her way out, the way our mother used to touch the hull of Papa's boat before any launch. For luck. For keeping. As if wood and plaster could be asked to hold what she loved.

She'd tucked the blanket to my chin. She thought I wouldn't remember that either. But I remembered everything now; that was the trouble with fevers. They burned off all the small courtesies, the little lies of politeness, and left only the true things glowing in the ash.

*One berth,* the notices said. *One passenger, granted by petition, upon the last vessel prior to closure of navigation.* I had heard the men in the yard reading it aloud the day before, their voices full of the particular hunger Hallowmere woke with every spring — the hunger to be elsewhere before the flood shut the town like a fist.

One berth. Three of us who needed it, though only two of us knew we were counting.

* * *

The fever dreams were always about water.

Not drowning — I'd been afraid of drowning since Papa, the way other children were afraid of the dark, but the dreams weren't frightening anymore. In them the river was warm, which I knew was wrong, because the Hallowmere in March was cold enough to stop a heart in ten minutes. But dream-rivers run warm. In the dream I stood on the ice in my bare feet and the whole white sheet of it was lit from below, lantern-light rising up through the frozen water in long amber columns, and under the light swam shapes I almost recognized. My father, probably. Or the idea of him, which was nearly the same thing by now, four years on. He'd been eleven years gone from my knowing and I had only seven years of him banked, and some of those had worn thin as a twice-mended shirt.

In the dream he put his hand flat against the underside of the ice, and I put mine flat against the top, and between us was six inches of winter, which is both nothing and everything depending on which side you're standing on.

"Tamsen," he said, or the river said, using his mouth the way rivers do.

"I know," I said. "I'm trying to stay."

"You're trying to be quiet," he said. "That's different."

Then the ice sang — a high silver note, the sound it makes just before it gives — and I woke with my own name in my throat and daylight lying thin and gray across the window.

Someone was sitting beside the bed.

For one blind moment I thought it was the dream leaking through. Then I saw it was Dr. Pruen, the company physician, the one whose fees were a fable Rosalind refused to believe in. He had his bag open on his knees and his spectacles pushed up into his hair, and he was looking at me with an expression I'd learned to dread more than anger: the look of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Ah," he said. "Awake. Good. Stay awake for me, boy. Can you do that?"

"I can count backward from a hundred if you want," I said. My voice came out like paper.

Something moved at the corner of his mouth that wasn't quite a smile. "Who taught you that?"

"The last doctor. Before he stopped coming." We couldn't pay him. Nobody said so; everybody knew. Charity in Hallowmere arrived wrapped in silence or not at all.

Pruen took my wrist without asking, turned it palm-up, and pressed the nail until it blanched and flushed. Slow, the flush. Very slow. He looked at my eyes with a small candle-flame, made me follow it left and right, listened to my chest in four places, and each place took longer than the last. At the fourth place his face did the arithmetic again.

"Where's your sister?"

"Warehouse. It's tally season."

"Mm." He closed his bag, but didn't latch it, and sat turning his hat in his hands. He was not an old man, but Hallowmere winters had salted him early; his beard had gone gray at the edges like frost forming. "Tamsen. I'm going to say some things plainly, because I think you're the one in this family who can hear plain things. Your sister counts everything — debts, tonnage, candles. She'll want to turn what I say into figures she can argue with. You don't have to carry it alone, but someone has to carry it accurately."

My chest went tight around the breath I was holding, and not from the sickness. "Say it, then."

"Your lungs are compromised. The fever is a symptom, not the disease — the disease is years of this damp, this coal smoke, this air going in and out of a body at the bottom of a river valley." He said *river valley* the way a man points at a wall between you and where you need to go. "There is a version of this winter where you die of it in April. I've signed certificates saying so for other boys. I don't wish to sign yours."

"And the other version?"

He stood and went to the window. Below, the yard was loud with thaw-noise, men shouting, the scrape of sledge runners giving up their winter argument with the mud. Beyond the company fence, past the roofs, you could see the river — a great white road laid dead straight through the world, and at this distance it looked peaceful, which was the most dangerous lie the Hallowmere ever told.

"The other version is upriver," he said. "High ground. Dry air. Pine country, past the first rapids where the fog never settles. I had a patient — a girl your age, worse than you, I promise you — moved to her aunt's in Aldermoor two springs back. I expected to hear of her death by midsummer. Instead I got a letter in August with a pressed flower in it and an impertinent postscript." He turned back to me. "River air would not cure you. But this air will kill you, and the difference between those two sentences is the width of one boat journey."

"The berth," I said.

His eyebrows rose slightly. "You read the notices?"

"I listen." Everyone believed a sick boy in bed heard nothing. It was the single advantage of my situation and I hoarded it shamelessly. "One passenger. Petitions close when the ice breaks."

"Petitions close when the ice breaks," he agreed. "And the ice—" he cocked his head, listening to the deep groan that rolled up through the floorboards right then, long and low, like the river clearing its throat before speaking, "—the ice has begun rehearsing its departure. Days, Tamsen. Not weeks. If you travel after the break, there is no travel; the flood closes this town and every road out of it for three months, and three months of this air..." He didn't finish. He latched his bag instead, which finished for him.

"How much does the petition cost?" I asked, because I was my sister's brother and I counted too.

"That isn't—"

"How much?"

"If the director grants it," Pruen said quietly, "nothing but the asking. Which is sometimes the steepest price in this town." He settled his hat on his head. "Tell your sister. Today. Don't dress it up for her and don't pare it down. She'll try to give you the berth and take the fever herself if she could figure out how to fit it in the manifest. Let her try. That's what sisters are for. But she should choose knowing, not guessing." At the door he paused. "And boy — the child in the storeroom. Whatever she is to your sister, keep her warm. Sickrooms forgive a lot, but I notice things. We doctors and lamplighters, we're paid to notice."

He was gone before I could decide whether to be afraid.

* * *

I slept, and dreamed shallowly, and woke to the sound of Rosalind crying, which she would have denied under oath.

It was evening. The lamp outside our window had been lit — I could tell by the particular gold slant of it through the shutter-cracks, warmer than moonlight, steadier than any flame but Isandro's. And Rosalind was at the table with Dr. Pruen's bill of advice copied out in her careful clerk's hand, and Marta stood behind her with flour to her elbows, and neither of them knew my eyes were open, so I did the decent thing and kept them mostly shut and listened.

"He says upriver," Rosalind said. Her voice was very level, which meant it was costing her everything. "Aldermoor. Aunt Verity would take him — she wrote last autumn, she's wanted him—"

"Then it's simple," Marta said. "You petition. You've served four years without a black mark. Who else wants it more?"

The silence that followed had a shape, and the shape was a baby.

"One berth," Rosalind said, barely aloud.

"Yes," Marta said. "And one child who cannot be on any manifest, and one sister who cannot breathe, and one woman who is too old to start over anywhere new and too stubborn to be asked." Marta's hand landed on Rosalind's shoulder; I heard it, the soft thump of it, the way our mother's hand used to land. "You're doing sums, girl. Stop it. Some sums eat the counter."

"There has to be another answer."

"There's always another answer," Marta said. "It's usually worse."

They thought I couldn't hear. They thought the fever had me too deep to fish out. But I lay there in the lamp-gold and the broth-smell and the river talking its slow coin-counting talk under everything, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like the ice singing, exactly what was happening in that room and what it would cost.

Rosalind was going to give me the berth. I knew it the way you know weather in your bones. She would walk into that manifest signing and surrender her own petition, the one escape she'd earned in four gray years, and she would do it smiling at me and lying with her whole face, and afterward she would stay in Hallowmere with Lantern hidden in the storerooms and Quill — I'd heard about Quill, everyone heard everything — circling closer like a crow over a lambing field. And if the hiding failed, my sister would be standing in the wreckage of it with no passage out and no position and no protection, because she'd spent hers on me.

And the worst part — the part that made my eyes sting against the pillows — was that I would let her. A month ago I would have grabbed that berth with both hands, because wanting to live isn't a sin. But that was before I understood the arithmetic. One passage. Two people who couldn't be left behind. And a third person, small and warm and nameless on purpose, whose whole existence was a crime someone powerful had ordered forgotten.

If I took the berth quietly, I saved myself and spent my sister.

Unless.

Dr. Pruen's words sat in me like a swallowed coal: *someone has to carry it accurately.* And Director Corvane — I'd seen him once, from the warehouse loft, a tall gray man walking the dock where the baby had been found, stopping, looking down at the planks as though the wood owed him an explanation. Rosalind thought I slept through everything. I'd heard her and Isandro whispering in the lamp room about the director's questions, his lingering, his odd grief-shaped silences. I'd heard Marta burn the blanket — the smell of scorched embroidery had come up through the floorboards two nights past, and Rosalind thought the smoke was the stove, and I had lain there understanding that grown people were sacrificing pieces of the truth to buy pieces of safety, and wondering when it would be my turn to pay.

The ice sang again, longer this time. Through the window, down the black street, I could see Isandro's ladder leaning against the lamp post, and the old man himself climbing it with the flame cupped in his brass, lighting the way for a town that never thanked him. That was the trade, wasn't it? That was the whole secret of the lamp room. You gave your light away every night trusting the world to be there tomorrow to walk home through.

I didn't want to die at sixteen in a rented room above a kitchen that smelled like onions and kindness. I wanted Aldermoor and pine air and my aunt Verity's orchard and letters with wax seals and a life on the far side of the flood. Wanting it wasn't the sin. But taking it silent, letting Rosalind bleed for it unknowing — that was.

So I did the hardest thing a bedridden boy can do.

I coughed — really coughed, letting the fever have me for a minute, honest and ugly — until the voices at the table broke off, until chairs scraped, until my sister's hands were on my face checking heat and her eyes were wide and wet and terrified, and I looked up at her, at the girl who had raised me half her life on a drowned man's debt, and I said:

"Rosie. Ask him for the berth. Ask Corvane tonight."

"I will," she whispered. "I already—"

"No." I caught her wrist. My grip was nothing, a bird's grip, but she went still. "Ask him for *mine*. And then ask him for hers."

She stared at me. Behind her, Marta had gone very still, flour drifting in the lamplight like snow falling indoors.

"Tam—"

"The ice is going to break," I said, and out on the river, right on cue, the deep bell-note cracked across the town, long and silver and final, and every dog in Hallowmere began to howl. "And when it does, there won't be time to hide anything anymore. Ask him for both of us. Tonight."
