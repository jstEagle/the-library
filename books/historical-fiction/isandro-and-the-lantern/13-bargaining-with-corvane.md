## 13. Bargaining with Corvane

The petition paper cost me three hours of wages.

I want that recorded somewhere, though there is nowhere left to record it — not in the Corvane ledgers, where every entry is a life compressed into tons and tallow, and not in my own memory, which had begun to feel like a ledger too, columns of debts and small mercies balanced nightly by lamplight. Three hours of wages for a single sheet of rag paper, stiff and cream-colored, the kind intended for gentlemen's correspondence. Mr. Hobbes at the stationer's on Weir Street raised his eyebrows when I asked for it, and again when I asked for sand to dry the ink, and a third time when I asked him not to look at me so.

"A fine sheet," he said. "Who's it for, then?"

"The river," I said, because it was nearly true, and he laughed as if clerks made jokes now, and wrapped the paper in brown paper as though it were something alive that might bruise.

I wrote the petition four times before I got it right. The first draft begged. I burned it in Marta's stove and watched the words curl — *I am sixteen years old and my brother is dying* — because begging was a coin I could spend only once, and I did not yet know what it would buy. The second draft was all facts: dates of service, sums owed, the terms of my father's indenture transferred down to me like a coat inherited from a drowned man. Too cold. A man like Corvane would read coldness and feel confirmed in his own. The third draft I do not remember writing; I think I fell asleep over it and Isandro carried me to my pallet behind the lamp room, and in the morning the ink had smeared where my cheek had rested, a gray bruise across the middle of the page like a thumbprint on the moon.

The fourth draft I wrote standing up, at the tally desk, with the warehouse breathing around me — crates of salt pork and bolts of wool and barrels of pitch, all of it waiting for the ice to go soft so the river could carry it away to somewhere warmer than Hallowmere. I wrote:

*To Ambrose Corvane, Director. I, Rosalind Amberdale, clerk of the second tier, indentured against the debt of Josiah Amberdale, diver, deceased, petition for the passenger berth of the spring manifest on behalf of Tam Amberdale, aged nine, whose lungs are failing and who cannot survive another winter in this valley. I ask nothing for myself. I ask only that you let a child ride the water before the water closes.*

Then I sat down, and looked at what I had written, and understood that I had lied twice in four lines. I asked nothing for myself — but if Tam went, I stayed, and staying meant keeping Lantern hidden another season in a town that would soon be sealed shut, cut off, searchable from door to door by whatever men Ambrose Corvane chose to send. And I asked only that you let a child ride the water — as though the child were not already riding it in Corvane's mind, or being thrown from it, whichever way his grief had decided to fall. Every word I put on that page was a step across a frozen river, listening for the crack underneath.

I folded the paper. I put it inside my shirt, against my breastbone, where it warmed to the temperature of my fear.

* * *

The director kept rooms on the top floor of the company house, and no one went up there. This was understood the way weather is understood — not as a rule but as a fact of the world, like the direction the wind came from or the month the ice went out. In six years of tallying cargo I had never seen Ambrose Corvane descend past the second landing except on the day of his arrival in December, when he had walked the warehouse floors in his gray coat like a man measuring a coffin, and then ascended again, and the whole building below him had exhaled.

So when I climbed the stairs on the morning after the ice first sang, each flight felt like a transgression against gravity itself. The stairwell smelled of cold stone and lamp oil — Isandro's oil, I realized; even here, at the top of the company's power, the light came from my old friend's rounds — and the walls were hung with maps of the river system rendered in faded inks, the tributaries branching like the veins in Tam's thin wrists. On the third landing a window faced the dock, and I made the mistake of looking out. The white scar of the loading planks. The place where a bundle had lain in the snow while the blizzard stitched its white seam over everything. From this height it was such a small space. From this height a child could be an oversight.

The door at the top was oak, blackened with age, and had no bell. I stood before it with my fist raised for longer than I will ever admit — long enough for my breath to fog and fade, fog and fade, a small metronome counting out my cowardice — and then I remembered Tam asleep that morning with his lips going blue-gray at the edges when the coughing finally released him, and I knocked.

"Enter." His voice was not loud. It did not need to be.

The room beyond was not what I expected. I don't know what I expected — gold, perhaps, or the bare austerity of a man who has decided the world owes him. Instead: books, hundreds, shelved crookedly as though placed in haste; a desk buried under papers; a chart of the river pinned to the far wall with red thread running along the channel like a wound; and in the corner, half-hidden by a screen, a boy's sea chest, brass-buckled, travel-worn, with a pair of skating boots tucked beneath it, one upright and one fallen on its side, exactly as they might have been kicked off in a hurry eight years ago and never touched since.

Ambrose Corvane sat at the desk, and he was smaller than I remembered. Not shrunken — compacted, the way ice compacts snow into something harder and heavier than it began. His gray hair was combed with soldierly precision. His hands, resting on the papers before him, were clean and still. He did not look up for a long moment, and in that moment I saw that he had been reading a letter, and that the letter was old — the fold-lines worn soft as cloth, the ink gone the color of dried blood — and that his thumb was pressed flat against the signature as if to hold the writer's hand through the paper.

Then the thumb lifted. The letter slid beneath a ledger. The face that came up was the face from December: cold-eyed, exacting, closed as a strongbox.

"You are the Amberdale girl."

"I am, sir."

"The one who found the—" He stopped. The pause lasted half a heartbeat, but I had spent six years learning to hear half-heartbeats in the company's accounts, the spaces between numbers where things were hidden. "Found the irregularity on the dock. In January."

An irregularity. So that was the word. A child, an infant, left in a blizzard with a note like a knife: *forget this face.* An irregularity.

"Yes, sir. I reported it to the watch, as required."

Something moved at the very back of his eyes — not suspicion exactly, more like a man testing a floorboard with his weight. "You are diligent, then."

"I try to be, sir."

He gestured at the chair opposite the desk. It was a gentleman's chair, upholstered in green velvet gone bald at the arms, and I sat in it feeling like a cat that had wandered into church. My petition paper came out of my shirt warm and slightly damp, and I laid it on the desk between us, and the sound it made — that soft papery whisper — seemed obscenely loud.

Corvane did not pick it up. He looked at it the way he must have looked at shipping manifests, letting it wait, letting it understand its place. When he finally drew it toward him, he broke the seal with one thumbnail and read it through once quickly, and then — and this I had not anticipated — he read it again, slowly, his lips moving faintly around certain phrases. *Whose lungs are failing. Before the water closes.*

"Tam," he said. "That is the brother."

"Yes, sir."

"Nine years old." Not a question. He knew. Of course he knew; he knew everything in his ledgers, and my family had been an entry in them since before I could write. "Your father dove for pearls off the Merrow Shoals. Winter contract, against regulation. The company paid the burial and applied the balance to his debt." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse — the flat recitation of arithmetic, a man's death reduced to a credit column. "You have been servicing that debt since you were ten."

"Since I was ten," I agreed.

"And now you sit in my office asking me for—" He turned the petition over, as if the back might contain the true request. "A berth. For a dying child. When there are men in this company with twenty years of service who have petitioned for that same berth."

"I know, sir."

"Do you." He set the paper down. "Do you also know that the berth is worth more than your entire remaining indenture? That passage upriver before the flood season is, this year, the most valuable object in Hallowmere? Men have offered me horses for it. Land."

"I can't offer you anything, sir." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "That's what the petition says. I'm not trading. I'm asking."

"Asking." He tasted the word. Outside, far below, the river groaned — a long, deep sound, like some enormous animal turning in its sleep, and both of us paused to listen to it, and in that shared pause something shifted in the room, some pressure equalizing, though I couldn't have said what.

"The doctor tells you river air would save him," Corvane said at last. It wasn't a question either, but I answered it.

"He tells me the valley air is killing him. He tells me that above the falls, where the pines start, a child with Tam's lungs can live to be an old man." I heard my own voice fray on the last words and hated it and let it fray anyway, because hiding it would have been its own kind of lie. "Sir, I have watched him cough since November. I have slept beside him and counted the seconds between the fits getting shorter every week. I am not asking you to feel what I feel. I am only telling you what the doctor says, and asking you to believe that I have no reason to lie about it."

For a long time he said nothing. He rose, instead, and went to the window — the same window I had passed on the stairwell, facing the dock — and stood with his hands clasped behind his back, and I sat in the bald green chair and looked at the room while he thought I wasn't looking at him.

I looked at the sea chest. At the skating boots, one fallen on its side. At the shelf nearest the window, where among the ledgers and atlases there stood a small jar of river glass — those smoothed, frosted pebbles children collect on shores — and beside the jar, propped against the books, a drawing in charcoal, done by an unsteady hand: two figures on the ice with fishing lines, one tall, one small, their faces smudged almost to nothing by years of light, but the small figure unmistakably laughing.

And I understood — the way you understand a current only when you're already in it — that I was not the first person to stand in this room and ask Ambrose Corvane to let someone go upriver. That once, years ago, there had been a son in these rooms, and boots kicked off in a hurry, and laughter in charcoal, and then the river had done what our river does, and taken him, and the man at the window had spent eight years pressing his thumb against old letters to hold a hand that was no longer there.

The son who had loved a laundress. The son who had fathered a girl-child now sleeping in a flour bin behind Marta's kitchen, wrapped — no, not wrapped anymore; Marta had seen to that — but once wrapped in a blanket embroidered with a Corvane monogram.

My secret sat in my chest like swallowed ice, and the warmth of the room began to melt it, and I was terrified of what it would do when it broke loose.

"You look at things," Corvane said suddenly, still facing the window. "In the warehouse. I've watched you. You look at the cargo the way the old factors used to — as if it could tell you something." He turned. His eyes were dry and bright and terribly tired. "Your father was the same. He'd read the water before a dive. Swore the river spoke to him." A pause. "It lied to him, of course."

"Perhaps," I said quietly. "Or perhaps it told him the truth and he chose to go anyway. People do that, when what they need is on the other side of the danger."

The silence that followed had a different quality than the ones before it. He crossed back to the desk slowly and stood looking down at my petition, and his hand came out and rested on it — not picking it up, just resting there, fingers spread, as though taking its pulse.

"There is a woman upriver," he said, and his voice had changed — lower, rougher at the edges, the voice under the voice. "My late wife's sister. She writes to me every autumn and I do not answer. She lost her own children. Her letters say—" He stopped. Recomposed himself, the way ice recomposes over open water, seamlessly, as though the gap had never existed. "Her letters say there is always room in a house for one more grief."

"Sir—"

"I know what you want me to say." He cut across me, authority flooding back into his frame like a tide returning. "You want a promise. I do not make promises about things the river has not yet decided. The berth exists. Your brother is sick — I believe you; your face believes itself. But the manifest is signed when the ice breaks, and not before, and until then there will be a hundred men at my door and a hundred petitions, and I will weigh them all." He slid the paper into a drawer — my three hours of wages, my four drafts, my whole heart in four lines — and closed it with a click that sounded final as a lock on a tomb. "The ice will decide, Miss Amberdale. It decides everything in this valley. When it breaks, come to me. Come to me *first*, before the others, and we will see what kind of man I have become over the winter."

It was not a yes. It was not a no. It was a door left unlatched in a house of locked doors, and I knew better than to press against it.

"Thank you, sir," I said, and stood, and my legs nearly failed me — not from weakness but from the strange lightness of having survived the audience, of having been seen by the most powerful man in Hallowmere and walked out of his office still holding the thing I could not show him.

At the door, his voice stopped me one last time.

"The Amberdale girl." I turned. He was seated again, the mask fully restored, but his hand — I could not help noticing — had drifted to the drawer that held my petition, resting on the wood above it. "When the child was found on the dock. In January. The note that came with it." His eyes held mine across the whole length of the room, and behind them, far behind them, something flickered — grief, or its fossil. "What became of the note?"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below us the ice groaned again, longer this time, and I thought: *it is beginning; it is all beginning.*

"It went to the watch, sir," I said. "With everything else."

He held my gaze a moment longer — long enough for me to learn whether lying to Ambrose Corvane felt different from any other lie, and it did; it felt like diving, like the pressure building in the ears past the point where you can clear them — and then he nodded once, dismissed me, and bent over his papers, and I went down the stairs past the maps and the window and the small white scar of the dock, and out into the morning, where the air smelled of thaw, and the river beneath its rotting ice was singing louder than it had sung in living memory.
