## 14. Quill Closes In

Marta Kessel had kept the Corvane kitchen through nine winters, and she knew the sound of every foot that belonged in it. The scuff of the delivery boys' boots. The deliberate tread of the clerks come begging bread at odd hours. The shuffle of Rosalind Amberdale when she carried a certain small weight against her chest beneath her shawl, moving like a woman made of glass and secrets.

And now this: a new sound, soft-soled and patient, pacing the corridor outside her kitchen door at an hour when no honest man walked the halls.

She did not look up from her dough. She kneaded it harder than it needed, driving her fists into the pale mass of it, listening. The footsteps stopped. Started again. Stopped. A man learning the geography of a place he intended to search.

"Your fire's low," said a voice behind her, and she did not jump, because jumping was a confession, and Marta Kessel had not confessed to anything in fifty-one years.

She turned, flour on her knuckles, and found Mr. Quill standing in her doorway with his hat in his hands, as if courtesy were something he had read about in a book and was now attempting from memory. He was a narrow man, dry as a December reed, with eyes the color of dishwater left standing too long. He'd arrived three days past with the spring ledgers, introduced himself as an auditor of company property, and since then had been everywhere at once — in the counting room, in the storerooms, down by the dock, asking his soft questions in his soft voice until people answered just to make him stop.

"Fires burn low before they're fed," Marta said. "Same as most things. You wanting bread, or are you wanting answers? Because I charge for one and don't have the other."

Quill smiled. It was the smile of a man who had learned that teeth unsettled people, so he showed them sparingly. "Bread, if you're offering. And conversation, if it's free."

"It's free." She slapped the dough down and turned to the oven. "Sit, then. Chairs don't cost either."

He sat at the scarred worktable where Rosalind took her meals, where Tam had sat last autumn when he could still walk to the kitchen on his own thin legs, where Isandro drank his morning tea with two lumps of sugar and claimed it was for medicinal purposes. Marta knew every ring in that wood. She set a heel of yesterday's loaf before Quill and a knife beside it, then went back to her work and let the silence do what silences do — press on a man until he filled it.

Quill cut the bread into neat, identical slices and ate them without hurry. "Cold winter," he offered.

"Cold winter."

"Hard on provisions. I hear the stores came up short. Lamp oil especially."

"Lamp oil goes where lamps go," Marta said. "You'd have to ask the lamplighter where that is. Though if you can't find a lit lamp in Hallowmere after dark, Mr. Quill, I'd question your eyes before I questioned his honesty."

The smile again, thinner. "I'm not questioning anyone's honesty, Mrs. Kessel. I'm auditing it. There's a difference. Honesty is a virtue; auditing is a profession." He brushed crumbs from his fingers with a handkerchief that appeared and vanished like a magician's dove. "Company property has gone missing, you see. Small things, at first. A blanket. Some milk that never reached the stores. A tin of oil. Individually, nothing. Together—" he spread his dry hands, "—together they begin to look like a pattern. And patterns interest me. Patterns are how a man makes his living."

Marta's hands kept their rhythm in the dough. In-out, fold, turn. She had raised two sons on this motion, buried a husband with these hands, and held a screaming infant in the dark of the wash-house while its mother's milk failed and Marta's own had been forty years dried up. Some motions a body does not forget.

"Patterns," she repeated. "In my experience, men who go looking for patterns find them whether they're there or not. Same as men who go looking for witches."

"Mm," said Quill, and rose, and put on his hat, and paused at the door as though remembering something pleasant. "That's a fine big kettle you keep on the back burner. Milk in it?"

"Porridge."

"At this hour?"

"I cook ahead. Poor families cook ahead, Mr. Quill. Rich ones eat on schedule and think it's character." She met his dishwater eyes and let her face do what her face did best: nothing at all. "Good day to you."

He went. The footsteps faded down the corridor, unhurried, patient, and Marta stood over her dough with her heart going like a trapped bird and her hands steady as stones.

* * *

She fed Lantern at noon, in the little room off the laundry where Rosalind had rigged a cot from crates and quilts, where the child slept through hammering and shouting but woke at the change of Marta's footsteps on the floorboards — knew her, somehow, the way a plant knows a window.

Lantern was four months old now, round-cheeked and solemn-eyed, with a fist that gripped whatever was offered — a finger, a ribbon, the chain of Isandro's pocket watch with the ferocity of someone who intended to keep it. When Marta lifted her, the baby made a sound low in her throat, half purr, half sigh, and pressed her face into the warmth of Marta's neck, and Marta felt the familiar treachery happen all over again: the softening, the surrender, the terrible arithmetic by which a child's weight in your arms outweighed every reason to hold her.

"There now," Marta murmured, settling onto the crate with the feeding cloth. Outside, the river muttered under its ice. The whole town had begun talking about the ice lately, the way people talk about a sick relative — in lowered voices, with glances toward the window. *Singing*, the fishermen called it, when the frost groaned in the night. *Getting ready.*

"You're going to be the death of us both, little one," Marta told her softly, and Lantern blinked up at her with eyes that were going from newborn gray to something darker, something Marta had decided privately not to think about. "You know that? Fat cheeks like yours and eyes like *that*, sitting under a roof full of auditors. We'll need more than luck."

She said it lightly, the way one says such things to babies. But the words sat in her afterward, cold and specific, because that afternoon the knocking started.

Quill worked his way through the servants' quarters like a man reading a ledger line by line. Marta heard him in the boot room. Heard him in the linen store, where drawers opened and closed with small courteous clicks. He spoke to each person alone, briefly, and each person emerged looking vaguely soiled, as though the interview had been perfectly polite and perfectly awful, like being searched by fog.

When his knock came to the laundry, Marta was ready.

Rosalind was not there — Marta had sent her to the market an hour earlier with instructions to take the long road and stay gone till dark, and Rosalind, who understood everything, had gone without a word, though her face as she left had aged ten years in a doorway. So it was Marta alone who opened the laundry door with a basket of washing on her hip and Lantern — God forgive her, there was no other place — asleep in the very basket, buried beneath linens, only the small rise and fall of her breathing to betray her.

Quill looked at the basket. Marta watched him look at it. Watched his eyes do their patient arithmetic: a basket of wash, heavy enough; a corner sagging where a bundle ought not sag; and beneath the smell of soap and wet wool — faint, unmistakable, sweet — the smell of a baby.

"My knees are bad," Marta said, before he could speak. "I carry what I can and drag the rest. You want to inspect a basket of sheets, Mr. Quill, you're welcome to them, but you'll be folding them after."

Something flickered across his face — disappointment, or respect, or the pleasure of a man who finds exactly the resistance he expected, which confirms the world is orderly. "No inspection today, Mrs. Kessel. Just questions. Do you know anything about a blanket? Wool, white, embroidered at the hem. Blue thread. An anchor, they say, or a bird — accounts differ."

"A blanket," Marta said.

"Left here in the winter. With the child who isn't here either." His voice stayed mild, conversational, the voice of a man discussing weather. "It's curious, Mrs. Kessel. Half the household has seen a baby that officially doesn't exist, and none of them will say so, and the other half says nothing at all, which is worse. Silence has a shape. I've gotten good at seeing shapes."

"The blanket went to the rag bin months ago," Marta said. "Embroidered things wear out same as plain ones."

"Did it." He nodded slowly. "I wonder that you remember, then. Where it went, I mean. Most people wouldn't remember a rag."

"Most people didn't raise seven children on mending. A blanket with good embroidery is worth saving the wool from." She shifted the basket on her hip, and beneath the linens Lantern stirred, sighed, and — Marta felt the whole world tilt — began to fuss. Not crying. Not yet. A small waking sound, a grumble, the complaint of an infant drifting between sleep and hunger.

Quill went very still.

Marta talked. It was the only thing to do — fill the air, fill the silence, drown the small sound in the larger noise of herself. "That'll be the cat," she said loudly. "Great yellow devil gets in the linen and I can't keep him out, and he yowls like a soul in torment when he's shut in somewhere. Shoo!" She stamped her foot, absurdly, an old woman frightening a cat that wasn't there, and the grumble under the linens stilled — hushed, mercifully, by some instinct or accident — and Quill stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands, listening to a laundry that contained, by his arithmetic, a cat.

"Well," he said at last. "The cat."

"The cat."

"Give my regards to the lamplighter," he said, turning. "And to Miss Amberdale, when she's back from her long road." And he smiled his sparing smile and went, and this time his footsteps were not unhurried at all.

* * *

She waited for full dark.

Not prudence alone — though prudence, always, first — but something older than prudence, some sense of ceremony, of fitness. What she had to do deserved darkness. It deserved, perhaps, not to be done at all, and she knew no hour would make it right, so midnight would serve.

Rosalind came at the appointed time, pale and tight-mouthed from the market, having heard already — news traveled faster than people in Hallowmere — that Quill had been to the laundry. Isandro came too, unasked, carrying his lantern with the shutter turned so it threw its light only downward, a small captive moon. They gathered in the wash-yard behind the kitchens, between the woodpile and the wall, under a sky scoured clean of stars by low cloud, and Marta took from beneath her apron the folded shape she had carried against her stomach all evening like a second heartbeat.

The blanket.

White wool, worn soft as breath at the folds. Along the hem, worked in blue thread by some mother's patient hand — a laundress's hand, Rosalind had discovered in the shipping records, a laundress who had signed nothing and vanished everything and was beyond anyone's finding — ran a border of anchors and small birds, and in one corner, stitched in letters tiny enough to hide in a palm, a name: *E.*

They had kept it hidden these many weeks, wrapped in oilcloth under a floorboard, and they had kept it because it was the only true thing the child owned. No name of her own, no claimant, no history but this: wool, blue thread, a single letter. To destroy it was to erase the last proof of where she came from.

"She'll want it someday," Rosalind said. Her voice cracked on the word *someday*. She was sixteen and had carried a secret through a winter and it showed in her now, the strain showing, like a rope beginning to sing under load. "Marta, she'll want to know—"

"She'll want to *be*," Marta said. "Knowing comes after being, girl. Ask any orphan." She heard her own harshness and gentened it, laying her free hand along Rosalind's cheek, rough palm against young skin. "Listen to me. Quill saw the basket. He knows the shape of what's under the sheets — maybe not his certainty yet, but near enough. Tomorrow he goes to the director, or he digs the floorboards, or he sets a man to watch the laundry door, and when he finds that blanket he won't hold wool in his hands, he'll hold evidence. Embroidery matches records. Records name names. That name is Corvane, and a Corvane child hidden in a cellar is a scandal that can bury everyone who ever fed her — you, me, Isandro, and her too. Do you understand? It isn't a blanket anymore. It's a noose with pretty birds on it."

Isandro said nothing for a while. He was an old man who wore his griefs the way other men wore coats, close-fitted and daily, and when he finally spoke it was slowly, feeling after words. "I burned my daughter's shoes," he said. "After the river. Her good shoes, for church. I stood at the stove and I fed them in and I watched them curl, and I have regretted it every day since, and it was still the right thing, because what a fire takes it keeps safe from worse hands." He looked at the folded wool in Marta's arms. "Some things you keep by losing them."

The wind came off the river and set the shutters creaking somewhere above them, and beneath everything, low and continuous, the ice sang its long note, like a drawn bow, like something enormous turning over in sleep.

Marta knelt at the copper ash-barrel where the laundry burned its refuse. She struck the flint. She fed the fire first with shavings, then splints, until it stood up orange and willing, and then she held the blanket over it a moment longer — one moment, no more; she had promised herself one moment — and let her thumb pass once over the small stitched letter, the *E*, the whole of a name, the whole of a mother's testimony written in thread: *you were here, you were loved, someone made this for you.*

Then she let it go.

Wool burns slowly, reluctantly. It blackens and curls and smells like hair, like a living thing protesting, and the blue thread caught last, flaring briefly green-gold, so that for one instant the anchors and the little birds blazed visible in the dark — a whole hemful of hope going up in a single breath — and then it was ash, and the wind off the river took the light flakes and scattered them toward the water, and Marta Kessel, who had not wept at her husband's graveside because there were children watching, knelt at an ash barrel in the dark and wept for a blanket.

Rosalind knelt with her and put her arms around her shoulders, and they stayed that way while the fire ate itself down. Isandro stood apart, lantern lowered, keeping watch the way he had kept watch all winter, and after a time he said quietly:

"There's a light moving by the east dock. Wrong hour for it."

They rose. Through the gap in the wall Marta saw it too: a lantern swinging low and methodical along the dockside, pausing at intervals — at the loading steps, at the coal store, at the exact spot, she would have bet her life, where a bundle had once been left in a whiteout with a note that said *forget this face*.

Quill. Searching in the dark now, which meant the daylight courtesies were done, which meant tomorrow would bring warrants or watchers or worse.

"He'll come to the kitchen again," Marta said. She wiped her face with her apron and her voice came out level, hard-used, a cook's voice, a voice that had fed people through famine and meant to feed them through this. "Let him. I've lied to better men than him and buried the ones I couldn't lie to. But girl—" she caught Rosalind's wrist, and felt beneath her own fury the fear she had been refusing all day, growing now, taking shape, "—the ice is singing, and the boats come soon, and whatever you mean to do about berths and directors and faces that powerful people want forgotten — do it quick. We're done hiding. A man like that doesn't stop searching. He only stops pretending."

Above them, along the frozen river, the ice groaned again — long, rising, almost like a voice — and in the ash barrel the last of the embroidery glowed red, then went dark.
