## 15. The Ice Begins to Sing

The river began to sing on a Tuesday, though no one in Hallowmere would have called it singing at first. They would have called it groaning, or moaning, or — as the dockhands did, spitting into the slush — the ice complaining like an old man made to rise too soon from a warm chair.

But Isandro had lived beside this water for fifty-one years, and he knew its voices the way a priest knows his psalms. There was the whisper of first frost in October, thin as silk drawn over a drumhead. There was the deep silence of midwinter, when the river slept so soundly beneath its grey lid that you could walk across it and forget there was ever any current at all. And then there was this — late March, the sun climbing higher each day, warmth seeping down through the ice like a secret told too many times — this was the singing. A long, low, resonant note that came up through the pilings of the warehouse and through the soles of his boots and settled somewhere behind his sternum, where grief keeps its rooms.

The ice was going to break. Not today, not tomorrow, but soon. Days, perhaps. A week if the cold returned to argue with the sun. And when it broke, the last boat of the season would pull away upriver, and Hallowmere would seal itself behind the floodwaters like a jar with its lid screwed tight, and nothing would come in or go out until the high water fell in June.

Isandro stood on the loading dock in the blue hour before dawn, his lamp unlit in his hand because he did not need it yet, listening to the river sing.

He was thinking about a child who could not sleep.

* * *

Lantern — for so they still called her, though Isandro had begun, privately, to think of her by other names, longer names, names with weight enough to carry her through a life — had been restless for three nights running. Marta blamed the moon. Tam, feverish in his cot above the kitchen, said the baby could hear the ice, the way dogs hear whistles pitched beyond human range. But Isandro thought it was simpler than that. He thought the child felt the change coming, the way all small creatures feel it, in the marrow, in the blood. The world was about to shift its weight. Even the youngest among them knew.

He climbed the narrow stair to the lamp room above the eastern storeroom, where he kept his tools and his oil and the small iron stove that made the place the only warm room in the building that did not smell of soup. Rosalind was already there. She was always already there; he had stopped being surprised by it sometime in January. She sat on his stool with the baby against her shoulder, swaying in that slow metronome rhythm the childless learn only from watching others, and she was looking out the little window at the river with an expression Isandro recognized because he had worn it himself, once, a lifetime ago, standing over a grave too small for words.

"She won't settle," Rosalind said, without turning. "She's been crying since midnight. Not loud. Just — talking. Like she's trying to tell us something."

"Maybe she is." Isandro set his lamp on its hook and fed a stick of kindling to the stove. "The ice is singing. Some folk can't hear it. Some folk can't stop hearing it."

Rosalind turned then, and in the stove-light he saw how thin she had grown since the notices went up. The berth announcement had been nailed to the company door eleven days ago, and in those eleven days she had aged in a way that had nothing to do with years. Her face had become arithmetic. He could almost see the sums moving behind her eyes: one berth, three candidates, one sick boy, one hunted child, one girl whose own name was not even hers but the company's, leased back to her at sixteen pence a week.

"Isandro," she said. "Tell me what you hear. In the ice. Truly."

He came to stand beside her at the window. Below, the river lay under its rotting lid, white gone grey gone the color of old candle wax, and along the far shore the meltwater ran black and quick in the channels it had carved. As they watched, a crack opened somewhere out in the middle distance — a sound like a door slamming in an empty house, followed by a long descending note, almost musical, almost mournful.

"There," he said. "That's the river saying goodbye to winter. Or warning us. Depends on your temperament."

"And how much time?"

"A week. Less, if the wind turns southerly." He looked down at the baby, who had gone quiet, her dark eyes fixed on him with the unnerving seriousness of infants, as though she understood every word and was reserving judgment. "Which means, little miss, that decisions are upon us."

Rosalind's arms tightened around the child. It was a small motion, involuntary, the kind of thing a body does when it hears a word it has been dreading. *Decisions.* Isandro watched her jaw set and thought: *she has already decided something, and she has not told us what.*

He was right. But he was also, as it happened, about to be wrong, and the wrongness would cost him a night's sleep and teach him something about the difference between rescue and salvation.

* * *

He made his offer that evening, after the lamps were lit.

The rounds took longer now than they had in January, when the route was merely habit — forty-one lanterns from the counting house to the far quay, each one trimmed and filled and touched to flame while the dark came down off the hills like a tide. Now the rounds were also cover, and both of them knew it. A lamplighter walking his route at dusk attracted no eyes. A lamplighter with a bundle wrapped against his chest, tucked inside his coat where the oil cans usually rode — that was another matter, and so Lantern stayed below with Marta, and the two of them walked the route alone, and talked the way conspirators talk, in low voices and sideways sentences.

"The boat comes in six days," Isandro said, stopping at the lantern on the end of the coal shed, the one that needed a new glass pane because a dockhand's elbow had cracked it in February. "The *Merrow's Daughter*. One berth. You've petitioned for Tam."

"I have."

"And Corvane has neither granted nor refused."

"He says the ice will decide." Rosalind's breath hung in the air between them. "I used to think that was cruelty dressed up as poetry. Now I think he genuinely doesn't know what he'll do. I've seen men lie, Isandro. That wasn't lying. That was a man standing at his own window, listening to the same song we're hearing."

Isandro trimmed the wick, struck the match, touched flame to flame. The lantern bloomed. He watched the light spread across the frozen mud and the stacked barrels and the black channel of open water growing wider each day at the river's edge, and he said the thing he had been carrying in his chest for three days, the way a man carries a stone he intends to put down but cannot find the right ground.

"Then let me take her."

Rosalind went very still.

"I have a sister in Millbrook, up past the falls. Widow Hester Crane. Good woman, sharp tongue, soft heart — the combination runs in our family. She lost her own babe thirty years ago and never had another. She'd take the child in and ask no questions, or she'd ask them once and then love the answers. I'd carry Lantern myself. Leave tomorrow, ahead of the thaw, cross at the ford at Stony Bottom before the ice goes rotten. Three days' walk. By the time Quill or anyone else thinks to look, we'd be a hundred miles upstream and the flood would close the road behind us like a wall." He heard his own voice gathering speed, the way it did when he argued with himself more than with his listener. "The child would be safe, Rosalind. Safe and fed and raised in a house with a garden. No ledgers, no watchmen, no director with his cold eyes. You could put Tam on that boat with a clear heart, knowing the other half of your family was already—"

"No."

It was not loud. It was not angry. It was the quietest *no* he had ever been given, and therefore the most complete. He stopped. The lantern between them hissed softly in the damp air.

"Isandro." Rosalind turned to face him fully, and the light caught her face, and he saw that she was not refusing him out of fear or stubbornness or even love, though love was in it, plain as salt. She was refusing him out of something colder and clearer. Understanding. "Where would she grow up?"

"In Millbrook. With Hester. I told you—"

"As what?" Rosalind's voice stayed level, but underneath it he heard the river itself, that long descending note. "A foundling. A nameless girl with no papers and no story, raised on whatever tale you and your sister invent — and you'd have to invent one, Isandro, because the truth can't travel with her. Someone powerful wanted this face forgotten. Do you think forgetting stops at the town line? Do you think a man like Ambrose Corvane spends money on a watchman named Quill and then simply gives up when the trail goes cold? Men like that don't give up. They wait. They wait ten years, fifteen, until the girl is grown and someone notices she has his son's chin, his mother's hands, and then—" She stopped. Drew breath. When she continued, her voice had dropped to nearly a whisper. "My father taught me the river, before he drowned in it. He said: the current doesn't fight the rock, it goes around, but it never forgets the rock is there. Every eddy downstream remembers it. You can't outrun a thing like that. You can only decide whether you'll meet it on your feet or spend your whole life looking over your shoulder."

"So you'd stay," Isandro said slowly. "Here. Where he can reach her."

"I'd stay," Rosalind said, "and I'd stop hiding her."

The words landed in the space between the lanterns like stones dropped into still water. Isandro felt the ripples move through him, outward, touching everything: the lamp room where they had named the child, the blanket Marta had burned, the ledger with its buried birth entry, the director's pale eyes lingering on the dock.

"You don't know what you're saying. A confrontation? With Corvane? Girl, he owns the ground you stand on. He owns your years. He could—"

"He could do a great many things," Rosalind agreed. "And none of them would be worse than what he's already done, which is make a child into a secret. Secrets are how people like him keep their power, Isandro. Not the doing — everyone does things, good and bad. The *hiding*. The note on the dock didn't say *kill this child*. It said *forget this face*. Forgetting is cheaper. Forgetting is what respectable men buy." She lifted her chin, and in the lantern light Isandro saw, briefly, not a sixteen-year-old clerk but someone older, someone forged, someone who had taken the weight of small hands and learned to carry it upright. "So we stop letting him buy it. We give the face a name. We give the name a witness. You. Me. Marta. Tam, God help us, from his sickbed. Four witnesses who will swear before God and the company and the whole assembled crew of the *Merrow's Daughter* that this child is a Corvane, granddaughter of the director himself, and that she was left on his own dock to freeze because her existence embarrassed him."

"And you think shame will move a man like that?"

"I think," Rosalind said, "that I've seen his face when he stands at that window. I think there's a grave in that man wearing a suit, and graves want company, and I think if we bring him his granddaughter's face and refuse to let him look away — some part of him, the part that wrote whatever he wrote and burned whatever he burned — will flinch. And a flinch is a door." She paused, and her voice softened for the first time. "And if it isn't — if I'm wrong, if he's stone all the way down — then at least she'll know, when she's grown, that someone stood in the light and said her name out loud. That's worth more than a garden in Millbrook. A hidden child survives. A named child *belongs*. I know the difference, Isandro. I've been the hidden one my whole life."

The river sang again, long and low, and somewhere out in the dark a sheet of ice the size of a chapel roof shifted and groaned and settled, and neither of them moved.

Isandro thought of his daughter. Fifty-one years, and he still thought of her at least once a day, usually at dusk, at the lighting of the first lamp — Mariel, who had gone into the water at seven years old on a June afternoon bright as a promise, and whom the river had kept. He had spent thirty years believing that grief was a debt you paid by hiding it, keeping it banked and dark like goods in a storeroom. It had taken a nameless infant and a stubborn girl to show him otherwise: that grief, spoken aloud, shared, witnessed, became something else entirely. Became guardianship. Became light.

"All right," he said.

Rosalind blinked. "All right?"

"I walked this route alone for thirty years, girl. Thirty years of lighting lamps for people who never once wondered who lit them. I know exactly what invisibility costs, because I chose it, and I chose it because the alternative — mattering, being seen, being *answerable* — frightened me more than the dark did." He reached out and laid his rough hand, lamplighter's hand, scarred and oil-stained, over hers where it rested on the baby's back. "I won't teach the child to run. I promised myself, the day I held her in the lamp room, that whatever else happened, she would never be a secret the way Mariel is a secret — a name only I say. So. If we stand, we stand together. All four of us. And we'd best be ready, because Quill is getting closer every day, and the ice has maybe six days left in it, and Corvane—" He hesitated.

"What?"

"Corvane keeps papers in the counting house that no clerk is meant to see. Locked drawers, locked cabinet, and a man who guards them like a dragon. But I've lit that room every night for thirty years, Rosalind. I know which locks are honest and which are theater." He held her gaze. "If you mean to confront that man, you should know everything there is to know about him first. Everything. Including the things he couldn't bear to send."

Rosalind stared at him, and he watched the meaning arrive in her face like dawn arriving over the water — not all at once, but in bands, color by color.

"The counting house," she whispered. "His letters."

"If there are letters," Isandro said. "Men like him always have letters. The question is whether you're brave enough to read them."

Above them, in the dark, the ice sang on — and far downriver, in a locked drawer in a locked room, something waited that would change the shape of everything they had just decided.
