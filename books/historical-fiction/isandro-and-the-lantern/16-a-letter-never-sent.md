## 16. A Letter Never Sent

The counting house was locked, but Rosalind had spent three years learning the architecture of locks.

Not breaking them — she had never broken anything in her life that could not be mended by patience. But every lock in Hallowmere was a habit wearing a brass face, and habits could be learned. The counting house key hung in Marta's kitchen on a nail behind the dried thyme, because Marta brought Director Corvane his morning coffee when he visited and did not trust the night watchman to lock up properly after him. And the drawer within a drawer — the one with the brass fittings gone dark from disuse — would yield to a hairpin, as all old drawers did, because furniture made before a man's grief was never built to keep secrets from women with pins in their hair.

She told herself these things while she stood in the corridor outside, her hand flat against the cold plaster, listening to the building breathe. The warehouse groaned at night the way old ships groan, timbers settling against the pressure of the frozen river, and beneath it all ran that other sound — thin, high, ceaseless — the ice singing its slow unraveling into spring. Three days, Isandro had said. Perhaps four. The river would decide, and the river did not negotiate.

Inside, the counting house smelled of tallow and cold iron and something else, fainter — cedar, perhaps, or the ghost of pipe smoke. The stove was banked for the night but not dead; a bruised orange light seeped through its grate and laid long shadows across the floor like the ribs of some enormous patient animal. She knew this room. She had stood in it a hundred times to hand over tallies, eyes down, hands folded, practicing invisibility the way other girls practiced dancing. She had never seen it empty. It looked different empty — smaller, humbler, the way a church looks different when no one is praying in it.

The desk sat beneath the window like a throne that had grown tired of being sat upon.

Rosalind crossed the floor and her footsteps sounded obscene to her own ears, each one a small confession. *Don't speak first. Don't want loudly.* The rules rose in her out of habit, and she set them aside the way she might set down a heavy basket — carefully, deliberately, knowing she would need her hands free.

* * *

The outer drawers held what she expected: manifests, correspondence with the upriver houses, invoices written in a hand so precise it looked printed. She went through them quickly, feeling less like a thief than an accountant, which was its own strange comfort. Numbers did not frighten her. Numbers had been her whole world since her father drowned — the debt ledger with his name at the top of it, the arithmetic of survival she performed nightly in her head: *Tam's medicine, so much; bread, so much; the difference between living and sinking, always so much.*

It was in the inner drawer — the locked one, the one that surrendered to her hairpin with a soft click that made her flinch as though the sound might carry through stone — that she found the letters.

Not letters. One letter. Unsent, unsealed, folded once and folded again, as if a hand had worried at it across many evenings. The paper was good stock, heavier than company stock, the kind bought for occasions rather than business. Her name was not on it. No name was. Only a single word in that same printed-precise hand, though here the precision trembled:

*Ambrose.*

She turned it over twice before she understood. Not the addressee's name. The signature. The director had signed himself by his first name, in a letter addressed to no living soul, and the handwriting of the salutation — *My dear boy* — had gone wobbly at the end of *boy*, the ink feathered where something wet had fallen and been left to dry without blotting.

Rosalind sat down in the director's chair without deciding to. Her legs simply stopped holding her.

She unfolded the letter.

*My dear boy —

I have written this eleven times. I know it is eleven because I burn each one in the stove and I have kept count the way you kept count of the ships in the harbor when you were small, calling them out to me from the window as though I could not see them myself. You loved numbers too. I never told you so, but I loved that about you more than I loved anything else in this world, and I did not tell you that either, and now the telling is all I have left and I am hoarding even that, folding it into drawers instead of sending it wherever unsent letters go.*

*You have been dead eight months and I still catch myself saving the second pour of coffee. Grief is a bookkeeping error, Edmund. The accounts will not balance. I enter debits and the ledger returns nothing, and I understand now that this is how it will be for the rest of my life — solvent on paper and ruined in fact.*

*I saw her today. You will know who I mean. I could not help it — the laundress's sister came to the gate with the bundle wrapped against the cold, and I looked, though Mr. Quill advised me against looking, and I looked. She has your eyes. There is no denying it and no mistaking it and I stood there at my own gate like a beggar wanting what is not mine to want, and then I gave the order you already know I gave, the order I will answer for in whatever court receives such accounts.*

*Forget this face.*

*Edmund, I want you to understand, though understanding will do me no good now — it was not cruelty. I need you to know it was not cruelty, even if it was nothing better. Your mother's family has lawyers like other families have dogs, and they would have taken that child and made of her a claim, a scandal, a lever to pry loose everything your grandfather built and everything I have broken myself trying to build it larger in your name. A baseborn granddaughter is not a person to men like that. She is an entry in someone else's ledger. So I chose the only kindness I could afford: I made her no one, so that no one could take her. I wrote forget this face because I could not write what I meant, which was forgive me, which was I cannot look at you, which was she has your eyes.*

*I sent money. Do not be proud of me; it was not generosity, it was interest on a debt I intend never to repay in any currency that matters. The laundress's people took it and went north, and I told myself that was an ending, and then the child came back to my own dock in the dead of winter as though the river itself were returning defective goods, and I do not believe in God, Edmund, but I believe in arithmetic, and this is arithmetic: everything I buried comes back up. The river keeps what is thrown in it. So do ledgers.*

*I am tired. The ice will break soon and there is work, and there is always work, and none of it is the work I should be doing, which is learning how to say out loud the things I can only say to a drawer. If you were alive I would tell you: I am afraid of her. Not of what she costs me. Of what she asks me. She has your eyes and she does not know it yet, but she is asking me, every hour she spends unclaimed in this frozen town, whether I am the kind of man who buries his son twice.*

*I do not know the answer. That is the truth of it. Eleven letters and I still do not know the answer.*

*Your loving father,
Ambrose*

Rosalind read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, her lips moving over the words the way Tam's lips moved over his letters when he was feverish, and by the third reading she was crying — not the way she cried at night into her sleeve, quick and silent and ashamed, but openly, ugly, in the director's chair with the letter shaking in her hands.

Because she had built him wrong.

She had built him out of dread and cold corridors and the way he said *the books, Miss Amberdale* as though the books were the only things in the world worth addressing. She had built him out of Quill's searching and the note at the door and every rule she had ever memorized about powerful men. And he was those things. But he was also this — a man writing eleven letters to a corpse and burning ten of them, hoarding grief in a locked drawer the way she hoarded coins in a boot, the way Isandro hoarded the memory of a daughter given to the river, the way everyone in Hallowmere who had lost someone carried them in the only container that felt safe, which was always secrecy, always darkness, always *forget this face* meaning *I cannot survive remembering it in daylight.*

He had not thrown Lantern away. He had hidden her — badly, the way frightened men hide things — the way you bury treasure on your own land because you cannot bear to put it beyond reach.

*She has your eyes.*

Rosalind thought of the baby sleeping in the lamp room above the storerooms, in the crate lined with wool that Isandro had padded with a sailor's carefulness. Thought of her fists, tiny and furious, gripping a finger with the whole of her unreasonable strength. Thought of the embroidery on the blanket Marta had burned — the little stitched boat, the initials — and understood at last that it had never been evidence of scandal. It had been a christening gift. Someone had embroidered a boat for this child the way you embroider for a wanted thing.

*Forgive me,* the letter said, without saying it. *I cannot look at you,* it said, which in the grammar of fathers has always meant *I cannot stop looking.*

* * *

She did not remember leaving the chair, but at some point she was standing at the window, watching the river. The moon had come up through the breaking cloud and lay along the ice in a long white seam, and the ice was moving under it — not breaking yet, not cracking open, but shifting, plates of it grinding and rising, the whole grey-white plain of the river breathing like something waking from a long sleep. In three days, or four, it would go all at once. It always did. Old men in Hallowmere claimed you could hear it coming for a week, and old men were right.

Her breath fogged the glass and she wiped it with her sleeve and made herself think, because feeling alone would drown her, and she had not survived six winters of ledgers by drowning.

*Empathy as strategy.* Was that what this was? She tested the thought and found it fit her hand like a tool. She had spent her life believing that power and feeling were opposites — that Corvane's coldness was the armor that kept him strong and her own softness was the weakness that kept her bound. But here was the proof otherwise, folded in a drawer: the most powerful man on the river was also the most afraid. His cruelty was fear wearing authority the way a lamp wore glass. And a man ruled by fear could not be beaten by courage alone, because fear simply grew harder. He could only be reached by the thing fear was protecting.

Grief. Love. The eyes of his dead son.

If she fled with Lantern — Isandro's plan, the boat upriver, the nameless child growing nameless roots — then Corvane's fear would never rest. He would search forever, gently perhaps, but forever, because the drawer could not balance while the entry was missing. The child would spend her whole life as an error waiting to be corrected.

But if she gave her back —

Not surrendered. Not abandoned. *Given.* Placed in his arms with the whole truth spoken aloud, in front of witnesses, in front of the river itself — *this is your son's daughter, and she has his eyes, and I am asking you to account for her* — then the arithmetic changed. Then he would have to choose what kind of man to be, with no drawer to fold the choice into. Fear might win. She knew that. She was not naive; she had read enough ledgers to know that some debts were never honored. But it was the only ending in which anyone's account could balance — his, hers, the child's.

And there was Tam.

The thought arrived quietly and settled in her chest like a coal. The berth. One berth, and the ice deciding. If she came to Corvane not as a petitioner begging for mercy but as a woman offering him back the single thing in the world he could not buy back — offering it freely, expecting nothing — then whatever he did next would be done in full knowledge of what he was refusing. Men could refuse beggars easily. Refusing a gift was harder. Refusing a gift while looking into eyes like his son's — that was the kind of act that followed a man into his eleven letters.

She looked down at the page one last time. *Whether I am the kind of man who buries his son twice.*

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead boy in the words, to the river moving under its moon. "You're going to find out."

She folded the letter along its old creases and — after a long moment — did not return it to the drawer. She slipped it inside her shirt, against her heart, where it lay warm and impossible, a stolen thing that weighed less than bread and more than the whole warehouse. Let him discover it missing. Let him come looking. Some conversations had to be begun by the other side of the drawer.

Then she pinched out her candle, let herself out, and locked the counting house behind her with Marta's key, and walked back through the frozen dark toward the lamplight and the small warm weight of the child who did not yet know she was about to be given a grandfather — while downriver, the ice sang louder, and somewhere above the town a light burned in a window of the great house, where a sleepless man sat with an empty drawer and the beginning of a twelfth letter he could not finish.
