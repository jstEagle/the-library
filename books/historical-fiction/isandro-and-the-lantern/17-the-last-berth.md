## 17. The Last Berth

The morning of the signing came in the color of pewter, with a wind off the river that carried the first true smell of water in five months — not ice, but the dark, living current beneath it. I woke before the bell as I always did, and for a long moment I lay in my cot above the kitchen and listened to Hallowmere breathing.

It is a strange thing, to hear a town hold its breath. But that is what it was doing. Every window on Ferry Lane seemed to lean toward the river. The ice had been singing for three nights now — a low, groaning music, like a great door being forced open somewhere under the world — and everyone knew what the singing meant. Days. Perhaps hours. The *Merrow's Daughter* sat low at the last berth, her holds full of furs and salt-fish and tinned oysters no one in Hallowmere could afford, and pinned to her cabin door was the manifest that would decide whether my brother lived or drowned in his own fever on this side of the water.

I dressed in the dark. My fingers knew the buttons without me; they had done this work since I was twelve years old, dressing by feel so as not to wake Tam, though Tam had not slept a whole night through since the fever took its firm grip of him. He slept now in shallow pulls, like a man hauling on a rope. When I bent over him his breath came quick and hot against my knuckles, and his eyes opened halfway.

"Is it today?"

"It's today."

"Ros." His hand found my sleeve. His voice had gone thin as paper. "You wrote your own name. Marta told me. You have to write your own name."

"I know what I have to do," I said, which was not an answer, and we both let it stand because neither of us had ever had the luxury of arguing about things that could not be changed.

His hand stayed on my sleeve a moment longer. Then he turned his face to the wall, and I went down into the grey morning to sign away whatever I had decided, in the night watches, to sign away.

* * *

The company office smelled of lamp oil and sealing wax, as it always did, but that morning there was something else under it — the river itself, seeping up through the floorboards, or perhaps only through the men standing in it. A queue had formed outside the door before the clerk had even unlocked it: dockhands with their caps wrung in their fists, a widow from Tannery Row, two young men from the sawmill who had drawn lots between them and were here on behalf of both. One berth. One name on one line. The whole town pressed at that single line like water pressing at ice.

Marta walked me there. She had put on her good shawl, the one with the faded roses, and she held my arm as we crossed the yard, though the ground was sound enough and my legs were steady. It was not for the walking. It was for the same reason she had burned the blanket in the kitchen grate three nights ago, weeping over the flames while Lantern — Isandra — howled in my arms: because some things must be witnessed, or they do not fully happen.

"You'll write it," she said. Not a question.

"I'll write it."

"And you won't look back at the line after."

"Marta—"

"Because you will want to. Your hand will shake, and you'll think, one letter more, one stroke of the pen, and it's my name instead. And if you look back at the line after it's written, girl, you'll scratch it out, and then you'll be the sort of person who scratches out her brother's life to save her own comfort, and you aren't that, and you mustn't give yourself the chance to learn otherwise." She squeezed my arm hard enough to bruise. "So don't look back."

"I thought," I said, "you wanted me gone. Off the boat, out of Corvane's pocket, free."

"I wanted you safe. There's a difference, and I've spent sixty years learning it too late." She stopped at the office steps and looked at me with those flat black eyes that had seen everything in this town and forgiven most of it. "Tam needs the air upriver. That's medicine, and medicine doesn't argue. You need leaving too, child, but yours isn't the kind a boat can carry. Write his name."

Inside, the office had been arranged like a courtroom. The manifest lay open on the long oak table, its blank passenger line ruled and waiting, and behind the table — not at it, behind it, standing, which he never did — stood Director Ambrose Corvane.

He had been in Hallowmere all week now, and the town had learned to move around him the way water moves around a stone. He was not a large man. He was precise; everything about him was precise, from the silver at his temples to the way his gloves were buttoned, each finger identical, nothing left to chance. But I had seen something else in him the day I petitioned for Tam's berth — a flicker, quickly shuttered, of a grief so old it had fossilized into manner. *The ice will decide,* he had told me. He had looked at me then the way a man looks at a window into a room he once lived in.

Mr. Quill stood near the far wall with his ledger of missing property and his patient, terrible eyes. When I entered he marked something in it. I made myself not care. Whatever he suspected, whatever he hunted through our storerooms and kitchens, today was larger than him. Today the ice was singing loud enough to drown any watchman's pen.

"Petitioners," the head clerk called, "by order received."

They went one at a time. The sawmill man gave his friend's name and his friend's reasons and stood with his hat over his heart while the clerk recorded them. The widow from Tannery Row asked for passage to her sister in Kelverton and wept quietly throughout, and Director Corvane watched her with a face like a closed ledger himself, and said nothing, and dismissed her gently enough. The dockhands went. The queue shortened. The room filled with other people's hopes, spoken aloud in the flat voices people use when hope embarrasses them, and every hope but one would be refused, because there was one berth and the river does not bargain.

Then my name was called, and the room did the thing rooms always did when I entered — it forgot me. I was the Amberdale girl, the drowned man's daughter, small and quiet and useful, the one who tallied cargo and lit lamps and asked nothing loudly. I felt myself go invisible the way I had trained myself to go invisible for four years, and for the first time in my life I understood the trick of it from the inside: invisibility is not absence. It is presence, withheld.

I walked to the table. The pen was there, and the ink, and the single ruled line, and beyond the window behind the director's shoulder the river lay white and groaning under the last of its winter.

"State your petition," the clerk said.

"My petition," I said, and my voice surprised me by holding, "was entered under my own name. Rosalind Amberdale. Passage upriver on the *Merrow's Daughter*, berth for one." I dipped the pen. "I withdraw it."

Somewhere behind me the widow made a small sound. Quill's pen stopped moving.

The director's eyes came to me. They were grey, winter-water grey, and they did not widen or narrow, but something in them attended — the way a lamplight attends when a draft passes through a room.

"On what grounds?" the clerk asked. "Withdrawals must be—"

"The grounds are mine." I turned to Corvane directly, because this part was not for the clerk. "I'm striking my name and writing another. My brother's. Tamson Amberdale. He's eleven years old and the doctor says the river air above the falls is the only medicine left to him, and the doctor is right, and I've known it since December and pretended otherwise because pretending cost less." The pen was in my hand. My hand did not shake. Marta had been wrong about that, or I had been stronger than either of us guessed, and I think the truth of it was that I had done my shaking already, alone, in the dark, over four years of nights. "He dives well past his depth for me every winter, my brother. He holds his breath and doesn't complain. It's time someone surfaced for him instead."

I drew the line through my own name. It was strange, how easy it was. Four winters of wanting — wanting a berth, wanting freedom, wanting to be the one who got out — and the strike of the pen took no strength at all, the way the heaviest doors swing lightly once you have finally decided to walk through them.

Then I wrote, beneath it, in the careful clerk's hand I had learned tallying cargo in the cold storerooms:

*Tamson Amberdale.*

And I did not look back at the line after, just as she told me.

* * *

The clerk reached to blot the page, and that should have been the end of it — one berth, one name, the arithmetic of Hallowmere satisfied, and me standing empty-handed on the dock of my own life with the flood coming.

But the director moved.

He came around the table without hurry, and the room stilled the way a room stills when something irreversible begins. He looked at the manifest for a long moment. He looked at the struck-through name — *Rosalind Amberdale* — and I watched him read it twice, and I remembered, with a lurch, that he knew my handwriting better than I knew he did. He had inspected the accounts in January. He had lingered over margins.

"There is a second line," he said.

"Sir?" The clerk's voice cracked. "There is one berth. The notice was—"

"I issued the notice." Corvane drew off one glove, finger by finger, each seam straightened, and I understood suddenly that the ceremony of it was the point — that a man who had spent a lifetime writing things in private needed witnesses for this one. "One berth, granted by petition. The second is granted by right." He took up the pen I had set down. "A ship's master may carry unrecorded passengers at the owner's discretion. The *Merrow's Daughter* is Corvane tonnage."

"What name shall I enter, sir?" the clerk asked.

"No name." And there — there it was again, the flicker behind the shutter, the grief that had ordered a child forgotten and had never once, I think, succeeded in forgetting her. He bent over the manifest. "No name. A description will serve. 'One infant, female, in the care of the ship's matron, discharged at Kelverton to family.'"

*To family.* He wrote it in a hand much finer than his reputation suggested, and I stood very still and watched the most powerful man in Hallowmere give a foundling back her future in eleven words, and I thought of a note I had found pinned to a bundle on a whiteout night — *forget this face* — and I understood at last that he had written that note to himself, and failed, and had been failing every day since, and that mercy, in men like him, wears the mask of procedure because feeling is the one cargo he never learned to declare.

He blotted the line. He set down the pen. Then he looked at me, and his voice, when he used it, was quiet enough that only our end of the room could hear.

"You found what I lost, Miss Amberdale."

"Yes, sir."

"You might have sold it to me. Others would have." His gaze went, briefly, to Quill by the wall, and Quill went pale beneath his tan. "You might have used it. Instead you spent your only coin on your brother and brought me my granddaughter like a debt collector presenting a bill." Something moved at the corner of his mouth that was not quite a smile and was worth ten of his smiles. "I find I prefer the bill."

"She'll need to travel warm, sir," I said, because my voice was going to betray me otherwise, and procedure was the only language we safely shared. "She takes goat's milk, warmed, not cow's. She sleeps best with a light burning — not bright. Just a light."

"A light," he repeated, softly, as if filing it among his accounts. "Yes. I had heard she was named for one."

"Named by one," I said. "Isandro Vael, the lamplighter. He'll want to see her off."

"He may come to the dock with the rest of the town." Corvane drew his glove back on, and the man of precision reassembled himself around the wound like ice re-forming over water. "The boat leaves when the pilot says the channel will bear her. See the child to the gangway yourself, Miss Amberdale. Consider it..." he paused, choosing, "...part of your severance."

The word fell strangely. I did not yet know — would not know until the flood had come and gone — what work he was doing with that word, or what it would eventually purchase. For now I only bowed my head the way clerks bow, and gathered the manifest copy the clerk pressed into my hands, and walked out of the office past Mr. Quill, whose pen had begun, very slowly, to write something new.

* * *

Marta was waiting at the foot of the steps, and Isandro beside her, his lamp already lit against the grey afternoon though the sun was barely sinking. I held up the manifest copy without a word. Isandro read it the way he read wicks — closely, tilting toward the light — and then he read it again, and his old hands began to tremble around the paper.

"Two lines," he said. "Child, there's two lines."

"There's two lines."

He turned away from us sharply, toward the river, and stood with the lamp raised like a man hailing a ship across water, and I saw the tears run into the seams of his face and did not pretend not to, because some things must be witnessed. Somewhere under the singing ice, the ghost of his drowned daughter was, I think, keeping pace with us up the bank.

At the top of the kitchen stairs Tam was awake and dressed, propped against the wall in his coat with his cap pulled down, gray-faced and grinning like a man already breathing waterfall air. Marta had packed his things in a fish crate lined with sacking; she had been packing it secretly for a week, I realized, believing in the outcome before I dared to. She pressed a parcel of bread and salt pork and a jar of goose fat into his arms, and kissed him hard on both cheeks, and told him that if he did not eat every scrap by Kelverton she would know, somehow, the way cooks know, and there would be reckoning.

"And the little one?" Tam asked, breathless. "Ros — the baby —"

"Goes too." I lifted Isandra from her basket by the stove, and she blinked at the lamplight, solemn as an owl. "Unrecorded but undeniable. To family, the manifest says."

Tam looked at me for a long moment, and then he laughed — a real laugh, the first in weeks, which turned into coughing, which he waved off — and said, "You struck your own name."

"Somebody had to surface," I said.

We went down to the dock as the light failed, the whole town flowing with us, and the ice sang louder than I had ever heard it, and out at the berth the *Merrow's Daughter* raised her lamps mast by mast. Isandro walked ahead of us lighting the quay brackets as he came, so that the child's road to the gangway burned the whole way bright.

And it was there, at the foot of the gangway, that we saw Mr. Quill already waiting — hat squared, ledger under his arm, planted square in the lamplight like a nail driven into the plank — and heard him say, in his patient voice, to the ship's master:

"That child doesn't board this vessel. I have orders regarding company property, and I mean to see them carried out."

Behind us, unhurried on the quay, came the sound of one man beginning to descend the steps.
