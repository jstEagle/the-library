## 18. Confrontation at the Dock

Quill had been paid to find things, and he had always found them.

That was the whole of his reputation, and the whole of his method: patience dressed as dullness, a face so forgettable that clerks confessed to it as they would confess to a wall. For nineteen years he had served men whose names were never spoken in his presence, and in nineteen years he had learned one truth above all others — that people did not hide secrets by building walls around them but by refusing to look at them, and so the searcher's task was simply to stand where they could not help but look.

He stood at the edge of the Corvane dock on the morning of the breaking ice, and he looked.

The river had begun to sing three nights ago — a sound he had never heard before and would not forget if he lived a hundred years, a groaning and cracking and keening that came up through the pilings and into the bones of his feet, as though the frozen water were an enormous animal waking in pain. Now, under a sky the color of old pewter, the ice heaved and shuddered downstream in grinding plates, and the *Merrow's Daughter* sat low at her berth with steam curling from her stack, taking on the season's last cargo before the flood closed Hallowmere like a fist.

Quill watched the people on the dock the way he had watched them for weeks: the cook, Marta, broad and stone-faced, directing the loading of provisions she had measured to the ounce; the lamplighter, Isandro, ancient and bent, standing near the gangplank with a lit lantern in his hand though the sun was fully up; the girl — Amberdale, the clerk, sixteen years old and careful as arithmetic — who had not let the child out of her sight since the night Quill nearly heard it cry in the kitchen.

He had almost had them, then. Almost. The baby had been inches from discovery, wrapped in some rag or other behind the flour barrels, and something in Marta's face — a stillness too perfect, a smile laid over panic like paint over rot — had told him everything her silence tried to deny. He had reported as much. He had recommended a quiet removal, a discreet question put to the right parties, the recovery of company property.

*Company property.* The phrase still sat wrong in his mouth, and Quill was a man who noticed when phrases sat wrong. In nineteen years he had recovered stolen ledgers, forged bills of lading, a director's mistress smuggled out in a wine crate. Never once had the property been a child. But the instruction had come down through intermediaries, as instructions did, unsigned and unexplained: *an infant left at the premises in the winter; find it; forget nothing else.*

He found things. It was what he was paid for. What happened after he found them had never been his concern, and he had kept his hands clean for nineteen years by keeping his curiosity starved.

And yet.

He watched the girl now, Amberdale, standing at the foot of the gangplank with her brother's coat sleeve gripped in one hand. The boy was thin as a winter branch, gray-lipped, swaying slightly even in stillness, and she held him upright by sheer will, the way a stake holds a young tree. There was no child visible anywhere near her. There was no child visible anywhere at all.

Which was, in itself, a kind of answer. Quill had searched every corner of that warehouse, every crate and crawlspace, and found nothing — and a thing that could not be found had to be somewhere it should not be. On the morning of departure, with the whole town gathered to watch the last boat fight the ice, where would you hide an infant you could not bear to part with?

You wouldn't hide it at all. You would hide it in plain sight, among the dozens of bundles and baskets and sea-chests being carried up the gangplank by stevedores and passengers and the hired help of wealthy folk. You would walk it past a hundred indifferent eyes.

Quill positioned himself beside the manifest table, where the second mate checked each passenger against the ledger, and waited. Patience dressed as dullness. He was very good at waiting. It was the only prayer he knew.

* * *

The crowd thickened as the hour drew on. Half of Hallowmere had turned out, because the breaking of the ice was the closest thing the town had to theater — the river's annual verdict, read out in groans and shattering. Vendors worked the edges of the crowd with roasted chestnuts and cups of something hot and dishonest. Children wove between legs. The air smelled of coal smoke and thawing mud and the green, mineral breath of the river coming unlocked, and beneath it all ran the constant percussion of the ice, plates colliding, buckling, shrieking apart.

The director arrived at half past nine.

Quill felt him before he saw him — a change in the crowd's texture, a ripple of straightened backs and lowered voices. Ambrose Corvane came down the warehouse stairs in a dark coat that made the morning look cheap, and the crowd parted for him the way water parts for a keel. He was not a large man. He did not need to be. He walked to the manifest table as though the dock had been built around the fact of his arrival, and the second mate snapped to attention so hard his cap slipped.

Quill had never spoken to Director Corvane directly. His reports went to intermediaries; his payment came through intermediaries; his existence, he understood, was meant to be invisible even to the man who employed him. But Corvane's eyes passed across the crowd that morning with an expression Quill had never seen on a director's face and could not immediately name — until he realized it was the expression of a man attending his own funeral, and finding the eulogy unsatisfactory.

There was grief in that face. Quill catalogued it and did not understand it, and the failure to understand troubled him more than any locked drawer ever had.

The boarding began.

Names were called; the ledger ticked down. A timber factor from upriver, traveling with two trunks and a self-satisfied wife. A surveyor's clerk. A widow with a canary in a cage, which sang madly against the river's roar. And then—

"Tamson Amberdale," the second mate read. "One berth. Employee petition."

The boy moved. The girl did not release him at once; there was a moment — long enough for Quill to see it, and Quill saw everything — where brother and sister stood fused at the sleeves, and her mouth moved with something that was either a blessing or a beggary, and the boy said something back that made her jaw tighten like a woman biting down on a scream. Then she let go, and the boy went up the gangplank one slow step at a time, turning at the top to raise a hand, and the girl raised hers, and Quill thought, with the flat professional detachment that was his only form of feeling: *one berth granted, one petition surrendered. She gave it to the boy. So the child is not going.*

Which meant the child was staying. In Hallowmere. Through the flood, through the search, through whatever came next — staying in the town where someone powerful wanted it forgotten.

Quill scanned the dock again, faster now. The cook stood near the warehouse door, arms folded, watching the gangplank with an intensity that had nothing to do with provisions. The lamplighter stood beside her, lantern still burning in daylight, absurd and deliberate. And between them, wrapped in a blanket against the cold, held high against the old man's shoulder—

A small woolen cap. Two fists. A face turned outward, toward the crowd, toward the river, toward everything.

Not hidden. Carried. In plain sight, at the front of the assembled company, as though the child belonged there — as though she had always belonged there and only the winter had lied about it.

Quill began to move.

* * *

He had planned this moment a dozen times in a dozen variations, and in none of them had he imagined hesitation. He was a finder, not a taker; the taking belonged to other men. But the intermediaries' instruction had been clear enough in its final iteration: *if the property is located, secure it quietly and hold it for collection.* Quietly. Hold it. He told himself the words again as he crossed the dock, angling through the crowd toward the lamplighter, and told himself that what he felt was not reluctance but merely the ordinary caution of a professional working in view of a hundred witnesses.

"Old man." His voice was pitched low, courteous, terrible. "I'll take the bundle."

Isandro did not startle. That was the first wrongness. Nineteen years of surprising people had taught Quill what surprise looked like, and the lamplighter offered none of it. He turned with the unhurried gravity of a man in no hurry to be anywhere but here, and the lantern in his hand swung gently, throwing its small warm circle over the sleeping child at his shoulder.

"Bundles is cargo," Isandro said. "This is a christening."

"I don't want trouble."

"No," Isandro agreed mildly. "You want the child."

The word traveled further than Quill intended it to. He saw it land — saw heads turn along the dock, saw the cook Marta step forward with the sudden mass of a fortress, saw the girl Amberdale go very still, the way prey goes still, and then — the second wrongness — the way she stopped being still. She set down her brother's abandoned trunk. She squared her thin shoulders. And she walked into the space between Quill and the child as though walking into weather.

"Her name," Rosalind Amberdale said, in a voice that carried, "is Isandra."

The river groaned beneath them. The crowd's murmur died into something taut and listening.

"She was born on the fourth of November, four years ago," the girl went on, louder now, addressing not Quill but the whole dock, the whole town, the hundred faces and the gray sky above them. "In the laundress's quarters of the Corvane house on Miller's Row. Her father was Edmund Corvane. Her mother was Sarah Finch, who stitched her name into the corner of every blanket she made for her, because she was afraid no one else would ever say it. *Isandra.* I found the entry myself, in the parish record and in the shipping ledger both, and I am done forgetting faces because powerful men tell me to."

Silence. Enormous. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere in the crowd a chestnut vendor's pan clattered to the cobbles and no one laughed.

Quill felt the ground shift under him in a way that had nothing to do with the ice. Nineteen years. Nineteen years of secrets kept and delivered, and never once had anyone said the thing aloud in public, because the entire architecture of his profession rested on the universal agreement that nobody says the thing aloud. This slip of a girl in a mended coat had just torn the architecture down with her voice, and the strange thing — the thing he would carry with him to his grave — was that the crowd did not gasp. They nodded. As though they had known. As though the secret had been sitting in their collective chest all winter, heavy, and she had only opened the window.

"You are mistaken," Quill said, because it was the only sentence available to him, and it came out thin.

"She isn't."

The voice came from behind Quill, and it was a voice he had heard only through intermediaries reporting on intermediaries, a voice that signed documents and ended careers without ever raising itself. He turned.

Ambrose Corvane stood six feet away with the manifest ledger closed under one arm, and his face — the funeral face, the grieving face — was now something else entirely. Not soft. Corvane would never be soft. But unmasked; and an unmasked powerful man is the most frightening and most honest object in the world.

"The entry exists," Corvane said. "I have read it. Twice." His eyes went past Quill, past the girl, to the child on the lamplighter's shoulder, and what passed across his features then was not anything Quill had language for — an old wound opening and, impossibly, choosing to breathe rather than bleed. "She has her mother's chin."

"Sir—" Quill began.

"*Enough.*"

One word. It was not shouted. It did not need to be. It fell into the morning the way the ice fell into the river, and Quill felt it go through him and out the other side, and understood with perfect clarity that his contract had just ended, his employer had just disowned him, and his nineteen years of clean hands had just become nineteen years of nothing at all. Around them the crew of the *Merrow's Daughter* had stopped loading entirely; the second mate stood with his pen dripping on the manifest; and the director of the Corvane Trading Company looked at Mr. Quill the way a man looks at a smudge on clean glass.

"The child travels with the housekeeper," Corvane said to the second mate, calmly, as though resuming a conversation interrupted minutes ago rather than years. "Add her to the manifest. No name — a line and my seal will serve. And Mr. Quill—"

Quill braced.

"—will find his fee waiting at his lodgings, doubled, with a note advising him that the next boat out of Hallowmere departs within the hour, and that the road thereafter is long and would reward his attendance upon it."

It was dismissal, severance, and mercy braided into a single rope, and Quill took all three because a man who finds things knows when he has found the end of something. He looked once more at the child — Isandra, asleep, unbothered, her small fist curled against the lamplighter's coat as though she had been listening all along and had decided the proceedings were beneath her — and he felt the strangest sensation, unfamiliar and unwelcome, which was the sensation of being glad a search had failed.

Then he collected his hat, and he left the dock, and behind him the crowd's murmur rose like water over a sill.

* * *

At the top of Warehouse Row he stopped, because the ice chose that moment to break in earnest.

The sound was not like anything. It was a cannonade and a cathedral bell and a giant's spine cracking all at once, and the great sheet below the dock heaved, split, and began to move — whole fields of white tilting and tumbling downstream, grinding at the pilings, the river underneath running brown and furious and free. The crowd cheered. The *Merrow's Daughter* blew her whistle, a long raw note of departure, and her mooring lines splashed into the water one after another.

From the elevation of the row, Quill could see the whole deck as she swung out. The thin boy at the rail, waving with both arms at his sister on the dock. The housekeeper holding a bundle wrapped in — he squinted — a plain gray blanket, new, unembroidered; whatever the old one had been, it was gone now, burned or buried with the rest of the winter's evidence. The director standing apart at the stern, hat in hand, watching the child not the river.

And on the dock, small and straight-backed amid the cheering crowd, the girl who had refused invisibility — the lamplighter's hand on her shoulder, the cook's arm around her waist, all three of them leaning together like lamps lit against the same wind.

Quill stood in the smoke and the spray and the enormous noise, a man whose profession was forgetting faces, and found that he could not forget hers.

Then the boat rounded the bend, the ice closed behind it, and Hallowmere was sealed for the season — with everyone in it, and everything in it, finally out in the open at last.

He pulled his collar up against the wind off the broken river and walked, and did not look back.
