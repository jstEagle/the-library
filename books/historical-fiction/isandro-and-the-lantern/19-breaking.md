## 19. Breaking

**Rosalind**

The river began to sing at dawn.

Rosalind heard it before she understood it — a low, groaning note that rose through the floorboards of the warehouse and up through the soles of her boots, as though the building itself had begun to hum. She was standing at the tall window of the counting room with the manifest ledger open in her hands, though she had not read a word in an hour. Outside, the Hallowmere lay under a sky the color of pewter, and along its whole grey length the ice was shivering.

She had grown up on this river. She knew its moods the way other girls knew their mothers' faces. She knew the flat silence of deep winter, when the water slept so soundly beneath its lid that you could walk out onto it and forget it was ever anything but road. She knew the thin, treacherous creak of first thaw, the sound that sent children skittering back from the banks. But this — this long, tectonic moan, this singing — she had heard only once before, the year her father drowned.

*The ice breaks like a door opening,* her mother used to say. *And everything the river has been holding all winter, it lets go.*

Below her, the dock was already alive with men. The *Corvane Star* sat low in the black water between the ice shelves, her holds full, her rail crowded with crates of furs and salted fish and barrels marked with the company's three-tower seal. Steam curled from her pipe and unraveled in the cold. And on her aft deck, roped off from the cargo by a painted line, stood the single berth's worth of passengers: one trunk, one chair carried up from the office for an invalid, and Mrs. Hobb, the director's housekeeper, in her good grey coat, holding a bundle wrapped in a plain wool shawl.

Lantern. Isandra, now — named in the lamp room by candlelight, named properly, named aloud. Rosalind said the true name to herself like a prayer she was still learning the words of. *Isandra Corvane.* Granddaughter of Ambrose Corvane, whether the world was ready for it or no.

And beside the chair, wrapped in two blankets and his father's old scarf despite the doctor's warnings against overheating, sat Tam.

He was so thin. She had known he was thin; she bathed his forehead every night, she measured the fever with her lips against his skin, she counted his ribs by touch in the dark of Marta's loft where he'd slept since February. But seeing him upright in the morning light, propped against the chair's arm with his eyes bright with fever or with hope — she could see now how the winter had whittled him. He looked like a boy drawn in charcoal and only half-shaded.

He found her at the window. Even across the distance, even through the warped glass, he lifted his hand.

Rosalind did not wave back. She could not. Her hand stayed pressed flat against the ledger, over the page where, in her own careful clerk's hand, the passenger manifest read:

*One berth. Tam Amberdale, age eleven. Passage granted by order of A. Corvane, Director.*

And beneath it, in ink that was unmistakably not hers — smaller, harder, written with a man who had learned to write in anger and had never entirely unlearned it — a second line, unnumbered, unrecorded in any duplicate book:

*Accompanying infant, in care of Mrs. E. Hobb. No name entered.*

No name entered. As if a child could travel nameless. As if forgetting were a thing you could write into existence. She had spent four months learning how well that worked.

The singing grew louder. Down on the dock, men stopped their hauling and turned toward the river, and Rosalind saw Isandro among them, small at this distance, leaning on his lamp-lighting pole, his face tilted up toward the ice as if listening to a sermon. She left the ledger open on the sill — let the director's clerks find it, let everyone find it; there were no more secrets worth keeping in this town — and went down.

* * *

**Tam**

Tam had thought dying would feel like this: light, and floating, and everything around him happening slightly too fast, voices arriving from far away and meaning more than they said. But the doctor had leaned close two days ago, breath smelling of camphor, and said, *You're not dying, boy. You're leaving. There's a difference, and the river is going to show it to you.* And Tam had decided to believe him, because believing the alternative required a strength he did not have left over after simply breathing.

The air upriver was the medicine. That was what everyone kept saying, as though air could be poured like syrup. River air above the falls, dry and pine-scented, where Aunt Verity kept bees and had once, in a letter six years old, offered to take them both if ever the debt allowed. The debt had never allowed. The debt had been a stone wall around their whole lives, mortared with interest. But the ice was breaking, and the wall had a door in it now, and the door was a boat, and the boat was leaving today, and Tam was on it.

He wished he felt happy. He felt mostly frightened and very cold and sorry — sorry down in the marrow, a bone-deep sorrow that had nothing to do with the fever. Because he had done the arithmetic too, in the long white nights. One berth. Three people who needed it. His sister had chosen him, which meant she had un-chosen herself, and she had also un-chosen the baby, whom she loved — Tam had watched her love her, had seen the transformation of Rosalind's whole sharp careful face when Lantern gripped her finger in her sleep — and Tam knew what it cost to be un-chosen. He had been un-chosen by life itself, in a sense, marked out by his lungs before he could walk.

So when Rosalind came down onto the dock, moving quick and stiff like a person carrying something breakable inside her chest, Tam made himself stand up.

It took everything. The dock tilted. The steam-smell of the boat mixed with the green rot-smell of the breaking ice, and his heart slammed the way it did when he climbed the loft ladder, but he stood, and he held out his arms, and when she reached him he got his own arms around her waist and squeezed as hard as his body would squeeze, which was not hard at all.

"You'll write," Rosalind said into the top of his head. It wasn't a question. It wasn't even a request. It was spoken the way she read out cargo totals: as established fact.

"Every week."

"They don't have weekly posts past Millbrook. Fortnightly."

"Fortnightly, then." He pulled back to look at her face because he wanted to memorize it — not the face she wore for the company, the smooth blank clerk-face, but this one, the one with her eyes shining and ruined. "Rosie. Listen. You did the right thing."

"Did I?"

"I'd have died here." He said it plainly, because it was the plain truth and because he owed her plainness in exchange for everything else. "You know I'd have died here. So whatever it cost you, it bought my life, and I intend to spend it well. That's the trade. That's fair trade, and you taught me about fair trade."

Something moved through her face then — grief and laughter fighting for the same doorway — and Tam held on tighter, because the boat bell rang, first call, and the ice sang again, louder, a sound like a giant drawing a fingernail along the underside of the sky.

**Isandro**

Isandro had lit lamps on these docks for thirty-one years, and he had heard the ice break eleven times, and each time it sounded to him like the river clearing its throat to speak. Today it was speaking to him directly. He was sure of it. At seventy-one a man earned the right to be sure of such things.

*You lost a daughter to the river,* he thought, watching the woman — girl, she was still mostly girl — hold her brother at the gangway. *Now watch it give one back.*

Because that was the shape of the morning, if you had eyes for shapes. The river that had taken his little Wren forty years ago, that had swallowed her red mitten and her laugh and every future she might have had, that same river was now shouldering off its winter coffin to carry a sick boy toward healing and a stolen baby toward her grandfather's house and her proper name. Rivers take. Rivers give back. Neither on any schedule a man can argue with.

Mrs. Hobb stood near the rail with the bundle, and Isandro drifted over, slow, unhurried, the way he did everything, pole tapping the planks. Up close he could see the baby's face above the shawl's edge: solemn, wide-awake, dark-eyed, taking in the enormous commotion of the world with the expression of a person being shown a house they were expected to live in.

"Well, little Isandra," he said quietly. "Named for a lamplighter. Mind you grow up worthy of it — it's a good trade. You walk in the dark, and you leave light behind you, and folk are gladder for having seen you pass. There's worse ambitions."

The baby blinked at him. Then she did the thing that undid him every time, the thing that had undone him in the lamp room the night they christened her: she smiled, gummy and sudden, as if some private joke had concluded in her favor.

Isandro had to turn away and study the ice for a while.

Rosalind came up the gangway then, and the mate checked his watch, and the captain shouted something about the current, and Isandro put his hand on her shoulder and felt how she trembled — not with cold.

"He'll live," he said. "I've watched fever take folk and I've watched it pass, and this one passes. A body that fights that hard for a whole winter isn't planning to quit in spring."

"You can't know that."

"No," he agreed. "But I've lit lamps through eleven breakups, and I know this: the darkest part of the night is always just before the route turns homeward. Every round. Without fail. Then you're walking back toward the dawn and your arms ache and the oil's nearly gone and it's the sweetest walk there is." He squeezed her shoulder. "You're on the homeward leg, girl. Both of you. It only feels like the dark part because it is the dark part."

The bell rang again. Second call. Below them, the great ice field groaned, cracked, and a seam shot across the channel with a report like cannon fire, and the men on the dock cheered, because spring, because survival, because noise is the only prayer a crowd knows.

**Marta**

Marta had wept twice in the night — once over the blanket, once over the fact that burning it had been necessary — and she had resolved, coming downstairs before sunrise with her eyes swollen, that she would not weep a third time on the dock where everyone could see. Cooks do not weep publicly. Cooks stand at the rail with flour on their aprons and their chins set, and they hand up parcels, and that is their love language: bread, given at departures.

She had baked since four o'clock. The parcel she now pushed into Rosalind's arms contained a loaf still warm through its cloth, a jar of honey, dried apples, and — folded at the bottom where only Rosalind would find it — a scrap of the burnt blanket's twin, the second swaddle she'd bought secretly in March, embroidered with the same tiny birds by her own thick fingers, sitting up late by the kitchen fire. Let the child have one bird-blanket in the world. Evidence no longer; evidence had been burned. This was inheritance.

"Hold still," Marta muttered, thrusting a second, smaller bundle at Tam — seed cake, enough for a fortnight if he rationed, which he would not. "Eat it all. Come back fat. That's an instruction."

"Yes, Marta." Tam's voice was a reed, but he smiled, and Marta had to look at the rigging for a moment.

Then Rosalind was climbing down to the dock, and the lines were being thrown off, coiled, stacked, and the deckhands were pulling in the fenders, and it was happening, it was actually happening, all the weeks of dread and arithmetic resolving into this ordinary mechanical business of departure. Marta put her arm through Rosalind's and felt the girl lean on her, just slightly, just enough — the first time she ever had.

"I couldn't do it," Rosalind said. Very low. Only for Marta, and maybe not even fully for her. "Standing on that boat. I started to think about it last night and I couldn't breathe. Sending him is one thing. Leaving myself—" She stopped.

"I know what it is," Marta said. "I told you to go. I was wrong to tell you. Not because staying is safe or wise, but because I told you what I'd do, and you're not me. You never were." She watched Mrs. Hobb adjust the shawl against the wind. "That one's got a family now, whatever comes. And so have you, if you want it. My kitchen's short a scullery girl and long on opinions. The pay's terrible but the bread is free."

Rosalind made a sound that was half sob and half laugh, and the captain's voice rolled out over the water — *all ashore who are going ashore* — and the ice answered with a long rising shriek as the first great pan tilted up and showed, for one flashing instant, the black living water underneath.

**Quill**

Mr. Quill stood at the dock's landward end with his hands folded and his hat brim level, and he watched the proceedings with the professional attention of a man paid to notice things.

His commission was nearly discharged. *Company property gone missing* — that had been the phrase, four months ago, delivered in the director's study with the blinds drawn. Quill had searched. He had searched the storerooms and the lamp room and the kitchen and twice the lofts, and everywhere he had found exactly what a man finds in such places: honest grime, dishonest ledgers, and women who looked at him with a steadiness he distrusted. He had come close in the kitchen, in April. Close enough to hear an infant's breath catch. Close enough to smell smoke afterward, and know what had fed it.

He had reported progress in vague terms, and the director had listened with his back turned, and had said only: *There will be a child on the boat today. See that she boards safely.* Those exact words. *Safely.* Not *see that she is dealt with.* Not *recover her.* Quill had turned the phrase over for two days like a coin of uncertain mint, and had concluded, provisionally, that his employer's instructions had changed direction somewhere between winter and spring, and that a wise contractor follows the newest instructions and asks nothing.

So he watched the baby board safely. He noted Mrs. Hobb's competence. He noted the second line on the manifest, which he had certainly not been shown but which explained a great deal. And when the *Corvane Star*'s engine woke with a shudder and the whole vessel trembled like a dog shaking off water, Mr. Quill touched his hat brim — to whom, he could not have said — and permitted himself the smallest satisfaction of a job closed without incident.

Some cases, he reflected, turning back up the hill toward the office, are best solved by everyone pretending they never existed. He intended to pretend extremely well.

* * *

**Rosalind**

The boat pulled away at half past nine, and the river let her go.

Rosalind stood at the dock's end with Marta on one side and Isandro on the other, and she watched. That was all there was to do now, all that remained of four months of hiding and hunger and arithmetic: stand, and watch, and let go.

Tam was at the stern rail, wrapped so thoroughly he looked like laundry, and he was waving — waving with his whole arm, huge sweeping arcs, the way you wave when you mean it — and she could see his mouth moving and could not hear the words over the paddle and the cracking ice, and it didn't matter. She knew the words. They were hers too.

Farther forward, a grey figure held a bundle up to the wind, carefully, showing the child the river, the town, the smoke, the world. And behind Mrs. Hobb, at the rail by the wheelhouse, stood a tall man in a dark coat whom Rosalind had not seen board — Ambrose Corvane, come to see the boat off, come to stand in the spray with his hat bareheaded and his face unreadable, watching his granddaughter be carried out of Hallowmere toward the country house above the falls where, Mrs. Hobb had whispered, a nursery had been opened and aired for the first time in twenty years.

The ice broke in earnest then. It did not crack gently; it failed catastrophically, sheet after vast sheet tilting and splitting, the whole river shrugging off four months of silence in a quarter hour of thunder. Great pans ground against the hull. The paddle churned black water studded with grinding white. And through all the din Rosalind heard — or imagined she heard, and decided it counted either way — a thin child's voice carry across the water, high and clear:

"Goodbye Rosie! Fortnightly! I'll be fat!"

Her legs nearly gave. Marta's arm came around her like a beam. And Rosalind stood in the ruin of the winter and waved until her arm burned and the boat grew small and the small boat grew a speck and the speck rounded the bend below Corvane Point and was gone, and the river ran open and brown and enormous where it had been, full of spring, full of everything the winter had held.

She did not cry, quite. Something larger than crying moved through her instead, tidal, and when it receded she found herself still standing, still held between the cook and the lamplighter, still on the dock, still in Hallowmere — unemployed, unindentured, unfixed, free in a way that felt less like a gift than a fall.

"Well," said Isandro at last, into the settling quiet. He tapped his pole on the planks, twice, the way he ended every round. "Sun's up. Lamps won't light themselves tonight, and there's a lot a lamplighter ought to teach before autumn."

Rosalind looked at the open water, then at the two people beside her, then back at the empty river that had taken her brother and given back, impossibly, a future.

"Show me the route," she said. "All of it. Start at the beginning."

Isandro smiled like a man whose daughter had come home by another road, and turned toward the lamp room. But it was Marta who spoke next, reading the folded paper the director's clerk had pressed into Rosalind's hand at the gangway — reading it, and going suddenly still.

"Girl," she said slowly, "this says you're summoned to the director's house. Sunday. For tea."
