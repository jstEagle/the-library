## 20. After the Flood

The river gave back what it took, but never in the shape it took it.

Marta Kessler stood at her kitchen window on the first morning the water had fallen below the second stair of the company dock, and she counted the things floating past that used to belong to Hallowmere: a door off its hinges, painted the green of the chandlery; a crate stamped with the Corvane mark; a child's boot, small as her fist, turning slowly in the brown current like something deciding which way to go. She had learned, in forty years of feeding a river town, not to look too long at what the water carried. Grief you could bake around. Grief you could salt and store. But staring — staring was how it got inside you.

Still, she looked. She was owed one good look. Everyone in Hallowmere was.

The flood had come the way old Toma the ferryman had promised it would, all his life and nobody believing him: not as a wall but as a rising mood, the river swelling through three days of rain until it swallowed the low streets, the fish market, the chapel steps, and finally licked at the warehouse foundations with a tongue grey as dishwater. For nine days the town lived in its upper rooms, rowing between rooftops in borrowed boats, trading bread for candles, listening to the ice-scoured pilings groan beneath them like an old man turning over in bed. And then, just as quietly as it had risen, the water lost interest and withdrew, leaving behind the smell of drowned earth, a silt line across every door in town like the river signing its name.

Now the sun was out, insultingly bright, and Marta had ninety mouths to feed and no flour delivery and a kitchen that still smelled faintly of the flood's leavings no matter how many times she boiled the copper pot.

She tied her apron, thumped the dough down hard enough to startle the cat, and began, as always, with bread.

* * *

Rosalind came to the kitchen before the bell.

This had been her habit since the winter — slipping in with the dawn, hair braided badly, hands already cold, and Marta would hand her a bowl of porridge without either of them saying why. But this morning Rosalind stood in the doorway as though she had forgotten what doors were for. She was holding a folded paper, and her face wore an expression Marta recognized from the mirror, once, thirty years ago, the day Marta's own mistress died and freed her without meaning to: the look of a person handed their own weight and told, *carry it wherever you like now.*

"They voided it," Rosalind said. "My indenture."

Marta wiped her hands on her apron, though they were clean. "Sit down."

"He called it severance." Rosalind sat the way she did everything — precisely, at the edge of the bench, spine straight, as if comfort were a debt she hadn't earned. "Director Corvane. He signed the paper himself. He said—" She stopped. Started again. "He said my term of service had been concluded *with distinction*, and that the company considered all obligations settled in both directions."

"In both directions." Marta set the porridge in front of her. "And what do you owe him now?"

"Nothing." Rosalind said it like a foreign word. "That's what the paper says. Nothing."

"Then eat your porridge."

But Rosalind didn't touch the spoon. She sat looking at the paper as though it might change its mind if she blinked, and Marta understood, with the particular clarity of a woman who had spent her life watching people eat, that freedom could arrive in a person like a meal after a long fast — wanted desperately, and then almost impossible to get down.

Everyone in the company knew what the paper really was, of course. Severance. Yes. And the moon was made of cheese. The director had taken a knife to the girl's indenture the way a man pays a debt he'll never speak aloud — because she had named his granddaughter on the dock in front of forty witnesses, because she had given up her own berth for the boy, because she had seen, in some ledger of her own, exactly what Ambrose Corvane owed and could not say. Gratitude wore a suit of paperwork in that house. It always had.

"Eat," Marta said again, gentler. "Free girls still need breakfast."

Rosalind picked up the spoon. And then, halfway through the bowl, with her mouth full and her eyes wet, she asked the question Marta had been waiting three weeks for:

"What am I supposed to do now?"

* * *

The town answered that question differently than it answered it for most people.

For most, the answer after a flood was hammering. By mid-morning the sound of Hallowmere rebuilding filled the air like weather — hammers on planking, saws through salvage, men shouting measurements across the mud, women hauling sodden mattresses into the sun to die there. The chandlery's roof was half gone; the fish market was a skeleton of black posts standing in muck; the chapel had taken two feet of water and the hymnals had swollen into bricks. Down at the dock, Corvane men worked in their shirtsleeves prying warped boards off the loading platform, and the great warehouse itself stood, as it always stood, square and indifferent, having shrugged off the river the way it shrugged off everything else.

Marta walked the high street at noon with a basket on her arm and watched the town mend itself, and everywhere she went she heard the same conversation, conducted in the shorthand of people who have survived something together.

"—heard the boat made Uppington in four days, the boy walking by the end of it—"

"—Amberdale's girl, yes, the one who—"

"—well, whatever it was, he tore the paper himself, I heard it from the harbormaster's wife, who heard it from—"

They said Tam's name carefully, the way you touch a bruise. The last boat had gone out ahead of the flood with eleven passengers, and among them a coughing boy of twelve with a knitted scarf and a paper parcel of Marta's ginger biscuits, and a baby who was not listed on any manifest but rode in the arms of the director's own housekeeper, wrapped in a plain wool blanket because the embroidered one was ash in Marta's stove. Marta thought about that blanket more than she let herself. Three generations of a woman's stitches, a laundress's love letter in cross-thread, and Marta had fed it to the fire while a hired watchman stood ten feet from the pantry door. The right thing. The necessary thing. Some nights she still woke and smelled the scorch of it.

The letters had started coming upriver-and-back with the supply barges once the water fell enough for traffic. Two so far. Tam wrote in a round careful hand that the air at Thornleigh was *like breathing soup you'd want to eat*, that Aunt Verity made him ride a horse, that he was gaining weight and losing the cough by turns and winning both races. He always ended the same way: *Tell Rosalind I'm keeping the berth warm. Tell Lantern I remember her face even if nobody else is supposed to.*

Marta had read that line over Isandro's shoulder at the lamp room and felt something in her chest go loose and strange.

Because that was the other thing everyone knew, now, whether or not they said it: the child had a name that wasn't a jest anymore. Isandra. Called Isandra by the housekeeper, by the bargemen, by the doctor's wife who had been dying to discuss the whole affair for months. The director had not acknowledged the girl publicly — had not needed to, after that morning on the dock when one word, *Enough*, had closed Mr. Quill's mouth like a purse — but neither had he sent her away. The housekeeper kept her in the director's own household, in the rooms that had been the late son's, and every Sunday the child was carried down to the lamp room in a clean bonnet to be adored by a lamplighter who had stopped moving like an old man.

Quill himself was gone. Dismissed the same week, quietly, wages paid through no fault of his own, and the town drew its conclusions the way towns do — swiftly, incorrectly, and with relish.

* * *

Marta found Rosalind at the warehouse that afternoon, and the sight of her stopped Marta's breath for a moment, because it was wrong in a way she couldn't immediately name.

The girl was standing in the doorway of the tally room, the little closet of ledgers where she had worked three winters, and she wasn't going in. Her hand rested on the door frame. Inside, another clerk — Pellory's nephew, hired in March — sat at the desk doing her sums in a handwriting like chicken tracks. Rosalind's ink bottle was still on the shelf. Her ruled notebook was still in the drawer, because no one else could read her abbreviations and no one had thrown it out.

"You're not working," Marta said, stating the obvious because sometimes the obvious needed saying before anything else could follow.

"No," Rosalind agreed. Her voice was very level, which Marta had learned was worse than tears. "I'm not."

"When did you last sleep past the bell?"

"I don't know. Before my father drowned, maybe." A ghost of a smile. "I got up at five for six years, Mrs. Kessler. My body still does it. I lie there in Marta's-old-room-which-is-my-room-now and listen to the bells and think I'm late, and then I remember there's nowhere to be late to, and I don't know what to do with my hands."

Marta leaned against the doorframe beside her, and together they watched the nephew miscount a barrel of nails.

"You know what I did," Marta said, "the week after my husband died? I kept setting his plate at the table. Six weeks. Couldn't stop. My hands knew the work and the work didn't know he was gone." She looked at the girl's thin profile against the dark doorway. "Freedom's the same shape as grief, sometimes. Nobody warns you."

"What am I without the ledger?" Rosalind asked the room, or the river beyond it, or perhaps only herself. "I gave everything to survive, Mrs. Kessler. Every hour, every winter, every rule about not wanting loudly. Survival was the whole shape of me. And now the debt's paid — *his* debt and *my* debt and Tam's passage, all of it — and I keep reaching for the arithmetic and there's nothing to count."

"There's bread," Marta said. "There's a town with its roof off. There's a lamplighter who's been asking after you every single day with a subtlety that would embarrass a bull." She pushed off the doorframe. "You've never once been idle, girl. Give it a fortnight before you decide idleness is who you are."

But Rosalind stayed there a long time after Marta left, and Marta knew it, because she looked back twice from the yard.

* * *

The fortnight passed the way floods recede — unevenly, with relapses.

Rosalind hauled planks for the widow Fenwick's roof and did it well, arms strong from six years of cargo. She mended the netting on Isandro's lamp poles without being asked, sitting in the lamp room of an evening while he cleaned glass and told his slow endless stories, and Marta noticed the girl's shoulders coming down from around her ears by degrees, a quarter inch a day. She held Isandra on Sundays and taught the baby a clapping game their mother had played with Tam, and Isandro watched them with an expression Marta privately filed under *things I will tease him about in September.*

But she would not take work. Not the chandler's offer, not Marta's own half-joking plea for help with the baking, not even the schoolmaster's suggestion that a girl who could keep Corvane's books might teach children their letters. Each refusal had the same flinch in it. Marta came to understand it at last, watching her refuse: every job offered was a life offered, and choosing one meant not choosing the others, and Rosalind Amberdale had spent her whole existence with no choices at all. She had become excellent at enduring. She had never once practiced wanting.

Wanting loudly, Marta thought, stirring the evening pot, was a skill like any other. Somebody ought to teach it.

* * *

On the first of June, the director sent for her.

Marta heard about it within the hour, because the kitchen heard about everything within the hour. He did not summon her to the office — that was the detail everyone chewed on. He met her at the rebuilt dock at dusk, when the crews had gone, and stood looking at the water the way he had stood looking at it all spring, and when Rosalind arrived he spoke to her for perhaps ten minutes with the housekeeper's shadow and the child's bonnet visible at the far end of the platform.

What was said, none of the gossips ever learned. But Marta saw Rosalind afterward, and that told its own story.

The girl came up the kitchen steps white-faced, holding nothing, carrying somehow a great deal. She sat down across from Marta and put her palms flat on the table like a woman steadying herself against weather.

"He offered me the ledger room back," she said. "Clerk of record. Wages. Or—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—or he'll sponsor papers. Any trade. Anywhere. He said I had proven I understand the difference between what a thing costs and what it's worth, and that the company had never before been able to tell the difference, and that I should take my time answering."

"And what did you say?"

"I said I'd take my time." Rosalind stared at her flat hands. "Mrs. Kessler, he thanked me. In four words, in that voice like a closing door, but he *thanked me*. And then Isandra laughed at the end of the dock, and he turned around to look, and for a moment he was just a man hearing a baby laugh on a dock in June, and I thought—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I thought, that's the grief under it. That's the son. He carries it the way the town carries the flood line. You can paint over the wall but you know how high the water came."

Marta said nothing. Some silences are the truest speech a cook has.

"He asked after you," Rosalind added, unexpectedly. "He asked if you'd burned the blanket, and I said yes, and he nodded, and then he said — Mrs. Kessler, he said, *tell the cook she chose well and paid dear, and that debts of that kind are remembered longer than debts of money.*"

Marta found, to her enormous irritation, that her eyes were stinging. She turned to the stove and attacked a pot that needed nothing.

"Well," she said gruffly. "Man can count after all."

* * *

That night the lamps went round as they always did, and Marta stood at her window above the kitchen and watched the lights bloom down the lane — Isandro's slow figure and, beside him now, a second figure learning the route, lifting her pole at each bracket the way he'd taught her, flame by flame bringing the drowned town back into the world.

Hallowmere would rebuild. It always had; rivers and towns were old married things that fought and forgave. The chandlery would get its roof. The chapel would dry out. Somewhere upriver a boy was riding horses and growing fat on his aunt's cooking, and in the director's house a baby slept under a name chosen in a lamp room, and the river ran on toward the sea carrying nothing of theirs anymore.

And in the middle of it all, Rosalind Amberdale stood at the end of the lane where the last lamp met the dark, unemployed and unindentured and free as a gull, and Marta saw her stand there a long while — not lost, exactly. More like a ship between harbors, sails down, feeling for wind.

Marta turned back to her kitchen, banked the fire, and allowed herself, for the first time since October, to plan a menu for a wedding-season feast rather than a famine ration.

It was two days later, at breakfast, that Rosalind set down her cup and said, in the voice of a girl who had finally decided to want something loudly:

"Mrs. Kessler. How much flour would it take to fill the lamp room with bread — enough bread for the whole town — and would you teach me to bake it? Because I've decided what I'm going to be, and I'm going to need an oven."
