## 21. The Lamp Room

The lamp room smelled of oil and iron and forty years of Isandro's hands. He had never thought of it that way until the morning he handed Rosalind the brass key and watched her close her fist around it the way a person closes a fist around a coal—carefully, knowing it would mark her.

"Turn it," he said. "Feel the teeth."

She turned it over. The key was old, its edges worn soft as river stone, the brass gone the color of weak tea where his thumb had polished it ten thousand times. "It's lighter than I thought."

"Heavier than you think," he corrected. "That's the first lesson. Everything about the rounds looks light. A lantern is a small thing. A route is a small thing. A flame is the smallest thing in Hallowmere. But you carry them all together, night after night, and you'll learn what they weigh."

She didn't argue. That was the thing about Rosalind Amberdale that Isandro had loved since the winter she was a silent girl tallying crates in the warehouse—she did not argue, she *listened*, the way ice listens to the river before it decides to break. Her father had been a pearl diver, drowned in the company's debt, and the company had taught her to be invisible. Isandro had spent a year teaching her the opposite: that a lamplighter is invisible too, but by choice, and for a purpose. The difference between being unseen and being a keeper of light—that was the whole trade, and no one could teach it in a day.

"Show me," she said.

So he did.

* * *

They began at dusk, as the rounds always began, at the corner of Corvane Row where the first lantern stood on its iron post like a patient sentinel. Isandro carried the ladder on his shoulder; Rosalind carried the oil can and the flint kit, and she carried them wrong, he told her, and she adjusted, and he said nothing more, because the second lesson was that a lamplighter learns mostly by being corrected and mostly corrects herself.

"First light is the hardest," he said, working the wick free. "Not because of the mechanism. Because of what it means. This one stands at the mouth of the row. Every person coming home sees it first. You're not lighting a lamp, girl. You're telling the whole street: the dark is not in charge tonight."

He struck the flint. The wick caught, bloomed, steadied. The glass chimney filled with a soft gold the color of the inside of a shell.

"Your turn."

She climbed the ladder at the second lantern, and her hands were steady—diver's daughter, he remembered, steady hands were in her blood—and she struck the flint twice before it caught. The flame wavered, then stood.

"Wind from the river," she said.

"There's always wind from the river. You'll learn to read it before you feel it. Off the water in the evening, on the water after midnight. You trim the wicks shorter on the water-side lamps or they'll gutter by the second bell." He watched her look down the long line of posts, thirty-one of them from the warehouse to the ferry landing, each one already becoming hers. "You've been walking this route with me for a year. Tonight you walk it as if it were yours. Because it is."

"Because you say it is."

"Because I say it is," he agreed, "and because the lamps don't care what I say. They only care who shows up."

* * *

They walked. The evening came down over Hallowmere the way it did in the first warm weeks after the flood, slow and honeyed, the river running high and brown and busy, barges groaning at their moorings, the town smelling of wet timber and bread and the green rot of things growing again. Isandro moved from lamp to lamp with the economy of a man who had done ten thousand evenings of this, and Rosalind moved beside him, and he found himself narrating without meaning to—the lamp outside the chandlery needed its glass replaced, the one by the fish market ran hot and ate wicks, the post at the ferry landing leaned two degrees east and the council had promised for six years to right it and would promise for sixty more.

"You know all their tempers," she said.

"You'll know worse. A lamp is like a horse. It remembers who's kind to it."

At the halfway mark they paused, as they always did, at the low stone wall above the water stairs. The river slid past below, swollen with spring, carrying sticks and petals and once, briefly, a white flower that might have come from anywhere. Isandro sat. He always sat. Rosalind sat beside him without asking why, which was its own kind of inheritance.

"My daughter's name was Elena," he said. He had told her once before, the night they named the foundling, and he had not said it since, and he found that saying it again cost less than the first time. Grief was like that. It did not shrink, but the throat learned to make room. "She would be thirty-one this year. I light the lamps because the night she went into the river, the lamps along the bank were dark. The lamplighter then was a drunk named Fenn, and he'd skipped his rounds, and I have spent forty years being the man who doesn't skip."

Rosalind said nothing. She was good at that. She let the river say what needed saying.

"I'm not telling you this to burden you."

"I know. You're telling me so I know what I'm carrying."

He looked at her. Sixteen, seventeen now, thin as a wick and twice as stubborn, with her brother upriver and mending and a child not her own asleep in Marta's kitchen, and she had looked at the route the way she looked at everything—like arithmetic, like a debt she intended to pay honestly.

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly it."

* * *

The formal part came on Sunday, because Sundays were the only day the company's clock stopped, and Isandro wanted witnesses.

He had asked Marta, and Marta had cooked—of course Marta had cooked, she expressed every emotion short of rage in pastry—and the kitchen of the Corvane house smelled of almond and browned butter when they gathered: Marta with flour to her elbows, Rosalind scrubbed and uncomfortable in her good dress, and little Isandra on Marta's hip, eleven months old now and round-faced and solemn as a magistrate, watching the room with her mother's gray eyes—or her grandmother's, or the laundress's, no one would ever say, and no one needed to.

The director did not come. Isandro had not expected him to. But a parcel had arrived that morning from the Corvane house, wrapped in good paper, and inside was a lamplighter's coat, wool, dark blue, sized for a girl who would grow into it, and no note, and no note was the director's way of speaking.

Isandro stood in the middle of the kitchen with the old brass key in his palm.

"There are no guilds for this," he said. "No papers, no oath, no master's marks. The council pays a wage and the town sleeps better and that's the whole of the ceremony the world requires. But a trade passed hand to hand deserves a word, so here's mine." He took her hand, turned it over, and pressed the key into it, and closed her fingers the way he had that first morning, and held them shut. "This key opens the lamp room, the oil store, and the cabinet where the spare glass lives. It has opened them for forty years. It opened them the night your father's debt drowned him and it opened them the night a baby was left on the dock, and it will open them for as long as you let it. I'm not giving you a job, Rosalind Amberdale. I'm giving you the nights."

Her eyes were wet but her voice was level. "And what do you get?"

He smiled. "Someone to carry the ladder when my shoulder finally quits. And someone to say Elena's route is still lit, if anyone ever asks."

Marta blew her nose into her apron with the force of a foghorn, and Isandra, startled, laughed—a bright, sudden, unguarded sound, the kind of sound that made a room feel briefly like a different, better world—and Rosalind lifted the girl onto her hip and stood there in her new coat that hung past her fingertips, and Isandro thought: *there. That is the whole ceremony. That is the guild entire.*

"Isandra," he said, testing the name. They had named her for him as a jest and then as a covenant, and still the name caught in his chest like a struck match. "Come here, little one."

The baby reached for him with both hands, the way babies do, as if the whole world were one long invitation, and he took her, and she grabbed his collar with a grip that meant to keep it.

"Sundays," Marta said, wiping her eyes. "She comes to the kitchen Sundays. That's settled. But I'll tell you what isn't settled—" and here Marta's voice took on the tone it took when she was about to make a problem everyone else had been politely ignoring, "—the director's housekeeper wrote again. Wants the child for a full day next week. Alone. At the Corvane house."

The kitchen went quiet. The river murmured beyond the wall. Rosalind's arms tightened around nothing, around the ghost of the baby she had already handed over.

"She's his granddaughter," Rosalind said at last. "Someday he'll have to look at her in daylight."

"Yes," Isandro said, and felt the old river-cold move through him, the feeling of ice deciding. "And someday is a dangerous word in this town."

He held the child a moment longer, this small girl with his name, this granddaughter of the most powerful man on the river, this foundling with no paper and no claim and a coat of good wool arriving in parcels without notes. Outside, the first lamp of the evening came on by itself—no, not by itself; by Rosalind, already at the door, already reaching for the ladder, already learning the weight of the light.

The route was hers now. And the route, Isandro knew, had never once ended where the last lantern stood.
