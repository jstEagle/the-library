## 22. What the River Keeps

The ledger smelled the way all old ledgers smell — of dust and tallow and the slow brown decay of things written down and forgotten. Rosalind Amberdale had spent four years of her life bent over books like this one, her fingers black to the second knuckle, her eyes trained to find the small errors that meant the difference between a ship's honest profit and a clerk's quiet theft. She had been good at it. Invisible by design, exacting by necessity. The Corvane Company had taught her that a person could be reduced to a column of figures, and she had learned the lesson so thoroughly that she had once believed herself capable of nothing but reduction.

Now she sat at Marta's kitchen table with a dead man's ledger open before her, and outside the window the river ran high and green with the first week of June, a year almost to the day since the ice had broken and carried her brother away from her.

It was not a Corvane book. It was older, humbler — a wherryman's manifest from the year of her birth, purchased for three coppers from a stall that sold old paper by the pound. Her father's name appeared in it eleven times. *J. Amberdale, wherry, 4 sacks meal, up.* Eleven times the ink said what he had carried, and never once what he was. That was the nature of ledgers. They kept the weight of a man and lost everything else.

She had found the entry she wanted on her third evening of searching. There, in the margin beside a shipment of salt pork dated the week of her sixth birthday, in a hand she would have known anywhere — that careful, schoolmaster's slant he'd been so proud of, the letters standing straight as fence posts because a man who couldn't afford schooling made his writing do the work of one —

*J. Amberdale, pearl diver, of Hallowmere. Debts discharged by drowning, 14 March.*

*Debts discharged by drowning.*

As if the river were a creditor. As if he had walked into the water to settle an account.

Rosalind dipped her pen. The nib trembled only a little.

She had thought about this for months. Through the flood season, when the roads were water and the town was an island and Isandra had cut her first tooth gnawing on the handle of a spare lamp hook. Through the thaw and the mud and the rebuilding, when the whole of Hallowmere hammered and sawed and argued about pilings, and Rosalind walked her rounds each dusk past half-ruined docks, lighting lamps for streets where no houses yet stood, because the route was the route and light did not wait for convenience. Through Tam's first letter, which had arrived creased into a boatman's boot and smelling of tobacco, and his second, which reported in his careful thirteen-year-old hand that Aunt Prudence's farm had *more chickens than sense* and that the doctor upriver had listened to his chest with a cold brass instrument and pronounced him *mending like iron*. Mending like iron. She had read that line forty times. She had read it to Marta over bread, and Marta had wept into her flour, though she claimed afterward it was the onions.

Through all of it, the thought had stayed with her, patient as sediment: somewhere in some book, in some margin, her father existed only as a debt discharged. And debts discharged should be allowed to rest. But men were not debts.

She wrote in the margin, beneath his hand, in hers:

*Edmund Amberdale. Husband of Lirie Amberdale. Father of Rosalind and Tam. He dove for pearls in the deep channel below the weir and brought up beauty with bleeding hands. He sang low on the water in the mornings. He is remembered.*

She sat back. The ink dried slowly in the kitchen warmth. It was not much — five lines against the weight of a drowned world — but it was true, and it was written, and it would stay written longer than any memory of him lived in any living head. Someday, maybe, someone would buy this old manifest by the pound and find those lines among the salt pork and wonder. Let them wonder. Wondering was better than erasure.

She closed the book. The sound it made was soft, final, like a door shut gently rather than slammed — the way Marta closed doors now, in this house, where a baby slept upstairs.

* * *

The house had belonged to no one, once. It was a lamp-tender's cottage at the bend where the warehouse lane met the water stair, owned by the company and listed in their books as *structure, obsolete,* worth less than the taxes on its footprint. When Rosalind's indenture was voided — *severance,* Director Corvane had said to his clerks, in the flat tone of a man recording an expense; everyone in Hallowmere knew what it was — she had expected to leave with the ice-melt, to follow Tam upriver or take service in Corvane itself, because survival did not ask where you wished to live.

But Isandro had come to her on the dock the last day of the flood season, when the water stood brown and fat between its banks, and had said only: "The lamps won't light themselves, girl. And my knees have opinions about ladders now."

And Marta had said: "I've cooked thirty years for men who never once tasted anything I set down. I'll cook for people who taste."

And Director Corvane, meeting her eyes across the width of his office with that terrible stillness he wore like winter clothing, had said: "You will want for nothing. That is not generosity. Do not mistake it." And then, at the door, quieter, not looking at her: "The child has her mother's chin. You wouldn't know that. But I do." Which was as close as that man would ever come to saying thank you, or forgive me, or anything at all that mattered.

So they had taken the obsolete structure and made it a home, which was, Rosalind thought sometimes, the family's story told in miniature: taking what the world had written off and insisting it still held light.

The evenings were the best of it. The rounds began an hour before dark, because Isandro had taught her that lamplighting done well was invisible — you finished before anyone noticed the shadows needed answering, and the town simply found itself lit, the way a child finds itself loved, without ever seeing the hands that did the work. She climbed the ladder at the customs house. She trimmed the wick at the ferry stair. She carried her long pole with its brass tongue down the lane where the flood had taken the Hendersons' boathouse, and lit the new post the town had planted there, and stood a moment looking at the water sliding past, high and calm now, keeping what it kept.

The river had given back most of what it took that spring. Boathouses floated home. Fences returned to their fields. Some things it gave back changed — the church bell came up green with a crack in its voice, and rang ever after slightly flat, and the town decided it liked the flatness better. Some things it never gave back at all. Rosalind had stopped watching the water for them. Her father had been in the river thirty years; the river owed her nothing and paid nothing. What remained of him she carried on land, in margins, in the name she wrote on the lease of a lamp-tender's cottage: *R. Amberdale, lamplighter.*

Her father had been entered in a ledger as a debt. She had entered herself in the world as a light.

* * *

Tam's letters came with the packet boats, irregularly, and were read aloud at the kitchen table as a kind of ceremony, Marta presiding with her arms folded and her face arranged into disapproval so that no one would see how much she treasured them. Tam wrote the way he had always spoken — sideways, funny, saving the important thing for last.

*Aunt Prudence says to tell you the fence will be done by harvest and then I may stop being useful and start being lazy, which is apparently a promotion. School begins in September. The schoolmaster here does not hit, which Marta will not believe, so tell her twice.*

Then, at the bottom, smaller:

*Dr. Hale heard my chest again. He says there is nothing left in it to worry about and I should worry about arithmetic instead. I dream about the river sometimes but not the bad parts. Mostly the lamps. Tell Isandro I keep count of the lights along the shore here and none of them are hung right and it bothers me. Tell Rosalind I am not small anymore. Tell Isandra I will bring her something from the fair and she had better not forget me before then.*

"She won't forget you," Rosalind said to the letter, and looked up at the doorway, where Isandra stood holding the doorframe with both fat hands, having recently discovered standing and having resolved to practice it at every opportunity, preferably while laughing.

She was fourteen months old and she had opinions about everything. She had her mother's laundress-hands, strong little fingers, and — though only three people alive knew to look for it — the director's heavy dark brow, which made her look perpetually like a small magistrate disapproving of the state of the floor. She wore coats of good wool that arrived in parcels without notes. Once a month, on no schedule anyone could predict, the director's own housekeeper came down the lane with a basket, handed it over without a word, and permitted herself exactly one minute of holding the baby before departing with military composure and, Marta swore, red eyes.

"Say Tam," Rosalind coached. "Tam. Ta-am."

Isandra said, "Da," with enormous satisfaction, which was her word for everything: the lamp, the cat, the river, Marta's spoon, the entire audible universe. Then she laughed — that sudden, full-body laugh that seemed to start in her belly and break upward like ice going out, and Marta dropped her ladle, and the dog barked, and the kitchen filled up with the sound the way a room fills with light when the wick is trimmed right.

There was no word in any language Rosalind knew for what that laugh did to the room. She suspected there was no need for one. Some things were not for ledgers.

* * *

On Sunday, Isandro came to walk with her before supper, as he did now, though the rounds were hers and he had no official business touching a single lamp. He walked anyway. A man who had lost a daughter to the river and gained one back out of a basket on a loading dock was entitled to walk wherever he pleased on Sundays.

They went up to the lamp room above the harbor light, the two of them and Isandra riding Rosalind's hip, and stood in the glass-smelling dark while the evening assembled itself below: the mended docks, the new shingles still pale as scars, the boats out on the green water, the smoke rising straight in the windless air.

"You wrote it, then," Isandro said. It was not a question; Marta told him everything, and Marta told him everything because he was incapable of gossip and therefore safe as a grave. "Your father's name. In the book."

"I wrote it."

"Good." He turned the great lens a quarter-inch with two fingers, a habit of sixty years, checking alignment that needed no checking. "The river keeps what it keeps. But paper — paper argues back."

Below them, at the foot of the harbor stair, a small figure stood looking up — the housekeeper, early this month, basket on her arm, waiting with the patience of furniture. And beyond her, out on the water road that had opened with the season, the packet boat was coming in with the evening tide, and Rosalind felt the familiar tightening in her chest that every arriving boat produced now, hope and fear braided together, because boats carried letters and letters carried Tam, and one of these days — not today, but someday soon, when the fence was done and the doctor agreed and Aunt Prudence finally ran out of reasons — a boat would carry Tam himself, standing at the rail, counting the lights along the shore to see if they were hung right.

He would find them hung right. She would make sure of it, every night, the way she had made sure of everything this year: not by surviving it, but by building it, stick by stick, wick by wick, name by name.

Isandra reached up and closed her fist in Rosalind's hair and said, "Da," meaning everything.

"Yes," Rosalind agreed. "All of it."

She struck the match. The harbor light took the flame and threw it far out over the water, further than any ledger line, further than any debt, out to where the dark was deepest — and held.
