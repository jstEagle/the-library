## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

There is a particular weight to a pocket watch that has stopped—not the weight of its brass and glass, which is nothing, but the weight of all the minutes it swallowed and never gave back. I held one such watch in my palm that morning and thought, as I always did, that we had a great deal in common.

The workshop of Master Aldous Vane occupies a space behind a shopfront on Candlewick Lane that the city surveyors have measured three times and gotten three different answers, which suits us both fine. A room the state cannot size correctly is a room it cannot tax, inventory, or remember. Vane says the walls lean because the building is tired. I say they lean because they have learned the same trick we have: stand slightly crooked, and no one looks at you twice.

I was hunched over the bench at half past seven, lamp trimmed low because low lamps make clerks' eyes slide off windows, working on the stopped watch with a loupe screwed into my eye socket like a second, more honest pupil. The mainspring had snapped clean through—a mercy, as breaks go. Snapped springs can be coaxed back into temper. Springs that stretch slowly, weakening over years until they haven't the strength to turn the wheels anymore, those you replace. Those are dead, and no amount of wishing otherwise will wind them again.

"Third rule," I said to the watch, because the watch was the only thing in the room permitted to hear me talk to myself.

Actually, let me be precise, because precision is the whole of my trade and most of my survival. The rules were not numbered aloud in our household. Vane considered numbering things a form of invitation—number three implies a number four, and a number four implies someone is counting. But I kept my own private ledger, and in it the rules went thus:

One. No names spoken aloud. Not mine, not his, not the street's, not the city's if I could help it. Names are handles, and handles exist so that someone may lift you.

Two. No eyes held too long. A glance is weather; a stare is a signature. Look at a man's shoes, his hands, the seam of his coat—anywhere but the face, which is the page the registries read.

Three. Be useful in small amounts. A man who fixes your watch is furniture. A man who fixes your watch *and* remembers your daughter's birthday is a witness.

And there were others, unwritten, worn into me like grooves into a pivot: walk at the pace of nobody in particular; carry tools because tools explain a man's presence better than papers ever could; never be the most interesting thing in any room, including rooms containing only yourself.

I set the new spring into the barrel with tweezers finer than an apology and felt the click travel up my wrist—that small, obedient *tick* of a mechanism accepting its purpose. Somewhere in the city above us, forty thousand clocks were doing the same thing at the same instant, because in Corvane's Harbor even time is registered. The Observatory's master clock distributed the hour by telegraph wire to every public dial in the district, and every public dial told every citizen when to present themselves for inspection, and so in a way I cannot fully articulate without bitterness, I had spent my twenty-three years taking orders from a clock.

Not that I thought of it that way before that morning. Before that morning I thought of it not at all, which was, of course, the point of me.

"Hazel."

Vane's voice from the doorway, pitched under the sound of the escapement rack, which he left running for exactly this purpose—cover noise, we called it, though never by that name.

"Yes, master." I did not look up. Rule two applies even indoors, even to the one man who knows what I am. Habit is the only armor that doesn't rust.

"The Harrowby watch?"

"Spring's cut. Fitting the new one now. It'll keep honest time by noon, or I'll eat the balance wheel."

"See that you don't. Balance wheels repeat on you." He crossed the room—I heard his bad knee, the left one, complaining in its native tongue—and stood at the bench's end doing the thing he did some mornings, which was look at me as though consulting an inventory taken years ago and checking nothing had been added or subtracted since. "You slept poorly."

"I slept."

"Poorly," he repeated, with the mild tyranny of a man who has diagnosed you and will not be argued out of the chart. "You wound the regulator twice in the night. I heard it through the floor."

This was true. There is a regulator clock in my sleeping loft, small and faithful, and when my thoughts will not settle I wind it and watch the pendulum until the pendulum's opinion becomes mine. It had taken four winds to subdue me that night. I did not mention this, because mentioning it would invite the question of what had me restless, and the honest answer—*nothing, everything, the same restlessness that has lived under my ribs since I was nine years old and learned that a boy without papers is a rumor the law would prefer to correct*—was not a conversation either of us wanted before breakfast.

"Drink your tea," Vane said instead, which was his method of saying several other things, and put a chipped cup beside the Harrowby watch where it would be safe from my elbows.

We worked in parallel silence for a while, which is the truest form of conversation either of us speaks. His hands are seventy years old and still steadier than a mounted vise; watching him file a pallet stone is watching argument itself submit to geometry. I finished the Harrowby, cased it, wiped my prints from the crystal with a rag kept solely for the erasure of evidence that I exist, and reached for the next job on the slate.

That was when the knock came.

Three knocks, evenly spaced, firm without being rude—the knock of a person who has never once wondered whether a door will open for him. Vane and I looked at each other for the length of half a tick, which in our household constitutes a shouted conference, and then he pulled the curtain across the inner bench, where certain commissions live whose provenance we do not discuss, and opened the outer door with the expression of a man mildly annoyed at the weather.

The young man on the step wore the gray-and-gold of the Observatory service and held a leather folio as though it might bruise. Behind him, on the lane, stood a municipal runner who very carefully looked at nothing, which is a skill I usually associate with people on my side of doors.

"Master clockmaker?" the gray-and-gold asked.

"Horologist," said Vane, which was true and also smaller-sounding. "What's wanted?"

"The great clock." The courier's voice had the flattened carefulness of a man delivering news he'd rehearsed. "It lost four seconds Tuesday, six yesterday, and this morning it stopped entirely between the ninth and tenth strokes of eight. Mr. Ellery—" the chief astronomer, though he didn't say so, "—requires a competent hand today. Today, mind. The city dials are already lying to everyone."

"Stopped between strokes," Vane murmured. He had gone very still in the way I have learned to read as loudly as other men shout. "Interesting."

"Is it?" said the courier, who had clearly never found anything interesting in his life and was proud of it.

"Between strokes means the fault is in the escapement or the gravity train, not the going train. It means the clock didn't die. It hesitated." Vane said this the way other men say *the patient has a pulse*. Then, louder: "My eyes are seventy. My apprentice does my climbing and my fine work. He'll come."

"He'll need a work order." The folio opened; the runner stepped forward with the eagerness of a man who has finally located something to look at, namely me. "Name and registration number for the ledger."

Here is the moment I should describe properly, because everyone believes moments like this arrive with drums. They do not. They arrive with a municipal runner breathing through his nose and a courier impatient to be elsewhere, and the drum is only your own heart, going hard against your ribs like something trying to get out before the door is opened on it.

I am unregistered. There is no gentler way to arrange those syllables. I came into Corvane's Harbor at nine years old with no papers, no name that anyone official had ever blessed, and a talent for fixing things that Vane discovered when he caught me mending his shop bell rather than reporting me for loitering near it. In eleven years he has built me a shadow of legitimacy—an apprentice's working card, a number borrowed so long ago and used so consistently that it has grown roots—but roots are not the same as ground. Under a clerk's lamp, under the right question, the whole arrangement comes up out of the soil like a weed, and weeds in this city are pulled.

"Boy," said Vane. Not my name—he never says it aloud, rule one, the rule that made me, the rule that keeps me—and I understood that too, the way you understand a lock by feel in the dark.

I recited the number. Eleven years of saying it in exactly that flat tone, and it still tastes like someone else's bread. The runner wrote it down in a ledger whose pages I watched the way one watches a dog that has bitten before. He wrote my description too—*youngish, dark-haired, steady hands*—and I thought, not for the first time, that if the registries ever employed poets instead of clerks, none of us would survive the week.

"Work order's valid until six o'clock," the courier said. "Registry rounds begin at dusk. Have your man out of the Observatory grounds before then or he'll be catalogued with the evening intake."

"Catalogued," Vane repeated pleasantly, as though the word were a type of screw thread. "Of course."

The door closed. We stood a moment in the smell of oil and cold tea and the tick of forty faithful mechanisms, and I picked up my tool roll and began choosing what to bring, because my hands knew the ritual even while the rest of me stood in the doorway of some larger decision, looking in.

"You needn't go," Vane said quietly. "I can send word my apprentice is ill. Illness explains many things; it is the poor man's alibi."

"And lose the Observatory commission? The great clock, the one mechanism in this city older than the registries?" I wound the strap of the tool roll around my wrist. "Besides, master. When else do I get to be somewhere?"

He looked at me then—really looked, rule two suspended by mutual treaty—and whatever he saw made him take his spectacles off and polish them longer than spectacles require.

"The observatory clock," he said, "is the only machine in this harbor the Magistrate's office does not own. Charter of the old king, never revoked. Its case has never been opened by any clerk, any inspector, any registry man. Remember that, if nothing else sticks."

"Why would I need to remember it?"

"No reason," said Vane, which in eleven years had preceded three separate catastrophes and one inheritance. He pressed the folded work order into my palm and closed my fingers over it like a man burying something for later. "Repair the clock. Speak to no one. Return before the lamps are lit."

"And if the clock wants more than a day?"

He was already turning back to his bench, bad knee complaining, hands finding their work the way drowning men find driftwood.

"Then," he said, "you will have to decide how long you are willing to be somewhere, before somewhere decides to keep you."

I went out into the lane with my collar up and my eyes down, past the runner, past the gray-and-gold courier waiting at the corner as though hired punctuation, into a morning that smelled of river fog and coal smoke and the particular burnt-metal breath of a city waking up to inspect itself. And all the way to the Observatory hill, climbing through streets that have never once spoken my name, I turned Vane's last words over like a coin of doubtful minting, and could not make them stop ringing.

Somewhere, decided otherwise. But I did not know that yet. All I knew was that the fog was lifting off the dome ahead of me like a lid coming off a pot, and beneath that lid, for one more ordinary morning, the great clock was keeping a secret it hadn't yet shared with me—

—and had stopped, between the ninth stroke and the tenth, precisely at the hour the registries call eight o'clock.

Which, I would later learn, is exactly when the girl was left on its doorstep.
