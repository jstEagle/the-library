## 2. Orders Come at Dusk

Master Vane believed that dusk was a conspiracy.

He never said so in those words, of course. What he said was: "The registry clerks make their rounds at half past six, boy, and a man on the street at half past six is a man who wants to be asked questions." But I had lived under his roof for eleven years, and I had learned to translate. In Vane's cosmology, the whole city conspired—lamplighters conspired to cast shadows in the wrong places, constables conspired to have eyes, and the evening clerks of the Bureau of Registry conspired most of all, because their ledgers were the machine that ground the rest of us into entries or erased us entirely.

So when the summons came, it came at the worst possible hour, and Vane received it the way other men receive news of a death in the family: with a long stillness and then a great deal of activity that looked like anger but was really fear wearing its work clothes.

The summons itself was a printed card, cream-colored, heavy stock—the kind of paper that assumed it would be obeyed. It had been delivered by a courier who did not knock so much as permit the door to be opened for him. Vane read it twice, holding it close to the lamp as though proximity might reveal an escape clause.

"The observatory," he said. "Their master clock is failing."

"Is that bad?" I asked, which was honest, if not clever.

"It's the most important clock in this city, boy. It sets the time for every registry office, every court, every checkpoint ledger in the district." He set the card down on the bench with two fingers, the way one handles something that might bite. "When the observatory clock runs slow, the clerks' schedules run slow, and the Bureau does not tolerate slowness in anything except justice."

I understood maybe a third of what he meant. I understood all of what he didn't say: that a job like this meant papers. It meant presenting myself at the observatory door, giving a name—*our* name, the registered one, borrowed and maintained like a fire that must never go out—to whoever kept the gate. It meant being visible on purpose, which for men like Master Vane and me was roughly equivalent to a diver volunteering to surface in the middle of a naval battle.

"We could refuse," I said.

Vane looked at me over his spectacles with an expression I can only describe as theological disappointment. "Refuse the observatory. Refuse a summons bearing the city seal." He picked up the card again and turned it over, as if the back might offer better terms. "A clockmaker who refuses work is a clockmaker who gets *noticed*, Hazel. We survive by being dull. We are the grayest men in Grayfall Lane, and I will thank you to keep it that way."

It was the longest speech about dullness ever delivered with shaking hands.

* * *

He gave me my instructions while the light died outside the shop windows, and I want to record them here exactly as he gave them, because they were the last orders he ever gave me freely, before fear made him give different ones.

"One: you repair the escapement. Nothing else. If the striking train wants attention, you note it in your report and you leave it. Two: you speak to no one. Not the gatekeeper, not the astronomers, not the sweep if he's in the corner. You are a pair of hands attached to a registration number, nothing more. Three—" and here he stopped polishing his spectacles, which was how I knew we'd arrived at the part he'd been building toward, "—you return before the evening rounds. Half past six. If the clock is not finished by five, you leave it unfinished. Do you hear me? Unfinished. A broken clock is a problem. An apprentice caught on Cordwell Street after dark is a catastrophe."

"And if they ask why the clock's still broken?"

"They won't ask, because you will be gone." He set the spectacles down. "Four. You do not look at anyone longer than courtesy allows. Faces are how they find people. Give them nothing to find."

I nodded along to all of it. I had known these rules since I was nine years old, when Vane found me sleeping in his coal cellar and—instead of doing the sensible thing and turning me out—taught me gears. Rule four was practically a lullaby; I'd heard it so many times I could have recited it backward. *No names spoken aloud. No eyes held too long. Be the man nobody remembers by remembering that nobody should remember you.*

What I couldn't explain—and didn't try—was the restlessness that had been growing in me all that autumn, quiet as a mainspring winding. Something in me was tired of being a pair of hands. Tired, even, of being safe. There is a particular kind of loneliness that comes from being successfully invisible, and by my twentieth year I had become an expert in it without ever wanting the title.

"I'll be back by six," I said.

"Half past five," said Vane.

"Half past five."

"And Hazel." He hesitated in the doorway of the back room, silhouetted against the dim rectangle of the yard, and for a moment he looked very old—not old the way grandfathers are old, but old the way bridges are old, sound-looking and secretly tired. "If anyone at the observatory asks you where you trained, you tell them the truth. The truth is the best disguise. It's the only lie no one ever checks."

Then he went inside, and I began to pack.

* * *

Packing, for a man in my position, is a philosophical exercise. Everything I owned fit into one canvas roll, and everything I owned had been chosen on a single criterion: could it be carried at a run?

This is not a metaphor for the human condition, though I've since decided it might as well be. Let me inventory it, because the inventory is the truest autobiography I have:

One loupe, brass-cased, chipped at the rim where I dropped it in '91. Three screwdrivers with handles worn to the shape of my grip—Vane says you can read a craftsman's history in his screwdriver handles the way a palm reader reads lines, and mine say *nervous, diligent, left-handed under pressure*. One oiler, one pair of tweezers fine enough to pick up a thought, a jeweler's file, a spool of brass wire, and a wallet of assorted pivots and pins organized by size in a way that would make sense only to another clockmaker, or possibly to God.

Beyond tools: one change of shirt. One wool coat, mended at both elbows by my own hand, badly. A bread roll wrapped in wax paper, because Vane's housekeeper believed no journey was complete without bread, even a journey of two miles. And beneath the lining of the roll, sewn flat, a folded paper bearing a name and a number that were legally mine and factually someone else's—a dead man named Edmund Sterling, registered 1868, deceased in no ledger anywhere, because dead men who were never quite alive don't get death certificates either.

Eleven years I had carried him. Some nights I felt him in the roll like a second spine.

I stood in the workshop a moment before leaving, doing the thing Vane called *taking the room's pulse*: the benches wiped down, the lamps cold, the day's jobs racked and labeled. A pocket watch waited under a cloth for a balance staff I'd have fitted tomorrow—or I wouldn't, depending entirely on how the next twelve hours went, a contingency I tried not to think about too hard. The smell of the place—oil and brass and the ghost of Vane's pipe—followed me to the door the way it always did, and the way it always did, I pretended not to notice.

You learn not to love things out loud. That was rule zero, the rule underneath all the rules. You can keep things—a workshop, a master, a life—but you must hold them loosely, the way you hold a watch crystal, ready for the moment it has to be set down without shattering.

Or so I believed, standing there at twenty years old with a canvas roll and a borrowed name. I mention the belief because the week that followed broke it into pieces small enough to fit through an escapement, and I like to remember what wholeness felt like.

* * *

Cordwell Street at dusk was performing its usual transformation—from a street where people merely ignored each other into a street where ignoring each other was a survival skill. Shopfronts pulled their shutters like eyelids. The lamplighter worked his way up the far side with his pole, and I timed my walking to stay behind his circle of light rather than ahead of it. Light draws the eye; the eye is the clerk of the body; therefore avoid light. It was simple arithmetic, and I had never once questioned it until that evening.

Because that evening, walking toward the observatory hill with the city seal folded in my coat pocket, I caught myself—there's no better way to put it—catching myself looking. At the lamplighter's face, orange-lit and ordinary. At a woman arguing with a fishmonger, her expression cycling from outrage to amusement in the space of a herring. At faces, plural, held a beat too long, as though some part of me already knew that this errand was going to cost me my anonymity and had decided to spend what remained of it on window shopping.

I put my head down. I counted cobbles. The old arithmetic reasserted itself, gray and comfortable as an old coat.

But the itch stayed. That's the honest record: the first crack in my obedience came not at any checkpoint or crisis, but on an unremarkable street at an unremarkable hour, in the person of a woman laughing at a fishmonger, and the terrible revolutionary thought that crossed my mind was simply: *I would like someone to laugh at me like that someday. Which requires being someone.*

Then I reached the foot of Observatory Hill, and the thought went back in its cage, because ahead of me the observatory rose against the bruised sky with its dome catching the last light, and somewhere above it, behind the great dial I could just make out, the master clock ticked on—slow, they said, sick, they said—keeping bad time for every ledger and gallows schedule in the city.

The gatekeeper's lantern was already lit. Behind him, a doorway, a book, a pen, a page waiting for a name.

I checked my canvas roll. I touched the coat where the summons lay. I gave Edmund Sterling's papers one final silent apology for the evening's misuse, and I walked into the light.

I did not know it then—I want to be fair to myself; no one could have known it then—but the clock was the least of what that building wanted repaired.

The gatekeeper lifted his lantern, looked at me, and opened the register.
