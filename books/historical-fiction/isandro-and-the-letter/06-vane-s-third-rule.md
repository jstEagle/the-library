## 6. Vane's Third Rule

Master Aldous Vane had three rules, and he recited them the way other men said grace—by rote, without conviction, and with the vague hope that somebody upstairs was listening.

Rule one: a clockmaker's hands are never wrong. Rule two: never take work from a man whose smile arrives before his money. Rule three—and this was the one he'd carved into the bench itself, so I'd have to feel it under my forearms every day of my apprenticeship—*the world does not see what it has not been taught to see. Stay untaught.*

I had broken all three rules before breakfast, and it wasn't even properly breakfast yet. The sun had barely cleared the rooftops of Copperlane. Somewhere below us, the city was shaking itself awake, clerks buttoning their collars, inspectors oiling their boots, and here I was in the workshop loft with a stolen child asleep on a sack of brass offcuts and a sealed letter burning a hole through my coat like a coal.

She had slept the whole way back. That was the remarkable thing. I had walked her through morning streets that catalogued faces for a living, past two constables and a registry crier bawling the week's notices, and she had simply held my sleeve with one small fist and trusted the arrangement completely. As though being invisible were something you could inherit. As though I'd passed it to her through the cloth.

Her name—I didn't know her name. She hadn't offered it and I hadn't asked, because asking felt like the first step down a staircase with no bottom. In my head I'd been calling her nothing at all, which is its own kind of cruelty, so somewhere on Fetter's Bridge I'd started thinking of her as *mine*, which was worse, and then as *ours*, which was worst of all, because *our* implied a second party who would have opinions when he woke up.

The second party was currently asleep in the room below, snoring with the metronomic regularity of a man who had never once in sixty years doubted that the world would still be arranged correctly by noon.

I should explain something about Master Vane. He was not cruel. I want that on the record, whatever record survives me. He had taken in an unregistered boy with no papers and no history and taught him a trade that could be practiced entirely in shadow, and he had never once asked where I came from, because asking was dangerous and Vane considered danger the way other men considered damp—a thing to be kept out of the walls. His kindness was architecture. It held up roofs. It did not open windows.

And now there was a window, and a child in it, and the light coming through was going to wake him whether I liked it or not.

She stirred. Sat up. Looked around the loft with the grave, total attention of a six-year-old taking inventory of a new country: the hanging coils of wire, the drawers labeled in Vane's cramped hand, the great face of the tower clock visible through the loft's round window like a moon that kept appointments.

"Is this your house?" she whispered.

"It's his," I said. "We're borrowing it."

"Does he know?"

"That," I said, as the stair below us groaned under a familiar weight, "is about to become the central question of both our lives."

* * *

Vane came up the ladder the way he did everything: precisely, quietly, and with the air of a man who already knew what he would find and resented being proven right.

He stopped at the top rung. His eyes went from the girl to the sack she sat on to me, and I watched him do the arithmetic—the summons yesterday, the observatory, the dawn return, the child—and arrive at a sum he found personally insulting.

"Hazel."

"Master."

He didn't shout. Vane shouting would have been a relief; shouting is a thing you can answer. Instead he crossed the loft, lifted the girl's chin gently with one finger, examined her face the way he'd examine a movement—balance, finish, provenance—and let go.

"Fine clothes," he said. "Good linen. Someone's daughter." He turned to me. "Someone important's daughter, or someone frightened's daughter. Both, likely. And she is standing in my workshop."

"She was left at the observatory door. There was a letter pinned to her cloak. The instructions on the envelope say—"

"I don't want to know what they say." He said it fast, and that speed told me more than any curiosity would have. Vane knew exactly what kind of letter got pinned to children. He had spent six decades making sure no such letter ever got pinned to me. "*Foundling intake* opens at the ninth bell on Wexler Street. It is a twenty-minute walk. You will deliver her there, hand her across the counter, sign nothing, and walk away without looking back."

"Master—"

"You will not look *back*, boy, that's the whole trick of it. Intake takes them, the sisters wash them, the rolls list them, and in eighteen years they're apprentices and maids and soldiers and nobody remembers a linen collar. That is mercy. Do you understand? That is the kindest machine this city runs." He was gathering his tools as he talked, not because he needed them but because his hands required employment when his voice was lying about how steady it was. "By nightfall she needs to be behind that counter, and by nightfall *you* need to have forgotten her face."

"There it is," I said.

"There what is?"

"The third rule. Carved into your own bench. I've been resting my elbows on it for eleven years, Master. I know how it goes."

Vane set down the pliers he'd been strangling. For a long moment the only sound in the loft was the tower clock overhead, ticking through its great slow thoughts, and the girl watching us the way she'd watched the gears—as if adults were mechanisms whose workings might be deduced if you stared hard enough.

"Sit down, Hazel," Vane said, and it was not an order, which frightened me more than an order would have. He lowered himself onto the stool by the bench, and suddenly he looked every year of his age and several he'd been concealing. "You think I made those rules because I'm careful. I made them because I buried people. Before you. Boys who held eyes too long at checkpoints. A woman who signed her true name on a petition because she thought paper protected things." Something crossed his face then, quick as a shutter in wind, gone before I could name it. "The city eats the visible. That is not philosophy. That is diet."

"She's evidence," I said. "The letter has a magistrate's crest on it. Corvane's crest. If she goes through intake, she goes into the rolls, and the rolls are—" I stopped, because I heard myself, because the sentence finished itself in a place I wasn't ready to look. "If whoever left her wanted her safe, Master, they wouldn't have written *forget the face* on the envelope."

Vane was quiet for a while.

"You read the envelope," he said finally. "Not the letter."

"I couldn't break the seal."

"No," he said softly. "You couldn't." He looked at the girl again, and I saw him do something I had never seen him do: hesitate. One full second of it. Then the shutters came down, and he stood, and he was Master Aldous Vane of Copperlane again, and his voice was bench-flat. "Ninth bell. Wexler Street. You'll take the coal lane so you don't pass the registry office, and you'll be home by the evening rounds. Am I understood?"

"Understood," I said.

And then I lied to him for the first time in my life.

It's worth describing, because it happened so smoothly I barely felt it—like a gear tooth engaging, all teeth cut and ready, waiting years for the wheel to turn. He was halfway down the ladder, back to me, and he said over his shoulder, "And Hazel. If anyone asks—and no one will—you took her straight from the observatory steps to intake yesterday morning. You never brought her here. There is no girl in this workshop and there never was."

"Yes," I said. "I already told them that."

His foot stopped on the third rung.

"Told who?" he said.

"The intake sister. Yesterday. When I delivered her." My heart was going like a detached balance wheel, whirring against its casing, but my voice came out level and even faintly bored, and I have thought many times since about where that boredom came from, and I believe it came from him—from eleven years of watching a man make untrue things sound inevitable. "She's across the counter. Signed nothing, as instructed. Linen collar and all. I never saw her face."

Vane climbed back up one rung. Just one. His eyes came level with the floorboards, and then he pulled himself up and stood there studying me, and I understood that I was seeing, in real time, the exact moment a teacher discovers what he has built.

"You delivered her yesterday," he repeated.

"Before I came home. I thought it was what you'd want. I didn't want a lecture about it first."

A muscle moved in his jaw. Behind him, the girl—my witness, God help me, a six-year-old witness who understood perhaps one word in ten—chose that moment to yawn enormously and ask me, in a whisper pitched for the entire city to hear, "Do I get bread here?"

"*Hush,*" I said, too sharply, and Vane's gaze flicked from her to me and back, and I watched him weigh it: the lie was clean, the story was plausible, the alternative was that his apprentice had defied him before his very eyes, and the second option meant the defiance existed, and existing was what Vane had spent a lifetime teaching me not to do.

"Bread," he said at last, heavily, "is in the crock by the stove. Downstairs." And he went down the ladder and did not look up once, and I learned later that he sat alone in the shop for two hours with an unassembled movement in front of him, fitting and refitting the same four wheels, which is what Vane did instead of praying.

* * *

We ate the bread in the loft, because I had become, in the space of one lie, a person who disobeyed in increments.

"So we're not going to the bread place," Pip—no. Not Pip. She had no name yet; naming her felt like signing something. So: *the girl.* The girl chewed thoughtfully. "The Wexler place."

"No," I said.

"The man said to take you there."

"The man," I said, "says a great many things, and most of them are true, and this one is true for everyone except us." Which was a terrible explanation, so I tried again. "There are places where they write down who you are. In books. And once you're written down, certain people can come find you in the book."

"Bad people?"

"People with crests," I said, and she went still, and I realized she knew the word. Six years old and she knew the word. Whatever house she'd come from had taught her to be quiet when that seal showed up on correspondence, the way other children are taught to be quiet when father's had bad news from the exchange.

"He's the one in the letter," she said. Not a question.

"How do you know what's in the letter?"

"I carried it a long time." She brushed crumbs from her fine linen with small precise hands, and for a moment I glimpsed the training underneath the child—someone had taught her to sit, to carry, to hush. "The lady said if I did everything right, the letter would do the rest. She cried while she pinned it. Grown-ups cry quieter than us. Did you know that? They've had practice."

I had known that. I had not known that a six-year-old had also worked it out, sitting on cold stone steps waiting for whatever came next.

"The lady," I said carefully. "Your mother?"

"My mother is dead," she said, with the flat efficiency children use for facts they've decided not to feel yet. "This was another lady." She looked at me then, directly, holding my eyes the way I had been trained since birth never to hold anyone's, and asked the question that would ruin my life in the best possible way: "Are you going to do everything right?"

Below us, the shop bell rang. A customer. Then—worse—two voices in the doorway, one of them carrying the particular clipped diction of men who introduce themselves by title rather than name, and Vane's voice rising in a register I had heard maybe three times in eleven years, each time just before something broke.

I blew out the lamp. I took her wrist. The coal chute was behind the scrap bins, and it had been my escape route since I was nine, and I had always assumed that someday I'd go down it running from inspectors.

I had never assumed I'd be glad of it.

"Everything right," I told her, as the voices below resolved into words like *missing* and *daughter* and *of quality*, "is about to mean something different. Can you climb down backwards?"

She could. Of course she could. Children born with letters pinned to them learn to move between worlds the way I'd learned to move between gears—one rhythm for hiding in, another for getting out.

And above us, muffled through the floorboards, I heard Vane say the words I would carry out of that chute and through every checkpoint to come: "Yes, inspector, my apprentice is in the loft. I'll fetch him down myself."

* * *

The coal lane smelled of soot and river, and the girl's hand in mine was very small and very warm, and the ninth bell rang out over Copperlane from a direction we were not walking toward.

"Wexler Street is that way," she observed, pointing with the unerring honesty of childhood.

"It is," I agreed.

"We're not going there."

"We are not."

She considered this for the length of a city block, swinging my arm slightly, a small person in good linen walking openly through streets designed to swallow the unlisted. Then she nodded, apparently satisfied, and asked the only question left.

"Then where are we going?"

And I realized, with the clarity of a mechanism reaching dead center—all resistance overcome, the wheel committed to its fall—that I had absolutely no idea, and that for the first time in my life, having no idea felt less like falling and more like flight.

"Somewhere with a clock tower," I said. "They hide almost anything."
