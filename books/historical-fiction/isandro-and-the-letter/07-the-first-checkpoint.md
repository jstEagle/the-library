## 7. The First Checkpoint

There is an art to being forgotten, and I had mastered it by the age of nine. You learn to walk at the pace of everyone else, to dress one shade duller than the crowd, to arrange your face into something a clerk's eye would slide across like water over glass. Master Vane calls it *the gray discipline*. I call it the only inheritance my master ever gave me that didn't come with a diagram.

The trouble with the gray discipline is that it was designed for one body. It had never been asked to accommodate a six-year-old girl who could not stop looking at things.

"Down," I murmured for the fourth time as we came up out of Tanner's Walk. "Eyes down, Pip."

"I'm counting dogs," she said.

"You can count them in your head. That's where counting lives anyway."

She gave me a look of profound pity for a man who did not understand that counting was meant to be witnessed, but she lowered her eyes. Small mercies. She was wearing the coat I'd bought from the rag stall on Cooper's Lane—too big in the shoulders, patched at both elbows, the particular brown of clothing that has given up—and beneath it, her own fine clothes were bundled into a parcel I carried under my arm like so much laundry. The parcel was the most dangerous thing about us. Silk embroidered with someone else's initials reads as evidence from forty paces. I had considered dropping it in the river. I had stood at the rail above the black water for a full minute, weighing it in my hands, and then I hadn't. I couldn't have told you why, then. The girl's whole history fit inside a bundle the size of a loaf, and I found I would sooner throw myself after it.

Her hair was the other problem. It was the color of new pennies and it caught light the way nothing gray ever has. I'd rubbed soot into it that morning until she looked like a chimney-sweep's ghost, and she had borne it with the grim patience of someone being fitted for armor, which in a sense she was.

"Niece," I said. We had rehearsed this on the stairs of the clock tower until the words lost their shape. "Say it."

"Niece," said Pip.

"And what are you called?"

"Elsie Sterling." She scrunched her nose. "Elsie is a wet name."

"It's a forgettable name. That's the entire point of it."

"My name isn't forgettable."

"No," I said, before I could stop myself. "It isn't."

And there it was—the first crack in my own instruction. Vane's rules ran through my skull in his dry voice: *no names spoken aloud, no eyes held too long, nothing carried that cannot be dropped.* He had never once needed to add *do not love the cargo*, because no apprentice before me had been fool enough to require the amendment.

* * *

The river checkpoint straddled Old Iron Bridge like a growth on a tree, all timber and iron and official varnish, built out over the water on stilts so the clerks could work with the river running beneath them, carrying away whatever the city preferred not to keep. Two lanes funneled toward the ledger house: the broad one for carts and gentry, the narrow one for foot traffic and the poor, which is to say for people whose faces were already half-inventoried.

I joined the narrow lane at mid-morning, when the queues are longest, because crowds are camouflage. A queue is the grayest place in the world. In a queue you are nobody together with two hundred somebodies, and even the clerks grow numb to the procession of faces.

Pip held my hand. Her palm was damp and small and it was doing more damage to my composure than any checkpoint ever could.

"What happens in there?" she asked.

"They look at your face. They look at a book. If they match, you go through."

"What if they don't match?"

I bought time by adjusting the tool roll slung across my back. "Then they ask questions."

"What if the answers don't match either?"

"Pip."

"Elsie," she corrected.

"Elsie. Some questions you answer by standing still and letting me do the talking."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate reviewing a case. "All right," she said. "But you're bad at lying. Your ears went red when you told the bread woman we wanted the stale loaves for charity."

My ears, I am told by no one, since I have no confidants, went red again on the spot.

* * *

Inside, the ledger house smelled of ink and wet stone and the faint vinegar of too many bodies breathing in too small a room. There were three desks behind a rail. At the first, a heavy man with a waxed mustache stamped papers without appearing to read them, a machine of magnificent indifference. At the second, a young clerk with a pimpled forehead flipped between a passport and the great face-ledger, comparing, frowning, waving a woman through with the reluctance of a boy abandoning a puzzle. And at the third desk—

At the third desk sat a woman with dark hair pinned back so tightly it seemed a rebuke to disorder itself, and she was doing something I had never seen a registry clerk do.

She was actually looking.

Not glancing. Not cataloguing. Looking, the way an astronomer looks—not the way a jailer does. Her pen hovered over the ledger and her eyes moved across the queue with a terrible attentiveness, and every instinct I owned stood up at once and screamed at me to leave by the door I had entered, consequences be damned.

But leaving is its own confession. The gray discipline says: when the hawk circles, become the field. So I became the field. I shuffled forward with the shuffling mass, kept my chin at the angle of a man mildly bored by bureaucracy, and steered Pip to my left side, where the rail's shadow fell across her face.

"Next," barked the mustache desk, to someone three places behind me. The line lurched.

Here is what nobody tells you about checkpoints: they do not test whether you are guilty. They test whether you can endure being treated as though you might be, without your body confessing it for you. Hearts are traitors. Hands are worse. Somewhere beneath my sternum a clock was running fast, and I am a professional judge of clocks.

"Next."

A fishwife ahead of us, then gone, her face matched to her sketch in four seconds flat. Four seconds. I counted. I always count.

"Next."

I stepped up and set our papers on the counter—my registration card, soft-cornered from years of handling, and the work order for the observatory clock, which was genuine and therefore my best weapon. Genuine paper carries a kind of innocence that forgives everything around it. Beside mine I laid the child's letter of accompaniment, forged the night before on Vane's own press with Vane's own seal, in an hour of work so careful I had used up three practice sheets. My master believed I was destroying evidence. My master believes many soothing things.

"Purpose of crossing?" the mustached clerk began, and then, seeing the observatory letterhead, softened by a degree. "Clockmaker, eh?"

"Apprentice. The master clock at the observatory's been losing four seconds a day. They want it sorted before the equinox observations." True, true, and true. Truth is a rhythm, and I can keep time.

"Mm." He took my card, turned it toward the light. "And the girl?"

"My niece. My sister's taken ill; she stays with us while—" I gestured vaguely upstream, the gesture of a man whose domestic arrangements are tedious and unremarkable.

He grunted and reached for the face-ledger, volume nine, the children's rolls, and here my heart performed its treachery, because I knew—I had spent the night knowing—that there was no Elsie Sterling in any volume, that her sketch existed nowhere except in the memory of whoever had left her on those steps with orders to forget her, and that everything now depended on a tired clerk deciding that checking the children's rolls properly was more effort than it was worth.

The mustached clerk licked his thumb. Turned pages. Ran a finger down columns of sketches, each one a small gray portrait of a small registered life.

Somewhere behind me, a cart driver swore at the delay. Somewhere below the floorboards, the river went about its patient work of carrying things away.

"Looks close enough to the mother, I suppose," he muttered—to himself, to the room, to no one—and stamped the accompaniment letter, and pushed the whole sheaf across the counter with the brisk finality of a man concluding that paperwork, like weather, is best endured quickly. Then he turned to the next person in the queue and the world resumed its ordinary speed, and Pip and I had crossed, officially, existentially, and I felt the specific vertigo of having gotten away with it.

That was when the third desk spoke.

"A moment."

* * *

Her voice was quiet enough that the room did not obey it, so she said it again, to my back: "The clockmaker. A moment, please."

I turned. The gray discipline says turn slowly, turn stupidly, wear the face of a man mildly inconvenienced. I turned. She was holding my registration card, which the first clerk had evidently passed along the rail without ceremony, and she was holding it in such a way that suggested she had been holding it for some time.

Behind the rail, the mustached clerk rolled his eyes at her back. Clerks, I would later learn, regard diligence the way soldiers regard a man who polishes his boots unprompted.

"You're listed to the workshop of Aldous Vane, Gilt Row," she said.

"Fourteen years," I said. "Since I was eleven."

"Mm." Her eyes came up from the card to my face, and I want to describe what that felt like, because I have thought about it nearly every day since, and I still lack the vocabulary. Being looked *at* is common. Being looked *into* is rare and catastrophic. Her eyes were gray-brown, unglamorous, exact, and they went through my carefully arranged face the way a probe goes through a watch case—finding the mechanism, reading the escapement, noting the repair marks. I felt inventoried. I felt, absurdly, *known*, and the sensation was so foreign that for one naked moment I could not remember what I was supposed to be hiding.

"Your number," she said. "Seven-four-one-nine. You'd know it by heart."

"One-one-seven-four-one-nine," I recited. "Registered to Vane, Aldous, Gilt Row, as apprentice. Renewed annually. Last renewal, March."

Something flickered across her face—not suspicion. Interest, which is far more dangerous. "You didn't hesitate."

"Should I?"

"Most people do." She glanced at the card again, and here she hesitated herself—a hesitation so slight that only a man trained to read the tremors of mechanisms would have caught it. Her thumb rested at the corner of the card and did not move for the length of two full ticks of the wall clock above the ledger shelves. I know, because I counted. Three thousand hours at a workbench buys you that.

"The observatory's closed this week," she said. "The astronomers were dismissed until the equinox preparations."

"That's why they're having the clock serviced now," I said. "Nobody underfoot."

"Yes," she said. "Convenient."

The word hung between us like a suspended pendulum, dead level, trembling.

And then—because she was a clerk, because the queue was long, because the mustached man behind her said, loudly, "Wren, for God's sake, send them through"—she stamped my card. The sound was very small and very final. She slid the papers back across the counter, all but the registration card, which she held one extra instant before releasing it, and in that instant her fingers adjusted their grip so that the card arrived in my hand brushed, feather-light, against my palm—an accident, surely, except that her eyes lifted to mine at precisely that moment and did not look away, and neither, God help me, did mine.

No eyes held too long. The oldest rule. I broke it across a checkpoint counter in front of the state's own machinery, and the breaking felt less like error than like surfacing.

"Go on, then," she said quietly. "Mind the clock keeps better time than your story."

I gathered Pip's hand and walked toward the far doors at the pace of an innocent man, which required everything I had, because every nerve in my legs was voting to run. Behind us the queue shuffled on. The wall clock ticked. The river carried on beneath the floor, taking the city's unwanted somewhere downstream.

We came out into the daylight on the bridge's far span and Pip said, "Your ears are red again."

"Counting dogs," I said. "Start counting."

* * *

It took four streets before my hands started shaking, which is typical; fear always presents its bill after the transaction clears. I steered us into the churchyard of St. Ambrose-in-the-Wall, where the beggars know me and ask nothing, and sat down on a tomb with our parcel of silk between my knees, and let the shakes run their course while Pip investigated gravestone carvings with the soot-stained intensity of a scholar.

She was safe. We had crossed. The letter rode inside my shirt against my breastbone, warm as a second heartbeat, and somewhere behind us a clerk named Wren—she had said it only in irritation, but I had caught it the way I catch the tick of a faulty train—was returning to her ledgers with my number written somewhere in her mind.

That was the part I could not shake, sitting among the tombs while the bells rang eleven. Not the stamp, not the near-discovery. The looking. Fourteen years of the gray discipline, and I had never once been truly *seen*, and now I understood, in the marrow-deep way one understands things too late, that being invisible had never actually protected me. It had merely ensured that no one would notice when I was taken. The ledgers did not need to find me interesting to destroy me. They only needed to find me.

And yet some traitorous chamber of my heart kept replaying a thumb resting on the corner of a card, two ticks of stillness, a voice saying *a moment, please* as though my existence were worth a moment—as though I were worth the pause.

Vane would have called it a catastrophic exposure of nerve. He would have been right. I had walked through the machinery of the registry and come out the other side, and the machinery had noticed me—not as a violation, not yet, but as a question. *You didn't hesitate,* she had said, and I had answered her honestly, and honesty, in my trade, is the one component that cannot be replaced once it's been installed.

"Are we going to see the sick mother now?" Pip asked, arriving at my tomb with the solemnity of her assumed biography.

"There is no sick mother."

"I know." She climbed up beside me and regarded the parcel of silk. "I'm asking if we're going to pretend there is."

I looked at this child—six years old, soot in her copper hair, already fluent in the grammar of pretending—and made a decision that would have appalled my master, which is perhaps the surest sign it was correct.

"We're going home," I said. "And then we're going to hide you somewhere the whole city will walk past every day and never think to look."

"Where?"

I nodded eastward, toward Gilt Row, toward the tower whose clock I had wound every Sunday for eleven years, whose gears knew my hands better than any living soul did.

"Above everyone," I said.

Pip considered the skyline with narrowed eyes, professional interest kindling. "Does it have a bell?"

"Twelve feet across and loud enough to loosen teeth."

She grinned, and the grin was entirely, dangerously her own, and I understood that I had already failed at forgetting her face, and that I was going to fail completely, gloriously, at everything Vane had ever taught me.

What I did not know—could not know, among the tombs of St. Ambrose with a child counting dogs against my shoulder—was that across the river, in the ledger house, a clerk was pulling an archive slip bearing the number one-one-seven-four-one-nine, and that the register would tell her the number belonged to a man who had been dead for eleven years.
