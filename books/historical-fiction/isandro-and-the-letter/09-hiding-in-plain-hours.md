## 9. Hiding in Plain Hours

There is a particular silence that lives inside a clock tower, and it is not silence at all. It is the sound of a hundred small decisions being made every second — the escapement ticking its verdicts, the gears arguing in their brass language, the pendulum swinging like a metronome for a piece of music nobody wrote. I had spent six years learning to disappear inside that sound. I had never once tried to teach it to anybody.

"You're going to have to be quieter than that," I said. "Even your breathing has opinions."

Pip looked up at me from the tower floor, where she sat cross-legged among the oil rags like a duchess holding court in exile. She was six years old and had already mastered the art of looking unimpressed, which put her roughly forty years ahead of me.

"My breathing is *fine*," she said.

"Your breathing sounds like a bellows with grievances."

"Maybe it *has* grievances." She hugged her knees. "You won't tell me why we're hiding. You won't tell me when my—" she stopped herself, which was its own kind of heartbreak, because whatever word she'd been about to use — *mother*, *father*, someone, anyone — she had apparently decided it was contraband too. "You won't tell me anything."

She was right, and I deserved that, so I sat down on the floor beside her, which Vane would have called undignified and which I considered the single most honest thing I'd done all week.

"Here's what I can tell you," I said. "The men who left you at the observatory don't want to be remembered. And there are other men — inspectors, they're called — who are looking for a girl of quality gone missing. That's you. So while we wait for things to settle, you live up here, and you live quietly, and you learn the one skill I actually have."

"Fixing watches?"

"Hiding in time."

* * *

The tower above Vane's workshop was three floors of scaffolding, counterweights, and dust, crowned by the municipal clock that told half of Coldharbour's riverside district when to open its shops and close its arguments. It was, by any reasonable measure, no place for a child. There were open gear trains that could take a finger as toll, drop shafts that swallowed dropped tools forever, and a smell of oil and old stone that got into everything, including — eventually — your sense of what counted as home.

But it had one supreme virtue: it was loud. Loud in the way forests are loud. A patrol could climb the workshop stairs, walk every corridor, hold a lantern to every corner, and hear nothing but machinery doing what machinery does. Nobody searches inside noise. My master taught me that before he taught me to file a tooth on an escape wheel. *We do not hide in dark places, boy. We hide in predictable ones.*

So I made Pip a curriculum.

"Lesson one," I said, walking her through the tower's middle level, where the great going-train turned slow enough to watch. "The clock speaks in patterns. Four seconds, tick. Four seconds, tock. Every quarter hour, the strike train wakes up and makes its speech. If you can count, you can know exactly when the loudest moments are coming."

"I can count to a hundred."

"Show-off. Then here's what matters: when the quarter strikes, you move. The bells cover everything — footsteps, doors, a cough if you must. When the clock is quiet, you're quiet. You become furniture."

"What kind of furniture?"

"The kind nobody moves."

She practiced that first day until dusk. When the quarter struck, she crossed from the stairwell door to the shadow behind the weight channel — nine steps, nine seconds of thunder to hide them. On her fourth attempt she made it without knocking a single pulley, and when she turned around her whole face was lit with a pride so fierce and so unguarded that something in my chest performed a maneuver I had no training for.

"That was excellent," I said, and meant it more than I knew how to say.

"Again," she commanded.

* * *

I brought her food twice a day, in the hours between Vane's rounds to the tea house and his return. Bread, cheese, the small sweet apples from the costermonger on Milver Street that she liked best. I carried it up in my toolbox, under the tray where the jeweling files lived, because nobody looks twice at a workman carrying tools, and I had built my whole life on that principle.

The strange thing — the thing I am only now, writing this, able to name — is how quickly terror and tenderness learned to share a table. I would climb those stairs with my heart hammering at every footstep on the landing below, and then I'd arrive and find her sitting on an overturned crate, using a screwdriver as a pointer to conduct some lesson she'd invented for herself, naming the gears aloud: "That's the third wheel. That's the big lazy one. That's the one that goes *don't touch me*."

"She's the barrel," I'd say. "And yes. She bites."

"And this one?"

"That's the escapement. The heartbeat."

"Why's it stop and go? Why doesn't it just go?"

Because stopping is how it measures itself, I almost said. Because a thing that never pauses never knows how much time it has spent. But that was philosophy, and I was feeding a six-year-old an apple, so instead I said: "It takes little rests so it can keep going forever. Even clocks need to breathe."

"Like my breathing," she said darkly. "Which is fine."

"It's adequate," I allowed, and she threw a rag at me.

* * *

On the fifth night, the patrol came early.

I heard them from the workshop — boots with that particular official rhythm, two sets, unhurried, the walk of men being paid whether or not they find anything. Vane was at the bench pretending magnificently to be absorbed in a movement that hadn't needed him for an hour. I was upstairs in an instant, taking the steps two at a time, and found Pip standing perfectly still in the open, eyes enormous.

"Behind the barrel housing," I breathed. "*Now.*"

She went. Silent. Nine steps through dead quiet, no bell to cover her, and she did it better than I could have — flat against the iron, knees folded into the barrel's shadow, hands tucked away, face turned to the wall. Furniture. The kind nobody moves.

The boots came up the workshop stairs. A lantern's light swept the gap under the tower door. One of them said something to the other about the cold, about his wife's stew, about a bet placed on a boat race. They never opened the door. The clock ticked above us, four seconds, tick, four seconds, tock, steady as a promise, and I stood alone in the middle of the floor listening to my own pulse misbehave while six feet away a child hid so well that even I half forgot she existed.

When the boots receded, I waited a full ten minutes — long enough that doubt began its usual work — before I whispered her name.

She unfolded from the shadow like it had grown her.

"Was I good?" she asked.

"You were perfect."

"I wasn't scared," she said, in the voice of someone absolutely terrified who has decided, provisionally, not to be.

I crouched down to her height, which is a thing I recommend to everyone; the world looks different from thirty-nine inches off the ground, mostly worse, occasionally truer. "You were scared," I said. "You were scared and you did it anyway. That's better than not being scared. Remember that. Write it on the inside of your skull."

"Where you keep your rules?"

"Exactly where I keep my rules."

* * *

Vane noticed. Of course he noticed. Extra bread vanishing, apples appearing in my purchases, my absences stretching past reason. He said nothing for days — Vane's silences were load-bearing structures — until one evening he set down his tweezers and looked at me across the bench with eyes like struck flint.

"You lied to me, boy."

"Yes, Master Vane."

"About the foundling intake."

"Yes."

He was quiet a long moment. Outside, the tower struck the half hour, and I watched him count it out the way he counted everything, as if time owed him an accounting. When he spoke again his voice was lower, and older, and I have thought about it every day since.

"When I took you in, I taught you the rules because the rules keep unregistered people breathing. No names spoken aloud. No eyes held too long. Nothing owned that cannot be carried, and nothing loved that cannot be abandoned." He rubbed his thumb across the scar on his knuckle, an old burn, an old story he never told. "Those rules kept me alive forty years. They will keep you alive sixty. But I will tell you what I have never told anyone, apprentice: every man who ever followed them perfectly died exactly as invisible as he lived, and the world never once noticed he had been in it at all."

I didn't know what to say to that. I still don't, entirely. He picked his tweezers back up, bent over the bench, and dismissed me with a sound like a door closing. But he didn't ask again where the bread was going, and the next morning there was a second apple in my toolbox tray, which neither of us ever mentioned, and which I understood, in the language of that house, to be a sacrament.

* * *

The nights were the hardest and the best. After Vane retired, I climbed to the tower with a candle stub and stayed, ostensibly to work on movements I couldn't risk at the bench below. Really, I stayed because Pip talked in her sleep, small administrative mutterings — counting gears, correcting imaginary children, once, devastatingly, saying *goodnight, Hazel* with total clarity before rolling over — and because I had discovered that hearing my name spoken aloud was an addiction I had developed at age twenty-three with no intention of curing.

She asked questions, in the waking hours. Endless questions, most of them about the clock, some of them catastrophic in their simplicity.

"Why haven't you got a paper?"

"Paper costs truth," I said. "And I've never had any to spare."

"Have you got a true name, though? Not for the papers. Just for you?"

"Hazel," I said. "Just Hazel."

"That's a *plant*," she said, scandalized. "That's not a proper name at all."

"It's the one I've got."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Pip's not a proper name either," she admitted. "I gave it to myself. For the seeds. Little things, before they're anything."

Little things, before they're anything. I have heard sermons less true.

"Well then," I said, and my voice did something embarrassing near the end of the sentence, "we match."

* * *

On the ninth night, I fell asleep in the tower. I woke at dawn to find my coat draped over me — she had covered me with it, clumsily, one sleeve hanging to the floor — and Pip sitting beside me, watching the light come gray through the clock face's translucent panels, turning the whole upper chamber the color of pearl.

"I worked out the secret," she announced.

"Of the clock?"

"Of you." She held up fingers. "You fix time for everyone else. And you never keep any for yourself. That's why your hands shook at breakfast yesterday. You were running out."

Six years old. I looked at her and felt the full weight of the future arrive in the room, wearing a child's coat and my own coat's sleeve.

"Pip," I said carefully, "if something happens to me—"

"Nothing's happening."

"—if something happens, there's a man named Oskar who owes my master a debt. Do you remember the name Oskar?"

"Oskar," she repeated solemnly. "Owes a debt."

"Say it every morning."

"Every morning," she agreed, and then, because she was six and merciless: "Why? What's happening?"

Before I could construct a lie adequate to the occasion, the answer arrived on its own — boots on the workshop stairs below, heavy, official, two sets, and these did not have the unhurried rhythm of bored patrolmen. These climbed like men with a warrant.

Through the tower door's gap I saw the lantern-light swing across the workshop floor, and heard a voice I did not know say, clear and cold:

"Registry office. We're asking after a missing child of quality."
