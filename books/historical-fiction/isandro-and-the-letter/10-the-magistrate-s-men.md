## 10. The Magistrate's Men

The knock came at eleven minutes past nine in the morning, which was wrong on two counts.

First, no one knocked on Vane's workshop door. People scratched, or tapped, or in the case of the milk boy performed a small percussion concert that could wake the dead and annoy them thoroughly. But a firm, patient, official-sounding knock—the kind that waits for permission rather than demanding entry—that belonged to another world entirely. The world of men whose papers preceded them like a herald.

Second, it came at all. In nineteen years of apprenticeship I had learned that trouble arrives either too early or too late, never at a civilized hour, because civilization is precisely what it has come to dismantle.

I was in the clock tower loft with Pip, her small hands wrapped around a broken alarm clock I'd given her to dissect. She had developed a technique of destruction so methodical it bordered on reverence—every screw saved, every spring laid out in rows—which made me suspect she'd been born into better tables than the one she'd been left on. We both froze when the knock sounded. Her eyes went wide as coins.

"Is it the gear man?" she whispered. She called Oskar the gear man, having met him exactly once and formed opinions accordingly.

"No," I said. "The gear man doesn't knock."

I lifted the floorboard, eased her down among the great slow teeth of the tower's motion work, and pressed her doll—a lumpy thing sewn from a chamois polishing cloth—into her arms. "Remember what I taught you?"

"When the big wheel sighs, walk. When it clicks, freeze."

"And if anyone comes up the stairs?"

"I'm not here." She said it with the flat certainty of a child reciting scripture, and then, because she was six and not a saint, added: "Neither are you."

* * *

By the time I descended and wiped the tower grease from my hands, Vane had already opened the door, and I understood immediately why he hadn't answered it quickly: he'd needed the time to arrange his face.

There were two of them. Inspectors, by their coats—registry grey with black collars, the uniform designed by someone who believed authority should look like an overcast day. The taller one held a leather folio against his chest the way a priest holds a missal. The shorter one held nothing at all, which frightened me more. Men who carry files want something recorded. Men who carry nothing want something else.

"Master Aldous Vane," the tall one read aloud, though he wasn't asking. "Clockmaker. Registered 1861. Sole proprietor."

"You have the advantage of me," said Vane, which was a lie dressed as courtesy, since they plainly had every advantage of him.

"Magistrate Corvane sends his regards." The short one smiled. It did not improve anything. "A child has gone missing from the Ashworth household. A girl, six years of age, last seen on the observatory steps some days past. Fine clothes. Dark hair. Answers to no name anyone will confirm." He let that sit in the room like a smell. "You take repair commissions from the observatory."

"The master clock," Vane said. "A routine escapement fault. My apprentice handled it."

And there it was. My name hadn't been spoken yet, but I felt it coming the way you feel a gear bind before it strips—not a sound yet, just a wrongness in the mechanism.

"Your apprentice," the tall one repeated, and opened his folio. "Hazel Sterling. Registered 1882 under the guardianship amendment." He looked up, and for a moment his gaze passed over me where I stood at the bench, deliberately bent over a movement, deliberately mediocre. "That registration number, Master Vane. You verified it at the time of the indenture?"

Every hair on my arms stood up. Because that question had only one honest answer, and Vane had never once verified anything in his life except the torque specifications of fusee chains.

"He came with papers," Vane said.

"Mm," said the tall one, in the tone of a man filing something away in a drawer that locks.

The short one drifted. That was the word for it—he drifted around the workshop like smoke with opinions, trailing a finger along the bench, pausing at the coal stove, glancing at the stairs. At the stairs. "Warm in here for a shop with one man working," he observed.

"My apprentice keeps the fire. He feels the cold."

"Does he." The short one looked at me directly, and I gave him what Vane had drilled into me across two decades: the glance-and-away. Eyes down. Hands busy. A person is a door; be shut. But my heart was going like a tourbillon gone mad, and I thought with sudden sick clarity: *he can hear it*. Somewhere below the workbench, Pip's spare shoe sat stuffed with the letter, and upstairs a child was holding perfectly still inside a machine, and the only thing between us and the registry vans was a lie about a fire.

"The Ashworth family," the tall one said, closing the folio, "is prepared to be generous. Fifty crowns for information leading to the child's recovery. More, if the information leads to the persons who took her." He laid a printed notice on the counter. Pip's face looked up from it—rendered badly, the sketcher had caught her chin but missed her eyes entirely—and beneath it, the words MISSING. PRESUMED ABDUCTED. REWARD. And smaller, in print fine enough to seem accidental: *Persons harboring the above may be remanded under Statute 44.*

Statute 44. Harboring. The word turned my stomach over, because it was accurate.

"We are simple tradesmen," Vane said. "We see nothing, we hear nothing. If the girl passes this door, I will send word to the magistrate myself. On my registration, I swear it."

It was beautifully done. It was also, I realized as the inspectors exchanged a look, not believed for a second.

"One more matter," the tall one said pleasantly. "The observatory informs us its clock was repaired by an apprentice who was not on the access ledger. An unrecorded entry. Someone signed the work register with a number we cannot match to a living registrant." He smiled without warmth. "Numbers are funny things, Master Vane. They outlive us. They turn up. Like bodies."

"They do," Vane agreed, and his voice had gone very quiet and very old, and I heard what was underneath it before either inspector did. Not loyalty. Arithmetic. He was calculating, right there in his own doorway, what an apprentice weighed against a workshop, a license, fifty crowns, and the continued operation of his own heart.

"Hazel," he said, not turning. "Fetch these gentlemen water. And then make yourself useful at Mr. Pruitt's—take him"—a fractional pause, a blade slid in gently—"the Corliss movement he wanted priced. Both of you go together. Walk slow."

Pruitt's shop was three streets away. There was no Corliss movement. What there was, was a plan with one purpose: get me out of the building, on foot, visible, alone, at a pace that would keep me in the open until the inspectors were finished and whatever came after them was done.

He wasn't offering me up, exactly. He was simply arranging for me to be elsewhere when he did.

* * *

I fetched the water. My hands did not shake, which surprised me; I had always assumed betrayal would feel like earthquake. It felt instead like a clock stopping—not violence, just the sudden absence of a tick you'd stopped hearing years ago and only notice now by the silence it leaves.

I set the tray down. The tall one thanked me. The short one watched my face with the idle thoroughness of a man memorizing it for later, and I knew—I knew with the cold clarity Vane had spent twenty years teaching me to avoid—that my face was already in some folio, filed between a dead man's number and a missing girl's chin.

"Walk slow," Vane said again softly, and touched my shoulder. To the inspectors it would look paternal. Under his fingers, it felt like a hand testing the weight of a thing before selling it.

I went to the stairs. And then I stopped, because there in the doorway, framed by morning light, stood the truth I'd been circling for days without letting myself say it: obedience had been the bargain. I vanish, quietly, and in exchange Vane's shop stays standing and nobody asks questions and I get to belong to the machinery of a life without ever appearing in it. That was the deal. And he had just broken it, not under torture, not at gunpoint, but for fifty crowns and a chance at staying comfortable.

His whole philosophy, handed down like scripture—*be nowhere, be no one, survive*—had lasted exactly as long as it cost him nothing.

Well. I had carried a letter through three checkpoints' worth of city for a child I'd known five days. It seemed I'd been trained all my life in disappearing by a man who'd never once asked what it was for.

"Pruitt's," I said aloud, dutifully. "Walk slow. Yes, master."

I went up the outside stair to the tower instead.

* * *

Pip was where I left her, wedged in the frame of the hour wheel, doll clamped to her chest. When my head came through the floor hatch she didn't cry out or even smile. She just studied my face with those eyes the sketcher had failed to capture, and said, "Bad men downstairs."

"Very good," I whispered. "How did you know?"

"The house got quiet wrong." She unfolded herself with the boneless ease of childhood and gathered her rows of screws without being told, sweeping them into a tin. Six years old, and she already knew how a building sounds when it's hunting. Somebody had taught her that before I ever found her on those steps. The thought made something in my chest go hard and bright.

"Listen to me, Pip. We're leaving through the coal chute. Have you ever gone down a slide?"

"It's not a slide."

"It's absolutely a slide."

Below us, through three floors of joists, I heard the murmur of voices rise and fall, and then—worse than anything—the front door opening, and the tall inspector's voice saying, clearly, cheerfully, "Then we'll look forward to hearing from you, Master Vane. Do give your apprentice our regards. We'll want a word with him ourselves, once he's back from this errand."

*Once he's back.* They weren't leaving. They were waiting downstairs, or posted nearby, or—simplest and most likely—they had marked the shop and meant to return within the hour, when a boy carrying a price list would come walking back into their hands, along with whatever story Vane told them about the strange noise overhead.

The chute ran from the cellar to the alley grate behind the farrier's, a drop of perhaps twelve feet at forty-five degrees. Coal dust, iron plate, darkness. I had gone down it exactly once, at fifteen, to hide from a registry sweep, and had emerged looking like I'd lost a fight with a mine.

"Boots off," I told Pip. "Socks stay. Tuck your dress in your coat."

"Why?"

"Because today you're my nephew, and nephews are filthy."

She considered this, nodded gravely, and removed her boots. God, the trust of it. The absolute, catastrophic trust.

I took the letter from the shoe and moved it to the inner pocket of my jacket, against my ribs, where it rode like a second heartbeat. Then I lifted the cellar hatch and listened. Water boiling somewhere—Vane making tea for gentlemen he hoped would pay him. The scrape of the short inspector's boots, pacing. Counting windows, probably. Men like him always count windows.

The chute's mouth gaped behind the coal heap, breathing cold air up at us. I went first, sitting into the dark, and the iron took me the way iron does—fast, loud, merciless—and spat me out into the alley in a cloud of black grit that I would be wearing for a week. I looked like every unwashed ghost this city had ever tried to forget. For the first time in my life, that was inconvenient.

"Pip. Come."

She came down shrieking with delight.

I hauled her out of the grate, clamped a grimy hand gently over the laughter, and pressed us both against the wall while the alley decided whether it had heard us. Farrier's hammer rang twice. A cart rattled past the mouth of the lane. Nothing else.

"There's a game now," I breathed into her ear. "The most important game. We are invisible. Can you do invisible?"

"I'm already doing it," she whispered back, offended I'd asked.

So we went. Through the gap behind the farrier's coal shed, across the yard where the tanner hangs his failures, down the passage the maps forgot, me moving on nineteen years of habit and her matching me step for step like she'd been born in the margins too. Behind us the workshop stood with its door politely closed, its master composing his fifty crowns' worth of regret, and its chimney smoking over an empty room where a fire had supposedly been kept by a boy who no longer existed anywhere at all.

Except that was the thing, lying in the tannin reek of the tanner's yard with a child's hand in mine and a magistrate's seal against my ribs: I had spent my whole life perfecting the art of being nowhere. And it had taken one knock at a door to teach me the difference between hiding and being hidden—one cost everything, and the other, I now understood, was about to cost more.

Because the letter had to reach the printing quarter by tomorrow. The printing quarter lay beyond the river gate. And somewhere between here and there, a checkpoint clerk named Wren was reading ledgers, and two grey-coated men were writing down my face, and Aldous Vane—the only father the state had ever permitted me—was pouring tea for the people hunting us.

"Cold?" I asked Pip, because it was the only safe thing to say.

She shook her head. Then, with the terrible economy of children: "We're not going back, are we."

"No," I said. "We're going forward. All the way forward."

Where forward began—at a smuggler's debt, a clerk's conscience, a gate that would soon be sealed—was a problem for the next hour. I had learned, at last, to stop counting on more than that.
