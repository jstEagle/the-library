## 12. The Second Checkpoint

There is a particular smell to a city that is hunting you, and it is not blood or smoke as the pamphlets would have it. It is bread. Ordinary bread, sold by ordinary bakers to ordinary registered people who have eaten breakfast every morning of their documented lives without once considering that breakfast is a thing you must be listed to enjoy.

Pip smelled it too. I knew because her stomach made a sound like a small bellows failing, and she looked up at me with the grave dignity of a duchess pretending she had not.

"When we're across," I said, "we'll find the printing quarter, and in the printing quarter there's always a woman selling buns off a tray, and clerks on their breaks buy them by the dozen."

"Do clerks like buns?"

"Clerks worship buns. It's the one honest thing about them."

She considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, which was more than the subject deserved and exactly what my conscience required. Six years old, three days on the run, and she had learned to walk when I walked, stop when I stopped, and never—not ever—say my name aloud. She called me *Uncle* in the flat, obedient tone of a child reciting scripture, and every time she said it I felt the lie settle into me like fine grit into a gear: invisible at first, then wearing.

The gate checkpoint stood where the Corrow Bridge met the printing quarter, and it was worse than the river crossing had been. The first checkpoint had catalogued faces; this one catalogued relationships. That was the cruelty of the second gate, and its genius. You could forge papers. You could not easily forge a child's resemblance to your own chin.

A line had formed already, though the morning was barely grey. Carters with manifests. A widow with a birdcage covered in cloth—I noted that they let the bird through without inspection, which told you everything about the registry's priorities. Birds could be counted. It was children who might turn out to be anybody.

"Uncle," Pip whispered, "there's bars."

"There are bars," I agreed. At the side of the checkpoint, past the clerk desks with their ledger stands and inkwells chained down like church silver, stood a low annex with a barred window and a door that opened inward. The sign above it read: **SEPARATE INSPECTION — MINORS**. Children went in alone. Adults waited outside. What happened inside was performed quickly and called procedure.

I had crossed checkpoints my whole life by being no one worth stopping. But the second gate did not ask whether you were someone. It asked whether you were someone's—and Pip, in her fine clothes gone ragged at the hems, with her too-precise vowels and her face that certain people in this city had paid certain other people to forget, was evidence wearing boots. If they took her behind that door and matched her against the missing-child circulars that Magistrate Corvane's office had surely distributed by now, no amount of my invisibility would matter. They would not arrest me. They would simply erase her, the way forty-one children before her had been erased, and the machine would close over the place where she had been, smooth as water.

"Uncle. Your hands are doing the thing."

I looked down. My hands were doing the thing. I pressed them flat against my thighs.

"New rule," I said. "Today you don't talk at all. Not a word. Can you be silent for me?"

"I can be silent better than you can," she said, which was true, and then she demonstrated it, which was showing off.

We joined the line.

* * *

Waiting is its own craft. Vane had taught me that much, though he'd have denied teaching me anything but clockwork. *A man who fidgets announces himself,* he used to say over the bench, tweezers steady, eyes down. *Stillness is a suit of clothes, boy. Wear it well and nobody looks beneath.* For eleven years I had worn stillness like skin. But there is a difference between stillness chosen and stillness performed under threat, and the second kind sweats.

Three desks ahead of us, a family was coming apart politely. A mother, two boys. The clerk—grey-waistcoated, pen moving with the horrible fluency of long practice—was comparing the younger boy's face to his ledger sketch, then to the mother's papers, then back to the boy, with the air of a man checking a sum that refuses to come out even.

"Resemblance is within tolerance," he announced finally, and stamped something, and the mother's shoulders dropped half an inch, and I understood that I was watching a family survive by a margin thinner than a hairspring. Within tolerance. That was the phrase for whether you got to keep your own child. Within tolerance.

Pip's hand found mine. Small, warm, gripping harder than was comfortable, which was the point of it.

"Next."

We stepped up. The clerk was younger than the grey-waistcoat, with the raw pink complexion of a boy promoted past his judgment, and he looked at Pip first—the way they were trained to, children before adults, smallest risks first—and then at my papers, which Wren had hesitated over at the river crossing, and which had carried me through eleven years of inspections by being perfectly, impossibly dull.

"Name of minor?"

Here was the trap laid flat and plain. Any name I gave, he would write down, and written things in this city became permanent the way fossils do. Any relationship I claimed, he would test against our faces, and Pip had my colouring in nothing and my habits in everything, which no clerk's eye should catch but some clerk's eye eventually would.

"She's—" I began.

"Out of the way, out of the way, some of us work for a living."

The voice arrived before the man did, and the man arrived like weather—a broad shape in a carter's coat pushing past the widow and her birdcage, one heavy arm already swinging around Pip's shoulders with the unearned confidence of a barrel rolling into place. He was maybe fifty, grey-stubbled, with a broken nose that had been broken long enough ago to have become character rather than injury, and he smelled of tar, wet rope, and the particular staleness of men who sleep in shifts.

"My daughter," he said to the clerk, cheerful as Sunday. "Takes after her mother, thank God. This is my apprentice—" a meaty thumb jerked at me— "well, he says he's my apprentice. I say he's a bad habit I can't break."

The young clerk blinked. "Your papers don't list a daughter."

"My papers don't list a *lot* of things." The carter produced a folded document from inside his coat with the smoothness of a card sharp, and did not quite hand it over—held it, tilted, let the seal catch the light. "Cargo manifest. Grain, mostly. Some of it legally grain." He leaned in conspiratorially, and his voice dropped into a register meant to sound like intimacy but functioning as threat. "Now, you can open all of it, son, and I'll stand here pleasant as you like while your inspector counts sacks till supper. Or you can see the manifest's in order, stamp it, and get on to the widow with the canary before she starts weeping. Your choice. I've got all day. You've got a queue."

Behind him the queue had grown to a dozen bodies, and queues in this city had opinions. The grey-waistcoated clerk at the next desk glanced over, weighed his morning against my life, and returned to his sums.

The young clerk looked at the manifest. Looked at Pip. And here was the terrible arithmetic of it: I watched him decide, in real time, that she was not worth the trouble—that a girl of six with a smudged cheek and a duchess's posture was within tolerance, whatever tolerance meant when the men writing the definition had been paid to make it mean nothing.

He stamped the manifest. "Move along."

"Always do," said the carter, and steered us both through the gate with his arm still around Pip, walking us past the barred annex without slowing, past the second barrier, out onto the printing-quarter side where the cobblestones changed colour and the air smelled of ink and horse and freedom that was probably temporary but was, for one whole minute, actual.

Then he let go of us both, turned down an alley, and expected us to follow, which we did, because when a man has just committed three kinds of fraud for you out of nowhere, you follow him, and also because Pip had hold of his coat hem now with the unshakable grip of a child who has decided something, and arguing with her decisions was a battle I'd lost every time I'd fought it.

At the mouth of the alley he stopped, turned, and looked at me properly for the first time. His eyes were pale river-grey and missed nothing, which frightened me more than the broken nose had.

"You're Vane's boy," he said. "Don't argue. You've got his hands. Man spends forty years bending over a bench, it prints on everyone he keeps." He spat, professionally, to the side. "Name's Oskar. And before you ask how I know your master—your master owes a debt older than you are, and today somebody collected it on his behalf, which means either Vane sent word ahead, which he didn't, or the world has a sense of humour, which it doesn't. So it's the debt. Debts find their own way home."

"I don't understand," I said, which was the truth, and rarer from me than lying.

"Don't have to. Yet." He crouched to Pip's level, which surprised me; men like Oskar, in my limited experience, crouched only for coin. "What's your name, sprout?"

She looked at me. I nodded, once.

"Pip," she said. "But I'm your daughter now, apparently. You eat a lot of grain."

Oskar laughed—a short, startled bark, as if the sound had escaped custody. "God help me. She's yours, clockmaker. I want that understood. Whatever she is, whoever she was—" and here his eyes flicked to me, sharp, knowing more than he should, "—she's yours now. I just carry things. It's what I'm for."

"Why?" I asked. "Why help us? You don't know us. You walked up to a checkpoint and risked the noose on two strangers, and I've been invisible long enough to know nobody does that for free."

Oskar straightened, and for a moment the carter's cheer drained out of his face and left something older underneath, something tired.

"Because twenty-two years ago there was a woman," he said, "who pulled me out of the Corrow with a boat hook when every registered soul in this city walked past me drowning, and that woman loved your master with the whole ridiculous catastrophic size of her heart, and your master was too busy being careful to love her back in time. And before she died she made me promise to look out for anyone Vane kept hidden. Which at the time meant nobody. Turns out promises are like clocks." He shrugged the great shrug of a man declining to feel things before noon. "They go off when they go off."

He started down the alley again, then paused.

"Understand one thing, though, boy. I've done you a good turn, and I'll do you another if it comes to it. But every friend you've got in this city is a door somebody can kick in. Every ally is a risk—you take on my enemies when you take on my help, and I've got a magistracy breathing on my neck same as you, plus a customs man named Ferro who owes me money and hates it. So." He looked from me to Pip and back. "You want to keep her safe, safe isn't hiding anymore. Hiding's finished. Corvane's men are pulling apart workshops on the north bank since dawn. They'll reach Vane's by tomorrow, maybe tonight. You need routes under the checkpoints, papers that breathe, and a place to stand while the letter does its work—if you've still got the letter."

I said nothing. Pip, magnificently, said nothing too, though her expression suggested she was storing every word for later examination.

"Hm," said Oskar, reading us both like a bad manifest. "That quiet means yes. Good. Then listen close, because the printing quarter eats strangers, and I know a safehouse where the soup is terrible and the loyalty is worse to betray than death." He grimaced. "Also the soup is genuinely terrible. Fair warning."

Pip took my hand with her right and, after visible deliberation, the hem of Oskar's coat with her left, and we went down the alley together toward the smell of ink and terrible soup—three people bound by nothing but a dead woman's promise, a smuggler's whim, and a letter sewn into my coat lining that would, before the week was out, either save forty-one forgotten children or bury us all in the attempt.

And trailing behind us, unhurried, at a distance no honest man would keep—

a figure in a registrar's grey coat, taking notes.
