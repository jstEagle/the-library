## 13. Ink and Ash

There are three kinds of trouble in this city, and I have made a modest living off the first two.

The first kind is the trouble that finds you — a patrol at the wrong corner, a debt called in by the wrong man. You can run from that kind, or pay it, or bury it under the cobbles with the rest of your mistakes. The second kind is the trouble you go looking for, which is the only honest kind, because at least you chose it and know what it costs going in.

The third kind walks into your safehouse at two in the morning carrying a sleeping child and wearing the face of a man who has been dead for eleven years.

That's the kind that ruins you.

* * *

I want it on record that I did not invite them in. Vane's boy let himself in through the coal chute of my cellar on Fetter Lane, which I keep for exactly this sort of occasion and resent being right about. He came down the ladder with the girl strapped to his chest like a parcel of contraband silk, and when his boots hit my floor he stood there dripping coal dust and said, "Oskar. Vane sent me."

"Vane," I said, "sent you nothing. Vane wouldn't send so much as a complaint to the registry office if it meant putting his name on paper." I lit the lamp low, the way you do when you don't want your visitors' faces remembered by anyone passing the window. "You came because there was nowhere else."

He had the decency not to argue. That was the first thing I noticed about him — the boy doesn't lie well, but he knows when a truth will do the work of one. The second thing I noticed was the child. She was asleep against his shoulder with her mouth open and her fists curled, and she had the particular boneless weight of a child who has been carried through a city that would kill her if it knew she existed, and who trusts the person carrying her completely.

I have moved a great many things through this city. Silk, brandy, papers, people. Nothing weighs what a trusting child weighs.

"Sit down before you fall down," I told him, "and tell me why I shouldn't open that chute again and pretend tonight was a draft."

* * *

Here is what you need to know about Aldous Vane, since the boy clearly won't tell you himself: he is the most frightened man I have ever known, and the most reliable, and those are the same fact seen from different windows. We were young together, back when the checkpoints were wooden barriers instead of iron ones and a man could still be unregistered out of philosophy rather than desperation. Vane was never a philosopher. He was a coward with a craftsman's hands, and he built himself a life small enough to hide inside, and he kept it hidden for forty years, and I respected him for it the way you respect a wall that holds.

And then there was Maren.

Maren Solde. If the name means anything to you, you are either very old, very well-read in the wrong newspapers, or a Corvane man, in which case stop reading and go report to someone. She ran letters for the unregistered — actual letters, ink on paper, the old-fashioned sedition — and she did it with a smile that should have been illegal even in a city with no laws left worth breaking. Vane loved her the way he did everything: quietly, thoroughly, and without once saying so out loud where anyone could hear. I loved her the way I do everything: loudly, badly, and with an eye toward the exits.

She chose him. Of course she chose him. Women like Maren choose men who will stay put, and I was never going to stay put anywhere except a cell.

The last thing she asked of me was not about Vane at all. It was a night in the autumn of '85, and she was packing her satchel for a route she didn't come back from, and she said, "Oskar. If it ever comes down to coin or conscience, take conscience. Coin spends. Conscience compounds."

I laughed at her. I said something clever — I always said something clever; it's how cowards like me survive — and she kissed my cheek and went out into the rain and eleven days later they pulled the river for her body and found nothing, which was its own answer.

So understand my position. In my cellar sat a dead man's apprentice holding a living child worth more to Magistrate Corvane than everything I've smuggled in twenty years combined. The reward notices hadn't gone up yet — they never do until the third day; Corvane likes to give the family time to reconsider publicity — but they would. A girl of quality, missing. The purse would be fat enough to buy me out of this business entirely, retire me to somewhere with a coastline and no ledgers.

And across the room, the boy was watching me weigh it. He knew exactly what I was doing. He'd been doing arithmetic of the same kind his whole life, I expect — the sums you run when you are worth nothing on paper and you need to know what you're worth to the person in front of you.

"I knew a woman once," I said, because apparently I am constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone, "who told me conscience compounds."

"Did you believe her?"

"No. I'm a smuggler. I believe in weight, distance, and the price of silence." I poured three fingers of the good brandy — the bottle I save for clients I intend to cheat — and drank it myself. "But I owed her a debt, and your master owes her a bigger one, and between the two of us we're going to pay it whether we like it or not. So. Tell me where you're taking her, and don't say the foundling intake, because I've seen your face when you look at her and foundling intakes are for children you mean to forget."

The boy looked at the floor. Then at the girl. Then at me, and held it — held my eyes, which no unregistered man does, ever, because holding eyes is how faces get remembered — and said, "The printing quarter. There's a letter. It needs to reach people who'll print it."

Well. There it was. The third kind of trouble, opening its account.

* * *

We spent the next day below ground, which is where I do my best teaching, because the city above is a liar and the city below at least lies consistently.

Underneath the checkpoints — all three of them, river, gate, and the sealed one Corvane was already building his little iron nest around — runs the drainage system the founders laid down two hundred years ago when they still believed cities were machines for keeping people alive rather than machines for counting them. The main channels are watched now, of course. But water goes where water must, and where water must, there are maintenance ways, and where there are maintenance ways, there are men paid too little to check them properly.

"The trick isn't avoiding the clerks," I told Hazel, walking him through the dark with a shuttered lamp, the girl riding my shoulders because her legs were short and my cynicism, that day, had a soft patch in it. "Anyone can avoid a clerk. The trick is understanding what a clerk actually does. They don't see faces. Faces are too much information. They see *discrepancies*. A man whose boots are too good for his coat. A woman walking too fast for a stroll. A child who looks at a checkpoint clerk the way children look at dogs." I felt Pip shift on my shoulders, listening with that terrifying seriousness children have. "So you become discrepancy-free. Boring. Forgettable on purpose. You walk like a man going to collect a debt he already knows is bad."

"And beneath?"

"Beneath is for cargo, not people. People panic underground. Cargo doesn't." I stopped at the junction where the Fetter Lane channel met the old culvert, and put my lamp hand on the wet brick. "But you may need it for both, before this is done. So learn it anyway. Count your steps. Left at every third arch. Never trust a passage that smells clean — clean means someone's paying to keep it empty, and empty means someone wants it empty for a reason."

He learned fast. That was the worst part. I've taught routes to dozens of runners over the years and most of them learn the way you learn a prayer — by repetition, without comprehension. This boy learned like a clockmaker: he wanted to know what each passage *did*, how the whole mechanism fit together, where the escapement of the city was and what would happen if you held it. By evening he could draw the western half of the underworld on my table in spilled brandy, and it was more accurate than the survey map the Corporation keeps locked in a drawer and pretends doesn't exist.

Pip learned faster. She learned that when Oskar says *still*, you are stone. She learned that the man at the tobacco shop on Cordwainer Street is mine and the man at the pie stand beside it is theirs. She learned to sleep in installments, light and shallow, waking clean. Six years old. I have smuggled veterans who never learned that much.

I caught myself, on the second night, telling her a story about a rat who became alderman, doing all the voices, and had to go up the ladder and stand in the coal chute breathing cold air for ten minutes like a man recovering from a wound.

Because here is the ledger I was keeping, and I kept it honestly, because dishonest ledgers get people killed:

Item: reward for the girl, forthcoming, sizeable. Item: reward for the boy, smaller but real — Corvane's men had been asking after a clockmaker's apprentice with a limp in his left hand, which meant someone had talked, which meant Vane had talked, which surprised me less than it should have and disappointed me more. Item: cost of harboring them, rising daily. Item: probability of the whole enterprise ending with me in a Corvane cell, high, call it certain.

Against that column, one entry: *If it ever comes down to coin or conscience, take conscience.*

Conscience compounds. Damn her. Twenty years of interest, accruing in the dark under Fetter Lane, and here came the payment notice.

* * *

On the fourth night I took him up to the roof.

Not my roof — I don't own anything as legible as a roof. The roof of the chandler's next door, which I have use of, reached through a window that has been "broken" for six years and is broken precisely often enough to remain plausible. From there you can see the gate checkpoint's lanterns, and beyond them, if you know where to look, the dark bulk of the printing quarter, and beyond that the observatory hill with its dome catching whatever light the city has left to spare.

"That's where it goes?" he asked. "The letter?"

"That's where it *starts*," I said. "A letter in a printer's hands is a match. What it sets fire to depends on the wind, and the wind in this city is bought and sold by the yard." I looked at him. In the dark, with the lanterns behind him, he looked older than his years and younger than his eyes. "You understand what happens after. Once it's printed, it's printed. There's no unpublishing. Corvane won't come after the printers first — he'll come after everyone the letter touched. Every name, every hand, every favor. He'll start with the child and work backward."

"I know."

"You don't. Knowing is a thing you do with your head. I'm telling you to know it with your hands, because your hands are what will shake when the inspectors come." I tapped the chimney stack between us. "I've buried friends of this enterprise, boy. Not many. But some. And every one of them knew, with their heads, exactly what they were risking."

He was quiet a while. Below us, a patrol went by on Cordwainer Street, four men and a lantern, walking like men going to collect debts they already knew were bad. Pip, who had insisted on coming up and whom I had lacked the spine to refuse, stood at the roof edge with her chin on her folded arms, watching the observatory dome the way other children watch festival barges.

"Hazel," she said suddenly. "Is that where the stars live?"

"It's where they keep the clock that follows them," he said.

"Do the stars have names written down?"

And I swear to you, the boy looked at her and did not flinch, did not glance at the street, did not perform a single one of the thousand small survivals I have spent my life perfecting. He just said, "Some do. The important ones."

I turned away and busied myself with the lamp, because a man my age has a reputation, and reputations are the only papers I carry.

* * *

That night I made my decision, and I'll tell you how I made it, since honesty is cheap at this point in the story.

I took out the slate I keep for accounts — real accounts, the smuggling kind — and I wrote the reward figure on it. Then I wrote what Maren's promise had cost me so far: four nights, two routes, one bottle of the good brandy, and the beginnings of an affection I had not authorized. Then I wrote the word *conscience* underneath, and looked at the three items, and understood that I had lost the argument before I sat down at the table, and that I had probably lost it in the autumn of '85 in the rain, kissing her cheek and saying something clever.

I wiped the slate. First time in twenty years I've wiped a ledger rather than balanced it. It felt terrible. I recommend it.

In the morning I gave Hazel the last piece: not a route but a name. "When you get to the printing quarter, you don't go to the big houses — Corvane owns their debts if not their presses. You ask at the chandler's on Quill Lane for a woman named Brannock. Tell her the rat who became alderman sends his regards. She prints things that can't be printed, and she owes me, and unlike me she has never once counted what she owes." I hesitated, hating the hesitation. "And boy — there's a clerk. River checkpoint. Dark hair, ink on her fingers, eyes that read people instead of papers. She looked at you too long, didn't she."

He went very still. Which was answer enough.

"She pulled your file," I said. "My people watch the archive door the way priests watch confessionals. A junior records clerk, Wren, requested the registration archives two days running. Whatever she found, she hasn't reported — yet. Clerks who find discrepancies and sit on them are either corrupt or converting. I've known both kinds. The corrupt ones sell you. The converting ones—" I shrugged, and felt the shrug cost me something. "The converting ones are worse. They break, and when they break, they break toward whatever they saw in you. God help you if what she saw was worth breaking for."

"What do I do?"

"Nothing. You do nothing, you go nowhere near the river district, and you let the mechanism run. Either she reports you and you're finished regardless of anything you do, or she doesn't, and—" I stopped. Because I had seen the way he'd talked about the checkpoints, the way he'd drawn the city's gears in brandy, and I recognized the look coming onto his face now, the look of a man who has just realized that a part he assumed was fixed is, in fact, moving.

"You're going to seek her out," I said. "Aren't you."

"I'm going to be findable," Hazel said, "in the printing quarter. If she comes looking."

I threw up my hands, which in my trade is roughly equivalent to resigning a commission. "Fine. Fine. But hear the last lesson, and it's free, which should tell you what it's worth: in this city, the state writes down what you are. That's all a registry is — a story the powerful tell about the powerless, in triplicate. The only thing they cannot write down—" I jabbed a finger at him, at the girl, at the whole ridiculous arrangement of my ruined retirement — "is what you refuse to surrender. Remember that when the inspectors come. Because they will come. Corvane has closed the third checkpoint and pulled the observatory apart looking for you, and when a machine that size starts hunting, it doesn't stop for weather."

Pip tugged my sleeve. "Oskar. If the bad men come, will you be still too?"

I looked down at her, this evidence, this letter with legs, this compound interest on a dead woman's faith, and I heard myself say the single least cynical sentence of my adult life:

"No, little one. When they come, I'll be loud."

* * *

They left for Quill Lane at dusk, the boy dressed as a journeyman bookbinder and the girl as his sister, both of them boring, both of them discrepancy-free, both of them walking like men collecting debts they already knew were bad. I watched them from the broken window until the patrol lanterns swallowed them, and then I went down to my cellar and did what I do best, which is prepare for the arrival of the third kind of trouble.

It arrived within the hour, though not in the shape I expected.

Boots on the chandler's stairs — light ones, quick ones, the boots of someone who had learned to walk softly in a building full of clerks. I put out the lamp and put my hand on the crowbar I keep by the chute, and through the gap under the door I saw lantern-light and a shadow, and heard a voice I did not know ask the chandler, quietly, professionally, whether a man and a girl had passed this way — and heard the chandler, bless his mercenary heart, begin to lie.

Then a second voice, lower, and my blood went cold, because I knew it. Not the voice itself — I'd never heard it speak. But I'd heard it described, twice, by watchers outside the archive door.

A woman's voice. Ink-stained fingers. Eyes that read people instead of papers.

Wren of the ledgers had left the checkpoint, and she was asking after my guests, and she was close enough that I could hear her breathing.

I picked up the crowbar, and then — thinking of a woman in the rain, thinking of compounding — I set it back down, and went to open the door.
