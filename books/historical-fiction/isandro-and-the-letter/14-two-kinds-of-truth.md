## 14. Two Kinds of Truth

There is a moment in every clerk's education when she learns that a ledger can lie.

Not the way people lie — clumsily, with sweating hands and averted eyes — but beautifully. A ledger lies in perfect copperplate, in sums that add and dates that align, in a column of forty-one names each closed out with a neat little stroke and a signature: *Corvane*. Nothing about it looks wrong. That is the horror of it. I had spent six years believing that anything which looked orderly *was* orderly, and here was six years of order arranged around an absence, like a house built over a well.

I had copied the page. That was the phrase I kept using to myself, in the voice I used for inventory: *I copied the page.* Not stole. Not took. Copied, as though the original were still safe in its drawer, as though the archive were undisturbed, as though I were still the woman who went home at six and ate bread and cheese standing up and slept like a stone.

The copy was folded inside my coat, against my ribs, where it had lived for four days. It had begun to feel less like paper than like a second heartbeat.

I should have taken it to my supervisor. I rehearsed that conversation on the ferry, on the stairs, in the queue at the bakery where the baker knows my name and gives me the burnt loaf at half price because I once corrected his ledger and saved him eleven crowns. *Sir, I've found an irregularity.* Eleven crowns. Forty-one children. The arithmetic of it made my hands go strange. You cannot report a fire by handing someone a single match and asking them to admire the craftsmanship.

So instead of the supervisor's office, I went to the printing quarter.

I want to be honest about my intentions, because honesty is what this story has cost me. My intention was arrest. I had written the words in my head the night before, neat as an entry: *I will locate the unregistered man and the child. I will take them into custody. I will surrender the page as evidence and let the institution do what institutions do.* It was a good plan. It was the kind of plan that would have been approved of by everyone I had ever worked beside, and that, I understand now, should have warned me.

* * *

The print district announces itself before you see it. The air turns to ink and hot metal, and the cobbles are black at the joints, and every third doorway has a boy in an apron sweeping paper trimmings into a pile that will never get smaller. I had been there twice in my career, both times to serve notices on presses operating without licenses, and both times the printers had looked at me the way cattle look at thunder.

Finding one man in that warren should have taken days. It took me an afternoon, because the district keeps its own ledgers too — informal ones, kept in bar tabs and favors — and because a clockmaker's apprentice, however invisible he believes himself, carries his trade in his hands. Two ink boys, a pipe seller, and a woman mending sails all gave me directions to Oskar Vennik without my ever saying a name. They thought they were sending me somewhere else. They were not good liars. Or perhaps they were exactly good enough, and wanted me found.

The safehouse was above a cooperage, up a stair that smelled of oak shavings and tallow. I climbed it with my hand on the warrant-shaped absence in my pocket — because that was the other thing, I had no warrant, no authority out here, nothing but a stolen copy and a clerk's conviction that paper is power — and I knocked.

And Hazel Sterling opened the door.

He looked worse than at the checkpoint, weeks ago. Thinner. There was a bruise gone yellow-green along his jaw like weather moving across a map, and his hands — I remembered his hands, they were the reason I had pulled his file at all, because they shook while his eyes stayed steady — his hands hung at his sides perfectly still, which somehow frightened me more than the shaking had.

Behind him, the room: a smuggler I knew by reputation, big and grey-bearded, sitting very casually near a very accessible iron bar. A pallet. A crate of candles. And a small girl sitting cross-legged on the floor amid a spread of clock parts, holding a mainspring barrel up to the light like a jeweler assessing a diamond.

Every sentence I had rehearsed evaporated. What came out instead was pure clerk:

"That girl is supposed to be at foundling intake."

Wonderful, Wren. Six years of training, and under pressure you produce *that*.

Hazel didn't run. I'll give him that. He put one hand back — not threatening, just there, the way you'd steady a door in wind — and said, "Would you come in? The stair's visible from the yard."

It was such a practical thing to say that I did it. I stepped into a smuggler's safehouse voluntarily, off the books, past a man who could have broken me in half without spilling his pipe, and I heard the door close behind me, and I understood with terrible clarity that whatever I had walked in here intending, I had already failed to intend it anymore.

"You've been asking after us," Hazel said. "In the district."

"Yes."

"With your face." He said it without accusation, almost with wonder, as if I'd committed some category error so fundamental he couldn't be angry about it. "A checkpoint clerk's face, walking through the print quarter, asking questions. Do you know what that costs? Half the people you spoke to will have moved their stock by morning."

"I know," I said. "That was rather the point. I wanted you to know I was coming."

Oskar shifted by the wall. "Wren of the ledgers," he said, tasting the name. "You're the one pulled the Corvane pages."

The room changed temperature. The girl looked up.

"I copied them," I said, and heard myself making the same distinction again, and hated it, and made it anyway.

"Copy's enough," Oskar said. "Paper doesn't care how it left the building. Question is what you mean to do with it." He nodded at my coat, and I realized the fold of it showed, the telltale thickness over my ribs, and that these people read coats the way I read columns. "Turn us in, or turn it loose. Those are the options, miss, and I'd take it kind if you picked one before my tea goes cold."

I reached into my coat. Hazel tensed. The girl — Pip, I had read the name in my own marginal notes, *the apprentice calls her something, listens for it* — Pip went very still, the way children do when adults go quiet, and Oskar's hand drifted toward the iron bar.

And I took out the ledger page. Unfolded it. Held it out.

"Forty-one," I said. "Six years. All signed by Corvane, all closed as 'relocated, no further interest.' I checked three of the names against parish records. None of them exist anywhere else. They were born, they were registered, and then they were erased." My voice did something I did not authorize it to do. "I came here to arrest you. Both of you. I told myself it was procedure. I have been telling myself things are procedure for four days."

Hazel took the page. He read it the way I had watched him examine papers at the checkpoint — thoroughly, without hurry, giving the document the dignity of full attention. Then he handed it, carefully, to the girl.

"Can you read?" he asked her.

"Some."

"Then read the number at the top. Out loud, if you're willing."

She read it. "Forty-one."

"That's children," Hazel said. "That's not a number. That's a girl about your size, times forty-one." He looked at me, and his eyes did the thing I had never been able to resolve from across the checkpoint desk — the steadiness, the terrible patience of them. "You could still arrest me. One more entry. It would even be true. But you copied that page knowing what it would cost, so let's not insult each other with the first plan you brought."

* * *

Here is what I remember most clearly from the next hour, and I have gone over that hour perhaps a thousand times since, looking for the exact hinge on which everything turned, and I will tell you now: it was not the letter. It was not the evidence. It was not Corvane's crest or the arithmetic of erasure or any argument anyone made.

It was a joke about gears.

Pip had gone back to her clock parts — children survive enormous things by the simple method of continuing to be children — and she held up two wheels, meshed, turning them against each other, frowning.

"They don't fit," she said. "The teeth."

"They do fit," Hazel said, settling beside her. "They're just arguing about it. Look — this wheel wants to lead, and this wheel refuses to follow, and between them they've stopped the whole clock. Like two clerks in one office."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she said, "Which one are you?" and laughed — a real laugh, sudden and unguarded, the laugh of a child who has somewhere recently learned that laughing is allowed — and the sound went through that grim little room like light through a prism.

I felt it land in me physically. I have searched since for a better description and cannot find one. Every ledger I had ever balanced, every crossing I had ever stamped, every sketch of a face filed neatly beside a registration number — I had built a career out of the belief that people were entries, that order was the same thing as goodness. And here was a child laughing on a smuggler's floor, a child whose entire existence in the records consisted of one instruction: *FORGET THE FACE.*

I had been trying to forget her face for weeks. It was the first thing I saw when I closed my eyes.

"The letter," I said, when I could trust my voice. "The original. Where is it?"

"Sewn into the lining of a coat," Hazel said, "in a location I'll only ever describe as elsewhere."

"Corvane is closing the net. I've seen the requisitions — the third checkpoint sealed, extra inspectors sworn in quietly, the observatory scheduled for search. He doesn't know precisely where you are. But he knows the letter exists, because he knows what he did, and powerful men are haunted most efficiently by their own memories." I knelt, and smoothed the copied page flat between us, and turned it toward him. "If you're caught with this, you hang, and the letter burns, and forty-one stay erased. If I'm caught having copied it, I'm dismissed at best. Which means we are, as of today, equally ruined. The only question left is whether we're ruined usefully."

Oskar laughed once, a bark, from the wall. "Gods preserve me. The clerk's gone radical."

"The clerk," I said, without looking at him, "has read enough ledgers to know when the accounts don't balance. Corvane has been running the city's conscience at a loss for six years and calling it profit. I want it audited."

Then I did the thing I had actually come to do, though I hadn't known it until my hands were already doing it. I held the copied page over the tallow lamp.

"What— " Hazel started forward.

"It's a copy," I said. "It's dangerous in my pocket and useless as evidence — Corvane's men would simply bury it, and my theft along with it. The original has to reach people who can't be buried." I watched the corner catch, curl, blacken. "This was me hedging. Me keeping a way back. There isn't a way back, and I'm tired of pretending there's a drawer in some office where the truth stays safe until someone responsible collects it. No one is coming to collect it. So."

The page burned quickly. Forty-one names became forty-one small flames and then forty-one absences, and the ash floated down onto the floorboards of a smuggler's safehouse, and I felt lighter and heavier simultaneously, which I have since learned is simply what it feels like to stop lying to yourself.

"So," Hazel echoed softly.

"I can wave you through checkpoints," I said. "My signature still means something, for another week, perhaps another month, depending on how long it takes Corvane's people to audit the archive and trace the pulled pages. They will trace them. I counted on that — I wanted the archive to itch." I finally looked up at him properly, clerk to forger, ledger to ghost. "I am not offering you my heart, Mr. Sterling. I'm offering you my hand. It's the only part of me that's ever been any use."

He was quiet a long moment. The lamp hissed. Pip, oblivious or wise beyond her years, fitted her two arguing wheels together again, patiently, tooth by tooth.

"My master taught me three rules," Hazel said at last. "Never be seen. Never be named. Never hold a place in anyone's ledger. I've kept them for twenty years, and they kept me alive, and last week I stood in a print shop and realized I couldn't remember the last time anyone had spoken my name out loud." He extended his hand, and I took it, and his fingers did not shake at all. "Wren. My name is Hazel Sterling. It's a pleasure to make your acquaintance under honest circumstances."

"Under criminal circumstances," Oskar corrected, reaching for the teapot. "Honest ones come later, if you live."

* * *

We planned until the cooperage below went quiet and the lamplighters had made their rounds. The shape of it came together the way mechanisms do — absurdly simple once assembled, impossible to unsee afterward. The observatory clock. Repairmen's exemptions. A case with room in it for more than gears.

But it was later, at the door, that the true transaction occurred — the one no ledger will ever record.

Hazel walked me down the crooked stair, out into the ink-smelling dark, past the yard where his shadow knew how to behave and mine did not. At the corner where my street met his, we stopped, and neither of us moved for a moment, and I understood that we were both aware of the same arithmetic: that one of us might very likely not exist, officially or otherwise, by month's end.

"If they take me," Hazel said, "there's a girl upstairs who thinks clocks have opinions. Don't let her learn that people don't."

"They won't take you," I said.

"You can't promise that."

"No," I admitted. "But I can enter it in my ledger, and my ledger has never once been wrong."

He smiled — the first time I had seen it happen all the way to his eyes — and then his face changed, quick as a cloud crossing the observatory glass, and he caught my arm.

"Wren," he said, low. "The junior clerk. At the third checkpoint. The one who logged signatures on sealed days."

"What of him?"

"He noted mine," Hazel said. "Weeks ago. I saw him write it, and I thought nothing of it, because I was a fool in love with the idea that being seen by you meant being seen by no one else." His hand tightened on my sleeve. "Corvane won't start with the archive, Wren. He'll start with the signatures. And yours is going to be on the gate book of the third checkpoint, dated the day after it was sealed."

Somewhere across the rooftops, the city bells began the hour — unhurried, indifferent, counting down something none of us had agreed to.
