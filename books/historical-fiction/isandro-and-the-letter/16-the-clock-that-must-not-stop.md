## 16. The Clock That Must Not Stop

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that happens at three in the morning in a smuggler's safehouse, and it is not the kind they teach apprentices. It is the kind where you count what you have left — one letter, one child, one clerk turned conspirator, one smuggler who keeps looking at you like a debt he regrets collecting — and then you count what the city will take from you for trying to keep them, and you notice the second column is heavier, and you do the sum anyway.

I was doing that sum when Wren laid the ledger page flat on Oskar's table and put her ink-stained finger on it like she was pinning a moth.

"Corvane sealed the third checkpoint this afternoon," she said. "And he's had men at the observatory since dusk. They're searching it."

"Searching it for what?"

"For us. For her. For whatever they think we know." She tapped the page again. "Which means we can't go around them, and we can't go through them, which leaves—" she paused, and I watched her decide to trust me with the shape of a thought she'd clearly been carrying for hours, "—going as someone they've been ordered not to touch."

Oskar, who had been cleaning a pistol with the leisurely air of a man pretending not to listen, snorted. "There's no such order."

"There's an exemption," Wren said. "It's older than Corvane and it's in standing statute because the observatory complains every time it's ignored. Certified repairmen admitted to municipal instruments are exempt from inspection. Any hour. Any circumstances." She looked at me. "The master clock is due for its quarterly service in six days. The astronomers return in five."

"And if the clock were to fail," I said slowly, feeling the idea assemble itself in my chest the way I'd once assembled an escapement, gear by gear, each tooth catching the next, "they'd summon a repairman immediately."

"They'd summon *a* repairman," Wren said. "The registry would send whoever was nearest. Unless the observatory itself requested a specific man by name. The astronomer who signed your original summons — Merrin, old, half-blind, remembers faces better than paperwork — he owes the clock his whole career. He'd request you."

"He doesn't know my name."

"He knows your work. He wrote *the clockmaker's apprentice* on the summons and let the registry fill in the rest. He'll write it again, and this time I'll make sure the name that reaches the checkpoint gate is one that opens doors instead of closing them." She said it simply, but her hand wasn't quite steady on the table, and I understood that she had just described the destruction of her own career with the calm of a woman reading a train schedule.

"That's assuming," Oskar said, blowing lint from the pistol's barrel, "that our boy here can get the letter inside the clock without the inspectors — who are currently camped in the building — watching him do it."

"The clock has a case," I said. "The case has a pendulum housing. The pendulum housing is opened exactly twice a year, by the servicing repairman, in front of witnesses, per protocol — and then sealed with a wax stamp over the latch, which no astronomer breaks except during service, because breaking it unregistered voids the observatory's calibration warranty, and the astronomers would sooner surrender their salaries than their calibration warranty." I looked down at my hands. They were steady now. They hadn't been steady in days, and I remember noticing their steadiness with something close to suspicion, the way you'd regard a dog that had stopped barking. "When the clock is serviced, the housing is opened. Whatever is inside the housing gets found. That's not smuggling. That's *protocol*. The most invisible thing in this city isn't a man without papers. It's a procedure nobody questions."

The table went quiet. Somewhere above us, water dripped through the old cistern works with a rhythm I found myself counting, because a rhythm is a prayer and I have never learned any others.

"You've been thinking about this," Wren said.

"Since the escapement," I admitted. "A clock that stops is a scandal. A clock that stops and gets repaired publicly, in front of the very men hunting us — that's a stage. And I've spent my whole life learning how to be furniture on a stage."

Oskar set the pistol down and grinned at me, and it was not a comforting grin; it was the grin of a man revising his estimate of another man upward, which in my experience always comes with a bill attached. "The repairman's coat," he said. "The one Vane kept for observatory calls. It's still at the workshop."

The workshop. My chest did something complicated and unhelpful at the word. "Vane sold us to the inspectors."

"Vane sold the *workshop*," Oskar corrected mildly. "The coat was in the coal chute storeroom. I checked yesterday. Your master talks fast when he's frightened, but he hides things slow and deep, like a squirrel with a guilty conscience. There's a coat, there's a certification chit sewn into its lining — Vane never trusted the registry to keep his records straight, with reason — and there's a key to the observatory tradesman's door." He shrugged. "I owed the woman he loved a promise, not him, but debts travel funny routes in this city. Same as people."

* * *

We planned until dawn, and then we planned the part that could not be planned, which is why I went up the ladder to the loft alone.

Pip was awake. She was always awake before anyone expected her to be; she moved through sleeping households like a small secret, and I suppose I loved her partly because she was the only person in the world worse at being noticed than I was, and better at it too, because she didn't do it out of fear. She did it out of preference. She had arranged Oskar's spare shirts into a row of shops along the windowsill and was conducting commerce between them in a whisper.

"Shops close at dawn," I said, sitting down on the floor beside her. My knees cracked. Six days of hiding in a smuggler's cellar will age a man twenty years, or so I'm told; I wouldn't know, since officially I have been dead eleven of them.

"Not this one," she said. "This shop stays open for important customers." Then she looked up at my face, and whatever she saw there made her fold her hands in her lap, the way children do when adults arrive carrying weather. "You're going away."

"I'm going to fix a clock."

"You're going away," she repeated, with the terrible precision of six-year-olds, who cannot be lied to with sentences but only with entire lives, and even then not for long.

So I told her the truth, or as much of it as fits inside a child's sleeping hours: that there was a very large clock, the largest in the city, and that it had swallowed something that didn't belong to it, and that I was going to reach inside and take the something back, and give it to the whole city all at once, so that everyone could see it. And that afterward, things would be different. I did not say *better*, because I have read enough history to be careful with that word, and because lying to Pip had gone out of fashion for me somewhere around the coal chute.

"And I stay here," she said.

"With Oskar. Yes."

She considered this. Pip considering is not silence; it is a full courtroom in session. "He says he knew a lady once who could whistle through her fingers and make patrolmen cry. Did you know her?"

"I never met her."

"She sounds like the sort of person who'd want me to stay put." Which was, translated from the language of children, an acceptance of terms, and also a reminder that Oskar talked about the dead woman more than he thought he did, and that Pip heard everything, filed everything, and would produce it later at some strategically devastating moment, like a good lawyer or a good clock, both of which run on tension stored in silence.

"Pip." I took her hands. They were small and warm and they had learned, in the weeks she'd lived in the tower above Vane's workshop, how to move between the strikes of a bell so that patrols passed beneath her unheard. I had taught her that. I did not know whether to be proud or ashamed, so I settled, as usual, for both. "While I'm gone, there may be noises. There may be men asking questions. If Oskar says hide, you hide. If Oskar says run, you run, and you don't wait for me, and you don't look back to check whether I'm following, because I *will* be following, and the only way you can help me is by not making me look for you."

"That's the rules of invisibility," she said. "Number three."

I blinked at her. "You know Vane's rules?"

"You say them in your sleep." She patted my hand, condescending in the gentle way of children consoling old fools. "Rule one, no names spoken aloud. Rule two, no eyes held too long. Rule three—" she screwed up her face, retrieving it, "—if you must be seen, be seen doing something boring."

I laughed. I shouldn't have — laughter is loud, and we were fugitives — but it came out anyway, low and cracked, and Pip looked so pleased with herself for producing it that I decided the breach of discipline was worth it. Then I held her face in my two hands for exactly as long as rule two permits, which is no time at all, and memorized it anyway, against regulations: the gap in her teeth arriving ahead of schedule, the freckles the winter had faded and the cellar had failed to restore, the small scar on her brow from the night in the clock tower when she'd stood up under the striking train and learned, permanently, about clearance tolerances.

FORGET THE FACE, the pinned instruction had said, six weeks and one lifetime ago.

I am a poor student. I have failed that assignment every single day since.

"Go fix your clock," Pip said, and turned back to her shirt-shops, and dismissed me with the magnificent indifference of a woman of commerce, and I went down the ladder before she could see that my eyes had stopped being steady even though my hands were.

* * *

Oskar walked me partway, through the maintenance runs beneath the river district — passages I will not describe, because Oskar made me swear it, on the grounds that "if they torture it out of you, boy, at least make them work for the whole map." He talked the whole way, which was his method of kindness. About routes. About which grates were watched and which were rusted and which were watched *because* they looked rusted. About the timing of the patrol changes at the third gate, down to the minute, because Oskar believed, as I do, that the city's cruelty ran on a schedule, and schedules can be beaten.

"At the final checkpoint," he said, stopping under a grate through which gray light fell in dusty bars, "you'll be waved through by a clerk. You won't question it. You won't look at her longer than—"

"Than the rules allow. I know."

"Do you?" He studied me with those river-colored eyes of his, and for once the cynicism in them had drained out through some leak I couldn't see. "Because I watched you with the girl, and I'll tell you what I told myself about Vane's lady, thirty years ago: the ones who love badly are the ones who love like it's contraband. Smuggled goods. You hold it wrong because you've only ever held it in places where holding it gets people killed." He pulled the grate up with a grunt. "Try, just try, to hold it like it's legal. Once. Before this is done."

Then he pushed me up into the light, and gave me the repairman's coat, which smelled of coal dust and machine oil and, faintly, of Master Aldous Vane's pipe, and which fit me the way a verdict fits a defendant, and he said: "Six days. If you're not back in four, I move her."

"To where?"

"Somewhere with a sea in it." He almost smiled. "She whistles now. Through her fingers. I taught her. A lady keeps her promises in strange currencies."

* * *

The observatory, when I came in sight of it across the frozen gardens on the fourth day, wore its inspectors like lice. Two at the tradesman's door. One pacing the colonnade. Their breath hung in the cold air in little clouds, and I counted the intervals between their turnings the way I count beats, and found them sloppy, unsynchronized, human — which meant beatable.

In my breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the letter. Magistrate's crest. Wax intact. Forty-one children's worth of weight, and it weighed less than the smallest gear in my kit.

Merrin the astronomer had sent my name to the registry two days prior — Wren's arrangement, though Wren herself I had not seen since the print district, and would see next at the third checkpoint, where she intended to commit professional suicide by signature. The summons had reached me through Oskar's channels, written in Merrin's trembling antique hand: *THE CLOCK IS FAILING. COME AT ONCE. BRING YOUR APPRENTICE IF YOU HAVE ONE. COME ALONE IF YOU DO NOT.*

The clock was failing because I had made it fail. Three nights ago, through the tradesman's door, in the dark, with the inspectors' boots audible in the corridor below, I had bent one pallet of the escapement by two degrees — a sabotage so small that only its author could find it, timed to worsen gradually, a death by fractions — and the great clock of the observatory had begun, with immense public dignity, to lose four minutes a day. The city sets its tide tables, its court sessions, and its executions by that clock. Four minutes a day is not an error. Four minutes a day is a scream.

I crossed the gardens with my toolbox in my hand and my heart going like a flywheel past its rated speed, and I presented myself at the tradesman's door, and I showed the inspector there a certification chit sewn into a dead man's coat lining, and I watched him read it and look at my face and find nothing worth recording — the one talent of my life, deployed one last time on purpose — and wave me through with the boredom of a man waving through furniture.

The stairs wound up toward the clock room. From far above, muffled through five floors of stone and gear, came the sound of the great mechanism limping through its damaged hours, each tick slightly wrong, a heartbeat with a murmur, and I climbed toward it through the cold.

Repairmen are exempt from inspection, the statute says. Nobody has ever asked what a repairman carries *out*.

In six days, when the astronomers returned and the case was opened per standing protocol, they would find the answer, and it would not stay in the housing long.

At the top of the stairs, the clock room door stood open, and a voice I did not know called down the stairwell: "That the clockmaker? He's been asking for you. Says the governor wants to watch the repair personally."

Governor. Not inspector. Not clerk.

*Corvane.*

I tightened my grip on the toolbox, and started up the last flight, into the light of the clock room, where my whole life waited to be either hidden or handed over — and I found, standing beneath the dial with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring a garden, Magistrate Corvane himself, come to watch the time get fixed.
