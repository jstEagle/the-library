## 18. What the Escapement Holds

There is a particular silence that lives inside a clock case. Not the absence of sound—clocks are never truly quiet—but the held-breath hush of a mechanism between beats, the tick gathered but not yet released. I had spent my whole life in that silence. It was the only country I was ever a citizen of.

I sealed the letter into the clock's case at four minutes past six in the morning, and I remember thinking, with the part of my mind that always stood apart and took notes: *so this is what it sounds like when you commit treason against a magistrate. Like nothing at all. Like a hinge.*

The letter went behind the strike-train plate, flat against the inner wall of the case, wrapped in oilcloth because I am sentimental, not stupid. Corvane's crest faced inward. I fixed it there with two drops of shellac and a scrap of leather, the way a man might pad a chattering gear—ordinary maintenance, invisible repair. Then I closed the case door, latched it, and stepped back into the dome where the dawn was coming grey through the shutter-slit windows, and the great orrery in the center of the floor hung motionless with its brass planets frozen mid-procession, like the whole solar system had been caught holding its breath too.

Fourteen hours until the astronomers returned. Fourteen hours until the standing protocol required them to open the case for scheduled service and find what I had planted there. Forty-one children's worth of evidence, sitting inside the most public machine in the city, waiting to be discovered by men whose entire religion was documentation.

I allowed myself ten seconds to feel it—the enormity, the terror, the strange bright weight of having finally done something that could not be undone. Ten seconds. Master Vane taught me that grief, like fear, is a lubricant: useful in small measures, ruinous if it floods the works.

Then I heard boots on the observatory stairs.

* *

They came up through the east entrance in the particular rhythm I had learned, over years of listening for footsteps I was not supposed to hear, means trouble: not hurrying, not patrolling, but *arriving*. Men who walk like that have already decided how things will go. They are merely present for the paperwork.

Three of them. Inspectors' grey coats, registry badges at the collar, and leading them a small neat man with the bloodless composure of someone who has never once been surprised by anything he did himself. I knew the type. I had been raised by him, more or less, in the person of every official who had ever glanced through me as though I were weather.

I was, by the grace of God and the sloppiness of the constabulary schedule, still holding my tool kit. So I did the only thing I have ever been genuinely good at.

"About time," I said, without looking up. "The third wheel's been chattering since Tuesday. Whoever serviced this last should be made to eat his files."

The small man stopped. "You are?"

"The clockmaker." I said it the way Vane had drilled into me across twenty years—not defensively, not too loudly, the way a man says something so boring it cannot possibly be interesting enough to check. "Sent notice three weeks ago. Escapement's failing. If she stops before noon observations, the whole week's readings are worthless, and I'll thank you not to stand where the light is."

It is astonishing, the power of a complaint. Officials can resist pleas, bribes, even tears—they have forms for all three—but a tradesman being mildly rude about scheduling dislodges them like grit in a bearing. The small man frowned, consulted a paper one of the others handed him, and found, presumably, that yes, a repair order existed, because Wren had forged one beautifully and filed it through channels so legitimate they practically smelled of ink and boredom.

"Name?" he asked.

And here is the thing about names, which I did not understand until that morning: they are gears too. Turn one and every other tooth in the machine must move. He would ask my name, my registration, my papers; some junior clerk would pull the file; and somewhere in that file, if Corvane's net had closed as tightly as I feared, was a description of Hazel Sterling that matched the face above my hands.

So I gave him the only answer a clockmaker owes anyone.

"You can read it off the work order," I said, "or you can let me finish the escapement before the sun ruins a week of astronomy. Which do you suppose the Magistrate's office would prefer?"

I turned back to the clock. My hands, I am proud to report, did not shake. Whatever else those years of invisibility cost me, they taught me this: a tremor is just a movement you haven't decided to own yet.

Behind me, silence. The particular silence of men deciding whether to be offended.

Then the small man said, mildly, "Proceed."

* *

Here is what stalling a clock looks like from the inside.

An escapement is the heart of any timepiece—the device that lets the gear train escape, tick by measured tick, instead of spinning free. It is also, conveniently, an instrument of infinite fiddliness. There are perhaps forty adjustments that can legitimately be made to one, each requiring the others to be redone, each taking time, each producing a small brass noise of industry that convinces bystanders that Something Is Happening.

I dismantled the anchor escapement of the master clock of the city observatory one piece at a time, slowly, reverently, and out loud, narrating as I went, because a man who talks about pallets and recoil angles is a man nobody watches closely.

"See here—" I held up the anchor to no one in particular. "Worn on the left pallet. That's your irregular drop. That's why she's been losing four seconds a day, which doesn't sound like much until you're timing the transit of a star." I set it down. I picked up a file. I put it down again, because filing takes longer than talking about filing.

The inspectors stationed themselves around the dome like crows on a fence. The small man—whose colleagues called him Inspector Reeve, when they called him anything—walked the perimeter, pausing at the orrery, at the star charts, at the shuttered telescope. Looking for something. I did not need to ask what. A letter. A girl. A trail that led, they hoped, to whoever had been foolish enough to carry both.

Pip was across the city, tucked into Oskar's safehouse beneath a false name and three layers of smuggler indifference. Wren was—I refused to think about where Wren was, because thinking about it made my hands want to shake after all. And the letter was four inches from my right elbow, sealed behind brass and oak, wearing its disguise as padding.

"Odd," Reeve said, near enough that I could smell the coffee on him, "that the observatory's empty. Astronomers dismissed for the week. Clock failing. All very convenient."

"All very Tuesday," I said. "Astronomers go to the coast congress every year at this time. Ask anyone with a calendar." This was true. I know, because Wren checked, twice, and then checked a third time in the way she does when she wants me to believe she's being thorough rather than frightened.

"And you? No apprentice? A workshop this size usually means hands under you."

"My master died," I said. "I never took anyone on." Which was true, and which hurt in a place I hadn't anticipated, because it sounded like such a small life when said aloud. One man. No hands under him. No one to carry the trade forward, no name spoken aloud, nothing passed down. Twenty years of craft, ending in a room full of hostile strangers because of one act of courage I could not take back and would not, even now.

"Lonely trade," Reeve said.

"It's a trade," I said.

He watched me work for a long moment. I let the file sing. Somewhere below us the city was waking—carts, gulls, the river bell—and up here under the dome, in the enormous hush, I worked on the heart of a clock that was about to stop a magistrate's, and I thought: *Vane, you old coward, you were wrong about one thing. Hiding isn't safety. It's just dying slowly, in installments.*

* *

They recognized me at half past nine.

I want to be honest about how it happened, because there is no dignity in it and honesty is the only thing I have left to hoard. It wasn't clever detection. It wasn't the ledger sketch matching my face. It was Pip's ribbon.

She'd tied it around my wrist that morning at Oskar's—a bit of blue thread she'd insisted, absolutely insisted, I wear "for luck," because Pip's theology holds that luck can be tied onto a person like a tag onto a trunk. I had forgotten it. Three weeks of living as a hunted man and I forgot a six-year-old's knot on my wrist while performing invisibility for three inspectors.

Reeve saw it when I reached into the case for the oiler. His eyes went to the ribbon, then to my face, then to a paper he drew from his coat with the unhurried certainty of a man turning the last page of a book he already knows the end of.

"Hazel Sterling," he said. Not a question.

The dome got very large around me. The clock ticked in my ears—or didn't, actually, since I had its heart spread across the table like surgery—but I heard ticking anyway, the phantom kind, the kind your own blood makes when it decides to get loud.

"Unregistered," he continued, pleasantly. "Impersonation of a deceased registrant. Abduction of a child of quality. And now, I expect, we'll find something worse, won't we, when we look in that case."

Time. The word of my whole life. I needed time. The astronomers came at noon; it was half past nine; I needed two and a half hours and I had, at best, the length of one breath.

So I picked up the anchor escapement—the worn one, the left pallet scarred from years of honest service—and I held it up to the light, and I looked Reeve in the eyes for the first time since he'd entered my domain.

"You'll want to arrest me," I said. "I understand. But consider what you're standing in."

He waited. Men like Reeve always wait; it costs them nothing and reveals everything.

"This clock times the city," I said. "Every registry stamp, every checkpoint log, every court summons bears her hour, and she gets that hour from this escapement. If I stop now—if you take me now—she sits apart, unassembled, and no one in this building can put her back together. Not your clerks. Not your locksmiths. Your Magistrate signs his own documents with her hand, Inspector, and if she's stopped, every timestamp he issues this afternoon is unverifiable. Think what that does to a case built on paper."

I set the anchor down, gently, the way you set down a sleeping child.

"Give me two hours," I said. "Arrest me at noon, with the clock running and the record clean. Or take me now and explain to the Magistrate why his own signatures can't be trusted."

It was a lie braided into a truth, which is the strongest rope there is. The timestamps mattered to no one. But the pride of the registry—infallible, meticulous, self-worshipping—that was real, and Reeve knew it, and I watched him weigh it.

He almost refused. I saw it come and go across his face like weather. Then he glanced at the ribbon on my wrist one more time, and whatever mercy or calculation moved him, he folded his hands behind his back and said:

"One hour. And I watch every stroke you make."

* *

You have never worked until you have worked under the eyes of the man sent to destroy you, rebuilding the heart of a clock with a letter's worth of treason sealed four inches from your knuckles.

I reassembled the escapement slower than I've ever done anything in my life. Every pallet dressed, every drop measured, every lock and release tested with the lightest touch of a fingertip, and all of it performed under Reeve's gaze while my mind ran ahead of my hands like a dog straining its lead. *Is the wrapping showing? No. Is the shellac set? Yes. Does the case sit true? True as a grave.* One hour. Fifty minutes. Forty. The sun crawled through the shutter-slit and laid a slow bright stripe across the floor like a fuse.

Somewhere in the middle of it Reeve said, conversationally, "The girl is six. You understand she'll be remanded to intake regardless of outcome. Records exist."

I did not look up. "Hand me the screwdriver. The small one."

"The records—"

"I said the *small* one."

And here is the strange thing, the thing I keep returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth: he handed it to me. Perhaps he wanted to see if I'd flinch. Perhaps six-year-olds mean nothing to men who speak of children as line items. Perhaps he simply didn't care. But he placed the small screwdriver in my palm, and I took it, and my hand was steady, and in that moment I understood something that Vane had spent twenty years trying to prevent me from understanding:

They could only make me invisible if I agreed to be. The rest was paperwork.

At twelve minutes to noon the escapement went back together for the last time. I set the pendulum swinging. I closed the case. I latched it. And the master clock of the city observatory—the slow heart, the keeper of every hour the state had ever stamped against me—took up its beat again beneath my hands, steady as a pulse, patient as justice arriving late.

"Done," I said.

Reeve stepped forward. Behind him, at the top of the stairs, came a new sound: many boots, ascending fast, and voices—the astronomers, early, arguing about transit timings the way scholars argue, which is to say at volume, in Latin, already pushing through the doors with their cases and their instruments and their absolute unshakable conviction that the world exists to be documented.

"Noon protocol," one of them announced to the room at large, not noticing the grey coats, not caring. "Case inspection and service log. Who's the clockmaker? Ah—there. Sign here, sign here."

He thrust the service ledger at me with one hand and reached for the case latch with the other, and Reeve shouted *stop*, and moved—

But the astronomers of this city are protected by charter, by habit, and by the magnificent obliviousness of men who have never once considered that a clock could contain anything but time. The latch clicked open under a professor's absent fingers before Reeve crossed half the floor.

And I stood there with the service pen still wet in my hand, watching the inspector's face, watching the professor's hand go into the case toward the strike-train plate where a magistrate's sealed letter lay waiting, and I thought, with a calm that felt almost like happiness:

*Tick.*
