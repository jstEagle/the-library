## 19. The Long Minute

There is a moment in every mechanism's life—the ones that fail, anyway—when a gear stops being a gear and becomes a piece of evidence. You can see it happen if you've spent enough years bent over a bench: the brass goes from being a thing with a purpose to being a thing with a history. Every tooth records something. Every scratch is testimony.

I thought about that a great deal while Magistrate Corvane's men held my arms and the magistrate himself walked a slow circle around me on the observatory floor, because it was either think about gears or think about screaming.

"Name," Corvane said. Not a question. A form to be filled in.

"Hazel Sterling," I said, which was true, which was the first entirely true thing I had said aloud in a registry's hearing in my entire life, and which I suspected would cost me more than any lie ever had.

"Registered number?"

I gave it. The dead man's number, borrowed eleven years ago out of Vane's drawer of convenient mercies. Somewhere in Wren's ledgers, I knew, that discrepancy had already been found—I knew it the way you know a mainspring is over-wound before it snaps, by feel, by the tension coming off a thing. But Corvane did not know what I knew, and I was not going to donate it to him.

Corvane stopped his circling directly in front of me. He was smaller than I expected. Tyrants usually are, in person; the size lives in the paperwork, not the body. He had gray eyes like ledger paper and the particular calm of a man who has never once in his life been interrupted.

"You carried a child," he said. "A girl of six, fine clothes, no listing. She passed through the river checkpoint two days ago under fraudulent papers. Where is she?"

"I don't know."

"You do."

"I really don't," I said. "That's the trouble with children. They don't file reports."

One of the inspectors—Hollis, the one with the kind face that wasn't—tightened his grip on my arm in a way meant to suggest that humor would be deducted from something. My humor, possibly. Or other things.

Corvane didn't smile. I got the sense he outsourced all smiling to subordinates and audited their output later. "Let me describe your situation plainly, since clockmakers are said to appreciate plain mechanisms." He gestured at the great clock above us without looking at it, the way a man doesn't look at furniture. "You entered this building yesterday on legitimate repair orders. You are here past your authorized window. When my inspectors arrived, they found the case of the master clock freshly opened and its escapement disassembled—a job that should take an hour stretched across a day and a night. Why?"

"Because it was badly made," I said. "Someone fitted an escape wheel cut too coarse for the train. It's been losing four seconds a day for years. Your astronomers probably blame the weather."

"Where is the letter?"

"What letter?"

"The letter that came into this building in the possession of a child. Sealed with my crest." He said *my crest* the way other men say *my wife*, or *my hand*. "Where is it?"

And there it was—the whole shape of my death, offered politely, in daylight, beneath the slow heart of the most public clock in the city.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being interrogated: it is boring. That is the design of it. Fear burns bright for ten minutes and then it banks down into coals, and what's left is tedium and cold hands and the growing certainty that your legs have gone to sleep. They had taken my tools off me within the first minute—all except the small burnisher tucked flat against my forearm in a leather sleeve, which is not a confession, it's just where burnishers live—and without tools my hands kept trying to work anyway, fingers opening and closing on nothing, mending invisible clocks. Vane used to say idle hands were a registration risk. Vane used to say a lot of things.

Vane had sold me. I had watched him do it from inside the coal chute, one leg already in the dark, and heard him say *he's only an apprentice, take him, he's nothing, I hardly know the boy* in the same voice he'd used to teach me to cut a pivot. I had thought, sliding down into the coal dust with Pip's hand in mine, that the pain would be enormous, and then discovered it was instead very quiet and very precise, like a tooth dying. Betrayal keeps excellent time. It arrives exactly when it was always going to.

So: no names. That was the whole architecture of my resistance, and it was less than nothing, architecturally speaking—it was negative structure—but it was mine.

"The girl," Corvane said again, sometime after the light through the dome had shifted by a hand's width. "Describe her."

"No."

"You will describe her eventually. Everyone describes things eventually. It is what description is *for*."

"There's a philosophy of description now?"

"There is a *practice* of it," Corvane said, "and it is mine, and it is thorough, and it has removed forty-one irregular entries from the civic rolls over six years with a completeness I am quite proud of." He paused, and I understood with a lurch that he was going to keep talking, that he wanted to, that this was the closest thing to intimacy available to a man who loved only line items. "Children are expensive to maintain and cheap to remove. Do you know what an unlisted child costs the city? Nothing. Do you know what it costs to make one listed? Everything. I simplified the arithmetic. History will call it efficiency."

"History," I said, "is going to need a very good clockmaker to get the time right on that."

Hollis hit me in the stomach—not hard, professionally, the way you tap a crystal to seat it—and I folded up around the pain and thought, distantly, *four seconds a day, that wheel loses four seconds a day, but not anymore, I fixed it.*

Which is when I looked up at the clock.

* * *

I want to be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of my last morning as an unregistered man: I had built the plan out of habit, not hope. Put the letter in the case. Let the astronomers find it per protocol when the mechanism is serviced. It had sounded clean in Oskar's safehouse, in the lamplight, with Pip asleep against Wren's shoulder and everyone pretending we might all walk away from this. Plans sound clean precisely in proportion to how unlikely they are to survive contact.

The flaw was timing. The astronomers weren't due back for three days. Corvane had sealed the checkpoint and come early specifically to search the observatory before anyone official could stand in front of anything official. I was supposed to stall—dismantle the escapement so thoroughly that no inspector could accuse me of finishing, buy hours, days if I was lucky. And I had done it. I had scattered the heart of the greatest clock in the city across the floor like a dropped dinner, and it had bought me exactly as much time as a man can buy with brass and patience, which is to say: enough time to be caught in.

But standing there with Hollis's fist still echoing in my ribs, I looked up at the dial and did the arithmetic I hadn't let myself do before, because hope is heavier than fear and I couldn't carry both up the ladder.

The clock read 11:58.

And it was running.

I had reassembled the escapement at dawn—had to, to test the repair, had meant to pull it apart again afterward—and in the chaos of the inspectors' arrival I had never pulled it apart. Which meant the master clock of the city observatory, the clock every lesser clock in the capital set itself by, the clock whose noon signal went out by wire to the railway stations and the registry halls and the checkpoint towers—

—which struck noon, everywhere in the city, at the same instant—

was currently accurate to within a second.

And it was about to strike.

* * *

You cannot stop a striking train from below. People don't understand this about large clocks; they think you can reach up and hold the hammer, the way you'd hush a mantel clock. But the observatory's strike weighs more than a horse. Its fly fans air to brake the train. Its hammers are cast bronze the size of a man's head. Once the warning pin lifts the locking piece, the whole apparatus commits, the way a confession commits, the way a signature commits, and nothing below the level of the dial can call it back.

Eleven fifty-nine.

Corvane saw me looking up. Perhaps he saw something in my face—clockmakers have poor poker faces, our whole trade is reading dials—and perhaps he simply had the animal sense of a man who has spent six years hunting children and knows what it looks like when the trap shifts under someone else's weight.

"What," he said, "are you smiling at?"

"Nothing," I said. "It's a tic. Tradesman's palsy."

"Search the case again," Corvane said to the inspectors. "All of it. The weight chamber, the trunk, the—"

The first stroke fell.

I have heard that bell my whole life through walls, through rain, through the roof of a workshop where I learned to disappear. I had never stood beneath it. The sound does not arrive so much as *occur* to you; it happens in the chest before the ears file their report. Dust came down from the beams in a fine gold rain. One of the inspectors flinched, which I intend to remember fondly for whatever remains of my life.

Twelve strokes. Twelve chances for the whole city to set itself right by us.

And on the twelfth, from the far side of the great doors—doors that were locked, doors that Corvane had sealed, doors that were therefore opening in defiance of every order he had given—came voices. More than one. Young voices, argumentative, sleep-deprived, carrying instrument cases and the aggrieved tone of men returning from an inconvenient conference to find their life's work allegedly repaired by a stranger.

The astronomers were early. Three days early. Because conferences, unlike children, are permitted to end ahead of schedule.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and it happened in order, because that is the only way I know how to tell anything.

Senior Astronaut Kessler—I never learned his first name, and I regret that, because I owe him a great deal—came through the doors first, squinting up at the dial, saying something to a colleague about *losing four seconds a day, we'll see if the fellow actually fixed it,* and then he saw us: five inspectors, a magistrate, and one apprentice kneeling on his observatory floor surrounded by the scattered organs of his clock.

Kessler was not a brave man. I could see that immediately; everything about him was calibrated for comfort, like a pendulum hung too low. But he was a *procedural* man, and I have come to believe procedure, in the right hands at the right hour, outweighs bravery the way the strike train outweighs the hammer.

"Who opened the case?" Kessler said.

"He did," Corvane said smoothly, "and he is detained pending—"

"Standing protocol," Kessler said, in the tone of a man reciting scripture to an intruder in his church, "requires that following any service to the master mechanism, the case is inspected and certified by two fellows of the observatory before the wire signal resumes. If the clock has been tampered with—" his eyes went to the beautiful, terrible accuracy of the dial "—the certification must occur *immediately upon discovery.* Those are the statutes. Observatory charter, article nine. Written, incidentally, by the magistrate's own office."

I watched Corvane arrive at the trap he had built himself, item by item, signature by signature. He could order the astronomers away—but the wire signal, the city's noon, had already gone out on the stroke; certification was now mandatory by law he had drafted to make the observatory answerable to *him*. He could refuse the inspection—but refusal would be witnessed, recorded, minuted by four young fellows with notebooks and grievances. Every road forward ran through the case of the clock.

"Open it," Corvane said at last, quietly. "Two of you. Quickly."

"Both fellows," Kessler said. "Article nine."

"*Open it.*"

They opened it. All of them, all five astronomers, with the unhurried ceremony of men who had waited their whole careers to be obstructed by a magistrate. And I knelt on the floor between two inspectors with my heart going like a flywheel and watched Kessler's assistant lift the false base panel I had fitted at dawn with wax and screws stolen from my own coat buttons, and slide it aside, and reach into the void where the letter lay flat against the inner wall, wrapped in oilcloth against the oil.

He drew it out. He turned it over. He read the crest.

"Magistrate," he said, in a voice that had gone very still, "this letter bears your seal. And it has been *opened and reseated*, which is a felony under the documents act, and it appears to be"—he unfolded it, because of course he unfolded it, curiosity is the one statute that overrides article nine—"a sworn deposition regarding the disposition of forty-one unlisted minors. Signed. Witnessed. Dated."

The silence that followed lasted one second. I know, because I have spent my life counting seconds, and it was exactly one second long, and in it I heard everything: the tick of the repaired escapement above us, keeping perfect time; the far-off bells of a hundred parish clocks and registry towers correcting themselves to our noon; and somewhere in my own chest, the sound of a door I had been holding shut for twenty-three years finally swinging open on oiled hinges.

Then everyone spoke at once.

The astronomers clustered around the letter like gulls on a dropped fish. The inspectors looked to Corvane, and Corvane—for one unguarded half-second—looked *small*, gray-eyed and gray-faced, a line item in a ledger that had finally refused to balance. And Hollis's grip on my arm loosened, because even professional hands must choose what they hold, and he was suddenly holding a witness rather than a prisoner, and everyone in that room knew the difference.

"Arrest him," Corvane said, and his voice recovered its calm, which was somehow worse, which told me the fight was not over, that men like him do not fall, they *restructure*. "He broke into this facility. He is unregistered. Whatever that paper claims, it was placed by a criminal—"

"With your seal," Kessler said, "on a document act violation. Yes." He looked at Corvane the way I look at a watch that has been dropped in water: assessing damage, deciding salvage. "I believe, magistrate, that the fellows of this observatory will need to consult the press before we certify anything. The statutes require publication of anomalies. Article eleven. Also yours."

Somewhere behind me, an inspector's boots started quietly toward the door—to send word, or to run, I neither knew nor cared. My legs had truly gone numb now. My hands had stopped trying to mend invisible clocks. For the first time in as long as I could remember, they were simply still, hanging at my sides, registered to no task at all.

Above us, the great clock ticked on into the first ordinary minute after noon, and I thought: *that is the trick of it, that is the whole secret Vane never taught me.* You can hide inside precision your whole life. Or you can build something precise enough, and true enough, that when it strikes, the whole city has no choice but to hear it.

Corvane straightened his coat. Two inspectors moved toward me anyway, loyal to the last breath of a dying order.

But the astronomers had formed a loose ring around the letter, and around me, and rings, unlike registries, cannot be undone by signing.

"Take him," Corvane said.

Nobody moved.

And through the great doors, running, out of breath, holding a folded page with archive stamps still wet on it—came Wren.
