## 21. Registered by Hand

The Registry Office on Candlewright Street had been designed, I think, by someone who hated both architecture and people equally. It was a long grey hall with a ceiling high enough to make you feel like a specimen under glass and windows narrow enough to remind you that glass could be broken. The queue for registration wound between brass stanchions, and every person in it had the particular posture of someone rehearsing their own name.

I knew the posture. I had worn it my whole life without knowing what it was for.

Three weeks after the observatory — three weeks of bandages, of broth Wren made too salty on purpose because she said salt was a form of argument, of sleeping propped upright because the scar along my ribs pulled whenever I lay flat — I stood in that queue holding nothing but my own hands, which still shook when I let them.

"Next."

The clerk behind the desk was a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the expression of someone who processed souls by weight. He slid the form across the counter without looking up.

*Full name. Parentage, if known. Trade. Place of dwelling. Bloodline declaration.*

I picked up the pen. It was an ordinary pen, cheap oak, nib slightly bent from ten thousand hands that had gripped it too hard. My whole life, this pen had been a weapon pointed at me. Master Vane's first rule — the rule beneath all his other rules — had been *never touch one*. Registration ledgers were how they found you. Registration was how the forty-one children had vanished, erased in tidy columns by men who thought of human beings as line items. A name on a page, Vane had taught me, was a hook, and the state held all the lines.

But Vane had offered me up to two inspectors in a heartbeat, so perhaps his curriculum deserved revision.

I pressed the nib to the paper. The ink bloomed slightly, the way ink does when the page is nervous for you.

"Hazel," I said aloud, because it seemed important to say it out loud at least once in an official building, "Sterling."

The clerk wrote nothing. He raised his eyes. "That's your function, sir, not your name. Full legal designation."

"That *is* my full legal designation." I found, to my surprise, that I was smiling. My ribs objected. I smiled anyway. "Sterling is the surname. Hazel is the given. I've checked. I've had rather a lot of time lately to check things about myself."

He looked at me the way clerks look at irregularities — which is to say, briefly, before deciding I was somebody else's problem. He stamped the line. The sound of the stamp was very small and very loud, the way significant sounds are.

"Trade?"

"Clockmaker."

"Dwelling?"

"The workshop above the old Vane premises, Fetter's Court." Then, because the truth was the entire point of today: "As partner. My partner holds the lease. Her name is Wren Ashgrave. Formerly of the river checkpoint. You may have heard she burned some papers."

The pen stopped. Somewhere behind me in the queue, a woman drew breath through her teeth.

The clerk's eyes flicked up again, properly this time, and stayed a moment longer. "Formerly," he said carefully, "of several things, if the morning sheets are accurate."

"The morning sheets are usually accurate. That's rather her point."

He stamped the second line harder than necessary, as though he could stamp the conversation into submission. And that was that. That was everything. Twenty-odd years of being a smudge in other people's peripheral vision, a number belonging to a dead man, a face trained never to hold anyone's gaze — and now there was a line in a ledger with my actual name attached to it, in my own handwriting, witnessed by a bored official and forty strangers in a queue.

I had expected to feel exposed. Hunted. The old dread said: *now they know where you live. Now the page exists that can find you.*

What I felt instead was lighter than I had felt since I was six years old. Because the difference between then and now was this: the ledger no longer belonged only to them. Corvane's ledgers were evidence now, sealed and photographed and quoted in the evening editions. The registry still catalogued faces — but the city had learned, in the space of a fortnight, that faces could testify back.

A name on a page was still a hook. But hooks work both ways, if enough people are watching the line.

* * *

Wren was waiting outside on the steps, doing a poor job of not waiting. She had positioned herself against the railing with a newspaper she clearly wasn't reading, folded to a page she'd read four times already — the one with Corvane's photograph, taken as they escorted him to the magistrates' remand house. He looked calm even in arrest. That was the thing about Corvane that would keep me up at night for years, I suspected: he looked like a man who simply hadn't finished his arithmetic yet.

"Well?" Wren said, folding the paper with unnecessary precision.

"Registered." I held up the receipt like a fishing trophy. "Hazel Sterling, clockmaker, Fetter's Court. There is now a government document that proves I exist."

"There was always proof you existed," she said. "It just wasn't theirs."

"You can't say things like that on the steps of the Registry. Someone will write it down."

"Good." She tucked her arm through mine — carefully, on the left side, away from the bandages; she'd memorized the geography of my wounds within days, the way she'd once memorized ledger columns — and we started down the street. "Let them write it down. I spent six years writing down other people. I'm told it's educational."

She was dressed plainly, no checkpoint uniform, no clerk's collar — those had been returned to her the day of her dismissal, along with a letter thanking her for her service in language so bloodless it might have been written by Corvane himself, which possibly it had, in spirit. Unprosecuted. That was the word the papers used. *Clerk dismissed but unprosecuted.* As though mercy were a technicality instead of a miracle. The junior clerk who had noted her signature at the third checkpoint had done the institution one final service: his testimony, coming from inside their own ranks, had made prosecuting her more embarrassing than letting her go. Institutions are like that. They will forgive almost anything faster than they'll admit a mistake.

"They gave you trouble?" she asked.

"The clerk got very interested when I mentioned your name."

"Flattering."

"He seemed to consider it a warning label. Like something printed on a bottle."

Wren smiled at the cobblestones. "Better than 'unlisted.' You know what I keep thinking about? The forms I used to process. There was a column — you never saw it, being on the wrong side of the desk — column fourteen. Disposition. Every person who came through got a disposition. *Passed. Held. Referred.* Forty-one children went through my checkpoints over six years and every one of them said *referred*, and I initialed it, and I never once asked referred to *where*." She was quiet for three steps. "Column fourteen is why I can't wear the collar anymore. Not shame, exactly. Just — I know what's printed inside the collar now."

"And what's that?"

"*Disposition.*"

We walked the rest of the way to Fetter's Court without talking. Some silences are doors and some are walls; hers, that day, was a door left open on purpose, and I let it stay open, because I was learning that love — the real article, not the stolen-increment kind we'd practiced in clock towers — mostly consists of knowing which silences to leave alone.

* * *

The workshop door stuck. It had always stuck; Vane had called it character. Now, standing in front of it with a key of my own and a partner beside me, I understood that it had never been character. It had been a lock pretending to be a hinge — the building itself colluding with its occupant to keep the world out.

I put my shoulder into it. My ribs filed a formal complaint. The door gave with a groan and swung open onto dust, dimness, and the smell I had feared I'd never smell again: oil and brass and old wood shavings, the particular perfume of time kept in small containers.

They had taken nearly everything in the raid. The benches were stripped, the drawers hung open like mouths, the wall of completed repairs gone bare except for the pale rectangles where tickets had been pinned. Even the great regulator clock that had dominated the east wall for sixty years was gone — confiscated as evidence, though what a clock could testify to I never learned. Perhaps they just took it because it was the biggest thing in the room and confiscation, like fear, prefers to be thorough.

But they hadn't found the strongbox under the floorboards, because Vane had hidden it so well he'd half forgotten it himself, and I had spent twenty years learning where everything in this room truly lived. Inside it: my master's oldest tools, three watches worth more than the building, and the deed to the premises, unsigned in his hand, dated eleven years ago — the year the man whose registration number I carried had died. He had prepared for my ruin the same week he purchased my disguise. I choose to believe those were two different kinds of love. Most days I manage it.

"Right," Wren said, rolling up her sleeves in a manner I had come to recognize as either foreplay or renovation. "Inventory."

"We have no inventory."

"Then that's the inventory. Zero. Write it down." She was already at the bench, sorting wreckage into piles with a clerk's ruthless taxonomy: *salvage, scrap, evidence-of-a-crime-someone-should-investigate.* "We start Monday."

"Today is Monday."

"Splendid. We're ahead of schedule."

I watched her work for a while. There is a particular happiness, I've decided, that can only be experienced by people who have almost lost everything: it looks exactly like a woman sorting broken gears in dusty light, arguing with herself about whether a cracked barrel spring counts as salvage. (It does not. She ruled against it twice.)

"I spoke to the landlord's agent yesterday," I said. "About the lease."

Her hands paused. "And?"

"He wanted to know if the premises' previous association with scandal would affect trade. I told him scandal was excellent for the clock business. People suddenly care very much about whether their clocks are honest."

"Hazel."

"He also wanted to know if I was the clockmaker from the newspapers. I said the newspapers had misspelled my name, which was true, and that the correct spelling was available in the Candlewright Street registry, which was also true, and that he was welcome to look it up." I turned one of Vane's old loupes over in my fingers. "You should have seen his face, Wren. He actually checked. He walked to Candlewright Street and checked. Nobody has ever checked on me before. I could get used to being worth the walk."

She crossed the room and took the loupe out of my hand and set it down, and then she took my face in both hands, which hurt nothing at all and fixed quite a lot.

"Say it once," she said. "For practice. In case a magistrate ever asks you who you are and you don't have a receipt handy."

"My name is Hazel Sterling," I said. "I am a clockmaker. I live at Fetter's Court with my partner, who is smarter than I am and salts the soup on purpose."

"On purpose," she agreed, and kissed me, and somewhere in the room a gear rolled off a bench and rang against the floorboards like a small bell approving of us.

* * *

We reopened on the first of the month, because Wren insisted openings required a date with a good column of numbers, and the first has a pleasing solidity to it. She painted the sign herself — *STERLING & ASHGRAVE, CLOCKMAKERS* — in letters tall enough to be read from across the court, which Vane would have called suicide and which I had come to call Tuesday.

Business arrived slowly, then all at once. The scandal did what scandal does: it made our door a destination. Half our early customers came to gawk and stayed to have their watches regulated, because gawking is hungry work and honesty, it turns out, is contagious. A widow brought in a carriage clock that had belonged to one of the forty-one — her grandson, taken at seven — and asked if we could make it run again, and Wren worked on it three nights running without charging her, and after that the shop acquired a reputation of a different sort, and mothers began bringing us clocks the way people bring flowers to graves, and we repaired every one of them free and kept a shelf for them in the window where the light could reach.

Oskar sent no message and appeared no times, which from Oskar was a warm embrace. Once a month a parcel arrived containing smuggled Swiss escapements of impossible quality and a note reading only *running tab, don't ask.* We paid him through a coal merchant who didn't exist. Everyone concerned found this arrangement deeply satisfying.

Vane I did not see. He had left the city the week after Corvane's arrest — quietly, of course, the way he did everything, a man so skilled at vanishing that he managed to do it even when everyone was finally looking for him. He left no note. He left, however, a debt of gratitude unpaid at the tobacconist on Corner Lane, which the tobacconist eventually forgave on the grounds that the story attached to it had become valuable gossip. I like to imagine him in some smaller, foggier city, teaching some other orphaned apprentice the rules of invisibility, and I like even more to imagine the apprentice ignoring every single one.

Pip I saw on Sundays, at first, until the adoption was finalized and Sundays became something negotiated with her new family — a good family, a loud one, with a house near the printing quarter and a dog with opinions. She had a new name now, legally, in a ledger, in her own handwriting once she'd learned cursive: I will not put it here, because some things a person gets to introduce themselves. But she kept *Pip* the way you keep a key to a house you've moved out of, and she used it with us, and when she used it, it sounded less like a nickname than like a title she'd earned.

The scar along my ribs settled into a dull weather-bone ache, and I learned its forecast. On damp days I moved slowly. Wren pretended not to notice, and I pretended not to notice her pretending, and this arrangement suited us both, being itself a kind of clockwork.

And the observatory clock ran. That was the part I came back to, on quiet evenings, closing the shop: somewhere above the city, past the river checkpoints that no longer separated children from adults because a certain letter had made such separations politically fatal, the great clock I had rebuilt stood in its tower keeping perfect time with a hollow place in its case where a letter used to sleep. The astronomers serviced it quarterly now. Protocol demanded it. Standing protocol, permanent protocol, the kind of procedure that outlives magistrates.

Every mechanism remembers what was put inside it. That is not poetry; that is horology. Brass holds its shape. Sealed cases hold their secrets until someone with clean hands and standing instructions opens them at the proper hour. I had spent my life believing order was the enemy — that precision belonged to ledgers and checkpoints and men who counted children like coins. I had been wrong by half. Order was only ever a tool, like a pen or a hook. The question had never been whether the world would keep records. The question was who would hold the pen, and whether we would dare to sign.

Some nights I dreamed of the queue at Candlewright Street. In the dreams the clerk always asked the last question on the form, the one I hadn't noticed that day, the one printed in letters so small they took years to read: *Disposition.* And in the dreams I leaned across the counter and said, clearly, so the whole grey hall could hear:

*Delivered.*

* * *

On a bright morning in our second autumn, I was at the bench fitting a mainspring when Wren came up the stairs two at a time, newspaper in hand, wearing the expression she wore when a column refused to balance.

"Hazel," she said. "There's a girl at the door asking for the clockmaker."

"Customer?"

"She's about seven. Fine coat. Very serious face. She says—" Wren glanced at the paper, and something moved across her eyes like a shadow under water. "She says she was sent by Magistrate Corvane's solicitor. She says she has a message for the man who stopped the clock."

Below us, in the shop, the bell over the door chimed, though no customer had entered.

I put down the mainspring. I wiped my hands very slowly, the way my master taught me — *clean hands before important work, boy, whatever the work turns out to be* — and I went down to meet her, and I did not look away.
