## 22. The Girl Who Kept Her Face

A year is nothing, a clockmaker will tell you. A year is the balance wheel turning thirty-one million times, give or take, depending on the month and how badly you treat it. Thirty-one million oscillations of a spring that wants only to return to center. I spent twenty-two years trying not to exist, which turns out to be roughly the same amount of work.

It was Wren who pointed out that the observatory clock had run for three hundred and forty-one days without losing more than two seconds.

"Two seconds," she said, over breakfast, in the workshop that was now half hers — her ledgers stacked next to my gear trays, her fountain pen perpetually migrating to my side of the bench like a cat with no loyalty at all. "In a year. That machine is more honest than any clerk I ever worked with."

"That's a low bar," I said. "I've met the clerks."

"You married adjacent to one."

"I did," I said, and found — as I still find, every time, a small private astonishment — that saying it aloud cost me nothing. No checkpoint. No ledger sketch. No eyes sliding past mine because looking too long was against the rules.

We were not, strictly speaking, married. The registry had no category for what we were: a dismissed checkpoint clerk and a formerly unregistered clockmaker's apprentice turned journeyman turned, as of four months ago, Master Sterling of Sterling & Ashworth, Horological Repairs, a shop with a sign and everything. But the word *married* fit the way a well-cut pallet fits an escapement, so we used it among ourselves and let the city draw its own conclusions. The city, to its credit, was busy with other things. Magistrate Corvane's trial had run through the winter, forty-one small coffins' worth of testimony, and even after the verdict the papers could not leave it alone. There are some stories a city tells itself to prove it has a conscience. I have complicated feelings about being one of those stories. Pip, when asked, said she thought it was "mostly fine, except the drawings of you make your nose look like a parsnip."

The drawings did, in fairness, do that.

* * *

The invitation came on a Tuesday, delivered by a boy from the school on Chandler's Row, and it was written in a hand I knew before I read a word of it: round-bottomed letters, the crossbars drawn last and slightly too long, as if each T and E were stretching its arms out to be picked up.

*Mrs. Halloway says if the clock man comes he can watch us wind it but he must not touch anything.*

Signed: *Edith Marchmont.*

Not Pip. Not Wren, not Sparrow, not any of the six other names she'd tried on during our months in hiding like coats borrowed from larger children. Edith Marchmont, adopted daughter of the Marchmonts of Bellwether Lane, a widowed schoolmistress and her sister the apothecary, both gray-haired, both gentle, both possessed of that particular patience that comes from having outlived the need to impress anyone. They had taken her in February of the worst year, when the letter was still traveling through the machinery of justice and Oskar was keeping her in a safehouse above a chandlery, teaching her card games of flexible morality. When the arrests came, the Marchmonts had walked into the intake office and asked, in the way of people who expect to be obeyed, whether there happened to be a child needing a home.

There happened to be one. She happened to be the most important child in the city. Nobody at the intake office knew this except Wren, me, and a smuggler who had vanished toward the coast with a purse of thanks and, I am told, a new boat named *the Forgetful Widow*, which everyone agreed was in questionable taste and exactly right.

Edith Marchmont knew it too. We had talked about it once, sitting in the clock tower where I'd taught her the rhythms of the gears, her legs swinging over the drop while patrols passed below us, back when passing below us was a thing to survive rather than a thing to laugh about.

"Do I have to be somebody else now?" she'd asked.

"You already are," I said. "That's the trick they never understood. You can be Edith Marchmont all day, every day, at school and at church and whenever Mrs. Marchmont makes you eat the boiled mutton. And underneath, you're still you. The name goes on top. It doesn't go *instead*."

She'd considered this with the gravity of a magistrate reviewing a case. "Like a watch case," she said. "The movement doesn't change because the case is different."

Out of the mouths of six-year-olds. Out of the mouths of seven-year-olds, now, which was worse, because seven-year-olds use your own metaphors back at you with interest.

So: Tuesday. Chandler's Row School, eleven o'clock, for the monthly winding of the observatory's little sister — the school kept a regulator clock, a fine old thing, donated decades ago by some astronomer with more money than heirs, and the children took turns winding it as a lesson in responsibility, which mostly meant a lesson in which child could be trusted not to turn the key into a weapon or a toy or a snack.

Wren closed the shop. We hung the sign together. She wore her good coat, the gray one, and I noticed she checked the window twice before we left — an old habit, the checkpoint clerk's habit, counting the faces inside against the faces reflected. Some habits you keep because they serve you. Hers served her still; it just served different purposes now. These days when she counted faces it was because she liked them.

"I'm nervous," she admitted on the tram. "Which is absurd. It's a school."

"You're nervous because the last time you saw her in daylight, in public, without a wall between you, you were committing a crime."

"I was committing several crimes."

"You were. You were magnificent." I looked at her hands in her lap, folded like a good student's, and remembered them trembling over a stamp pad at the third checkpoint, waving me through with a signature that ended her career in the Registry and started, though neither of us knew it yet, everything else. "For what it's worth, I'm nervous too."

"Why?"

"Because a year ago I was ordered to forget her face, and tomorrow I'm going to stand in a room full of witnesses and look at it."

Wren put her hand over mine on the seat between us, and neither of us said anything else, because there was nothing else, and because the tram was full of people who had read the newspapers and might recognize my parsnip nose.

* * *

Chandler's Row School smelled of chalk, coal smoke, and boiled cabbage — the trinity of institutional childhood, unchanged since time immemorial and presumably eternal. The headmistress, Mrs. Halloway, was a tall woman built along the lines of a lighthouse, and she received us in the corridor with the wary courtesy of someone who has read the newspapers and decided that famous people are probably trouble until proven otherwise.

"Mr. Sterling. Mrs. — Ashworth, is it?" A beat. Wren's surname, borrowed for convenience, worn so long now it fit like her own gloves. "You may observe the winding. From the back of the hall. The children have prepared remarks."

"Prepared remarks," I murmured to Wren as we were escorted in. "For a clock winding."

"There are prepared remarks for everything," Wren murmured back. "You should know. You dismantled an escapement in front of armed inspectors as a stalling tactic. That's showmanship."

The hall held perhaps sixty children in rows, fidgeting with the collective discipline of a sack of cats, and at the front, beneath the regulator clock in its walnut case, stood a girl with dark hair braided by someone who loved her but not skillfully, wearing a pinafore with one knee patched and her chin lifted at the exact angle of a person about to do something important.

She didn't see us yet. Or she pretended not to, which was more sophisticated, and which made my chest hurt.

Mrs. Halloway introduced the exercise. The regulator, she explained, was the school's most precious object; it kept the time by which the whole street set its clocks; and it required winding once a month, on the first Tuesday, and this month the honor fell to — a pause, a card consulted with theatrical gravity —

"Edith Marchmont."

My heart, which has been stopped, beaten, and dropped down a coal chute in its time, performed a maneuver I can only describe as a stumble-and-recover, like a novice on a staircase.

Edith walked to the front. Took the key from the hook. Turned to face the room.

And then, before winding anything, she delivered the prepared remarks.

"The regulator clock," she announced, in the carrying voice of a child who has rehearsed in front of a mirror, "works by an escapement, which lets the gears move a little bit at a time instead of all at once, because if they moved all at once it would just be falling, not telling time." A rustle of approval from the front rows. Somewhere a teacher beamed. "Master Vane always said—" she corrected herself, smoothly, deliberately, and I saw the flicker in her eyes as she did it, saw the case open and the movement glance out — "*somebody* clever always said that a clock is just a machine for making time behave. Thank you."

She wound the clock. Three turns, steady, unhurried, the key fitting her small hand the way keys fit hands in every generation, and the click-click-click of the ratchet went around the silent hall like a heartbeat everyone could hear.

Then she looked up. Found me at the back of the room, standing beside Wren in our good coats, invisible to nobody.

And waved.

I waved back.

Understand what that means. Understand what it costs to write it plainly, because plainness was never my language. For twenty-two years I lived by three rules: no names spoken aloud, no eyes held too long, no faces remembered. Master Vane taught me that invisibility was survival, and he was right, and it nearly killed me anyway, and the rule I broke first and last and always was the face. I was ordered to forget hers — *FORGET THE FACE*, pinned to a letter with a seal I should never have touched — and I did not forget it, not for one hour, not asleep, not bleeding on the observatory floor while Magistrate Corvane explained to me, in the reasonable tone of a man discussing inventory, that children were line items and line items did not have faces at all.

She survived. She got a new name, a warm house, a bad braid, a patch on her knee. The state wrote her down as Edith Marchmont, aged seven, ward of Bellwether Lane, and for once — for the first time in this whole crooked city's history of writing things down — the ledger told something close to the truth, because the truth was never the name. The truth was the girl doing the naming-back. The truth was what one refuses to surrender, and she had refused, at six, with more spine than the entire Registry, and I had refused alongside her, clumsily, at cost, and here we both were, alive on a Tuesday, waving across a school hall like ordinary people.

He does not look away. That was the old rule, the deepest one: *no eyes held too long.* I held hers. I let her hold mine. I let every lighthouse-built headmistress and every fidgeting child in Chandler's Row see a grown man's face go soft over a girl with a clock key, and I did not care, because caring was the whole point, because visibility was the whole point, because I had registered myself under my true name eleven months ago with my hand shaking only a little, and the world had declined, remarkably, to end.

Afterward there was the business of being presented. Mrs. Halloway marched us up front — "children, this is Mr. Sterling, the horologist from the newspapers, and his wife" — and sixty children looked at my nose and confirmed, visibly, that yes, it resembled a parsnip. Edith shook my hand formally, Mrs. Marchmont-style, and then ruined the formality entirely by whispering, "Your coat's nicer than your newspaper drawing."

"So is everything," I whispered back. "How's the mutton?"

"Still boiled."

"Some things resist reform."

Wren crouched to her level, and the two of them had a conversation in the shorthand of people who have shared a hiding place: a look, a squeeze of hands, a question about the Marchmonts' cat answered with grave detail. Then Edith turned to the clock, freshly wound, its pendulum swinging with the smug steadiness of a mechanism that knows it will outlive everyone in the room, and asked, casually, terribly casually:

"Are you still fixing things? At the shop?"

"Every day."

"Good." She nodded once, satisfied, a magistrate closing a file. "Somebody has to."

* * *

We stayed for cake. There is always cake, at these things; childhood is administered in cake. Wren sat with the teachers and answered questions about the Registry with the careful diplomacy of a defector — no, she did not miss it; yes, the ledgers really were that bad; no, she couldn't say more, some habits being permanent. I stood by the regulator with a slice of seed cake I had no intention of eating and listened to the case tick, and diagnosed, purely out of habit, a slight dryness in the second-wheel pivot that would want attention within the year.

I left a card with Mrs. Halloway. *Sterling & Ashworth — happy to service the school's clock, no charge, any first Tuesday.* She read it, looked at me, looked at Edith, and something moved behind the lighthouse beams — understanding, or near enough.

"That was well done," she said simply. "All of it. What you did." Then, because English headmistresses are constitutionally incapable of ending on sentiment: "The cake is going fast. Take another piece for your wife, she's been talking for twenty minutes and hasn't had any."

* * *

We walked home through the late afternoon, the light coming down long and gold across the rooftops, the river running the color of hammered tin. On the bridge — not the checkpoint bridge; the bridges had been checkpoints once, all of them, and now were only bridges, which still struck me as miraculous some days — Wren slipped her arm through mine.

"She called you 'somebody,'" Wren said. "From the podium. *Somebody clever.*"

"She almost said Vane's name."

"She said yours instead. Whatever name it actually was in her head." Wren smiled at the water. "That's a eulogy and a christening in the same sentence. That girl is going to be dangerous."

"She's going to be a magistrate," I said, "and God help the city, because she'll be a fair one, and fair people are exhausting."

We came home as the lamps were lit. The workshop sign caught the lamplight — STERLING & ASHWORTH, freshly repainted since summer, when a journalist had pried loose a chip of the old letters for a souvenir and I'd decided that if the city was going to keep pieces of my sign, my sign might as well be worth keeping. Inside, the benches waited, the trays of gears lay sorted, the great regulator I was building for the Exchange Hall ticked softly to itself in the corner, three hundred parts behaving, one escapement letting time through a little at a time instead of all at once.

Wren hung her coat. I banked the fire. And on the bench by the window, where I do my finest work and my worst worrying, sat the small parcel that had come by afternoon post: a pocket watch, returned for repair, sent from a chandler's office on the coast.

I opened the note inside.

*Master Sterling — The watch runs slow. Enclosed find payment in advance. Also enclosed, because I know you'll want it: news that the *Forgetful Widow* is for sale cheap, and a reminder that a certain debt runs both directions, and that some old friends will be in the city the first week of autumn, and would consider it a professional courtesy if a certain clockman could tell them exactly how the Registry's new archive vault locks work. No hurry. — O.*

Below the signature, in different ink, smaller, added later like an afterthought that wasn't one:

*Tell Edith the cat sends regards. She'll know which one.*

Wren read it over my shoulder. We looked at each other in the lamplight, and I confess we both laughed — tired, delighted, resigned laughter, the laughter of people who know that a repaired world, like a repaired clock, requires regular winding, and that somebody has to do it.

"Well," said Wren, reaching for her pen. "Autumn is four months away. Shall we start planning now, or be irresponsible about it?"

I set Oskar's slow watch on the bench, opened the caseback, and looked at the movement — a good one, honest, worth saving, like most things that arrive broken.

"Irresponsible," I said. "But only until Tuesday. Somebody has to wind the school clock."
