## 1. Letters Before the Seal

The letters arrived on the morning post like weather — inevitable, indifferent, and occasionally destructive.

Elodie Almeida had learned to read them before Marguerite Voss did, and she had learned it the way one learns most useful things in service: by accident, by necessity, and without anyone ever quite admitting it was happening. It had begun three years ago, when a letter had arrived addressed in a hand that made Marguerite go white as milk and retire to her room with a headache that lasted two days. Elodie had taken one look at her mistress's face and understood that some letters were not correspondence at all. They were artillery. And Marguerite Voss — suffragist, widow, owner of the second motorcar in the county and the first in the village to frighten the horses properly — had no one to stand between her and the artillery but a chauffeur who happened to read very fast.

So it became the arrangement, unspoken and therefore unbreakable. Elodie collected the post from the box at the lane's end at half past seven, walked it back up the gravel drive with the letters face-down in a leather satchel, and in the morning room, while the kettle heated and Marguerite buttered her toast with the concentration of a woman defusing something, Elodie slit each envelope with the bone-handled knife kept for the purpose and read.

Not all of them. That was the fiction, anyway, and both women maintained it with the tenderness people maintain a fiction they cannot live without. Elodie read the dangerous ones — the ones with the heavy official crests, the ones in handwriting that shook, the ones that smelled faintly of a man's tobacco and a man's certainty. The rest she passed along unopened, or nearly. She had a rule about it. She was rather proud of the rule. *A letter is a door,* she told herself. *I am a locksmith, not a burglar. I open what must be opened and I look at nothing I don't have to.*

The rule had held for three years. She expected it to hold for thirty.

"You've made that face," Marguerite said that particular Tuesday in September, without looking up from her toast. "The one where you've read something and decided I shouldn't."

"I haven't read anything."

"You've read everything. You always have. You have the posture of a woman holding a letter at arm's length." Marguerite was fifty-one and built like a well-made chair — square, unyielding, upholstered in tweed — and she had the particular gift of seeing through people while appearing to look at the marmalade. "Is it the Home Office again?"

"It's the county," Elodie said, and passed it across. "The crest has more gilt on it than usual. I expect they're proud of whatever's inside."

Marguerite opened it, read it in the time it took Elodie to pour two cups, and set it down with a small, precise motion, the way she set down anything she intended to deal with later, or never. "Magistrate Harrow's office. He wishes to remind me that public assemblies on the village green require his sanction, and that his sanction will not be forthcoming."

"Will you hold the assembly anyway?"

"I will hold it in a different village, which is the same thing but with better scenery." She buttered more toast. "You see how history is made, Elodie? Not with trumpets. With a man's gilt-edged paper and a woman's decision to be elsewhere."

"I wouldn't know," Elodie said. "I'm not in history. I'm in the motorcar."

Marguerite looked at her then — that long, level look that Elodie had learned to bear the way one bears cold water, by breathing steadily through it. "You are the most historical person in this house and you are the only one who doesn't know it."

"I drive," Elodie said. "History can walk."

* * *

The morning proceeded as mornings did. Marguerite went to her desk and wrote letters that Elodie would later seal, and which would travel to London carrying arguments about the franchise written in a hand so elegant that hostile MPs reportedly read them twice just to admire the penmanship before voting no. Elodie went out to the motorcar, which was a Humber and which she loved with a devotion she found embarrassing and therefore never mentioned.

Her father had been a mechanic in the Portuguese quarter of Bristol — a small, quick man who smelled of oil and could make an engine confess anything. He had taught her hands before he taught her much else. *The machine doesn't lie,* he used to say, wiping his fingers on a rag that had long ago stopped being a rag. *People lie. Engines only tell you the truth slowly, if you're patient.* He had died when she was nineteen, and she had taken the first position she could find that let her keep her hands in an engine, which was this one, and she had expected to stay two years.

That had been five years ago.

She lifted the bonnet and listened to the Humber the way other people listened to weather. Something in the carburettor was sulking — nothing serious, a mood rather than a malady — and she was elbow-deep in persuasion when the kitchen girl, Tilly, came down the drive at a trot with her apron full of post that had arrived on the second delivery.

"Miss Voss said bring it straight to you," Tilly panted, "and she said—" the girl paused, enjoying her role as messenger, "—she said, *have Elodie look before I do, there's a dear.*"

"Did she now."

"She did." Tilly hovered, incurious in the way of no one under twenty. "Is it true you read all her letters?"

"I read the envelopes," Elodie said. "The envelopes tell you a great deal."

"What do they tell you?"

"Who's afraid of her," Elodie said, "and who she should be afraid of." She took the letters. There were four. A catalogue. A bill from the ironmonger. A note from the vicar, which she passed over unopened because the vicar's envelopes told you only that he was lonely. And the fourth — heavy paper, no crest at all, which was in its way more alarming than a crest, because official men liked their crests and it took a particular confidence to write on good paper without one.

The hand was cramped, hurried, pressed hard into the page. The postmark smudged. The address had been written twice, the second attempt over the first, as though the writer had not trusted the post to find its way and had wanted to help it.

Elodie stood in the drive with the engine ticking and the September light coming down through the chestnuts, and she did the thing she had done three hundred times. She slit it open.

Inside was half a page, and the half page said: *They know I know. If anything happens, it was Harrow. Tell no one how this reached you. Forget the face.*

She read it twice. Then a third time, standing quite still, while a wren — an actual wren, a small brown bird — scolded her from the hedge as though it had opinions about her posture.

There was no signature. There was no explanation. There was a sentence about a face, and a name she half-knew — Harrow, the magistrate whose gilt-edged paper lay on Marguerite's desk that very moment — and a fear so dense it had soaked through the paper like water through canvas.

Elodie's first feeling, which she would be ashamed of later and would never quite manage to confess to anyone, was irritation. Genuine, domestic irritation — the kind one feels when a delivery arrives damaged, when a guest arrives early, when the well-ordered machinery of a morning develops a rattle. *I don't have time for this,* she thought, and the thought was so absurd, so magnificently beside the point, that she almost laughed. Someone, somewhere, was afraid enough of Magistrate Harrow to write *forget the face* in a hand pressed through good paper, and her response was to check her watch and note that the Humber needed taking into Ashford by two o'clock and the carburettor was still sulking.

That was the trick of her life, she understood then — or half-understood, the way you half-understand a word in a foreign language, enough to repeat it, not enough to be changed by it. She had built herself a small, clean room at the edge of other people's history, and she kept it swept, and in it she was safe, and the price of the room was that everything important happened somewhere else, to someone else, and arrived at her door only as paper — paper she could open, read, file, and seal.

She folded the letter along its original creases. She put it in her apron pocket, where it lay against her hip like a stone from a riverbed, smooth and cold and much heavier than its weight.

"Anything interesting?" Tilly asked.

"Catalogue's come," Elodie said. "Miss Voss will want the winter coats page."

She carried the rest up to the morning room. She gave Marguerite the vicar, the bill, the catalogue. She did not give her the fourth letter. She told herself this was prudence — that one did not hand a woman a stone on an empty stomach, that one waited, that one found out first what one was holding. All true, probably. But underneath the prudence, in the place where the real reasons lived, something older and quieter was speaking, and what it said was: *Not yet. Not yet. Let me have one more ordinary day.*

Marguerite took the catalogue and looked at Elodie over the top of it.

"You're holding out on me," she said pleasantly.

"I'm holding a letter," Elodie said, "which is not the same as holding out."

"Elodie."

"It's probably nothing. A wrong address. Someone's nerves." She heard her own voice doing something it had never done before — smoothing, soothing, the voice of a woman standing in front of a door. "I'll look into it."

Marguerite held her gaze a moment longer, then returned to the catalogue with the grace of a woman who had won arguments by losing them for forty years. "The car goes to Ashford at two," she said. "Constable Pike will want to chat. He always wants to chat. Be duller than usual."

"I'm never dull," Elodie said, "I'm discreet. There's a difference."

"There is indeed," said Marguerite, "and it's the whole of your character," and she turned a page, and the morning closed over the moment like water over a dropped coin.

* * *

At two o'clock Elodie drove to Ashford with the letter in her apron pocket and the carburettor finally persuaded. The road ran between orchards — it was orchard country, all of it, apples and pears and the small hard cider apples nobody ate but everybody pressed — and the trees were heavy with September, boughs bowed, ladders leaning, the air sweet and bruised-smelling where the windfalls lay. She drove with her hands easy on the wheel and her mind anything but easy, turning the letter over and over. *They know I know. Forget the face.*

Whose face? Whose knowledge? What had the writer known?

At the crossroads outside the village she passed the Bell orchard — everyone called it that still, though the Bells were years gone — and she saw that the burned corner of it had finally been cleared, the blackened trunks dragged out and stacked like bones at the field's edge. A fire had taken it, what, five years back? Six? There had been a man hanged over it. Tomas Bell. She remembered the name the way one remembers a name from a newspaper one read in a waiting room — attached to a face she had never seen, in a story that had happened, as everything happened, to other people. The village had muttered about it for a season. The village muttered about everything for a season. Then the apples came in and the muttering moved on.

She drove past the stacked black trunks without slowing, because there was no reason to slow, because it was not her story, because history was a country she had never held a passport for — she drove a motorcar in it, she read its mail, but it did not know her name.

At the Ashford checkpoint — a plank across the lane and a hut, because the county had discovered in the last year that it liked to know who went where — Constable Pike lifted the barrier himself and leaned on the running board, hat back, face full of the particular joy he took in these exchanges.

"Miss Almeida. Miss Voss's motorcar. Off to the station?"

"Off to the station."

"Anyone with you?"

"Four crates of apples and my own bad temper."

Pike laughed, delighted, and waved her through, and Elodie drove on with the letter against her hip and the afternoon opening out ahead of her, ordinary as bread.

She would remember, later, how ordinary it was. The light on the hedges. The rattle of the crates. Pike's laugh. She would remember that at the moment her life was already turning — that the letter was already a door standing open somewhere behind her — she had driven straight through the checkpoint of a man who would matter terribly, and had told him a joke, and had felt nothing at all.

She was back by four. She put the Humber away, wiped her hands, changed her apron, and came down the drive toward the house in the long gold light of early evening — and stopped.

There was a child sitting on her doorstep.

A small girl, perhaps seven, sitting very straight on the stone step with her hands in her lap and a coat buttoned to her chin, in the manner of a child who has been told to wait and is waiting with everything she has. Her face was pale and composed and utterly still, and her eyes — dark, enormous, unblinking — were fixed on Elodie with an expression no seven-year-old should have had to learn.

Elodie stood in the drive. The chestnut leaves moved overhead. Somewhere in the house, Marguerite was sealing letters, and the evening was coming down soft as wool, and the child on the step did not blink.

*Forget the face,* the letter had said.

The face, Elodie thought, with the strange floating calm of a woman standing at the edge of her own history, was looking straight at her.
