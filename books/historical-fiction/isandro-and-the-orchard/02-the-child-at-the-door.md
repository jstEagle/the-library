## 2. The Child at the Door

The sensible thing — and Elodie Almeida was, by her own careful accounting, a sensible woman — would have been to fetch Constable Pike. Found children were a constable's business. Lost parcels, stray dogs, abandoned girls: all of it went into Pike's ledger, and out of it came forms, and from the forms came arrangements, and by Tuesday the whole affair would be somebody else's.

Elodie stood on her own step in the last grey light and did not fetch anybody.

"Are you cold?" she asked, which was not the sensible thing at all, because it invited an answer.

The child considered the question with the gravity of a magistrate weighing a sentence. She was perhaps seven, small for it, with a smudge of soot along one jaw as though she had been pressed against something dirty and then forgotten there. Her coat was a man's coat cut down badly — the sleeves had been taken in with stitches that meant well and achieved little — and she held a paper-wrapped parcel against her chest the way soldiers in photographs hold their rifles: out of habit, and because letting go seemed unthinkable.

"No," said the child.

"You're shivering."

"That's different."

"It is," Elodie admitted. "Shivering is usually colder than being cold." She heard herself say it and winced internally; it was the sort of thing her mother used to say, back when her mother still had opinions to offer. The child did not wince. She appeared to file the remark away for later examination.

The wind came up the lane smelling of apples and wet stone — orchard country began a mile east, though you'd never know it from Elodie's cottage, which faced the road to Voss House like a servant facing the big house, politely and always. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Well," said Elodie. "You can't stand on my step."

Something crossed the child's face — quick, trained, gone before it could be named. Not tears. Something more practiced than tears, the look of someone who has been moved along before and knows the choreography.

"I didn't mean—" Elodie pinched the bridge of her nose. "Come inside. That is what I mean. Come inside where it isn't October."

The child came inside. She did it carefully, wiping her boots twice on the mat even though they were barely dusty, which told Elodie something. Children who had never been scolded about boots did not wipe them twice without being asked. Somebody had taught her manners under conditions where manners mattered.

*Forget the face,* said the letter in Elodie's apron pocket, and the face went past her into the parlour and sat down on the very edge of the chair nearest the door, as if ready to leave at a moment's notice or as if she had been told to sit near exits, and Elodie, who noticed things professionally, noticed both.

* * *

Elodie lit the lamp and put the kettle on because there was nothing on earth that could not be approached sideways over a cup of tea. Her cottage was small and ferociously tidy: two rooms down, two up, a garden the size of a tablecloth, and on the mantelpiece a row of objects arranged by height, which was the only extravagance of disorder she permitted herself. The child watched the room the way Elodie read letters — thoroughly, without moving her head.

"What's your name?"

A pause long enough to count to four. "Wren."

"Wren what?"

Another pause. This one had a different texture. "Just Wren."

"Just Wren," Elodie repeated. "Well. I'm Miss Almeida. Elodie Almeida. I drive a motorcar for Mrs. Voss at the big house, and I read her post, and I am very good at mending things, and none of those facts explain why you are sitting in my parlour at half past eight on a Thursday."

Wren received this inventory without comment. Her eyes had gone to the mantel, to the tallest object on it — a brass telescope, purely ornamental, a gift from Marguerite three birthdays ago on the grounds that everyone should own something they couldn't afford.

"Would you like bread?"

"Yes please."

Bread, butter, and a scrape of honey bought the silence Elodie needed. She watched the child eat with a hunger that was polite about it — small bites, no crumbs wasted, the plate tilted at the end to catch the honey that ran — and felt the first cold thread of something that was not yet fear but knew its address.

Because here was the shape of the evening, and Elodie had spent eleven years reading other people's correspondence and so could not help but see shapes: nobody leaves a child on a step by accident. Somebody had walked this girl up the lane, past the church, past Pike's window, in the blue hour when the village lamps were lit and honest people were indoors. Somebody had chosen Elodie's door out of every door in Kent. And somebody had left a letter that said *forget the face*, which was not the sort of sentence one wrote to arrange the care of an orphan, unless one wanted the orphan's care arranged very quietly indeed.

The parcel was still clutched against Wren's chest. She had eaten around it, one-armed, without once setting it down.

"That looks heavy," Elodie said lightly. "Shall I hold it while you finish?"

"No."

"All right." Elodie poured the tea. "It's yours. Nobody here will touch what's yours."

She meant it kindly. It landed strangely. Wren looked up at her with an expression too old for the face it occupied — a flicker of assessment, almost suspicion, as if *nobody will touch what's yours* were a phrase she'd been given before by people who hadn't meant it.

They drank their tea. The clock on the shelf ticked with the smugness of clocks. Outside, full dark settled in, and Elodie became aware of how far her cottage sat from any other light, and of how loudly the stairs creaked, and of the fact that she had never once, in six years of living alone, turned the key in the front door, and that tonight she wanted to.

Fear, she told herself firmly, was for people whose lives contained material. Hers did not. Her life contained petrol orders, Marguerite's correspondence, the quarterly accounts, and the small ongoing comedy of Mrs. Hobbes next door's feud with the baker. History happened to other people, in cities, in newspapers. It arrived printed. It did not arrive seven years old, wearing a dead man's coat.

"Where is whoever brought you?" Elodie asked, as gently as she could.

Wren set down her cup with great exactness. "He said walk to the light. The second light. And don't stop even if people call."

"He."

Wren's mouth closed like a purse-string. Elodie had interrogated nothing in her life larger than a delivery discrepancy, but she recognized a locked drawer when she saw one.

"And he gave you a letter," Elodie said. "Which I have. Which says — and I'll read it to you exactly, because you deserve to know what's written about you — 'Forget the face. Keep her fed. Burn this.'"

"You won't burn it," Wren said.

Elodie blinked. "No," she agreed. "I won't. How could you know that?"

"Because you read everything." Wren said it without accusation, flat as a weather report. "Mrs. Voss reads everything. Everybody says."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Elodie sat very still and felt, distinctly, the sensation of a door she had believed private swinging open onto a corridor of strangers who had all, apparently, been walking through it for years. Her employment had one unspoken condition, and it was known. In the shops. At the crossroads. *Everybody says.*

"Well," she said at last, with a brightness she did not feel, "then we understand each other better than most of my acquaintances do."

* * *

The child washed her own cup. Of course she did. Then she stood by the parlour door in the manner of someone waiting to be told where sleep happens, and Elodie, feeling her way through the evening as through fog, said, "There's a bed upstairs. Mine. You'll have it. I'll take the settle."

"I've slept on floors."

"I haven't," said Elodie, "and one of us should keep her standards."

Almost — *almost* — the child smiled. It was a small movement, quickly arrested, the ghost of a smile rather than the article, but it rearranged the soot-smudged face entirely, and Elodie felt something turn over in her chest with an audible internal creak, like floorboards under an unexpected weight.

Upstairs, the ritual of undressing revealed the coat's true condition: the lining torn at the left shoulder, restitched clumsily, the stitching recent. Wren surrendered the coat with visible reluctance and kept the parcel in bed with her, wrapped in her arms, so that lying there she looked like a small boat carrying one precious, awkward cargo.

Elodie knelt beside the bed with a candle, ostensibly to fix the sleeve-stitching, which genuinely offended her.

"Hold still. This seam's a disgrace."

"It's Papa's coat."

The words fell into the room and lay there. Elodie's needle stopped.

"Is he coming for you?" she asked. It was the obvious question. It was the question any sensible woman would ask.

Wren was quiet for so long that Elodie thought she'd slept. When the answer came it was barely above the candle-flame's whisper.

"He's dead."

"I'm sorry."

"They hanged him." The child said it the way other children reported the weather, or what they'd had for dinner — a fact, absorbed, filed, carried. "In spring. Before the apples flowered. He liked it when they flowered."

Elodie's hands, which had threaded buttons and patched chauffeur's gloves through eleven uneventful years, shook once, badly, and she covered it by biting her thread. She finished the shoulder seam in silence. The candle guttered. Downstairs the clock counted the house's heartbeats, and outside, far off, an owl called across the dark fields, and Elodie Almeida sat on the edge of a stranger's bed with a dead man's coat in her lap and understood, with the slow certainty of ice forming, that history had not happened elsewhere at all. It had simply been waiting for her address.

"Sleep," she said softly, and blew out the candle.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat at the kitchen table with the letter and the oil lamp turned low, and she was honest with herself, because dishonesty with oneself was a luxury readers of other people's secrets could not afford.

She was afraid. There. Named. She was afraid the way she had been afraid at fourteen when the bull got into the lane: a clean, cold, clarifying fear, the kind that made the world sharp-edged and the body suddenly valuable. Somebody had done a wicked thing to put this child on her step. Somebody feared whatever rode in the child's arms enough to abandon her rather than keep it. And Elodie had spent her whole adult life perfecting invisibility precisely so that such somebodies would never, ever look in her direction.

She was a chauffeur. She was a woman who drove a car and read mail and kept her mouth shut and her ledgers square. Her politics were Marguerite's politics borrowed and returned unmarked. When the suffragettes marched through Ashford two summers ago she had watched from the garage roof, admiring them the way one admired fire — from a distance, with insurance.

*Burn this,* the letter said.

Instead she took the dead man's coat from where she'd hung it, and sat with it across her knees, and turned the torn shoulder lining to the lamp.

The restitching was clumsy outside but systematic within; whoever had sewn it had sewn in haste and fear, but they had sewn *thickly*. Elodie fetched her sewing scissors, and hesitated one final moment — the last moment, though she didn't know it, in which she was the woman she had been at supper — and cut the stitches.

The lining gave up its secret the way all linings did: reluctantly, in pieces. Folded papers, eight or ten of them, thin as autumn leaves, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age or sweat. And beneath the papers, flattened small, a photograph in a pasteboard frame no bigger than a playing card.

She did not look at the photograph. Some instinct told her she would need both hands free and a steady heart for the papers, and she was right.

The topmost sheet was a deposition — she knew depositions, having typed copies of enough of Marguerite's — sworn by one Tomas Bell, orchard-keeper, of Bell's Orchard, regarding the sale of fruit to one Magistrate Harrow, and regarding certain sums Harrow had declined to pay, and regarding certain threats made thereafter in the presence of witnesses. The second sheet was a bill of sale, witnessed. The third was a letter in a hand Elodie found herself, absurdly, recognizing — she had catalogued Marguerite's entire circle's handwriting years ago, a habit she'd never confessed to anyone — and the hand belonged to none of Marguerite's circle at all. It belonged, if the signature were believed, to R. Harrow himself, and it spoke of *the Bell matter* and of *the fire being the tidy solution* and of *the assizes being arranged*.

Arranged. Like flowers. Like furniture. Like a hanging.

Elodie read all ten sheets twice. The lamp burned lower. The clock below the stairs ticked on, indifferent, and somewhere upstairs a child slept with a photograph-shaped weight in her arms, and outside the owl called again, and Elodie sat in her small tidy life with the evidence of a judicial murder spread across her kitchen table like a hand of cards she had never asked to be dealt.

The fire, said the papers. The cider-house fire in March, two men lost, and Tomas Bell tried and condemned for it in a courtroom where the magistrate before whom he stood was the very man he'd accused. A hasty trial. A purchased witness. A rope in spring, before the apples flowered.

And these papers proved it. Proved it the way arithmetic proves — dates, sums, signatures, the boring indestructible machinery of fact. Enough to hang nobody, but enough to unhang a man. Enough, if it reached the right bench, the right inquiry, the right ears in London, to crack the county of Kent open like a walnut.

Enough, also, to ruin whoever carried it. The thought arrived without drama, plain as a milk bill. The letter had said *forget the face*, and now she knew why. Whoever had stitched this coat had not been hiding the child from harm. They had been hiding the papers inside the child, and the child inside anonymity, and both inside the one direction no one would think to search: the doorstep of a chauffeur who believed history happened elsewhere.

Elodie folded the papers along their original creases, wrapped them in the oilcloth, and sat holding them while the lamp hissed itself toward empty.

She should burn them. The letter said so; prudence said so; eleven years of careful survival said so, in Marguerite's own voice: *the wise thing, Elodie, is never to hold what cannot be put down.* She could feed the sheets to the stove and in the morning fetch Constable Pike, and by Tuesday there would be forms, and the girl would go to the workhouse school at Ashford, and in twenty years' time some other woman might find a different parcel on a different step, because the world would go on making them, unimpeded, well supplied.

Or.

Elodie looked up the dark stairwell, toward the room where the daughter of a hanged man slept in her bed.

Or she could do the other thing. The unthinkable, unsensible, entirely unprecedented thing. She could carry this. Past whom, to where, at what cost — she didn't know yet. But she could feel the shape of it already, the way one feels weather coming in a healed bone: nine days, some instinct whispered, though she could not have said whence the number came; a journey south; checkpoints; the whole fat complacent apparatus of the county standing between these papers and the truth they told.

"History," Elodie said aloud to the empty kitchen, and her voice sounded strange to her, younger, "happens to other people."

The stove ticked. The owl had gone. From upstairs came no sound at all, and somehow that was worse — the silence of a child sleeping the way only children sleep after the worst has already happened, trusting the house above her to hold.

Elodie rose, wrapped the oilcloth bundle in a spare pillowcase, and put it not under a floorboard, nor in the coal shed, nor anywhere clever — cleverness could be searched for — but in the bottom of her workbasket, under the darning, where a woman's mending was the one thing in England no man would think to rummage.

Then she climbed the stairs on noiseless feet and stood in the doorway looking at the small shape beneath her quilt, at the parcel still hugged fast even in sleep, at the soot-smudged face turned toward the door.

*Forget the face.*

"No," Elodie whispered to the dark. "I don't think I shall."

Below her, in the basket, the dead man's truth waited out the night. Above the orchard country the stars stood in their courses, indifferent as magistrates. And nine days — though she would not learn the number until morning, until the newspaper she'd have cause to read differently than she'd ever read anything — nine days was exactly as long as she had.
