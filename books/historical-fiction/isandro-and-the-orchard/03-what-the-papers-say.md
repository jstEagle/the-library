## 3. What the Papers Say

The child slept like the dead, which was more than could be said for me.

I had put her in my own bed — the narrow one under the eaves of the coach-house, where I'd lived since taking the position at Voss House three years prior — and she had burrowed into the quilt like a badger into a hedge bank and gone out entirely. No nightmares. No questions. Just a small, snoring certainty that whatever the world intended to do next, it would do it without her assistance.

I envied her enormously.

It was past two in the morning. The kitchen of the coach-house was cold, and I had lit only one lamp because light, I had decided, invited trouble. I sat at my little table with the bundle from her coat lining in front of me, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string, looking for all the world like something a fishmonger had lost.

"Forget the face," the note had said.

Well. I had not forgotten the face. The face was asleep eleven feet above my head, and I suspected that forgetting it would require a kind of moral surgery I was not equipped to perform. But there was a difference between not forgetting a face and reading a stranger's papers, and I stood on that difference for the better part of twenty minutes, telling myself sensible things. That the documents were none of my affair. That in the morning I would take the whole business — child, note, bundle, all of it — to Mrs. Voss, whose judgment was sounder than mine and whose conscience, unlike mine, had been properly exercised by years of causes. That my duty extended precisely as far as the drive and no farther.

I am pleased to report that I believed every word of this for almost a quarter of an hour, which for me is a record.

The trouble with sensible resolutions is that they require an absence of imagination, and I have always had too much of the stuff. So I sat there imagining: imagining the child's mother or father or whoever had carried her through dark lanes to my door, imagining the hands that had sewn this bundle into a coat lining with stitches so careful they must have been made by lamplight, by someone who knew that what was inside mattered more than anything else in the world. And imagining, finally, that whoever those hands belonged to, they had chosen *me* — not Mrs. Voss at the great house with her committees and her lawyers, but me, Elodie Almeida, chauffeur, a person whose chief qualification for receiving desperate trusts was that nobody ever noticed her.

That last thought did the damage. I untied the string.

* * *

There were perhaps thirty pages. Some were written in a clerk's fine hand; others in a rounder, less certain script, as though the writer had learned penmanship late and held a grudge against it. There were letters, copies of letters, a page torn from a ledger, and at the bottom of the pile — folded twice, soft with handling — a small newspaper clipping, its print faded to the colour of weak tea.

I began at the top, as one does, and worked down, and by the time I reached the clipping my tea — untouched since supper — had gone stone cold beside me, and my hands were not entirely steady, and the single lamp seemed too much light after all.

Here is what the papers said. Not the newspaper — the papers in the bundle, though as you will learn, the newspaper agreed.

They said that in the autumn of 1911, the Bell orchard on the far side of Hallowmere had burned. Forty acres of cider apples, two hundred years in the growing, gone in a night. They said that Tomas Bell, who kept the orchard, was arrested within the week; that he was tried before the county sessions in December; that Magistrate Edmund Harrow presided; that Tomas Bell was convicted of arson and malicious destruction, and hanged at Maidstone Gaol the following March.

That part I knew. Everyone in the county knew it. It had been a famous fire — you could see the glow of it from the ridge road, my first employer's cook used to say, and she had seen it, and crossed herself, and gone back to her pastry. The hanging had been quieter news. Orchard-keepers are not fashionable martyrs. A paragraph in the *Kentish Gazette*, a shrug from the county, done.

But the rest of the papers said other things. They said that the fire had begun in the east field, where Tomas Bell could not have set it, because Tomas Bell — this according to a statement sworn before a justice in Rye, witnessed and sealed — had been in Rye that night, selling his season's cider, and had not left until morning. They said that the witness against him, one Silas Crane, a man of no fixed address and considerable debt, had settled four pounds of outstanding obligation at the Hartlip Arms *three days after* giving his testimony — and here was the ledger page, in the publican's own hand, marking the debt paid by "E.H., per arrangement."

*Per arrangement.*

They said — in a letter, undated but referencing the trial, signed only with an initial — that Magistrate Harrow had reason to want the Bells off their land, because the Bell orchard bounded the Harrow estate on its western side, and a syndicate of Hop Exchange men wanted the acreage, and Harrow had already taken their money for it. There was even a copy of the option agreement, the land priced absurdly low, conditional — God help us — upon "clear title unencumbered by present tenancy."

And then there was the letter in the rounder hand. The uncertain hand. It took me longer to read, not because the writing was poor but because I kept having to stop and press my palms flat against the table.

It was from Tomas Bell himself. Written from Maidstone Gaol — the number of his cell was stamped at the top — and addressed to *whosoever shall find these papers.* He wrote plainly, without flourish, the way men write when they have given up on being believed and intend only to be understood. He wrote that he had never set fire to his own orchard, that he had loved those trees "like relations, and quarrelled with them oftener." He wrote that he knew why Harrow had chosen him, though he did not say the reason outright, which told me he feared for someone still living. He wrote that he had entrusted copies of everything to a friend whose name he would not set down, and that if these papers came into honest hands —

And then he wrote the sentence I will carry to my grave, though I did not yet know whose grave it would be:

*I do not ask to be saved. I ask only that my girl should one day know her father died honest, whatever the law said, and that somebody somewhere said so out loud.*

My girl.

I looked at the clipping last, because it had slipped to the bottom and because some instinct in me already knew what it would be. The *Gazette*, March 1912. Two inches of type. *BELL, Thomas, orchard-keeper, of Hallowmere, executed this morning for the crime of arson.* And beneath it, smaller, the sort of detail a sub-editor includes to fill a column: *The deceased is survived by a daughter, aged five, now a ward of the parish.*

Aged five.

Two years ago.

Which made the child upstairs seven, which matched, which meant —

I put my hand over my mouth, which is a thing I have seen women do in melodramas and always considered silly, and there I was doing it myself in a cold kitchen at half past two in the morning.

She was not somebody's daughter sent away for her health. She was not a niece, a ward, a mistake in delivery. She was Wren Bell, if that was her name, daughter of a hanged man, carrying in her coat the proof that the law of England had murdered her father so that a magistrate might sell forty acres of apple trees to his friends.

* * *

I did not sleep. I want that on the record, because later — much later — people would ask how I had come to make the choices I made, and I would like history (which had, until that night, concerned itself with other people) to know that I made them with my eyes open and my nerves shot.

I went up the stairs maybe six times. Each time I told myself I was only checking the lamp risk, or whether the child had kicked her covers off, and each time I stood in the doorway listening to her breathe and thought: *you are the entire contents of your father's hope, packed into a coat with crooked buttons.*

Somewhere around four o'clock, she woke. Not fully — she turned in the bed, and her eyes opened halfway, and she looked at me standing foolishly in the door with the lamp behind me.

"You're the motor lady," she said.

"I'm the motor lady," I agreed.

"Da said motors frighten the horses but horses oughtn't to be such cowards about it." Her eyes closed again. Then, from the pillow, muffled: "He said you'd keep the papers safe. He said the lady with the motor would know what to do with them."

"Did he," I managed.

"Mm." A long pause. Her breathing slowed. And then, right at the edge of sleep, in the flat, matter-of-fact voice children reserve for the things that are truest: "Da's dead, you know. They hanged him. But he wasn't a burner of things."

"I know," I whispered, to someone already asleep.

* * *

Morning arrived grey and businesslike, entirely indifferent to the fact that my life had been rearranged overnight. I fed the child porridge — she ate like a stevedore, which reconciled me to her somewhat — and installed her in the coach-house with strict instructions regarding the hiding of bundles, and drove up to the house as I did every morning of my working life, and took up my post as though nothing had happened, because nothing was allowed to have happened.

Mrs. Voss took her breakfast at nine. Marguerite Voss was sixty-one years old, built like a churchwarden, and possessed of the finest mind for strategy in three counties, a fact she disguised beneath cardigans and a manner of mild distraction so convincing that cabinet ministers had underestimated her at their considerable cost. She read everything. Letters, newspapers, the marginalia of other people's pamphlets. Nothing entered or left Voss House unread — not by her, anyway. Officially I was her chauffeur. Unofficially, I was the person who opened the post, sorted it, read it aloud to her over the marmalade, and burned what needed burning. Three years of other people's secrets had taught me two things: that everyone lies in correspondence eventually, and that I had a talent for keeping still.

"Elodie," she said, not looking up from the *Times*. "You've read the post."

"There's nothing requiring your attention before luncheon. A circular from the Federation. Your cousin Arthur wants money again."

"He always does. Give him twenty and tell him it's fifteen." She turned a page. Then she looked up, and I felt the look go through me like a beam through fog. "And you've read something else."

It was not a question. Marguerite Voss did not ask questions she had answered herself.

"A letter came late," I said. "By hand. Last night."

"Indeed." She set down the paper and folded her hands, and waited. That was the thing about Marguerite: she could wait the way water waits on stone.

"It's not mine to discuss," I said.

"Nothing is anyone's to discuss. Discussion is merely the tax we pay on knowing things." She smiled — the mild, cardigan smile. "You've gone pale, Elodie. You've also lied to me, which you've never done, and done it badly, which suggests you're new to it. Sit down."

I did not sit. I stood by the sideboard with the silver behind me catching the morning light, and I thought about the oath of silence I had never actually taken but had honoured for three years, and about a cell number stamped at the top of a dead man's letter, and about a child eating porridge like a stevedore in my coach-house.

"If I tell you," I said, "it stops being mine to carry alone. And I don't yet know whether that's a relief or a theft."

Marguerite's eyebrows rose a fraction — genuine surprise, a rare coin in that house. "How interesting," she said softly. "Most people assume that sharing a burden is always the generous act." She poured tea she did not drink and studied me across it. "Tell me this instead, then, if you won't tell me the rest. Is anyone in danger?"

"Yes."

"Is it urgent?"

"Yes."

"Does it concern anyone in this county?"

I hesitated one heartbeat too long, and she saw it, and something moved behind her eyes — something old and quick and frightened, there and gone like a bird crossing a window.

"No," said Marguerite, mostly to herself, and her hands, folded on the table, tightened very slightly around each other. "No, I don't suppose it does."

She dismissed me then — kindly enough, but firmly, the way one dismisses a topic rather than a person. I drove the motorcar around to the front as usual, and checked the tyres as usual, and walked back through the kitchen garden as usual, and it was not until I was halfway down the yew walk that I realised what had happened in that bright breakfast room.

I had asked my employer for help. And my employer — Marguerite Voss, who had never once in three years refused help to any cause under heaven — had looked at my question the way one looks at a snake in a boot, and had changed the subject.

The child was waiting for me at the coach-house door, the bundle hugged to her chest despite my instructions, her chin set at an angle I would come to know very well indeed.

"We're going to London, aren't we," she said. It was not quite a question, and it was not quite a child speaking. It was Tomas Bell's round, stubborn hand, writing its next page.

"Wren," I said — for I had got her name at last, out of her, over porridge, along with the fact that she was seven and disliked raisins — "it's nine days until the tribunal sits, and three checkpoints between here and it, and every one of them is held by the men those papers would hang. I haven't said yes. I haven't said anything."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate.

"But you've read them," she said.

Yes. I had read them.

"And Da said you'd know what to do." She pushed the bundle into my hands, turned on her heel, and marched inside to see whether my cupboard contained biscuits, leaving me alone in the yard with thirty pages of a murdered man's truth and the distinct, vertiginous understanding that the decision had been made for me — by a dead orchard-keeper, a seven-year-old, and my own inconvenient heart — sometime around two o'clock that morning, the moment the string came off the oilcloth.

History, I had always maintained, happened to other people.

Other people, apparently, had been maintaining the same thing about me.
