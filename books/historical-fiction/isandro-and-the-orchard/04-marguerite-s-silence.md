## 4. Marguerite's Silence

Marguerite Voss had a theory about breakfast, which was that it ought to be conducted like a military campaign — swift, decisive, and without sentiment. She took her toast dry, her coffee black, and her newspaper folded into quarters so that she could read it like a general consulting a map of enemy territory. On the morning after the child had appeared on Elodie's doorstep, Marguerite read the paper for eleven minutes, drank half her coffee, and then set both down with the particular stillness of a woman who has heard something that was never said aloud.

"Elodie," she said, "you've moved the good silver."

Elodie, standing at the sideboard in her chauffeur's coat, did not immediately answer. It was true. The fish knives had been rearranged, the entree forks shifted a quarter-inch left. Marguerite noticed everything in her household the way a spider notices vibrations in a web, and Elodie had lived in that web long enough to know it.

"I dusted," Elodie said.

"You have never dusted in your life. You once told me, with some pride, that dust was a form of insulation." Marguerite turned her cup a slow half-circle on its saucer. "You've also been up since five. I heard the kitchen range. And there is a child's coat drying on the boiler in the scullery, which is either the saddest or most alarming thing I've seen this year, and 1913 has been a competitive year so far."

Elodie had prepared, in the small hours, three separate explanations. None of them survived the light of the morning room. She had known Marguerite for six years — had driven her to suffrage meetings in Kent and inquests in Surrey, had watched her face down magistrates and landowners and one memorable bishop — and she knew that lying to Marguerite Voss was not merely difficult but faintly insulting, like offering counterfeit coin to a bank.

"There's a girl," Elodie said. "About seven. She was left at my door last night."

"Left by whom?"

"I don't know. There was a note." Elodie recited it from memory, because she had read it perhaps forty times: *Forget the face.* "She doesn't speak much. Her name is Wren. And Mrs. Voss—" She stopped. In six years she had never once used the Christian name; it came out now before she could stop it, and Marguerite's eyebrows rose a fraction, which from her was the equivalent of another woman dropping her teacup entirely. Elodie pressed on. "The documents in the child's coat. They're about Tomas Bell."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like the felt lining of a jewel box.

"Tomas Bell," Marguerite repeated, "was hanged four years ago."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you know this because—"

"The papers name him. They name Harrow, too. They name the assize dates, the witnesses, the sums paid to two of them. It's all there, ma'am. It's a confession written in somebody else's hand."

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. Then she rose, crossed to the window, and drew the curtain back precisely one inch — enough to see the gravel drive, not enough to be seen looking. Elodie had watched her perform that exact gesture before every risky meeting for six years and had never until now wondered what she was looking *for*.

"Sit down, Elodie."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down anyway. If we are going to ruin ourselves, we may as well do it comfortably." When Elodie remained where she was, Marguerite almost smiled — a small, dry flicker, gone as quickly as it came. "Stubbornness. Well. You learned it somewhere."

* * *

Here is what Elodie did not know, and what Marguerite had carried for longer than either of them could conveniently count.

In the summer of 1907, when she was still Marguerite Ashcombe and the world had not yet thought to make her difficult, she had spent six weeks at her cousin's farm outside Farnham, recovering from influenza and an engagement broken off under circumstances the family still referred to as *that unpleasantness*. The farm bordered an orchard. The orchard belonged to a man named Tomas Bell.

He was not a romantic figure. That was rather the point of him. He was broad and sunburnt and he spoke slowly, as though words were apples he wanted to hand over undamaged, and he had once spent an entire afternoon teaching Marguerite how to graft a scion onto rootstock because she had asked him a question about it and he could not imagine refusing anyone a true answer. He called her *miss* for two weeks and then, abruptly, *Marguerite*, and the first time he said it she felt the word go through her like warm water through cold.

Nothing happened. That was the tragedy of it, or one of them. Nothing happened, and nothing happened, and then her cousin wrote to say her mother was coming down from London, and Marguerite went home, and married a man named Voss two years later because he was kind and dull and asked nothing of her except that she be beautiful at dinner parties, which even she could manage. She read about the fire at Bell's orchard in the newspaper. She read about the trial. She read about the hanging, in smaller type, on an inside page, between a church fete and a shipping notice, because that was the size of a man's death when the man was poor.

She had done arithmetic since. Cold, wakeful, three-o'clock-in-the-morning arithmetic. If things had been otherwise — if she had stayed a fortnight longer, been braver by a fortnight —

But history, Marguerite had long ago concluded, is a machine for turning might-have-beens into ash, and she had built her whole life since on the principle that feeling was fuel and action was engine, and never the two should meet without a purpose. So she had joined the cause. She had marched and been jailed and force-fed and come out thinner and harder and useful. And when a letter had arrived, three years ago, unsigned, containing half of what now lay folded in a child's coat lining, she had read it twice and burned it in the drawing-room grate, and told no one, and sent money through an intermediary to a woman named Bell in Farnham, and called it conscience, and slept badly, and told herself that sleeping badly was a form of payment.

It was not. She had known it was not. That was the trouble with being honest with oneself: it was a habit, once acquired, that spread everywhere.

* * *

"A tribunal," Marguerite said now, carefully. "You want to take these documents to the tribunal sitting in London."

"In nine days. Yes."

"And how exactly do you propose to get them there? By post? By carrier? Carried in your own hands through three counties with your face attached to them?"

"I'd drive. In the motorcar. Nobody stops a lady's car."

"They don't stop it," Marguerite agreed, "because they look inside it." She returned to the table and sat, folding her hands, and Elodie saw with a small shock that they were not quite steady. "Elodie. Listen to me very carefully, because I am going to say something I have never said to you, which is that I am frightened. The tribunal is not an opportunity. It is a trap — a well-dressed one, with a ribbon on it. Do you know why Harrow's people allowed a tribunal to be convened at all? Men like Harrow do not permit scrutiny. They permit theatre. The hearing will sit, hear two days of testimony about *irregularities in rural administration*, absolve everyone involved, and close. Anyone who arrives carrying accusations against a county magistrate will not be applauded. They will be noted. Names taken, addresses recorded, and if the accuser happens to be a woman with a known connection to the suffrage movement—" she let the sentence end itself, because its ending was a cell, a tube, a photograph in the papers under the headline UNRULY WOMEN.

"Then the evidence needs to reach someone outside the tribunal," Elodie said. "A newspaper. An MP."

"With what guarantee it's believed? Forged documents are easily alleged. A servant girl turns up with a folder full of scandal concerning a respectable magistrate — Elodie, they will eat you. Not loudly. Quietly, administratively, the way this country eats people. There won't be a moment you can point to afterwards and say *there, that's where it went wrong.*"

For a while neither of them spoke. Out on the drive, a jackdaw was quarrelling with the gravel, hurling stones about with the conviction that one of them must eventually be a worm.

"You knew," Elodie said quietly. "When I mentioned his name. You didn't ask who Tomas Bell was."

Marguerite's hands unfolded. Refolded. "I knew of him."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Marguerite said. "It isn't." And there it was again, the fracture — Elodie felt it open beneath them both like ice shifting underfoot, the thin bright crack between the woman she worked for and the woman she was only now meeting. Six years of reading other people's letters together, of the comfortable conspiracy of their silence, and underneath it all this buried thing, humming like a wire.

"How long have you known the Bell name, ma'am?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters if you tell me to burn those papers. It matters a great deal then."

Marguerite closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them she was, visibly, deciding something — Elodie could watch the decision happening behind her face like weather crossing a field. "I met him once," she said at last. "A summer. Longer ago than I like to count. He was—" She stopped, and Elodie understood with a jolt that Marguerite Voss, who had addressed three hundred public meetings without a tremor, could not finish this sentence. "He was a better man than any of the men who judged him. That will have to suffice."

The jackdaw gave up on the gravel and flew off, aggrieved.

"You could help me," Elodie said. "Money for the journey. A name in London, someone to receive the documents if I can't testify myself. You know everyone. You know which MPs can't be bought."

"I know precisely which MPs can't be bought. There are four of them and one is dead." Marguerite stood and walked the length of the room and back, a caged measure of eight paces, and Elodie realized she was watching the argument Marguerite was having with herself, and that it had been running, in one form or another, for years. "If I help you, I am in it. My name, my money, my motorcar. Everything Harrow's people would want is mine already. They wouldn't need to manufacture anything; they'd simply read the file." She stopped at the window again. The curtain twitched its inch. "And there's the child to consider."

That landed harder than anything else had. Elodie thought of Wren asleep upstairs in the blue room, in a nightgown borrowed from the housekeeper's daughter twenty years too big, curled small at the very edge of the mattress as though the middle of beds were not to be trusted.

"She's his daughter," Elodie said. "You must know that too. You know everything else."

"I suspected. The note said *forget the face*. One forgets documents. Faces are harder." Marguerite turned. Something in her expression had changed — settled, like sediment finding the bottom of a glass. "Elodie, I am going to say the unforgivable thing now, and I want you to remember, later, that I said it and meant it as kindness. Give me the papers. I will hold them. In two years, three, when the political weather changes, when there's a Home Secretary who owes me, they can surface safely and do their work and no child will spend her girlhood visiting her only friend in Holloway. That is the sensible course. That is the course I have recommended to braver women than either of us, and watched them refuse, and watched what happened after."

"No," Elodie said.

It came out very simply, without the trembling she'd expected. She heard herself say it again, testing it: "No. She stood on my step last night holding her father's death warrant folded up in her coat like it was a hymn book. Somebody trusted *me*, ma'am. Of all people. The woman whose great ambition in life was to remain unphotographed." Her voice did waver here, slightly. "I've spent thirty-one years believing history is something that happens in London to important people. It knocked on my door wearing one shoe. I can't put it back out on the step."

Marguerite looked at her for a long time. And Elodie, watching, saw the strangest thing: the older woman's fear did not diminish, but something else arrived alongside it, shoulder to shoulder, like reinforcements.

"You sound like him," Marguerite said softly. Then, briskly, before the sentence could be examined: "Nine days. Three checkpoints between here and that tribunal — Pike at the village boundary, the county mail inspector at Ashford, and Harrow's own clerk handling exhibits intake in London. You'll not get past the third by luck; the third is designed to catch people who got past the first two."

"So I need more than luck."

"You need a plan, money, and a miracle, in ascending order of difficulty." Marguerite pulled the small watch from her waistcoat — she wore a waistcoat, having concluded decades ago that dresses were a conspiracy against competence — and consulted it. "I have a deputation at eleven and I cannot cancel it without comment. We will speak tonight. Bring the documents down after dinner, and bring the child — she should hear whatever she can bear to hear. Children are owed the truth early; it's the only interest they collect on it."

At the door, Elodie paused. "Ma'am. Why did you really burn that letter three years ago? The first one."

Marguerite did not pretend not to understand. She smoothed the newspaper flat on the table, aligning its edges with unnecessary precision.

"Because I was a coward with excellent handwriting," she said. "Go and mind your engine, Elodie. And check the spare petrol cans — if we're doing this, we're doing it properly."

* * *

Marguerite sat alone in the morning room for a long while after the door closed, listening to the house resettle around her. On the table lay the newspaper, and beneath the newspaper, in the locked drawer of her writing desk, lay a bundle she had not opened in four years: letters, a pressed sprig of apple blossom gone brown as old lace, and a grafting knife with a worn walnut handle that she had stolen, at nineteen, with the giddy conscience of a thief who intends to return everything and returns nothing.

She had told Elodie the truth. Nearly all of it. What she had not told her — what she would carry a little longer, one day more, perhaps two — was the identity of the intermediary who had delivered that unsigned letter three years ago, and the promise he had extracted in exchange for his silence, and the fact that his name was Daniel Foy.

She unlocked the drawer, touched the blossom once, and locked it again.

Outside, the Daimler's engine turned over, caught, and settled into its low steady purr — the sound, Marguerite thought, of a decision being made in the language Elodie spoke best. Nine days. Three checkpoints. One child asleep upstairs who did not yet know that the two women downstairs were about to spend everything they had on the question of her father's name.

"Well, Tomas," Marguerite said to the empty room, and was surprised to find her voice held. "It appears we're doing it properly after all."

Then she rang the bell for more coffee, because courage was courage, but there was no sense facing it dehydrated.
