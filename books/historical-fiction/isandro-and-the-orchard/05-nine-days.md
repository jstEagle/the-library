## 5. Nine Days

The morning after the child arrived, I did what I always did when the world became unmanageable: I polished the motorcar.

There is a particular satisfaction to be had in the smell of beeswax and cold metal, in the ritual of chamois leather over bonnet, in an engine that behaves exactly as an engine ought — combustion, compression, exhaust, all of it honest and predictable and entirely without secrets. Machines do not arrive on one's doorstep at midnight wearing documents sewn into their coats. Machines have never once asked me to decide what kind of person I am before breakfast.

"You're talking to it again," said Wren from the kitchen doorway.

I had not been talking to it. I had been *explaining* things to it, which is entirely different, and anyway she was seven years old and had no standing to comment on my methods. She stood there in one of Mrs. Voss's old blouses, which hung off her like a tent pitched hastily for a very small expedition, eating bread and butter with the concentrated solemnity of a cabinet minister.

"Finish your breakfast," I said.

"I finished it."

"Then finish the crusts. Growing girls finish crusts."

"Do they?" she said, with such genuine scholarly interest that I nearly laughed, and laughing felt dangerous, like a cork popping on a bottle I could not yet afford to open.

Here is what I had learned about Wren Bell in the space of one night and one morning: that she slept curled at the very foot of a bed rather than in it, as though prepared to run; that she ate everything offered without complaint and asked for nothing; that she watched doors. Not idly — watched them the way a sailor watches weather. And that when I had said, gently, over the cold remains of supper, *your father — tell me about your father*, she had looked at me with those enormous grey eyes and said only:

"He was hanged for something he didn't do. You already read it."

She knew I'd found the papers. Of course she knew; children always know what adults are hiding, because adults are catastrophically bad at it. She had not asked me what I intended to do. She was waiting, with the terrible patience of the bereaved young, to see whether I was another person who would look away.

I intended — I told myself, buffing the bonnet with unnecessary vigour — to be sensible. Sensible meant Marguerite. Marguerite would know precisely what to do with a bundle of papers accusing Magistrate Harrow of murder by process. Marguerite knew judges and journalists and men whose names appeared in the *Times* between items about regattas. Marguerite had spent twenty years making trouble for powerful people in the most respectable possible way, and if anyone could transform a dead orchard-keeper's case into a cause célèbre without anyone hanging for it twice, it was she.

The fact that this reasoning also conveniently deferred the whole business to someone braver than myself is a thing I admit now, here, in the flat light of hindsight. At the time I called it prudence and polished harder.

* * *

Marguerite took her breakfast at nine in the morning room, and I took my place at ten past, having deposited Wren in the stillroom with a plate of biscuits and strict instructions to count them, catalogue them, and report any discrepancies. The girl had taken to inventory work with alarming enthusiasm. It kept her hands busy and her eyes off the door.

Marguerite was reading *The Suffragist* and eating a soft-boiled egg with the air of a general reviewing troop movements. I set down the tray, poured her second cup, and said, "Madam, I need to speak with you about something serious."

"Mm," she said, not looking up. "You've been calling me 'madam' since yesterday evening, which you only do when you've rehearsed a speech. Out with it, Elodie, before the egg goes cold and I become unreasonable."

So I told her. Not everything — not the note, not yet, not *Forget the face*, which sat in my breast pocket like a coal — but enough. A child on my step. Papers in her coat lining. Tomas Bell, hanged three years ago for the burning of Harrow's granary. Witness statements never taken. A confession signed in a hand that didn't match anything else in the file. Payments recorded. The name Harrow appearing where names like his should not appear.

I had expected shock. What I got instead frightened me considerably more: Marguerite set down her spoon, folded her hands, and was quiet for a long moment. And Marguerite, I will have you understand, is never quiet. Silence, in Marguerite Voss, is a full sentence.

"Tomas Bell," she said at last, softly, as one might test the weight of a stone.

"You knew him?"

"I knew *of* him." She rose, went to the window, stood with her back to me — a habit of hers when she wished to lie, though I did not yet appreciate the distinction. "Elodie, listen to me very carefully. You will burn those papers."

The words landed so far from anything I had anticipated that for a moment I simply blinked at her back.

"Madam?"

"Burn them. Tonight. And take that child somewhere — Mrs. Prewitt at the Kestrelford home takes foundlings, she asks nothing — and forget her face and her father's name." She turned then, and her face was pale and set in a way I had seen perhaps twice in six years of service: once during a forced feeding bill, once when a window came through. "This is not cowardice. This is arithmetic. Do you know what Harrow does to people who carry his sins about in their pockets? He has constables, Elodie. He has inspectors. He has the entire machinery of a county that he has spent fifteen years oiling. You are a chauffeur with strong wrists and good instincts, and I love you for both, but neither will stop a bullet or a cell door."

"The tribunal," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "There must be—"

"There *is* a tribunal." Something crossed her face then, quick as a pike under water. "The autumn assizes sit in Ashford. There's to be a review of county convictions — quietly, no fanfare, one of those committees convened to make reformers feel heard and then dissolved. It sits in nine days. Which means that even if you survived the road, even if you got past every man Harrow has bought between here and London, you would arrive to find a committee of elderly gentlemen who owe their positions to men like him, reviewing a case he personally closed." She came back to the table and took both my hands, and hers were cold. "Elodie. I have fought these men for twenty years. I am telling you as someone who has buried friends: some fires you cannot carry. You put them out. You save yourself. Burn the papers."

I withdrew my hands. I did not do it dramatically; I did it the way one removes a hand from a stove.

"You knew the tribunal sits in nine days," I said. "Before I told you."

The silence stretched. Outside, a thrush was conducting its business with more composure than either of us possessed.

"I receive correspondence," Marguerite said finally.

"You read everyone's letters. Who reads yours?"

For a moment she almost smiled. "That, my dear, is the question at the heart of my entire life." Then the shutters came down again. "Nine days, three hundred miles, and every mile watched. Burn them, Elodie. Please. That is the last time I will ask nicely, and after this I shall have to be your employer again, and neither of us wants that."

I curtsied. It is difficult to convey contempt through a curtsy, but I have been assured by persons better qualified than I that I managed it.

* * *

The stillroom smelled of lavender and dried apples, and Wren had arranged the biscuit tins in ranks with military precision. She had eaten two biscuits — she confessed this immediately, unprompted, holding up the evidence — and I told her that honesty in matters of pastry was the foundation of character.

"Did she say burn them?" Wren asked.

I stopped in the doorway. "How—"

"Grown-ups always say burn them first." She said it without bitterness, the way one reports that rain falls downward. "Miss Hartley said it too. Before they took her."

The word *they* sat between us. I crouched down to her level, which was becoming a habitual posture and would, I feared, ruin me for dignity altogether.

"Wren. Who is Miss Hartley?"

"A lady who helped Papa. She wrote letters for him because he couldn't write quick. When the papers were done — when Papa—" Her hands made a small, terrible motion. "They came for her too. She ran. I don't know where. But before she went, she sewed the coat." She touched her sleeve, where the lining lay flat and innocent. "She said if ever I couldn't keep going, find a woman who reads letters and give her the coat. Because a woman who reads other people's letters knows what people really are." She looked up at me. "Do you?"

It is a strange thing, being interrogated successfully by a person whose chin does not clear a table. I thought of Marguerite's cold hands and her folded silences. I thought of Constable Pike, who tipped his hat to me and looked through me in the same gesture. I thought of myself, six years of driving past history with my eyes politely forward, reading the whole county's correspondence and believing none of it applied to me.

"Not yet," I said honestly. "But I mean to find out."

* * *

That afternoon I did the most subversive thing available to a woman of my station: I sat down with paper and made lists.

The first list was the route. Ashford was not, as I had half-feared, a mad cross-country scramble; it was ninety-one miles by the roads I knew, the same roads I drove Marguerite twice monthly to her sister's. Ninety-one miles, and I could cover them in a long day in the Darracq if the Darracq held together, which the Darracq did with the sulky reliability of a large animal that has decided, on balance, to tolerate you.

The second list was the problem, and it was longer.

Because I had spent six years listening — truly listening, the way servants and chauffeurs and postmistresses listen, the invisible ears of England — and so I already knew, before I sat down to enumerate them, precisely who would stand between me and that courthouse. I wrote their names and, beside each, what I knew of them, which proved to be a great deal more than I had realized I carried.

**One. Constable Pike, Millbrook village.** Every vehicle leaving the valley passed his crossroads. Pike recognized cars the way farmers recognize weather; he'd known the Darracq's particular rattle since the day we bought it. But Pike was also a man of habits so rigid that his own wife reportedly checked his pockets for change of era. Habits can be predicted. Predictable men can be routed around, or spoken to, or spoken *past*. Beside his name I wrote: *knows the car. Does not search it — searches carts. Thinks in carts.*

**Two. Inspector Grieves, County Mail Examination Office, Ashford Road depot.** Here my stomach tightened, because this was the checkpoint nobody talked about but everybody knew: since the postal reforms, parcels moving by carrier between counties passed through Grieves's depot, and Grieves had a reputation for opening things. A reputation, notably, for opening things belonging to people who lacked the confidence to object. Beside his name I wrote: *searches packages, not machines. Searches the nervous.* And beneath it, after consideration: *so do not be nervous.*

**Three.** And here my pen actually hesitated on the paper, because the third checkpoint was not a man one could flatter or fool or avoid. The third checkpoint was Daniel Foy, clerk to Magistrate Harrow himself — Foy, whom I had driven past a dozen times on the courthouse steps, a thin, careful man with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of somebody perpetually apologizing for existing. Any evidence touching Harrow's own convictions would pass across Foy's desk in the ordinary course of preparation. He would read it. He would recognize it. And he owed his bread, his rooms, and very possibly his liberty to the man those papers condemned.

Beside his name I wrote nothing at all. I sat looking at the blank space for a long while, because I could not think of a single true sentence to put there.

Three checkpoints. Nine days. One motorcar, one child, one bundle of papers worth a man's life, and one employer who had said *burn them* in a voice that meant something older and sadder than caution.

* * *

I want to record honestly how fear felt that evening, because later — when people wrote about me, and they did, briefly and badly — they preferred to imagine I had been brave from the start. I was not brave from the start. That evening I was frightened in the specific, undignified way of a person doing arithmetic about prison cells. I counted my liabilities like a miser: no money beyond my wages, no family to appeal to, no name that mattered in any room where decisions were made, and a face — this I had learned early and used professionally — that people forgot within the hour. For six years invisibility had been my armour. Now the plan required me to drive ninety-one miles carrying the most visible burden in the county, and every checkpoint was a mirror.

And yet.

There was a moment — I mark it precisely, because it was the hinge of my whole life — when I went up to check on Wren before retiring and found her asleep sitting upright against the headboard, one hand inside her blouse, holding the corner of that coat lining as though it were a rosary. Even asleep, her fingers were curled around it. Whatever else happened to me, whatever cells or scandals or ruined employments waited down that road, this small person had walked out of a burned orchard with the truth sewn against her ribs and carried it, alone, until it reached my door.

History, I had always believed, happened elsewhere — to ministers and martyrs, to people whose names went in newspapers. But it had come to my doorstep wearing a child's boots with a hole in the left toe, and it had asked me, in effect, a very simple question: *are you a woman who reads letters, or a woman who reads people?*

I sat on the edge of the bed until the coals settled, and then I went downstairs and did not burn the papers.

Instead I opened the document bundle one final time, by candlelight, and studied it not as evidence but as cargo. How much did it weigh? Fourteen ounces. Could it be divided? Yes — statements separate from ledger pages, so that losing one half might preserve the other. Where on a Darracq tourer does a searching man look, and where does he not? Under the seats, obviously. Behind the cushions. In the toolbox, in the spare-tyre well, in my own pockets and hatband. But the engine housing — the tin shroud above the exhaust manifold, which required tools, patience, and burned fingers to open, and which no inspector in England would rummage while the manifold was still warm from the road—

I sat back, blowing out a breath that guttered the candle.

I was going to do this. The realization arrived not with trumpets but with the mild surprise of discovering one has been walking upstairs all along and is somehow already on the landing. I would need money — wages saved, and more than wages, which meant either Marguerite or a bank I did not possess. I would need a story for the journey, a reason a chauffeur and a small girl were bound for Ashford. I would need Wren drilled in what to say and, more importantly, in the noble art of saying nothing. I would need the Darracq serviced, the tanks filled, the engine housing bolts loosened tonight, in advance, so that roadside improvisation would not require a wrench I might not have time to find.

I made a third list. It was the longest, and by the time I finished it, dawn was greying the window and the fear had transmuted into something quieter and far more durable: schedule.

Resolve, I find, is mostly bookkeeping. Courage is simply a plan you refuse to abandon after it stops being comfortable.

* * *

At eight o'clock sharp I presented myself in the morning room with the tea tray, and Marguerite regarded me over her newspaper with the expression of a woman who has lost an argument and intends to pretend she merely postponed it.

"You didn't burn them."

"No, madam."

"You intend to go to Ashford."

"The tribunal sits in nine days, madam. Eight, now."

"And you propose to drive there with stolen court documents and a fugitive child, through three checkpoints staffed by Harrow's creatures, in a car that Constable Pike could identify blindfolded by ear alone." She folded the newspaper with a crack like small-arms fire. "Absolutely not. As your employer I forbid it. I withdraw your use of the motorcar, I withhold your references, I—"

"With respect, madam," I said, setting down the teapot, "you'll want to sign the petrol requisition before noon. Simmonds won't fill the cans without your initials."

The pause that followed deserves recording in the history of our sex. Marguerite Voss, who had faced magistrates, mobs, and the Home Secretary, stared at her chauffeur as at a card trick performed by furniture. And then — I saw it happen, the exact moment — the outrage in her face broke apart and reassembled itself into something older, wearier, and dangerously fond.

"You," she said, "are going to be the death of me."

"No, madam. I'm going to be the life of a certain set of papers, if you'll initial the requisition."

She signed it. She complained throughout — about petrol prices, about insubordination, about the ingratitude of the employed — but she signed, and when I reached the door she said, quietly, without the newspaper between us: "Elodie. If Grieves opens anything, don't argue. Arguing is for people with rights. Drive."

I thanked her. And I was halfway across the hall, already composing my route around Pike's crossroads, when Wren came pelting down the stairs with her hair half-plaited and her eyes alight, and skidded to a halt before me with the air of someone delivering urgent intelligence.

"The biscuits," she announced breathlessly. "I counted them twice. There's four missing and I know exactly who took them, and also—" her voice dropped to the thrilling whisper children reserve for genuine conspiracy — "if we're going anywhere secret, you'll need to know I can hold a horse's bridle and keep a lookout, and I never sneeze when it would be inconvenient. Papa said so."

Behind me, from the morning room, came the sound of Marguerite Voss laughing — actually laughing, helplessly, into her tea — and I took Wren's hand, and we went out together to make ready the motorcar, and the clock began.

Eight days now. Ninety-one miles. Three men between us and the truth.

I only hope, reader, that you will forgive me for what I did to poor Constable Pike at that crossroads, because heaven knows he never has.
