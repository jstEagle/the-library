## 6. The Orchard Keeper's Ghost

Wren Bell knew three things about the woman with the motorcar, and none of them were good.

The first thing was that she smelled of petrol and lavender, which was a strange combination, like a flower that had been in a fight. The second thing was that she had looked at the papers for a very long time by candlelight, her face going greyer with every page, which meant the papers were true. Papa had always said you could tell a lie because it made people comfortable, and the truth because it made them look like they'd swallowed a wasp.

The third thing was that she had not sent Wren away yet.

That was the worrying one. Grown-ups always sent children away when there was trouble. Mrs. Hobbes next door had sent Wren to fetch water the night the men came, and by the time she'd got back — walking slowly, because the well was far and the bucket was heavy and nobody had told her to hurry — the orchard was burning and Papa was gone and Mrs. Hobbes was crying into her apron as though aprons could hold that much.

So Wren sat on the edge of the strange bed in the strange room above the garage, and she did not take off her boots, and she waited for the woman to send her away too.

* * *

The room smelled of leather and oil, because it was over the garage where the motorcar slept. Wren had decided the motorcar was probably a horse underneath all its metal, a horse that had died and been stuffed, and that the woman drove it around out of stubbornness rather than admit it was dead. This thought made her feel better for nearly a minute.

There was a window. Through the window she could see the big house, all its windows dark except one, where a lamp burned behind a curtain like an eye that couldn't sleep. Wren knew about eyes that couldn't sleep. She'd had one herself since October.

Under the mattress — not under *her* mattress, under the other one, the flat one leaning against the wall — she had hidden the button. It was Papa's button, off his Sunday coat, brass with an apple tree stamped into it, because he'd been proud of being an orchard keeper even when other men laughed at orchard keepers and called it women's work with dirtier hands. Wren had cut it off the coat before they took the coat away. She'd used Mama's sewing scissors, which were still in the house, which meant Mama's scissors were still in the world, which was a thing Wren held onto at night the way other children held blankets.

She pressed her thumb against the apple tree on the button and said, inside her head, the thing she always said: *I'm keeping it.*

Papa had taught her that. Not about buttons. About names. "A Bell keeps things," he used to say, lifting her up to reach the highest apples, the ones the birds couldn't get. "Keeps the trees, keeps the accounts, keeps his word. That's the whole job, Wren. Everything else is just climbing."

Papa had kept his word right up until they hanged him for it. Wren didn't understand that part yet. She understood that the magistrate man had said Papa burned Mr. Ashcombe's cider barn, and that Papa had said he didn't, and that the magistrate man's saying had counted more than Papa's saying, which seemed to Wren like the whole world was run on a rule so stupid even a seven-year-old could see through it, if only anybody would ask a seven-year-old.

Nobody had asked a seven-year-old.

* * *

In the morning the woman — Miss Almeida, though Wren thought of her as Petrol-and-Lavender — brought bread and jam and set it down on the little table without a word, and then stood there as though waiting for something.

Wren ate the jam first. You always ate the jam first, because grown-ups took plates away when they remembered them, and jam was the part worth remembering.

"You'll want the lavatory down the passage," the woman said. "Second door. The first door is a cupboard full of my employer's hats, and I mention this only because I once spent ten minutes in it myself, believing it to be the lavatory, and came out wearing someone else's feather."

Wren looked at her.

"It's a joke," the woman said. "You're allowed to laugh at it. I've been told it's my best one."

Wren did not laugh. Laughing was for after, whenever after came. But she noted, in the ledger she kept behind her eyes — Papa had kept ledgers, and she was a Bell, and Bells kept things — that the woman had told her about the cupboard instead of pretending the cupboard wasn't there. That was either kindness or a trick, and Wren hadn't decided which.

"Where is my coat?" she asked. Her voice came out rusty, like a gate nobody opened anymore.

The woman's hands went still. "Being mended," she said. "There was a tear in the lining."

*You found the papers,* Wren thought. *You found what I carried and you know what it is and now everything depends on whether you're the sort of person who burns things or the sort who keeps them.* She pressed her thumb against her own sleeve, where the button would have been if buttons could be kept in sleeves.

"I want it back," she said.

"When it's mended."

"Mended how?"

"With thread," said the woman, "and considerably less paper inside it. Paper is heavy, you know. I'm surprised you could walk."

Wren put down her spoon. "It wasn't heavy."

"No?"

"Heavy things are heavy because you don't want to carry them." She said it the way Papa said things, flat and sure, and then was surprised at herself, because Papa's voice had come out of her mouth like water from a pump, and now the kitchen of their old house was in her head — the blue jug, the crooked shelf, the smell of apples going soft in the corner bowl — and she had to look very hard at the jam so nothing leaked out of her face.

The woman didn't say anything for a moment. Then she said, quietly, "That's very true," and went downstairs, and Wren added another line to the ledger behind her eyes: *Maybe kind. Still might be a trick.*

* * *

The days arranged themselves into a pattern, and Wren hated patterns, because patterns meant staying, and staying meant the woman hadn't figured out yet that Wren was trouble with a capital T and a small w.

In the mornings, Petrol-and-Lavender drove the big motorcar away and came back smelling of the road. In the afternoons, another woman appeared sometimes — tall, straight-backed, with grey hair pinned like she'd fought it and won — and the two of them talked low in the garden, and once Wren heard the tall one say, sharply, "*Nine days*, Elodie," and then both of them went quiet as mice when Wren came around the hedge.

Nine days until what? Wren turned it over. Nine days was soon. Soon enough that the woman's face had gone tight around the mouth, like a drawstring bag.

At night Wren lay in the bed over the garage and did her remembering, because Mrs. Hobbes had said forgetting was healthier and Mrs. Hobbes had been wrong about everything else, including the bucket.

She remembered Papa's hands. They were the map of him: nicked from pruning, stained brown to the second knuckle from October to March, one fingernail permanently black where a ladder had got him in '09. He could tell an apple's ripeness by cupping it in his palm, no biting, no looking, just weighing it against some secret scale in his skin. "Trees talk," he told her. "Not in words. In weight. You have to be quiet enough to hear it."

She remembered the grafting knife, and how he let her make one cut, one, supervised, on a scion he was going to throw away anyway, and how he'd said "well done" in a voice like she'd built a cathedral.

And she remembered the night. She tried not to, but trying not to was like trying not to think of a wasp in the room; your mind went straight to it.

Boots. Many boots, on the lane, out of rhythm, which was how you knew they weren't Papa coming home. Papa walked in rhythm. These boots argued with each other. Then lantern light sliding across the ceiling like something spilled, and Papa's voice low and terrible in a way she'd never heard, saying *not in front of the child*, and Mrs. Hobbes's hand hard on her shoulder steering her toward the well, and the look on Papa's face as the door shut — not scared. That was the worst part, the part she circled every night like a moth at glass. He hadn't looked scared. He'd looked *sorry*. Sorry for her, standing there in her nightdress with cold feet, sorry for whatever was coming, sorry the way a man is sorry when he already knows how a story ends but has to sit through the telling anyway.

They hanged him in January. They wouldn't let her see. They said it wasn't fitting. Wren had decided that when she was grown she would go and stand wherever it happened, for as long as it took, because somebody ought to witness it who loved him, and everybody else who'd been there had been there to watch a man die instead of to remember one.

*Keeping it,* she told the ceiling. *I'm keeping it.*

* * *

On the fourth day, the woman caught her in the garden.

Wren had been investigating. Investigating was not snooping; snooping was rude, Papa said, but investigating was a duty, and anyway the garden had a wall with a gap in it and gaps were practically invitations. Through the gap was a lane, and past the lane a field, and at the field's far edge a line of trees — real trees, apple or pear, she couldn't tell at this distance, but *trees*, and her feet had carried her forty yards along the hedgerow before her brain caught up.

"Wren."

The woman's voice. Not angry. Worse — careful.

Wren stopped. "I was looking at the trees."

"I can see that."

"They might be apple trees."

"They're pear," said the woman. "I checked, the first day. For the same reason you did."

That knocked something loose in Wren's chest, and she hated the woman for it, briefly and completely. She turned around. Petrol-and-Lavender stood on the path with her hands folded, and her hat was slightly crooked, and there was a smudge of engine grease on her wrist that she hadn't noticed, and all of these ordinary things made Wren angrier, because it was easier to distrust a person who was all sharp edges, and this one kept turning out to have smudges.

"My papa's trees burned," Wren said. It came out like an accusation.

"Yes."

"You weren't there."

"No."

"Then you don't know what they sounded like."

The woman went very still. "What did they sound like?"

"Like rain," Wren said, "but backwards." She had heard it from the top of the hill with Mrs. Hobbes's hand clamped over her ear, fire sucking and roaring, and that was the only way she could say it. Rain, but running the wrong direction, up instead of down, taking instead of giving.

The woman crouched down then, which grown-ups almost never did, so that their faces were level, and Wren saw that her eyes were brown and tired and not lying.

"I can't give you back your father," the woman said. "I want you to understand that I know that. Whatever happens in the next nine days — and things will happen, Wren, I won't pretend otherwise — I cannot fix the part that's already broken. But there's a piece of paper in this world that says your father was a thief and a burner, and it's a *lie*, and lies written on official paper are the most dangerous kind, because everyone believes them and no one has to. I am going to try to replace that paper with a true one. That's all I can do. It isn't enough. But it's what there is."

Wren looked at her for a long time.

"How do I know you're not one of them?" she asked. "The magistrate's people. How do I know they didn't send you?"

Something flickered across the woman's face — surprise, and then, oddly, respect. "You don't," she said. "Not for certain. Trust isn't knowing, Wren. It's deciding. And I'll tell you my deciding: I read those papers four times, and each time I got angrier, and I have never once in my life gotten angry on behalf of Magistrate Harrow. He makes me angry the way a stone in your boot makes you angry. Continually, and personally."

Despite everything — despite the wasp in her chest and the burnt trees and the button under the mattress — Wren felt the corner of her mouth move. Just a fraction. A gate opening one inch on a rusted hinge.

"That wasn't a joke," she said.

"No," the woman agreed. "But I'm told my best material is accidental."

* * *

That night, Wren did something new. She lay in the bed over the garage and she did not do the remembering first. Instead she lay on her side and spoke into the dark, very quietly, in the direction of London, which she imagined as an enormous grey place full of paper.

"Papa," she whispered. "There's a woman here. She smells like flowers in a fight. She says nine days. She found the papers and she didn't burn them and she crouched down to talk to me, which you always said was the test of a person." She paused. "I haven't shown her the button. I'm not showing anyone the button. But if she's telling the truth — if the true paper gets made — then I'll show you the photograph again, the one of us by the grafting bench, and I'll say your name out loud in a proper building where they have to listen. Wren Bell, daughter of Tomas Bell, keeper of the orchard, and he kept his word."

Downstairs, faintly, through the floor, she heard the woman moving about — a drawer opening, the scratch of a pen, the particular sigh of someone sitting down to do a difficult thing late at night.

Wren pressed her thumb hard against the dark, where the button would be if she'd brought it to bed, which she never did, because keeping a thing safe meant not risking it.

*Nine days,* she thought. *Soon enough to matter. Long enough to be afraid.*

She closed her eyes, and for the first time since October, she dreamed not of rain running backwards, but of a ladder against a tree, and her father's voice from somewhere above her in the leaves, saying: *quietly now, Wren. Weigh it in your hand.*
