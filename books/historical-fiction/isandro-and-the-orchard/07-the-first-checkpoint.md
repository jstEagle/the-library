## 7. The First Checkpoint

The trouble with a lie, Elodie discovered at half past five on a Tuesday morning, is that it wants rehearsing like anything else worth doing well.

She stood in the coach-house with the Daimler's bonnet propped open and the documents spread across an upturned crate — eleven sheets of thin paper, folded to the size of a prayer book, each one capable of hanging her if Constable Pike were the reading sort. He was not the reading sort. She had watched him squint at a parish notice for the better part of two minutes once, and she was fairly sure he'd been sounding it out.

Still. Fairly sure was a currency that spent poorly when the stake was a rope.

"Right," she said aloud, to the car, because Wren was asleep upstairs and Marguerite had not yet risen and there was no one else to lie to before breakfast. "You're taking Mrs. Voss's laundry list to Ashford. That's all. A woman of quality requires her collars starched in the county town, and you are the woman who drives her there. Nobody questions a laundry list."

The Daimler said nothing, which she took as agreement. Cars were better listeners than people. They only ever interrupted with mechanical complaints, and those could be fixed with a spanner.

She had decided on the engine housing sometime around three o'clock, after rejecting — in order — the spare-tyre well (first place any fool would look), beneath the seat cushions (second), and inside the toolbox (third, and Pike *loved* toolboxes; he'd once held up her adjustable wrench and asked, with the gravity of a bishop, whether such implements were "regular"). But the housing above the magneto, behind a plate secured by four brass nuts, was a place no constable in England would put his hands. Men feared engines the way they feared mathematics. You could tell them a motor ran on compressed lightning and bottled temper, and they'd nod along solemnly.

It took her twenty minutes and two skinned knuckles to get the plate off. The documents went in wrapped in an oilcloth rag, snug against the frame where vibration couldn't worry them. Then she wiped her hands, replaced the plate, and sat back on her heels to admire her work.

From the outside, nothing. The bonnet sat closed and innocent as a sleeping cat. Somewhere under its painted skin lay the truth about Magistrate Harrow and Tomas Bell, riding in state, and nobody — nobody — would know to look.

Elodie found, to her considerable surprise, that she was smiling.

* * *

Wren came down at six, dragging her coat, and stood in the doorway of the coach-house watching Elodie check the tyres with the expression of a child deciding whether a strange dog was friendly or merely strange.

"You're going away," Wren said. Not a question. Children of seven did not ask questions they already knew the answers to; they simply stated facts and waited to see if adults would have the decency to confirm them.

"I'm driving to Ashford. Laundry." Elodie held up the list, which Marguerite's housekeeper had written out in her round hand. It even smelled faintly of lavender, which struck Elodie as excessive honesty for a forgery.

"For your mistress."

"That's right."

Wren considered this. Her eyes went from the list to the car to Elodie's face, performing some private arithmetic. "Papa said grown-ups lie best when they've got something true to hold onto while they do it."

Elodie's hands went still on the tyre pump. "Did he."

"He said pick something small. Like a button." Wren looked down at her own coat, at the horn buttons, and touched one briefly, the way another child might touch a locket. Then she climbed into the passenger seat without being asked, arranged herself with terrible dignity, and folded her arms.

Elodie should have told her to go back inside. She knew that even as she started the engine. Every sensible instinct she owned lined up and said so, politely, in the voice of Miss Almeida, chauffeur, keeper of schedules and other people's secrets. But the girl had been left on her doorstep with a note that said *forget the face*, and Elodie had not yet worked out how to leave her anywhere, even for a morning drive.

Besides — and this was the thought she examined later, turning it over like a coin of uncertain mint — besides, the papers were Tomas Bell's. And the child was Tomas Bell's. And it seemed to Elodie that evidence ought to travel with its own witness, even a witness who still had a gap where her front tooth should be.

"We'll be back by dark," she said, which was either a promise or a lie, depending entirely on how the day went.

* * *

The road out of the valley ran through hop country and then orchard country, and Elodie drove it the way she drove everywhere — precisely, unhurried, at a speed calculated to suggest a woman thinking of nothing but carburettors. The morning was doing its best October impression: gold light, woodsmoke, mist sitting in the low fields like spilt milk. Beside her, Wren narrated the hedges under her breath. *Blackbird. Rooks. That one's just a crow, they think everything's a crow.*

At the top of Harrow Hill, where the road narrowed between two brick walls, Elodie saw the checkpoint.

She had known it would be there — Foy's letter had been explicit — but knowing a thing and seeing it were different animals altogether. A trestle barrier across the road. A lantern, unlit and unnecessary in the daylight. And leaning against the barrier with his thumbs hooked in his belt, eating an apple in the manner of a man who has been given authority and an apple and intends to enjoy both, stood Constable Pike.

Elodie's heart performed a manoeuvre she had previously only read about in novels.

"Pike," she said under her breath, testing the name. "Morning, Constable. Lovely day for it. Collars won't starch themselves." She glanced sideways. "Wren. When he speaks to us, we're going to Ashford for my mistress's laundry. Nothing else exists. Can you hold onto something true?"

Wren touched her coat button again. "This one."

"Good girl. Best button in England."

She brought the car to a stop four feet from the barrier — close enough to seem eager, far enough to seem harmless — and cut the engine, and smiled.

Here is what Elodie Almeida had learned in nine years of driving for Marguerite Voss: that a smile, correctly deployed, was a document. Signed, sealed, delivered. She had smiled customs men past a crate of French brandy, smiled a bishop out of complaining about the motor's noise during his sermon, smiled her way into three county balls she had no business attending. But she had always used it in service of small things. This morning she aimed it at a man whose signature could send her to prison, and felt — distinctly, alarmingly — the click of a lock turning over inside herself.

"Morning, Miss Almeida!" Pike pushed off the barrier, apple in hand, face opening like a shop shutter. Recognition, warm and uncomplicated. He knew the car. Of course he knew the car; he'd waved it past this spot forty times in the last year. "Out early."

"Laundry, Constable." She held up the list. "Mrs. Voss is particular about her collars, and Ashford's the only steam laundry that doesn't return them looking like they've been through a dogfight."

"Aye, they're particular folk, the quality." Pike craned amiably to peer into the car, and his gaze landed on Wren, and stopped. "And who's this young lady, then?"

Elodie felt the moment tilt. This was the hinge. Everything turned on the next three seconds, and she was aware of her own pulse in her wrists, and aware — with a sensation like stepping off a curb into unexpected air — that she was *enjoying* it. That was the part that frightened her. Not Pike, not the barrier, not the eleven folded sheets humming quietly behind the engine plate. The part underneath, the part that had spent thirty-one years believing history happened to other people, and was now discovering that it rather liked the weather here.

"My mistress has taken an interest in the child," Elodie said. "A charity case. Orphaned this summer — sad business, fire in the hop gardens. She's to be fitted for proper clothes in Ashford, since Mrs. Voss says charity begins with good boots." She sighed, a small, tired, long-suffering sigh, the sigh of a woman burdened with errands. "Between you and me, Constable, I've driven that lady to suffrage meetings, church bazaars, and once — God help me — a séance in Tunbridge Wells, and I have never known her to take on a project she couldn't improve."

Pike laughed. It was a good laugh, honest, the laugh of a man who had also once been dragged somewhere by a determined woman. "Séance! Did she get her money's worth?"

"She got a message from her late aunt recommending a new cook, so yes, apparently."

He laughed harder, slapped his thigh, and stepped back from the car. And here was the thing — the thing Elodie would turn over in bed that night, unable to sleep, both appalled and thrilled: he never asked to search it. Not the boot, not the seats, not the bonnet. Because she had not given him a reason to want to. Because she had handed him a story so comfortably dull that searching it would have been an insult to them both.

"Well." Pike hitched up his belt and lifted the barrier with the air of a man granting a great favour. "Give Mrs. Voss my respects. And tell her—" he lowered his voice, conspiratorial, glancing up the road as though the hedge might inform on him, "—tell her there's talk. County level. New inspection orders coming down, mail and roads both. Man named Harrow's behind it, they say. Keeping things *tidy*." He pronounced the word with a sourness that suggested tidy meant something else entirely in the county offices.

"Tidy," Elodie repeated. Her mouth was dry. Eleven sheets of paper sat twelve inches from this man's elbow, and he was handing her intelligence about the very machinery hunting them, free of charge, because she'd made him laugh about a séance. "I'll pass it along."

"Mind how you go." He touched his helmet. "And mind the little'un. Pretty manners, that one. Reminds me of someone." He frowned briefly, a dog losing a scent, then let it go. "Safe travels, miss."

The Daimler rolled through. Elodie kept her speed at a decorous crawl until the wall ended and the road bent, and then she kept it at a decorous crawl for another quarter mile, because a woman fleeing a checkpoint drives fast, and a woman carrying laundry does not.

Only when the hill dropped away behind them and the road ran empty through the orchards did she allow herself to breathe properly. Her hands on the wheel were steady. Her knees, she noted, were conducting their own separate crisis.

"You lied good," Wren said beside her, with the tone of a critic awarding modest marks.

"Thank you."

"You didn't even do the button thing."

"No. I did something else instead." Elodie thought about it. "I made him feel clever. People don't search cars belonging to drivers who make them feel clever."

Wren absorbed this. "Papa said that too. About the magistrate's men. He said they never look at what makes 'em laugh." She looked out the window at the orchards passing, row upon row of bare trees, and her voice went smaller. "That was Papa's orchard. Over there. Before."

Elodie followed her gaze. The trees stood grey and patient in the morning light, and somewhere among them, presumably, was the ground where a man had been arrested for a fire he hadn't set, and the cottage where a seven-year-old had learned that constables' eyes slide off whatever amuses them.

"We'll come back," Elodie heard herself say. "When it's done. When it's safe."

Wren didn't answer. She touched her button once more and looked forward, and Elodie drove on toward Ashford with the engine purring and the truth strapped tight behind the magneto, feeling the strangest sensation of her orderly life: as though she had been walking through fog for thirty-one years and had just, without noticing the exact moment, stepped out of it into hard clear daylight — and found, to her horror and delight, that she could see a very long way indeed.

Behind them, faint on the wind, she thought she heard Pike whistling. Ahead, at the crossroads where the Ashford road met the London mail route, a telegraph boy was pedalling hard, satchel bouncing, toward the county office.

New orders coming down, Pike had said. Mail and roads both.

Elodie pressed the accelerator a fraction lower and began, silently, to rehearse her next lie.
