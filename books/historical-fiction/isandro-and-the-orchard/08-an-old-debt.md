## 8. An Old Debt

Marguerite Voss had always believed that a woman's conscience, like her correspondence, ought to be kept in good order — sorted, filed, and reviewed at regular intervals. It was a discipline she had practiced for thirty years with the diligence of a bank clerk and the self-deception of a woman who has learned to call certain drawers "archival" when what she means is *buried*.

The drawer in question was not in her writing desk. It was in her memory, third from the top, behind the war years and ahead of the question of whether she had ever truly loved Gerald Voss or merely admired his tailoring. And on the morning after Elodie Almeida stood in her study with rain still beaded on her shoulders and asked — carefully, respectfully, in that voice she used for asking for anything, as though permission were a currency she had to earn — *"Miss Voss, do you know the name Tomas Bell?"* — Marguerite opened that drawer whether she wished to or not.

She had said no.

She had said it smoothly. She had even managed the small, puzzled frown she kept for awkward questions at dinner parties, the one that suggested the topic was mildly distasteful and could they please speak of something else. Twenty-two years of practice had made the frown flawless.

Elodie had believed her. That was the worst of it. Elodie, who read every letter before it was sealed, who noticed everything, who had once deduced from a smudge of ink alone that Marguerite's cousin was lying about the state of his finances — Elodie had looked at her employer and seen only an old woman being asked to remember a stranger.

*You are getting careless,* Marguerite told herself, standing at the window of her study while the motorcar coughed itself awake in the yard below. *Or perhaps you are simply tired.*

Below, Elodie was checking the tyres with the methodical attention she gave all machinery, and beside her — small, solemn, wrapped in one of the housekeeper's spare shawls — stood the child.

Wren.

Even the name was a blow. He had chosen it himself, Tomas, walking through the orchard the spring Wren was born, pointing up at the birds nesting in the cider apple and declaring that his daughter would answer to nothing so ordinary as Rose or Mary. "A wren," he'd said, laughing, "is small but nobody tells a wren what to sing." Marguerite had laughed too. She remembered laughing. She remembered everything about those years with the terrible fidelity of a woman who has spent two decades trying not to.

She turned from the window. On her desk lay the morning's post: three letters from the Women's Social and Political Union, one bill from the ironmonger, and a plain envelope with no return address that she did not need to open to know its contents. She had been receiving them, off and on, since 1907. Four shillings here, ten there, always in small notes, always unsigned. Payments, she understood now, for silence she had never agreed to sell — or rather, payments that made her complicit whether she sold it or not, because a woman who takes the money and burns it in the grate is still a woman who took the money.

She had burned every one. But burning, she had learned, is not the same as refusing.

* * *

It had begun, as these things do, with weather.

The spring of 1891 had been wet, then suddenly, gloriously dry, and Marguerite — twenty-six years old, newly widowed by nothing more dramatic than a broken engagement, visiting her aunt in Kent for her health (a phrase her aunt used to mean *for her spirits*, which meant *because you have been crying again*) — had taken to walking the lanes beyond the village each afternoon to escape her aunt's commentary on her prospects.

That was how she came to the orchard. Bell's orchard, though she hadn't known the name then. She had climbed a stile she was almost certainly not permitted to climb, because the sign said PRIVATE and Marguerite had already learned that signs saying PRIVATE were usually erected by men who owned things and enforced by men who feared them, and she had walked between the rows of apple trees in the long grass with her boots getting wet and her heart doing something unfamiliar, which was simply being quiet.

He found her there an hour later, of course. It was his orchard.

"You're standing in my best russets," he said, and he didn't sound angry; he sounded amused, and he was leaning on a ladder with a grafting knife in his hand and bark dust on his sleeve, and he had the kind of face that made a person want to know what he was thinking, which was dangerous, because most faces told you everything and asked nothing back.

"I'm not harming them," Marguerite said.

"No," he agreed. "But they'll remember you."

"They're trees."

"Trees remember everything," said Tomas Bell. "That's why we prune them. They'd grow entirely out of shape otherwise, following old grievances." He considered her a moment. "Like people."

She should have left. She knew that even then. Instead she said, "And what old grievances are you following?" and he grinned — actually grinned, a working man grinning at a gentlewoman as though they were equals, which was either insolence or honesty depending on where one stood — and said, "Come and see," and held out his hand toward the far row as though offering her the whole county.

She took the tour. She told herself it was botany.

For three summers she walked to that orchard, telling her aunt she was sketching wildflowers, and Tomas taught her grafting — *cut clean, cut once, don't hesitate, the tree forgives clumsiness but never doubt* — and she taught him, without quite meaning to, everything she read: Mill, Darwin, the suffrage pamphlets she had begun collecting the way other women collected fans. He listened the way few men of her class ever had, not politely but hungrily, arguing back, once throwing his cap on the ground over a point about property law and then retrieving it sheepishly when a branch nearly lost its blossom to his gesticulating.

"You argue like a lawyer," she told him once.

"I argue like a man who's been to court," he said, and laughed, and she hadn't understood then why he said it that way, with that small shadow crossing under the laugh.

She loved him. She had never written the word down anywhere, never said it aloud, had barely admitted it in the privacy of her own skull, but there it was, filed in the third drawer: *I loved an orchard-keeper in Kent in the year 1891, and I let my aunt and my class and my own cowardice take him from me without a fight.*

Because that was what happened. Not drama. Nothing so honest as drama. Her aunt discovered the walks — a neighbour saw them, word travelled, word always travelled — and there was a conversation at the breakfast table conducted in the glacial code of respectable families, about reputation, about *that sort*, about what Marguerite imagined her poor dear mother would say, and Marguerite, twenty-six years old and armed with nothing but pride, had said, "Of course. I understand entirely," and stopped walking to the orchard.

She wrote him one letter. She still remembered the first line — *Dear Mr Bell, I fear our acquaintance must end* — and hated herself for every word of it, the *Mr*, the *fear*, the *must*, as though the ending were weather rather than choice. He wrote back once. The letter was kind. Kindness, from him, was worse than anger would have been. *I understand,* he wrote. *Trees remember. So will I.* And then, some months later, he married a girl from the hop gardens, a bright-eyed girl named Susannah, and Marguerite told herself she was glad, and almost believed it.

She might have let the whole thing settle into harmless regret — the sort of regret that improves a woman, gives her eyes a thoughtful quality at dinner parties — if it weren't for 1898.

* * *

1898. The fire. The trial. She had followed it the way everyone did, in the newspapers, with that appetite for calamity that respectable people disguise as concern. An orchard-keeper near Ashford, convicted of burning a rival's barn, hanged at Maidstone that November. The name in the paper had gone through her like a splinter finding its old channel: *Tomas Bell.*

She had told herself it couldn't be the same man. There were many Bells in Kent. And then she had gone — taken the train down alone, wearing her plainest coat, telling herself she was going for closure, a word she despised — and stood outside the court in Maidstone among the crowd, and heard enough of the gossip to piece it together. The same man. Her Tomas. Accused by Magistrate Harrow, whose land bordered the burned farm, whose testimony about a quarrel had weighed heavier than any alibi, whose clerk had produced accounts that no one thought to question because the accused was poor and the accuser wore a magistrate's chain.

She had done nothing.

That sentence deserves its own line, and she gave it one, over and over, across twenty years: *I did nothing.* She had stood in the crowd with her heart hammering and her mind supplying arguments — *I have no standing, no evidence, only a summer's acquaintance fifteen years stale; who would believe me; what could I change* — and every argument had been true and none of them had mattered, because the truth underneath them was simpler: she was afraid, and she chose her safety, and a man was hanged, and his daughter — his daughter, whom she had not known existed until nine days ago, until a child appeared on her chauffeur's doorstep with a note reading *Forget the face* —

Marguerite pressed her fingers to her eyes.

The letters had started a year later. Small sums, unsigned. She had investigated, of course — quietly, through a solicitor in Canterbury who owed her a favour — and what she learned had curdled her blood: Harrow knew. Not about the love; about *her*. A witness statement, never entered, from a woman who claimed to have seen Harrow's own men near the barn that night — a statement the woman had recanted, and the solicitor's guess was that someone had paid for the recanting, and that Harrow, tidying loose ends, had traced the statement's earlier drafts to a lady visitor who'd been asking questions in Maidstone. The money was not payment. The money was a leash. Each envelope said, in the language of envelopes: *We know you asked. Be quiet, and stay quiet.*

So she had been quiet. She had built her life into a fortress of useful work instead — the suffrage committees, the letters, the causes that were safe precisely because they were large, because a movement cannot break your individual heart the way a single unjustly hanged man can. She had hired servants who were clever and asked no questions. She had become very good, she told herself, at fighting history in the abstract.

And then Elodie Almeida had knocked on her study door with rain on her shoulders and a child's future in her coat pocket, and asked if she knew the name Tomas Bell, and Marguerite Voss, champion of truth, had lied.

* * *

She found Elodie in the garage that evening, lying half under the motorcar with a lamp balanced precariously on the running board, a smudge of grease across one cheekbone like a beauty mark applied by accident.

"If you're checking the engine housing," Marguerite said, "you needn't bother on my account. I have no plans to travel."

Elodie slid out, sat up, and regarded her with those level grey eyes. "Begging your pardon, miss, but everyone says that right before they travel."

Despite everything — despite the drawer open in her chest, despite the child asleep upstairs in the blue room — Marguerite felt her mouth twitch. "You are impertinent, Miss Almeida."

"Yes, miss. You keep me on for it."

A pause. Somewhere in the walls a pipe ticked as the house cooled. Marguerite looked at the car — her car, bought new in 1910, the first in the parish — and thought about engine housings and checkpoints and the particular courage it apparently took to lie to a constable.

"I am going to ask you something," she said, "and I would like you to answer honestly, which I recognise is ironic coming from me this week."

Elodie said nothing, but she set down the rag, which from her was a form of full attention.

"The documents. The ones you found. Do you intend to take them to London yourself?"

"Yes, miss."

"Through three checkpoints controlled by the men those papers condemn."

"Yes, miss."

"With the child."

"She doesn't leave my sight," Elodie said, and there it was — not the flat servant's voice but something underneath it, bedrock, the voice of a woman who had decided. Marguerite felt an absurd, maternal flicker of pride and crushed it, because pride was not what this moment required.

"It will cost money," Marguerite said. "Petrol, lodgings, bribes — and it will cost bribes, Miss Almeida, whatever your conscience hopes; men like Pike and the mail inspector run on tea and flattery, but the third checkpoint runs on cash. Foy can be reached, but not for free." She heard herself and marvelled: she had known the names, the routes, the weaknesses, catalogued over years of pretending not to care. "Perhaps four pounds a day, conservatively. More if the car misbehaves. Nine days."

Elodie's composure flickered — just once, just visibly — the look of a woman doing arithmetic against a savings tin. "I have eleven pounds put by," she said. "I'd manage."

"You would manage," Marguerite repeated. "Yes. I imagine you would manage beautifully and arrive in London having eaten nothing and slept in the car, and you would testify, if it came to that, in a coat with frayed cuffs, and they would destroy you for the cuffs before they touched the evidence." She drew a breath. It was astonishing, she thought, how heavy a chequebook could feel before you had even picked it up. "I will fund the journey."

"Miss—"

"Not charity." She raised a hand before Elodie could speak. "I know what you're about to say, and I won't hear it. This is not charity and it is not generosity. It is—" She stopped. The true word sat there, enormous, twenty years overdue. *Repayment.* "Consider it an investment," she said instead, "in an outcome I should have purchased long ago."

Elodie studied her. And Marguerite saw — with the peculiar clarity that comes when one has finally stopped lying — that the girl was not fooled. That perhaps she never had been. That behind those grey eyes some quiet ledger of her own was being consulted, and that what she saw written there was not *generous employer* but something closer to *guilty woman, finally paying*.

"All right, miss," Elodie said at last. Softly. Without judgement, which was somehow harder to bear than judgement would have been. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Marguerite turned toward the door, then stopped, her hand on the frame, and spoke without turning around, because some things could only be said to a grease-stained garage wall. "Tomas Bell had a way with trees. Cut clean, cut once, don't hesitate. He used to say the tree forgives clumsiness but never doubt." Her voice held steady; she was rather proud of that. "I doubted for twenty years, Miss Almeida. See that you don't."

She walked back across the yard in the dark, past the sleeping house, up the stairs, and paused at the blue room door, where a wedge of lamplight fell across the floor and a small shape lay curled beneath the quilts, one fist closed around the edge of the blanket as though the blanket might be taken away.

Marguerite stood there a long moment. Then she went to her study, unlocked the third drawer of her desk — the real one, the wooden one, which she had never shown anyone — and took out a letter. Old, creased, smelling faintly of apples and smoke. *I understand. Trees remember. So will I.*

She fed it to the grate and watched it burn, every word of that careful kindness curling to black, and when it was ash she sat down and wrote a cheque for fifty pounds, payable to cash, and beneath it a note in her firmest hand:

*For the journey. Spend it like a woman with right on her side. — M.V.*

And it was only as she sealed the envelope that she heard, from the direction of the lane, the unmistakable crunch of gravel under wheels that were not hers — a slow, deliberate motorcar passing the gate once, slowing, and passing again — and understood that somewhere out in the dark, someone had already begun to count the days.
