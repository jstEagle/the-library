## 9. The Road to Ashford

They left before the sun had properly committed to the day, the motorcar coughing out of the stable yard with its lamps still lit against a grey that could not decide whether it was fog or merely indecision. Elodie had packed in the manner she had learned from Marguerite: everything essential, nothing sentimental, and one item of foolishness for morale. The foolishness was a tin of barley sugar. The essentials were the documents, sewn now into the lining of Wren's small canvas satchel beneath a layer of marbles and a jam sandwich going stale.

"You'll want to hold that on the bends," Elodie said, nodding at the satchel on the girl's lap.

"Why?"

"Because if you don't, the marbles will roll about and I shall imagine I can hear them thinking."

Wren considered this. She was not, Elodie had discovered over two days of careful observation, a child who asked *why* as a stalling tactic; she asked it as an auditor. She wanted the actual reason or she wanted silence, and she could tell the difference between a real answer and a grown-up's noise.

"The papers are in there," Wren said at last. "Not the marbles."

"They're in there too. Under the marbles."

"Under," Wren repeated, satisfied by the physics of it. Papers sank; marbles rolled. A sensible arrangement.

Elodie drove. It was what she was for, after all — that had been the whole architecture of her life until nine days ago: she drove, other people arrived, history happened to them at the end of the journey while she waited with the handbrake on. But the road out of the village was hers in a way no road had ever been. Nobody had told her where to go. She had chosen Ashford because it was south-east and indirect, because Marguerite's money was folded in her boot, and because a woman on the tribunal clerk's list had once written to Marguerite from a boarding house there, which meant a name, an address, a thread to follow. Indirect was the point. Direct roads had constables on them.

Wren had been told to be "Miss Bell's niece" only insofar as Elodie was "Miss Almeida" — which was true, and therefore the sort of lie Elodie found she could carry without it rattling. The girl practised it gravely for the first six miles.

"Are you my aunt?"

"For today."

"What am I called?"

"Whatever you like that isn't Bell."

A long pause, the hedges flickering past, the early light coming up lemon-coloured over the Weald. Then: "Wren is fine. He named me for the bird. He said they nest in orchards and nobody bothers them." She said *he* the way children say it — as though there were only one *he* in the world worth the capital letter.

"He sounds like a man who chose his words well," Elodie said, and was surprised by how much it cost her to say it evenly, and how much Wren's small nod paid her back.

* *

The trouble began, as troubles on the road generally do, with a smell.

It came at the top of a long rise outside Horsmonden, a hot oily bitterness drifting up through the floorboards, and Elodie's stomach dropped before her brain caught up. She knew engines the way farmwives know weather. She brought the car over the crest and coasted down the far side with the ignition cut, saying nothing in the firm belief that saying nothing would make it less true, and steered onto the verge beneath a chestnut tree just as the engine gave a shudder like a dying animal and stopped for good.

Steam rose gently from the bonnet seam, apologetic and total.

"Well," said Elodie.

"Well," said Wren, who had apparently decided some time ago that adults required echoing in moments of crisis, the way vicars require responses.

Elodie got out, lifted the bonnet, and looked at what she already knew she would find: a cracked water jacket weeping its contents onto the road, and behind it something worse she did not have the parts to name. She stood there with her hands dirty up to the wrists and performed the arithmetic. A garage, if there was one, would take a day. A garage with a telephone would report a lady motorist travelling alone with a child and no luggage worth the name. A garage whose proprietor read newspapers might remember Magistrate Harrow's name from the assize coverage, might remember a photograph, might —

"Might nothing," she said aloud, mostly to steady herself. "We walk."

"Where?"

"Away from the car." She wiped her hands on a rag, thought better of it, and buried the rag under a stone. The documents stayed in the satchel, on the child's back, where no searcher looking at a motorcar would think to look. That much of the plan still held. The rest of the plan, such as it had been, lay under the bonnet with the steam.

They walked perhaps a mile down a lane that smelled of wet wood and apples gone over, Wren keeping pace with the tireless, maddening stride of a seven-year-old who has never once considered being tired, before the lane opened onto a field striped with bines — hop gardens, the poles marching away in ranks, and strung between them great green garlands heavy and dusty and alive with people. Pickers everywhere: women on ladders with their skirts pinned up, children running between the rows with baskets, an old man at the near end calling measurements in a voice like a barrel rolling downhill. The whole field smelled of hops and woodsmoke and somebody's frying onions, and the noise of it was enormous and cheerful — talk and laughter and the dry rattle of bines and, somewhere, a concertina.

It was, Elodie thought, the most people she had seen in one place since London, and every one of them busy at work so old and ordinary that it made her own emergency feel briefly, mercifully small.

A woman came down off her ladder when they reached the gate — broad-shouldered, sleeves rolled, forearms green to the elbow — and looked them over with the frank efficiency of someone accustomed to assessing strangers by their hands.

"You're not pickers," she said.

"We're not," Elodie agreed. "Our motorcar has died about a mile back and we've walked toward the smoke. Which was, I confess, entirely a strategy for finding breakfast."

The woman laughed — a short, surprised bark, as though she had not planned to like this stranger and had failed to prevent it. "Breakfast we can do. Repairs you'll want my Tom for, and Tom's down at the oast till noon. You'd best come and sit. Mind the bins. And mind Dolly — she bites them she stares at."

Dolly, a dog of mixed and unapologetic convictions, sniffed Wren's satchel, found the jam sandwich, and became their sworn ally for life.

* *

The picker family's name was Crouch, and their camp at the edge of the hop garden consisted of two wagons, a tarpaulin kitchen, and a level of organised chaos that Marguerite Voss would have called picturesque and Mrs. Crouch called Tuesday. There were, by Elodie's count over the next hour, at least nine children, though the number shifted whenever she recounted, as though some of them were shared among neighbouring camps on a rotating basis. Breakfast was bread fried in bacon fat, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in, and a general air of hospitality so immediate it made Elodie realise how long it had been since anyone had fed her without first wondering what she was worth.

Mrs. Crouch — "Nan, everyone calls me Nan, Mrs. Crouch is my mother-in-law and she's dead" — set them on an upturned crate and studied Wren with a professional eye.

"Yours?"

"I'm — looking after her. For now."

"Ah." Nan packed an entire biography into the word. "Well, she'll do. Hands aren't soft. Somebody's kept her useful." She turned and bellowed across the camp, "Peg! Fetch our little visitor a stool, she can sort hops if she wants pocket-money like the rest!" And then, lower, to Elodie, with the tact of a woman who had raised nine children through every variety of trouble: "And you can sit and do nothing for half an hour. It won't kill you. I've tried."

So Elodie sat, and drank her tea, and did nothing, and it very nearly did kill her, because doing nothing meant listening, and listening meant hearing the men at the far wagon talking as men do when the work is rhythmic and the morning is long — talking about prices, and rain, and a wedding, and then about the old fire.

"Bell's place, that was," one of them said, stripping hops into a bin with a sound like rain on a roof. "Before it burned. My dad carted his apples every year. Best cider in the parish and he'd never sell it, mind. Gave it away. Said selling it would make it commerce instead of pleasure."

"There was a man," said another voice, older. Old Amos, the measurer, barrel-voiced. "There was a man who knew every tree on that hill by name. Not by kind — by *name*. My missus thought it heathenish. I thought it the only proper way to treat a thing you tend twenty years."

Elodie held her breath. The steam from her tea climbed straight and thin in the still air.

"They hanged him for it," the first man said, quieter now. "And I'll go to my grave saying the fire started the night of the storm, same as lightning struck the Farley barn that selfsame evening, and no keeper burns his own trees. What's a man got left if he burns his trees? His trees are his — " he groped for it — "his *sentence*, like. His life wrote out in wood."

"Magistrate said different," said a third man, not unkindly but flatly, the way one cites weather.

"Magistrate," said Amos, and spat, with tremendous economy, into the dust. "Harrow wouldn't know an apple from his own backside."

Laughter, but the tired kind, the kind that has been laughed for years and has worn a groove.

Nobody looked at her. Nobody lowered their voices. That was the thing that undid her, sitting there on the upturned crate with her hands around a tin mug: nobody lowered their voices, because there was nothing secret in it. It wasn't a scandal to these people. It was a fact, carried the way you carry the year of a great flood or the name of a dead child — folded into ordinary talk, passed mouth to mouth, kept alive in kitchens and hop gardens by people who had never been asked to testify and would not have been believed if they had been.

*I always believed history happened elsewhere,* she thought. *To ministers and marches and women throwing bricks. To other people.*

But here was Tomas Bell, three years hanged, sitting among the bines in a borrowed mug of tea, alive in the mouths of strangers. History didn't happen elsewhere. It happened *here*, in fields, in frying pans, in the space between *magistrate said different* and the spit in the dust. It had simply never happened to anyone willing to write it down.

She looked across the camp to where Wren sat on Peg's stool, sorting hops with quick serious fingers beside children who had already accepted her without question, and felt something shift in her chest with an almost audible click — the sound, she thought later, of the last door in her heart unlocking from the inside.

*He named her for the bird,* she thought. *They nest in orchards and nobody bothers them.* And here she was, nesting among strangers, and every soul in the garden bothering to feed her.

"More tea?" Nan asked, materialising with the pot.

"Please," said Elodie, and then, because it seemed suddenly important to say true things to this woman: "You knew him. Bell. You knew him."

Nan poured, unhurried. "Everyone round here knew him. Good man. Wrong end of Harrow." She set the pot down and looked at Elodie directly for the first time, and her eyes were not soft at all; they were the eyes of someone who had watched a great deal and forgotten none of it. "You're going somewhere about it. About him. Don't tell me — I don't want to know and you shouldn't want to say. But you've got the look." She nodded once, towards the road, towards London, towards whatever lay at the end of both. "My Tom will fix your motor by noon. After that, get her" — a jerk of the chin at Wren — "and yourself and whatever you're carrying clean out of this county. Harrow's men were through Tuesday asking after strangers. Asked at every camp." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was an old, hard one. "We told them we hadn't seen any. Hop-pickers are famous for seeing nothing. It's the dust."

* *

Tom Crouch proved as good as his wife's word, arriving at the car at half past eleven with tools, a boy, and the serene confidence of a man who had repaired everything from wagons to marriages. By two o'clock the water jacket was patched with solder and optimism, and the engine turned over with a sound more cough than death-rattle, which Tom pronounced "good enough to reach Ashford and no further, so don't ask further."

They left late in the gold light, the hop gardens glowing, the whole Crouch camp turned out to wave — nine children, eight of whom were definitely the same nine, Dolly the dog barking herself hoarse, Nan pressing a paper twist of boiled sweets into Wren's hand and a last look into Elodie's that contained instructions no court would admit as evidence.

The road south ran through orchard country, and Elodie understood, too late to choose otherwise, that it must: mile upon mile of trees, most of them pruned and tended, some of them old and crooked and heavy with the year's apples, and all of it, now, unbearable and precious. Wren watched it too, kneeling up on the seat despite instruction, her chin on the door's edge, her eyes moving over the rows.

"That's how his looked," she said, after a long time. "Only more. Only all the way to the sky."

"Yes," Elodie said. "I expect it did."

And then, ahead, where the road bent down toward a bridge and the evening mist was already gathering white along the hedgerows, she saw the glint of lamplight where no lamp ought to be — a lantern swung low over the roadside by a figure in dark clothes, and beyond it, half-hidden in the mist at the bridge's far end, the unmistakable shape of a second man standing beside a mounted police horse.

A checkpoint. Here, in open country, miles from anywhere on the map that mattered.

"Sit down slowly," Elodie said quietly, "and put your arms around the satchel."
