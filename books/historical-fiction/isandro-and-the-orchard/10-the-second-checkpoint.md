## 10. The Second Checkpoint

The mail inspector at Ashford was a different species of animal from Constable Pike.

Pike had been a village dog — all bark and territorial pissing, easily satisfied by a confident voice and the smell of another man's authority. The man waiting at the weighing shed on the London road was something older and more patient. He stood beside a folding table in the yard of the receiving office with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, and he watched every vehicle come through the archway the way a heron watches water: without any visible interest at all, until the moment came to strike.

"Stop here," he said, before Elodie had even brought the car to a full halt. "Motorcars get opened. All compartments."

Elodie's hands did not tremble on the wheel. She had spent two days teaching them not to, and it seemed the lesson had finally taken root somewhere below thought, in the marrow.

"Of course," she said, in the tone of a woman mildly inconvenienced by procedure but far too sensible to argue about it. She had learned that tone from Marguerite, who could make an entire drawing room feel scolded with nothing but posture. It worked nearly as well on inspectors.

The yard smelled of oil and canvas and the sour-sweetness of overripe apples nobody had bothered to sweep. Wren sat beside her on the bench seat, very straight, hands folded in her lap like a child at church. They had rehearsed this. Not the details — you couldn't rehearse what you couldn't foresee — but the shape of it. Elodie would talk. Wren would be a child: bored, wriggling, forgettable. Children were invisible precisely because everyone believed they were harmless. Elodie had been counting on that since Kent, and so far the arithmetic held.

"Name?"

"Almeida. Elodie Almeida, driving for Mrs. Marguerite Voss of Thornfield House." She produced the letter of introduction Marguerite had written before her arrest — the last practical thing her employer had done for her, pressed into her hand along with a roll of banknotes and one fierce, wordless look through the study door. "We're wanted in town by Thursday. Family business."

"Family business," the inspector repeated, without inflection, writing it down anyway as though family business were a criminal offence he intended to prosecute at his leisure. He walked around the car once, slowly, trailing one knuckle along the mudguards like a man appraising a horse's legs. Then he opened the boot.

Elodie's heart performed a small, controlled manoeuvre that would have interested a physician.

The documents were not in the boot. The documents had not been in the boot since Dover, when Elodie had woken at four in the morning in a lodging-house bed with the certain, sick knowledge that she had made a mistake leaving them anywhere obvious, and had gone down to the yard in her coat to move them. They now lived wrapped in oilcloth inside the spare inner tube, which lived strapped under the chassis behind the nearside running board, where no inspector in England would look because no inspector in England wanted to kneel in road filth for a woman's laundry.

That had been the whole plan: hide things where dignity forbade searching.

What Elodie had not planned — what she discovered only now, watching the inspector's hands move with terrible thoroughness through the boot's contents — was that Wren had made plans of her own.

* * *

The girl had changed in nine days, or perhaps in however many days had passed since men came to the orchard in the night; Elodie suspected the change predated all of them. She ate without fuss and slept without warning and asked questions that arrived sideways, like stones thrown from cover. *Why does the constable have a whistle if he doesn't run? Do ladies in London still burn their letters? How do you know when a man has decided to be cruel?*

Elodie answered what she could. Some questions she let lie in the road behind them.

But Elodie had also noticed, with the particular attention of a person who read other people's correspondence for a living, that Wren watched hands. Everyone else watched faces. Wren watched hands — the postman's, the innkeeper's, Elodie's own on the crank and the wheel — as though hands were where people kept their intentions. It was a habit Elodie recognized with a small cold shock, because it was her own habit, learned in Marguerite's morning room among sealed envelopes.

So when the inspector lifted out the valise and unstrapped it, and when Wren slid off the bench seat with the liquid unhurried motion of a child going to look at something dull, Elodie did not stop her. She talked instead. Talking was her post, and she held it.

"You'll want the tool tray too," she said pleasantly. "It's got a jack in it somewhere. I confess I don't hold with engines — I drive the thing, I don't pretend to understand it. My late father was a farrier, and he said much the same about horses. You can love a creature thoroughly and remain baffled by its insides."

The inspector grunted. It was, Elodie judged, a grunt of provisional sympathy, and she pressed the advantage.

"Is it the letters you're after? Because I'll save you the trouble there — everything worth reading went through Mrs. Voss's parlour first. She reads every letter before it goes out. Says a letter unsealed is a promise kept." A small true thing, offered like a bribe. It was remarkable, Elodie thought, how much genuine material she could spend to buy a lie passage. "You'll find nothing in that bag but mending and my employer's opinions, and the opinions aren't actionable yet, though give her time."

The inspector almost smiled. He emptied the valise onto the table anyway: stockings, a sewing kit, a tin of barley sugar, two novels, a folded paper that —

Elodie's mouth went dry.

A folded paper, creased into quarters, soft at the edges as though it had been carried in a pocket for days. The inspector shook it open with two fingers.

It was a laundry list.

*Cotton shifts, 3. Pinafore, 1 (patched). Collars, 6 — starch. Handkerchiefs, 8. Table napkins, 12. Sheets, large, 4 — mend tear at hem.* And at the bottom, in a rounder, younger hand, added as an afterthought: *One grey coat — DO NOT LOSE.*

"That yours?" the inspector said, to Wren.

"Yes sir," said Wren, in the smallest, most bored voice Elodie had ever heard her produce. "Mrs. Hobbes does ours. She loses things." A pause, weighted with the whole grievance of childhood. "*Everything.*"

"And what's all this?" The inspector had moved on to the sewing kit, poking the needle cushion, unscrewing the thimble, holding the little scissors up to the light.

Wren drifted closer to the table — one step, two — and then her hand went out, quick and artless as a sparrow, and took the laundry list off the wood. "Can I have it? Mrs. Hobbes counts."

"Counts?"

"Counts the list against the things. If the list is wrong we all get shouted at." She folded it back into quarters with fingers that did not hurry, and put it in her pocket, and looked up at him with the flat patient stare of a child who considers the entire proceeding a waste of a fine afternoon.

Elodie understood, in one vertiginous instant, what she was looking at.

There was no Mrs. Hobbes. There had never been a laundry list. Somewhere between Dover and here — probably at the hop-pickers' long table, while Elodie had been laughing at old Mr. Cobbett's imitation of Tomas Bell falling out of an apple tree — Wren had taken a scrap of paper and copied, in her careful schoolroom hand, a page of the evidence. Folded it. Carried it. Waited.

Not the ledger itself — Elodie checked the geography of her fear carefully — not the pages that mattered. A decoy. A thing for an inspector to find and dismiss, planted in plain sight so that his eye, having found *a* paper, would stop hunting for *the* papers. The child had built a false bottom into the world and tucked it in her pocket.

And the last line — *one grey coat, do not lose* — that was the coat Wren had arrived in. The coat whose lining had held everything. Wren had written her own history down as laundry, and handed it to a stranger to read, and dared him to see it.

The inspector saw a laundry list. He grunted again, repacked the valise with the rough tenderness men use for other people's belongings, and closed the boot.

"Underneath," he said. "Bonne tuck up your skirts, miss. I've got to look at the chassis."

* * *

Here was the moment the plan was built on, and Elodie felt it approach the way you feel a bridge take weight.

He would kneel. That was the indignity the whole scheme depended on — a grown man, an official man, going down on one knee in the yard muck of his own receiving office to inspect the underside of a lady's motorcar, while the lady stood over him. Men like this inspector did not mind searching women. They minded being seen stooping for them.

He hesitated. He looked at the ground, which was genuinely filthy, and at his trousers, which were genuinely good, and at Elodie, who arranged her face into polite incomprehension and gazed off at a pigeon as if the pigeon were more interesting than anything happening below the running board.

Then the yard clock struck eleven, and a clerk leaned out of the office window and called, "Hargreaves! Mail van's stuck at the level crossing and the driver wants words!" in the tone of a man reporting a crisis he would very much enjoy watching.

Hargreaves swore — softly, officially — and straightened up without ever having bent down.

"Chassis looks sound," he said. "Move along. And tell Mrs. Voss" — he wrote something in his book without looking at it — "that sealed letters are sealed letters, whatever parlour they've been through. We're not running a private postal service for the quality."

"I'll tell her," said Elodie truthfully, "though I doubt it will change anything."

She cranked the engine. It caught on the second pull, coughing blue smoke across the yard, and Hargreaves waved them through the archway with his pencil, already half-turned toward the level crossing and its promised entertainment.

They were a quarter mile down the London road before either of them spoke.

"Wren." Elodie kept her eyes on the road. Her voice came out strange — squeezed at the top, as though it had been wrung. "The laundry list. Where did you learn to do that?"

Wren considered the question with the seriousness she gave all questions. "Papa said the best place to hide a leaf is in a tree." A pause. The wind through the windshield took the rest of the sentence and Elodie had to wait for it. "So I made you a tree."

Elodie pulled the car over. She hadn't planned to; her hands decided. She stopped by a hedgerow with the hawthorn just coming into flower, turned off the engine, and sat for a moment not trusting herself to speak, because there was a hard hot thing in her throat that had no business appearing during a successful smuggling operation.

Nine days ago — ten now — she had believed, sincerely and comfortably, that history happened to other people. To parliaments and martyrs and newspapermen. To orchard-keepers hanged for fires they never set, in counties she'd never visited, reported in columns she skimmed before Marguerite got the page. She had driven through a country on fire with its own future and thought herself a spectator at the window, safe behind glass, useful only for carrying other people's convictions in a motorcar.

Now here she sat on a verge in Kent with a forged laundry list in the pocket of a seven-year-old girl, and the glass was gone. The window was gone. History was sitting beside her swinging her feet, having hidden a leaf in a tree, and Elodie understood — fully, finally, in her wrists and her ribs and not just her head — that she had not been driving these documents to London at all. She had been driving *Wren*. The papers were the excuse. The child was the cargo, and the destination was not a tribunal but a life, and Elodie would have lied to every constable, inspector, and magistrate between here and the sea to deliver it.

"It was a very good tree," she managed at last.

"Papa said it better," Wren said, with a child's scrupulous honesty. "But mine worked."

Elodie laughed — really laughed, the first time since the step and the knock and the small face in the lamplight — and it startled a pigeon out of the hedge, and Wren looked at her with faint suspicion, as she always did when adults made sudden noises, and then, after a moment, allowed herself the ghost of a smile.

Elodie restarted the engine. London lay ahead through the bright spring haze: forty miles of road, one more checkpoint somewhere past Rochester where Harrow's own clerk kept the books, and a tribunal that would sit in six days whether or not the truth arrived wearing a clean collar.

They had gone perhaps three miles when Wren, who had been quiet in the way that preceded her important remarks, said:

"There's a man on a bicycle behind us. He's been there since the crossing."

Elodie looked in the mirror.
