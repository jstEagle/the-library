## 11. Betrayal at the Inn

Wren had a system for judging people, and it had never once failed her.

It was simple. You watched their hands. Papa had hands like spades, honest and cracked, that smelled of apple peel. The men who came for him in the night had soft hands, white at the knuckles from holding pens instead of anything real. Hands told you everything if you were patient enough to look.

The lodger at the Waggoner's Rest had beautiful hands.

His name was Mr. Callow, and he'd appeared in the taproom the evening before like a coin dropped out of someone's pocket — shiny, round-faced, all dimples. He'd bought Wren a ginger beer without being asked and said, *Well now, aren't you a little poppet*, which was exactly the sort of thing people said to children they wanted to believe they liked.

Wren did not drink the ginger beer. She held it in her lap until it went flat and warm, and she watched his hands fold and unfold a newspaper he never read.

*Poppet*, she thought. Nobody who meant it said poppet.

Elodie didn't see it. Elodie was busy being two people at once, which Wren had noticed was her special talent. There was the motorcar Elodie, with her goggles and her confident voice that could make a constable apologize for breathing near her, and there was the other one, the tired one, who checked the engine housing three times a night even when nothing needed checking, whose fingers hovered over the hidden papers like a mother over a cot.

"You should eat your pie," Elodie said now, sliding onto the bench beside her in the morning room. "Cold mutton pie is one of England's great treasures."

"It's grey," Wren said.

"That's what makes it a treasure. Anything colourful in this country is either a flower or illegal."

That was a joke. Wren knew it was a joke because Elodie's mouth did the small twitch it did, the one that wasn't quite a smile but was thinking about becoming one when it grew up. Wren didn't laugh anyway. She was watching the doorway, where Mr. Callow stood paying his bill, counting out coins with those lovely soft hands, saying something to the innkeeper's wife that made her laugh and press her hand to her collarbone.

"Who is that man?" Wren asked.

"Mm? Oh — Callow. Commercial traveller, he says. Vinegar and pickles, would you believe." Elodie tore her pie in half and put the bigger half on Wren's plate without making a ceremony of it, which was another thing about her: she gave things sideways, as if kindness were shy. "He was very interested in our motorcar last night. Interested in an admiring way, I think. Most men are."

"Did he ask where we're going?"

"He asked if we were going to Ashford, and I told him we were going to visit my aunt in Tonbridge, who does not exist." Elodie's mouth twitched again. "I have rather a lot of nonexistent relatives. They come in useful."

Wren turned her pie over. Grey side up, grey side down. Her stomach was a closed door.

"When Papa—" she started, and stopped, because she hadn't talked about Papa out loud yet, not once, not to anyone, and the words got stuck in her throat like plum stones. She tried again, smaller. "When the men came for Papa, there was a man first. Before. He sat in our kitchen and drank tea and said how sorry he was about Mama, and Papa liked him, and Papa liked everybody, that was his whole trouble." She looked up. "He had nice hands too."

Elodie went very still. It was the stillness of the engine housing at night — something underneath being checked.

"You think this Callow man—"

"I think nobody's ever followed us because of my face," Wren said. "Except maybe for my face being dirty."

Elodie looked at her for a long moment, and then she did something Wren hadn't seen before. She reached across and took Wren's hand, right there in front of the innkeeper's wife and the vinegar man and God and everybody, and squeezed it.

"Right," she said quietly. "We leave within the hour. Eat the pie first. Heroes have been undone by less than an empty stomach, and I refuse to let history record that we fled villainy on an unfilled belly."

* * *

They did not flee within the hour, because Elodie's motorcar had other ideas.

The crank handle refused, then caught, then made a noise like an insulted cat and died altogether. Elodie swore under her breath in a way that was somehow elegant, the curses arranged like flowers in a posy, and lifted the bonnet to glare into the engine as though it had personally betrayed the suffrage movement.

"The magneto," she announced to no one. "Or possibly the entire concept of internal combustion."

"Can you fix it?" Wren asked.

"I can fix most things. Whether I can fix them before half past six is a question philosophy cannot answer."

While Elodie wrestled with the machine, sleeves rolled, a smear of grease arriving on her cheekbone like war paint, Wren did what she always did in strange places: she walked the edges. The Waggoner's Rest sat at the lip of orchard country, and behind the inn the ground climbed away into rows of old apple trees, gnarled and silver-barked, their branches still bare but swelling with the green promise of April. Papa trees, she thought, and then corrected herself. Not anymore. Just trees now. But her feet knew orchard ground the way her tongue knew English, and she wandered up between the rows with her hands in her pockets.

At the top of the slope she could see down over the inn yard, and she saw Mr. Callow come out of the stable block where the motorcar was housed, adjusting his coat, moving quick and light, and she saw him pause by the inn's gate and take out paper and pencil.

Nice hands write fast, she thought, when they've got something to say.

She didn't run. Running made people look. She walked back down the rows at the speed of a bored child, which was its own kind of acting, and found Elodie elbow-deep in machinery.

"He's writing a letter," Wren said. "Or a telegram. He went to the gate and wrote something small and folded it and put it in his pocket, and now he's talking to the ostler, and the ostler has gone toward the telegraph office on foot."

Elodie's head came up so fast she clipped it on the bonnet, and the curse she produced this time was not elegant at all.

"Wren." She wiped her hands on a rag, slowly, buying time to think. Wren could see her doing sums behind her eyes — how far, how fast, whether the car would run, whether running at all would make it worse. "How sure are you?"

"He bought me a ginger beer and called me poppet and didn't drink with us or eat with us and his hands are soft and he never once opened his newspaper."

"That," said Elodie, "is the most damning catalogue of evidence I have ever heard, and I include the entire case against Magistrate Harrow." She slammed the bonnet. "The magneto's dry-jointed. I can get her turning in twenty minutes if I wrap it, ten if I don't care about burning the house down."

"Don't burn the house down," Wren said. "Inns are expensive."

Elodie stared at her and then laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, bright in the cold morning air. "Where do you *come* from?" she said, and ruffled Wren's hair, and for one moment Wren let her, because it was nearly as good as Papa's hand, nearly, if you didn't think too hard.

Then Elodie's face went serious and stayed there.

"Wren. Listen to me. If I'm wrong about this man, we'll have wasted twenty minutes and a perfectly good excuse for second breakfast, and no harm done. But if I'm right—" She crouched down so they were level, and her eyes were the colour of strong tea, steady. "If I'm right, then someone told him about us before we ever arrived here, and that means the trouble is ahead of us, not behind, and I need you to promise me something. If I say the word *orchard*, you go uphill into those trees and you keep going until you can't hear the road. You don't wait for me. You don't come back for me."

"No," said Wren.

"Wren—"

"Papa said the same thing to Mama. Go, don't wait for me. And she waited, and they took her too, and I was alone for eleven days before the lady brought me to your door." Wren's chin was up. Her voice only shook a little, which she counted as a triumph. "So you can say orchard all you like. I'll go into the trees if there's real trouble, because I'm not stupid. But I'll be able to see the road from the top of the hill, and I'll know what happened, and you can't stop me knowing."

For a moment neither of them moved. Somewhere below, a horse stamped; the inn woke in small sounds, a pump handle, a door.

"All right," Elodie said at last, softly. "New terms. We stay together unless there's no choice, and neither of us plays the hero. Deal?"

"Deal," said Wren, and spat in her palm, because Papa had taught her that bargains weren't real until you spat, and Elodie looked at the offered hand with an expression of profound theological struggle before shaking it anyway.

* * *

The magneto took nineteen minutes. Wren held the lamp and handed Elodie the wire, the tape, the rag, and the wrong spanner twice, and at seven minutes past seven the motorcar coughed, blasphemed, and caught, settling into its grumble like an old dog circling twice before lying down.

They were rolling out of the yard when Wren saw it: Mr. Callow at the breakfast-room window, teacup raised, watching them go. He lifted the cup to them, pleasant as Sunday.

And he smiled with every tooth he had.

"There's a telegraph office in Headcorn," Elodie said, eyes forward, hands easy on the wheel, voice tight as wire. "And a constable. And a level crossing where a motorcar must stop, which is where I would arrange to meet a motorcar, if I were a man with soft hands and a grudge against my own face."

"So we don't take the road."

"We take the road as far as the mill, and after the mill there's a lane — I saw it on the map — that cuts up through the orchards toward Sutton Valence. It will be slow and it will be rough and the car will hate every inch." She glanced sideways, and there was that almost-smile again, but strained at the corners now. "You wouldn't happen to know orchard country, would you? Any chance you could navigate by tree?"

Wren looked up at the slopes unrolling ahead of them, the long ranks of bare apples climbing away into mist, ordered and ancient, planted in lines by men who'd understood patience.

"I could find my way through an orchard blindfolded," she said, "in fog, upside down. Papa said I had sap in my blood instead of proper blood."

"Sap in the blood." Elodie nodded gravely. "That explains a great deal about you."

The mill appeared where the map promised, its wheel turning slow and dripping, and past it the lane bent away from the road and up between hedgerows into the trees. Elodie slowed, checked the mirror — empty, for now — and turned off the hard road onto earth and old leaf-mould, and the motorcar shuddered like a dowager asked to wade a stream but went.

The orchards closed over them.

It was different from the road. On the road you were exposed, a beetle on a plate; here the trees leaned in overhead, knitting their branches into a low grey-green roof, and the light came down dappled and green-gold even at this hour, and the air smelled of wet wood and last year's windfalls going sweetly back into the earth. Wren rolled down her window and breathed it in, and something in her chest that had been clenched since November loosened one careful notch.

"This is what it smells like at home," she said. "Said. What it smelled like. At home."

Elodie said nothing, but her hand left the wheel for a moment and found Wren's knee and pressed it, once, and went back to the wheel, and that was enough. That was the right amount.

They climbed for an hour, the lane worsening, the trees older, until the motorcar could go no further and Elodie declared it a rest, not a defeat. They walked the last stretch, Wren leading by instinct more than memory — these weren't Papa's trees, but they were somebody's trees, planted by the same kind of somebody — until they came out on a ridge above a hamlet strung along a stream, smoke rising from chimneys, hens arguing in a yard.

And below, at the crossing where the lane met the Ashford road, a motorcar stood waiting. A dark motorcar with two men leaning against it, smoking, patient as cats at a mousehole. Watching the road. The road they should have been on.

"They didn't follow us," Wren whispered. "They went ahead."

"They went ahead." Elodie sank down slowly behind the hedgerow, pulling Wren beside her, and for a moment she just breathed, staring at the men below. Then she laughed, very quietly, a shaky sound. "A child out-thought a professional informer before breakfast. When you testify someday, Wren Bell, do let them record that."

"I'm not testifying. You're testifying." Wren frowned. "Aren't you?"

Something crossed Elodie's face then — quick, like a bird's shadow — and didn't leave entirely. She looked at the papers' hiding place, at the road, at the smoke rising from the safe little hamlet, and she said, "Yes. Of course. Eventually. First we get to London." She stood, brushed off her skirt, and held out her hand, and her grip when Wren took it was warm and certain. "Come on. We'll ask at the farm for bread and directions, and we'll trust precisely no one with beautiful hands."

But when they came down into the hamlet, the first person in the farmyard — a woman with flour to her elbows, drawn outside by the impossible sight of a motorcar parked in her orchard lane — took one look at Wren's face and went white, and dropped into a curtsy to nobody, and said:

"Lord preserve us. You're his. You're the Bell girl. We thought you were dead with the rest of them."

And behind her, pinned to the barn door, flapping in the morning wind, was a printed notice, and on it Wren could read her own description, and beneath it, in letters taller than her hand: **TRAVELLING WITH AN ACCESSORY TO THEFT — REWARD FOR INFORMATION — BY ORDER OF MAGISTRATE HARROW.**
