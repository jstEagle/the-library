## 12. Marguerite Arrested

Marguerite Voss had been arrested twice before in her life, and she had developed theories about it.

The first time — Manchester, 1908 — had been an accident of geography. She had been standing on a pavement that the police decided, retroactively and with great enthusiasm, was obstructed. The second time had been deliberate, a matter of principle and newspaper columns, and she had worn her best grey coat for the occasion because she intended the photograph to look dignified.

This third arrest, on a bright Thursday morning in October 1913, was neither accident nor principle. It was strategy. She understood that almost at once, the way one understands a chess move not by seeing it but by feeling the board tilt.

"Mrs. Voss," said Sergeant Ellery, standing in her front hall with his hat in his hands like a man delivering flowers rather than catastrophe, "I'm very sorry about this."

"Sergeant," said Marguerite pleasantly, "you are holding your hat wrong for an apology and right for an arrest. Which is it?"

He flushed. Behind him, two constables stood on her gravel path looking as though they'd rather be anywhere else, which she supposed was some small comfort. She had known most of these men for fifteen years. She had driven past their mothers' cottages, paid their sisters to do her laundry, watched their sons grow up stealing apples from hedgerows. Arresting Marguerite Voss was rather like arresting the church clock — technically possible, but it disturbed something in the natural order.

"The charge is obstruction," Ellery said. "And... breach of the peace. In relation to the meeting at Ashford last week."

"The meeting I did not attend?"

"There are witnesses, ma'am."

"Witnesses who saw me where I was not." She kept her voice light. She was rather good at light. It was one of the three voices she owned — light, firm, and the third one, which she used perhaps four times a year and never without consequence. "How industrious of them."

Ellery's flush deepened. He was not a clever man, but he was an honest one within the limits of his profession, and honesty made him terrible at this. His eyes went to the stairs behind her, then away again, quickly.

"Is Miss Almeida at home?" he asked, and there it was.

The board tilted. Marguerite felt it in her stomach, that cold sliding sensation, and smiled through it the way she'd learned to smile through hunger strikes and hecklers and her late husband's relatives.

"Elodie is out," she said. "She drives me, Sergeant; you know that. Where would she be if I am here?"

That was, of course, precisely the question they wanted answered, and she had just handed it to them gift-wrapped in innocence. She watched Ellery understand this, and watched him also understand that he could not un-ask it.

"We'll need to search the house, ma'am."

"Of course you will. Mind the blue room — the wallpaper is older than the Queen and nearly as fragile. And do not touch the letter case on my desk. That's locked, and the key is in my pocket, and I shall be delighted to describe its contents to any magistrate who asks."

* * *

They took her through the village in the plain trap rather than the Black Maria, another courtesy that was really an insult — everyone would see her, and everyone did. Mrs. Harkess from the bakery pressed a hand to her mouth. The schoolchildren lined the fence like sparrows, solemn as little judges. Old Mr. Tunn, who had been feuding with Marguerite over a boundary wall since the previous century, removed his cap and held it against his chest until she passed, which nearly undid her entirely.

*The cost of the cause,* she thought, sitting straight-backed in the trap while the lanes unwound around her. *I have spoken those words a hundred times from a hundred platforms. The cost of the cause is worth the prize of the cause.* It had always been abstract currency when she spent it. Prison was a story she told other women about. Hunger strike was a chapter in a memoir she hadn't yet written. Now the bill had arrived at her own address, and she found — this surprised her — that she wasn't afraid for herself at all.

She was afraid for Elodie.

Because Harrow had done the arithmetic, and so could she. A suffragist magistrate's wife somewhere would have been charged on general principles. But *Marguerite Voss*, whose meetings were temperate, whose subscriptions were paid promptly, whose name appeared in newspapers beside words like *measured* and *formidable* — Marguerite Voss did not get arrested on fabricated charges unless someone wanted something. And what Harrow wanted, obviously, beautifully, terribly, was to cut the thread between the woman and the girl. No funds. No counsel. No letters. Take the spider out of the web and see how long the fly lasts alone on the road to London.

*She has nine days,* Marguerite thought, watching the county gaol come into view, grey and squat among the elms like a toad waiting for rain. *Or fewer, now. She'll have counted better than I did.*

*Good,* she thought, and meant it, and was frightened by how much she meant it. *Good girl.*

* * *

The cell was cleaner than she'd expected, which she attributed to her lawyer's letters over the years having made the local gaol vaguely embarrassed in her presence. There was a cot, a stool, a window with a view of the exercise yard, and a wardress named Mrs. Prewitt who had once been a subscriber to *The Suffragist* before her husband found out and burned it.

"They say you're in for obstruction," Mrs. Prewitt said, locking the door with ceremonial slowness.

"I am in for being useful to someone the magistrate dislikes. It's a common crime among women of my acquaintance. We commit it constantly."

"Hmph," said Mrs. Prewitt, which in her language was an amen, and left a second blanket on the shelf though regulations said one.

Marguerite sat on the stool and permitted herself, for exactly ninety seconds by the yard's clock, the luxury of despair. She let it move through her like weather: fear for Elodie, guilt about Tomas Bell — *thirty years ago, and still it follows me down every corridor* — and the old, familiar shame of the privileged, who could afford for their causes to be abstract because someone else paid in blood. Then the ninety seconds ended, as she had trained them to end since Manchester, and she folded the despair up small and put it away and began to think.

First: they would interrogate her, gently at first, then less so, about the child. About documents. About where her motorcar was and why its mileage didn't match her diary. They would be polite about it because she was gentry, but polite interrogation is still interrogation.

Second: she must tell them nothing, and more importantly, she must *not lie*, because lies were traps for the liar and Harrow knew all the tricks of that garden. Silence was defensible. Silence was her oldest skill.

Third — and this was the thought that made her smile, alone in her cell like a madwoman, so that Mrs. Prewitt, passing, gave her a wary glance —

Third: Harrow believed he was punishing her. But he had made an error of classification. He had put her in a cell thinking a cell was a punishment. For thirty years Marguerite Voss had wondered whether she was brave enough for the things she asked of braver women. Now she would find out, at fifty-eight, in a clean cell with a spare blanket and a view of the yard, and whatever else happened, Harrow had given her the answer free of charge.

"You're smiling," Mrs. Prewitt observed on her evening round, bringing bread and a jug of water thin enough to read through.

"I'm doing arithmetic," said Marguerite. "It always makes me smile when it comes out in my favour."

* * *

They came for her on the second day.

Not Ellery — he had been replaced after the fiasco of the front hall, replaced by a solicitor's clerk with a leather folder and a face like a closed shop. He introduced himself as Mr. Crake, representing interests he did not name, and sat down across the table from her in the visiting room with the air of a man who had rehearsed being reasonable.

"Mrs. Voss. This needn't be unpleasant."

"Mr. Crake. It's already unpleasant; I'm wearing gaol linen. Do go on."

He laid out photographs on the table, squared them like cards. Her house. Her motorcar. The Ashford road. And — her heart did something complicated and stayed perfectly still on the outside — a photograph of a small girl, taken at what looked like a fair or a market, blurry, unaware.

"A child was seen at your property," Crake said. "We'd like to know her name."

"Would you?" Marguerite looked at the photograph for a long moment, letting him watch her look, letting him think the stillness was calculation rather than grief — Wren at a fair, Wren laughing at something out of frame, Wren with jam on her chin in Marguerite's kitchen calling her *the lady with too many books*. "I don't know her."

"You don't know her."

"I employ a chauffeur. Chauffeurs have nieces, cousins, strays. I sign the wage book and mind my own affairs. It's the great privilege of my class, minding one's own affairs. I recommend it."

Crake turned a page in his folder. "Your chauffeur left two days ago in your motorcar. She hasn't returned. Your household staff say she took clothing belonging to a child and provisions enough for a week." He looked up. "You've funded worse journeys than this one, Mrs. Voss. Manchester. Bristol. The WSPU's legal fund knows your handwriting very well."

"My philanthropy," Marguerite said, "is extensive and entirely public. I fund schools, hospitals, and the vote. If I funded anything else, Mr. Crake, you may be certain I'd never leave a paper trail — and your file there is nothing *but* paper trail. Subscriptions, minutes, letters I signed with my own name because I have never yet done a thing worth hiding." She leaned forward. "You are hunting a girl and a child across three counties on the strength of a magistrate's nerves. Ask yourself what he's so afraid a child might carry. Because I have asked myself, and I know, and I will carry the knowledge comfortably to whatever sentence you care to give me."

There was a silence. Crake gathered his photographs with slightly less squareness than he'd laid them down.

"You could help yourself," he said at the door. "Cooperate, and the charge softens. Obstruction becomes a fine. You'd be home by Sunday."

"Mr. Crake," said Marguerite, "I have been home by Sunday many times. It is overrated."

* * *

The days arranged themselves into a rhythm, and the rhythm became a strange kind of peace.

She rose when Mrs. Prewitt rattled the door. She walked the yard in the hour allowed, nodding to the other prisoners — two poachers, a drunk, and a girl of nineteen committed for throwing a brick through a recruiting-office window, who looked at Marguerite's grey head with such naked hope that Marguerite spent half an hour teaching her how to hold her silence under questioning, the way one teaches a dance step. She ate the bread. She read the Bible left in the cell, not from piety but because Deuteronomy was excellent material and she was composing speeches in her head against the day she needed them.

At night she lay on the cot and talked to Elodie in her head, which she suspected was a habit she would not easily break even when — *when*, she insisted, *not if* — she was free.

*You will want to turn back,* she told the ceiling. *When it goes badly, when the checkpoints close like teeth, when you look at that child asleep in the motorcar and feel the whole weight of what you've undertaken — you will want to drive home to a house that no longer has me in it, and you will think that proves you were sensible all along and history happens elsewhere. It doesn't. It never did. You've been carrying history in your coat pocket since the night she arrived on your step.*

*I should have told you everything. About Tomas. About why my silence was bought and who bought it. I told myself I was protecting you. That is what cowards call protection, Elodie, and I have been a coward in exactly one direction my whole life, and it was always that direction.*

*So: I refuse to name you. Whatever they ask. Let that be the thing I finally get right.*

On the fifth day they tried again, this time with a magistrate present, this time with the word *conspiracy* spoken aloud in a tone meant to sound casual. Marguerite declined to be casual. Asked for the name of the child, she said: "I decline." Asked whether she had financed Elodie Almeida's journey, she said: "I finance my chauffeur's wages, which are, incidentally, now owed." Asked whether she knew of documents relating to the Bell case, she paused — only a heartbeat, but Crake pounced on it like a cat on a dropped stitch — and said:

"I know that Tomas Bell was hanged eleven years ago for burning an orchard that a dry summer and a careless neighbour burned for him. Everyone in this county knows it. We simply agreed, all of us, not to know it out loud. That is not conspiracy, sir. That is manners. The English are famous for it."

The magistrate wrote something down. Marguerite watched his pen move and thought, with a calm that alarmed even her: *You are writing my testimony for me. Every question you cannot bear is another line of it.*

* * *

On the seventh evening, Mrs. Prewitt brought the supper tray and set it down and did not leave immediately. She stood by the door, keys quiet in her hand, in the manner of a woman arranging words in order before releasing them.

"There's a prisoner's privileges form," she said at last. "Next of kin notification. Standard paperwork. You're entitled to send one letter, one per fortnight, subject to inspection."

"I know the rules, Mrs. Prewitt. I helped write half of the complaints about them."

"Well." The wardress examined the floor. "Form wants a name and address. Thought you might like paper and a pencil stub. No reason. Regulations say I've got to offer. Never said regulations say when."

She produced a grubby envelope, a single sheet, and a pencil sharpened with what looked like a bread knife. Marguerite took them as though receiving a decoration from the crown.

"Thank you, Mrs. Prewitt."

"Hmph," said Mrs. Prewitt, and locked the door with unusual gentleness.

Marguerite sat on the cot with the paper on her knees and considered. The letter would be read. Of course it would be read — Harrow's people would hold it to the light before the wax on her seal was cold. So it must contain nothing. Nothing about roads, checkpoints, tribunals, children, ledgers, London. Nothing that could be a map.

But it could contain one thing. One true thing, deniable, ordinary, that would reach Elodie if Elodie ever heard of it, and that would tell her everything without saying anything at all.

She thought for a long time. Then she smoothed the paper and wrote, in her upright, unhurried hand:

*My dear Mrs. Harkess —*

*Forgive the informality of writing from here, but the view from my window puts me in mind of your bakery, and of what you told me once about bread: that the loaf always knows when the baker is afraid, and refuses to rise for her. I have thought of that daily. Fear spoils the dough; patience raises it. Tell anyone who asks after me that I am resting well, eating regularly, and remain, as ever, confident in the oven.*

*Yours faithfully,*
*M. Voss*

She read it twice. *Confident in the oven.* Elodie would understand — Elodie, who had sat in that same kitchen while Mrs. Harkess lectured them both on the moral philosophy of sourdough. *Keep baking. Keep going. Don't let them smell fear on the bread.*

And more: *one letter, subject to inspection* — a reminder that Elodie's own letters were watched, that every post between here and London now passed under Harrow's eye, and that she must trust nothing written and no one convenient. And more still, underneath everything, the simple fact Marguerite had paid a stamp to deliver: *I am alive, I am unbroken, and I have not named you.*

She folded the letter. Addressed it to the bakery, of all innocent places — Mrs. Harkess would puzzle over it, show it to her husband, and eventually, because she was canny under all that flour, pass a version of its contents down the village grapevine until it reached whichever ears needed it.

Then Marguerite lay back on the cot, folded her hands, and listened to the yard clock count the eighth night.

*Nine days since the tribunal notice. Two spent driving, five spent here. Which means she is nearly out of road, my girl, and out of friends, and out of money, and the last checkpoint ahead of her belongs to the man himself.*

*Well,* Marguerite thought, staring into the dark with her eyes open, *so was I ever the last checkpoint, once, and look how well I served him. Thirty years of silence, and the truth walked in my front door anyway wearing a child's coat.*

Somewhere beyond the walls, in the dark of an orchard country she could not see, Elodie was either turning back or driving on.

Marguerite closed her eyes and placed, for the first time in her careful life, the entire weight of her hope on another person's hands.
