## 13. The Clerk's Conscience

Daniel Foy kept the county records office in Ashford the way a spider keeps a web — patiently, invisibly, and with an air of having been there since before the building itself. I had seen him twice before in my life: once when Marguerite had gone to contest a rate assessment and won points she had no business winning against a man who clearly knew where every file slept, and once at the county fete, where he'd stood at the edge of the coconut shy for forty minutes without throwing a single ball, watching everyone else miss.

He was not a man you noticed. That was rather the point of him. Magistrate Harrow's clerk — the third checkpoint on our road to London — was designed by God and the Civil Service to be furniture.

Which is why, on the sixth day of my nine, I sat across a greasy table from him in the back room of the Ashford tea rooms and said, "Mr. Foy, I believe you've been committing a crime."

The effect was everything I might have hoped for and several things besides. His teacup, halfway to his mouth, developed a tremor that sloshed tea into the saucer with a sound like small applause. He set it down. He picked it up again, thought better of it, and folded his hands instead, which were long and pale and ink-stained at the second finger of the right hand — the hand of a man who writes all day, every day, for thirty years.

"I'm sure I don't know what you mean, Mrs.—"

"Miss Almeida." I let it sit there. "Chauffeur to Mrs. Voss. And formerly, I suspect, the sort of person you were told never to look at directly."

Something moved behind his spectacles. Not guilt exactly. Recognition, maybe, of what he was — the man whose whole profession was being looked through.

"I really must ask—"

"You keep a duplicate ledger," I said.

I want it recorded, wherever such things are recorded, that this was a guess. A good guess, but a guess. I had reasoned it out on the road from the hop-pickers' camp, lying awake under a canvas roof while Wren slept against my arm: *a man who watches other people miss at the coconut shy is a man who wants very badly to throw and cannot.* And men like Daniel Foy — careful, invisible, decent in the way of people who have never been given permission to be anything else — do not survive thirty years beside a man like Harrow without either rotting or hiding something. The rotters become Harrows. The others keep accounts.

His face did something complicated. It was like watching a house decide whether to bolt its doors or open them.

"I don't know who told you—" he began.

"Nobody told me," I said. "That's rather the trouble with this whole affair. Nobody ever tells anybody anything. They leave children on doorsteps instead."

And here I must confess something about myself that surprised even me: I lied beautifully to Constable Pike, but to Daniel Foy, in that tea room smelling of burnt toast and floor polish, I found I didn't want to. There is a kind of man you lie to because he is your enemy, and a kind you lie to because it is easier than being honest, and then there is a third kind, rare, before whom honesty feels like the only weapon that will cut.

"Three years ago," I said, quieter, "Tomas Bell was tried and hanged for the fire at Harrow's own tenancy at Millbrook. I hold papers proving the fire was set to cover Harrow's own debts, that witnesses were paid, and that the magistrate who sentenced Bell knew it. Those papers are real, Mr. Foy, and they are going to London whether you stand in the way or not. But I think — I only think — that somewhere in that records office there is a second set of books. Kept by a clerk. Kept at night, after hours, in a hand steadier than his conscience."

The silence went on so long that the girl behind the counter brought us fresh tea neither of us had asked for, set it down with the brisk sympathy of one who has seen many difficult conversations end in toast, and withdrew.

Then Daniel Foy reached into his coat.

I will admit my heart did something undignified. In my experience of the last six days, coats yield knives, warrants, and constables. But what came out was a notebook — cheap, gray-covered, fat with inserted papers — and he laid it on the table between us with both hands, as one lays down a wounded bird.

"Not a ledger," he said. His voice had changed; it had dropped its clerical varnish. "Ledgers are official things. This is..." He turned it around to face me and opened it. "This is thirty years of not being able to sleep."

* * *

Here is what was in the book, and I will set it down plainly because it deserves plainness:

Page after page, in Foy's neat clerk's hand, of everything Harrow had ever said or done within earshot that did not bear inspection. Sums taken. Sums hidden. Names of witnesses and what they'd been paid, cross-referenced against what they'd sworn. Dates. Amounts. Against the entry for the Millbrook fire, dated two days after Bell's trial, a single line in smaller writing, pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn it:

*Bell swore his innocence to the last. H. laughed about it in chambers. I wrote the minutes of that laughter.*

"He doesn't know," Foy said. "About this. Obviously. If he knew I'd be— well." He adjusted his spectacles with a finger that trembled slightly. "You understand why I never threw a ball at the fete. One gets out of the habit of letting go of things."

Despite everything — despite six days of fear worn thin over resolve — I felt the corner of my mouth move. "How many coconut shies have you attended, Mr. Foy?"

"Fourteen," he said, with dignity. "It's a good fete. The brass band plays Sousa."

"And in fourteen years of watching other people miss, did it ever occur to you simply to *send* this? To anyone? A newspaper. A bishop. Anyone."

"It occurred to me every night for thirty years," said Daniel Foy, "and every morning I got up and came to work, because a clerk who acts on what he knows is a clerk who loses his position, his pension, and quite possibly his liberty, whereas a clerk who keeps quiet keeps all three and loses only—" he tapped the gray notebook— "this. Sleep. Self-respect. The ability to look at my wife across the breakfast table without feeling she can see straight through me, though she can't; she thinks I'm worried about the drainage at the office." He paused. "She's wrong, but it's a comfortable wrong, and I've let her have it a long while."

I thought of Marguerite then. I confess it — sitting in a tea room with a stranger's confession open on the table, I thought of Marguerite Voss in her careful silence, buying safety year upon year and calling it prudence, and I understood her a little better and forgave her a little less, which I believe is called growing up.

"Why now?" I asked. "If you've kept this thirty years, why does my saying *duplicate ledger* crack you open like an egg?"

Foy looked out the window at the Ashford high street, at the market stalls and the boys loitering by the pump, at the ordinary world going about its ordinary business above the buried weight of what he carried.

"Because you named it," he said. "Thirty years I've told myself it was a private matter. Conscience is a funny thing, Miss Almeida. Kept alone, it's just indigestion. But when another person says the word out loud — *you kept a record* — then it stops being indigestion and becomes evidence. Evidence wants things. It wants to go places." He closed the notebook and rested his hand flat upon it. "You should know that yours isn't the only copy in the world of what happened at Millbrook. Mine is older. Mine goes back further than whatever those poor souls gave you. Mine has the *payments* in it."

"The payments," I repeated. My voice sounded far away to me. On the road, in the dark of the motorcar, Wren had asked me once whether the men who took her father would remember him, and I had said yes, that remembering was the one thing guilty men could not help. I had not known how right I was. "You have names."

"I have every name," said Daniel Foy, with the first shadow of something that was almost pride, and it was heartbreaking, that pride, pride in the mere act of having written down the truth and hidden it in a drawer for three decades like a man hoarding letters he'll never post. "Harrow paid four men to swear. Two are dead. One emigrated. And one—" he drew a breath— "one is Constable Pike's brother-in-law, which may explain certain attentions you received at the crossroads checkpoint."

So he knew about that too. Of course he knew. A clerk hears everything; hearing is most of what a clerk is *for*.

"They stopped us," I said. "Pike looked straight into this car and let us pass, and I've spent two days wondering if I was brilliant or lucky."

"Neither. Pike telephoned Harrow from the box at the crossroads, and Harrow told him to let you through."

The tea room went very still around me. The clock over the counter ticked with enormous patience, the way clocks do when they know something you don't.

"Why?" I said. "Why would he—"

"Because," said Foy, gently, the way one delivers a diagnosis, "a tribunal that receives no evidence dismisses itself, and nobody is embarrassed. But a woman who arrives with papers that a magistrate's own constable waved through — that woman can be *dealt with*. Charged. Discredited. Made into a story about hysterical females and stolen documents. He doesn't want your papers stopped, Miss Almeida. He wants them *arriving*. He wants to know exactly what he's burying."

I sat with that. Six days of running, hiding documents in engine housings, forging laundry lists with a child's quick hands — and we had been permitted. Escorted, nearly. Every gate opened onto a larger trap, and I had walked through each one congratulating myself on my cleverness, while at the far end of the corridor a man in magistrate's robes waited with the lid of the box in his hands.

Except.

"Foy," I said — I had stopped using the Mister, and he had not objected — "he expects papers. Papers can be discredited, stolen, burned. What does he do about a witness?"

Foy went very white. It was extraordinary to watch; the color left his face in orderly stages, like a tide going out.

"A witness," he said carefully, "would have to be someone who could speak to the facts firsthand. Someone with standing. Preferably someone respectable, because his counsel will make nothing of a servant or a—"

"Woman," I supplied. "Yes, I've met the type of counsel. Go on."

"—and any witness could simply be *denied*, you see. Called a liar, a thief, an abductor of children. Unless—" and here the tide came back in, all at once, and something new appeared in Daniel Foy's face, something I suspect neither of us had seen there before and were both slightly alarmed to find — "unless the witness brought corroboration that could not be denied. Original records. Official records. Records kept in the ordinary course of duty by an officer of the court, produced under oath by the officer himself, who would then be asked, under examination, to account for why such records existed outside the official files."

The clock ticked. Somewhere in the kitchen a pan clattered, and the smell of burnt toast renewed itself bravely.

"You'd lose everything," I said. "Position. Pension. You said so yourself. Your wife thinks you're worried about the drainage."

"My wife," said Foy, "has been married to me for twenty-six years and deserves to finally hear about the drainage." He put both hands flat on the table, and I noticed they had stopped trembling. "Here is what will happen, Miss Almeida, and you will listen carefully because we will get one attempt at it. Day after tomorrow, all incoming evidence passes through the tribunal's receiving office, and I am the examining officer for the southern circuit. My signature on the manifest means your papers travel unopened and unquestioned to London. That is the third checkpoint, and it is me, and I have spent thirty years being the most reliable furniture in this county."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I resign my position, citing health. Tomorrow evening I copy nothing and destroy nothing and change nothing, because a record altered is a record worthless. Tomorrow night I take the early train as far as it goes and walk the rest, and on the morning of the hearing, I will be sitting on a bench outside the tribunal doors with a gray notebook and thirty years of insomnia, waiting to be called."

I looked at this man — this gray, careful, invisible man who had watched fourteen coconut shies and missed nothing — and felt my throat do something inconvenient.

"Why?" I managed. "Truly. Thirty years is a long time to hold a thing. Most men would die holding it."

He was quiet a moment. Then he took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, and without them his face looked strangely young and undefended, like a house with its shutters open.

"There was a boy once," he said, "who worked in my father's shop. Clever with figures. I taught him double-entry myself, of a Sunday. When a sum went missing — twelve shillings, it was, and later I found it hadn't gone missing at all, Father had miscounted — Father accused him anyway, publicly, and I stood there and said nothing, because I was seventeen and afraid, and the boy was dismissed with no reference and walked out of town carrying his box. Tomas Bell," he added, seeing my face change. "His mother's people took the name when they moved to the orchard country. I didn't connect it — wouldn't connect it — until the trial. And then I read the transcript, and I heard that same silence, my own silence, thirty years older and wearing a wig, and I went home and started a notebook."

Outside, the market bell rang the hour, and somewhere a cart horse complained about the price of oats, and the ordinary world went on, and Daniel Foy put his spectacles back on and became a clerk again — but a different one.

"Go and collect your papers, Miss Almeida," he said. "Wherever you're keeping them. And keep the child away from Ashford between now and the hearing; Harrow's reach is shorter than his malice, but not much shorter. Day after tomorrow, bring your documents to the receiving office at half past nine, and I will examine them with exemplary thoroughness and find absolutely nothing worth reporting."

"And if Harrow countermands you? If he's already suspicious?"

Foy smiled. It transformed him entirely; I saw at once that it was a rare article, that smile, saved up like the notebook.

"Magistrates," he said, "may command anything they like. But the record, Miss Almeida — the record is mine. It has always been mine. That is the one thing he never understood about clerks." He stood, buttoned his coat, and tucked the gray notebook inside it, against his heart as it were, though I suspected that was merely where it fit. "We don't serve the men who give the orders. We serve the paper. And the paper remembers."

He settled his hat, wished me good day with perfect propriety, and went out into the high street, where he passed, I observed, the coconut shy's winter storage shed without so much as a glance — a man, at last, done watching others miss.

I sat alone with two cold teas and the wreckage of my plan, which lay about the table in pieces, every piece improved. We had meant to smuggle papers past a checkpoint. Instead we would arrive — openly, on the appointed morning, at the front door — escorted by the checkpoint itself. Harrow expected a frightened woman to be caught. He would receive, instead, a resignation letter and a subpoena-shaped problem.

There remained exactly one difficulty, and it was shaped like a seven-year-old girl asleep in the motorcar at the edge of town who had made me promise, hand on heart over the gearstick, that I would tell her everything before I told anyone else.

I paid for the toast — someone ought to, in this story — and went to wake her.

"Elodie," she said, before her eyes were fully open, in the flat tone of a child who has already read the ending, "you've got a *face*. Whose fault is it?"

"Wren," I said, buckling her coat, "have you ever heard of double-entry bookkeeping?"

She considered this, thumbs hooked in her straps, the way she considered everything — as a possible weapon.

"No," she said. "But I already don't trust it."

"That," I said, starting the engine, "is precisely the spirit that's going to see us through Thursday."

What I did not tell her — what I would not learn until the fog-bound streets of London taught it — was that papers and ledgers, however honest, however brave the clerk who carries them, are only half a case. A tribunal wants a voice attached to its evidence. And the voice it wanted was mine.
