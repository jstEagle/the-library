## 14. The Third Checkpoint

Daniel Foy had ink on his fingers the way other men had scars — earned, permanent, and quietly telling.

Elodie noticed it first at the county depot in Ashford, two years earlier, when she'd driven Marguerite down to argue about a disputed land valuation and had stood about in the parcel room while the great man himself — Magistrate Harrow, that is — held court from a chair that had been carried in specially for him. Foy had been the one doing everything: carrying the ledger, fetching the ledger, opening the ledger, licking his thumb to turn the pages of the ledger. He had looked, Elodie remembered thinking at the time, like a man who had mistaken his whole life for a waiting room.

Now he was standing in the doorway of the inspection shed at the Ashford mail depot with a clipboard in one hand and her future in the other, and he still looked like that. Except for his eyes. His eyes had changed.

"You'll want to open the bonnet," Elodie said, before he could say anything at all. She had learned by checkpoint two that the fastest way through a search was to offer more than they dared ask. Men expected a woman to guard things. Give a man a thing freely and he assumes it was never hidden at all.

Foy didn't move. The shed was cold and smelled of paraffin and damp sacking, and somewhere behind the partition wall a sorting clerk was whistling something mournful and out of tune. "Mrs. Almeida."

"Miss," said Elodie automatically. "Almeida. Miss Almeida."

"Miss Almeida." He said it as though committing it to memory for purposes she would rather he didn't. His gaze went past her shoulder to the motorcar, where Wren sat in the back seat with a picture book she couldn't read upside down and an expression of studied boredom that would have fooled nobody above the age of four. "You've come up from Kent."

"We have. Is there a difficulty?"

"There is always a difficulty." He stepped aside, gesturing her into the shed. "That's rather the point of me."

* * *

She had known, of course, that the third checkpoint was Foy. She had known since the hop-pickers' kitchen on the road to Ashford, when old Mrs. Pargeter had stirred her tea and said, in the tone of a woman reporting weather, *Harrow's clerk inspects every post leaving the eastern districts now. Every sack. Poor devil.* And she had known what it meant: that the last door between the documents sewn into Wren's coat lining and the tribunal in London was guarded by the very man whose name appeared, in his own tidy hand, in the margins of the false ledger. *Examined and found consistent. D. Foy.*

She had spent the whole of the last forty miles composing arguments. She had prepared appeals to justice, to decency, to his immortal soul. She had rehearsed a version where she wept, which she privately suspected would be terrible, and a version where she threatened him, which she suspected would be worse.

None of them survived contact with the actual man, who closed the shed door behind them and then, to her astonishment, did not ask her to open the bonnet at all.

"I know what you're carrying," he said.

The whistling on the far side of the partition went on cheerfully. Elodie heard her own pulse. "I'm sure I don't know what you mean, Mr. Foy."

"No," he agreed. "I imagine you're sure of a great many things." He set the clipboard down on the inspection table, squared it with its edge — a small gesture, fastidious, the gesture of a man who had spent twenty years squaring things with edges — and looked at her properly. "The Bell papers. The true ledger. The girl."

Every plan she had made emptied out of her like water through open fingers. "How—"

"Because I kept the duplicate myself, or saw to it that someone did. Because Harrow has been asking after a grey Napier with Kent plates since Tuesday. And because—" here he hesitated, and the ink-stained hand rose briefly to his collar, "—because seven years ago I watched a man hang, and I wrote *found consistent* in the margin of the book that killed him, and my hand did not shake, and it has not stopped shaking since."

The sorting clerk's whistle trailed off into silence. Somewhere a clock ticked with terrible patience.

"Mr. Foy," Elodie began carefully.

"You needn't give a speech," he said. There was something almost gentle in it, and something almost funny underneath, as if the absurdity of the moment had struck him too — the two of them standing in a cold shed, conspirators by accident. "I've rehearsed this scene myself, you understand. On the walk over. In the scene, you make a passionate appeal, and I resist, and eventually I am persuaded by the force of your conviction. It's a very good scene. I cried once, rehearsing it."

Elodie blinked. "Did you?"

"Not outwardly," said Foy. "Inwardly I was a fountain." He picked up the clipboard again and turned it over in his hands, and when he spoke next the lightness had gone out of him and left something quieter and much heavier in its place. "But here's what I learned on the walk. Nobody persuades anybody of anything worth doing. You simply arrive at a morning when the arithmetic won't balance anymore. When the sum of what you've swallowed comes to more than the size of your throat." He looked at the motorcar, at the small shape in the back seat pretending to read. "I've been swallowing for seven years, Miss Almeida. I find my throat is full."

* * *

What he told her then, while the cold deepened and the clock ticked and Wren's pretended reading became real reading out of sheer boredom, was this:

That Harrow had ordered full inspection of all post and traffic east of Ashford not because he suspected Elodie specifically but because he suspected everyone; guilt, Foy observed, was a generous landlord and rented rooms in every direction. That the tribunal in London had already received — from Harrow's office, with Foy's own signature at the bottom — a packet of documents establishing the official account, so that whatever arrived unsanctioned would be treated as forgery unless a sworn witness accompanied it. That the inspector at the second gate, the one Wren had so neatly defeated with a laundry list, reported everything to Harrow's people within the hour, which meant they were already three days behind their pursuers and did not know it yet.

"And that is why," Foy finished, "you cannot simply slip past me, though God knows I wish you could. If I wave you through without inspecting, Harrow will know within a day, and he will have the packet stopped in London regardless. It has to look genuine. It has to go through my hands, be examined, be recorded — *examined and found consistent*" — his mouth twisted around his own words — "and only then can it travel with any hope of arriving. Do you see? I am not the obstacle, Miss Almeida. I am the last disguise."

Elodie thought of Marguerite then, unexpectedly and painfully — Marguerite who had taught her that the safest lie is the truth arranged carefully, and who was at this hour sitting somewhere in Harrow's widening net, silent as stone. She thought: *we are all of us arranging truths now. The whole country is a parcel room.*

"What do you need from me?" she asked.

"The documents. For one hour." He said it quickly, as if speed might make it painless. "I will enter them in the register under a routine customs heading — agricultural correspondence, it's dull enough that no one ever looks twice — and I will certify them. Then I will put them back exactly where you've hidden them, better than you hid them, if it comes to that, because I have been hiding things in this shed for longer than you've owned a motorcar." A ghost of a smile. "And tomorrow morning I will sign the inspection sheet, and Harrow will read my signature, and he will sleep soundly, and by the time he wakes to discover otherwise, you'll be past Reading."

"And you?" Elodie asked. "When he discovers otherwise?"

Foy was quiet for a moment. Outside, a train shunted somewhere in the dark with a long iron sigh, and the sorting clerk resumed whistling, having apparently concluded that whatever happened in the shed was none of his business — which, Elodie reflected, was precisely the national arrangement they were all gathered here to overturn.

"When he discovers otherwise," Foy said at last, "he will look for the man who certified it, and he will find him, and that man will be standing in front of the ledger with his hat in his hands saying *yes, it was I, and I will say so at the hearing.*" He straightened the clipboard against the table edge one final time, unnecessarily, the way a man touches a thing to prove his hands are steady. "A duplicate ledger is evidence, Miss Almeida, but evidence written by a stranger is a rumour. Evidence written by the magistrate's own clerk, sworn and signed — that is a verdict waiting to happen. He'll want me discredited. He can't discredit the handwriting without discrediting seven years of his own filings. So I must come to London too. I must stand up in that room and say the words out loud."

"That will end your career," Elodie said. It wasn't a question.

"Oh, instantly," said Foy, with such brisk cheerfulness that she nearly laughed. "By luncheon, I should think. Possibly by elevenses if the newspapers are quick." And then the cheerfulness failed him all at once, like a lamp running dry, and he was just a tired man in a cold shed looking at the ruin and the freedom ahead of him in the same glance. "Twenty-two years, Miss Almeida. I entered the service at seventeen because my father said a man who keeps honest records can never be made party to a dishonest act." He turned the words over as if checking them for damage. "He was very nearly right. It took me thirty-seven years longer than it ought. But the records are honest now, or they will be, if you'll give me the hour."

Elodie looked through the shed's small window at Wren. The child had given up on the book entirely and was watching them with her chin on the seat back, those serious eyes going from Elodie to the clerk and back, measuring, the way she measured everything — the way, Elode realized, a child measures who can be trusted with the weight of a father.

Wren gave the smallest nod. It was, Elodie thought, possibly the most solemn permission ever granted in a motorcar.

"All right, Mr. Foy," she said. "One hour. And I'll want tea while I wait, because if I'm going to commit conspiracy with the instrument of my own destruction, I refuse to do it with a dry mouth."

Something in Foy's face unlocked then — not quite joy, but the cousin of it, the poor relation that lives next door. "There's a kettle," he said, "behind the sacks. It's not good tea."

"It never is," said Elodie, "at the turning points of history."

* * *

The hour took two. Foy worked with the absorbed precision of a man performing, at long last, the job he had always believed he was hired for: he copied entries, cross-referenced dates, annotated the false ledger against the true one in his small exact hand until the two books together told a story neither could tell alone. Twice he stopped, pen hovering, and Elodie thought he might fail — not from fear, she understood, watching him, but from the sheer strangeness of writing the truth after so many years of the other discipline. It was like asking a man who had walked bent double for a decade to stand upright. The spine remembers the shape of the wound.

At half past ten he laid down his pen, blotted the page, and sat back.

"Well?" said Elodie.

"It's done." He flexed his fingers, wincing. "I haven't written that much truth in one sitting since my diary at school, and that ended badly when my sister found it." He rose, gathered the documents, and restored them to the engine housing with a deftness that was frankly humiliating to watch — the false fuel-line panel reseated, the rag packing adjusted, the whole business finished in ninety seconds and invisible even to Elodie, who knew where it was. He wiped his hands on the sacking and stood a moment with his palm flat against the cold metal of the car, as if taking leave of something.

"Mr. Foy," Elodie said. "Why? Truly. Not the arithmetic of the soul — why tonight? You've had seven years of mornings."

Foy considered it. Through the window, Wren had fallen asleep mid-page, the picture book sliding from her fingers, and perhaps it was the sight of her that settled whatever he was settling.

"Last month," he said, "Harrow had me burn some correspondence. Routine, he said. Old files. And because I am thorough — because thoroughness is the one virtue I was ever certain of — I read them first." His voice stayed level, but his thumb moved along the edge of the clipboard, back and forth, back and forth. "They were letters from Tomas Bell. Petitions for clemency, written in a hand that had clearly taught itself to write. Spelling like a field full of stones, and every sentence a plea. He wrote eleven of them. Eleven letters, Miss Almeida, from a man who knew he was going to die and was not pleading for his life — did you notice? Did anyone notice? He was pleading for the orchard. *Mind the grafts on the north row,* he writes. *Whoever gets the ground, mind the grafts.*"

Foy stopped. The clock ticked. Down the shed, the sorting clerk had gone quiet at last, whether from eavesdropping or exhaustion no one would ever know.

"I burned the letters," Foy said. "I stood at the grate and I fed them in one at a time, and I minded the flame the way he asked me to mind the grafts, and I thought: *this is what I am, then. This is the whole of me. A man who burns letters and writes found consistent.*" He turned and looked at the sleeping child in the car, and his voice dropped to nearly nothing. "And then a week ago a grey Napier came through my town with a girl in it who has his chin, and I understood that the arithmetic had finally come due. You don't get to choose the morning, Mrs.— Miss Almeida. The morning chooses you. All you choose is whether you're wearing your boots."

Elodie found, to her considerable embarrassment, that her eyes were hot. She turned it into a cough, badly.

"The road to London," Foy said, briskly restoring the world to order, "is clear of Harrow's men until the Maidstone turn at earliest. Take the old Pilgrims' Way where it forks — it adds an hour and subtracts an ambush. And when you reach the tribunal—" he hesitated, and for a moment the waiting-room look returned, the little man with the ledger, "—they will want a witness. They will demand one. Whatever you do, whoever you are when you give your name — don't let them file the papers unsigned. Unsigned paper is how men like Harrow stay magistrates. Signed paper is the only weapon the ordinary ever get."

"I understand," Elodie said. And she did — she understood it in her ribs, where the understanding had been travelling for nine days and had finally arrived.

Foy opened the shed door. The night beyond was enormous and starless and smelled of coal smoke and coming rain. He handed her up into the driver's seat, and then, as she set the crank, he leaned in at the window, and the last thing he said was not about ledgers or checkpoints or careers at all.

"Tell her," he said, nodding toward the sleeping child, "when she's older — tell her the north row grafts were saved. Somebody minded them. Tell her it wasn't nobody."

The engine caught. Elodie let in the clutch, and the grey Napier rolled out of the yard into the dark, and in the mirror she watched Daniel Foy stand in the doorway of the shed with his hat in his hands, square his shoulders against the doorframe like a man setting a thing with its edge — and go back inside to write the lie that would buy them the road, knowing it was the last lie he would ever sign.

They were through the third checkpoint. Ahead lay forty miles of night, a fork at Maidstone, and a city in fog where a tribunal waited — and where, Elodie now understood with a cold settling in her stomach, the packet would be worthless without a name attached to it.

Her name. Her invisible, careful, unremarkable name.

Behind her, Wren slept on. Ahead, the road ran black toward London, and Elodie drove into it with a stolen child, a dead man's truth in her engine, and — though she did not yet know it — news waiting at the first telegraph office that would stop her heart: *Marguerite Voss taken into custody at Ashford, charged under the Cat and Mouse Act. No word since.*
