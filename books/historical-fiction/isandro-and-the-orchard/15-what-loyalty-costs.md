## 15. What Loyalty Costs

The cell at Marlborough Row police station had been designed, Marguerite decided, by a man who had never once been uncomfortable in his life. A plank bed. A blanket that smelled of wet wool and despair. A window set so high that one could see only sky — which was, she supposed, meant as either mercy or mockery.

She had been given worse accommodations at better prices.

They had taken her at half past seven in the morning, on the steps of the post office in Ashbourne, with two constables and a warrant that cited the Cat and Mouse Act as though it were scripture and they its priests. The charge was technically "incitement to unlawful assembly," which was the charge they used for women who spoke too loudly in public, and occasionally for women who merely looked as though they might. Marguerite had looked, she flattered herself, extremely as though she might. She had worn her best grey coat for the occasion, the one with the deep pockets, and she had gone with them quietly, hands folded, chin up, the very portrait of a lady being inconvenienced rather than destroyed.

That had been four days ago.

Four days of tea served in tin mugs, of bread she suspected had been baked during the previous reign, of a matron who pretended not to recognize her and then, when the shift changed, whispered *God keep you, ma'am* through the grille as though passing contraband. Four days of knowing precisely why she was here, and it was not for inciting anything. Magistrate Harrow did not care about her politics except insofar as they were useful. He cared about a motorcar that had left his county carrying papers that should have burned in an orchard fire six years ago.

He cared about Elodie.

Marguerite lay on the plank bed and watched the rectangle of sky move across the high window, and she conducted, as she had conducted every day of her adult life, an internal audit.

She was, by nature and long practice, a keeper of silences. It was the great work of her middle years, the way other women kept gardens or albums: she maintained silence the way one maintained an orchard, pruning here, propping there, patient through seasons, trusting the fruit would come. Silence had kept her fortune intact when her brother gambled. Silence had kept her name out of the newspapers when the suffrage movement grew teeth. Silence had kept Tomas Bell's memory private and therefore safe — hers alone, unwitnessed, unspoken, and therefore, she had told herself for six years, preserved.

It had not occurred to her, until quite recently, that silence could also be a kind of rot. That fruit held too long in the dark went bad in the hand.

* * *

The interrogation came on the fifth morning, after breakfast, which she suspected was deliberate — a man argues poorly on an empty stomach, and they believed this of women doubly.

Inspector Gorse was not a cruel man; that was what made him effective. He had the soft, apologetic manner of a vicar collecting for the roof fund, and he arranged his papers on the little table between them with the air of someone deeply pained to have any business with her at all.

"Mrs. Voss," he said. "Thank you for coming." She had been fetched, but there was no sense correcting him; courtesy was the only coin he had to offer and refusing it would gain her nothing. "I'll be brief. I know your time is valuable."

"My time is currently worth approximately nothing," Marguerite said pleasantly. "You've rather cornered the market."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — appreciation, quickly suppressed. He consulted his papers anyway, though she was quite sure he knew them by heart.

"You are acquainted with Magistrate Harrow."

"I have had the misfortune."

"He has brought to our attention certain irregularities. Documents missing from county records. A vehicle observed travelling south under suspicious circumstances." He looked up. "Your vehicle, Mrs. Voss."

"My vehicle is frequently travelling somewhere. It has very little imagination."

"Mrs. Voss." He folded his hands. He had kind eyes, she noticed, or eyes that had once had the potential for kindness and had let the subscription lapse. "The magistrate is prepared to be generous. This needn't go to trial. You are a woman of standing. Your health—" he glanced delicately at her fifty-one years as though checking them for cracks "—is not suited to prolonged detention. All we require is a statement. Who took the documents? Where is she now? Where were they bound?"

There it was. Laid out as neatly as he laid out his papers.

"And if I provide this statement?"

"You'd be home by Saturday. With the magistrate's gratitude and, I'm led to understand, his discretion regarding the current charges."

Home by Saturday. Marguerite sat with that. Home — meaning Voss House, cold and enormous, with its silent rooms and its single lit study. Meaning freedom, of a sort, purchased with a name.

She thought of Elodie at that moment — could not help it. Thought of her the way she had first seen her four years ago, standing in the drive at an interview for the chauffeur's position, hands gloved, spine straight, answering every question about engines and timing belts with perfect competence and volunteering nothing whatsoever about anything else. A young woman built entirely of careful surfaces. *History happens to other people,* she had said once, over the steering wheel, with the serene conviction of someone who had never been required to test the theory. Marguerite had envied her that, and pitied her for it, and said nothing — because keeping silent about other people's illusions was, after all, her specialty.

And then a child had appeared on the kitchen step with a note sewn into her coat, and the illusion had cracked down the middle like ice in March, and underneath it Marguerite had found — she had not known what to call it at first. Steadiness. Not the steadiness of habit but the steadiness of decision. Her chauffeur had stood in the study holding six years of buried truth and had asked only one question: *Where does this need to go?*

Not *should I*. Not *what will it cost me*. *Where does it need to go.*

"Mrs. Voss?" Inspector Gorse prompted gently.

"I'm considering my answer," Marguerite said. "You'll forgive me if I take my time. I find that at my age, decisions made hastily tend to require apologizing for later."

"There's nothing to apologize for. Cooperation is understood."

"Oh, I don't doubt you'd understand it. I doubt I would."

He sighed — a small, honest sigh, the sound of a man who had done this many times and found most of his subjects tiresome rather than difficult. "Let me put it plainly, then, since plainness seems to be the order of the day. If you do not assist us, the magistrate will assume the worst. He will assume you directed this. And a woman of your — history —" now he did look at the file, and she watched him choose not to read from it aloud, watched him extend that small mercy the way one tosses a bone to a dog one doesn't wish to kick, "— cannot afford his assumption. Do you follow me?"

"With perfect clarity," Marguerite said. "You're telling me that loyalty to my own interest requires me to give up a young woman whose only crime is carrying the truth somewhere it can do some good."

"I'm telling you that the truth, as you call it, is six years old and dead. Whatever happened with Bell—"

*Tomas.* His name was Tomas. He had laughed from his belly and he had shown her, in the spring of 1906, how to tell a healthy graft from a failed one, pressing his thumb to the join and saying, *there, feel — it takes, or it doesn't, no arguing with it.* He had never once asked her for anything, which was how she had known he loved her, because asking is easy and he had been far too proud for easy things. He had gone to the gallows calling a lie a lie, and she had stood in her study three counties away reading the report in *The Times*, a column inch, *local magistrate sentences orchard-keeper in arson case,* and she had closed the paper and poured a glass of sherry and said nothing to anyone for six years.

"— is finished," Gorse was saying. "Whatever documents exist, they change nothing. The man is dead. There is no remedy for the dead, Mrs. Voss. Only for the living."

"No," Marguerite agreed quietly. "There isn't. Which is rather the point, Inspector. There's no remedy for Tomas Bell. But there is a remedy for everyone who let it happen — and I have been one of them for six years, and I find I've lost my appetite for the position."

Gorse studied her for a long moment. Then he gathered his papers, squared their edges, and tucked them under his arm.

"For the record," he said, almost wearily, "you're declining to cooperate."

"For the record," Marguerite said, "I am doing the first cooperative thing I've done in years. I'm simply cooperating with the wrong side, from your point of view."

He paused at the door. Kindness surfaced briefly, like a fish. "They'll move you to Holloway by Friday. It's worse there. The food especially."

"Thank you, Inspector. I'll write to my grocer."

* * *

The cell again. The window again. Sky moving its slow rectangle from east to west, the only clock they allowed her.

She had refused. Well. That was settled, then, and strangely light was the word for how it felt — not triumphant, nothing so clean as triumph, but light, as though she had set down something she had carried so long she'd forgotten it had weight. Six years of silence about Tomas. Twenty years of silence in the movement, deferring to louder voices, funding braver ones, believing that money and quiet were her proper contributions. Silence upon silence, stacked like sandbags, and for what? For safety. For the pleasant fiction that a person could stand adjacent to history, writing cheques to it, and remain untouched.

A girl of twenty-three had driven through a police checkpoint last week with the evidence of a hanging hidden in an engine housing, and had talked her way past Constable Pike — Marguerite knew this, in whatever way one knows things in prison, through the matron's cousin who worked the toll house — and had not even had the grace to be frightened about it afterward, according to reports. *Cool as you like, ma'am, the toll man said. Asked him about his mother's rheumatism.*

Elodie Almeida, who believed history happened to other people, was learning otherwise at forty miles an hour, and Marguerite was learning the mirror lesson from a plank bed: that loyalty and silence were not the same virtue, and had perhaps never been, and that she had confused them because confusing them was comfortable.

*Loyalty* had demanded she protect Elodie by staying ignorant of the plan. Loyalty, properly understood, demanded she protect her by any means necessary — including, if it came to it, letting Harrow believe the journey was Marguerite's own design. Let him punish her for it. A woman of fifty-one, well-fed and well-read, could survive Holloway. She had marched for women who were fed by force and wrote letters with their eyelids. She could survive a bad mattress and a worse cook.

What she could not survive — what she had finally understood she could not survive — was another six years of the Times' column inches, read alone, sherry in hand, saying nothing.

The trouble, of course, was practical. They had cut her off cleanly. No letters out except through the censor, no visitors except solicitors, and Harrow's people had seen to it that the solicitor who arrived Thursday was less interested in her defence than in her signature on a statement. Her funds were frozen pending inquiry. Every road she might use to warn Elodie — that the third checkpoint was Foy, that Harrow knew, that the tribunal itself might be theatre staged for their benefit — ran through walls with bars in them.

Except.

Marguerite sat up slowly on the plank bed, and the blanket slid from her shoulders, and she was aware of a feeling she had not experienced in some years, which was the specific pleasure of a plan assembling itself.

Miss Hartwell came Thursdays. Miss Hartwell was the matron's sister-in-law, who delivered laundry and collected mending, and who had, in 1909, stood on a platform in Manchester while Constables laid into the crowd, and who had never forgotten who pulled her out of it. Marguerite had pulled her out of it. One did not fund the movement for twenty years without accruing debts in odd corners, and the beautiful thing about debts accrued among ordinary women was that no magistrate ever thought to freeze those accounts.

There was a girl called Wren in the world, and a motorcar heading south, and a clerk named Foy whom Marguerite had met twice and both times caught looking at Harrow the way one looks at a door one is deciding whether to open. There was, in short, still time — if the message moved faster than the machinery.

"Matron," Marguerite said to the empty cell, in the tone she used for dictating correspondence, and then, rising, smoothing her skirt, lifting her chin to the rectangle of sky as though it were an audience: "Matron, I should like a word about the mending."

Because there was mending to be done. There was always mending to be done. And somewhere between this cell and London, a woman who had spent her whole life paying for silence intended, at last, to spend something better.
