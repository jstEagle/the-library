## 16. London in Fog

The motorcar and I arrived in London together on the eighth evening, both of us rattled, filthy, and running on fumes — the car on what remained of its petrol, and me on a meat pie purchased somewhere outside Croydon that I chose not to examine too closely.

Wren had slept for the last hour against my shoulder, her head a warm weight, her hands still curled around the crust of her own pie even in sleep, as though she feared someone might take it from her mid-dream. She had eaten it twice over, actually — once in reality, and once more in retelling, because she had decided it was the finest meal of her life and deserved narration.

"There was gravy," she had said solemnly, three separate times, "in the *middle*."

Fog had come down over the city like a lid on a pot. It was not the clean grey mist of the countryside, which drifts and lifts and lets you see a hedgerow now and again; this was London fog, yellowish and thick, tasting of coal smoke and river, folding itself around the streetlamps so that each one hung in the air as a soft orange moon with no street attached. The motorcar's lamps made almost no impression at all. I drove at the pace of an elderly cow and was nearly overtaken by just such an animal, pulling a milk cart, whose driver gave me a look of profound pity.

"Is this London?" Wren asked, waking as we turned off the Embankment.

"It is."

"It's got too much sky in it," she decided, after some consideration. "Sky that came down. It oughtn't to be allowed."

I laughed — the first proper laugh, I think, since the night I'd found her on my doorstep, and it surprised us both. She smiled sleepily and put her head back against my arm, and I felt something in my chest do a thing I did not have time to examine.

We were here. Eight days. Three checkpoints behind us — Pike with his knowing eyes, the mail inspector with his clever hands, Foy with his ledger and his ruined conscience. The documents rode where they had ridden since Ashford: not in the engine housing anymore, that trick spent, but sewn into the lining of my coat by a hop-picker's wife with a needle and a grudge against magistrates generally. I could feel them against my ribs when I breathed. A person gets used to carrying contraband alarmingly fast. I had begun to find the weight comforting, which is the sort of thing that ought to worry a woman, if she had leisure to be worried. I did not have leisure. I had until nine o'clock tomorrow morning.

* * *

Finding lodging in fog-bound London at half past nine at night is its own small purgatory. The first two boarding houses I tried took one look at a dusty motorcar, a travel-worn woman, and a child, and developed sudden vacancies in their manners if not their rooms. The third — a narrow place off Whitehall run by a widow named Mrs. Petchy — looked at Wren rather than at me, saw the child's exhaustion, and softened.

"You'll want the room at the back," she said. "It's quiet. And there's a bath if you can bear the water being shared." She took my coin, then squinted at me. "You've driven a long way. You've got the look."

"What look is that?"

"The look of somebody about to do something the papers will disapprove of." She said it mildly, the way one might say *you've got rain coming*, and shuffled off before I could decide whether to be alarmed.

Mrs. Petchy, it turned out, was either very wise or merely very tired, and I did not have the energy to investigate which. We bathed in shifts. Wren fell asleep mid-sentence — she had been explaining that London fog tasted of pennies, and had licked the air once more to confirm it — and I carried her to bed like a sack of warm flour, and sat for a long while afterward in the chair by the window with the documents spread on my knees in the lamplight, reading for the hundredth time the words I knew by heart.

*Tomas Bell, committed for trial upon the evidence of J. Harrow, Esq., magistrate.* A ledger of bribes. A confession of sorts in Harrow's own hand, in the form of instructions to his clerk regarding certain witnesses who were to be *discouraged*. And beneath it all, in a clerk's careful copperplate, the duplicate record Daniel Foy had kept out of shame and pressed into my hands with tears standing in his eyes.

Tomorrow at nine, the tribunal sat. Tomorrow at nine, if I walked through that door with these papers and no name willing to stand behind them, the clerk would send me away.

I repacked the documents. I did not sleep so much as lie adjacent to it for some hours, listening to Wren breathe and to the fog pressing at the glass like a great damp cat wanting in.

* * *

The square outside the tribunal building was called — I never learned what it was properly called. In the fog that morning it was simply a grey room without walls, walled instead by the shapes of buildings that might have been banks or churches or anything, their tops dissolved entirely. Pigeons moved through the murk in ones and twos, materializing and vanishing like poor stage effects. A man sold chestnuts from a brazier at the far corner, and his fire was the only color in the world, a small defiant orange.

Nine o'clock was two hours away. I had left Wren sleeping at Mrs. Petchy's with strict instructions and a shilling, and Mrs. Petchy's promise — delivered with that same maddening mildness — that she'd keep one eye open, having raised four boys herself and survived.

I had come early because I wanted to see the door before I committed to it. This had been my method all my life: survey the room, learn the exits, arrive unnoticed, leave unremarked. Invisibility was not something that happened to me; it was something I had built, brick by brick, over thirty-one years. A chauffeur learns it young. Stand at attention beside the motorcar and you become part of the machine — useful, silent, beneath remark. Read every letter your employer writes and file every secret, and you become the keeper of other people's history, which is a fine dry profession for a woman who has decided her own history ended the day she took the livery.

Marguerite had said once, in one of our rare honest moments on the drive between her committee meetings, *"You watch the world like a woman reading over someone else's shoulder, Elodie."*

She had meant it as observation. It had landed as diagnosis.

I stood in the square and watched the tribunal door. It was a grand door, oak with brass fittings, flanked by columns that pretended to be Greek. Men arrived in ones and twos, hatted and collared, showing cards to a uniformed attendant who admitted them with the weary courtesy of a man who has admitted ten thousand men and expects to admit ten thousand more. Nobody arriving looked nervous. Nobody arriving had sewn anything into a coat.

At half past seven, I went to the door myself.

The clerk sat at a desk inside the vestibule, behind a rail, a small bald man with spectacles and the air of one who has been interrupted his entire life and has stopped expecting apologies. He looked up at me with exactly the expression I had been promised by everyone who had warned me: the expression of a rule being consulted.

"Business?"

"I have evidence for the tribunal sitting at nine. Documents concerning the Bell case."

Something flickered in his face — interest, quickly suppressed, which told me he had heard of the case, which told me perhaps others had tried before me and failed. "Evidence may be submitted in writing," he recited, "by a party of record or a witness of standing, accompanied by—"

"By a named witness. Yes. I know."

He paused. Behind the spectacles, his eyes did the arithmetic — the worn coat, the dust still in the seams of it despite Mrs. Petchy's bath, the childless-looking set of a woman who had slept badly — and then, unexpectedly, he lowered his voice.

"Then you understand your difficulty, madam. Papers without a witness go into the pile. There is a *pile*." He said the word with disgust. "Eleven inches of it, this session alone. Every crank and crackpot in England sends us papers. The pile goes nowhere. If you want these read — truly read, entered, weighed — there must be a name. A name prepared to stand in that room and say *this is mine, I vouch for it, I was there.*" He straightened his spectacles. "A name the counsel can tear at. That is what a name is, in a courtroom. Something to tear at."

"And if I don't give one?"

"Then the pile swallows them, and Mr. Harrow dines well tonight, and every night after."

I thanked him. He looked, as I turned away, as though he wished to say something else — *good luck* or *God help you* or possibly merely *mind the step* — but the rules reasserted themselves, and he bent back over his register.

* * *

I went out into the fog and found the bench nearest the chestnut brazier, because I had discovered over eight days that courage travels better with a hot chestnut in hand. The vendor was a cheerful soul with a voice like gravel in a bucket, and when I bought two he told me unprompted that the fog was the worst since '91 and would be the death of him, though he said it in the tone of a man who intended to outlive several worse fogs yet.

I ate the first chestnut. I held the second and thought.

Here is what I had believed, all my life, with the quiet certainty of a woman arranging her furniture around a load-bearing wall she prefers not to discuss: that history happened elsewhere. To generals and suffragists and magistrates, to people whose names appeared in print with their opinions attached. My job, self-assigned, was to ferry other people through their histories and keep mine in the glovebox, unlabeled. Marguerite marched and was arrested and made the papers; I drove the car. Daniel Foy kept his guilty ledgers; I would have kept none, because I would never have been close enough to anything worth recording.

And then a child had arrived on my doorstep with a note that said *Forget the face*, and I had spent eight days discovering that I could lie beautifully to constables, that I could sew documents into coats, that I could drive through a county full of men who wanted me stopped, and that somewhere along the way I had stopped doing it for Tomas Bell's ghost and started doing it for a small girl who narrated gravy.

The papers in my coat could go into the pile. Eleven inches of it. The clerk had not lied to me; I had seen the pile's cousins in every office I had ever worked. Or the papers could go in under a name — *my* name, spoken aloud, in a room where Harrow's counsel was waiting with instructions to tear. *Elodie Almeida. Chauffeur. Servant. Unlettered woman. Liar.* They would say all of it. They would say it slowly and enjoy saying it, and whatever happened afterward, I would never again be the woman standing beside the motorcar whom nobody remarked upon.

Invisibility had kept me safe for thirty-one years. It had also kept me useless, though I had dressed the uselessness in respectable words like *discretion* and *loyalty*.

There is a moment — I expect every person has one, though most are quieter than novels pretend — when you understand that the wall you built is not protecting you but entombing you, and that the door in it was always unlocked and merely painted shut. Mine came somewhere in the middle of the second chestnut, in a fog-bound square, watching pigeons materialize and vanish.

I thought about Marguerite, in custody somewhere in this city, refusing — I was certain of it — to give them my name. Loyalty, hers, finally pointed in the right direction. I thought about Mrs. Petchy and her mild prophecies. I thought about Wren asleep with gravy on her chin, and about her father, who had tended trees for other people's children to pick fruit from, and been hanged for it.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. Well. I had forgotten enough faces. Mine had gone longest of all.

"Another chestnut," I said to the vendor, "and your opinion, if I may."

"Opinion's free with the third one," he said. "Always has been."

"If a woman has spent her whole life being nobody—" I began, and stopped, hearing how it sounded. I started again. "When does a person stop hiding?"

The vendor turned his chestnuts with his little tongs and considered the question with the seriousness it apparently deserved, or perhaps he was only checking for burning.

"Fog's like this," he said at last. "You can stand in it all day telling yourself there's nothing down the road. But the road's there whether you look or not. Only difference looking makes is, you get to walk it instead of being led." He dropped a hot chestnut into my paper bag. "That's a penny for the chestnut and tuppence for the philosophy, mind."

I paid him. I sat on my bench in the grey room of a square, and I took out my pocketbook, and in my neat chauffeur's hand — the hand trained for ledgers and letters not my own — I wrote:

*Elodie Almeida, of Thornfield House, Kent. Witness for Tomas Bell, late of the Bell orchard. Guardian of his daughter, Wren.*

Three lines. Thirty-one years undone in less than twenty words. My hand did not shake, which surprised me; it felt steadier than it had in years, as though the writing had taken a weight out of it rather than put one in.

Somewhere above the fog, I supposed, the sun was doing its ordinary work. Somewhere in a boarding house off Whitehall, a child was waking and asking Mrs. Petchy for breakfast with the confidence of an empress. Somewhere in a cell, Marguerite Voss was holding her silence like a drawn bow.

And at nine o'clock, in a building pretending to be Greek, a clerk with eleven inches of pile was going to hear my name.

The tribunal bells began — muffled, half-drowned, but unmistakable, counting toward the hour. I stood, brushed the chestnut salt from my skirt, buttoned my coat over the documents against my ribs, and turned toward the oak-and-brass door where a uniformed attendant was now admitting the day's parties of record.

He looked at me as I approached — the dust, the worn hem, the woman alone — and held out his hand for my card.

I gave him my pocketbook page instead, my own handwriting, my own name.

His eyebrows rose. Through the doors behind him, already, I could hear the murmur of the room assembling — and one voice above the rest, dry and unhurried and horribly familiar from eight days of nightmares, giving quiet instructions to counsel.

Magistrate Harrow had arrived early too.
