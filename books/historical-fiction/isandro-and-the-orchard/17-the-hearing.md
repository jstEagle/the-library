## 17. The Hearing

I had rehearsed my testimony forty times on the train from Ashford, twice in the lavatory of the tribunal building, and once — disastrously — to a pigeon on the steps of Westminster who flew off before I reached the part about the coat lining. Pigeons, I have found, are a poor audience. They lack stamina for narrative.

None of the rehearsal helped.

The hearing room was larger than any church I had ever stood in, and colder, as though the law kept its temperature deliberately low so that witnesses would not grow comfortable. The benches were oak and unforgiving. The commissioners sat behind a raised desk like a row of disapproving fathers, and at the centre of them, slightly elevated above even his colleagues — Harrow had arranged the furniture too, of course — sat Magistrate Edmund Harrow himself, in his best judicial grey, wearing the expression of a man watching a wasp at a picnic and deciding whether it warranted movement.

He should have recused himself. Everyone knew it. Nobody had said it aloud yet, because saying things aloud is precisely what the powerful are worst at permitting.

Wren was not permitted in the room. She was sitting with a kind-faced steward's wife two corridors away, forbidden from following me, and I had made her promise three times not to listen at doors. She had promised with the particular solemnity of a child who intends to interpret the promise very broadly. I thought of her face as they called my name — *Elodie Almeida* — and my name sounded strange in the room, like a coin dropped on marble.

"That is the witness," said the clerk. "Step forward."

I stepped forward. My legs carried me, which surprised me. I had half expected them to resign on the spot.

* * *

They began gently, the way butchers begin gently.

Commissioner Hale, a round man with spectacles that magnified his eyes into permanent astonishment, took me through the preliminaries. My name. My age. My occupation.

"I am chauffeur to Mrs. Marguerite Voss of Thistlecombe House."

"A servant, then."

"A driver," I said. "The distinction matters to me."

A ripple somewhere in the public benches — small, quickly suppressed. Hale looked over his spectacles at me, and I understood that this was the first test: whether I would accept the word *servant* and everything folded inside it, all the shrinking and the deference, or whether I would insist on the shape of my own life.

"Very well. A driver." He made a note. "And you are literate?"

The question hung there. It was not a question about reading. It was a question about whether I could be believed — whether letters that passed through my hands were truly read by me or merely carried, the way a mule carries sacks without understanding grain.

"I am," I said. "Thoroughly. Annoyingly, my aunt would have said."

Another ripple, this one warmer. Somewhere behind me a woman coughed in a way that might have been a laugh being strangled by propriety.

"You read correspondence on behalf of your employer?"

"I do."

"And you are therefore accustomed to handling private documents."

"I am accustomed to handling other people's words carefully," I said. "Which is, I understand, more than can be said for some magistrates."

There was a silence of the sort that has weight. Harrow's counsel — a sleek man named Pemberton, whose voice sounded like butter poured over gravel — leaned forward and asked, without being asked, whether the witness intended to offer insults instead of evidence.

"I intend to offer both, sir, if you'll permit it," I said, "but the evidence first."

Hale's mouth twitched. He motioned me on.

So I told it. All of it, in order, the way I had rehearsed for the pigeon: the night of the ninth of September, the knock that wasn't a knock, the child on my step with her shoes on the wrong feet and a note pinned inside her collar that said *Forget the face.* The coat lining. The documents folded eight times. I described each one — the warrant signed in Harrow's hand, dated two days *before* the fire at Bell's orchard; the constable's deposition withdrawn and rewritten; the statement of the ostler James Creevy, paid fourteen shillings to swear he'd seen Tomas Bell with a lantern and a can of oil. I described how the dates contradicted one another like liars at separate windows.

Pemberton objected to nearly every sentence. Hearsay. Inadmissible. Improper foundation. The commissioners sustained some objections and overruled others, and I learned something in that hour which no one had ever taught me: that objections are weather. You keep walking.

* * *

It was when I produced the bundle itself — Foy's duplicate ledger wrapped in oilcloth inside my bodice, because I had trusted no bag since Ashford — that the room changed.

"Commissioners," Pemberton said, rising with his hands spread like a man presenting an empty stage, "before we proceed further down this remarkable road, perhaps we should examine the road itself. This woman" — he did not say *this witness*; the omission was the whole speech — "is an employee. A domestic dependent of Mrs. Marguerite Voss, a known militant, currently held on charges of obstruction. She arrives here bearing documents she claims fell into her lap via an abandoned child — a child she will not surrender to the parish, I might add — and asks this tribunal to overturn a lawful conviction on the strength of papers of unknown provenance that she has carried across three counties hidden in her *clothing*." He turned to the bench with the smile of a man who has already won. "Gentlemen, either this witness is a fool, or she is a liar, or she is a tool. I submit she may be all three."

I felt it then — the terror, finally arriving. It had been strangely absent through all the telling; I had been, without noticing it, in the same state I entered when driving the motorcar at speed: hands steady, mind narrowed, the world reduced to the road ahead. Now Pemberton had pulled me out of the machine. He wanted me standing in the open, small, in my plain coat with my mended gloves, while he explained to a room full of men what a woman like me was worth.

My hands were shaking. I let them shake. I had decided something on the train, one of those decisions that gets made in the body before the mind is consulted: I would not pretend to be calm. Calm was their costume. I would simply speak anyway.

"Sir," I said, "you've offered three possibilities and I'd like to answer all of them, if the commissioners will allow a little arithmetic."

Hale waved a hand — *proceed* — with the air of a man glad someone else was fighting.

"A fool. It may be so. I believed, until eleven days ago, that history happened to other people — to gentlemen in rooms like this one, and to the names in newspapers. I drove a car and read letters and considered myself lucky to be a spectator. A fool believes the spectacle is the whole of the world. That was me, sir, until a seven-year-old girl stood on my step with her shoes on the wrong feet, and I discovered that history had been waiting on my doorstep the whole time, cold and hungry, expecting me to open the door."

I heard my own voice go quiet, which surprised me; I had expected it to rise.

"A liar. Sir, if I wished to invent a conspiracy, I would not invent one that ends with me standing here, in this room, in front of the magistrate it accuses, with no money, no counsel, and my employer in Holloway. Liars choose easier rooms than this."

"And a tool?" Pemberton's smile had thinned. "Whose tool?"

"That," I said, "is the question you ought to be asking Magistrate Harrow, because the papers say his hand signed a warrant before the crime existed. But if you mean the ledger—" I unwrapped the oilcloth and laid it on the table, and the sound of it, that small heavy slap of paper on wood, was the loudest thing I have ever heard. "If you mean the ledger, then I am its courier and nothing more. It was kept in the clerk's own hand, in duplicate, page for page against the official record at Thistlecombe assizes. The official record says Tomas Bell's warrant issued on the fourteenth of March. This one says the seventeenth of February. A month, sir, before the fire he was hanged for. You cannot sign a warrant for a crime that hasn't happened unless you already know it will."

The room did not erupt. Rooms in real life rarely do; what happens instead is worse and better — a stillness, everyone leaning forward by an inch, the whole chamber becoming an ear.

Commissioner Hale put on his spectacles properly, which he had not done for anything else I'd said.

"Where did this ledger come from?"

"From Mr. Daniel Foy," I said. "Clerk to Magistrate Harrow. Kept for nine years. Out of conscience, sir — though he'd likely call it shame, and I'd call them the same animal seen from different windows."

Pemberton was on his feet instantly: provenance unverified, forgery alleged, the witness a partisan, the clerk a traitor or a fabulist, and in any case hearsay upon hearsay upon—

"Mr. Pemberton." It was not Hale who spoke. It was the commissioner at the far end, an old man with a soldier's voice, whose name I never learned and did not need to. "If the ledger is forged, the forgery will be exposed in an hour, by comparison with the official record, which I assume the Crown can produce. If the record cannot be produced, or differs, that too will be instructive. Sit down."

Pemberton sat. Harrow, on his raised chair, had gone the colour of porridge, and his counsel's confidence now served him like a coat several sizes too small.

* * *

They adjourned for forty minutes to compare the ledgers, and I spent those forty minutes in the corridor outside the ladies' retiring room, being quietly sick into a potted fern, and then washing my hands and my face and my courage in cold water, and then eating a biscuit Wren had smuggled into my coat pocket days earlier — she had a policy, apparently, of provisioning me against emergencies, and I have never loved anyone more absurdly than I loved that stale biscuit.

When they recalled me, everything had shifted half an inch, and half an inch was enough.

"The entries correspond," Commissioner Hale said. "Page for page. Save for the dates." He looked at me differently now — not kindly, exactly, but as a man looks at a document he must decide whether to believe. "Mrs. Almeida—"

"Miss," I said, before I could stop myself.

"—Miss Almeida. You understand that your testimony places you at odds with a magistrate of the Crown. You understand what may follow. Your employment, your character, your liberty."

"Yes, sir."

"Do you wish counsel? The tribunal can appoint—"

"No, sir." And then, because it seemed suddenly important that the record hold more than my fear: "I have spent six years reading other people's letters and saying nothing. I am very good at saying nothing, sir; it is the one trade I have thoroughly mastered. Today I would like to be amateur at something."

The old soldier at the end of the bench laughed outright, and did not apologize for it.

They entered the ledger. They entered the warrant. They entered the note from Wren's collar — *Forget the face* — and when the clerk read it aloud in his flat official voice, something in my chest turned over, because in this room, written in a clerk's fair hand and spoken into the record of the Crown, the words had changed direction entirely. They had been a threat once, aimed at a child. Now they were an exhibit. Now they accused whoever wrote them. *Forget the face*, the record said, and the record would remember.

Pemberton rose once more, for cross-examination, and it was brief and vicious and I survived it. He asked whether I had accepted money for the documents (no). Whether I stood to gain by Mrs. Voss's cause (my gain, sir, is that I sleep indoors at night, and intend to go on doing so, which requires that I be able to look through windows). Whether it was not true that I had lied to Constable Pike at the Wealdbridge checkpoint (yes), deceived the mail inspector at Ashford (technically, sir — a laundry list was involved), and fled an inn at dawn to avoid a magistrate's officers (yes, yes, and yes again).

"So the tribunal is asked to accept the word of a self-confessed deceiver."

"No, sir. The tribunal is asked to accept the word of a ledger that matches the Crown's own records to the letter, and differs only where the difference is a hanging. I am the least of what's on that table. I only carried it."

I looked at Harrow as I said it. I had avoided his eyes all day, the way one avoids a dog one suspects of biting; but now I looked, and he looked back, and I saw that he was not a monster. That was the terrible discovery of the whole affair, and I want to set it down honestly: he was a tired, vain, frightened man who had ruined a family years ago for reasons that had seemed convenient at the time and had spent every year since hoping the ground stayed packed down over it. He saw me looking, and I watched him understand — in the way of men watching water rise past their boot-tops — that the ground was not going to hold.

* * *

They did not rule that day. Commissioners never do; ruling, like remorse, is deferred until the room has emptied. They announced that the ledger would stand entered, that one further witness would be called tomorrow morning, and that the tribunal would sit at ten o'clock.

One further witness. I asked who, and Hale looked at Harrow, and Harrow looked at nothing, and the clerk consulting his list read out, in that same flat voice:

"Mr. Daniel Foy. Clerk of assize, called for the Crown."

For the Crown. Not for me. For the Crown.

I came out of the hearing room into the corridor's gaslight with my legs gone strange beneath me and my ears ringing with the ordinary sounds of the building — footsteps, doors, a trolley of tea things — everything ordinary, unbearably ordinary, while somewhere in my chest a door I had kept shut for six years stood swinging in the wind.

The steward's wife was waiting with Wren, who had listened at zero doors, she informed me solemnly, holding up one finger, but had pressed her ear to one wall, which was different, and had heard everything through it anyway, because walls, she said, are just thin doors that gave up.

"Well?" she said. "Did you tell them?"

"I told them."

"About Papa?"

"All of it. Every page."

She considered this with the terrible thoroughness of seven years old, and then she took my hand, which she had never done first before, and we walked toward the fog-bound square where our lodgings waited, and I thought: tomorrow Daniel Foy stands up in that room with nine years of shame in his hands, and Edmund Harrow will watch his own clerk turn against him under the same roof where a man was condemned by a warrant signed too early.

I did not know yet what Foy would say when they swore him in.

Neither, I think, did he.
