## 18. Harrow Breaks

There is a particular sound a fountain pen makes when the hand holding it has stopped pretending to work. I know this because I had been listening to mine for the better part of an hour, poised over the clerk's bench in the tribunal chamber with a fresh page of notes before me and nothing — not one word — going onto it.

Daniel Foy has kept minutes for Magistrate Edmund Harrow for eleven years. I have recorded his rulings, transcribed his summations, filed his correspondence, and sharpened his pencils to a point he considers neither too timid nor too theatrical. In eleven years I have written down a great many things for that man.

I had never before written down something *against* him.

The duplicate ledger sat in my inside pocket, over my heart like a guilty conscience, which is precisely what it was. Forty pages of entries in two hands — Harrow's dictation on the right, my own shameful record on the left — documenting every payment, every witness "reimbursed," every report quietly amended in the matter of Tomas Bell, orchard-keeper, hanged four years ago for a fire he did not set.

I had kept it out of shame, as I have said to myself a hundred times since. Shame, and the superstitious feeling that a man who writes things down twice must eventually answer for both versions.

That morning, it seemed, had arrived.

* * *

The tribunal chamber at Westminster was smaller than I had imagined, and colder, with the particular damp of a building that had been important for centuries and comfortable for none of them. The commissioners sat behind a long oak bench like twelve owls on a branch. Counsel for the Crown occupied one table. Counsel for Magistrate Harrow — a silvered, silky gentleman named Prewitt who smelled of expensive mints — occupied the other.

And between them, in the witness box, looking for all the world as though she had been summoned to read the gas meter, sat Elodie Almeida.

I had seen her perhaps six times before that week, always at a distance: the Voss woman's chauffeur, gloved and correct, holding open a motorcar door. I confess I had thought of her the way one thinks of furniture that drives well. Then she had come to my lodgings three nights ago, pale as candle wax, with Wren Bell asleep against her shoulder, and she had put the duplicate ledger on my table between the teapot and the cold toast, and she had said — and I will remember this until I die —

"Mr. Foy, you've been carrying this alone for four years. I thought you might like to put it down."

She hadn't accused me. That was the unbearable part. A woman who could not vote, could not own the motorcar she drove, could not even sign her testimony without a solicitor's permission — a woman whose entire profession was *not being noticed* — had looked at me, a man of law and ledgers, and offered me absolution as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world.

So there she sat now, delivering her evidence in a voice that only shook on the word "Wren," while Prewitt circled her like a cat deciding whether the thing on the floor was prey or beneath his dignity.

"You admit," Prewitt was saying, "that you are unlettered in matters of law?"

"I can read, sir. I read everything. It's rather my job."

"Your *employment*," said Prewitt, with the delicacy of a man laying a dead mouse on a doorstep, "is that of a domestic chauffeur."

"Chauffeur, mechanic, reader of other people's post, and lately courier of documents concerning a miscarriage of justice," said Miss Almeida. "The last position came open unexpectedly."

Somebody in the public gallery — I suspected it came from the direction of a small girl swinging her boots against a bench — giggled. The chairman rapped for order. Prewitt's mints clicked audibly against his teeth.

But I was barely listening, because the doors at the back of the chamber had opened, and Magistrate Edmund Harrow had come in.

* * *

He looked older than he had a fortnight ago. That is a thing people say without meaning much by it, so let me be precise: the Harrow who entered that chamber moved like a man walking on ice that had already cracked once under him. He had heard about the ledger. He had heard about Miss Almeida's testimony. And yet he came anyway, took his seat at counsel's table, and arranged his papers with those small, fastidious movements I knew better than my own.

Eleven years of loyalty sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Eleven years of "Foy, take this down" and "Foy, we don't record the informal remarks" and "Foy, burn that draft." I had obeyed nine times out of ten. The tenth time, I had copied.

"Harrow will call no witnesses," Prewitt announced, "beyond the magistrate himself, whose character requires no defence. The allegations before this tribunal rest upon a document of dubious provenance produced by a serving girl and a disgraced clerk—"

"Then perhaps," said the chairman dryly, "the magistrate will consent to be questioned on it, since its provenance appears to be *his own office*."

They swore Harrow in at half past eleven. I watched him settle into the witness box the way a man settles into a bath he suspects has gone cold — carefully, bracing.

Crown counsel began gently. Dates. The Bell arson trial. Harrow answered smoothly; eleven years of hearing him sum up had taught me he could be smooth in his sleep. Yes, he recalled the case. Tragic business. Orchard country takes fire badly. The man confessed, did he not?

"He confessed," said Harrow.

My pen stopped.

Because that was the lie. Not a shading, not an omission — a flat, sworn lie, and I knew it, because I had typed the statement myself, and I had typed the second version of it too, the one where the confession appeared only after two days without food in a cell that flooded when it rained. I had told myself for four years that a confession was a confession, that the law was a machine and I merely oiled it. Sitting there with forty pages of oil-stains against my heart, I discovered that I had never believed it for a single day.

The chairman turned. "Mr. Foy. You were clerk to the magistrate's office at the relevant time. The tribunal would see the ledger."

* * *

Walking from the clerk's bench to the front of the chamber took, I am quite sure, four steps and roughly one full year off my life. I carried the ledger in both hands, like something that might bolt.

I was sworn. I gave my name — Daniel Foy, clerk of the magistrate's court at Ashford, eleven years' service — and I heard my own voice come out steadier than I had any right to expect.

"Do you recognise this document?"

"I compiled it, sir. Every entry is in my hand or dictated by Magistrate Harrow and countersigned by me. It duplicates the official record of the Bell proceedings — and differs from it, in nineteen material particulars."

"Differ how?"

"The official record shows one payment to a witness, Joseph Crane, for 'travelling expenses.' The true figure, entered here, is eleven pounds — rather more than train fare — paid the week after Crane testified he saw Bell near the fire. The official record shows Bell's confession taken on the fourth of October. The true date of the statement is the sixth, after—" my voice did waver here, and I let it, because the wavering was honest— "after two days during which, per the governor's private note also copied here, the prisoner was held in irons and denied his meals."

"And who instructed these discrepancies?"

Silence. Every eye went to the counsel table. Harrow did not look at me. He looked at his folded hands, and I understood, suddenly, with a clarity that felt almost tender, that he had known this moment might come for four years — had known it every time I sharpened a pencil, every time I coughed in the wrong register — and had simply chosen, as men of his kind do, to believe that the future was something that happened to other people.

"Answer the question, Mr. Foy."

"Magistrate Harrow instructed them," I said. "All of them. Usually after dinner. He liked to dictate amendments with a glass of port, said it improved his prose."

A sound went through the chamber — not laughter, not gasps; something more like a great building settling.

Prewitt rose for cross-examination, and I will grant him this: he tried. He suggested forgery, grievance, drink, an old dispute over wages. I answered each one, and found — this was the astonishing discovery of my fifty-third year — that telling the truth requires no preparation whatsoever. You just keep saying what happened. It is lying that demands rehearsal.

At last, exasperated, Prewitt made his error. "You expect this tribunal to believe that you served a corrupt magistrate for *eleven years*, kept a secret record of his crimes, and never once reported them? What sort of man does that?"

The honest answer was: a coward, sir, a comfortable one, and I have met him every morning in the glass. But what I said was:

"A minor man, Mr. Prewitt. Your question is really the whole of this case, isn't it? How do decent little men keep an indecent thing alive? Answer: one amendment at a time, each too small to resign over." I turned to the commissioners. "I can't give you back the four years I didn't speak. I can give you this book, and I'll answer for every page of it."

* * *

They recalled Harrow late in the afternoon, and it went badly for him from the first minute, the way a wall goes badly once one stone is properly loose.

"Why," asked the chairman, "did your clerk keep a duplicate record?"

Harrow wet his lips. I watched him reach for the smooth voice and find it gone. "Clerks keep copies. It is — it is standard practice—"

"In your office it was forbidden," I said, from the bench, before I could stop myself. "You forbade it yourself, sir. Minute 114 of the office manual. In your handwriting."

"Foy will *be silent*—" Harrow began, and then caught himself, in front of twelve commissioners, shouting at his own clerk like a man shouting at the tide.

It unravelled quickly after that, the way these things do — slowly for four years and then all at once in forty minutes. Crane, produced by the Crown, recanted his identification in a voice like wet paper. The prison governor's notes went in. Someone produced the charred lantern with Bell's initials that had been "found" in his cottage, and someone else observed that the initials faced *outward*, as if placed rather than dropped, and Prewitt asked for a recess that was not granted.

The end came not with a shout but with a shrug, which is how evil usually ends when it ends at all. Asked finally whether he had caused the prosecution of Tomas Bell knowing him innocent, Harrow opened his mouth, closed it, and then said, in a small grey voice I had never heard him use:

"I acted for the county. There were interests. Insurance. The orchard land was wanted for the railway spur." He paused. "Bell would not sell."

Twelve pens moved. Twelve owls wrote down, in their several fair hands, the plain sentence: *he would not sell.* A man hanged, in the end, for saying no.

The chairman removed Harrow's commission then and there, and officers of the court walked him out past the public gallery. As he passed the third row, a small girl stood up on her bench to watch him go. She did not say anything. She did not need to. Wren Bell had her father's level grey eyes, and she spent them on Edmund Harrow like a debt being collected.

He flinched. I saw it. So did everyone.

* * *

Afterwards, the chamber emptied slowly, the way churches do. I gathered my pens. My hands, which had been steady all afternoon in the manner of hands belonging to someone else, chose this moment to shake so hard I dropped one, and it rolled under the bench, and I knelt on the cold stone of His Majesty's tribunal at my age, groping for a pen, and laughed until my eyes watered.

"That's not funny," said Elodie Almeida, appearing beside me, though she was smiling.

"It's the funniest thing I've ever done," I said. "Eleven years of taking down other men's words. Today I said two sentences of my own and brought down a magistrate." I found the pen. "Miss Almeida — I owe you an accounting. You knew what I was. You came to my door anyway."

"You weren't what I came for," she said. "You were what the truth had left lying about. One doesn't blame the furniture."

"Ah," I said. "So I'm furniture."

"Well-driven furniture," she conceded, and then her smile faded into something quieter and truer. "Mr. Foy. He'll appeal. Men like him keep lawyers the way other men keep dogs. And Marguerite Voss is still in Holloway on a charge that won't survive the week now, but the week still has to pass. There's a reporter outside who wants Miss Almeida's story, and I haven't decided whether I'm a person today or a curiosity, and I find—" she looked at the empty witness box, at the oak bench, at the whole grey magnificent machinery of it— "I find I need to sit down somewhere quiet and work out who I am tomorrow."

"With respect, miss," I said, "you're the woman who drove the truth ninety miles through three checkpoints in a tourer with a weak magneto. Tomorrow tends to take care of itself after that."

She laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and for a moment the exhaustion lifted and I saw the young woman underneath, the one history had ambushed on her own doorstep and who had, against all her stated convictions, answered the door.

Then a clerk of the tribunal came hurrying down the aisle with a telegram in his hand, calling my name, and the look on his face made us both go still.

"For Mr. Foy. Marked urgent." He hesitated. "It's from Ashford, sir. It concerns the Bell child."

Elodie went white. I tore the envelope with fingers that had forgotten how to shake, and read it twice, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath the day's victory.

"What is it?" she said. "What's happened?"

I looked at her, and at the small figure waiting in the doorway with the light behind her, and I wished with everything in me that the news in my hand were different.
