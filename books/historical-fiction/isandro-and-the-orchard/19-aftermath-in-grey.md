## 19. Aftermath in Grey

The morning after the verdict, London did not fall down.

Elodie had half expected it to. She had spent three weeks believing that the truth was a load-bearing wall, that once it was spoken aloud in a courtroom everything built on top of the lie would shudder and come away in sheets. Instead she woke at half past seven in her narrow bed at the Bloomsbury boarding house, listened to the milk cart rattling below, and understood that the world intended to go on exactly as before — except that somewhere in it, a dead man's name had been made clean.

She lay there a while, testing herself for joy the way one tests an injured ankle for weight. It wasn't there. Something was there, but it was smaller and further off than she'd expected, like music from a neighbour's window. *Tomas Bell is exonerated.* The words sat in her head and refused to become a feeling.

Downstairs, the landlady had pinned the evening editions across the parlour mantelpiece like hunting trophies, because Elodie was famous now, and landladies know how to make fame pay for itself in toast.

*THE ORCHARD INJUSTICE: MAGISTRATE RESIGNS UNDER CLOUD.*

*SERVANT GIRL'S AMAZING COURAGE — FULL STORY INSIDE.*

And one paper, more honest than the rest about what its readers actually wanted: *WHO IS THE CHAUFFEUSE? MOTOR-MAD MAID TELLS ALL.*

"I've cut them all out for you, dear," said Mrs. Pargeter, appearing with a teapot and the particular smile of a woman who has decided to be proud of someone whether or not she understands why. "You'll want to keep them for your grandchildren."

"I haven't got any grandchildren," said Elodie.

"You will have. It's always the quiet ones who surprise you." Mrs. Pargeter set down the pot, straightened the papers so their headlines faced outward like a row of soldiers, and added, with the delicacy of a woman negotiating a minefield: "There's a gentleman waiting outside. Fourth one this morning. He says he's from *The Daily Clarion*."

"Tell him I'm dead."

"He'll want a photograph of that too, knowing his sort."

* * *

The trouble was that they didn't want her. They wanted a version of her that could be folded into four columns.

The first journalist to actually corner her — on the pavement outside Marguerite Voss's house, where Elodie had gone to collect the motorcar — was a cheerful young man with a hat worn at the angle of a man who has never been struck by weather. He had a notebook already open to her name.

"Miss Almeida! Just a few words. How does it feel to have brought down a magistrate?"

"I didn't bring him down," Elodie said. "I drove some papers to London. The motorcar did most of the work; it's a very good motorcar."

He laughed as though this were wit rather than fact, and wrote something. She watched his pencil move and felt a small cold dread.

"And the child? Wren, is it? The public is simply desperate to know about the little orphan heroine."

"She's not an orphan." The words came out sharper than intended. "Her father's name is Tomas Bell, and he was innocent, which is the entire point of everything that happened, if you're planning to write any of it down."

"Of course, of course." His pencil kept moving. "Where is she now?"

"That," said Elodie, "is none of you."

He wrote that down too. She realised, watching him, that it didn't matter what she said; the notebook was a mill, and anything fed into it came out flour of the same grade. She could say the sky was green and by Thursday there would be an engraving of it.

She left him on the pavement and went inside to sit with Marguerite, who had been out of Holloway for two days and was still moving through her own house like a guest, touching things gently, as if to ask permission.

* * *

Marguerite looked thinner. Prison did that — not dramatically, not in the way of the engravings in the suffrage newspapers, where women emerged gaunt and luminous like saints — but in small ways. Her wrists were narrower. Her laugh arrived a half-second late, as though it had to be sent for.

"They offered me my own column," Marguerite said, without looking up from her tea. "While I was inside. Can you imagine. A page a week. *Letters from a Political Prisoner.* They'd have photographed me through the bars if I'd let them."

"What did you say?"

"I said no. They seemed genuinely puzzled." She looked up then, and her eyes had the tired sharpness Elodie had learned to trust more than any speech. "They're going to keep offering, Elodie. You especially. Do you know why?"

"Because I'm new?"

"Because you're *usable*. A chauffeur who saves the day — it's a lovely story. It asks nothing of anyone. A woman reads the papers over her mistress's shoulder for years and learns nothing of politics, then stumbles into one injustice and fixes it in nine days, hooray, and everyone can go home comforted. It lets them believe the system works, because look, it corrected itself!" Marguerite set down her cup. "It doesn't ask them why a man had to hang for eleven years' worth of nobody looking. It doesn't mention the forty other cases Foy's ledger touches. It makes you a curiosity, dear, and curiosities are consumed."

Elodie thought of the young man's pencil, grinding her into flour.

"There were forty other names?" she asked quietly.

Marguerite's mouth curved, the old wry curve, but sadder. "You've read Foy's duplicate ledger. You tell me."

She had read it. She had read it twice, once in the fog-bound square before deciding to testify and once after, unable to sleep, and both times she had stopped at the same place: a column of names, mostly poor, mostly men without orchards or advocates, each with a figure beside it that could only mean payment received. She had told herself the tribunal would handle it. Tribunals handled things. That was what tribunals were for.

"Tomas Bell's pardon is settled," Marguerite said. "Harrow has resigned — 'for reasons of health,' naturally; his health is excellent, he's simply moved it to the Continent. And the rest—" She spread her hands. "The rest is paperwork. Committees. Motions that will be tabled. The machine that killed Tomas Bell is not broken, Elodie. It has merely been embarrassed, and machines are very good at surviving embarrassment."

"Then what do we do?"

"We?" Marguerite raised an eyebrow. "You've done your part. More than. There's a girl who needs you, if the newspapers haven't carried her off yet. Go home. Let the machine be embarrassed in peace."

But it was not peace Elodie felt. It was the specific hollowness of finishing a long journey and finding the destination smaller than the road.

* * *

Wren was upstairs in the nursery that had briefly been hers, sitting on the windowsill with her knees drawn up, watching the street. She had taken against journalists early, with a child's unerring instinct, and had developed a habit of hiding behind curtains whenever a man raised a camera, which meant she spent a great deal of time behind curtains.

"There's one pretending to be a lamplighter," she reported, as Elodie came in. "Lamplighters don't work in the daytime. He's very bad at it."

"Then we shall leave by the back stairs like criminals," Elodie said. "Or like celebrities. They're nearly the same thing now, I find."

That earned her half a smile. Wren had smiled more since the verdict — carefully, experimentally, like someone trying on gloves — but grief does not pack its bags just because a court says something kind. Some mornings she woke calling for her father in a voice Elodie pretended not to hear, because hearing it out loud would have been a theft.

"Wren," Elodie said, sitting on the sill opposite. "When all this noise dies down — and it will, they're already bored of me, they only want to know what I eat for breakfast — what would you like to do?"

Wren considered the street below with great seriousness.

"Go home," she said at last. "Not the room we stayed in. Home-home. The orchard."

"The orchard's burned, love."

"It'll grow things anyway," Wren said, with the flat certainty of a seven-year-old stating physics. "Daddy said burnt ground is good ground. He said it crossly, when the west field burned, but he said it."

Elodie looked at her — this small person who had been handed to her on a doorstep with a note saying *forget the face*, whom she had dragged across three counties and past three checkpoints, who had saved the evidence with quick fingers and a forged laundry list while grown men searched the car — and felt the thin victory in her chest shift slightly, like furniture being moved toward a window.

"Yes," she heard herself say. "All right. We'll go. When the roads clear and the press finds someone else to eat, we'll go to the orchard."

* * *

The official notice arrived that afternoon, and it was this, more than anything, that taught Elodie the true texture of aftermath.

It came in a stiff government envelope, and it was beautiful, in the way that institutions are beautiful when they finally do the correct thing: heavy paper, a wax seal, calligraphy. *Whereas Tomas Bell, orchard-keeper, of Little Fenn, was convicted of arson in the year of our Lord 1902, and whereas fresh evidence hath satisfied His Majesty's Tribunal that the said conviction was unjust…* On and on, in language designed to sound like history rather than like a mistake being tidied. At the bottom, a signature. Not Harrow's. Harrow's signature would have been too much to hope for.

Daniel Foy had brought it himself. He stood on Marguerite's step holding his hat, a grey, careful man with the air of someone who has recently been taken apart and reassembled with one screw left over. His career was finished — resigned, "for reasons of conscience," which the papers spelled *nervous breakdown* — and he looked strangely lighter for it, the way luggage looks lighter once you accept you're carrying it the whole way.

"Miss Almeida." He held out the envelope. "This is for the family. For the girl, when she's ready. I thought it ought to come from a hand, not the post."

"Thank you, Mr. Foy."

"I keep thinking I should say something more," he said. He turned his hat once, completely around. "Eleven years I kept that ledger. Every entry, I told myself I'd need it someday, and every day I hoped I wouldn't. That's not courage, miss. That's cowardice with good handwriting." He looked at the door behind her, as if hoping Wren might appear, and seemed relieved and disappointed in equal measure that she didn't. "Tell her— no. No, there's nothing I can say that fits in a sentence. Tell her the pardon is real, and that real things sometimes arrive too late, and that the lateness is ours, all of ours, mine most particularly."

He put his hat on and left. Elodie stood in the doorway holding eleven years of institutional apology in a heavy envelope, and found she couldn't hate him, which somehow made it worse.

* * *

That evening she took the motorcar out alone, ostensibly to have the engine inspected after its ordeal, actually to drive, because driving was the only prayer she knew.

London in the early dark, lamps coming on one by one, the motorcar grumbling contentedly beneath her hands. She took the long way round the park without deciding to. And somewhere between the park gates and the river, the feeling she'd been waiting for finally ambushed her — not at the moment of the verdict, nor the pardon, nor Marguerite's release, but here, alone, at thirty miles an hour with her goggles speckled with drizzle.

It arrived as memory: Wren asleep in the passenger seat outside Ashford, one hand still closed around the forged laundry list, and Elodie realizing with a lurch that she would burn down the entire machinery of England before letting that child be taken. Not principle. Not politics. Just that.

*Tomas Bell had been somebody's whole world too,* she thought. *And the machine had processed him on a Tuesday.*

She pulled over by the railings and let the engine idle and cried for the first time since the doorstep — not elegantly, not for the newspapers, just a woman in a motorcar with rain on her goggles, grieving a man she'd never met and a version of herself that had believed history happened elsewhere.

Then she wiped her face, checked her mirrors, and pulled out, because idling wastes petrol and she'd been raised frugal.

* * *

Mrs. Pargeter was waiting up, which meant trouble or kindness, and it proved to be both.

"Two things," she said, in the hall. "First, there's a letter come for you, hand-delivered, no card." She produced it with the reverence of a woman who believed all correspondence was potentially scandalous. "Second, the newspaper man left again, but he left this." She held up a folded proof sheet. "Says it runs Saturday whether you talk to him or not. Says he's got your story 'anyway.'"

Elodie opened the proof standing in the hall, under the gaslight, with Wren's letter still unread in her other hand.

It was worse than she expected and better written than she feared. *CHAUFFEUSE OF COURAGE: THE FULL TRUTH AT LAST.* There was her photograph — taken without her knowledge, outside the tribunal, caught mid-step and looking, she thought sourly, exactly like a servant going somewhere quickly. And beneath it, the story: accurate in its dates, invented in its heart. It had her falling in love with the cause at first sight of the documents. It had her making speeches on the road. It had Marguerite as a tragic mentor and Daniel Foy as a shadowy hero and Wren as a "little angel of justice." It had everything except the truth, which was that it had been fear and stubbornness and a breakdown outside Ashford and a child's steady hands, and that she had never once felt brave until afterward, when it was too late to matter.

At the bottom: *"Who is Elodie Almeida? Page Four asks the questions London wants answered. EXCLUSIVE INTERVIEW — SATURDAY."*

"They'll print it regardless," Mrs. Pargeter said gently. "Might as well give them your side."

"My side isn't printable," Elodie said. "My side is that I was terrified for three weeks and angry for good, and that being famous is like being a photograph of yourself: flat, and hung where other people can point."

She climbed the stairs slowly, unfolded Wren's letter at last, and found it contained exactly six words in careful pencil:

*Can we go before they follow us?*

Elodie stood a long moment in the corridor, the proof sheet in one hand and the note in the other, and felt the hollowness of the last two days fill, slightly, with purpose — the way a room fills when someone opens a window.

The press wanted a curiosity. London wanted a story with the bones removed. But there was a burned orchard in Kent that belonged by rights to a girl who believed burnt ground was good ground, and a sapling that wanted planting, and Marguerite convalescing downstairs with a motorcar at her disposal and nothing left to lose but her privacy, which the papers had already taken.

Elodie went into her room, took out pen and paper, and began, for the first time in her adult life, to plan a journey she could not read over anyone's shoulder beforehand.

Saturday could print whatever it liked. By then, she intended to be gone.
