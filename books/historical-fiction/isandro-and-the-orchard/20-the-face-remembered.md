## 20. The Face Remembered

Wren had a system for keeping things secret, and it had never once failed her.

The first rule was: don't look at the hiding place. Looking was how you got caught. Papa had taught her that without teaching her on purpose — she'd watched him tuck his wages under the loose brick by the hearth and then spend the whole evening not glancing at it, easy as breathing, while Mr. Pike's constable boots stamped around the cottage asking about fires. Papa had just smiled at the man like Sunday was coming.

The second rule was: if you have to think about the secret, think about something else at the same time. Wren thought about horses when things got bad, and about the smell of apples when they got worse.

She had kept her father's photograph for two years using both rules, wrapped in oilcloth inside the hollow leg of the kitchen table at Mrs. Almeida's house, and in all that time she had looked at it exactly eleven times, which she counted, because counting made it smaller somehow. Eleven looks in two years. Once every ten weeks, near enough. It was like rationing sugar. You could make anything last forever if you only ever took a little of it.

But now the trial was over, and Papa's name was clean — *clean*, like laundry, like the word itself was soap — and Mrs. Marguerite was out of prison and staying in the guest room where she'd already rearranged all the furniture to suit herself, and nobody was hiding from anybody anymore. And Wren had begun to feel a terrible thing.

She had begun to forget.

Not the photograph. The photograph was safe as houses. But the other things — the things that weren't paper. She could not remember anymore whether Papa's hands smelled of apples or of pipe smoke or of both together. She tried and tried at night and it was like reaching into a pocket she'd already emptied. She knew he had a laugh because she remembered knowing it, but she couldn't find the sound. She knew he called her *little wren* but she couldn't find the voice that said it, and the voice mattered more than the words, and the voice was gone, gone, gone, slipped out under the door like water.

And there was one other thing she couldn't stop thinking about, which broke rule two entirely.

*Forget the face.*

That was what the note said, the note pinned to her coat that cold night on Mrs. Almeida's step, written in a hand shaking so hard the letters leaned against each other like tired sheep. Forget the face. She'd understood it then, in her seven-year-old way: the men who burned the orchard would come looking for anyone who remembered. A girl who forgot was a girl who lived.

But the men were done. Harrow was finished — she'd heard the grown-ups say it forty different ways, and Mrs. Marguerite said it best of all, with her chin up and her teacup steady: *finished, dear, quite finished.* Nobody needed her to forget anymore.

And forgetting, it turned out, was like riding downhill on a cart with no brake. Easy to start. Very hard to stop.

So on a grey Tuesday in February, with rain writing its long letters down the windows, Wren sat at the kitchen table with her legs swinging and made a decision the size of a mountain, and then did what you do with mountains, which is climb them one stone at a time.

The first stone: she waited until Elodie sat down across from her with the mending basket.

Elodie mended on Tuesdays. This was as reliable as weather in reverse — always happening, always slightly surprising. She sat with her back straight and her thimble on and she sewed buttons with the concentration of a general planning a battle, and she talked while she did it, little bits of nothing: the motorcar wanted a new fan belt, Mrs. Marguerite had eaten the last of the seed cake and denied it with her whole face, the postman had a limp today and wouldn't say why. Wren loved these bits of nothing. They were the sound of a house where nobody was going to be arrested.

"Elodie," Wren said.

"Mm." Elodie bit her thread.

"I want to show you something."

Elodie looked up. She had this way of looking at people like she was reading them faster than they could read themselves, which used to frighten Wren terribly and now just felt like being held by the eyes. "All right," Elodie said, and set down the mending, and gave Wren her whole attention, which most grown-ups never managed even half of.

Wren went to the table and reached underneath, feeling for the hollow leg, working her fingers into the gap the way she'd done a hundred times, and drew out the little oilcloth bundle. It was lighter than she remembered. Or heavier. She couldn't decide, so she decided it didn't matter, and carried it back, and sat down again, and put it on the table between them, and did not push it across, because pushing it across would mean it was really happening.

Elodie looked at the bundle. Then at Wren. She didn't touch it. "Is that yours?" she asked, very carefully.

"It's Papa's." Her throat had gone tight. "It was in my coat. The coat I was wearing when—" When we left me at your door, she almost said, but that wasn't right either; the truth was more complicated and sadder and had her aunt's frightened handwriting all over it. "When you found me."

Elodie still hadn't touched it. Her hands were folded on the table like a good pupil. "You don't have to show me, Wren. Not ever. Not unless you want to."

"I know."

"All right."

"But I'm going to." And then, because honesty was a muscle you had to use or it went weak — Mrs. Marguerite said that about speaking up, and Wren had decided it applied everywhere — she added, "I've been forgetting him. Little bits. That's why."

Something moved through Elodie's face then, quick as a fish under ice, and Wren saw — really saw, the way children see everything and understand most of it — that Elodie had known. Had known and said nothing and let Wren keep her secret safe in the table leg, all these weeks, out of respect, or kindness, or whatever the grown-up word was for leaving a person their privacy even when you ached to reach in.

"You can open it," Wren said. "I want you to see his face before I lose mine."

* * *

Elodie unwrapped the oilcloth slowly, like the photograph might be shy.

It was a cabinet card, the kind photographers did in market towns for people who saved up: Papa standing beside his ladder, one hand resting on it like the ladder was a horse he trusted. His sleeves were rolled. His hair was dark and pushed back carelessly, the way hair gets when a man does it with one hand on his way out the door. And his face —

His face was Papa. Round-cheeked and squinting slightly against the sun, with the beginning of a smile in it, the kind of smile that hasn't decided yet but is leaning toward yes.

"There he is," Elodie said softly.

"That's the orchard behind him." Wren pointed, though the pointing finger felt enormous and clumsy next to the small grey trees. "Those are his apples. Russets. He said russets were the only honest apple because they're ugly and good instead of pretty and useless." She paused. "He liked saying things like that. Things that sounded wise but weren't really."

Elodie laughed — a real one, surprised out of her. "Mrs. Marguerite does exactly that."

"I know. I think that's why they'd have been friends."

The photograph lay on the table between them, and Wren found that looking at it now, here, out loud, was different from all eleven rationed looks combined. Those looks had been stealing. This one was sharing. Papa looked back at her off the card, and for the first time in two years it didn't feel like peeking through a window at something lost. It felt like introducing him.

"He doesn't know about any of it," she said. "The trial. The pardon. You planting things." She stopped, and started again, because there was more and it wanted out. "He doesn't know you kept me. He just — left me at your door, sort of, or Aunt Cissy did, and then you kept me, and he doesn't know."

"No," Elodie said quietly. "But I know. And Mrs. Marguerite knows. And Daniel Foy knows, and the hop-pickers know, and half of London read about it in the papers." She turned the photograph so it faced Wren properly. "Being known isn't the same as him knowing, I'll grant. But it's not nothing, Wren. He's not forgotten. He's the opposite of forgotten. There are men in a courtroom who will be old men still telling the story of how a chauffeur and a seven-year-old undid Magistrate Harrow."

"A seven-year-old with quick hands," Wren corrected, because the laundry list trick was hers and she was proud of it.

"A seven-year-old with very quick hands," Elodie agreed solemnly, "whom I intend to keep indefinitely, pending your consent."

Wren considered this. "My consent's given," she said, and Elodie had to blow her nose on the tea towel, which Wren pretended not to notice, because that was kindness too.

* * *

They looked at the photograph together for a long while. Rain ticked at the window. Somewhere upstairs Mrs. Marguerite was moving furniture again, thump by determined thump, redecorating the world.

"Can I tell you what I remember?" Wren said at last. "Before it goes?"

Elodie put down her cup. "Tell me everything. I'll keep it. If you lose a piece, I'll hold it for you."

So Wren told. She told about Papa's hands — pipe smoke *and* apples, she'd found it, it came back the moment she stopped chasing it, both together, always. She told about the loft over the pressing shed where he kept the crates, and how the smell up there was so thick with apples you could nearly chew it. She told how he whistled through his teeth when he worked, two notes over and over, not a tune exactly, more like a habit with wings. She told about the fire, a little, not much — the shouting, and Papa's hands putting her in the cupboard and saying *stay, little wren, stay* — and she told it steadily, because Elodie was listening in the way that made steadiness possible, and because telling it out loud to someone who had carried the proof of it across three counties felt like handing her the last crate down off the wagon.

"And I remember," Wren finished, "that he said the orchard would grow back even if they burned it, because apple roots go deeper than fire thinks. I thought that was one of his wise-but-not-really things. But maybe it was actually wise."

"Maybe the wise-but-not-really ones are secretly the wisest," Elodie said. "It would explain a great deal about Mrs. Marguerite."

Wren laughed. Then she went quiet, and looked at the photograph, and asked the thing she had climbed the mountain to ask.

"Take me back."

Elodie went still.

"To the orchard. Where it was." Wren pressed her palm flat on the table, next to Papa's face. "Everybody keeps not taking me, I can tell. You talk about it in the other room. Even Mrs. Marguerite goes quiet, and she's never quiet. But it's mine. It's the only thing of his that's still *there* — the ground is still there, roots don't burn, he said so — and I want to see it awake, in daytime, walking in, not running away in the dark. I want to stand in it and remember him on purpose instead of losing him by accident." She ran out of breath and took another one. "Please."

For a moment Elodie said nothing, and Wren braced herself for the grown-up answer, the *when you're older* answer, the gentle door closing.

Instead Elodie picked up the photograph, studied Papa's face for a long moment, and set it down facing Wren again — returned, not confiscated.

"Yes," she said. "We'll go. Both of us. And I'll drive us myself, and we'll take the long way through Ashford so you can thank the hop-pickers properly, and Mrs. Marguerite will insist on coming and complain about the roads the entire way, and it'll take twice as long as it should, and it will be perfect." She paused, and her mouth did the thing it did when she was about to say something true and couldn't find a joke to hide it in. "You're right that I've been keeping it from you. I thought I was protecting you. But I've learned this year that protecting people from their own history never works. History sits at your kitchen table eventually, whatever you do. Better to drive toward it than wait for it to arrive."

"History arrived at our door once," Wren said. "In a coat."

"In a coat," Elodie agreed, "with quick hands."

They shook on it, formally, across the table, which made them both laugh again, and then Elodie stood to fetch the map, and that should have been the end of the morning, tidy as a buttoned cuff.

But Wren stayed sitting with Papa's face in front of her, and felt the strange new lightness of a secret given away, and thought about the ground that was still there, and the sapling Elodie would certainly insist on bringing, because Elodie planned everything like a campaign and had already mentioned nurseries twice this week in that too-casual voice she thought fooled people.

And it was then, tracing the edge of the photograph with one finger, that Wren noticed something she had never seen in eleven careful, rationed, stolen looks — something visible only now that she was looking openly, in daylight, at a table with an ally across it.

Papa's other hand — the one not on the ladder, hanging at his side — was holding something small and pale. A folded square of paper, tucked half into his fist, the corner just showing.

And in the bottom margin of the card, in faint pencil she had never once noticed, someone had written four small words:

*Ask the keeper's daughter.*
