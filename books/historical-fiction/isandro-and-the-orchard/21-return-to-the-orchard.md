## 21. Return to the Orchard

*Elodie*

The motorcar did not want to go to Kent. It said so at every opportunity — a shudder in the steering, a cough from the engine that sounded suspiciously like a cough of protest — and by the time we crossed into orchard country it had settled into a grumble so constant I had begun to answer it.

"Yes, I know," I told it, rounding the bend past Ashford. "Neither do I."

"You're talking to the car again," Wren said from the back seat. She said it without judgment, in the tone of a person cataloguing facts. She had been cataloguing facts about me for six months now, and I had long since given up defending myself.

"It listens better than most people."

"Uncle Daniel doesn't listen at all," she observed. "He waits until you stop and then says his thing."

Daniel Foy, wedged beside her with his hat on his knees and his good suit already creased beyond rescue, cleared his throat. "I am present, you know."

"I know," said Wren. "That's how I know."

Marguerite laughed from beside me — properly laughed, head back, and I felt the sound land somewhere in my chest and stay there. Six months ago she had come out of Holloway thin as a lath and grey to the lips, and I had privately feared they had broken something in her that would not mend. They had not. They had, if anything, simplified her: the strategems and careful silences burned away like undergrowth after a fire, leaving the stubborn timber underneath. She was thinner and plainer and more herself than any woman I had ever known.

"Turn left at the mill," she said, and then, quieter, "I used to know these lanes so well I could have driven them blindfolded."

"Then you can direct us blindfolded," I said. "It would be no worse than your directions generally."

"My directions are excellent. Your listening is what's in question." But her hand had gone to the window frame, gripping it, and I saw her knuckles whiten as the hedgerows gave way to the first straggle of apple trees — wild ones, unpruned, throwing blossom at the sky with the reckless generosity of things nobody owns anymore.

We had not spoken much about this day. We had planned it — trains checked, Foy's document carried in oiled cloth against rain, the constable at Maidstone politely warned that a party might pass through and would prefer not to be gawped at — but we had not *spoken* of it, and the silence on the subject had grown its own weight, riding in the car with us like a fourth passenger.

The road climbed. And then the hedge fell away on the right, and there it was.

The Bell orchard.

Even burned, even two years dead, it was unmistakably an orchard still — rows legible beneath the char, trees standing black against the green hillsides like type on an unprinted page. Half of them had thrown up shoots from the rootstock, foolish bright green wands rising from trunks the fire had killed, as though the trees had not been informed of their own deaths and declined to accept the news.

Nobody spoke. The engine ticked. Somewhere down the valley a cuckoo was making its annual claim on somebody else's nest.

"Well," said Marguerite at last, unclipping her goggles with hands that were almost steady. "It's smaller than I remembered."

"Everything is," said Foy. "Except the past."

I parked beneath the one tree that had survived whole — a great gnarled perry pear at the field's edge, too twisted and too old for the fire to find much purchase on, its bark seared on one flank only, like a man who has turned his face from a blast. Wren was out of the car before I'd set the brake, running down the row with her hair streaming, and none of us called after her. There was nothing left here to hurt her. That was the terrible arithmetic of the place: everything that could burn already had.

She stopped at the foot of the third tree in the third row, and stood very still, and I knew without asking which tree it was. The one her father had been cutting when the men came. She had described it to me once, at bedtime, in the flat reciting voice children use for the worst things: *the one with the split, where he could put his hand in and feel the sap going round.*

I got out and went to stand with her, because some silences should not be kept alone.

"That's it?" I said.

"That's it." She reached up and laid her palm flat on the black trunk, exactly as high as a seven-year-old could reach. She was eight now, and taller; she had to stretch. "The split's gone. The fire took it." A pause. "But the roots will remember."

I have heard theologians say less defensible things.

* * *

*Marguerite*

I had loved him in this orchard. That was the fact I had carried out of Kent twenty years ago and never once set down, and it sat in my chest now, heavy and familiar, while the others arranged themselves around the ruined rows like people arriving at a church.

Twenty years ago the trees were heavy with fruit and Tomas Bell had been heavy with plans — cider he meant to press, varieties he meant to graft, a world he thought was essentially decent and improvable. He had talked to me with his hands full of blossom, and I had thought: *here is a man who tends things.* I had been twenty-two and certain that history was made in halls and newspapers, and I had gone back to London to make it, and I had supposed he would always be there, in his orchard, tending.

By the time I understood that decency requires defenders, he was dead, and the man who killed him wore a magistrate's chain, and I had paid — quietly, through intermediaries, for fifteen years — to keep the village from saying so aloud. Silence, I had told myself, was strategy. Silence bought time. Silence kept other people safe.

Silence had kept a little girl walking the roads alone with her father's evidence sewn into her coat.

Elodie found me at the gate. She does that — appears at one's elbow precisely when one's composure is negotiating terms with itself, and offers neither sympathy nor comment, only company. She handed me a flask of tea without a word.

"He proposed to me under that pear tree," I said. It came out before I had decided to say it, which is the only way true things ever leave me.

"And you said no."

"I said *not yet*. Which is a coward's no." I drank the tea. It was over-strong and slightly stewed, the way Elodie insists tea must be to survive a journey, and it was exactly right. "I had a cause. I had meetings. I had a whole architecture of importance." I looked out at the black rows and the green shoots fighting up through them. "He had trees. I have spent a great deal of money and twenty years learning which of us chose better."

"You chose wrong and then you chose again," Elodie said. "Most people never get to the second part."

That is the other thing about her. She forgives in the same voice she uses to report the weather, and somehow it counts more.

Down the rows, Foy had opened his case on the tailgate of the motorcar and was reading over his papers with the anxious thoroughness of a man who has rehearsed a thing forty times and expects to be examined on it. His collar was wilted. His hands shook slightly. He had resigned his post by letter three weeks ago — the letter printed in half the papers in England, *the clerk who kept two ledgers*, the minor character promoted overnight into a kind of secular saint — and he still flinched when strangers recognized him on trains.

Wren had appointed herself his keeper somewhere along the road, possibly because he was the only person in our party more frightened than she was.

"Mr. Foy," she called. "Are you going to be sick?"

"No, Wren."

"Mama was sick before her hearing."

"Wren," I said.

"Well, he looks like it."

"I am perfectly well," Foy said, with dignity, and then sat down abruptly on the tailgate and breathed into his hat for a moment. Wren patted his knee twice, briskly, as one pats a nervous horse, and went back to her investigation of the burnt fence posts. Between the two of them, I reflected, they made nearly one whole courageous adult.

A cart came up the lane. Then another. By half-past eleven there were a dozen people standing among the black trees — hop-pickers in their Sunday clothes, the Ashford family and their cousins, a woman I did not know carrying a covered basket, two or three farm labourers with their caps literally in their hands. Word had travelled, as word does. Nobody had organized them. Nobody needed to. They had simply woken this morning and remembered what day it was, and come.

One of the old pickers touched his cap to me, uncertainly. I realized he was trying to place where he knew my face from, and could not, and I let him wonder. There are worse fates than being unplaceable.

* * *

*Wren*

The pear tree was Papa's favourite because perry pears are stubborn. He said apples give up in a bad year but pears sulk and then bear anyway, out of spite, and spite is a virtue in a fruit tree. He said it the same way he said everything, like it was a joke and also not a joke.

I showed Elodie the row where the saplings were going to go. We had brought six of them in the boot of the car, wrapped in wet sacking — russets, from a nursery in Kent that Mr. Foy had found, grown from stock that was probably descended from Papa's own trees although nobody could prove it, which Mama said was the best kind of descent anyway.

"This one goes there," I said, pointing. "And that one there. So the rows come back the same."

"They won't be the same trees," Elodie said. She crouches down to talk to me properly. Most grown-ups don't. Most grown-ups talk at the top of you, like you're boots.

"No," I agreed. "They'll be new ones. That's the point of them."

She looked at me for a second in that way she has, like I've said a thing bigger than I meant to. Grown-ups do that look a lot around me lately. I don't see why. It seemed obvious.

Mr. Foy called us together then, and everyone came in from between the rows, quiet as sparrows, and he stood under the pear tree holding the paper with both hands and I could see it shaking so I stood next to him where I could hold his coat hem without anyone seeing. That helps. It helped me at the hearing, holding Elodie's glove.

He read.

I won't write out all of it because it was long and had a great many *whereas*es in it, and *whereas* is a word that means *everyone is pretending this is complicated when it isn't*. What it said, underneath all the whereas, was this:

*Tomas Bell was innocent. Tomas Bell was honest. The court that hanged him was lied to, and the lie was made on purpose, and the Crown is sorry, and his name is clean.*

His name is clean.

Mr. Foy's voice cracked once, at the part where it said the pardon was granted *in restitution of honour*, and he stopped, and blew his nose, and carried on, and nobody minded. Old Mrs. Ashford cried openly. One of the labourers said *hear, hear* in a low voice, like a shout that had been folded up small.

When it was done, nobody clapped. That surprised me. Instead everybody just — stayed. Stood there among the dead trees, in the sunshine, breathing. As if the words needed somewhere to settle, like pollen.

Then Mrs. Ashford unwrapped her basket, and it was seed cake, and that broke whatever spell it was, and everyone started talking at once.

* * *

*Elodie*

There was lunch, because there is always lunch, and English grief and English joy both demand cold mutton. We ate it spread on a tarpaulin between the rows, and the pickers told stories.

They told stories about Tomas all afternoon — that he'd paid his pickers a penny above the rate and pretended it was the orchard's idea; that he'd once carried a lame boy two miles on his back rather than let him miss a fair; that he could tell an apple's ripeness through the wall of the store by smell alone, which everyone agreed was impossible and everyone believed entirely. Marguerite sat in the middle of it with her tea going cold in her hand, drinking in every word with her eyes shut half-closed, hoarding them. History lives in ordinary mouths, she had said to me once, on the road, and I had not fully understood her until I watched her understand it.

"And what will become of the land?" one of the young ones asked, eventually. "It's still in the estate, isn't it? Harrow's people?"

"Not any longer," Marguerite said, and there was something in her voice I hadn't heard before — not triumph; relief, maybe, wearing triumph's coat. "The trustees settled it last week. The orchard passes to the Bell heirs." She glanced at Wren, who was showing Foy a beetle. "Or to whomsoever the heir shall appoint. Which is to say—" she smiled, "—to whoever Wren decides."

Everyone looked at Wren. Wren, who has the finest instincts of any negotiator I have ever met, finished examining the beetle first, looked up at the circle of waiting faces, and said, with tremendous gravity:

"The trees stay. And people can pick them. And nobody gets charged for windfalls."

A murmur of approval ran round the tarpaulin like warmth through a house. Old Mrs. Ashford declared it the wisest settlement she'd heard since the Enclosures and demanded it be written down. Foy produced a pencil. By four o'clock there was a document, witnessed three times, governing the perpetual commons of the Bell orchard, drafted by a former crown clerk under the direction of an eight-year-old girl eating seed cake. I have sat in rooms with cabinet ministers. I promise you no law of the realm has ever been better made.

Later, when the carts had gone and the light had gone long and gold across the valley, Marguerite walked the rows alone. I watched her from the gate. At the third tree of the third row she stopped, and took off one glove, and laid her bare hand flat against the black trunk — higher up than Wren could reach — and stood there a long moment with her head bowed, saying whatever it is one says to the dead when the argument between you is finally over.

Then she came back, and squared her shoulders, and said, "Right. If we're planting tomorrow, the holes should be dug tonight. Ground settles better."

"You've planted trees before, then," I said.

"I grew up in Kent, Elodie. Before I became a monster of the metropolis, I could graft a russet in my sleep."

"Then dig," I said, and handed her the spade, and there is a particular pleasure — I commend it to anyone — in handing your employer a spade.

* * *

*Wren*

We dug six holes in the evening, in the places I picked, and the soil came up dark and good-smelling underneath the burnt top, the way Papa said it always would. Fire only takes the top. Roots go deeper. That's the whole secret of orchards, Papa said, and maybe of everything.

Mama dug. She was slow because of her arms, from the prison, but she didn't stop, and Elodie dug next to her matching her pace without being asked, which is how Elodie loves people, I think — by matching them. Uncle Daniel measured the holes with a tape measure because he is Uncle Daniel. He let me stamp the bottoms down. That is the important job. Everybody thinks planting is about the tree but really it's about stamping the earth so the roots can hold on.

Before we put the first sapling in, Mama stood at the head of the row with her hand on the pear tree, and she said, "Tomas. Look what we've brought you." Just that. Not a speech. She talks to him like he might answer, and I think he might.

We left the saplings in their sacking overnight, lined up at the field edge like passengers waiting for a train. In the morning we'd set them in the ground, all six, and water them from the ditch, and the orchard would begin coming back.

Last thing, when it was nearly dark and the others were packing the tools, I walked down to the third tree of the third row by myself. I took Papa's photograph out of my pocket — the one I finally showed Elodie in London, him laughing, holding an apple up like a toast — and I held it up so he could see.

"We did it," I told him. "You're forgiven. Well — you didn't need forgiving. But it's *said* now. Out loud. Among the trees."

The blossom on the wild hedge moved in the wind, all at once, the way a crowd turns its head.

I put the photograph away carefully, both hands, and went back toward the lamp, where Elodie was calling me for supper, and behind me the six little trees stood wrapped in the dark, waiting for morning.
