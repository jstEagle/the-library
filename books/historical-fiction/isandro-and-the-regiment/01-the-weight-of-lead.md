## 1. The Weight of Lead

The graver sang against the steel, a thin silver note that only Felix Eastwick could hear.

She worked by the tallow lamp at her father's bench—the third hour of the cut, past midnight, past the hour when any respectable household had banked its fire and surrendered to sleep. Outside, Vellamont muttered. It always muttered now, in this last winter of the century: the river cracking its ice in long groans, carts groaning over cobbles glazed with frost, and beneath it all, voices. Men's voices, low and urgent, passing along Quay Lane like sparks down a fuse. She did not listen closely. Listening closely was how people disappeared.

The punch before her was an *h*, lowercase, old-style, commissioned by Alderman Prue for a commemorative hymnal he would never pay for promptly. Her father would have called it bread work—bread work being any job that kept flour in the bin while the true work waited. Felix thought of it differently. Bread work was honest. A letter cut well was a small cathedral; you could kneel inside the counters of a fine *o* and feel the architecture hold. She had learned that from him, and she had learned it young, kneeling on this same stool with her chin barely clearing the bench's edge, watching his hands move like two intelligent animals that needed no instruction from his eyes.

Three months since he died. Ninety-one days, if she counted—and she counted everything, because counting was what a craftsman did, and grief, she had discovered, could be carved into hours the same way steel gave way under the graver: slowly, deliberately, each stroke removing only what must go.

His stool still held the shape of him. That was the cruelty of a workshop: it preserved a man better than a grave did. The leather seat had molded itself to his weight over thirty years, and when she sat in it she sat slightly inside him, in the hollow his body had worn, breathing where he had breathed. His apron hung on the fourth peg, stiff with old ink, and she could not bring herself to wash it or move it or even brush against it, though sometimes in the dark her elbow found it and the smell of turpentine and lampblack rose up so suddenly and completely that she had to set down her tools and sit with her hands flat on the bench until it passed.

Tonight it passed quickly. Tonight there was work.

She turned the punch under the lamp and examined the shoulder of the *h* with a loupe ground by her father's own hand. The serif wanted refinement—the left foot was a hair too heavy, giving the letter a slight forward lean, as though it were hurrying somewhere. She could see it. Only she could see it, which was precisely the problem, and precisely the point.

*Felix Eastwick cuts a better letter than Eastwick ever did.*

That was what old Marnett the ink-grinder had said once, drunk, at the funeral supper, before catching himself and looking at the floor. He had meant it kindly. It had been the only true sentence spoken in that room.

But no one would put such a sentence in print, because in Vellamont, in the year 1792, the trade rolls of the Stationers' Guild listed one Eastwick, type-founder, deceased, and beneath his name a blank space where an heir should be. Daughters did not inherit benches. Daughters did not sign punches. When Felix delivered finished sorts to the shops along Printer's Row, the masters accepted them without comment, examined them with narrowed professional eyes, and said, *Your father's hand improves,* and she said nothing, because correcting them would mean explaining, and explaining would mean the fiction collapsed, and the fiction was the roof over her head.

So her letters went out into the world nameless, wearing her father's name like borrowed coats, and somewhere in ten thousand books and broadsheets across three counties her letterforms lived and multiplied while she herself remained—as Corvel's apprentice Betto had once said, not unkindly, in the way of boys who do not know they are cruel—invisible as a woman's opinion.

She fitted the loupe back into its drawer and took up the graver again.

* * *

The shop below the bench-room was hers in every way but law. She descended to it now to check the furnace banked for the morning's casting, and the act of descending was itself a kind of devotion: eleven stairs, each one knowing her weight. At the bottom, the workshop breathed its familiar darkness—smell of molten metal remembered in the walls, of oil and size and the particular cold-sweetness of paper stored through winter. Two presses stood shrouded under canvas like furniture in a dead man's house, which was what they were, in a sense. Her father had bought the second press the year she turned fourteen, saying, *When you have your own shop, you'll need capacity.* He had said it plainly, as fact, in the way he said all true things, and it had not occurred to either of them then that the Guild would have opinions about who could own a shop.

Her father had opinions about the Guild too. She remembered his voice on winter nights exactly like this one, both of them at the bench, his hands moving, hers watching: *A letter is the most dangerous thing a man can make, Felix. A knife kills one person. A letter convinces ten thousand.* He'd laughed then, soft, not looking up from his work. *That's why they keep us poor. They're afraid of the alphabet.*

She ran her fingers along the type cases lining the wall—jobbing case, roman case, the italic case he'd made himself from pear wood, the joints so fine no lead dust escaped. Each case held its alphabet in compartments like a city seen from above, every letter in its quarter, waiting. There was comfort in that order. The world outside the shop grew more disordered by the week—the grain riots in autumn, the pamphlets everyone denied reading, the magistrate Halden's name spoken in the tone people reserved for weather that kills—but here, in the case, every character knew its place, and if you had skill enough, you could take chaos apart and set it right, line by line, into sense.

If only the world composed as easily as a galley.

She was checking the italic case—for no reason except that touching her father's joinery soothed something in her—when she heard the knock.

Not a knock, precisely. A scrape. Knuckles dragged once down the street door, the sound of a man who wanted in but had perhaps forgotten why.

Felix stood very still. The lamp was upstairs; the workshop held only the banked furnace's faint orange seam. Her hand found the burnishing stone on the bench—a heavy thing, good for one honest blow—and she moved toward the door on feet she had trained, these three months alone, to make no sound on her own floors.

Through the seam of the shutters she saw him: a man, doubled over against the doorframe, one arm wrapped around his middle. Snow had whitened his shoulders and cap. As she watched, his legs folded under him, slowly, the way men fold when they have run out of some essential currency—not strength, exactly, but permission to remain upright.

She should not open the door. Every instinct bred into a woman alone in Vellamont said so, said it loudly, said it in her father's cautioning voice: *We are invisible, Felix. Invisibility is armor. Do not put your hand out the window; do not let them learn your face.* And there were other instincts too, newer ones, born of these muttering months: the knowledge that men who collapsed against print-shop doors at midnight were not delivering almanacs.

He knocked again. Weaker. Then she heard words, slurred by cold and something worse, pressed flat against the wood:

"Eastwick. The type. For God's sake—the type."

Her father's name worked on doors even now, three months buried. Felix stood with her hand on the bolt for the length of four heartbeats—long enough to know she would hate whatever she chose—and then she drew it back, and the night came into the shop carrying snow and blood.

* * *

He was younger than his voice had promised, and he was dying; she understood that within moments, with the terrible practical clarity that crisis grants women who have managed workshops and ledgers and their own griefs alone. He had been stabbed, twice, low on the left side, and bound up the wounds with rags gone rust-black, and he had walked, she would learn later, nearly a mile on legs that should have refused him.

She got him inside onto the settling bed by the furnace, and the heat brought him back enough to speak, and what he spoke first was not a name, not a prayer, but instructions.

"The case." His hand seized her wrist with shocking force, fingers black with cold and blood. "Third case from the window. The pear-wood one."

"That's my father's case," she said stupidly. "That's the italic case."

"He knew," the man said, and something in his face suggested he found this funny, or would have, in a world where men weren't knifed in alleys for paper. "Beneath the sorts. Sewn under the shell. Don't look now—" his grip tightened, and his eyes went past her to the shuttered windows, listening to the street, where boots were now moving, several pairs, unhurried, the walk of men searching rather than patrolling "—look when I'm gone. Or don't. Burn it, maybe. Burning's kindest." He coughed then, and the cough was bad, wet and deep, and Felix knelt and did what her hands knew to do with wounds because her hands always knew, and while she worked he talked, low and broken, a river going under ice: names she didn't recognize, a place beyond the river, a publisher, *tell them the ledger is whole, tell them Eastwick's daughter has it*—

*Eastwick's daughter.*

He had said daughter. He had known. Her father had known this man, had known this cargo, had sewn it into his own pear-wood case with his own fine joints, and had told no one, not even her, and Felix felt the betrayal and the inheritance of it arrive together, twin children of the same dark moment.

Outside, the boots stopped. Someone tried the handle, gently, twice.

The courier looked at her. In the furnace light his eyes were already far away, but they focused on her face one last time with an urgency that burned off the distance, and he said, clearly, almost conversationally, as though remarking on the weather:

"They'll ask whose shop. Give them a name that isn't yours."

Then the light in him went somewhere else, and his hand slid from her wrist, and the fists on her door became thunder.

* * *

What Felix did in the next ninety seconds, she did without deciding to, the way her fingers found letterforms in the dark without looking. The bolts. The chain. The lamp doused and relit at half, turned low, domesticated. The body arranged on the settling bed with a blanket drawn to his chin, as though sleeping off drink—she could not hide a death, only delay its discovery, and delay might be everything. And then the case. The pear-wood italic case, lifted from the wall, carried to the composing stone in plain view, opened, its compartments of delicate italics spread to the lamplight like any craftsman's tools awaiting morning work.

The thunder stopped. A voice, official and bored, announced the watch, announced Magistrate Halden's authority, announced that the door would be broken if it stayed shut, which it did not, because Felix Eastwick opened it herself, lamp in hand, wearing her widow's cap and her most exhausted expression, and informed the three men on her step that her lodger had come home drunk again and she wished the magistracy joy of him, because she certainly could not manage it.

It was a performance assembled entirely from her father's stories—his imitations of the widows on Quay Lane, his delight in the theater of ordinary complaint—and it worked, mostly, because invisibility was armor, and armor was a costume, and she had spent her whole life learning to wear one. The soldiers peered in. One stepped across the threshold, close enough that she smelled wet wool and the vinegar of fear that wasn't hers. He looked at the presses, the cases, the sleeping—or dead—shape under the blanket, and asked whose shop this was.

"Eastwick's," Felix said. "I keep it for the family."

"And you are?"

"A relative." She met his eyes with the flat, apologetic emptiness of a woman who has been interrupted at some tedious chore, and after a moment he lost interest, the way men lost interest all her life, gratefully, blindly, and withdrew.

At the door he paused. "There's trouble on the river tonight," he said. "Keep it barred. Keep it quiet."

"I always do," said Felix, which was true, and closed the door on the law of Vellamont, and stood in the dark with her heart slamming like a press in full operation.

* * *

Later—the deep later, the hour when even revolution sleeps—she knelt at the composing stone with the pear-wood case open before her, and she looked, as the dead man had told her not to.

The false bottom was a masterpiece. Her father's masterpiece, she corrected herself, feeling the seam with a fingertip: invisible unless you knew, a shell of thin pear board resting above the true floor of the case, and beneath it, sewn in oilcloth, flat as a wafer, a book. She drew it out. Small, dense, bound in grey board, pages crowded with a clerk's precise hand: columns of names, dates, sums, and at intervals a signature she recognized from the reward posters on the customhouse wall—the sharp, self-satisfied hand of Magistrate Halden himself.

She read one entry. Then another. Then she stopped reading, because the shop seemed to tilt around her, and the names on the page were not names, they were weights, and each one settled into her chest like a piece of type into a stick: *the mill village, eleven souls, orders given, orders signed.* Eleven. Then another line. Nine. The numbers marched down the page the way her letters marched down a galley, orderly, composed, set into sense by someone with skill enough—and the sense of it was massacre, recorded in a magistrate's own hand, proof beyond burning, proof worth walking a mile on opened veins to deliver, proof worth killing for, evidently, and therefore proof that would kill her too unless she was cleverer than she had ever needed to be.

Felix closed the ledger. Held it. It weighed less than a handful of type. It weighed more than the presses.

Somewhere across the frozen river, a bell rang the three-quarter hour, and the sound crossed the ice and entered the shop and reminded her that time existed, that the century was dying, that dawn would come to Quay Lane whether she survived to see it or not. She thought of her father's voice: *They're afraid of the alphabet.* She thought of the courier's last instruction—*give them a name that isn't yours*—and understood, finally, in the marrow-deep way, what her invisibility had been training her for.

She had spent her life cutting letters that no one would let her sign.

Here, in her hands, was a book that could not be unsigned.

Felix wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth, returned it to the coffin-like dark beneath the italics, and slid the shell home until the seam vanished. Above the bridge, snow was falling again, erasing the street, the blood, the bootprints, the whole evidence of the night—everything, she thought, except what was written down.

Tomorrow the bridge would matter. Tomorrow she would learn why the soldiers watched it so closely. But tonight there was only the lamp, the bench, the case, and the weight—and somewhere out in the muttering dark, men who already knew that Eastwick's shop had opened its door.
