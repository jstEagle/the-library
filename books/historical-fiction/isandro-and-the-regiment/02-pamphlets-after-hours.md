## 2. Pamphlets After Hours

The shop after midnight was a different country.

By day it belonged to Corvel and his apprentices, to the smell of ink and the clatter of the press and the customers who came in for handbills and funeral notices and never once looked at the woman behind the type-cutting bench. But past eleven, when Corvel sent Betto home and shuttered the front room, the shop became something else—a held breath, a church with its candles snuffed. Felix Eastwick knew that country better than she knew her own bed. She had been living in it since she was nine years old, when her father first let her stay up to watch him cut a punch, his loupe glinting, his breath held like a man threading a needle in a windstorm.

Now the loupe was hers. The bench was hers. The gravers, laid out in their roll of chamois leather—hers, though no one in Vellamont would say so aloud. *Eastwick's daughter*, they called her, as if her father had left behind a daughter the way a man leaves behind an umbrella: useful, unremarkable, belonging to someone else.

She worked by lamplight with the shade drawn low, cutting the lowercase *g* for a wedding invitation—someone named Aubry, someone rich enough to want swashes on their joy. The steel took the curve beautifully. It always did, if she was patient. Her father had taught her patience before he taught her letters: *Lead remembers everything you do to it, Lix. Every hesitation, every hurry. So don't hesitate, and don't hurry.* She could hear his voice still, worn smooth as the handle of his oldest burnisher, and some nights it was a comfort and some nights it was a knife, and she never knew which until she heard it.

Outside, Vellamont muttered.

That was the only word for it. The city had always made noise—the river slapping the pilings, the fishwives crying the morning catch, the bells of Saint Aldhelm's—but this winter the noise had changed its character. It had gone underground, into doorways and taverns and the spaces between market stalls. Men spoke in lowered voices about the bread price. Women stood too long at windows when soldiers passed. And twice this week Felix had seen papers nailed to the customs house wall by dawn, papers no one would claim, torn down by noon but read by hundreds before they fell.

*Halden*, the muttering went. *Magistrate Halden.*

She set the finished *g* on the proofing stone and flexed her fingers, stiff from cold even beside the stove. The fire had burned to a red murmur. Through the wall that separated the workroom from Corvel's private office, she could hear him moving—not the ordinary sounds of a master printer locking up his shop, but something slower. Heavier. The sound of a man doing something he did not want interrupted.

Felix told herself she wasn't curious. She was simply still working, and the wedding invitation wanted its capital *A*, and the steel for capitals lived in the cabinet in the corridor outside Corvel's door.

That was all true. It was also true that she moved quietly, because quiet was a habit ten years deep, and that she did not knock, because knocking would announce her, and announced people were visible people, and visibility was the one luxury her life had never afforded her.

The door to Corvel's office stood open an inch. Lamplight lay along the floorboards like spilled honey.

Through the gap she saw the second press.

Not the great Stanhope in the front room, the one every customer in Vellamont knew. A smaller press, older, half-hidden behind a screen of blank canvases—and Corvel bent over it, pulling proofs by lamplight, his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, his face lit from below in a way that made him look younger and older at once, the way candlelight does. He was printing broadsides. She could see them hanging from a line strung across the ceiling, drying in ranks like small dark laundry.

And she could read them. Even upside down, even at a distance, a printer's daughter reads everything—it is not a skill but a condition, like being unable to unhear a scream.

*THE TRUTH OF THE MILLBRIDGE FIELD. On the fourteenth of October, by order of Magistrate Edouard Halden, soldiers fired upon unarmed weavers gathered at Millbridge Field. Eleven dead. Three were women. One was a child of seven. The magistrate's own ledger records the order. Ask why the newspapers are silent. Ask who pays for silence.*

Felix's hand found the doorframe without her telling it to.

The names were there. Not just Halden's—she'd have expected that; the whole city whispered Halden's name the way sailors whisper about a reef. But the dead had names too. *Anselm Prout, weaver, aged thirty-four. Marta Prout, aged twenty-nine. Tomas Prout, aged seven.* A family. An entire family with one surname, lined up in type like sorts in a case, each one a small hard piece of metal that had once been a person who breathed.

She must have made a sound. She didn't think she did—she was trained to silence, silence was her native tongue—but Corvel's head came up sharply, and for a long moment neither of them moved. The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Somewhere out in the street a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Come in, then," Corvel said at last. His voice was tired, not angry. Somehow that was worse. "You may as well. You've read half of it already, I expect."

Felix stepped through the doorway. There seemed nothing else to do with her body. "I came for the capital steels," she said, and heard how thin the lie sounded, like paper held up to light.

"Mm." Corvel turned back to the press. He pulled another proof, laid it on the stone, examined it with the squinting professional attention he gave to every sheet that ever passed through his hands—even these, even now. That, more than anything, undid her composure: that he brought the same craft to sedition that he brought to wedding invitations. That treason, in his hands, was still typesetting. "How much did you read?"

"All of it." She crossed the room slowly. Up close the hanging sheets filled her vision, black columns on cheap grey stock, the kind of paper they used for fish-wrapping. He hadn't spent good stock on this. He hadn't dared. "Corvel—these are the Millbridge dead. These are real names."

"They're real men," Corvel said. "Real women. Real children. That's rather the point." He hung the fresh proof with the others and stood looking at the line of them, his back to her. In the grey paper and the bad light he looked suddenly old, and she remembered that he had worked beside her father for nineteen years, that he had carried the coffin at the funeral, that he had wept openly and then never spoken of it again. "Your father printed psalms, Felix. Forty years of psalms and almanacs and bills of lading, and never once put his neck out. And when he died, do you know what I thought? I thought: there goes the last honest coward in Vellamont."

"That's a cruel thing to say about him."

"It's a cruel thing to be one." He turned around. His eyes behind the spectacles were red-rimmed. "I'm not brave, girl. Understand that before anything else. I'm frightened every hour of every day, and I print anyway, because the alternative is to know what I know and say nothing, and I find at my age that saying nothing has begun to taste like ash."

Felix looked at the broadsides. At the name *Tomas Prout, aged seven*. Her father's voice said something in her memory—*lead remembers everything*—and she thought: so does paper. Paper remembers. That was the whole secret of the trade, the thing that had made her love it when she was small enough to hide under the composing stone. Everything else in the world faded or lied or died, but a well-pulled sheet kept its word forever. Ink did not flatter. Ink did not forget.

"How many have you printed?" she asked.

"Two hundred. I'll strike three hundred more tomorrow night, and Betto will carry them to the fish-market men, and by Sunday they'll be nailed up in every parish from here to the border, and by Monday Halden's men will be asking questions in every tavern." He rubbed his eyes under the spectacles. "Which brings us to you."

"I saw nothing."

"You saw everything. You've the best eyes in this shop, everyone knows it, they simply pretend otherwise because eyes attached to a woman make men uncomfortable." He said it without heat, the way he might report the weather, and the flatness of it stung more than anger would have. "So here is what happens now. You swear to me—on your father's bench, if you like, since I know what that bench is worth to you—that you will speak of this to no one. Not to Betto. Not to the priest. Not to yourself in your sleep. If Halden's men come asking, and they will come asking, you are a woman who cuts type for weddings and knows nothing of her employer's private business. Can you do that?"

"Yes," Felix said.

It came easily. Too easily, perhaps. But it was true in the way that mattered: she could keep a secret. She had kept the secret of her own competence for ten years, had signed her finest punches with her father's mark because a woman's mark on a matrix would have raised questions no one in the trade wanted to answer. She knew how to be invisible. Corvel was asking her to be invisible in a new direction, that was all.

"On your father's bench," Corvel pressed. "Say it properly."

She almost laughed. Almost. "On my father's bench, I'll say nothing."

"Good." He exhaled, and some of the tension went out of his shoulders, and he looked ten years older and five years lighter all at once. Then, briskly, in the voice he used for closing accounts: "It's late. Take your steels and go to bed. And Felix—" She paused at the door. "The invitation for the Aubry wedding. The swash on the capital. Your father couldn't have done better."

It was the closest thing to praise he had given her in two years, and it landed strangely, warm and worthless, like a coin found in a burning house.

* * *

She did not go straight to bed.

Her room above the shop was cold; the stove had gone out hours ago, and she was too restless to rebuild the fire. She sat instead at the little table beneath the window, where she did her private work—the commissions she took secretly, the matrices she cut for printers in other towns who paid her by post and believed, because it was easier to believe, that F. Eastwick was a man. Her tools were there. Her father's loupe was there. And folded inside her apron, which hung on the back of the chair, was a sheet of grey paper.

She had taken it without deciding to take it. That was the part that troubled her, sitting alone in the cold with the sheet before her and the moon coming through the frost on the glass. When Corvel had turned to hang his last proof, when his back was turned and the lamp threw his shadow long across the floor, her hand had simply moved—quick, sure, the trained hand of a woman who lifted hot type off the stone a hundred times a day—and the topmost broadside had come away with her, up under her apron, against her stomach, warm from the press.

Warm from the press. As if it were alive. As if it had a pulse.

She unfolded it now and spread it flat and read it again in the moonlight, slowly, the way she read a proof for errors, because reading was a kind of ownership and she wanted to own every word. *Eleven dead. Three were women. One was a child of seven.* She had heard the rumors, of course—everyone had heard the rumors, rumors were the winter's only crop—but rumor was smoke, and this was fire. Names. Dates. *The magistrate's own ledger records the order.* A ledger. Somewhere there was a book with Halden's signature in it, a book that proved what everyone whispered and no one could say, and Corvel knew of it, or believed in it, or was gambling three hundred sheets of fish-paper and his own neck on the hope of it.

She should burn it. That was the sensible course, the safe course, the course her father would have counseled with his sad kind eyes. A single sheet found in her possession would hang them both. Corvel had sworn her to silence, and she had sworn, and keeping the proof was a breach of the spirit if not the letter, and the letter was all that would matter if soldiers kicked in the door.

She got up. She took the sheet toward the stove. She knelt and opened the iron door, and the coals inside had kept a faint orange heart of heat, and she held the paper near it, near enough to feel the warmth push back against her fingers.

*Tomas Prout, aged seven.*

Paper remembers. That was the whole secret of the trade. Ink did not flatter, ink did not forget, and somewhere in this city a magistrate believed himself safe because the only people who knew the truth were frightened, and frightened people, given time, forget. That was how men like Halden worked—not with fire but with patience, waiting out memory the way winter waits out grass.

Her father had been patient for forty years. Where had it gotten him? A coffin carried by men who wept and then said nothing. A bench inherited by a daughter whose name appeared nowhere in the shop's books except as *E. Eastwick, deceased*.

Felix closed the stove door unlit.

She refolded the broadside—not carelessly, but along clean creases, the way a compositor folds a proof, small and square and tight—and she sewed it into the hem of her spare apron with thread and a sailmaker's needle she kept for mending, six stitches, invisible unless you knew to look. Then she put the apron in the bottom of her chest, under the winter petticoats, and sat back on her heels in the cold room, and listened to herself breathe.

Nothing had changed. That was the strange thing. The shop below was the same shop; the street outside was the same street; she was the same woman with the same stiff fingers and the same unfinished wedding invitation waiting on the stone. Tomorrow she would rise and cut type and be invisible, exactly as she had sworn.

But something had changed. Something had shifted in her the way a case of type shifts when you tilt it—all the sorts sliding, settling, finding new positions—and she recognized the feeling the way you recognize weather. It was the feeling of having chosen. Not largely, not bravely—a stolen proof sheet was a small treason, a coward's treason, hidden in a hem—but chosen nonetheless. For ten years she had waited for the world to notice her, and tonight she understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that the world would not. That noticing was not given. That if she wanted to exist—if she wanted her hands and her name and her father's craft to mean anything beyond swashes on a rich man's wedding—she would have to step into the light herself and stand there.

Somewhere across the frozen river, a courier was already riding. Though she could not have known it, though no one in Vellamont could have known it, the man who would change everything was that night tightening his horse's girth in a dark stable, wrapping a bundle in oilcloth, and looking north across the ice toward the lights of the city.

Felix blew out her candle and went to bed, and slept better than she had any right to.
