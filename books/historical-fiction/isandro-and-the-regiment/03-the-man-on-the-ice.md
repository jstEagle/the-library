## 3. The Man on the Ice

The river spoke first.

Isandro heard it before he saw anything—heard it the way a man hears a floorboard groan in a house where no one should be walking. The Vellamont in December was supposed to be silent, locked under its first skin of ice, muttering only to itself in the dark places beneath. But tonight the ice was talking, and the ice only talked when something crossed it that had no business being there.

He set down the lantern.

That was instinct, older than thought: light on the water at night meant death, whether from the river or from the men who watched its banks. He crouched instead at the top of the ferry stairs, letting his eyes do the work his hands wanted to do, and stared out at the black-and-silver expanse between Vellamont and the far shore.

A moon three days off full hung low and smeared behind cloud, laying a dull pewter road across the ice. And on that road, small and staggering, a man.

Isandro counted his steps without meaning to—the habit of a soldier who had once measured ground for guns. Ten paces, a fall. A struggle up. Ten more, another fall, longer this time. The figure rose again, slower, one arm clutched against his body as though holding something there, and came on.

Against orders. That was the thing Isandro understood immediately, the thing that made his chest tighten with something between pity and fury. No courier worth his salt crossed the Vellamont at night in the week before the bridge closed. The ice was barely four days old, thin as a promise over the deep channel where the current ran. Every smuggler, every runner, every desperate soul in the trade knew the rule: wait for hard frost, wait for the fishermen's report, wait. This man hadn't waited. Which meant whatever he carried mattered more to him than his life, and whoever chased him was close enough behind to make waiting impossible.

Isandro turned his head and listened to the far bank.

There. Not sound exactly—a quality of silence, the held-breath quiet of men standing still in snow. Somewhere along the northern shore, watchers. Two, maybe three. They would have seen the courier too. They were letting him come, letting the river do their work, patient as butchers.

* * *

He should go inside. He knew that the way he knew the weight of a musket, the way he knew the taste of orders refused. Livia was asleep above the boathouse, her cough finally quiet after three weeks of winter's cruelty, and every coin Isandro earned went into the jar beneath her floorboard—coins earned mostly in ways he did not name to her. Contraband, the word was. Tobacco, brandy, letters that official post charged too much for, once a bundle of lace that had made him blush to touch. He ferried it across the ice when the ice allowed, poled the flatboat through the channels when it didn't, and told himself each night that the sin was small and the hunger it fed was real.

But a man crossing against orders, pursued, carrying something wrapped against his body like a wound—

Isandro had been a soldier. He knew what it looked like when a man ran toward something rather than away from it. Deserters ran away. Smugglers ran away. This man was running *toward* the city, stumbling, falling, rising, spending his blood with every yard, and the oilcloth bundle strapped beneath his arm was not merchandise. Merchandise you could drop. Isandro had dropped plenty.

He took the stairs down to the ice.

* * *

The cold came up through his boot soles like a living thing, patient and enormous. Isandro moved out onto the river in the long gliding stride the ferrymen taught their sons—weight low, feet rolling heel to toe, never stopping, because stopping was how men went through. The ice sang under him in strange minor chords, thickening here, thinning there, and he read it with his ears the way his father had read it, the way his father's father had, back when the ferry stairs had belonged to honest work.

"Hey." His voice came out low, pitched to carry ten yards and no farther. "Hey. Friend."

The courier turned, and even in the bad light Isandro saw the mistake happen—the man's hand darting toward his coat, toward some weapon or some last desperate lie—and then the man's knees simply quit. He went down sideways, hard, and did not get up.

Isandro reached him in six strides and knelt, keeping his own weight spread wide, palms flat on the ice.

Up close the courier was younger than the dark had made him look. Twenty-five, perhaps thirty, with a beard crusted in frost and snot frozen at his nostrils, and eyes that were still working even while the rest of him failed—still moving, checking Isandro's face, checking the far bank, checking the bundle.

"They're—" The voice was ruined, scraped down to gravel. "Behind me. On the—"

"I know." Isandro glanced north. The silhouettes on the far shore had begun to move now, spreading out along the bank like ink dropped in water. They'd realized their prey had help, or might. "Can you stand?"

The man tried. Isandro got a shoulder under his arm and hauled, and the two of them stood swaying together like drunks, breath smoking in the space between their faces. Up close, Isandro could smell him: sweat gone cold, blood, horse, and under it all the particular sourness of a man who had ridden and run for days past sense.

"The bundle," the courier said. His hand pressed the oilcloth shape against his ribs, and Isandro felt the man's whole body flinch around it, protective even now, even dying. "It has to reach—there's a printer, in the city, Eastwick—no. Listen. Listen to me." His fingers closed on Isandro's collar with shocking strength, the strength of the last coin in a purse. "Corvel's shop. Off Tanner's Row. You know it?"

Everyone in Vellamont knew Corvel's shop; half the city's broadsides came out of it, and none of the dangerous ones, officially. "I know it."

"Tell no one else. Only—" A cough bent him double, wet and terrible. When he straightened there was blood on his lip, black in the moonlight. "Only the shop. Swear it."

"I don't swear things to dying strangers on rivers," Isandro said, and hated the hardness in his own voice, and kept it anyway, because softness now would kill them both. "I swear I'll get you off this ice. Walk."

They walked. Or rather, the man walked and Isandro happened to be holding him up, steering them away from the deep channel by a route only he knew—a wandering, drunken-looking line that was in fact the safest path on the river, learned from a father who'd drowned learning its limits. Behind them the figures on the far bank stopped at the waterline. One raised something—a horn, or a lantern shuttered and opened twice.

A signal. To someone on *this* side.

Isandro's stomach went cold in a way the river couldn't account for. There were men waiting in Vellamont too, then. Halden's men, if he had to guess, because everything rotten in the province lately traced back to Magistrate Halden sooner or later, the way all streams trace back to the high country. The courier had run the gauntlet of the far shore only to land inside the trap's other jaw.

"Almost there," Isandro said. "Thirty yards. Stay with me."

"I'm—" The man laughed, a horrible cracked sound. "I've stayed with worse. Longer."

* * *

The ferry stairs received them like an old dog receiving its master—wooden, worn, indifferent. Isandro got the courier up the steps by main strength, the man's boots drumming weakly on the boards, and into the boathouse where the smell of tar and rope and old water wrapped around them like a blanket. He lit no lamp. Instead he dragged the man behind the stacked timbers where the light of the city wouldn't find him, and knelt, and pulled the man's coat open to see what was killing him.

The bullet had gone in below the collarbone, low, and there was no exit wound. Blood had soaked through shirt, coat, the ragged padding beneath, and pooled and refrozen so that the fabric crackled when Isandro touched it. Days old, this wound. The man had taken the ball somewhere upriver and kept moving for—what? Two days? Three? Across country, across the ice, on foot when the horse gave out, because the bundle mattered more than the bleeding.

"You fool," Isandro said quietly. It came out almost tender.

The courier smiled with bloody teeth. "You should see the other fellow." Then the smile fell away and he grabbed Isandro's wrist, and his grip was going, Isandro could feel it going, the strength draining out of the hand like heat from a stone. "Listen. The bundle goes to Corvel's. Not to me—I'm done, I know I'm done, don't waste your—listen. Inside the case. There's a type case, in my pack, the false-bottomed one. It's sewn in. Under the sorts. Do you understand? Under the sorts."

"A type case," Isandro repeated. He understood nothing except that the man's eyes were beginning to shine in the way eyes shine when the person behind them is leaving.

"Names," the courier whispered. "Dates. What Halden did at Marrow Field, what he ordered at—everything. Signed. In his own hand, some of it. It has to be printed. It has to be *printed*, do you hear me, before the amnesty, before New Year's, because after that they'll burn it all and call it peace—" Another cough, longer, and when it ended the man lay very still for a moment, gathering himself from somewhere far away. "Promise me. Not God. God's got no hands in Vellamont. Promise *me*."

And there it was, the thing Isandro had spent two years avoiding since the regiment, since the order he'd refused, since the discharge that was really an exile: a request that could not be refused without becoming someone he despised. He thought of Livia asleep above them, her cough, the jar beneath the floorboard, the safe small sins. He thought of Marrow Field, which he had never seen but whose name he knew the way you know the name of a disease.

"I'll see it to the shop," Isandro said. "That's all I promise. The rest isn't mine to give."

"It's everyone's to give." The courier's head rolled toward the gap in the timbers, toward the city, where lights burned yellow and indifferent along the embankment. "That's the whole— that's the whole trouble with this world. Everyone thinks the truth belongs to somebody else."

His breathing changed then, and Isandro, who had knelt beside enough dying men in the regiment to know the change, did the only things left to do: he put the man's bundle in his arms, so that his hands had something to hold, and he sat with him in the dark among the tar and the ropes while the Vellamont muttered under its ice, and he did not leave until there was nothing left in the man to leave.

* * *

Afterward he sat a long time with his back against the timbers, listening to the silence where breathing had been.

The far bank was empty now. The watchers had withdrawn, satisfied or repositioning—he didn't know which, and both possibilities were bad. Somewhere in the city, men with a horn signal waited for a courier who would never arrive. Somewhere above him, Livia slept, and the jar under her floorboard held forty-one coins, and every one of them suddenly felt like it had been paid in a currency he didn't want to understand anymore.

The pack lay where he'd set it: a courier's satchel, scarred leather, one corner reinforced and heavier than it should be. The false bottom. The type case. *Under the sorts.*

Isandro looked at it for a long time.

He could take it to the river and open the ice and let the Vellamont keep its secrets. He could take it to the magistrate's office and buy Livia a doctor and a warm room and maybe a year of safety, until Halden's gratitude curdled, because gratitude from such men always curdled. He could take it to Corvel's shop, as promised, and wash his hands, and go back to smuggling brandy across a frozen river until the river or the soldiers finished him.

Or he could open the satchel and look at what a man had died carrying, and let it cost what it cost.

He didn't open it. That, somehow, felt like the courier's dignity, and he found he cared about the dignity of a dead stranger whose name he would never learn. But he lifted the satchel, and slung it across his back, and felt its weight settle between his shoulder blades like a hand.

Above the boathouse, faintly, Livia coughed in her sleep. Once. Twice. Quiet again.

Isandro pulled his coat collar up, took the lantern he had finally permitted himself, and climbed the stairs toward the sleeping city, toward Tanner's Row, toward a print shop he had passed a hundred times without ever once going in—carrying a dead man's errand in a case of type, and knowing, with the certainty of a man reading ice, that the thin places in his own life had just begun to crack.
