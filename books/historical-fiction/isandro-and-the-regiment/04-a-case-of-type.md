## 4. A Case of Type

**Felix**

The lamp had burned down to its last finger of oil when Felix heard the knocking.

Not knocking—she corrected herself, quill stilled above the ledger of accounts she would never show to a bank that would never deal with her anyway. Not knocking. Dragging. A slow, rhythmic scrape against the bottom panel of the shop door, as if something heavy were being drawn up the step by main force and could not quite manage it.

She set down the pen. The shop around her was the shape of her whole life: the iron press with its screw worn bright by her father's hand, now hers; the composing stone dusted with last week's failure; the wall of type cases rising floor to ceiling like a honeycomb built by some enormous, patient insect, each pigeonhole labeled in her father's upright clerk's hand—*brevier caps, long primer, pearl italic*—labels she had learned to read before she could read anything else. It was past eleven. Corvel had gone home at dusk with ink to his knuckles and a look on his face she had not been able to name. The streets outside had gone quiet in the way streets go quiet before snow, or before soldiers.

The scrape came again. Then a voice, muffled through oak and four inches of winter dark.

"Eastwick."

A man's voice, cracked down the middle like a dropped plate.

Felix did not open doors after hours. Her father had taught her that before he taught her anything about metal—*a print shop at night is a purse left open in an alley*—and she had kept the rule for three years alone. But the voice said it again, "Eastwick," and there was something in how it was said, not a name but a summons, a password spoken by someone who had used up all his other words getting here.

She took the poker from beside the stove and lifted the bar.

Cold poured in first. Then the man came after it, folding over the threshold like a coat falling off a hook. She caught him under the arms—he weighed nothing, which frightened her more than weight would have—and dragged him across the floor to the warmth of the stove, and only then, kneeling, saw what the dark had hidden.

His side was black. Not stained—*black*, soaked and dried and soaked again, the front of his coat frozen into a stiff plate that crackled when he breathed. There was ice in his hair. His lips had gone the gray-blue of lead type, and his hands, when she took one to hold, were so cold they burned.

"You're Eastwick's girl," he said.

"I'm Eastwick," said Felix, and did not know until the word was out of her that she had decided it, once and finally, in front of this dying stranger.

Something moved in his face—not a smile, but the place where a smile might have lived if he'd had the material to spare. His eyes went past her shoulder to the wall of cases, climbing the pigeonholes the way a drowning man looks up through water. "Good," he whispered. "Good. He said—the ferryman's boy said—follow the river to the shop that smells of ink. Follow the smell." A sound came out of him that was half laugh and entirely pain. "Smelled you from the bridge."

"Who hurt you?"

"Does it matter?" He coughed, and the cough bent him double, and when he straightened there was blood on his teeth, black in the lamplight. "Listen. Listen, typesetter. I don't have the time to be polite." His hand closed on her wrist with a strength that startled her, strength borrowed from somewhere, spent all in one grip. "There's a case. A double case, jobbing sorts, mixed fonts—no compositor mixes fonts unless he's hiding something. Do you understand me? No honest galley ever needed two alphabets."

"I understand."

"The false bottom. Under the sorts. Paper sewn—" another cough tore through him, longer this time, and she held his shoulders while his body tried to turn itself inside out— "sewn flat. Names. Dates. Orders. Everything Halden signed himself because no one dared tell him not to. It has to reach the press beyond the river before the year turns. Before the amnesty. After the amnesty it's worth nothing but our lives, do you hear, nothing but—"

"I hear you."

"Say it back."

Felix looked at him—at the frost melting out of his eyebrows, at the blood going tacky at the corner of his mouth, at eyes that were already receding from her, drawing up their anchors one by one and pulling for a farther shore—and she said it back. Every word. *Before the year turns. Beyond the river. The false bottom.*

"Good," he breathed. "Good girl. Your father cut a punch for me once. A merchant's mark, years ago. Charged me half what it was worth because I had—" the ghost of the smile again— "because I had a face he trusted. I've remembered that. All these roads. All this ice. I thought—I'll get to Eastwick's. Eastwick's girl will know what paper is worth."

And then his gaze slid off her face to the wall of cases, and stayed there, and Felix understood that he was looking for the right one even now, even dying, still doing his work, still faithful to it—and she turned to follow his eyes and help him, and his hand slipped from her wrist and struck the floor with a small, terrible click, knuckle-bone against plank, and he did not move again.

She knelt with him for a long while. Long enough for the stove to tick and settle. Long enough for the blood at his mouth to dry matte. She closed his eyes with her thumb, the way her mother had closed her grandmother's, and arranged his hands—one on his breast, and one, because she could not think what else to do with it, resting on the nearest type case as though he had merely paused, mid-labor, a workman at his bench.

Then she stood, and took the lamp, and began at the top left of the wall of cases, reading labels by her own father's handwriting, hunting a mixed font.

**Isandro**

He had followed the smell of ink up Tanner's Row the way he had promised a dead man he would not have to follow it himself, and so it was Isandro who stood in the lee of the cooper's doorway across the lane, lantern shuttered to a slit, watching the light in the Eastwick window gutter and shift and once go almost out altogether.

He had left the courier on the far bank with his orders still warm in the man's ears—*not tonight, wait for the ice to thicken, wait for the moon*—and the courier had waited exactly as long as it took Isandro to turn his back. He should have been angry. He had rehearsed anger the whole length of the towpath, composing the speech he would deliver over the fool's grave. But standing now in the coal-smell and river-smell of Tanner's Row, watching that lamp waver, he found there was no room in him for anything but the flat, mineral patience of grief. Men who cross bad ice are not fools. They are men who have calculated, correctly, that waiting will kill them surer than the water.

The shop door opened. Closed. Opened again—a wider gap, a rectangle of lamplight laid down the lane like a plank—and Isandro pressed into the cooper's shadow as a figure stepped halfway out and looked both ways, up and down, with the careful sweep of someone checking for wolves.

A woman. Young. Sleeves rolled, forearms bare in the cold, ink to the second knuckle of both hands. She stood in her own doorway breathing white and listening, and Isandro watched her and thought: *the daughter.* He knew the shape of the story without being told it—old Eastwick dead three winters, the shop limping on, the trade pretending the daughter was a housekeeper because the trade could not afford to imagine otherwise. He had passed this window a hundred times. He had never once seen her.

She went back in. The light steadied.

Isandro counted to two hundred, the way his father had taught him to count before crossing thin ice—*two hundred, then your weight, then listen*—and crossed the lane and knocked.

The door opened the width of a hand. Inside, the poker's shadow on the wall, held high.

"Ferryman's boy," Isandro said quietly. "From the north bank. He told me where he was going before he went."

The door did not move. The eyes in the gap—dark, level, unimpressed by him, which he was unaccustomed to and found he did not dislike—went over his face the way she probably went over type: sorting, rejecting flaws.

"He's dead," Isandro said. "I know he's dead, because I pulled him out of the shallows and he walked away from me against my orders, and nobody walks away from those shallows twice. I carried his errand as far as the row. You carried it the rest." He let that stand a moment. "I'm coming in, or I'm freezing in your doorway, and one of those options makes noise."

A pause. Then the door opened, and he stepped down into the shop, into warmth and ink and the particular close metallic smell of a thousand pounds of lead, and stopped, because the courier lay on the floor by the stove with his eyes closed and his hand on a type case, laid out as neatly as a monument, and Isandro had seen enough dead men to know how much care that arrangement had cost somebody.

"You covered for him," he said. "The eyes. The hands."

"He didn't deserve to be found crooked."

"No," Isandro agreed. "He didn't." He crouched by the body, keeping his distance from the woman, who had put the stove between them and stood holding the poker with the ease of long acquaintance. "What did he say to you?"

"Enough." Her chin came up. "Why does the ferryman's son care what a courier says in a print shop? Why did you guide him at all? What's in this for Livia's brother?"

So she knew him too, or knew of him. The city talked. He filed that away. "I ferry things," he said. "That's my trade, since the regiment spat me out. Most of what I ferry is brandy and untaxed lace. Tonight a man asked me to point him at a smell of ink, and I pointed, and he died pointing. That puts me in the story whether either of us likes it. And the men who killed him—" he nodded at the frozen black plate of the courier's coat— "will come asking the river who saw him cross. The river answers to Magistrate Halden. So yes. I care."

She considered this. Outside, wind ran down Tanner's Row and rattled a loose shingle somewhere above them, once, twice, like a finger drumming.

"Help me look, then," she said, and lowered the poker. "Mixed fonts. Two alphabets in one case. He said no honest galley ever needed two."

Isandro knew nothing of galleys. But he knew hiding places—knew them the way a poacher knows hedgerows—and he understood at once the beauty of what the courier had described. Not a strongbox. Not a floorboard. Something sitting in plain view on a shelf among ten thousand identical objects, legible to everyone and readable by no one. He felt the same cold admiration he always felt when contraband was well hidden: the pleasure of craft, wherever you found it.

They worked down the wall together. She read labels and drew cases out an inch, tilting them to the lamp; he stood at her shoulder and learned, faster than he expected, the geography of the alphabet in compartments—this box all capital letters, that one a chaos of punctuation, e's everywhere and z's hoarded in their little drawer like coins. Her hands moved without hesitation. Once, reaching past him for a high case, her sleeve brushed his jaw and smelled of oil and lamp smoke, and he stepped back too quickly and cracked his elbow on the press, and she did not laugh, but something in her face suggested she had considered it.

"There," she said suddenly.

Bottom row, third from the corner. She hauled it out onto the composing stone with a grunt—it weighed half again what it should—and set the lamp beside it, and Isandro saw what she meant. The case was a madman's alphabet: three faces jumbled together, fat letters and thin, italics shoved in among roman, a compositor's nightmare. Unless you were not meant to compose with it at all.

Felix Eastwick went very still. Then she reached to the rack above the bench and took down a bodkin—a printer's bodkin, he learned it was called, a steel spike with a wooden handle worn smooth—and worked its tip along the frame of the case until it found the seam of a false bottom that was fitted so finely he had looked straight at it and seen nothing.

"Lift the sorts," she said. "Both ends. Evenly—they're set in wax, they'll come out whole."

Together they lifted the tray of type out entire, a shallow wooden box bristling with lead, and set it aside, and there beneath it, where the depth of the case should have continued down into darkness, lay paper.

Sewn. She was right, the courier had said sewn and it was true: sheets upon sheets, folded flat, stitched edge to edge with fine waxed thread directly into the wooden bottom of the case, so that the whole document could not be removed without ripping it apart—which meant, Isandro thought, with a lurch, that whoever made this had intended it never to be read in haste, or carried away in one piece, or found by anyone unwilling to sit down and take the case itself apart, patiently, in private, with tools.

Felix picked the stitches. Her fingers were steady. His were not, and he noticed that, and disliked knowing it about himself.

The first page came free and she flattened it on the stone under the lamp, and Isandro leaned in over her shoulder, and the room went very quiet.

It was a fair copy, clerk's hand, dated entries in columns. He could read—soldiers learn to read orders, it is the first thing they teach you to fear—but he had never read anything like this. *Vellamont, March the twelfth. By order of the Magistrate. The weavers assembled at the Corn Exchange to number above two hundred. Dispersed.* And beneath, in a different ink, a tally, a butcher's count: *Eleven. Bodies conveyed by cart to the north quarry. No register.*

Page after page. August: strikers at the ropewalk, dispersed, nine. October: a debtors' petition, its signatories arrested by name—all the names written out, forty-one names in a neat column—and the column ended in a second hand, a heavier, bolder hand than the clerk's, a hand that pressed hard enough to bite through the paper, and beside the final entry the bold hand had signed itself.

*Halden.*

He heard Felix breathe. One breath in, long and shaking; one breath out, slower, mastered. When he glanced at her, her face in the lamplight had gone the color of the paper, but her jaw was set, and she was reading the way she must read everything, completely, missing nothing, and he understood that whatever was happening behind that steady face was larger and colder than anything he had room for just now.

"These are massacres," she said. Flat. Precise. "These aren't rumors. These are his own counts. In his own hand." She turned a page. Another column of names. "My father printed broadsides for the guild for thirty years. Half these men ordered type from him." Her finger stopped on a line. "*Apprentice, aged fourteen, name unrecorded.* They didn't even—" She shut her mouth.

"They wrote everything down," Isandro said quietly. "That's the thing about magistrates. They never believe the day will come when the writing gets read."

Silence. The stove ticked. On the floor between them the courier lay with his hand still resting on the emptied case, and Isandro thought: *he carried this across the river in his ribs. Every mile. This weight, in a wound.* And the thought did something to him he did not examine, because examining it would have required admitting he was already decided.

"We burn it," Felix said.

He looked up sharply. "You said—"

"I know what he said. I'm saying what a sensible woman says." But she wasn't looking at him; she was looking at the ledger, at the bold biting signature, and her hand had come to rest flat on the page, fingers spread, the way a person lays a hand on something to keep it from blowing away. "Burn it and live. Nobody in this city can carry this past one checkpoint, let alone three, let alone Halden's constables, let alone the river in January. Sensible women burn things and survive."

"Did you learn that from your father?"

Her head came around. For a moment he thought she would strike him with the poker after all.

"My father," she said, very evenly, "taught me that type exists to be read."

"Then it doesn't get burned." Isandro crouched, took the last stitched sheet, and folded it closed over the others with a care that surprised him. "It gets across the river. Before the year turns, he said. Before the amnesty burns every charge in Vellamont—including, if we're slow, ours." He rose. Through the window, over the roofline, the citadel on the hill wore its ring of watch-fires like a dark crown set with sparks, and somewhere beyond the river, faint as a heartbeat, the garrison drum was changing to the winter watch. He had heard that change every night of his life. Tonight it sounded like a countdown.

"Ferryman," Felix said behind him.

He turned. She had taken the ledger and was already working—the needle was in her hand before he saw where it came from, thread drawn tight, her fingers moving with the blind sure speed of real craft—as she sewed the pages back into the bones of the type case, restoring it, making it once more a madman's alphabet and nothing more.

"If we're doing this," she said, without looking up, "we do it my way. Paper hides best in a print shop. And I know things about lead and ink that no magistrate alive knows."

Above Tanner's Row, far off toward the bridge, a bell began to ring out of hour—three strokes, a pause, three strokes again—and Isandro, who knew the signals of the city the way other men knew psalms, went cold from the soles of his feet upward.

"That's the harbor watch," he said. "They're closing the bridge."
