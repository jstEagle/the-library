## 5. The Bridge Closes

The bridge closed on a Tuesday, and the whole city heard it close.

Felix was at the bench when the first hammer fell—she felt it before she understood it, a dull concussion traveling through the floorboards of the shop, up through the legs of her stool, into the bones of her wrist where she held the graver against a half-finished punch. The steel chattered. A sliver of type-metal sheared wrong, ruining an hour's work.

She swore—not loudly, because Corvel had a rule about swearing in the shop that he himself broke hourly—and set down the tool. Through the frost-furred window she could see nothing but the street: the lamplighter making his rounds in the blue hour before dawn, a milk cart, breath rising from horses like smoke from chimneys. But the sound kept coming, rhythmic as a press at full run. *Thud. Thud. Thud.* Something being built or something being broken, and from the direction of the river there was only one thing it could be.

She climbed the stairs to the composing room, where the windows faced the water, and looked out over the rooftops toward the Vellamont Bridge.

They were driving piles. Great squared timbers rose at either end of the span, and soldiers moved along the wooden deck in a line like ants across a plank, and at the far side—already half-raised—a gate. Not the old customs gate with its peeling green paint and its bored clerk who had known her father. A new gate. Black timber, iron-studded, with a roofed platform behind it where muskets would rest on rails for men to shoot from.

The winter bridge closing was not new. Every year the ice came down the river in February floes and made the crossing dangerous; every year the city posted a notice and the ferrymen tied up their boats until spring. But this was December still. The river ran black and open between its banks, steaming faintly in the cold, and the ice would not come for weeks. Whatever was closing the bridge had nothing to do with the weather.

*Thud.* Another pile. She counted the soldiers again. Twelve on the deck. More massing at the near approach, their coats dark as spilled ink against the snow.

"Felix." Corvel's voice from below, quiet in a way she had learned meant trouble. "Come away from the window."

* * *

He did not say anything more while the light came up. He set her to work on a jobbing order—five hundred handbills for a chandler announcing new prices in tallow—and he worked beside her at the second press, which was unusual, and said nothing about anything, which was more unusual still. The shop around them did what shops do: the stove ticked and breathed its smell of coal and hot iron; the paper in the racks rustled when the door opened and shut; somewhere upstairs Betto, the apprentice, was sweeping and singing under his breath a song about a girl and a nightingale that he thought no one heard.

But the hammers kept sounding from the river. All morning. All afternoon. By the time the light failed and Corvel lit the lamps, Felix had stopped flinching at each blow and started counting instead, the way she counted when she cut punches—one, two, three, breathe—until the rhythm became almost bearable, became almost music, became almost nothing at all.

At four o'clock a town crier went down the street, and his bell had a cracked note she'd never noticed before, or perhaps the crack was new.

*"By order of Magistrate Halden, acting under authority of the Provincial Council! The Vellamont Bridge is closed to all traffic effective this day! No person shall cross by bridge, ferry, or ford! Any person found upon the river shall be liable to arrest and examination! The bridge will reopen upon the restoration of order!"*

The crier passed. His bell faded. In the silence after, Corvel set down his composing stick very carefully, as if it might go off.

"Restoration of order," he said. "There has been no disorder."

"There has been you," Felix said.

It came out sharper than she intended. Corvel looked at her over the top of the composing stone, and his face—which was a good face, a kind face, lined like a well-used letterpress bed—did something complicated. Not anger. Something older and tireder than anger.

"Yes," he said. "There has been me."

* * *

After Betto went home—they let him go early, because there was no work that needed an apprentice and because Corvel seemed to want the shop empty—the master printer bolted the door, drew both shutters, and stood with his back against them for a long moment, a broad man making himself into a barricade.

Then he turned and looked at the case of type.

It sat where it had sat since the stranger died with his hand on it: at the back of the store-room, beneath the rag shelf, ordinary as any of the hundred other cases in the shop. Brevier sorts, mostly, if you lifted the lid and looked. Lowercase e's and n's and a's in their compartments, worn smooth by other hands, other years. Only Felix knew that beneath the trays, sewn into the false bottom her own father had built for smuggling French engravings during the last war, lay forty pages of names.

The dead man's blood had been cleaned from the floor, but she could still find the spot by standing in the wrong place on purpose.

"Show me again," Corvel said.

"There's nothing more to show. You've seen it."

"I want to look at it while I tell you what we're going to do."

So she fetched the lamp and knelt and slid the case out, and worked loose the bottom board, and unwrapped the oilcloth, and there they were: the pages in their careful clerk's hand, the columns of dates, and the signatures. *Halden.* Signed with a small, precise, self-satisfied flourish, over and over, forty times, like a man practicing autographs on the deaths of villages. Rencourt. Marais Field. The hamlets upriver whose burning had been called quarantine, whose dead had been called fever, whose survivors had been scattered through these streets and were even now walking past this shuttered shop not knowing that their testimony lay sewn under a tray of lowercase letters.

Corvel looked at the pages for a long time. The lamp guttered and steadied.

"Burn it," he said.

The words dropped into the room like type metal into water—hiss, sink, done.

"No."

"Felix—"

"You know what I'm going to say, so don't build up to it. Say whatever you have to say and I'll say no at the end of it anyway."

That startled something like a laugh out of him, brief and bitter. He pulled the stool over and sat down heavily, knees cracking, and rubbed his face with both hands the way her father used to when a job went wrong.

"The bridge is closed," he said. "The ferries are seized—I watched the water bailiff's men drag Livia's father's boat up the stairs this morning and put a chain through it. The roads beyond the bridge are checkpointed already; my cousin saw them on the north road at noon. There are warrants out—we don't know for whom yet, but Halden does not print pamphlets naming himself and then sleep soundly. Sooner or later, and I promise you it is sooner, soldiers will come to this shop, because I have printed what I have printed, and when they come they will search, and when they search they will find that case, and everything that follows from that finding lands on you as much as me. On you worse. You're nobody, Felix. That's your one protection—you're invisible, a woman at a bench, no name on any register. Don't spend your invisibility on paper. Burn it, sweep the ashes, and we live."

He said it all quietly, kindly, the way you explain arithmetic to a child. And every word of it was true, and she had turned each of those truths over herself in the dark hours since the courier died, testing them for flaws the way she tested a matrix, and found none.

"It named someone," she said.

"What?"

"The courier. Before he died. You weren't here yet—he was past speaking, mostly, but he gripped my wrist and he said it twice so I couldn't mistake it." Her own voice sounded strange to her, level and cold, like reading aloud. "*Aurel Dantz, at Tervane.* Aurel Dantz, at Tervane, beyond the river, three days' ride north where Halden's writ runs thin. There's a press there that isn't yours, Master Corvel, and a man who has been printing this same truth for years with the whole country between him and the magistrate. That's where this goes. Not into the fire. Across."

"And how does it cross?" Corvel spread his hands at the shutters, at the walls, at the whole sealed city. "The bridge is closed. The river is watched. The ferries are chained. You are a typesetter, not a miracle worker."

"I don't know yet."

"Then—"

"But I know it doesn't burn." She was kneeling on the cold floor with the ledger open in her lap and she realized her hands had begun shaking and she made them stop, made them be steady, the way they went steady over a graver, because this too was cutting work, precision work, and a slip here cost more than a spoiled punch. "You think I don't understand what you're offering? You're offering fear. It's a fair price. I've been paying it since he put my hand on the bench and died, paying it every day I sign nothing and stand in the back of the shop and let the guild men talk over my head as if I were furniture. Fear for safety, silence for survival—I know the exchange rate better than anyone in this city. And I am telling you the rate has changed. Because these—" she touched the pages, lightly, one finger, "—are people who were told their sons died of fever. Their wives died of fever. They were lied to by the same hand that signed this. If we burn it, then Halden wins twice: once with the killing and once with the forgetting. I won't help him forget. I'd rather die visible than live invisible, and I've been dying invisibly for years anyway, so the choice costs less than you think."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, faintly, the hammers had stopped—or she had stopped hearing them.

Corvel studied her for a long time, the way he studied a proof sheet, hunting errors. Then he sighed, and the sigh seemed to take ten years off him and give back twenty.

"Dantz," he said slowly. "I've heard the name. Everyone in the trade has heard the name; half of us have prayed we'd never earn his attention and the other half have prayed we might. He prints on a stone press smuggled piece by piece over the mountains in flour sacks, they say. He's been at it eleven years." He looked at the ledger with an expression Felix had never seen on him before—not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man meeting his own conscience in someone else's handwriting. "Three days north. Through checkpoints that didn't exist yesterday."

"Then I need someone who knows how things move in this city when they're not supposed to."

"Who?"

"The ferryman's son. Isandro. He guided the courier down from the north bank—he must have, the man crossed the ice at Miller's Reach where no one else would go. If anyone knows the river, knows the gaps in the watch, it's him. Find him for me. Or bring him to me. I'll pay whatever the shop can pay."

Corvel was silent a moment. "You trust him?"

"I don't know him. That's different from trusting him. But the courier trusted him with his life to get him this far, and the courier knew what he was carrying. That's a character reference written in risk, and I've learned to read it."

Her master printer got up, stiffly, and went to the window and put his eye to the shutter gap. From the street came the sound of the watch changing at the corner—more boots than usual, she thought, though maybe that was imagination breeding in the dark.

"When I was young," Corvel said, without turning, "I believed what you believe. That ink was stronger than iron. I've spent thirty years learning otherwise and thirty years doing it anyway, which I believe makes me a hypocrite rather than a coward, and I'll thank you to remember the distinction." He let the shutter fall. "I won't burn it. God forgive me, I won't burn it. But hear me, Felix, and hear me fully: if it comes to a moment where the ledger burns or a person burns, choose the person. Paper can be reset. It's what our trade *means*. Nothing printed is ever truly lost—it lives in memory, in hands, in the shape it left on the world. But people only set once."

"I know," she said softly. "I've cast enough of them."

* * *

She stayed late in the shop, alone, which was against every rule Corvel had made and which he pretended not to know about. She trimmed the wicks and banked the stove and sat at her father's bench with the single lamp, and took up the ruined punch from the morning, and began again.

Outside, Vellamont lay sealed like a fist. The bridge gates stood black against the last grey light, and watch fires bloomed along the far bank, and somewhere in the city there were already men being questioned, doors being knocked upon, lists being consulted. The trap had closed with all the patience of a thing that had always known it would close. She could feel the walls of it, the whole river-bent city become a box with her inside it, and the ledger beneath the type case like a heart beating under the floorboards.

Let it be a box, she thought. Boxes were her trade. A chase locked tight around loose metal became a page; a page pressed to paper became a thousand voices. Everything she had ever made had begun shut up small and dark and immovable.

She set the graver to steel and cut, stroke by stroke, the letter taking form under her hand, and if her mind was on the river, and the ice coming, and a ferryman's son she had never spoken to, her hands did not tremble once.
