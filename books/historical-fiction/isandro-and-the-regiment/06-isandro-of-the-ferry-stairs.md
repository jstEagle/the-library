## 6. Isandro of the Ferry Stairs

The river had a smell in winter, Isandro thought, that no summer ever quite erased. Under the frost and the woodsmoke and the iron tang of the ice there was always the river's true breath—mud and weed and the ghost of everything it had carried down from the high country and never given back. He had grown up on that breath. It was in his lungs the way his mother's lullabies had been, before the fever took her voice and then her. When he stood at the top of the ferry stairs at first light, watching the mist come off the black water like souls reluctant to leave, he could not tell where the city ended and he began.

Vellamont did not end anywhere, really. It frayed. At its heart were the stone quarters—the magistrate's hall, the customhouse, the cathedral with its lead roof gone green—and then the streets grew poorer by degrees until they dissolved into lanes like his own, where the houses leaned together for warmth and the gutters ran brown even in hard frost. And below all of it, stitched to the cliff face above the water, the ferry stairs: two hundred and forty steps cut into the rock, slick with spray, descending to the landing where his father's boat knocked against its mooring like a patient animal.

His father was sleeping off last night's brandy. His sister Livia would be at the washhouse by now, hands red to the wrist. And the courier—the courier was three days dead, laid out in the plague-house at the edge of the salt quarter because no parish would take him, and the thing he had carried was gone from under the floorboard where Isandro had hidden it.

Gone, because Isandro himself had told the girl at the print shop where to look.

He turned that over as he took the stairs down, one hand on the wet rope rail. He had watched her go into the shop with the oilcloth bundle clutched inside her coat and he had thought, *that is the last I will see of it*, and had felt—not relief exactly, but a loosening, like setting down a load he had carried farther than he meant to. The man on the ice had paid him half in advance to see him across the river, and Isandro had done it, and the man had died anyway, and whatever war or vengeance rode inside that wrapped rectangle was no longer Isandro's affair.

Except that it was. Because the dying man had said a name—*Eastwick*—and pressed his last coin into Isandro's palm with instructions that if the package reached the print shop, Isandro should return in three days' time for payment. Payment. From a print shop the size of a chapel vestry, run by a dead man's daughter who cut type smaller than rice grains. What could they pay him with? A few coins, surely. Perhaps a book. Perhaps trouble, which was the only currency Vellamont minted in abundance this winter.

He climbed the two hundred and forty stairs back up because he could not afford not to. Livia needed winter shoes. The ferry tax had doubled since Halden's new ordinances, and his father's boat leaked worse every week. Three days ago a stranger's blood had soaked through his coat sleeve, and he had washed it out in the river and told himself the story ended there.

Stories did not end. They only changed tellers.

* * *

The Eastwick print shop sat on Quill Lane behind the chandler's, and you smelled it before you saw it: ink and lamp-black and hot metal, a smell Isandro knew from his father's brief and disastrous employment as a pressman's porter, back when Isandro was small enough to sleep under the compositor's stone. The sign above the door said EASTWICK & SON in letters gone soft with weather, though everyone on the lane knew the son was long dead—or rather, that there had never been a son worth the name, only the daughter, whom the trade had decided not to see.

He raised his hand to knock and the door opened before his knuckles fell.

The girl stood in the gap with a bodkin in her fist—a real one, steel, held low and forward the way a butcher holds a knife, not the way anyone held a sewing tool. Her eyes went to his face, then his hands, then the street behind him, in that order, quick as a clerk counting change. She had ink on her jaw and a smear of red lead across her apron, and her hair was coming loose from its knot in wisps that caught the lamplight behind her like fine copper wire.

"You," she said.

"Me." He kept his hands visible, open at his sides. "Isandro. Of the ferry stairs. I brought your courier across the ice."

"I know who you are." She did not move out of the doorway. Behind her the shop was dim, and Isandro's eye, trained by years of scanning a horizon for patrol boats, catalogued what lamplight showed him: a press like a great black ox; frames of drawers along the wall, each cell a mouth full of lead teeth; and there, on a workbench near the back, the oilcloth wrapping, opened flat and empty, laid out with a care that looked almost like mourning.

"You've been watching the street," he said. "Good."

"Have you come to rob me? Because if you have, you should know my father built this doorframe and he built it thick, and the watch is slow but the chandler's dog is not."

"I've come for payment," Isandro said. "The man who died—he hired me to see him to your door. Half before, half after. That was our bargain, and I keep my bargains, even with dead men. Especially with dead men."

Something shifted in her face—not softening, exactly. Recalculation. She stepped back just far enough that he could enter if he chose, and kept the bodkin where he could see it, which he understood to be a courtesy and a warning both.

He chose to enter.

Inside, the shop was warmer than the street and darker than it had looked, lit by a single lamp turned low and hooded with a tin shield so no glow escaped the windows. That detail told him more than anything she might have said. Honest shops did not hide their light. He thought of Livia asleep with her knees drawn up, and hated, briefly, the clarity with which he understood what this room was for.

The girl shut the door and shot a bolt he had not seen, iron, hand-forged, set into new-cut wood. New work. Her work, or her father's last.

"The ledger," she said, without preamble. "You knew about it."

"I knew a man carried something he was willing to drown for. That's a different thing from knowing."

"Is it?" She set the bodkin down on the composing stone, within reach of her hand, deliberately outside reach of his. "He crossed against orders. In December. On ice that was three days old and lying about itself. Nobody crosses that river in December unless what they carry outweighs what they are." Her voice was steady but he heard the seam in it, the place where the steadiness was sewn over something raw. "You put him in your boat. You watched him walk up the bank. You watched him die at my threshold. And then you came back to your ferry stairs and mended nets for three days."

"Yes."

"That's all? Yes?"

"What would you have had me do?" Isandro asked, and heard how flat his own voice went, the ferryman's flatness, the voice he used on the water when the current argued. "Carry him through the streets calling for a surgeon? Bring him to the garrison hospital, where Magistrate Halden's men ask every dying man what name he carries and who sent him? He'd have been dead a day sooner and searched an hour earlier. I did what the river does. I carried him to the far bank and let go."

"The river doesn't choose."

"No," Isandro said. "That's what makes it safe. And what makes it useless."

She looked at him then—really looked, the way she might look at a piece of type she suspected of being cracked. He stood it without flinching. He was used to being weighed by people who expected to find him light.

"There's bread," she said finally, as though the words cost her something. "And there's payment. Sit down before you say anything else clever about rivers."

* * *

They ate standing, which suited them both. The bread was yesterday's and honest, and there was a crock of dripping she set out without comment, and Isandro understood he was being measured against some standard her father had left in the furniture of the room: men, presumably, had been fed at this bench before, and judged here too.

The payment, when she counted it out, was fair. More than fair—she added a coin and pushed it back across the boards when he would have left it, and said, "For the ice. He told me you walked ahead of him and tested each step with your whole weight so he wouldn't have to. He was very clear that you didn't have to do that."

"He paid me."

"He paid you to row. Walking ahead was yours." She folded her arms. "So now we're square, and you can go back to your stairs, and I'll go back to pretending this shop cuts nothing but almanacs and wedding invitations, and in twenty years we'll tell each other lies about the winter of 'ninety-two. Isn't that how these stories close?"

"That depends," Isandro said, "on whether you're alive in twenty years to tell them."

Her chin came up. He watched her decide to be angry and then, mid-decision, decide against it, which told him she was smarter than her anger, and lonelier than either.

"Say what you came to say, ferryman."

"Three things." He held up fingers, a habit from the regiment, from briefing men who might die if they miscounted. "One: Betto."

"I know the name. Corvel's apprentice. He's been hanging around the lane asking questions about my father's old customers."

"Betto has been asking questions in four taverns, not one, and last night he drank at the Sign of the Ledger—which is not named for account books—on Halden's money. I know the man who buys for that table. Your secrets are already leaking, and they haven't even reached the interesting ones yet."

He watched that land. Her jaw tightened; her hands, resting on the composing stone, curled slightly, and he saw the calluses—thumb, forefinger, the graver's callus, the fileder's ridge—saw thirty hours of cutting in every finger and understood suddenly, precisely, what kind of person he was dealing with. Not a merchant's daughter gone slumming in her father's shop. A craftsman. Someone who knew, in her hands, what patience cost.

"Two," he said. "The bridge closed yesterday. You know that."

"I know that."

"Closed means checkpoints. Three of them between here and the far shore of the question you're carrying. Customhouse, toll gate, river fort. I've run goods past all three since I was fourteen—tea, mostly, then lace, then pamphlets, then people. The guards rotate on saints' days and never otherwise. The sergeant at the customhouse owes my father money and hates him for it, which makes him predictable. The toll gate searches wagons and ignores widows. The river fort—" He paused. "The river fort is Halden's own house, and Halden likes to inspect travelers personally when the mood takes him. You cannot bribe a man whose vice is certainty."

"And three?"

"Three is that whoever carries your ledger will be carrying paper into a country that burns paper. Unless the paper isn't paper." He nodded at the empty oilcloth, at the drawers of type, at the galley of printed verse sitting on the stone beside the bread crumbs. "I read a little. Enough. That's an almanac page."

"It's a proof of the January almanac. Weather verses. Frost prognostications." Her voice was carefully nothing.

"Mm," Isandro said. "And if I asked you whether the line about the wolf moon and the drowned fields meant anything besides weather, you'd tell me it was superstition, and I'd believe you, because you'd be right—it is superstition. To me. To any guard who opens a case of galleys looking for a ledger written in a dead courier's hand, it's superstition too. But somewhere past that bridge there's a man who can read it properly. Am I wrong?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the lane the chandler's dog barked twice, at nothing, and settled.

"You were a soldier," Felix said at last. It wasn't a question.

"Four years. Dragoons. Discharged without color." He gave her the standard lie by telling her the truth in a shape she couldn't use; that was a skill the army had taught him too. "The point is not my biography. The point is a bargain."

"I don't make bargains with men I met over a corpse."

"No. You make bargains with men you've fed bread to and counted coins with and watched to see if their eyes go to the cash box." He smiled, thin. "Mine didn't. Notice that? You noticed. That's why you're still talking to me."

He spread his hands on the composing stone, mirroring hers, close enough that the space between their fingers was its own kind of sentence.

"Here's the shape of it. You have a thing that must cross three checkpoints, and you cannot carry it yourself because Halden's warrant—if there is one yet, and there will be—has a woman's shape in it. I have knowledge of the road and a face that no one writes descriptions of, because I'm the kind of man nobody looks at twice. What I don't have is a way to move paper past men whose whole profession is paper. That's yours. Your father hid things in plain sight—I've heard the stories, everyone on the wharf has, the printer who smuggled a bishop's letter inside a funeral sermon. Teach me the trick of it. Or better—do it, and let me carry the harmless-looking thing you've made."

"And what do you get," Felix said slowly, "besides coins?"

Isandro looked at the oilcloth wrapper, empty now, and at the drawer where he knew—even before she followed his glance and her face answered for her—that the ledger lay sewn beneath the sorts, under forty pounds of lead and a century of other men's words.

"Livia gets shoes," he said. "And I stop waking up having dreamed about a man on the ice who trusted the wrong ferryman's son to finish his crossing." He paused. "Also, whoever reads those pages past the checkpoints will need someone at the fort checkpoint who knows which sentry drinks and which one prays, and where the wall is lowest, and what time the shift changes when it snows. You can't buy that knowledge in a market. You're unlikely to grow it yourself before New Year's."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take my coins, and I wish you long life, and in the spring I'll stand on my stairs and watch them march you to the customhouse, and I'll hate myself competently, the way I'm good at, and it will change nothing." He said it evenly, without heat, because it was simply the weather ahead, and he had learned not to argue with weather. "But you won't say no. Because you've spent three days guarding that case like a wolf over a kill, and a wolf is a hungry animal, and hunger is just another word for a plan that hasn't found its legs."

Silence. The press stood black and patient at the room's heart, and Isandro thought it looked like the hull of a ship waiting on cargo, on tide, on a decision made by someone braver and more foolish than the sea.

Felix picked up the bodkin—not as a threat; she turned it in her fingers the way another woman might turn a ring, thinking. Then she set it down on the far side of the stone, out of both their reaches.

"My father used to say," she said, "that the difference between a printer and a counterfeiter is only a matter of what the reader wants to believe. Everything can be faked except the reader's faith." She looked up at him, and there was iron in it now, and exhaustion, and something underneath both that he was careful not to name. "If I teach you how testimony hides inside weather verses, ferryman, you'll know it forever. There's no unlearning it. And men who know it tend to die knowing it."

"I've been dying of ignorance instead," Isandro said. "It's slower and it pays worse."

Almost—almost—she laughed. What she did instead was pull a drawer from the frame, set it on the bench between them, and run her finger down the ranks of little lead letters like a general reviewing troops.

"Sit down," said Felix Eastwick. "Lesson one: nothing is hidden by darkness. Darkness is for amateurs. We hide things by making them boring."

Outside, the sleet had begun again, ticking against the shutters like a slow Morse. And down in the lane, past the chandler's, past the corner, a narrow-shouldered young man in a borrowed coat turned his collar up against the weather and lingered a moment in the lee of the sign that said EASTWICK & SON—long enough to count the lamplight in the cracks of the shutters, and mark the hour, and walk on whistling toward the Sign of the Ledger.
