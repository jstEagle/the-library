## 7. Hiding Truth Among Letters

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Felix finally understood what her father would have done.

She had been staring at the ledger for three nights. It lay open on the type-cutting bench, weighted at the corners with quoins, its pages soft as old linen from handling. Forty-one names. Dates going back six years. Orders in a clerk's neat hand, countersigned in Halden's heavy, downward-slanting script—*let the example be made thorough*—and beside each order, in a different ink, the arithmetic of the dead. Not numbers only. Descriptions. *Weaver, lame left leg, carried a red kerchief.* *Boy of twelve, ran messages for the weavers' circle.* A woman named Marta Ollen who had asked, in writing, why her husband's loom was confiscated before his trial and was drowned in the millpond nine days later with her petition folded in her bodice.

Felix had read it so many times she could recite pages of it, and still she could not make it small enough to carry.

The bridge was closed. The city was a box with them inside it. Corvel wanted the ledger burned; Isandro wanted it moved; and every plan either man proposed came down to the same impossible problem: forty pages of damning paper, sewn beneath a case of type that any soldier with a bayonet could pry apart in under a minute. The ledger could not walk out of Vellamont as itself. It needed to become something else.

Her father's voice came back to her the way it always did—not as memory exactly, but as instruction, arriving at the moment of need like a hand on her shoulder. *A punch is not a letter, Lass. A punch is a decision. You cut away everything that isn't the letter until only the truth of it remains.*

He had been talking about steel. But he had also, in the lean years, when the shop took whatever work paid, taught her the other thing—the trick of the trade that printers passed along in low voices over ale: how to hide one text inside another. He called it the widow's psalm, because the first time he'd seen it done was in a smuggled Bible printed during the old persecutions, each forbidden verse disguised as a funeral elegy, readable only if you knew which letters to count and which to skip. He had shown her once, laughing, using a recipe for pickled herring as the cover text. She had been eleven years old and had thought it a game.

She was not eleven now, and no one was laughing.

Felix pulled a fresh galley toward her and began to rule it lightly in pencil, dividing it into lines of the length an almanac would use. Almanacs were perfect—no one read almanacs closely, and everyone read them constantly. Weather prognostications. Verse for the months. Home remedies. Farmers bought them by the bale; soldiers confiscated nothing but weapons and sedition, and no officer alive had ever considered a weather rhyme dangerous. Her father had printed two thousand almanacs some years just to keep the presses warm through winter.

The ledger had to become almanac verse. And the verse had to be built so that every line of testimony lived inside it, recoverable by a key—a counting key, a cipher of position—that she would cut into a single steel punch and send separately, or carry in her own head, or both.

It was the most delicate work she had ever attempted, and she had once cut a punch for a character so small it took a lens to proof it.

She started with the names. Names were the spine of the thing; without the names, dates and orders were noise. She wrote each name out and counted its letters, then built a line of verse around the count. *Marta Ollen* became:

> *In March the martins o'er the llen-slope fly,*

—with the nonsense syllable standing out to any compositor as a misprint, but to a reader holding the key, marking the seventh and eighth letters after the marker word *martins*, spelling the name plain. It was clumsy. It was beautiful. Each page of testimony would need a dozen such lines, and each line would need to scan as passable doggerel, because the first test of the disguise was not cleverness but boredom: the galleys had to be so dull that a tired sergeant reading them upside down by torchlight would wave them through rather than read them twice.

By the third night she had a system. Marker words—the humble, forgettable furniture of almanac verse: *when, then, sown, frost, tide*. After each marker, count forward. Take the letters. Discard the rest. The testimony assembled itself in a reader's hands like a picture rising from a field of static. She worked the way her father had taught her to work steel: patiently, cutting away everything that wasn't the truth until only the truth remained, buried where no one thought to dig.

The candle died. She lit another from the banked stove and kept working. Outside, the wind off the river found the gap in the shutters and breathed cold across her knuckles. Somewhere below, in the street, a patrol went past—their boots had a rhythm she knew now, four men and a corporal, making the midnight round—and she sat perfectly still with her hands flat on the bench until the sound faded, listening to her own heart argue with itself.

*This is how they win,* she thought. *Not with muskets. With people sitting still.*

She went back to work.

* * *

Corvel found the first finished galley two mornings later and held it at arm's length the way he held everything, as though it might be damp.

"Almanac verse," he said.

"Prognostications for the spring quarter," Felix said. "Weather rhymes. A remedy for ague."

"It reads like something I'd print to fill a slow week." He turned it over, frowned at the pencil rulings half-erased, and then—she watched it happen—saw it. His eyes stopped moving. He set the galley down on the composing stone very carefully, the way a man sets down something that might go off. "How many?"

"Forty pages of ledger. Thirty-eight galleys so far. Two more days."

"And the key?"

Felix reached under the bench and brought out the punch she had been cutting at night, after the verses, when her eyes ached. It was a single character—unremarkable, a common sort—but cut with a flaw so fine it took a lens to see: a hairline nick in the serif that marked, in the printed almanac, which words were markers. Anyone comparing the printed page against the punch could find the code. No one would ever have reason to compare them. "One copy of the punch goes to the publisher beyond the river. One stays here, wrapped in oilcloth, under the floor by the stove. If the galleys are taken and the punch survives, the truth survives. If the punch is taken and the galleys survive—" she shrugged— "the truth survives. They'd have to take both, from two places, and know what they were looking at."

Corvel was quiet a long time. In the grey light from the window his face looked older than it had in autumn, the skin looser around the jaw, the eyes retreating behind their lids. Since the bridge closed he had aged the way fruit ages in a cellar—slowly, invisibly, all at once.

"You understand what you've built," he said finally. "It's not a hiding place anymore. It's a printing block. You've made the massacre portable."

"I've made it boring," Felix said. "That's better."

Something moved through his expression that might have been pride, or might have been terror wearing pride's coat. "When do you rehearse it?"

"Rehearse?"

"A dry run. You don't set forty pages of cipher and never test whether it decodes. You'll want a clean proof, won't you? Decoded against the original?" He tapped the galley. "And you'll want to move it. A galley that never leaves the shop teaches you nothing. Take one through the streets. Walk it past the patrol. See if your boredom holds."

He was right, and she hated him a little for it, because she had been planning exactly that and had not admitted it even to herself. Taking the galleys out meant risking them. Keeping them in meant trusting them untested. There was no safe choice, only the choice that taught you something.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Market day. Everyone carries paper to market. Bills, receipts, broadsheets for the criers."

"Broadside sellers get searched," Corvel said. "Go as a delivery girl. Deliveries are invisible. Nobody interrogates a girl carrying a printer's parcel; they interrogate what she's delivering *to*." He almost smiled. "Deliver my compliments to the chandler on Tanner's Row. It'll even be true."

* * *

She slept badly and woke before light, which suited her. The shop at that hour belonged to her alone—to the smell of ink and lampblack and the tallow-and-metal scent of hot lead, to the soft industrial breathing of the presses, to the ghost of her father in every measured distance between bench and stone. She dressed warmly, bound her hair up under a plain cap, and packed the test galley—the fourth one, the one containing the account of the millpond, Marta Ollen and the woman's folded petition—between two boards of ordinary stock, tied with twine, wrapped in brown paper addressed in Corvel's hand to the chandler on Tanner's Row.

Before she left she did what she always did now: touched the case of type where the courier had died. The bloodstain had soaked into the wood and gone dark, and she had stopped trying to scrub it out weeks ago. It seemed right that it should stay. The case sat in the corner under sacking, looking like every other case in the shop, holding its forty pages of dead men's testimony the way a coffin holds what it holds.

The market was already loud when she reached it. Winter market day in Vellamont had a desperate energy since the bridge closed—prices climbing, tempers short, the fishmongers shouting about ice-blocked barges—and Felix moved through it the way her father had taught her to move through unfamiliar print shops: quickly, unremarkably, eyes down, hands occupied. A girl with a parcel. Nothing.

Tanner's Row lay on the far side of the square, which meant crossing the open ground in front of the customhouse, and the customhouse had grown teeth since the bridge closed. What had been two bored clerks in autumn was now a checkpoint in miniature: a sawhorse barrier across the row, a brazier, four soldiers, and a corporal with a list who was stopping everyone coming off the square. Felix saw him turn back a drover, saw him make a fishwife open her baskets, saw him detain a young man whose crime appeared to be having a beard and a nervous face.

She could turn around. She told herself she could turn around, walk home, try again next week—but next week the galleys would be finished and the route would be longer and colder and there would be no market crowd to hide in. If the disguise failed, she needed it to fail here, now, with thirty-seven galleys still safe on the composing stone, so she could learn why and fix it.

*A punch is a decision. Cut away everything that isn't the letter.*

She got in the queue.

The wait was the worst part. Ten minutes in the cold with nothing to do but feel the parcel's weight against her hip and compose, line by line, the harmless sentences she would say. Delivery for Corvel the printer, yes sir, chandler on Tanner's Row, yes sir, spare stock, paper for wrapping, nothing printed, sir, just stock. The soldier ahead of her was laughing at something the corporal said. The brazier smoked. Somewhere behind her a child cried, and its mother shushed it with a tenderness that made Felix's throat hurt for reasons she couldn't have explained.

Then it was her turn.

"Papers."

"No papers, sir. Delivery." She held out the parcel before he asked, which Corvel had told her was the trick—you offered, you didn't wait to be asked; a searching hand resented being summoned. "From Corvel the printer, for Master Hedley the chandler. Wrapping stock."

The soldier took it and weighed it in his hand, and Felix watched his face with the outer part of her attention while the rest of her stood very still inside herself, the way she stood when proofing fine work, when one tremor ruined a morning. He turned the parcel over. Read the direction aloud, slowly, the way men read when they want you to know they can. "Chandler. Tanner's Row." He looked at her. "You're the printer's girl?"

"His delivery girl, sir."

"Hm." He hefted it again. "Open it."

Her hands did not shake. That surprised her later, remembering—her hands went to the twine and untied the knot with the same competence they used for everything, and she peeled back the brown paper and the two boards and exposed the galley lying face-up on the ordinary stock, and the soldier bent over it, and she watched him read.

She watched him read almanac verse.

"*When frost lies long on llen-slope ground,*" he recited, frowning slightly, because it was bad verse and he knew it the way a man knows a false coin without knowing why. "*The sower stays his hand...*" He looked up. "What is this?"

"Almanac, sir. Prognostications. Master Corvel prints them every winter. For the farmers." She let a little apology into her voice. "It's not very good verse, sir. He writes it himself."

Something flickered in the soldier's face—the specific misery of a man who has caught a whiff of irregularity and cannot name it. Bad verse. Wrong somehow. But almanac verse was *supposed* to be bad; that was the whole genius of it, and the whole peril. He stared at the line a moment longer, and Felix stood in the cold and felt the entire architecture of her father's craft balanced on this one bored man's patience.

Then, from the far side of the square, a bell began ringing—the chandler's own shop bell, insistent, someone wanting service—and the corporal shouted at the queue to close up, and the soldier straightened, and handed the galley back with the parcel.

"Mind the twine's tied proper," he said. "Move along."

Felix tied the twine proper. She walked through the barrier at the pace of a girl with errands, not too fast, not too slow, and did not let her legs remember how to run until she had turned the corner onto Tanner's Row, where she leaned against the chandler's wall among the smells of tallow and smoke and shook so hard the parcel rattled against her hip.

She had passed. The verse had held. Boredom had beaten suspicion, and somewhere in the city a soldier was going about his day with a line of her cipher lodged in his head like a splinter, finding it dull, thinking no more about it.

She delivered the compliments, took the chandler's receipt, and walked home through the thinning market with her heart still hammering and something new settling into her chest—not triumph, exactly. Something quieter and harder. The feeling of a tool that fits the hand. Her father had left her steel and skill and a trade that made her invisible, and for the first time since his death she understood that all of it—all of it—was a weapon, and she had just learned to hold it.

* * *

Isandro was waiting in the doorway of the shop when she returned, and she knew before he spoke that the news was bad, because he stood the way he had stood on the ice: weight forward, eyes moving.

"Patrol came by an hour ago," he said. "Not the usual round. Six men and a sergeant, and they asked the knife-grinder across the lane who works here." He stepped inside and pushed the door shut. "They asked about a girl."

Felix set the parcel down on the composing stone, carefully, next to the thirty-seven galleys that were no longer a secret project but evidence awaiting discovery. "What did he tell them?"

"That Corvel keeps a woman at the benches. That she does the fine work." Isandro's jaw tightened. "The knife-grinder sees everything and charges nothing for it. Someone's been asking questions up the chain, Felix. Someone who knows the courier came here."

Betto. She thought it without wanting to—the apprentice with the quick eyes and the quicker tongue, who had carried proofs and swept the stone and known, as everyone in the shop had known in the way of households, that something moved through this place after dark. She put the thought away. It was too large to look at directly yet.

"The galleys can't stay here," she said. "Finished or not."

"They can't travel either. Not all together, not yet." Isandro crossed to the window and stood sideways to it, watching the street in the old soldier's way, presenting only half himself to the glass. "But they can't sit on the stone waiting for the raid either. Where's the safest place in Vellamont for thirty-eight pages of treason?"

Felix looked around the shop—at the cases, the stones, the racks of standing type, the long shelves of jobbing work and stock and half-finished commissions, the accumulated sediment of twenty years of printing. Thousands of pages. Tens of thousands. Every one of them hers to hide things inside.

"Inside more printing," she said slowly. "Scattered. A galley here in the jobbing rack. Three in the standing type. One in the pile of last year's almanac overs—" She stopped, hearing her own idea complete itself. "No single search finds them all, because no single search knows it's looking for thirty-eight. They'd have to inventory the whole shop."

"Then we scatter them tonight," Isandro said. "Before whoever's asking questions finishes asking."

He was already moving toward the racks, pulling the sacking off the courier's case, and Felix reached for the first finished galley—and that was when they both heard it: boots on the lane outside. Many boots. Unhurried. The particular unhurried rhythm of men who did not need to run, because everywhere they went, they arrived.

Isandro's hand closed on her wrist. Through the shutter gap, in the last grey light, they watched six soldiers and a sergeant stop before the Eastwick door—and the sergeant raise his hand to knock.
