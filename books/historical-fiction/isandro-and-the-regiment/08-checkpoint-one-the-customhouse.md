## 8. Checkpoint One: The Customhouse

The boots outside the Eastwick door did not belong to them.

Felix knew this only after an age of standing in the dark with her heart slamming against her ribs like a compositor's hammer, listening to the sergeant's knuckles fall twice upon the wood, and then a voice—bored, muffled—saying, "Not this one. The one with the red sign. Corvel's."

The boots moved on. The knocking began again, three doors down, and this time it was answered with screaming, and with the splinter-crack of a door that had not been asked to open.

"They're sweeping the row," Isandro breathed. His hand was still on her wrist; she could feel his pulse through his fingers, quick as a bird's. "Corvel first. They'll want the presses."

"Then we go tonight." Felix heard her own voice as if from across the river—flat, decided, belonging to someone braver than the woman saying it. "Before they come back with a warrant instead of a knock."

They worked by no light at all, which was its own kind of prayer. Felix's fingers had spent eleven years learning the geography of the shop in darkness—she could find a quoin key blind, could tell a 12-point em from her father's old long primer by the shape of its nick—and now that blindness saved her. The galleys went into a plain deal box, the box into sacking, the sacking under a bolt of mourning crepe that had hung in the loft since her mother's funeral and had never once been asked to do honest work until now.

Isandro appeared at the loft ladder holding her cloak and an expression she would learn to dread and rely on in equal measure: the look of a man doing arithmetic with other people's lives.

"The customhouse opens at seven," he said. "The widow's trick will pass—*if* nothing about you says printer. Wash your hands raw. No ink under the nails, none. They look at hands first, customs men. Hands and eyes."

"And you?" She pulled the crepe over the box and knotted it. "You'll be where?"

"Behind you." He said it simply, the way he said most things, as though courage were merely the sensible course. "A widow travels with company or she invites questions. I'm your late husband's cousin, come to see the goods shipped. Grief makes strange families; nobody questions grief."

Outside, down the lane, Corvel's door stood open and light poured out of it onto the cobbles like something spilled. Felix looked once—only once—at the silhouette of the master printer standing between two soldiers with his coat over his nightshirt and his hands, those beautiful ruined hands, held out from his sides in a gesture that was either surrender or benediction. Then Isandro drew the curtain, and the image was gone, and she carried it anyway, and would carry it for years.

* * *

Isandro had crossed at the ferry stairs perhaps four hundred times in his life, and had never once done it feeling like a man walking to an altar.

The ice had taken the river's voice away. That was the worst of this season—not the cold itself but the silence underneath it, the river muzzled beneath grey glass, so that Vellamont seemed a city holding its breath. He led Felix along the bank road with the sacking-wrapped box riding his shoulder like a sleeping child, and every crunch of their boots on frozen mud sounded to him loud enough to wake the garrison.

She walked half a step behind him, as a widow would, and wore her grief like the black veil it was. But he had watched her put the disguise on in the loft, and what struck him was not the costume but the ease of it. She had pinned the veil without a mirror. She had wound the wedding band—her father's, turned on a lathe to fit a woman's finger—without hesitation, and when he'd asked, gently, whose ring it was, she had said only, "It's a working ring now," and he had understood that Felix Eastwick did not borrow grief. She converted it. It was the same thing she did with lead.

*Don't think about her hands,* he told himself, and thought about them anyway, and made himself stop, because ahead of them the customhouse rose out of the river fog like a fact neither of them could argue with.

It had been a granary once, before the war taxes; its great doors were tall enough to admit carts, and above them hung the magistrate's arms—Halden's, newly carved, the paint still bright against two hundred years of stone. Lanterns burned on either side of the entrance even at midmorning, because checkpoints wanted to be seen. Two sentries flanked the doors with muskets grounded. Through the open doorway Isandro could see the interior as a warm amber gloom: the long counter, the clerks behind it, the weighing beams hanging from the roof beams like gallows in miniature.

"There's a queue forming," Felix murmured. "Good. Crowds are cover."

"Crowds are witnesses," Isandro said. "Both useful. Remember—if they separate us, you're Mistress Verley, shipping your husband's things to her sister in Grenouille. You've never set type in your life. Your hands ache from needlework."

"My hands ache from hauling type," she said, but she flexed them, and when she raised them into view they were red and chapped and innocent as any washerwoman's. She was good at this. That frightened him more than the soldiers did.

They joined the queue behind a fishwife and a carter and a man leading a goat, which alone, Isandro thought, should occupy any clerk for an hour. The line shuffled forward at the pace of all official misery. Inside, someone was reading manifests aloud in a voice like wet paper. The smell was tallow, tar, and the river's dead cold breath.

Then the line moved them through the doors, and the lantern light took them, and Isandro felt the change come over the room the way one feels weather before it arrives—a stillness, a turning of heads, a sergeant near the far wall lifting his chin toward the woman in the black veil because women in deep mourning traveling alone were either rich or interesting, and both kinds were worth looking at.

Felix gave her name in a low voice, cracked just slightly, perfectly. *Verley. Goods for shipment, household effects, my late husband's.* The clerk—a young man with ink-stained cuffs, and Isandro noted the irony the way one notes a stone in a shoe—took up her manifest and read it without interest.

It was the wrapping that killed them. Isandro saw it before anyone spoke: the brown paper she had folded around the box, decent, anonymous, except that when the clerk turned the parcel to weigh it, the paper caught the lamplight along its fold, and there, faint as a watermark ought to be, ghosted the maker's mark of the Vellamont paperworks—the little bridge within a circle—and below it, smaller still, the private sign Corvel had bought exclusive: a kestrel in flight.

Corvel's paper. Corvel's watermark. In the hands of a grieving widow who had never in her life needed to wrap anything so fine.

The clerk stopped reading. He turned the parcel again. Slowly, deliberately, he tilted it toward the light, and Isandro watched the kestrel swim up out of the sheet like a fish rising, and watched the clerk's eyes widen by a fraction—an amount a stranger would miss, an amount Isandro had been trained by four years in the regiment to read at forty paces.

"Mistress Verley," the clerk said, and his voice had shed its boredom the way a snake sheds skin. "Fine paper for funeral goods."

"It was his best," Felix said, and let one tear arrive, precisely, like a professional. "He was particular."

"I'm sure he was." The clerk did not smile. He raised one hand—not high, not dramatic—just two fingers, the smallest possible summons, and by the far wall the sergeant began to walk toward them unhurriedly, with the particular unhurried rhythm of men who arrived everywhere eventually.

* * *

Later, Felix would try to reconstruct what Isandro did in the space between the clerk's two fingers and the sergeant's fifth step, and she never quite managed it, because she had been looking at the kestrel in the paper and seeing her own hanging.

What she caught was this: a crash, a bleat, a cartwheel shrieking, and suddenly the whole amber room was full of goat.

The animal came through the doorway as though fired from a mortar, rope trailing, eyes rolling white, and behind it came Isandro with his hat off and his face a masterpiece of apology, shouting that the beast had broken loose, that he was terribly sorry, that it had gone for him first—this last delivered while visibly limping, one arm cradled—and the goat, possessed by whatever demon inhabits all goats at the moments least convenient, chose that crowded room, that counter, those weighing beams, as its personal Golgotha. Scales went over. A clerk went into the arms of another clerk. The manifest book took a hoof directly through the V's.

And the sergeant stopped. Turned. Did what sergeants do, which is manage chaos, because managing chaos is the whole of their education.

"Out!" he roared at Isandro. "Out, you clumsy—get that devil of yours—"

"He's not mine, sir, I was only—" Isandro stumbled into the goat, the goat screamed, a lantern swung wild, and for eleven seconds—it would be eleven seconds, Felix counted them the way prisoners count—the entire attention of the Vellamont customhouse belonged to a barnyard war.

Felix's hands moved without consulting her. The parcel went back into her sack. From her sleeve—her left sleeve, sewn there yesterday by lamplight for exactly this purpose—came a second wrapping, plain cheap grocer's paper bought that morning from a chandler who asked nothing, and her fingers, those traitor clever fingers, folded and tied and tucked in the time it took the goat to overturn one more scale. When the sergeant finally ejected Isandro and the goat together into the street, and turned back flushed and furious to his interrupted suspicion, the woman at the counter stood in her black veil with her red hands folded, presenting for inspection a parcel wrapped in the humblest paper in the city, tied with hemp string, smelling faintly of tallow candles.

The clerk looked at it. He looked at her. The kestrel was gone into her sleeve like a sin confessed and absolved.

"Contents," he said shortly, and she recited them—winding sheet, mourning gloves, a psalter, small clothes, black-edged stationery—in a voice gone dull with rehearsed sorrow, and he cut the string, and lifted the paper, and found beneath it the bolt of mourning crepe, and unrolled a yard of it with the contempt of a man denied his drama, and found beneath *that* the deal box, and opened the box, and found—

Felix's heart stopped, and started, and stopped.

—type. Loose metal type. A case of sorts, mixed faces, some worn, worth perhaps six sous to any dealer in the city. Her father's oldest case, the one with the cracked sides she had emptied and filled with junk lead that morning precisely so that something honest and worthless would be found if anything was found at all.

The clerk stared at the little metal letters. A widow, shipping her dead husband's printing equipment—no. A widow shipping her dead husband's things, among them a box of old type no one had thought to leave out of the lot. Grief made strange families; grief also made strange inventories. He closed the lid.

"Type," he said, disgusted. "Half these houses are full of it, since the pamphlet fever started. Everyone fancies themselves a publisher till the magistrates explain otherwise." He waved her through with the manifest stamped, his eyes already on the goat-keeper filling out a damage claim in a hand that shook with rage. "Next."

Felix walked out of the Vellamont customhouse into the white day with her face composed and her legs made of water, and did not trust them to carry her past the corner until the building was out of sight.

* * *

She found him two streets on, sitting on a bollard by the ferry stairs with his hat crushed and blood coming from his nose in a thin, businesslike line, and his lip split, and one eye beginning to swell shut in a rainbow of colors yet to arrive. The goat was gone. The sergeant's parting gift, he explained later, had been administered in the alley beside the customhouse, thoroughly and with commentary.

"You're bleeding," Felix said, which was the stupidest sentence she had ever spoken, and her voice broke on it, which redeemed it somewhat.

"It matches the manifest." He tried to grin and winced. "Did it pass? Tell me it passed."

"It passed." She sat down beside him on the bollard, right there in the open street, propriety be damned, and pulled off her glove, and pressed her bare hand to his cheek where it wasn't swollen, because her hands needed to do something and that was the thing they chose. His skin was cold from the river wind and hot from the beating, both at once, and under her palm he went very still, the way a startled animal goes still.

"Felix—"

"You stood in front of a sergeant and a goat," she said, "for a box of junk lead."

"For your box," Isandro said. "Junk was the point."

She laughed—one short, wet sound that was mostly not laughter at all—and the tear that had come so professionally inside the customhouse came now for real, tracking down her cheek and off her jaw onto his sleeve, and he did not wipe it away and did not look away, and the ferry bell rang somewhere behind them, counting the hour, indifferent.

"Thank you," she said. It seemed too small. Every language she knew, including the language of type, lacked the word for what she meant: that he had put his body between her and the magistrate's machine, and had done it grinning, and that she had watched him do it and understood, with a terror braided so tightly through her gratitude she could not tell the strands apart, that next time the price might be higher than a broken nose, and that there would be a next time, because they had two checkpoints still to pass and Halden's men had Corvel now, and Corvel knew her face.

"Save it," Isandro said softly. He covered her hand with his own, there on his bruised cheek, and held it a moment longer than the joke required. "Spend it at the toll gate. We'll need it."

He stood, swaying once, and she steadied him with a hand at his elbow, and they went on north together along the frozen road, the widow and her cousin, the box between them.

Neither of them saw, back at the customhouse door, the small figure watching them go—the apprentice with the rat's face and the new coin in his pocket, Betto, who had that very morning learned what questions earned, and who was even now memorizing the direction of their road, and the color of the widow's coat, and the limp of the man beside her, for the men who paid in clemency rather than coin liked their information fresh.

The toll gate lay two days ahead. Betto intended to arrive first.
