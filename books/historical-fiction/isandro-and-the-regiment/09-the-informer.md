## 9. The Informer

Corvel had always believed that a man's courage could be measured in the small hours, when the shop was dark and the presses slept and no one watched him be brave or fail to be. It was a comfortable theory. He had built a life on it the way he built a book: carefully, margin by margin, with the ugly things caught in the binding where no reader would think to look.

He was measuring his courage now, at three in the morning, and finding it shorter than he'd hoped.

The knock came the way winter comes—not suddenly, but with a certainty that made everything before it seem like a dream of warmth. Three blows of a fist on the street door, spaced like a man counting. Corvel lay in the loft above the shop with his eyes open and his heart going like a boy at a treadle, and he listened to the city hold its breath around him.

They were back. He had known they would come back.

Below him lay forty years of evidence. The press his father had bought with money earned printing sermons. The chases locked with type that had, three nights out of every ten, set not almanacs and invoices but the truth—the names Halden wanted forgotten, the villages emptied, the winter the bridge guards shot the ice-fishers at Marren's Ford and called it riot control. Corvel had printed it all in runs of two hundred, inked at midnight, dried behind the false wall where his late wife had once kept her preserves. He had told himself for eleven years that this made him a brave man. A printer who dared. A keeper of the flame.

Now, listening to boots on his stairs, he understood that he had merely been a coward with excellent timing.

*Betto*, he thought. *God forgive me, Betto.*

* * *

The boy had come to him at fourteen, all wrists and hunger, orphaned by the same purges whose names Corvel printed. There had been something fitting in that—Corvel had never been able to resist a symmetry—and he had taken the apprentice without wages for the first year, teaching him to distribute type, to mind the friskets, to keep the ink rollers from gumming in cold weather. Betto learned fast. Betto learned *everything* fast, which should have warned Corvel, because the boy also learned where the false wall was, and which case held the seditious sorts, and on which nights the master worked alone with the shutters down.

Corvel had told himself the boy was loyal. He had told himself it so many times that the telling wore smooth as a well-handled quoit, and he never examined the hollow underneath: that he had never once tested it. That he had left Betto alive in the middle of his secret the way a man leaves a lamp near gunpowder—because moving it would require admitting what he kept in the room.

The knocking stopped. The door did not break; it opened. They had a key, then, or Betto had given them one, or the magistrate's men simply carried the confidence of men who have already won. Corvel heard them come into the shop below, four sets of boots at least, and a fifth pair that dragged—a little—heavier than the rest.

He went down to meet them because the alternative was to be found in the loft, and a man found hiding has already confessed.

* * *

The one in charge was called Voss; Corvel knew him by sight and reputation, a captain of Halden's constabulary with a face like a closed ledger and a voice that never rose above the volume of a man reading accounts. He stood in the middle of the workshop turning a guttering lamp so its light moved across the presses, the stone, the racks of galleys, cataloguing everything without touching anything.

"Master Corvel." He said the name the way you read a title off a spine. "You're up late."

"I sleep badly in winter."

"Yes," Voss said. "I imagine you do."

Two soldiers began at the far wall, working toward the false one, and Corvel felt the whole architecture of his life tilt toward collapse with each unhurried step they took. He thought absurdly of his father, of the sermon the old man had printed on the subject of Jonah—that we are swallowed not by the whale but by our own delay. He thought of Felix Eastwick, and here his thoughts snagged and tore.

She had been in this room nine days ago, her hands black to the wrist, arguing with him about kerning with that ferocious precision her father had bred into her. She had seen the broadsides. She had taken a proof sheet he was too weak to stop her taking, and she had gone out into a city that was tightening around all of them like a screw in a vice, and she had said nothing, and he had let himself believe that silence was safety.

And there was the other matter. The courier. The dead man in the alley behind the corn exchange with his hand hooked toward the print shop like a compass needle. Corvel had not seen him die—but Voss's men had been asking about a stranger on the ice for a week now, and asking about the ferry stairs, and Isandro the ferryman's son had been seen carrying something heavy toward Eastwick Lane. The city talked. Cities always talked; it was why Corvel had chosen one with a river through it, believing water would dilute rumor the way it diluted ink.

"Your boy," Voss said, and gestured, and the soldiers brought Betto forward from the doorway where he'd been standing.

That was the worst of it—not the accusation but the sight of the boy himself. Betto's lip was split. His shirt was wrong, buttoned crooked over a chest that had been introduced to something harder than a fist, and his eyes had the particular shine Corvel had seen only twice before in his life: in a man about to betray his brother, and in a woman about to drown herself. The shine of someone who has already crossed the line and is now only walking away from it as fast as he can.

"He came to us," Voss said mildly. "This afternoon. Volunteered quite a lot. But he's an apprentice, you understand—nervous, uncertain of his facts. He says—" Voss took a folded paper from his coat but did not open it, holding it instead like a card a gambler declines to show— "he says you print sedition. He says there's a wall that isn't a wall. He says you've been running Halden's name through your press for eleven years." He smiled, faintly, at the arithmetic. "A boy like that can't possibly know all this. So I've come to ask the man who does."

Betto would not look at him. That was how Corvel knew the boy had not invented any of it under duress; duress at least allows a man the mercy of meeting his victim's eyes afterward and hating himself. Betto had volunteered. Betto had walked to the customhouse in daylight and sold the only person who had ever fed him, and now he stood in the ruin of it with split lips and shining eyes, and some cold, clear, unforgivable part of Corvel understood exactly why. Because Corvel had spent eleven years doing precisely the same thing in slower increments—selling the dead of Marren's Ford for the pleasure of calling himself brave in the dark.

"Captain Voss," Corvel said, and was surprised by the steadiness of his own voice. "You've had a wasted walk."

* * *

They searched the shop while they waited for him to elaborate, and he elaborated by saying nothing, and the silence between them filled with the sounds of destruction conducted politely. Drawers pulled. Cases tilted. One soldier ran his hand along the false wall and stopped, and looked back at Voss, and knocked, and the panel swung, and eleven years of drying paper came into the lamplight like moths.

Voss leafed through the broadsides. He did not appear angry. Anger, Corvel realized, was for people who still had something at stake.

"This one," Voss said, holding up a sheet. *THE TRUTH OF MARREN'S FORD.* "My cousin's regiment was at Marren's Ford." He set it down carefully, squared its edges with the others, and Corvel understood that this was worse than rage. "Now. The Eastwick girl."

The words went into Corvel like a punch going into soft lead—clean, deep, permanent.

"I don't know what you mean."

"A week ago," Voss said, "a man died in the alley behind your premises. Before he died he was seen at the ferry stairs, in company with the Vasquez boy, and afterward the boy was seen on Eastwick Lane, where the Eastwick girl keeps her father's bench and her father's silence. My informant—" and here Voss glanced at Betto, who flinched like a struck horse— "tells me she has been in your shop since. Tells me she saw your work. Tell me, Master Corvel, does the girl know? Does she carry anything?"

And there it was: the fork in the road, except Corvel had been walking down one tine of it for eleven years and had somehow convinced himself the ground was level.

He could give her to them. It would take four sentences. She had found the ledger, yes; she meant to move it; the ferryman's son was guiding her; they would try the checkpoints before the amnesty. Four sentences and he bought himself a cell with bread in it instead of a rope at the gate, and perhaps even something better than that—Halden rewarded informers; everyone knew the shape of those bargains; clemency, position, a quiet license to go on printing sermons and almanacs until his hands gave out. Forty years of craft. His father's press. All of it preserved by spending one girl who had done nothing in this world but cut beautiful letters and bury a father and keep, God help her, choosing the side Corvel had only ever pretended to choose.

He thought: *she is twenty-three years old.*

He thought: *her father cut punches for me when my father's press broke, and never sent a bill.*

He thought, most cowardly of all, most humanly: *if I say her name aloud, I will hear myself say it for the rest of my life, and the rest of my life will be long, because that is exactly what the saying purchases.*

"The girl knows nothing," Corvel said.

Voss waited. It was a professional patience, the patience of a man who has watched many people decide, and Corvel felt the full weight of how familiar his hesitation must look—how many mouths had hung open at this exact moment in this exact quality of silence before closing around somebody's name.

"She knows my sins," Corvel went on, and heard his own voice change, roughen, take on grain like unseasoned wood. "She swept this floor as a child. If she saw a proof sheet, she saw a dead man's argument, and she'll carry it nowhere, because she has more sense than the fools who write them." He met Voss's eyes. "I printed it all. Every sheet behind that wall. No one helped me. I wrote to no one. I am, if you want the truth of it, a vain old man who liked being feared a little more than he liked being safe. Hang your conspiracy on me. It will fit. I tailored it myself."

It was, he knew, a bad lie. It was thin as a proof sheet and just as transparent, and Voss must have seen through it before the second sentence ended. But something happened in the room anyway—something Corvel had not anticipated and could not have engineered, though he would spend his remaining years convinced it was the only genuinely free act of his life. Betto looked up.

The boy looked at him across the ruined shop, past the soldiers, past the spilled sorts glinting like grey seed, and his shining eyes lost their shine. What replaced it was older and heavier: the beginning of understanding, arriving years too late, that courage is not a thing you have but a thing you do, once, at cost, usually wrongly timed—and that his master, this soft complicit man he had judged and sold, had just done it.

"Take him," Voss said, bored again. "Both of them."

* * *

They took Corvel first. In the doorway he turned, and Voss permitted it the way a man permits a dog one last look at its yard, and Corvel used the look not on his press nor his father's wall of hidden paper but on the composing stone, bare since the raid began, and on the rack of galleys above it—and he prayed, with a specificity he had not managed since childhood, that whatever Felix Eastwick was building somewhere in this city would sit in plain view when these men came for her, and pass their eyes unread.

Then he looked at Betto, one long moment, and did not curse him. That, too, was an act, and it cost more than the lie had.

The cell they put him in smelled of wet straw and other men's winters, and it was cold enough that Corvel could see his breath, and he sat against the wall with his ruined hands in his sleeves and began, methodically, the way he had been taught to begin everything, to compose.

Not type. There was no type here. But a compositor's mind works in columns whether the hands have metal or not, and so he set the page of what he owed: to Marren's Ford, line by line; to the ice-fishers, justified flush both margins; to Ambrose Eastwick, who had died owing Corvel nothing and Corvel owing him a debt compounded across fifteen winters; to the girl with ink in her knuckle-creases who had inherited a bench, a craft, and now, through no wish of hers, a war.

There was a way out of this cell. Not for his body—for his testimony. A man who had authenticated Halden's orders for eleven years, who knew the watermark, the ink, the very punches of the clerk's hand, could authenticate them again, publicly, before the world. His confession was worth more than his freedom; it might even, in the merciless arithmetic of such things, be worth more than the girl's safety, if he could get it out of this cell and into her ink-stained hands.

Footsteps in the corridor. Keys. His cellmate, a thief with kind eyes and a psalm always half-muttered, rolled over and whispered, "They'll question you at dawn."

"Yes," Corvel said, and for the first time in eleven years, in the dark, where he had always measured his courage and always found it short, he felt it lengthen. "Then we have until dawn to learn a psalm by heart—one with room in it for a great many names."

Above the prison, beyond the walls, the river was freezing solid, and somewhere on its far bank a press waited, and somewhere in the city a girl was running out of time.
