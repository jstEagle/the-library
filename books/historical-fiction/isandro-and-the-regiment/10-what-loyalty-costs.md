## 10. What Loyalty Costs

The river had begun to speak in the voice of winter—not the loose, garrulous sound of moving water but a low groan beneath the ice, like something turning in its sleep. Isandro heard it as he came up the ferry stairs at dusk, his palms raw from the day's hauling, and he stood a moment on the third step from the top because the third step was where you could hear it best, and because standing there was the nearest thing to prayer he had left.

Then he heard the other sound: Livia, in the room above, moving furniture.

She only moved furniture when she was frightened. When she was angry she threw things; when she was sad she went quiet as a held breath; when she was frightened she rearranged the world—dragged the table from the wall and back again, turned her chair toward the door, shifted the crockery so that every cup faced handle-out, as if readiness were a thing you could achieve by geometry.

He climbed the rest of the stairs and opened the door on a room that had been rebuilt twice since morning.

"You're late," she said without looking up. She was on her knees at the cupboard, sorting nothing. Her hands were red to the wrist from the wash-house, cracked along the knuckles where the lye got in, and she kept them moving over the shelves as if they had their own errands and she was only following.

"The ice is taking longer to set below the weir. Men want crossing anyway."

"Men are fools."

"Some of them pay."

That made her stop. She sat back on her heels, and in the lamplight he saw how thin she'd gotten this winter, the fine bones of her face coming up through her skin like stones through a thawing field. Seventeen years old and already she had the look of a woman who tallies everything—the coal, the bread, the hours, the debts—and finds the sum short no matter how she carries it.

"Sit down," she said.

"I smell like the river."

"Sit *down*, Isandro."

He sat. She took the chair opposite, turned it toward him, and folded her cracked hands in her lap with a formality that frightened him more than shouting would have done. On the table between them sat a twist of paper he had not noticed before, small, tied with string, grease-spotted at one corner. He knew its shape. He knew what lived inside shapes like that: sugared almonds, when there was money; boiled sweets, when there wasn't.

"Livia—"

"There's a man," she said. "Comes to the wash-house. Not to wash anything—he comes to talk to the forewoman, and the forewoman talks to us, and we're meant to listen." She said it flatly, the way you report weather. "He stands near the copper where I work. He has soft boots. He asked me did my brother still run the ferry stairs, and did he ever carry parcels for gentlemen, and wouldn't it be a fine thing if a young man with no trade found favor with Magistrate Halden. There's amnesty in it, Isandro. Clemency. A clean slate for anything—*anything*—a man might have done, or be about to do. All it costs is names."

The stove ticked. Under the floor, faint as a pulse, the river groaned.

"How long has he been asking?"

"A week." Her chin came up. "I didn't tell you because I thought if I arranged the room right, and kept my mouth shut, and washed their filthy linens until my hands bled, they'd see I was useless and go away. But today he gave me this." She touched the paper twist without picking it up. "Sweets. For a good girl. And he said—" and here, for the first time, her voice broke, not into tears but into fury, which was worse, "he said, *tell your brother the ledger is worth more than his life, and Halden pays better.*"

The room went very still. Somewhere across the city a bell rang the hour, muffled by snow.

"They know," Isandro said. It came out steadier than he felt. "About the ledger."

"They've known for days. Maybe weeks. Maybe since the night that poor man died in the printer's shop." Livia leaned forward. "Isandro. Brother. Listen to me. Give it back."

"It isn't mine to give."

"Then give back whatever piece of it you carry. Tell them where it went. Tell them anything—they'll take anything, he *said* they'd take anything—" She was on her feet now, pacing the little room, stepping around the furniture she had spent the afternoon arranging, undoing her own work without noticing. "You think I don't know what you've been doing? You think I don't count the coins you bring home? Ferry men don't pay in silver like that, not in December, not across ice. I've known since the customhouse. Since you came home with your ribs taped and told me you fell on the stairs. Nobody falls on stairs in a way that turns their back the color of a storm cloud."

"It healed."

"It's *still healing*, I can see it when you reach for the lamp—" She stopped herself. Drew breath. When she spoke again it was low, and careful, and that carefulness was the most frightening thing in the whole cold room. "I will tell you what I told the man with the soft boots. I said I'd consider it. That's what he wanted, a maybe. A door left open. So I have until Friday, and then he comes back, and either I give him something or he decides I'm protecting you, and then we're both of interest, Isandro. Both of us. The wash-house, the stairs, the room, all of it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? They won't even have to catch you. They'll just come."

He looked at his sister—at the red ruined hands folded now into fists, at the stubborn line of her jaw that was their mother's jaw, at the sweets she hadn't eaten because eating them would be the first step down a road she could already see the end of—and he understood that he loved her more than he loved the truth, and that this was exactly the test, and that he was going to fail it.

"I'll handle it," he said.

"No. You'll *say* that. Saying it is free. I want to hear the actual words. What will you do?"

"Friday will come and go and no one will trouble us. I promise you."

It was the first time in his life he had lied to her face. He watched it land—watched her search him the way you search ice before trusting it to bear weight, probing for the hollow sound, the dark seam under the crust—and he kept his eyes level and let her find nothing, because the truth was a thing he could not hand her without watching it crush her, and the lie at least would hold for a while. A few days. Long enough.

Livia sat back down slowly. Whatever she read in his face—or chose to read—she folded away somewhere deep, and when she spoke her voice had gone light and terrible.

"Sugared almonds," she said, nudging the paper twist toward him. "From a magistrate's man. We can't eat them and we can't throw them out because he'll look for the wrapper. So they sit there. That's what it's like, Isandro. Everything they touch just sits in the house getting sweeter and more rotten."

She pushed back her chair and went to the window, arms crossed, watching the snow come down over the roofs toward the river, and Isandro sat in the lamplight with his lie between his teeth like a coin bitten to test it, and hated the taste.

* * *

He did not sleep. Around midnight he dressed in the dark and went out, and walked the frozen towpath north past the tanneries, because walking was the only way he knew to argue with himself, and because there was a place he went when the argument turned on the regiment.

There was a stretch of the old canal, three miles out, where the bank stepped down in wide shallow terraces, and two summers ago a crowd had stood on those terraces—mill workers, mostly, and their wives, and a scatter of boys too young to have opinions worth a bayonet. The mill owners had begged the garrison for troops. The troops had come at dawn. And Lieutenant Isandro Ferro, twenty-two years old, three years served, commended twice, had stood in line with thirty men on the topmost terrace while a captain whose name he had made himself forget read the Riot Act into a wind that carried it nowhere.

They didn't disperse. That was the whole of it. The crowd didn't disperse, and the captain's order came down the line like something dropped from a height, and Isandro stood with his musket shouldered and looked at the faces on the terrace below—a woman with flour on her apron, an old man holding his cap, boys with their fists up who had never in their lives been afraid of soldiers because soldiers were just poor men dressed by the state—and he heard his own voice say, quietly, so that only the men nearest heard it:

"No."

Not a speech. Not a refusal in the grand manner. Just the word, spoken once, the way you'd say *no* to a child reaching for a knife. And then Sergeant Bray, beside him, gray-bearded Bray who had drilled him as a recruit, had turned his head half an inch and said, without moving his lips, *"Shoulder arms, boy, or I'll have you broken,"* and Isandro had said, "Break me," and Bray had looked at him a long moment—and then, in front of God and the line, Bray had grounded his own musket and stood at ease, and after him four more men, and after them eleven, and the order died on that terrace not with a mutiny but with a slow soft collapse, thirty-one muskets pointed at the frost-rimed grass while the crowd below watched and did not understand what they were being spared.

They broke him, as promised. Court of inquiry, quiet, fast, the record sealed—Halden's office handled such things even then, though Isandro hadn't known the magistrate's name meant anything yet. Conduct unbecoming. Dismissed without pension. The captain got a commendation for restraint; the mill owners got their strike broken a month later by hired men with cudgels, and two of the boys from the terrace went home that night with skulls the surgeons couldn't mend.

Bray got transferred north. Before he went he found Isandro at the gate of the barracks yard, where the discharged man stood with his kit in one canvas bag, everything he owned in the world lighter than a sack of grain.

"You know what you cost those men who followed you?" Bray had said. No anger in it. Something worse: arithmetic. "Four of them had pensions due within the year. Eleven more had families."

"And what did firing cost the people on the terrace?"

Bray had looked at him the way you look at a wall you have no ladder for. "Same thing it always costs," he said. "Nothing, for us. Everything, for them." And then, at the gate, pausing: "You'll spend your life paying for that word, boy. *No*. Make sure you spend it well."

Isandro stood on the canal terraces now, three winters later, snow gathering on his shoulders, and tested the old coin of his refusal against the new one burning in his pocket. *No* to the captain. *No*—or its twin, silence—to Livia. Both lies in their way. Both refusals that passed their cost downward onto someone smaller.

Because here was the truth he could not say at their table: he could not stop. Not because the ledger was precious—it was, it was the only true account of what Halden had done at Marrowfield and the quarry camps, forty names written in a dead courier's careful hand—but because stopping meant handing the whole weight of it to Felix Eastwick, alone, a woman the magistrate already had a warrant shaped for. Because Corvel was in a cell and Betto's silence was bought and Livia was the last unguarded door, and every door he closed put another body between the soldiers and the pages. That was the arithmetic Bray had taught him whether he meant to or not: somebody always pays, and the only choice a man ever really gets is whose name goes on the bill.

His? He'd pay it gladly. Livia's—never. Never, never, never.

And yet he had lied to her face with sugared almonds sitting on the table between them, and by Friday the man with the soft boots would come back, and Livia would have to choose between betraying her brother and being destroyed alongside him, and Isandro had taken even that choice away from her, because he hadn't trusted her to make it.

Snow thickened. Downriver, far off, the fort lanterns burned in a ragged line along the palisade, and among them, he knew, men were sharpening the winter's boredom on travelers' papers. Three days to New Year's. One checkpoint left.

When he turned for home he saw, in the white distance, a figure already ahead of him on the towpath—small, quick, familiar in the way that drops the stomach before the mind catches up. Livia, out in the storm at midnight, walking fast toward the city with her shawl over her head.

Going, he understood with a certainty that arrived whole and cold as the ice underfoot, to the wash-house. To the man with the soft boots. To make her own bargain before Friday, before he could stop her—his sister, loyal as blood, going to sell something to save him.

Isandro ran.
