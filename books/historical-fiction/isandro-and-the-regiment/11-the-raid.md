## 11. The Raid

The knock came at half past four in the morning, and it was not a knock so much as a verdict.

Felix had fallen asleep at her father's bench again, cheek against the worn maple where a decade of his leaning had polished the wood to the color of dark honey. She woke with his ghost in her nostrils—lamp oil, lead, the vinegary breath of the etching acid—and for one merciful second she was a girl of eleven who had drifted off waiting for him to come up from the pressroom, and the banging downstairs was only Corvel locking the street door.

Then the second blow split the doorframe and the whole shop shuddered, and she understood.

She was moving before she had finished understanding. That was the strange mercy of terror: it did not wait for thought. Her hands went to the composing stone where the almanac galleys lay in their neat rows—the false verses, the harmless winter proverbs, each line stitched through with the dead courier's testimony like a bone sewn inside a goose's carcass. There was no time to hide them. There was no place left to hide anything; they had hidden everything already, in plain sight, in the only vault her father had ever taught her to trust: the blindness of men who could not read what was set before them.

So she did the most difficult thing she had ever done. She left them where they were.

She pulled the apron off over her head, wadded it into the coal scuttle, struck a spill, and lit the lamp on the stairs as if she had been up all night working—which was true, and true things read better than invented ones—and went down just as the door gave.

They came in like water finding a breach. Six soldiers first, then a sergeant with a paper he did not offer to let her hold, then two men in plain coats whose boots were too good for their coats and whose eyes were too quick for their boredom. And behind them, hands cuffed, face gray as unprinted stock—

Corvel.

His collar had been torn open. There was blood at the corner of his mouth, dried to the color of rusted type metal, and when his eyes found hers across the wrecked threshold they did not plead. They calculated. She saw him take inventory of her: unwounded, upright, awake. She saw him decide, in that half-second, whether to say her name.

He said nothing. He looked away, at the floor, like a man reading something long and sad.

"Felicia Eastwick?" The plain coat nearest her had a voice like a file drawn across soft metal. He did not wait for her nod. "Master Corvel's journeywoman."

"Typesetter," she said. "Cutter." Let them chew on the word *cutter*. Let them think of knives and be careful.

"Where is your master's warrant drawer?"

"In the back room. Third case from the wall, under the window that sticks." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "It sticks because it sticks. I don't have the key to it. He does." She looked at Corvel. "Had."

*Had.* The word landed between them like a sort dropped from the stick. Corvel flinched. The plain coat wrote something down.

Then the ransacking began in earnest, and there was nothing left in Felix Eastwick but her eyes.

* * *

You would think, she thought later, that they would go for the presses first. But soldiers do not understand presses; a press is only a machine until someone shows you what it can make, and these men had been sent for paper, not principles. They went for paper.

They pulled the drying lines down and trampled the damp broadsheets into the stone floor, bootprints stamped across half-finished sentences as though the words themselves were suspects to be subdued. They emptied the paper cupboard onto the counter and ran their thumbs along every ream, holding sheets to the lamp hunting for watermarks. One found the stack of Corvel's good rag stock—the stock with his private watermark woven into it, the lily-and-anchor that had already nearly killed her at the customhouse—and held it up with the light behind it like a priest elevating the host.

"Marked," he said, satisfied, and the plain coat took the ream and set it apart, and Felix felt the noose draw one loop tighter around a neck that was not yet hers but might be by noon.

They went through the type cases with less care than she used for washing them. Cases that had taken her father months to fill—his three-line pica, his small pearls, the long primer with which he had set her mother's burial notice with his own hands—they dragged off the racks and overturned into the sorting trays, scrambling the distribution of a lifetime into chaos. A man cannot cut type faster than fools can scatter it. She heard a case hit the floor and its contents go everywhere at once, ten thousand pieces of lead and antimony and tin skating across boards, each letter a small cold syllable of her father's patience, and she made a sound she did not authorize, low in her throat, and the soldier who had done it grinned at her.

"Nothing breaks," he said, picking up a handful of sorts and letting them run through his fingers. "It's only letters."

*Only letters.* She would remember his face. She was a typesetter; she kept a good register.

Upstairs, they found her father's bench. She heard them before she saw it—the scrape of the tool drawer, the clatter of her gravers and scorpers being dumped out—and when she followed them up she stood in the doorway and watched a corporal sift through forty years of her father's craft, holding punches to the lamp one after another, reading the backwards letters stamped in steel. *A. B. R.* The capital S her father had cut the year she was born, the one he'd said had cost him two weeks and one bad eye. The italic *f* he had made to celebrate her christening, because Felix began with *f*, and he was a sentimental man beneath the steel dust.

The corporal tossed them back into the drawer by the fistful. Steel rang against steel. It sounded like a bell rung wrong.

"That one's worth more than your horse," she heard herself say.

The corporal looked up. For a moment his face held genuine confusion—a man confronted with a claim he had no instrument to evaluate. Then he shrugged and threw the punch he was holding—the lowercase *e*, the commonest letter in the language, the one her father had cut eleven times before he was satisfied—onto the pile, and moved on to the cabinets.

Felix stood very still among the violated tools and did her arithmetic. Every punch on that bench was a week of her father's life made permanent. They could not destroy them—not really; steel survives soldiers—but they could dishonor them, and dishonor, she was discovering, cuts deeper than destruction. A thing destroyed can be mourned. A thing scattered must be gathered again, letter by letter, by the person who loved it, and the gathering is grief with labor added.

She would gather them. She filed the promise where she filed everything now, in the case behind her breastbone, in the compartment reserved for debts.

* * *

Downstairs, the plain coats had found the composing stone.

Felix had positioned herself by the staircase, close enough to see, far enough not to seem to care, and when the taller of the two leaned over the galleys her heart performed a maneuver she felt in her wrists. There they sat. Twelve galleys of "almanac verse," locked up tight with furniture and quoins, exactly as a careful journeywoman would leave them overnight. Winter proverbs. Frost dates for the Vellamont valley. A morsel of verse about Saint Swithin she had composed herself at two in the morning, her heart hammering, while Isandro's testimony—*fourteen men shot at the mill on the magistrate's own order, I carried two of them to the water and they died asking me to write their names*—lay encoded in the very spacing of it, in the choice of this word over that, in the count of letters per line that any compositor trained in her father's method could expand back into the ledger's full confession.

All it required was the key. All the key required was her head staying attached to her shoulders and outside a cell.

The tall plain coat read the top galley aloud. "'When the ice bears, walk wide; when the mill turns thin, the river's lied.'" He snorted. "Poetry."

"The *Vellamont Almanack*," Felix said, from the stairs. "We do it every year. My father did it before me. Thirty-one editions." She descended two steps, deliberately, letting them watch her come closer to the thing they were hunting. Confidence is a door; walk through it slowly. "There's an error in the fourth line, if you want something to arrest me for. 'Mill' should be 'millrace.' It scans badly otherwise. My master will tell you the same, if you ask him politely."

Silence. The tall plain coat looked at the galleys. He looked at the shorter one, who had picked up a proof sheet of genuine almanac matter from the spike by the press—three weeks old, printed for the rehearsal run, bearing the same verses in the same hand. The two halves of her deception confirmed each other like a signature matched against a register.

He set the galley down. He did not even tilt it to look at it sideways. Why should he? Men who burn books never bother to learn to read the ones that survive them.

"It's clean," he said. "Note the stone. Note the cases. Move on."

Felix climbed the rest of the stairs and asked for the lamp so she could check the ink fountain, and her hands, which had gone cold as chase, warmed gradually around the excuse. *Unread.* Her father had told her once, cutting the little italic *f*, that a letter was a small machine for putting a soul into another person's keeping without their knowledge. Tonight the machines had held. Fourteen dead men at the mill, and thirty more whose names she knew by heart now, all of them standing in plain view on a composing stone, wearing winter proverbs for uniforms, and six soldiers and two informers had walked past them twice.

*Say their names,* she thought, inking the roller with unnecessary thoroughness. *I'll set you in a finer face than Halden ever hired.*

* * *

They took Corvel at seven, in the gray hour when the lamps gutter and the river fog comes up the streets like something paid by the page.

By then the shop had been turned out thoroughly enough that the sergeant seemed almost embarrassed by the poverty of the haul: the watermarked paper, a bundle of Corvel's correspondence, the warrant drawer pried open to reveal nothing worse than invoices and a locked box that yielded, before witnesses, sixty-two crowns and a woman's miniature that made Corvel close his eyes. No pamphlets. No proofs of the broadsides. Felix had burned those on the second night, all but the sheet in her apron, and the sheet in her apron had traveled since—folded into the spine of a psalter, then into the false bottom of the coal scuttle, then back into the psalter—and it was upstairs in her father's bench drawer at this very moment, under the scattered italic *f*s, and no one had looked there because no one looks at what a woman keeps among her father's things.

"Master Corvel," the sergeant read from his paper, "is detained on suspicion of publishing false and seditious matter touching the person of Magistrate Halden." He glanced up. "Anything further to declare?"

The shop was silent except for the drip of the melted snow off the soldiers' boots, ticking on the stones like a slow press coming down.

Corvel looked at Felix. In the ruin of his shop, in the lamplight failing toward morning, his face was the face of a man standing at his own galley, reading his last setting, checking it line against copy before the stone is imposed forever. His lips moved—she watched them, a printer's daughter, reading lips the way she read backwards type—and what they shaped was not *save me* and not *betray them* and not even *forgive*.

It was: *the route.*

As in: the route is yours now. As in: I taught you the trade; finish the job.

Then they took him out into the fog, and Felix stood in the doorway of the shop her father had built and her master had bled for, and watched the bayonets bob away down the hill toward the Customhouse jail, and discovered that she was still breathing. That was all. Breathing, and the fog lying cold on her face, and behind her the wreckage of forty years' work, and on the composing stone in the middle of it all, untouched, unexamined, patient as lead, the truth.

* * *

She did not weep. She wanted to; her body collected the payment for the night in her hands, which shook so badly she had to sit on them. But weeping is loud and grief is a luxury with a schedule, and somewhere across the city Isandro was watching the ferry stairs for the signal—a towel hung at the loft window, white side out, meaning *safe*—and she owed him accuracy more than she owed herself relief.

She hung the towel gray side out. *Not safe. Come anyway, carefully.*

Then she began to gather the type.

Not all of it. Not yet. But the long primer, her father's long primer, she knelt among and sorted by hand, letter by letter into the case, because a person must put one true thing back in order before she can ask anyone to believe she can carry disorder across a river. The soldiers had knocked two of the case's brass labels loose. She pressed them flat with her thumb and reseated them. *H. K. L.* Her father's arrangement, learned in Amsterdam, older than the magistrate, older than the regiment, older than any amnesty that might someday be declared by men who believed forgiveness was theirs to give.

She was still kneeling there, an hour past sunrise, when the stair door opened without a knock and Isandro came through it sideways, fast and quiet, closing it with his shoulder. Snow in the seams of his coat. A bruise going yellow-green along his jaw that had not been there at checkpoint one.

He took in the room in one turn of his head—the emptied cupboards, the trampled paper, the overturned cases, the scuffed floor, the absence of Corvel—and Felix watched the arithmetic complete itself behind his eyes, and watched him arrive, without her having to say it, at the only sentence that mattered now.

"They'll have described you," he said quietly. "A journeywoman, alone, in the shop. By nightfall there'll be a name on it."

"There's already a name on it," Felix said. She rose from the case, and her knees cracked like quoins being set, and she found that her voice, when it came, had the flat certainty of justified type. "Halden sent men for 'the Eastwick girl.' They just haven't finished spelling it."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment. Then he crossed the ruined shop, picked her cloak off the hook where the soldiers had thrown it, and held it out.

"Then don't be here when they spell it," he said.
