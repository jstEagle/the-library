## 12. Checkpoint Two: The Toll Gate

Felix had learned from her father that fear lived in the hands. The mind could lie to itself all morning—*you are only a typesetter, you are only going to country work, your papers are in order*—but the hands told the truth if you let them. So she had trained her hands for three days: she had handled the decoy galley of almanac verse until the smell of ink stopped making her heart stumble; she had counted coins with cold fingers by candlelight until the counting was smooth as water; she had practiced folding her travel pass along its worn crease so that when the moment came it would open like something long and often carried, not like a document born last week.

Now the toll gate rose out of the gray morning like a jaw.

It stood where the Vellamont road narrowed between two hills, a stone arch older than the magistrate's office that taxed through it, and this year the arch had grown teeth—a timber barricade, a lantern still burning against the fog, and six soldiers of the city regiment with muskets slung and breath steaming. Beyond them the road ran white and empty toward the hills, toward the publisher's press she had never seen, toward everything.

The cart she rode on belonged to a farmer's widow named Pell who took paying passengers when her son was away, and Mrs. Pell talked the whole way as though talking were a duty owed to God, about turnips and the price of salt and how the frost had come early and hard this year, harder than the year her mother drowned herself, though perhaps Felix didn't want to hear such talk, girls these days were so delicate. Felix heard almost none of it. She sat with the job case on her knees—a real case, half-filled with real sorts, because Isandro had insisted that any lie built on a false foundation would collapse at the first touch—and watched the soldiers watch the road.

Her papers were in the inner pocket of her coat, folded in oilskin. Her name was on them, which was either the bravest thing she had ever done or the stupidest, and she had stopped trying to decide which. *Felix Eastwick, typesetter, engaged for work at the Harrow Street press in Ashcombe.* Corvel was in the citadel by now; Betto's tongue had seen to that. Halden's men knew an Eastwick girl was carrying something. They did not know what, or how, or when—but they knew the name, and the name was stitched into her own skin, and there was no leaving it behind.

*They will look for a smuggler,* Isandro had said, the night before, in the stable where they'd parted. *Smugglers hide things. So don't hide anything. Carry your tools openly. Let them read every paper twice. A woman with nothing to conceal is the most invisible creature in Vellamont.*

*A woman with nothing to conceal,* she had repeated, and he had almost smiled—the corner of his mouth moving without quite committing—and said, *You have the ledger memorized. That is your concealment: there is nothing on you but paper anyone can read.*

That had been the true genius of her father's method, may God keep him and forgive him his small vices. The galleys in her case were genuine almanac verse, set line by line, perfectly printable, worthless to any officer who read them—which they would, now, since the raid. But each line's measure, its very length in ems, its breaks and spacings, encoded dates and names in a cipher her father had devised for hiding political jokes inside funeral sermons back in the bad years before she was born. She carried testimony in the width of white space. She carried the dead in the shape of silence between words.

The cart jolted to a stop beneath the arch. Mrs. Pell fell silent at last, which was the only miracle of the season.

A sergeant with a face like a shut door came around to the cart bed. "Papers."

Felix handed hers up along with Mrs. Pell's, and the sergeant read them by lantern light, unhurried, while another soldier walked the perimeter of the cart and a third looked under it with a pole, prodding at the boards. Fog moved through the arch in slow ropes. Somewhere upslope a bird made one raw sound and thought better of it.

"Typesetter," the sergeant said.

"For the Harrow Street press." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "Almanacs. Country folk want their weather and their saints' days same as anyone."

He grunted and waved the cart through the barricade—and then, as the wheels began to turn, a voice from the guardhouse said, "Wait," and the world tilted.

The man who came out into the fog was tall and stooped, forty or more, with the squint of a lifetime over small type. He wore an officer's sash over a civilian greatcoat, the mismatched dress of a man given rank quickly and cheaply, and Felix knew him before he reached the lantern. She knew the walk, the squint, the way he held printed matter at arm's length like a man reading an accusation. Mr. Ambrose Fenn. For eleven years the foreman of Eastwick & Son. Dismissed the month after her father's funeral—no, not dismissed; he had left, quietly, taking his tool roll and saying nothing to her, and she had hated him for it with the clean, uncomplicated hatred of grief.

Now he stood at the toll gate wearing Halden's colors, and her papers were in his hand.

"Eastwick & Son," he said, reading. Not looking at her yet. "Vellamont."

"The shop is closed," Felix said. "I'm seeking work."

"I know the shop is closed." He turned the pass over. Read the back of it, which had nothing on it, thoroughly. "I set type in that shop for eleven years. I pulled proofs on that press." His eyes came up at last, and there was nothing in them she could read—or rather, there was too much: anger, shame, calculation, and underneath it all something that might have been sorrow, worn down to a nub. "You're the daughter."

"I'm a typesetter."

"The daughter," he repeated, softly, as if she hadn't spoken. And then, louder, to the sergeant: "This one waits. Search the cart properly. I'll examine her case myself."

The world went narrow and ringing. Mrs. Pell began to protest about turnips spoiling and the frost coming on, and was ignored. Felix climbed down with the job case hugged to her chest like a child, and set it on the plank table inside the guardhouse where Fenn indicated, and stood while he opened it drawer by drawer, lifting out compartments, running his fingers through the sorts the way he had run them ten thousand times at the bench behind her father's.

"You still sort your boxes wrong," he said. "Lowercase e should sit nearest the thumb. Your father never corrected you. I always wondered if it was kindness or blindness."

"It was respect," Felix said. "He said a compositor arranges her case as her mind arranges the world, and no two minds are alike."

Fenn's hands stopped moving. In the fog outside, one of the soldiers laughed at some joke and was silenced by the sergeant. The lantern hissed. Felix watched the man who had taught her the difference between a thin space and a hair space hold a compartment of her type up to the light, and she understood with terrible clarity that he was looking for galleys, that Betto would have described galleys, that if he lifted the stone slab at the bottom of the case—if he tipped it—he would find the second galley, the one she had not been able to leave behind, the one whose lines were pure cipher, unreadable except to someone who knew the code, and Fenn had worked eleven years in her father's shop and might have been shown the old funeral-sermon tricks, might have—

"You were at the funeral," Fenn said abruptly. "I saw you. You didn't cry."

"I cried after."

"There wasn't anyone after. That's why I left." He said it flatly, to the type rather than to her. "Your father was the best hand in the city and they buried him like a pauper and I stood there and did nothing, and then I came to the shop and looked at you—at a girl—at that bench, and I thought, it dies with her, the trade dies in a girl's hands, and I couldn't bear to stay and watch it die." He set the compartment down. "So I took the colonel's commission instead. It pays. There's that."

Outside, suddenly, the morning broke open.

She heard it before she understood it: the crack of wood, a horse shrieking, a man's voice pitched high with pain and fury—the universal music of an accident. Through the guardhouse door Felix saw a farm cart slewed sideways across the road above the arch, its wheel folding under it like a knee, sacks of grain bursting across the frozen mud, and the carter—broad-shouldered, scarf-wrapped, unmistakable even at a distance to a woman who had watched him walk away from her three nights running—cursing the horse, the wheel, the road, the government of Vellamont, in a voice that carried beautifully.

The sergeant swore and sent three men up the slope. Another went to help wrestle the horse, which had decided to make the situation theatrical, rearing and tangling its traces in the ruined cart. Within moments the checkpoint was two soldiers, one distracted, and a great deal of noise.

Fenn alone did not move. He stood over the open case, and his eyes went once to the door, once to the stone slab at the bottom of the case, and then—to her.

And Felix, who had spent her life reading faces over type, reading customers, reading the difference between a compliment and a warning, saw in his face a thing she would turn over for the rest of her life: a man standing at a crossroads with his hand on the lid of a box, choosing.

"Eleven years," Fenn said quietly. "You know what your father paid me? Nothing worth having. You know what he gave me? The trade. Everything else I own came out of that trade, and I sold it for a colonel's sash, and the sash buys coal." He closed the compartment. He slid the tray home over the bottom of the case without lifting it, his palm pressing the stone down flat as he did so, one motion, natural as breathing. Then he straightened and called through the door: "Sergeant! The woman's clean. Country typesetter, honest papers, and a case of type any fool can read. Let her pass before that idiot's horse costs us the whole morning."

The sergeant, elbow-deep in spilled grain, waved irritably. Fenn picked up the job case himself and carried it out to the cart, and set it in Felix's arms, and for a moment his stooped face was close to hers, close enough that she saw the tiny flecks of lead still caught in the seams of his knuckles, eleven years deep, that no commission would ever wash out.

"Your e's are still sorted wrong," he said. "Sort them right. Someone has to carry the trade." And he stepped back, and touched his hat brim, and became again an officer of Magistrate Halden, calling orders into the fog.

Mrs. Pell clicked her tongue and the cart rolled through the arch, past the barricade, onto the open road, and the toll gate sank behind them into the white morning like a stone dropped in milk. Felix did not look back. If she looked back she would have to think about what Fenn had done and why, and whether mercy from such a man was worth more or less than none at all, and she could not think about any of it yet, because ahead, half a mile on, at the bend where Isandro would be limping back to his staged wreck to scatter his evidence and vanish, a figure in a carter's scarf stepped out of the ditch and raised one hand.

She made Mrs. Pell stop—for coin, for gratitude, for the excuse of helping with the wreckage—and walked back along the frozen ruts to meet him.

Isandro's cheek was scraped raw where he had thrown himself clear of his own catastrophe. Grain dust frosted his shoulders. He was favoring his left arm, and her eyes went to it before her voice found itself.

"It's sprained, not broken," he said. "I've had worse falling off ferry stairs sober."

"You could have been beaten again. At the customhouse they beat you, and now—"

"At the customhouse I was surprised." He worked the scarf loose from his throat. "Today I chose the ditch I fell in. There's a difference, and the difference matters." He glanced past her at the receding cart, at Mrs. Pell's silhouette driving on without them, having accepted a bribe of two silver coins and no questions. "He let you pass. The foreman."

"How do you know he was my father's foreman?"

"You said 'the trade' the way you say it. Like holding a knife." Isandro studied her face with that patience she had begun, cautiously, to trust. "What did it cost?"

"Nothing. That's what frightens me. He lied for me—or didn't lie, maybe he just chose not to look under the stone—and I don't know which is worse. A man who risks himself for me or a man who couldn't be bothered to risk himself." She shifted the case on her hip. "He told me to sort my e's right. As if—" Her voice caught on something unexpected, and she was silent a moment, furious at the catch. "As if the trade mattered more than the war. As if I were the trade now."

Isandro was quiet. Down the valley the toll gate had become a smudge; somewhere behind it his ruined cart was being cursed over by soldiers who would find nothing but grain and honest rot in the axle.

"My sister says debts are chains," he said at last. "She's watched our mother spend her life paying other people's. So I've tried to owe nothing to anyone." He flexed his bad hand, wincing, watching the fingers. "But I've been counting, these last days, and I find I owe you a courier's fee, a beating at the customhouse that was taken on my account, and now a sprained wrist I chose freely, which doesn't count, except that it does. And you owe me nothing that I can name. That's a bad balance. Debts like that grow teeth."

"Then call it even," Felix said, "because without you I'd have died at the first checkpoint, and without your father's river I wouldn't have known there was a far bank at all."

"That's not even. That's arithmetic." He smiled—actually smiled, brief and unguarded, and it changed his whole face, made him briefly a boy, briefly someone she might have known all her life in another story. "Livia would say we should stop keeping accounts altogether. She'd say a debt between friends isn't a debt, it's just—" He stopped. Chose again. "It's just what people are to each other."

Friends. The word hung in the cold air between them, steaming faintly like their breath, and neither of them took it back.

They walked. The road climbed away from the river country into hills where the snow lay unbroken and the fences leaned like tired men, and the job case rode on Felix's hip, heavy with its harmless verse and its hidden testimony, and ahead of them, by nightfall tomorrow, lay the press. Isandro cradled his sprained arm and told her, in pieces, about the ferry stairs in summer, the brown water and the bargemen's songs, and Livia at nine years old diving for dropped coins, and Felix found herself telling him about her father teaching her to cut a punch, the burin biting the steel, the way a letter grew backward under your hands until it was ready to stamp itself forward on the world forever.

"Backward to go forward," Isandro said. "Like everything worth doing."

They crested the second hill as the light failed. Below, in a fold of the land, a hamlet showed its windows burning yellow, and beyond it, black against a bruised sky, the ridge road wound on toward Ashcombe—and along that ridge, small in the distance, moving with the unhurried confidence of authority, came riders. Four of them. Six. Too many for farmers, too organized for travelers, spaced like men following a route they knew.

Felix's hand found the case at her hip. Isandro's found her shoulder, drawing her both of them down behind the drift fence, and his voice at her ear had gone flat and quiet, the voice of the man who ferried contraband.

"They're not riding toward the gate," he murmured. "They're riding the ridge. Toward Ashcombe. Ahead of us." He watched them a long moment, jaw tight. "Nobody rides ahead of the traveler unless they know where the traveler is going."

Behind them, a day's ride south, someone had talked—or someone had repented of mercy—and the road forward had grown eyes.
