## 13. Snowbound

The storm came down out of the western hills like something with intent.

They had seen it building all afternoon—a bruise-colored wall dragging itself across the sky—and had raced the last two miles along the toll road with the wind already picking at their collars, plucking at their hoods, testing them the way Halden's men tested a crate at a checkpoint. By the time the inn's sign swung into view, creaking on its bracket like a hanged man, the snow was coming sideways, in sheets, in fists, and Felix could no longer see the road behind them. Which, she thought, might be a mercy.

The inn was called the Halfway Hearth, and it was full.

Not full of travelers—travelers had stopped crossing this country weeks ago—but full of people who had nowhere else to be. Felix understood it within moments of stepping through the door, shaking snow from her skirts, Isandro's shoulder brushing hers in the narrow entry: these were not guests. These were refugees. The smell told her first—wet wool and woodsmoke and boiled turnips and under it all something animal, the close sourness of too many bodies sleeping in one room for too many nights. Then the sound: not conversation but a low continuous murmur, like a congregation between prayers, or a river moving under ice.

Every bench was taken. Children slept in heaps on the floor by the hearth, three and four together under a single blanket, breathing in unison. An old woman sat nearest the fire with her hands wrapped around an empty bowl as though warmth itself were soup. A man with a carpenter's forearms sat sharpening a knife that was already sharp, the whetstone circling and circling, his eyes on nothing.

The innkeeper, a broad woman with flour to her elbows and grief worn smooth into her face, looked them over with the flat assessment of someone who had stopped being able to afford kindness. "Two coppers for the floor by the east wall," she said. "Supper's turnip and barley, what there is of it. And you'll want to dry those things before the fire gets claimed."

Felix paid without haggling. In her satchel, wrapped twice in oilcloth beneath the harmless-looking galleys of almanac verse, she felt the true ledger shift against her hip like a sleeping animal turning over, and she moved her hand to still it—an instinct now, three days old, the gesture of a woman carrying a live coal through a crowd.

* * *

They found a place against the east wall, between a family of mill workers and a young priest who had fallen asleep sitting upright, his breviary open on his knee, his lips still moving faintly in a dream. Felix unwrapped her shawl, hung her stockings to steam by the nearest of the three hearths, and discovered that her hands would not stop shaking—not from cold, exactly, though there was that too, but from the long sustained vibration of fear that had carried her from Vellamont, through the customhouse, past the toll gate, and now into this room where, for one night at least, no one could travel and no one could pursue.

The blizzard had closed the road behind them. It had also closed the road ahead. There was nothing to do now but sit, and be warm if possible, and wait for morning or for spring, whichever came first.

The mill worker's wife began talking to her almost at once.

Felix would think about this later—how easily they talked, how starved for it these people were, how a stranger with a listening face became a confessional. The woman, Maren, had flour-burned fingers and a nursing infant asleep against her chest, and she spoke in the low even voice of someone who had told the story so many times it had worn smooth as river stone.

"We came from Threnn Vale," she said. "You'd know it if—" She stopped, corrected herself. "No. You wouldn't. Nobody knows it now. There's nothing left to know." She turned the infant's small cap in her fingers. "My husband worked the sluice gates. Twelve years. When the levy came due last spring he said the numbers were wrong—he said it to the assessor's face, mind, politely enough, just that the figures didn't match what we'd actually harvested. That was all. That was everything he did."

She was quiet for a moment. The knife-sharpener's whetstone circled. Snow hissed against the shutters like sand thrown at glass.

"They burned the mill in October," Maren said. "Officially it was a boiler accident. Eleven families lived around that yard. My sister-in-law's boy, Peter, he was four." She looked up at Felix then, and her eyes were perfectly dry, which was somehow worse than weeping. "He was four. He liked to count things. Gates and geese and steps. He'd have counted the whole town if anyone had let him."

Felix's hands had gone still in her lap.

"There were others from the Vale here earlier in the winter," Maren went on, because once such a story started it seemed it could not stop, had been waiting too long for any door to open. "The Coels, the widow Anser, the ferryman Brandt—the one whose son was pressed into the work gangs on the north road and never wrote back. They're scattered now. Some went south over the pass before the snows. Some..." She shrugged, a small defeated motion. "Some are in the ground between here and there, and nobody counted them either."

*The ledger is paper,* Felix thought, *and paper burns.* She had known this since the night the courier died with his hand on the type case; she had carried the knowledge through every checkpoint like a stone in her shoe. But sitting in this room, hearing a four-year-old boy reduced to a habit of counting, she understood it differently. Understood what the names in the ledger actually weighed.

Because Maren was the ledger. The sleeping child at her breast was the ledger. The old woman with the empty bowl, the man with the knife and the circling stone, the priest dreaming his way through the psalms—they were pages, every one, written in the hand of Magistrate Halden, signed and dated and filed away in flesh instead of ink. What lay sewn beneath the sorts in her satchel was only the fair copy. The original walked and breathed and boiled its turnips and sharpened its knives and would keep testifying, silently, for as long as any of them drew breath—unless the world never learned to read it.

"Your husband," Felix said quietly. "What was his name?"

Maren blinked. Of all the questions she might have been asked, this one seemed to surprise her most.

"Dane," she said. "Dane Ostrew. Why?"

"Because I'm going to write it down," Felix said, and heard her own voice come out level and certain in a way that startled her. "His name, and your sister-in-law's boy. All of it, if you'll tell me. Names and dates. It matters."

"Why does it matter?" The words came out cracked, flung rather than asked. "Paper doesn't raise the dead."

"No," Felix said. "But it stops the living being made into liars. They want this to have not happened. Every year it goes unwritten, it happens again—did you know that? Forgetting is a thing they do to you twice."

Maren stared at her for a long moment. Then she reached over and gripped Felix's wrist, hard, with her work-roughened hand, and said, "Then write, girl. Write until your fingers bleed."

* * *

Isandro returned from seeing to the horses with meltwater in his hair and an expression Felix was learning to read: the wariness banked down but not out, the constant arithmetic of exits running behind his eyes. He lowered himself onto the bench beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and did not ask what Maren had said, because he had been in rooms like this before. His face said he had heard a hundred versions of the same story and carried each one somewhere behind his ribs.

"The stable boy says the drifts will be over the fences by midnight," he murmured. "Nobody moves tomorrow either. Maybe not for two days."

Felix nodded. Two days. New Year's was eleven days off now, and every hour the storm stole from them was an hour given to Betto, to Halden's men, to whoever else might be following the route. But there was nothing to be done about it tonight, and Isandro's hand, resting on the bench between them, palm up and half-open, suggested he knew that too.

They ate their barley and turnips in silence. Around them the inn settled into its long evening rhythm: a mother humming, the fire popping, wind throwing itself against the shutters and falling back defeated. Someone produced a fiddle from somewhere and played three bars of something slow before an old man told him to stop, not angrily, just—wearily—as though music were more than the room could bear.

Later, when the lamps had been trimmed low and most of the refugees had wrapped themselves into sleep, Felix took out the little notebook she had bought at the toll market and, by the light of a guttering candle stub, began to write. Dane Ostrew, sluice keeper, Threnn Vale. Mill burned October the ninth. The child Peter, aged four. The widow Anser. The ferryman Brandt's son. She wrote in her father's cramped, economical hand—the hand that had labeled ten thousand punches in a case she now kept—and the act of writing steadied her the way cutting a punch always had, the way making anything true steadied her.

"You're doing what Corvel does," Isandro said softly.

It was the first thing either of them had said in an hour. She did not look up. "Corvel prints other people's courage and hides behind anonymity. This has my name going on it eventually. Everything I do from here to the press has my name on it, whether I sign it or not."

"That's not what I meant." He shifted, and the bench complained beneath him. "I meant he collects testimony. He told me once—he was drunk, and bitter—that a printer is only ever a servant of other people's words. He hated it. You seem to have decided to love it."

"Perhaps because I don't intend to survive being only a servant." She said it lightly, meaning it heavily, and felt him go still beside her.

"Felix."

"I didn't mean it the way it sounded."

"Yes, you did." His voice was low, pitched beneath the room's breathing. "You've meant it since the courier died. You carry that case like it owes you a death and you mean to pay it."

The candle guttered. Outside, the storm found a new register, a deep subterranean groan, the sound of the whole winter leaning its weight against the world.

"Tell me about the regiment," she said, to turn him from her, and immediately regretted the cowardice of it—but he surprised her by not refusing.

For a while she thought he would. He looked at the fire, jaw working, hands loose between his knees. When he finally spoke it was in the flat careful voice of a man reading from a document.

"There were strikers at the salt works, the winter before my discharge. Maybe two hundred. Women and boys at the front, mostly, because the men had already been arrested. We were ordered to clear the yard." He paused. "Ordered to fire, is what I mean. The captain gave the order twice. The second time his voice broke, which I think about more than I should. I stepped out of the line. Just—one step, sideways. Out of the line of muskets. And Sergeant Bray put his hand on my shoulder like I was furniture and moved me back, and the man next to me fired where I would have refused to, and after it was done the captain tore up my commission papers himself rather than see me court-martialed, because he didn't want the order read aloud in a courtroom. He wanted it forgotten." He turned his head and looked at her. "That's what I learned in the regiment. That forgetting isn't passive. Somebody has to work at it. It's a trade, like yours."

Felix closed the notebook slowly.

"Livia thinks I should take Halden's clemency," he went on. "She said so before I left, and she was right to say it, and I lied to her and told her I'd consider it. I've been telling myself I came on this road for the money, for the ferry debt, for my sister. All true. None of it true." He exhaled. "I stepped out of line once, and it cost me everything, and I have spent three years wishing I'd stepped further. This is me stepping further. That's all. I wanted you to know that, since we may both die before we can be honest at any better time."

The fire settled. Somewhere in the dark a child murmured in sleep and was hushed by a mother's hand.

"My father cut punches for forty years," Felix said. Her voice came out strange, thickened. "Forty years making letters, and never once made a sentence of his own. He used to say the letters weren't his to use. I used to think that was humility. Now I think it was fear wearing humility's coat, and I am terrified—" Her breath caught. "I am terrified I inherited the coat as well as the bench. That I'll carry this ledger right to the press and then stand outside while someone safer prints it, and spend the rest of my life being very proud of my discretion."

"You won't."

"How can you know?"

"Because I watched you talk to that woman." He nodded toward where Maren slept, the infant rising and falling on her chest. "You didn't ask for her story to add to a file. You asked her husband's name. Corvel would never have asked the name." He was quiet a moment. "And because when I put my hand over yours on the ferry stairs, that first night, you didn't pull away. People who intend to stand outside their own lives pull away."

Felix looked down at their hands on the bench between them, not touching now but near, near the way two banks of a river are near, defining the same current. Something rose in her that had no safe words, so she let it sit unspoken, and found that the unspoken thing was warm anyway.

"Isandro," she said. "When this is done. Whatever 'done' means. When the ledger's printed or we're in the ground beside the people in it—find me. Not at the shop. Find me somewhere with no type in it at all, and we'll sit and be two people who once carried something heavy together, and we will talk about absolutely nothing, for hours, and it will be the best conversation of my life."

He smiled then—the rare one, the one that reached his eyes and made him briefly, impossibly young. "There's no such place," he said. "There's type everywhere. You'll start reading the innkeeper's chalkboard by morning."

"Then find me somewhere with bad type," she said. "Crooked serifs. I'll complain about them and you'll pretend to care, and that will be enough."

"It would be enough," he agreed softly, and reached over, and moved a strand of hair from her face with two fingers, careful as a compositor lifting a fragile sort, and neither of them said anything else, because nothing else needed saying yet, and the storm sang on, and the refugees slept their battered sleep around them like a congregation keeping faith.

* * *

She woke before dawn to a changed silence.

The blizzard had spent itself; the wind had dropped to a mutter, and the light through the frost-ferned window was that pale, held-breath gray that comes before snowlight. Felix lay still for a moment, cataloguing—the satchel beneath her head, the galleys, the notebook, Isandro's breathing across the narrow space between them—and then she heard the other sound underneath the quiet.

Hooves. More than one horse, coming slow through deep snow on the road behind the inn.

Beside her, Isandro's eyes were already open. He had heard it first. His hand found hers in the dark and gripped it, and his mouth shaped one silent word against the gray beginning of the morning:

*Betto.*
