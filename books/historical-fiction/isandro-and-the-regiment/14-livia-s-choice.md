## 14. Livia's Choice

The laundry of the wealthy told stories to those who knew how to read them. Livia Isandri had learned the language at eleven years old, when her mother's hands were still strong enough to wash for three great houses and her father was still alive, and every collar that crossed their scrubbing board had whispered something: which magistrate drank, which wife wept behind shutters, which merchant had begun folding his shirts with the care of a man expecting to be searched. Linen was honest. People lied with their mouths and their papers, but linen could not help telling the truth about the bodies it touched.

So Livia knew things in Vellamont that people with names and station did not know she knew. She knew that Magistrate Halden's housekeeper soaked his collars in vinegar because the sweat of a frightened man eats cloth differently than the sweat of an angry one, and Halden's collars were eaten through at the neck as if by acid. She knew the sergeant who came weekly for his uniform pieces had begun sending his washing out in bundles tied with red cord—the color of men who wanted to be seen wanting favor. She knew, in the way that water knows the shape of the stone it runs over, that the winter had turned the whole city into a bundle of wringing-wet secrets, and that sooner or later hers would be wrung out too.

She was wringing one out now, in fact—her own sleeve, scalded pink to the elbow in the washtub they kept in the lean-to behind their rooms on the Ferry Stairs. Steam climbed the frost-furred window. Outside, the river muttered under its lid of ice, and somewhere down the bank her brother was presumably doing whatever it was he had stopped explaining to her.

That was the newest story the laundry had told her, though no shirt had confessed it directly.

It had come together the way stains come together into a shape: a cuff here, a smear there, until suddenly you saw the whole handprint. Isandro had money again—not much, but coin where for two years there had been only debts and the small gray economies of poverty. He came home at hours that made no sense for ferry work, since the ferry had died with the bridge closing and the ice was not yet thick enough to bear a sledge. His boots were wrong. She had noticed the boots first, actually, because boots were her scripture: river mud on them, yes, but under the mud a fine gray ash, the kind that comes from burned paper, and once—a thread of blue wool caught in the lace hook, a color no ferryman's client wore on the far bank. A soldier's color. Or a printer's.

And twice now he had come home with knuckles split in a pattern she recognized from their father, who had been a man who settled things with his hands: not the ragged tears of a fall or a fight among equals, but the neat, deep cracking of bone meeting bone through someone else's face, over and over, in the same place.

She had asked him nothing. That was its own kind of lie, and she carried it in her chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

The soldiers came on a Thursday, at the hour when the light went the color of dirty dishwater and honest people were lighting lamps.

Livia was hanging sheets when they filled the alley—four of them, and a fifth man who was not a soldier. She knew him from the great houses: a thin, patient-faced clerk named Voss who did the magistrate's quiet business, the kind of man whose linen never stained because he never sweated, never hurried, never did anything so human as bleed. He stood just outside the drip-line of the sheets with his hands folded, courteous as a funeral.

"Signora Isandri." He pronounced it correctly, which somehow made it worse. "Thank you for receiving us."

"You didn't ask," she said. She kept her wet hands in view. Her heart was going like a paddle-wheel, but she had learned long ago that frightened women who looked frightened became easier to handle, and she had no intention of being handled. "You came into my yard."

"Property of the city," Voss said mildly, glancing around the lean-to, the tubs, the lines. "More or less. May I?" He did not wait for permission to lift a sheet from the basket, hold it to the gray light, examine the weave. "You do good work. The customhouse clerks speak well of you. Discreet, they say. You see a great deal and say little."

"I see sheets," Livia said. "I say what they cost."

"Mm." He set the sheet down with exaggerated care, as if it might go off. "Let me tell you why I've come, and then let me tell you a story, and you can decide which of us is wasting the other's time." He drew off his gloves, finger by finger. "You know the name Betto Corvel?"

She knew it the way everyone near the print quarter knew it: the master's apprentice, a narrow boy with quick eyes and a habit of laughing half a second after everyone else, as if jokes reached him by slow post. She said none of this. She waited.

"Betto has been very helpful to the magistrate," Voss said. "Helpful boys eat well in Vellamont this winter. He has told us about pamphlets, about a ledger, about a girl from a print shop who is carrying something east along the mill road. And he has told us—" and here Voss smiled, small and bloodless, "—that the girl does not travel alone. That she travels with a ferryman's son. A discharged soldier. A man named Isandro."

The cold came up through the cobbles then, through her shoes, through her bones, and it was not the weather.

"Isandro," she repeated. It came out flat. Good. Flat was survivable.

"A common enough name," Voss agreed pleasantly. "Half the river answers to it. Which is why I'm here and not a squad of muskets. Because I would prefer, Signora Isandri, to be wrong." He put his gloves back on. "Here is the story I promised. There was once a young officer in the Fourth Regiment who refused an order at the salt-works strike. Strikers, women and children in the front rank, and he would not give the word to fire. They broke him for it—stripped his commission, blackened his name. But you know this story. You lived it. Bread and shame ever after."

"Get to the end," Livia said.

"The end is that such a man is dangerous to himself and fatal to everyone who loves him. He cannot stop being what they made him. So here is my offer, offered kindly: the magistrate will restore what was taken. Full pardon, back pay, and a letter that says what the salt-works order truly was—which is to say, illegal, and the men who gave it cowards. All your brother must do is bring in the Eastwick girl and the paper she carries. Or even simpler. Even kinder. Tell me where they cross the millrace, and walk away, and let other men do the rest." Voss adjusted one glove at the wrist. "You have until Sunday. You may leave word at the fish stall on Copper Lane. Ask for red cord."

He bowed, actually bowed, and the soldiers parted to let him pass, and Livia stood among her freezing sheets and understood that her life had just been weighed against her brother's and found, by a stranger, to be worth trading.

* * *

She did not cry. Crying was for afterward, and she was not sure yet that there would be an afterward worth crying for. She finished the washing. That was the discipline her mother had left her: the tub does not care, the linen does not care, finish the work and let the work hold you up while the rest of you shakes.

Isandro came home after dark, smelling of river smoke, with bread under his arm and that new careful brightness he wore like a borrowed coat.

"There's a man selling herring off a cart on Copper Lane," Livia said, without turning from the fire. "Since when? There's never been a fish stall on Copper Lane."

The silence behind her had a texture. She could hear him deciding whether to lie, and she could hear the moment the lie died in his throat, because whatever else her brother was now—smuggler, courier, fool, hero—he had never managed to lie to her successfully, and some merciful instinct told him not to start tonight.

"Liv—"

"They know." She turned. His face in the firelight was all planes and shadow, her handsome stubborn brother, twenty-six years old and already carrying more ruin than most men met in a lifetime. "They came today. A clerk named Voss and four soldiers. They know about the girl. They know about the ledger. They know about you." She watched him absorb it the way she'd watched their father absorb bad news: body first, jaw tightening, hands going still. "They want me to sell the millrace crossing. By Sunday. In exchange they'll unbreak you."

For a long moment Isandro said nothing. Then, quietly: "You should have taken the offer."

"Don't you dare."

"It's true." He sat down heavily on the bench by the wall, and for the first time since he'd come home from the regiment with his life in a canvas sack, he looked tired all the way through. "Look at what I am, Liv. Look at what I've been doing. If they catch me, they don't hang me—they just erase me, the way they erased me once already. But you. You have a trade. A name. You could—"

"I could be the woman who sold her brother." She crossed the room and took his face in her wet-red hands, forcing his chin up, the way she had when he was seven and had broken the neighbor's window and tried to hide. "Listen to me. I washed for the wife of the man who signed your discharge. I have held the shirts of half the officers in this city, and I can tell you what they are by the sweat in their collars. They are frightened men, Isandro. Halden frightens them, and they frighten the city, and it all holds together out of fear—and the day somebody stops being afraid, it starts coming apart. You've stopped. I can see it on you every time you come through that door. Well." She let go of him. "I've stopped too."

He caught her wrist gently. "Voss doesn't forget. Sunday passes and I haven't crossed him, he comes back with worse."

"Then we make Sunday not matter." And even as she said it, the shape of it was forming in her mind, arriving whole the way the truest things always arrived—as if it had been waiting in the laundry all along, folded inside everything else.

Because here was the thing about Betto Corvel's betrayal, the thing Voss in his neatness had not thought to conceal: he had *told* her. He had walked into her yard and told her exactly what the enemy knew—that Betto knew the route, the millrace, the timing. Men like Voss believed information flowed one way, from the powerful down, the way orders flowed. But Livia worked in the one economy where everything flowed both directions. Every secret she'd ever learned, she'd learned from linen; and linen, unlike magistrates, did not check whether the reader was worthy before it spoke.

Betto knew the route. Therefore Felix Eastwick needed to know that Betto knew the route. Therefore—

"I need your help," Livia said, "and you're going to hate it, because it means telling me everything."

* * *

They talked until the fire burned down to a red eye and the kettle went dry twice. She heard it all: the courier dying on the shop floor with his hand on the case of type, the ledger sewn beneath the sorts, the names and dates and Halden's signature repeated like a heartbeat through every page. She heard about the customhouse, the beating Isandro had taken and shrugged off with a joke; about the galleys disguised as almanac verse, harmless lines that carried testimony in their bones; about the raid, and old Corvel taken away, and the millrace ambush where the pages had scattered across the ice while musket balls cracked the frost around them. She heard him describe Felix Eastwick—her father's punches, her ink-stained competence, the way she'd crawled the ice gathering scattered truth into her arms—and heard in his voice something he probably did not hear himself, and filed it away with all the other things she knew and did not say.

"She doesn't know about Betto," Isandro said. "I didn't want to frighten her off the route before I knew how much Betto actually gave them. If I change the crossing now, in a panic, we might walk straight into—"

"You won't change anything," Livia said. "That's the point. You'll keep exactly the route and exactly the schedule, because the moment you deviate, Voss knows his bait took. No. Betto knows the route—so we make sure the route is ready for him." She was already pulling paper toward her, the cheap gray stock she used for marking bundles. "But she deserves to know what's coming. Both of them do. And I can't be seen anywhere near the print quarter."

"So how does the warning travel?"

Livia smiled, and it was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a woman who had spent fifteen years making herself invisible in plain sight, in the yards and doorways of the mighty, carrying their linens and their secrets, underestimated by everyone because she was poor and female and smelled of lye.

"Laundry," she said.

* * *

The system went out the next morning, stitched into the ordinary traffic of the city.

Felix Eastwick, traveling as a hired typesetter, would have washing—it was the first thing any traveler arranged in winter towns, and inns sent bundles out to whichever washerwoman served the district. Livia could not reach the mill road herself; Voss would have her watched until Sunday whether she saw the watchers or not. But washerwomen talked to washerwomen, over fences and tubs and at the public pumps, and Livia had spent years building exactly those small bridges, woman to woman, favors banked like coins: a mended shift here, a shared load there, a debt of kindness owed her in four neighborhoods between Vellamont and the mill road.

The message itself was simple, and invisible unless you knew where to look. Laundry marks were a private alphabet—every household's linen tagged with stitches, knots, thread-colors, so bundles found their ways home. To anyone inspecting a bundle, the marks on Felix's washing would look entirely conventional. But Livia knew the conventions, and so she broke them deliberately, in a grammar she and every washerwoman on the relay had been taught by the same grandmotherly tongue: *a red knot at the corner means a man marked; two knots means the man is close; blue thread in the seam means trust nothing from the house of Corvel.* And beneath the third fold of a pillowslip, worked in tiny cross-stitch where only a woman who ironed that linen would ever press her thumb: *the apprentice talks. Change nothing. Trust the mark.*

By Friday evening, three relayed bundles had gone out through three pairs of hands that belonged to women nobody had ever thought to interrogate, and Livia sat by her window with her sewing and allowed herself, for one breath, to believe it would arrive.

Then she thought about Sunday.

Voss would come for his answer. When there was none, he would come back with worse—she had promised Isandro as much, and she did not lie to her brother, not even in her own head. They could run; the amnesty was coming, only days away now, and if Felix and Isandro could stay ahead of Betto's knowledge until New Year's, the old charges would burn and the ledger would land on the far shore like a shell bursting open. But amnesty would not shield *her*. She had done nothing yet—nothing chargeable—but Voss did not require crimes, only usefulness, and a woman who refused to be useful became an example instead. She could picture it precisely: the yard, the sheets, the soldiers, her name spoken in the fish-market tone reserved for traitors and informers' kin.

She thought of Isandro at the salt works, standing in front of a firing line because an order was wrong. He had lost everything for one refusal. She supposed families were like that—same spine, different burdens.

Her needle moved steadily through the cloth. On the table lay the last clean shirt of Isandro's, waiting to be folded, and she picked it up and pressed her face briefly into the fabric, smelling river smoke and soap and her whole childhood, and then she folded it in the old way, the way their mother had taught them both, corners meeting corners, and set it on his pile.

Sunday was two days off. Somewhere on the frozen mill road, a girl with ink-stained fingers would open a bundle of washing and read a message written entirely in knots, and live.

And Livia Isandri would sit in her yard with her tubs, in full view of the street, and wait for the magistrate's men to come and learn what her blood was worth.

Down in Vellamont, unseen in the dark, a cart rolled east through the snow toward the millrace, carrying a case of type and two people who did not yet know they had until Sunday—or that they no longer traveled unwarned.
