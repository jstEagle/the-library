## 15. Ambush at the Millrace

### Isandro

The millrace froze in layers, and each layer lied. The top was clean as window glass; beneath it, Isandro knew, the current still moved, black and hungry, eating at the ice from below the way Halden's men ate at the city. He tested the surface with his heel before letting Felix step out onto it, and told himself the groan that rose through his boot soles was only water settling.

"We keep to the shallows' edge," he said. "Where the race widens. If it gives, it gives us a wet leg, not a grave."

Felix said nothing. She carried the satchel slung across her body now, not her shoulder—a lesson from the toll gate, learned on someone else's blood. Inside it rode the galleys of *The Vellamont Almanac for the Year 1793*, forty lines of innocent verse about frost fairs and saints' days, every third letter a name, every fifth a date, every page an indictment dressed in iambic humility. He had watched her compose them by candlelight with her father's tools and had understood, watching, that he had been carrying weapons all his life without knowing what they looked like.

The mill itself stood above them, dead since autumn—wheel locked in its own ice skirt, windows blind. Beyond it the road bent north toward the river fort, and beyond that, if God and the publisher were willing, a press.

"Snow's coming," Felix said. Her breath came out white and vanished. "I can smell it."

"Then we move faster."

They had gone perhaps two hundred paces along the race when Isandro heard the sound he had been trained to hear and had spent four years trying to forget: the particular cadence of boots moving in step. Not travelers. Not refugees. Soldiers who had not yet bothered to disguise their order as disorder.

He caught Felix's arm and pulled her toward the mill wall, into the lee of the stone where the wind couldn't carry their voices.

"How many?" she whispered.

He counted the sound. "Eight. Maybe ten. And ahead of them—" he listened again, and his stomach turned, "—someone light-footed, walking apart. Someone who knows the ground."

"Betto."

She said the name flatly, without hatred, which frightened him more than any shout could have. Betto had mixed ink for Corvel for three years. Betto had carried Felix's lunches up from the kitchen when she worked through the night. Betto knew her face, her route, and—the thought landed like a stone in still water—the trick of the almanac. If they had beaten it out of Corvel or out of the boy, then the pages in her satchel were no longer hidden in plain sight. They were evidence with a confession attached.

"They'll reach the race before we clear it," Isandro said. "We can't go back—Betto will have covered the road behind too, if he's worth anything to them. And forward is worse."

"Then we cross." Felix's chin lifted toward the far bank, where alder scrub ran thick down to the ice. "You said the wide place holds."

"I said it holds a wet leg."

"You also said," she replied, "that ice forgives what men don't. You were talking about your father's ferry in a thaw. I'm asking you to mean it."

For one absurd moment, crouched against a frozen mill while soldiers closed on them through the falling snow, Isandro wanted to laugh—and did, briefly, helplessly, at the way she quoted him back to himself. Then the first musket flashed somewhere up the road, and the ball went shrieking past the mill wheel and buried itself in the ice thirty feet ahead of them with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"Run," he said.

* * *

### Felix

The ice spoke to her the whole way across, and everything it said was terrible.

It boomed under her like a struck bell. It ticked and sighed. Somewhere beneath her feet, close enough to touch through eight inches of frozen blackness, the millrace went on with its own business, indifferent as winter itself. Felix ran the way she set type—looking always two steps ahead, trusting her hands and feet more than her eyes—and the satchel bounced against her ribs with every stride like a second heart.

Behind them, shouts. Ahead, the alders, close enough to see individual branches now, close enough—

The volley came all at once, six or seven reports smashing flat across the valley, and the ice around her erupted. Not balls punching through—balls skipping, ricocheting off the frozen surface, screaming away over the alders, because the soldiers had fired low, firing low at running figures on glass, and the ice threw their lead back at the sky. One chip struck her cheek, cold as a needle. Another sang past her ear. Beside her Isandro stumbled, swore, kept running, and she thought: *he's hit, he's hit,* but he didn't fall, and there was no time.

They reached the far bank and plunged into the alder scrub, thorns raking her sleeves, and crouched there gasping among the bare whips of branches.

And that was when Felix realized the truth that would shape the next hour of her life.

"The satchel strap." Isandro's voice came out strangled. He was holding the leather up into the gray light. A ball—not a ricochet, a true ball, fired straight and low—had cut it clean through at the buckle. The satchel hung open in his hands, empty.

She turned and looked back across the race.

The galleys lay scattered on the ice behind them, thrown loose in the last strides, spread in a bright fan of printed pages across half a hundred yards of frozen white. She could read them from here—she had set every line, she could have read them upside down in the dark—verse about Saint Stephen and the frost fair and the price of tallow, and between the lines, in letters only the patient could find, the names of the dead of Marrow Lane and the quarry pits and the bridge riot, signed in effect by the hand of Magistrate Ambrose Halden. Forty pages. Lying in the open. Between her and eight armed men.

"It's nothing," she heard herself say. "It's paper. Paper can be re-set. We know the cipher, we—"

"No." Isandro was already turning back toward the ice. His face had gone very still, the way it went still before he did something foolish. "That's Corvel's confession in those pages. That's the proof that ties the almanac to the shop, the shop to the ledger, the ledger to Halden. Loose verses are a curiosity. Those pages, gathered, are a case. And Betto knows exactly what he's looking at."

"Then they'll gather them, not us."

"Then we gather them faster."

She caught his sleeve. "Isandro—"

But he was already moving, sliding down the bank onto the ice, and after one breath that felt like her whole life compressed into the space between heartbeats, Felix followed him.

* * *

### Isandro

Crawling across a frozen millrace under musket fire was, Isandro reflected, not the strangest use to which his army years had been put, but it ranked.

He moved flat on his belly, spreading his weight the way his father had taught him to cross spring ice with the ferry nets, elbows and knees, palms flat, head low. Twenty feet away a galley lay crumpled against a pressure ridge, snow gathering already in its folds of type. He reached it, folded it once, twice, slid it inside his coat against his chest. The metal was cold enough to burn.

From the bank above the mill, a voice—Betto's voice, high and cracking, younger than Isandro had imagined: "Don't shoot the pages, you idiots! Shoot the crawlers! *Low! Fire low!*"

The next volley came lower. He heard the strikes now not as skips but as punches, lead biting into ice around him, throwing up little white spouts like the ice trying to grow teeth. One passed so close he felt its wind on his neck. He kept crawling. Behind and beside him he could hear Felix doing the same, her breathing ragged but steady, and there was something in that steadiness—something in the fact that she was here at all, a woman who had never in her life been shot at, gathering testimony off a frozen river because it was hers, because her father's hands had taught her hands, because the dead deserved better than driftwood—he carried that steadiness with him the way he carried the galleys, against his chest, warming them.

A page. Another. A third had blown out toward the middle of the race, where the current ran deepest, and lay trembling on the thin dark ice like a moth on black water.

"Felix. Not that one."

"That one." Her voice was muffled against the ice. "It's the quarry pits. Every name."

"It's the deep channel—"

"Every *name*, Isandro."

She went out toward it while he gathered the nearer pages, and he watched her weight distribute, careful, careful, the ice flexing under her with a long whale-groan that silenced even the shooting for a moment. She stretched out full length, arm extended, fingers just touching the corner of the page—

The volley from the mill bank was ragged this time, panicked, and one ball found its mark.

Not her. Him. He had risen half onto his knees reaching for a galley pinned under a fold of ice, and the ball took him high on the left side, below the shoulder, and knocked him flat as a door slammed in the dark.

The pain arrived a moment later, enormous and strangely distant, like thunder after lightning. He lay on his back on the frozen race with the gray sky wheeling above him and snow beginning in earnest now, fat flakes descending unhurried, indifferent, and he thought with perfect clarity: *get up. Get up, you fool, she cannot gather forty pages alone.*

He got partway up.

Felix was suddenly there, dropping the quarry-pit page into her coat, her hands on him, pressing hard below his shoulder blade where the blood came through, hot enough to steam. "You're hit. You're hit, you're—"

"I know." His teeth had started chattering; the cold was finding the hole in him quickly. "Press it. Keep pressing. Gather with the other hand."

"I am not leaving you on this ice."

"You are going to drag me to that bank," he corrected, "and then you are going to finish gathering, because Betto is coming down onto the race right now, look—" and he tilted his head, and yes, there they were, four soldiers sliding carefully onto the ice from the mill side, and Betto among them in his borrowed red coat, small and hunched, already crawling.

So it became a race, the last one, the strangest of Isandro's life: two of them on their bellies on the south ice, five on their bellies on the north, crawling toward scattered pages through falling snow while the millrace ticked and boomed underneath them all like some vast impatient clock. Felix dragged him backward by the collar, one-armed, cursing him steadily in language that would have made her father wash her mouth with soap, stopping to seize pages whenever they passed within reach, pressing his wound with her knee, and Isandro laughed once—wetly, painfully—at the sheer ridiculous courage of it, and gathered two more galleys himself because his arms still worked and he refused to be cargo while there was work.

By the time they hauled up over the south bank into the alders, he held eleven galleys against his chest and Felix held thirteen, and two remained on the ice between the lines.

Betto reached one of them. Even across the distance Isandro saw the boy unfold it, read it, and freeze—the whole small body going rigid, because whatever Corvel had or hadn't told him, seeing it printed was different, seeing it *set in type* made it real in a way whispered names never were. The boy knelt on the ice a long moment with the page in his hands while the soldiers shouted at him.

The last galley lay near the deep channel, rocking faintly when the wind caught it.

"Leave it," Felix whispered. She had torn the lining from her own coat hem and was packing it into Isandro's wound, her fingers slick and red to the wrist. "Leave it, let them think—"

But the officer on the bank was shouting now, and Isandro heard the words come across the race clearly, because the wind had turned:

"—the fort! Seal the fort road! And send word ahead to the River Fort checkpoint: the Eastwick woman is here, *the Eastwick woman*, tell Halden she's coming to *him*—"

Felix's hands stopped moving.

Isandro lay in the alder scrub with his blood soaking into frozen leaves and the snow coming down soft and thick over the whole ruined valley, and he watched her face as the sentence landed, watched her understand what it meant: that the ambush had failed to take the pages, and succeeded in taking the one thing left to take. There would be no slipping through the final checkpoint now. There would be a warrant waiting, and a magistrate with her name already written in his book.

"There has to be another way through," she said at last. Her voice was steady. It was the steadiest thing in the world.

"There isn't," Isandro said. "There's only someone else wearing your coat."

She looked down at him then, and he watched her begin to refuse it—all of it, the whole shape of what was coming—and the snow fell on them both, and far out on the ice the last galley turned slowly in the wind like a leaf deciding which way to fall.
