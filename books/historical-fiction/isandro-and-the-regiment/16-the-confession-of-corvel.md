## 16. The Confession of Corvel

The cells beneath the Customhouse had been granaries once, in the years when Vellamont traded in grain instead of fear. Corvel knew this because he had set the type for the city's historical gazetteer, twenty years ago, and because knowing things was the last occupation left to him. He sat on a plank bed with a blanket that smelled of mildew and mice, and he listened to the river moving somewhere behind the stones, and he counted what remained of himself.

Not much. A man is a shop, he thought, and I have let mine be stripped to the bare walls.

They had taken his coat with its hidden pocket, his boots with the false heel, the little knife he had carried for thirty years and never once used for anything but trimming paper. They had taken his glasses first of all, which was either stupidity or cruelty, and he suspected it was neither—it was policy. A man who cannot read cannot destroy documents. He could still make out shapes at close range, the way a compositor can, holding letters to his nose, but the cell's one high window was only a grey coin of sky now, sometimes bright, sometimes not.

Betto had talked. Of course Betto had talked. Corvel had known from the moment the boy was pulled out of the shop, still wearing the leather apron Corvel had given him at fourteen, that the boy would talk. It was not treachery exactly; it was arithmetic. Betto had never been taught any sum but his own survival. Corvel thought of the evenings he had spent teaching the boy to justify a line, hand over hand on the composing stick, patient as a father, and he found that the memory did not sting anymore. It had gone past stinging into something flat and mineral, like lead itself.

He had been three days in the cell when the door opened and a clerk came down with a lantern and a sheaf of papers and the particular smile of a man carrying someone else's ruin.

"Master Corvel," the clerk said. "The magistrate sends his compliments."

"I doubt it," Corvel said. "Halden doesn't compliment. He itemizes."

The clerk laid the papers on the bed: the inventory of the shop, the transcript of Betto's statement—Corvel could read it only by lifting page after page nearly to his eyes—and, on top, a document drawn up in fair copy, awaiting signature. Confession and implication. His own crimes enumerated in careful legal prose, and beneath them, in a list that went on longer than his crimes, names. Names of couriers, of ferrymen, of readers. And near the bottom of the second page, in ink still wet enough to gleam: *the Eastwick girl.*

"The magistrate," the clerk said, "is prepared to be merciful. Sign, name your accomplices, authenticate nothing, and you will see spring from a window instead of a wall."

Corvel looked at the paper for a long time. He could smell it—the size in the rag stock, the iron-gall ink—and his hands ached with the old professional hunger to handle good paper properly, to fold it against the grain, to leave it better than it came to him. Thirty years he had printed other men's words. Some seditious, most merely stupid, all of them somebody's truth or somebody's lie, and he had told himself through all of it that ink was neutral, that the press served whoever paid, that a printer's conscience lived in his hands and not his heart.

That lie had kept him alive while braver men drowned in the river with their wrists bound.

"I'll want a pen," he said.

The clerk's smile widened. "Wise."

"And I'll want to write it myself. In my own words." He tapped the list of names with one finger. "I know more than your master thinks I do. Dates. Watermarks. Which of Halden's own clerks took delivery of certain crates in certain winters, and what was in the crates besides flour. If he wants my hand, he can have it—but the hand writes what I choose, or it writes nothing. Tell him a printer knows the difference between setting type and being set."

* * *

They let him have his own words. That was their mistake, though Corvel doubted they would ever understand it as one; men who believed everything was negotiable could not imagine that a negotiation might be conducted in a language they could not read.

What he wrote, in the long nights by the tallow candle the clerk grudgingly allowed, began as the confession Halden wanted. He gave it to them honestly enough at first: the pamphlets, the watermarks, the routes, the years of quiet sedition conducted under the cover of almanacs and psalters and a profitable trade in shipping invoices. Let them hang that around his neck. It was all true, and he was tired of the weight of it.

But then his pen—he had asked for a good one, and got it, because Halden's clerks feared him slightly and fear is generous—kept going past where the clerk's instructions ended.

*Item,* he wrote, *in the winter of '89 I printed at my own expense two hundred copies of a broadside concerning the deaths at Saltmarsh Wharf, and I will state under my own name, being a master printer and therefore competent to swear to the work of my own press, that the orders reproduced therein were true copies of documents which passed through my hands bearing the seal and signature of Magistrate Aldous Halden.*

*Item:* the ledger. He knew about the ledger now; he had heard the guards speak of it, had heard the word pass down the corridor like something contagious. The courier had died before talking, but Betto had described the type case, the loose sorts, the case Felix Eastwick had guarded like scripture. Corvel did not know where the ledger was. But he knew what it would contain, because he knew what Halden's clerks had burned and buried and bribed to forget, and he could swear to the pattern of it if not every page.

*I am an old man,* he wrote, near the end, *and I have spent my life making other people's words look true. This is the first time I have set down words that are true in themselves, and I find the work no different, only heavier. Let the weight fall where it belongs.*

He signed it. Then he did the thing he had decided on in the fourth night, staring at the grey coin of sky until it seemed to open: he composed, in the same fair hand, a second document—a shorter one, addressed to no court, sealed with a thread and folded the way printers fold proofs, twice lengthwise and once across—and he hid it where only another of the trade would think to look.

Then he waited for his cellmate.

* * *

The cellmate's name was Anselm, and he had been locked up for debt, which made him almost invisible in a prison full of seditionists. He was a former lay brother of the almshouse, defrocked for drinking the alms, and he had a voice like gravel in a sack and hands that were surprisingly fine for such a large man—hands that had copied out psalms for the sick, back when he was still pretending to virtue.

They played cards in the afternoons, on a blanket, with a deck missing its knaves. Corvel lost steadily and deliberately, small coins accumulating in Anselm's cap, and in exchange Anselm brought him news down the corridor, and gossip, and eventually—when Corvel judged the time right—he asked him about psalms.

"You were a copying man," Corvel said, shuffling the crippled deck. "Could you copy a psalm for me? From memory? My eyes are shot."

"Which one?"

"The hundred-and-thirtieth. Out of the depths." Corvel smiled without much humor. "It seems apt."

Anselm studied him across the cards. He was not a clever man, but he was not a fool, and prisoners learn to weigh requests like moneylenders. "You want it written?"

"I want it written well. On clean paper. Folded small. There's a woman comes with the bread carts on Saturdays—dark hair, works fast, doesn't talk." This much Corvel had learned from Anselm already, unprompted; the bread-cart girl was one of Anselm's small economies of information, offered early in their acquaintance like a coin flipped across a table. "She carries notes both ways, I'm told, for those who pay her. Give her this. Tell her it goes to anyone connected with the Eastwick print shop. Anyone at all. She'll find the way."

"And what do I tell her it is?"

"A psalm," Corvel said. "Folded inside a psalm. Even if she reads it, even if they take it from her, it's only a psalm. A dead man's devotions. Who hangs a woman for the hundred-and-thirtieth?" He pushed the pot of winnings toward Anselm. "And there's more where these came from, if you do it tonight."

Anselm took the pot. He hesitated, then, in a rare flicker of the brother he had maybe once been, asked quietly: "Is it true, what you've written? Whatever it is you're smuggling out in God's own words?"

"It's the truest thing I've ever set down," Corvel said. "That's why it has to go out in disguise. Truth travels badly in this city. It needs a false cover sheet."

* * *

Saturday came gray and smelling of snow. Corvel heard the cart before he saw anything—the scream of an axle, the clop of a half-lame horse—and he stood at the high window listening to bread being distributed to the cells along the row, counting doors away, until the loaf arrived at his own grate wrapped in a cloth that had been washed too many times and carried a laundry mark stitched in red thread at the corner.

A mark. Not just a mark—a letter. *F.* Or perhaps a fleuron; his eyes were too far gone to be sure. But his heart was sure, and his heart had been a compositor's heart for forty years, accurate about characters even when the eyes failed.

The psalm was there, in Anselm's heavy抄 hand, the *De profundis* copied out with a care that moved Corvel unexpectedly—the old drunkard had written it beautifully, the letters upright and humble, as if some long-buried devotion had surfaced in his fingers. And inside the fold of the third page, sewn flat into the gutter with thread pulled from the cloth itself, the second document. Corvel felt its edges with his fingertips before he ever dared unfold it: the specific thickness of the paper he had requested, folded the printer's way.

He did not unfold it. There was no need. He knew what it said, because he had written it, and he knew what it would do, because he had spent his whole life understanding what printed things do when they reach the right hands.

It would put his name beside Halden's. His name, sworn, public, authenticating the ledger's testimony—not the ledger itself, which might be anywhere, but the truth of it, attested by the one man in Vellamont whose trade made his oath worth something. A confession bought back too late to save his own neck; Halden would see him hanged for it regardless, would probably hurry the hanging along. But a dead man's sworn testimony, published, was a thing that outlived magistrates and amnesties alike. Ink was patient. Ink was the one mercy of the trade.

He stood at the window while the cart rattled away down toward the river gate, and he listened until the axle-scream faded under the sound of the water moving behind the walls.

Out of the depths, he thought. I have cried unto thee, O Lord. Well—the Lord had never answered Corvel. But a girl with dark hair who worked fast and didn't talk might. A girl who cut punches by lamplight in her dead father's shop, who had chosen her side while he had spent thirty years choosing none.

"Forgive me, Betto," he said aloud to the empty cell, because the boy deserved to hear it even if the boy would never come. "And forgive me, old Eastwick. I kept your daughter's name off my lists for three days. It was the bravest thing I ever did, and it wasn't nearly enough."

The grey coin of sky darkened toward evening. Somewhere above, bells were practicing for the New Year, and Corvel, who had set the type for a thousand almanacs predicting honest weather and honest harvests, leaned his forehead against the cold stone and waited to learn whether his last composition would arrive in time—or whether the world outside those walls would close over it like water.

Three checkpoints, the guards gossiped. One river fort. One warrant for the Eastwick girl.

Corvel pressed his palms together in the dark, an old habit from a life he'd barely led, and prayed the only way a printer could: that his words would run faster than Halden's men.
