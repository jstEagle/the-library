## 17. Checkpoint Three: The River Fort

The river fort at Marrow's Reach had been a toll house once, back when the river was only a river and not a border. Felix could see the bones of it under the skin of the new regime — the old arched windows bricked to slits, the slate roof weighted with stones against the wind, and the flag snapping above it all, gray with frost-rime, too stiff to show whatever device it carried. Beyond it, on the far bank, she could just make out the smoke of Harrow's Lane, where the publisher's press stood behind a shuttered chandler's shop. Half a mile of water. A hundred yards of frozen mud. And between her and it, Magistrate Halden, inspecting travelers by torchlight in the falling dusk.

They lay in the reeds of an osier bed a quarter mile downstream, where the willow whips grew thick as hair and the ground was soft enough to muffle even Isandro's careful boots. Felix had learned, over these weeks, to read the terrain the way he did: not as scenery but as sentence — here the cover runs out, here the sightline opens, here is where you would die. She hated how fluently she had come to speak it.

"He's brought a table," Isandro said. He had been watching through a gap in the whips for a long time without moving. "A table, and a chair, and a brazier. He wants to be seen. He wants everyone on both banks to watch him turn the city inside out before midnight."

Through the bare branches they watched him. Halden sat wrapped in sables that made him look less like a magistrate than like something the winter had coughed up, his hands folded on the table before him with the patience of a man who had never once in his life been cold. Soldiers moved travelers along in a line that snaked from the fort down the bank and out of sight among the hulks of moored barges. Torches guttered in iron baskets. The wind came off the ice in long shoves, carrying fragments — a child crying, an officer's bored shout, the rasp of the flag.

"There," Isandro said quietly.

An officer standing beside the table held up a paper so that the torchlight caught it, and though the words were lost across the water, Felix knew what it was. She had known since Livia's laundry-mark warning, since Betto's name had entered their calculations like a splinter working toward the heart. A warrant. For *the Eastwick girl*. Her father's name spoken in Halden's mouth, her own face described on official paper — she had heard it secondhand from a bargeman who'd heard it from a washerwoman, and the description was already legend on both banks: *dark hair cut short like a boy's, ink-stained fingers, walks with her chin up.*

*Walks with her chin up.* Her father again, in everything now, even her betrayals.

"They're not checking papers tonight," Isandro said. "They're checking faces."

Felix pulled the decoy type case into her lap. It had cost them two days in a barn outside Millrace while Isandro's wound suppurated and a farrier with kind eyes and no questions stitched him; two days in which Felix had built, with her own hands, a case of type that would pass any inspection in Vellamont. Real sorts, real galleys, real almanac verse set line by line in her father's pica — harmless weather rhymes, saints' days, advice for the pruning of orchards. Under the tray, sewn flat as a pressed flower between two boards of pear wood, nothing. The ledger itself rode against her ribs, bound in oilcloth under her shawl, warm from her body, heavier than lead has any right to be.

But the warrant changed the arithmetic. They were not looking for a courier tonight. They were looking for *her*, and the coat she wore — her father's greatcoat, dyed and resewn, the one thing in all the world that still smelled like him — had been described to them by Betto down to its horn buttons.

"Isandro." Her voice came out strange, flattened by wind. "Say what you're thinking. You've been thinking it since the barn."

He turned from the gap in the whips. In the last gray light his face looked carved, older than his years, the scar at his temple silver-white now instead of pink. He had almost died on the millrace ice for these pages; he had crawled on his belly gathering them while pellets sang off the frozen race like hail off a coffin lid. He had never once complained, which frightened her more than complaining would have.

"You know what I'm thinking," he said.

"Say it anyway."

"Halden knows your face. He doesn't know mine — I was discharged eight years ago, half-starved, before he took the bench. My papers say a dead man's name. Your coat is famous now, but coats can change owners." He paused, and the wind filled the pause with the small dry music of the reeds. "Let me wear it through."

"No."

"Felix—"

"No." She heard her own voice crack on it and hated that too. "You've taken a beating for me at the customhouse. You've wrecked a cart for me at the toll gate. You've taken shot for me on the ice. That's three times, Isandro. There isn't a fourth. There can't be — people don't survive their own generosity."

"That's not arithmetic," he said. "That's fear doing sums."

"Then let it. Fear has kept us alive this far." She gripped the decoy case until her knuckles whitened. "If they take me, they take the ledger and it dies with me. If they take you—"

"If they take me," he said, very gently, the way one speaks to someone standing on a ledge, "they take a discharged soldier with no family name worth the hangman's trouble and no papers that can hurt anyone. The warrant says *the Eastwick girl*. I am, demonstrably, not her. I walk through wearing your coat, carrying the decoy, being loud and wrong in every particular — and every eye at that checkpoint goes to me, because I am the interesting one, and you walk past in the dark on the ice road like a woman going home from market."

"And if they arrest you?"

"Then the amnesty comes at midnight and burns every charge in the drawer." He smiled, and it was the old crooked smile, but something new lived underneath it, something that made her chest ache. "Eight hours, Felix. Even Halden can't make a charge outlast the year."

"He can convert charges. Livia told us—he told us himself, when we thought Betto was the worst of it—"

"He can try."

"He *will*." She was shaking now, and not from cold. "Isandro, listen to me. Men like Halden do not lose gracefully. If he cannot have my body he will take yours out of spite, and the amnesty won't save you, because men like Halden write the interpretations too. I have read enough law set in type to know that the law is just another font, and whoever owns the press decides what it says."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Downriver, the line of travelers shuffled forward. Somewhere a soldier laughed. The brazier at the table flared in a gust, and Halden's shadow leapt enormous against the fort wall, a giant with folded hands.

"I refused an order once," Isandro said. His voice had gone somewhere far away. "To fire on strikers at the salt works. My captain gave the order four times. I counted. Four." He looked at his own hands, the ferryman's hands, the rope-calloused hands. "I have spent eight years learning what that refusal cost me, and I would pay it again, and here is the thing I have never told anyone: I didn't refuse for them. Not only. I refused because some part of me knew, even then, that I was going to meet you someday, or someone like you, and I wanted to be a man you could stand beside. Every choice since the ice has been that same choice, made again. Let me finish making it."

"You're asking me to watch you walk into a noose and call it love."

"I'm asking you to let me carry the heavy end. Just once. To the door." He reached out and touched the collar of the greatcoat, the famous horn buttons. "Besides — it will fit me badly. Everyone will know it isn't mine. That's the point. I'll be wearing the wrong coat, carrying the wrong case, answering to the wrong story, and Halden will be so busy proving I'm not the Eastwick girl that he'll forget to wonder who is."

She wanted to strike him. She wanted to hold him. She did neither; she sat in the freezing reeds with the ledger burning against her ribs and understood, with terrible clarity, that he was right, and that being right was the cruelest thing he had ever done to her.

"If they hold you past midnight," she said, "I'll come back for you."

"I know."

"If they hurt you—"

"They won't. Beaten men are boring, Felix. Boring is safe." He took off his cap and ran a hand through his hair, and then, matter-of-factly, began unbuttoning his own jacket. "Give me the coat. The tide of travelers turns at full dark; after that the line thins and stragglers look like stragglers. We go with the crowd."

The sky had gone the color of a bruise fading toward morning. Felix undid the greatcoat's horn buttons one by one, and each button was a small defeat, and she made herself feel every one of them, because she refused to let this be easy — for either of them. When she shrugged it off, the cold hit her like water, and beneath it the ledger seemed to pulse against her ribs like a second heart.

Isandro put on her father's coat. It hung short at his wrists and strained across his shoulders, and he looked, as promised, exactly like a man wearing another man's coat — which was somehow unbearable, and somehow perfect. He settled the decoy case under his arm and tapped its lid once, gently, the way a printer taps a galley to check the lockup.

"Now," he said. "The ice road runs from the willow yard to the middle span. Stay low, walk like your feet hurt, and don't stop for anything — not shouting, not shots, not me. You reach the far bank, you go up Harrow's Lane to the chandler's, you knock twice and once. And Felix—" He stopped. In the last light his eyes were very clear. "When it's done. Print your name. Big."

* * *

The line at the fort moved like a wound digesting. Isandro joined it at the tail, where the torchlight was thinnest, and felt the greatcoat settle around him like a borrowed argument. It smelled of ink and lamp oil and, faintly, of pipe tobacco long smoked out, and he understood that Felix had not exaggerated its fame: even the soldiers' eyes snagged on it, on the horn buttons, on the cut of the shoulders. Good. Let them look. Let them build whole certainties out of a coat.

The decoy case bumped his hip with each step, real lead singing softly inside it. Ahead, the table, the chair, the brazier, the man in sables. Closer, and he could see Halden's face at last — smaller than legend, paler, with the bloodless composure of a man who has spent decades signing other people's deaths and considers it tidiness rather than cruelty. The warrant lay on the table under a stone. Isandro did not look at it. He looked, instead, at the officer holding it, and rehearsed his wrongness: a ferryman's son with discharge papers under a dead name, carrying almanac verse in a woman's famous coat, walking with a limp he did not have and a guilt he did not own.

Ten travelers ahead. Five. Two. The brazier's heat rolled over him, almost pleasant after the river wind, and he thought, absurdly, of Livia's hands kneading bread in the dark of their kitchen, and of Felix's hands setting type, and of how strange and lucky it was that hands could learn tenderness at all in a century that taught them mostly gripping.

"Name." The officer did not glance up.

"Tomas Ferro," Isandro said. "Carrying type for a country printer. New Year's job."

Halden raised his head.

It was like watching a well open. The magistrate's eyes went first to the coat — Isandro actually saw the recognition arrive, saw the pupils sharpen — then to the case, then to Isandro's face, and traveled over it slowly, thoroughly, the way a man reads a document he intends to dispute.

"The Eastwick girl," Halden said, "has short hair and ink-stained fingers and walks with her chin up."

"Then I'm fortunate," Isandro said, "to be none of those things."

Someone behind the table laughed and choked it. Halden did not smile. He lifted the warrant, unfolded it with two fingers, and set it down again facing Isandro like a card.

"Turn around."

Isandro turned. The silence behind him stretched; he counted heartbeats the way he had once counted orders. At four, Halden spoke again.

"The coat."

"Bought off a rag cart in Millrace."

"The buttons are horn. Cut for a woman's shoulders."

"My sister resells. She doesn't discriminate."

For a moment the wind owned everything — the flag cracking, the torches hissing, the river muttering under its ice. Then Halden nodded to the officers flanking the table, almost lazily.

"Take him. The coat too. If the Eastwick girl is fool enough to come to my checkpoint, she is also fool enough to send her coat ahead of her." His gaze slid past Isandro, out over the dark water, patient as frost. "Hold him past midnight. Sedition converts nicely into the new year — I have written opinions on the subject myself."

The soldiers' hands closed on Isandro's arms. He did not resist. He let the decoy case be lifted from him, let it be opened on the table, let Halden read saints' days and orchard-pruning verse by torchlight with growing, magnificent irritation. And as they turned him toward the fort's black doorway, Isandro looked back once — not at Halden, not at the soldiers, but out at the river, at the low pale seam of the ice road running to the far bank, empty now, and dark, and free.

*Walk like your feet hurt,* he thought, and smiled, and let the door swallow him.

* * *

Felix did not stop for shouting, and she did not stop for shots — there were none, which was worse, which left her imagination alone in the dark with the whole arsenal of the world. She crossed the ice road bent and limping among eleven other travelers, a woman with aching feet going home from market, the ledger riding her ribs like a stolen sun. The far bank rose to meet her, black willows and the chandler's shuttered windows and, above the lane, a single lit window where a press waited to be fed.

At the top of Harrow's Lane she stopped once, only once, and looked back. Across the river the fort's torches still burned. The door was closed. Somewhere behind it, wearing her father's coat, Isandro was counting heartbeats.

Eight hours to midnight. Eight hours to the amnesty — and Halden's voice was already in her ear, calm as frost: *sedition converts nicely into the new year.*

She knocked twice, and once, and the bolt drew back.
