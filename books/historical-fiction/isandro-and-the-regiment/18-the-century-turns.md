## 18. The Century Turns

The last day of the year came down gray and hard, the sky pressed flat as hammered pewter over Vellamont, and Felix Eastwick woke before dawn on the floor of a dyer's loft with the ledger's galleys strapped against her ribs and the taste of iron in her mouth.

She had not slept well. She rarely did now. Sleep had a way of loosening her hands from the case of harmless-looking almanac verse she carried everywhere, and some part of her had learned to keep its grip even dreaming. So she lay in the dark listening to the city breathe beneath her—the groan of cart wheels on frozen cobbles, a dog somewhere near the tanneries, the long slow exhale of the river under its lid of ice—and she counted what remained. One day. One checkpoint. Half a mile of streets between this loft and Marchetti's press behind the rope-walk.

Half a mile, and Isandro walking it in her coat.

She dressed without light. The coat she gave up willingly—it was only wool, only dye, only a thing—but when she lifted it from the hook her hands remembered the weight of him in it, the breadth of his shoulders filling out seams cut for a narrower frame, and she stood a moment in the cold dark holding the empty shape of what he had done. Then she folded it over her arm and went down the stairs into the last morning of the old century.

* * *

Vellamont wore its strange holiday face. New Year's Eve, and the amnesty with it: by midnight every warrant unsigned by the new assizes would burn, every pending charge dissolve like frost on glass. Halden's criers had been at it since yesterday at the market cross, a boy with a cracked bell proclaiming *grace, grace, full and free grace*, while the magistrates' clerks fed armfuls of paper into braziers outside the Customhouse and the smoke rolled down Water Street smelling of everyone's ruined lives. Debtors walked out of the Fleet grinning like men concussed. A woman who had spent eleven years charged over a bakers' riot wept openly in the fish market and no one looked away from her, because half the city was doing the same.

It was a kindness, on the surface of it. It was meant to look like one.

Felix moved through it all with her collar up and the galleys tight against her, and she watched mercy fall on Vellamont like snow that blessed every roof equally—the arsonist's roof and the orphanage alike—and she felt the thing underneath her breastbone that had been growing since the millrace twist another quarter turn.

Grace for the smugglers. Grace for the debtors. Grace for the girl whose name was currently sitting in a cell under the river fort, wearing another woman's coat and answering—had answered, these three days—to the charge of *sedition, in the person of Felix Eastwick*.

Grace, come midnight, even for him.

But for Halden? The warrant that named Magistrate Aurel Halden as author of the massacres at Coldbridge and the Saltings and the drowned lane at Merrow—that warrant existed nowhere but sewn into almanac verse, strapped to her ribs. And when the bells rang and the century turned, the law would reach out with both its hands: one to open Isandro's cell, and one to close over Halden's shoulder, patting it gently, saying *all is forgiven, all was never proven, go home to your warm house.*

She bought bread she could not taste and ate it standing in a doorway, watching the Customhouse burn its own records.

* * *

She went once, at midday, to the place where she could see the river fort.

There was no approaching it; she had known there wouldn't be. The walls rose from the island midstream, gray stone going darker where the water stained them, and the ice had been broken around it in a wide black channel so that the fort sat moored in its own moat. Smoke stood straight up from the guardhouse chimney in the windless cold. She stood on the far bank among the winter-dead reeds, an ordinary woman watching the river, and she let herself imagine the interior with the terrible precision of someone who had spent her life imagining what lay inside locked things.

A cell. A cot. A small high window with the gray light coming through it sideways. And Isandro—was he standing at that window now? Was he watching the same smoke, the same flat sky? Did he know she had made it across the millrace country with the pages whole? She had sent no word; word was danger. He had walked into the fort wearing her name the way a man walks into deep water, deliberately, without looking back, and the last thing he had said to her was *go*, just that, his hand flat between her shoulder blades, pushing her toward the towpath while he turned to face the lanterns.

Three days. If they had beaten him—and they beat people at the fort, everyone knew it, the way everyone knew the depth of the river without ever having sounded it—then somewhere under that straight gray smoke was a man with split lips who still knew nothing worth telling except that Felix Eastwick was free, and free was exactly what he had paid for her to be.

"You can't get him out," she said aloud to the reeds, to the river, to herself. "You can get the truth out. That has to be enough. Make it be enough."

The smoke went on rising. She turned her back on it and walked toward Marchetti's.

* * *

Marchetti's press hid behind the rope-walk the way honest work hides behind duller honest work—a long low building that smelled of hemp and size, with a rope-maker's sign bolted over the door and a printer's sign nowhere at all. Felix had carried the address in her head for six weeks, spoken once by a dying man with his hand on a case of type, and now she knocked on the rope-walk door in the pattern the courier's ledger had specified—two, pause, three—and waited while her heart performed its slow heavy gymnastics.

The door opened on a chain. An eye in a seam of shadow. Then the chain rattled off and a small stooped man with ink worked permanently into the creases of his knuckles pulled her inside.

"You're late," said Marchetti.

"The roads are late," Felix said. "The century is late. Everything is late."

He took her measure in one long look—the frostbite healing on two fingers, the shadows under her eyes, the way she stood with her shoulders curled protectively inward like a woman carrying coals in her apron. Then he jerked his chin toward the back and said, "Composing stone's warm. Don't touch anything."

It was the kindest thing anyone had said to her in a month.

* * *

The hours until midnight she spent working, because work was the only prayer she knew. Marchetti read the first galley through twice, his lips moving, his eyebrows climbing slowly toward his hairline, and when he finished the second reading he set it down very carefully, the way her father had handled finished punches, and said nothing for the better part of a minute.

"These names," he said finally. "Coldbridge. The Saltings. You're certain of the orders?"

"I'm certain of the signature. I've studied Halden's hand since I was twelve—he buys his paper from a stationer two doors from our shop and I've cut facsimiles of his seal for practice." She heard her own voice, level and dry, and marveled a little at it. "The ledger is his. Corvel will authenticate it publicly if he lives to stand anywhere public. There's a confession besides, folded in a psalm, and it names the dates."

"Corvel." Marchetti's mouth tightened. "They took him Tuesday. He'll hang anyway, amnesty or no—they'll find charges with fresh dates on them. They always can, when they want a man dead." He rubbed his ink-blackened thumb along the edge of the galley, and something in his face was very old and very tired and very angry. "And you understand what the amnesty means. For Halden."

"I understand."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear the woman carrying this paper say it."

Felix looked at the composing stone, at the galleys lying there in their innocent disguise, lines of almanac verse that held forty-one names of dead men stitched inside them like shot inside a dove.

"At midnight," she said, "the law forgives him. Everything. The massacres stop being crimes and become misunderstandings. He keeps his office, his house, his name in the church register. And this—" her hand flattened on the lead, cold even in the warm room, "—this becomes not evidence, only gossip. Libel, maybe. They could prosecute *me* for printing it fresh, if they wanted a joke."

"Yes," Marchetti said. "That's the shape of it."

"Then we print it tonight, and after midnight it isn't a prosecution anymore. It's a verdict. Ours instead of theirs."

The old printer studied her a moment longer. "Your father's daughter," he said at last, and turned to the case.

* * *

The city's bells began at half past eleven.

They were meant to begin at midnight, but Vellamont had waited a hundred years for this turning and could not wait the extra half hour—all down the river the towers opened their throats early, St. Aubin's and the Chapel of the Waters and the great cracked bell of the Customhouse itself, and the sound poured through the streets like floodwater, overlapping, quarreling, magnificent. Felix stood in the open yard behind Marchetti's with frost forming in her eyelashes and listened to a century die loudly and badly, out of tune, too soon, exactly on time.

People came out of their doorways. She saw lamplighters abandon their poles, saw neighbors embracing across fences, saw a knot of soldiers from the fort garrison standing awkwardly at the corner of the rope-walk with their muskets grounded and their young faces uncertain—men whose warrants were burning even now, men made clean by calendar. Somewhere a fiddler started up. Somewhere a woman sang. The smoke of the record-burning had settled over everything, that papery ash-sweetness riding the cold air, and the whole city breathed it in like incense.

*Forgiveness*, the bells said. *Forgiveness, forgiveness, forgiveness.*

For whom? Felix thought, and the thought rang in her harder than any bell.

At the stroke of twelve—measured by Marchetti's watch, because the bells had already spent themselves—the compositor began setting the first true page. Felix stood beside him and fed him the decoded testimony line by line, her voice steady under the din, and the names came off her tongue one after another into the waiting type: the miller from Coldbridge, the three brothers from the Saltings, the boy from the drowned lane at Merrow whose body had never been given back. Forty-one names. Each one locked into lead as it left her mouth, lettered, spaced, justified—made permanent the only way she knew how to make anything permanent.

Her father had taught her that lead remembers. People forget; paper burns; but a good punch struck into copper gives you the same letter ten thousand times and never once gets it wrong. She had spent her grief on that fact for two years, and now she spent it again, gladly, feeding the names into metal while the century turned overhead.

When the last name was set and the forme locked up, Marchetti pulled the first proof himself and handed it to her wet, the ink still shining, and Felix held the truth of Magistrate Aurel Halden in her two hands and found she was crying without any of the usual warning, tears freezing at her jaw, and she did not make any sound about it. The bells were done. The fiddler was done. Only the press breathed in the yard, and the compositor whistling softly at his work, and the future arriving at four hundred impressions an hour.

*Triumph*, she thought. This is what triumph feels like: cold, and wet, and not nearly tall enough to see over.

* * *

Marchetti's boy came at two in the morning, out of breath, with news the way news travels in Vellamont—garbled, overheated, half right.

"They've turned him out," the boy panted. "The fort. At midnight, on the dot—the amnesty, see, they had to. But it's gone wrong, mistress, it's gone proper wrong—"

Felix's whole body came up off the composing stone. "Isandro. Isandro Ferro, arrested as Felix Eastwick—is he out? Is he—"

"He's out, he's walking, they say he's headed for the bridge. But listen—" the boy caught her arm, and his young face was frightened, "—they didn't void the charge, they *converted* it. Before releasing him. The magistrate's clerk did it special, quick, before the cutoff. Old charge dies at midnight, right, so they wrote him a new one. Sedition—fresh sedition, dated today. Printing and dispersing seditious libel, in the person of Felix Eastwick." The boy swallowed. "The warrant's brand-new. It don't burn till next New Year's."

The room went very quiet. Even the press seemed to hold its stroke.

Felix stood with the wet proof sheet still in her hand, the ink cooling to black, forty-one names drying into permanence under her fingers, and she understood with perfect clarity the shape of what Halden had done—not in spite of the amnesty, but *inside* it, elegantly, the way he did everything: he had taken her name, which he could not yet touch, and hung it around Isandro like a millstone, and set the clock running for a full new year. Every hour she stayed free made him guiltier. Every page this press printed tied the knot tighter.

He was not forgiven. He had simply arranged to be untouchable while the man who carried her name walked the streets with a fresh warrant on his head.

"Well," Felix said at last, and her voice came out low and even and dangerous, the voice she used at the bench when a punch refused to cut true. "Then we'd best print fast."

Marchetti looked at her across the warm stone, at the forme locked and waiting, at the girl with the frozen tears and the ink-stained hands, and something like grim delight moved behind his tired eyes.

"And after it's printed?" he asked. "When the city's read every name and Halden sits safe behind his amnesty with your warrant out on that ferryman's son—what then, Eastwick?"

Felix laid the proof sheet flat on the stone and smoothed it with both palms, once, the way her father had smoothed a sheet before pulling the first impression of anything that mattered.

"Then I'm going to find the man wearing my name," she said, "and I am going to give it back."
