## 19. Print It Anyway

The press house of Aldous Renner stood on Tanners' Row, where the river fog came up through the floorboards in winter and the smell of hides and lye had soaked into the beams so thoroughly that even the paper smelled faintly of tallow. Felix had walked past it a hundred times in her life without once going in. Now she stood at its door with a psalm folded in her pocket, Corvel's confession pressed against her ribs like a second heart, and the true ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath her cloak, and she was afraid of the man inside more than she had been of Halden himself.

Halden, at least, had been an enemy. Renner was only tired.

He let her in by the side door, glancing at the street both ways before he barred it behind her. He was sixty, maybe older, with ink gone permanently into the creases of his knuckles and spectacles he polished constantly, a nervous tic that had worn two little silvered patches into his waistcoat where the cloth rubbed against them. The composing room beyond him was dark but for one lamp over the stone, and the presses stood silent under their sheets like animals sleeping through bad weather.

"You're the Eastwick girl," he said.

"I'm the Eastwick typesetter," Felix said. "There's a difference tonight."

He did not laugh. She hadn't expected him to. He lit a candle from the lamp and led her back to his office, a closet of a room papered with old proofs—ballads, almanacs, a shipping list, forty years of harmless work layered like sediment—and there he took Corvel's confession from her hands and read it twice, slowly, moving his lips around the worst names as if tasting something gone off.

"The magistrate's own signature," he said at last. "Witnessed twice."

"And the ledger." She unwrapped it on his desk. "Orders, requisitions for powder, payment tallies for the men who fired. Every massacre at Millbrook, at Corder's Field, at the Salt Wharf. Names of the dead beside names of the paid."

Renner looked at the pages for a long time. Then he closed the ledger gently, the way a man closes a door on a sickroom, and sat down heavily on his stool.

"Do you know what tomorrow is," he said. It wasn't a question. "Midnight comes, and the amnesty with it. Every charge pending in this province burns. Halden's included. His men's included." He tapped the ledger's cover with one ink-stained finger. "This is evidence of nothing now. There will be no trial. There cannot be. The King's grace has already forgiven what happened here, whether anyone in Vellamont consented to be forgiven or not."

"Then printing it costs you nothing legally."

"It costs me everything else." He took off his spectacles and rubbed his eyes. "Renner prints almanacs. Renner prints sermons for two parishes that don't agree with each other, and neither has ever complained. I have survived thirty years in this city by being beneath everyone's notice, Miss Eastwick. You are asking me to put my name—the shop's name—on the most scandalous document this province has seen in a generation. Not to see justice done. There'll be none. Just to see shame done. And shame has longer arms than any magistrate."

"The dead can't be tried either," Felix said. Her voice came out low and level, which surprised her; inside she felt the way the river looked in January, ice moving over black water. "You said it yourself—there'll be no trial. This ledger is the only courtroom they'll ever get. If we burn it or bury it, then Halden wins twice. Once for the killing, once for the quiet after."

Renner put his spectacles back on and looked at her over their rims. "And your soldier? The one wearing your name in the fort? Does he win anything from ink?"

The question was meant to wound, and it found its mark, because Isandro was the reason she had not slept in four days and the reason she could not stop now. But she made herself answer it plainly.

"He didn't do what he did so that I could come here and be talked out of it. He did it so the pages would arrive. They've arrived." She laid her hand flat on the ledger. "Print it, Mr. Renner. Or stand aside, and I'll find another way—but I promise you there is no other press between here and the border that isn't watched, and every hour we spend arguing is an hour closer to Betto finding this room."

At the name Betto, something moved in the old man's face—not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing the bill come due for debts he'd hoped were forgotten.

* * *

He did not refuse. That was the shape of it, when she thought about it later: not refusal, just erosion, the slow collapse of a man's careful life under the weight of a thing he couldn't unsee. He read the confession a third time. He sent his apprentice home—he had only the one boy left since the raid on Corvel's shop, a stuttering lad of fourteen named Pell—and then he stood at the window watching the empty street until full dark, and finally he said:

"The forme is yours. The stock is yours. I'll open the shop and pay the boy and keep my mouth shut, and when they come—and they'll come, Miss Eastwick, amnesty or no, because scandal bites harder than law—I'll say you forced your way in and I was too old to stop you."

"That's a coward's story," Felix said.

"It's a survivor's story," Renner said. "I've been telling it to myself for thirty years. It wears a groove." He turned from the window at last. "But I'll tell you one true thing tonight, since I'm spending my courage all in one place. My father printed seditious material in the year of the bread riots. They broke his hands for it. Both of them. He set type afterward by holding the composing stick between his wrists, and he never printed anything again, and he never forgave himself, and on his deathbed he asked me whether I thought God kept accounts of things left unsaid." Renner smiled, small and terrible. "I have wondered about that question for forty years. Set your type, girl. Let's find out what the answer weighs."

* * *

She began at half past nine.

First she read the whole ledger through, standing at the composing stone with the pages spread before her, because she would not set one letter of testimony she had not read entire. She had carried the thing across a frozen country; she had crawled on her knees on river ice gathering its disguised sisters while lead flew. She owed it the dignity of being read straight through, all of it, the way a priest reads a confession without stopping to weep.

It was worse in full. Reading page by page over weeks, as she had on the road, let each horror sit alone in its compartment. Read end to end, the ledger became arithmetic. Three hundred and eleven names of the dead, entered in Halden's neat clerical hand alongside sums disbursed: *to the sergeant, for powder expended, 12 livres; to the widow-maker Ollier, consideration rendered, 30 livres.* Consideration rendered. As if the widows were customers receiving goods.

Felix discovered she had stopped seeing the letters as letters. That frightened her, because a compositor who stops seeing letters is a compositor who makes errors, and errors in this document were unforgivable—a transposed date, a misspelled name, and the whole pamphlet could be dismissed as the work of a liar. So she did what her father had taught her to do on the hardest days, when grief sat on the bench beside her and her hands wanted to shake. She worked the ritual.

Wash the hands. Trim the lamp. Square the case.

Her father's voice, in the old cadence: *The type doesn't know you're sad, Lixie. The type only knows if you're careful.* And after: *Careful is a kind of love. Most people never learn that.*

She set the first line at eleven minutes past ten o'clock.

*To the Honourable Magistrate Aurelien Halden, from the commissary of the district of Vellamont, being a true account—*

The letters came to her hand the way they had come since she was nine years old, without searching, each sort in its compartment like a word in a mind. She had spent her whole life learning where every character lived, and now the knowledge served the dead. Brass rule. Leads between the lines. Quoins tightened at the corners of the chase until the forme rang faintly when she rapped it with the knuckle of her thumb—a good sound, a locked and honest sound, her father's sound.

Outside, the bells of Saint-Aubin rang eleven.

Pell came back near midnight with bread and a jug of small beer and stood in the doorway of the composing room with his eyes enormous, until Renner shooed him to bed in the loft. Renner himself did not sleep. He pulled a stool to the far end of the stone and corrected proofs as she produced them, reading each galley against the ledger with a scholar's frown, and somewhere past one in the morning he began, quietly, without announcing it, to help—pulling sorts, spacing lines, his old hands quick and sure, and neither of them spoke about it, because speaking would have made it a decision again instead of simply work, and work they could both survive.

* * *

Somewhere past three, her hands began to fail her.

It was the cold, partly—Tanners' Row got no sun in winter and the composing room held its chill like a grudge—and it was the hour, the dead hollow of the night when the body keeps its own accounts. Her fingertips went numb around the sorts. Twice she dropped a line and had to redistribute it, letters pattering back into their compartments like cold rain. And in the pause after the second spill, sitting very still with her hands in her lap, she heard the silence of the room become enormous, and in it she heard Isandro's voice saying, on the night in the snowbound inn, with the refugees snoring around them and the fire down to embers:

*My whole life I've been told where to stand and when to fire. Discharge papers said I showed poor judgment. Poor judgment means I wouldn't shoot a miller holding his daughter in front of him as a shield. That's what they call poor judgment, Felix.*

And then, softer, the thing he had said with his face turned away: *You cut letters. Letters last. Somebody has to be the one whose work lasts.*

She got up, stamped feeling into her feet, and heated a brick at Renner's stove for her hands. Then she went back to the stone and set the entry for Corder's Field.

Ninety-one names. She set every one of them, spelled out in full, because abbreviations were for clerks and these were people. Somewhere in the middle of the third column her eyes blurred and a *b* nearly went where an *h* belonged—Berthe Collonges, aged six—and she caught it, caught it because careful is a kind of love, and she stood there in the lamplight with the tiny letter pinched in her tweezers and understood, finally and completely, what her hands were for.

Not punches for other men's names. Not invisible labor behind a curtain of someone else's imprint. This. Lead and pressure and repetition. A court of metal type, in session all night, with the dead called as witnesses one name at a time.

The law had chosen forgiveness. Fine. Forgiveness was the law's business. Verdicts were hers.

* * *

Dawn found the forme finished and locked, all eight pages, standing on the press bed like a table laid for the last supper of the old year. Renner had dozed in his chair; he woke when the light changed in the windows, gray and grainy, and came to stand beside her at the press. Through the floorboards, faintly, came the sound of the city waking—carts, a dog, someone singing badly—and underneath everything, distant and irregular, the bells of Saint-Aubin rehearsing for the New Year.

"Pull a proof," Renner said hoarsely. "Before we commit. I want to see it in ink."

She inked the balls and pulled the proof herself, one clean impression on good paper, and they stood looking at it together while the ink dried: the title, the confession, the tally of the dead, and at the foot of the last page, where an imprint belonged—

*CUT AND SET BY F. EASTWICK, OF THE OLD EASTWICK SHOP, VELLAMONT.*

Renner read it. Read it again. "You understand what that name on the page does. Halden's warrant died with the amnesty, but the name doesn't. Every copy that leaves this door carries you."

"I know," Felix said. "That's why it's there."

The old printer looked at her for a long moment, and then he did something she had not seen him do all night: he laughed, short and rusty, like a gate opening after years shut. "Corvel told me once you were the best pair of hands on this side of the river. He undersold you." He squared his shoulders, wincing. "Well. Press is yours. How many?"

"As many as the paper allows. All of it."

They were hanging the first wet sheets along the drying lines when Pell came tumbling down the loft stairs, still buttoning his breeches, hair on end.

"Monsieur Renner—there's people at the corner. Watching the shop. A boy and a grown man, and the grown man—" The stutter got worse. "He's got the look of the ones that came to Master Corvel's. Soldiers' look, but no uniform."

Felix crossed to the window, careful to stand to one side of the glass, and looked down Tanners' Row through the early gray.

Two figures by the corner post. Unhurried. Watching. The taller one turned his head just enough for her to catch the profile, and her stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world, because she knew that face—soft-chinned, sharp-eyed, a face she had last seen leading soldiers across the ice toward a frozen millrace.

Betto had found the route after all.
