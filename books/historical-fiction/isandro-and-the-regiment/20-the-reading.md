## 20. The Reading

The pamphlets came into Vellamont the way water comes into a cellar—quietly, from below, finding every crack.

Felix heard it first from the fishwife who came to buy a handbill announcing the market days. The woman counted her coins, tucked the bill into her shawl, and then leaned across the counter with the conspiratorial breath of someone who has already decided to tell you something wonderful.

"They're saying there's a paper," she said. "Came in on the morning boats, hundreds of them, stuffed in with the herring crates. Names and dates. Halden's names. Halden's dates." Her eyes were bright and frightened at once, the particular shine of a person holding something that could burn her hands. "They say the magistrate himself signed the orders."

"Who says?" Felix asked, though she knew. She had set every line of it herself, had washed the ink from her fingers at dawn while the pressman's boy slept against the wall, had watched the last sheet come off the stone still warm as bread.

"Everyone," the fishwife said, and left.

By noon the city knew. By evening, the bridge knew.

* * *

Isandro heard the crowd before he saw it.

He had come down to the ferry stairs to mend a gunwale split by the winter ice, his sister's mended cloak around his shoulders and the ache of the millrace wound still pulling at him whenever he reached too far. The river ran high with the thaw, brown and muscular, carrying the last of the season's floes down toward the sea. He was working a splinter out of the oak with his knife when the sound arrived—not shouting, not yet, but a low continuous hum, like a hive disturbed, coming from the direction of the bridge.

He climbed the stairs two at a time.

The bridge was full. Not passing-full, not market-morning-full, but *filled*, packed shoulder to shoulder from one stone abutment to the other, and more people arriving every minute up the hill lanes, carrying candles, carrying children, carrying sheets of paper held out before them like lamps. The garrison had turned out too—a double line of soldiers at the far end, bayonets fixed, an officer with a drum-major's staff trying to form them into something like a wall.

And in the middle of the bridge, on the old toll plinth where Halden's clerks had collected a penny from every cart for twenty years, a man stood reading aloud.

Isandro knew him distantly—a cooper from the Salt Lane, a big slow man with sawdust permanently in his beard. His voice was not made for public speaking; it cracked and stumbled. But he read anyway, holding the pamphlet so close to his face that Isandro knew he must be reading from memory of the heart rather than the eyes.

"*On the fourteenth of October, in the year 1789, upon the written order of Magistrate Edouard Halden, the hamlet of Braselet was put to the torch—*"

"*Eleven souls,*" someone called out. A woman's voice. "*My mother was one.*"

"*On the second of December—*"

The cooper read on. And the strange thing, the thing Isandro would carry for the rest of his life, was what happened with the names. Each time the dead were named—and Felix had named every one, had cut the letters of their names herself with the same care she gave to wedding invitations—the crowd took them up. Softly at first, uncertainly, the way a congregation answers a litany.

"*Marta Osselin. Jem Osselin. The child Ansel.*"

"Marta Osselin," the bridge said back. "Jem Osselin. The child Ansel."

Two hundred voices. Then more. The sound went down into the water under the bridge and came up again changed, and Isandro stood on the stairs with his hand pressed flat against the cold rail and felt the city turn over beneath him like a sleeper waking.

At the far end, the officer with the staff was shouting orders. Isandro saw the front rank of soldiers look at one another—saw it plainly, even at that distance, the small sideways glances of men being asked to do a thing they had suddenly begun to measure. Most of them were river boys. Some of them had mothers on that bridge.

"Disperse!" the officer cried. "In the magistrate's name, disperse!"

Nobody dispersed. The cooper read on. The litany of names rolled up the span of the bridge like weather.

A sergeant—one Isandro half recognized, a man who drank at the Anchor and paid his debts—took off his helmet.

He did not throw it down. He only held it, awkwardly, under his arm, and looked at his boots, and did not move forward when the order came again.

Then another soldier did the same. Then three.

The wall dissolved without breaking. The soldiers did not join the crowd—they were not fools, and Halden's writ still ran until midnight—but they stopped being a wall, and a wall that is no longer a wall is only a row of tired men standing in the cold, and nobody has ever dispersed a crowd with that.

Isandro stayed until the candles came out, hundreds of them, little flames trembling in the river wind above black moving water, each one held beside a name. Then he went to find Livia.

* * *

Livia had heard the names before anyone read them aloud.

She heard them in the nights of that winter, all of them, in the voice of the man who had come to the wash-house door with Halden's seal on his card and clemency in his mouth like a sweet he wanted her to take. *Names are currency, girl,* Betto's master had told her through Betto, before Betto had gone over entirely, before she understood which side of the ledger her own brother walked on. *Yours can buy your brother's life.*

She had spent three days deciding, and she had decided in a laundry mark—a stitch in a hem, a code she'd invented with Felix Eastwick over a basket of shirts—and ever since she had been waiting to learn whether a sister's small treachery against power counted for anything in the world.

Tonight she went to find out.

She dressed in her good dress, the grey one, because she had learned long ago that grief travels best in grey, and she walked to the magistrate's court through streets gone strange with candlelight. The building was lit in every window; Halden's clerks had not gone home. There were papers being burned somewhere inside—she could smell it on the air, the sweet-acrid ghost of documents, though burning was useless now. What was in the pamphlet was also in a thousand heads now, and heads do not burn well.

Betto was in the guardroom, giving evidence. That much she learned from the clerk she waylaid, a soft young man whose eyes kept sliding toward the window and the glow of the crowd on the bridge. When Betto came out, escorted, self-important, a soldier's greatcoat over his apprentice's shoulders, Livia stepped into his path.

"You told them about my brother," she said.

Betto looked at her the way a boy looks at a debt he had hoped was forgotten. "I told them the truth."

"You told them what you were paid to tell them. There's a difference, and it's the whole width of the world." She had rehearsed nothing; the words came up from somewhere below rehearsal. "You sold the route to Halden's men and a man bled on the ice at the millrace because of it. My brother carried a wounded woman's papers through three checkpoints, and you would have had him shot in a ditch for thirty crowns and a coat."

"I'd have been ruined otherwise—"

"Yes," Livia said. "That's exactly it. That's the whole of it. You'd have been ruined, and other people would have been dead, and you chose." She let that stand in the cold air between them. "Well. Now I choose."

She turned to the clerk and the two soldiers who had drifted close, drawn as people always are to the smell of a scene. "This boy informed on the print shop in Cutler's Lane. He informed on the route past the millrace. Everything he's told this court tonight about informers and clemency is a story he's telling to save his own skin, and I will say so, under my own name, wherever anyone asks me. I've already said it once." She touched the hem of her grey dress, where the stitches were. "Ask the Eastwick girl's laundress if you doubt me."

"Livia—" Betto began.

"Don't," she said. "Don't say my name. My name is spoken tonight among better ones."

She left him standing in the corridor with his borrowed coat, and she walked out into the candlelit streets, and she found she was shaking, and she found she was smiling.

* * *

Halden resigned on the third day.

Not in disgrace—he would never use that word, and neither, formally, would anyone with the power to make it stick. The amnesty was the amnesty; New Year's had come and gone, bells had rung, charges had burned in the civic fires along with the old year, and Edouard Halden walked out of the winter session protected on every side by the law he had bent for twenty years. No court would touch him. No tribunal would convene. The pamphlet named him, proved him, buried him in his own signatures, and the law folded its arms and said: *that was then.*

But a man cannot resign from shame the way he resigns from office.

Felix heard the news in the shop, from Corvel's lawyer, who had come about the disposition of Corvel's estate—Corvel having died in custody in February, quietly, of a bad heart and a worse conscience, his confession published posthumously in the fourth printing. "He submitted his letter of resignation to the council at nine o'clock this morning," the lawyer said. "Read it aloud himself. Very dignified. He spoke of 'the burden of office' and 'the judgments of history.'"

"What did the council say?"

"The council said thank you." The lawyer's mouth twisted. "The law thanks him. The courts excuse him. He'll retire to his estate at Merle with his pensions intact."

Felix was setting type as he spoke—almanac verse again, real verse this time, honest nonsense about spring—and her hands did not stop. She had learned something in the long night at the press: that work is a place to put rage when rage has nowhere lawful to go.

"And the crowds?" she asked.

"Larger every evening. They read at the bridge. Every evening, at dusk, the names." The lawyer gathered his papers. "There's talk of a commission, someday, of an inquiry—someday, when the climate changes. Nothing in my lifetime, I should think. Nothing in yours." He paused at the door. "Miss Eastwick. Your father cut type for forty years and no one outside this street knew his name. It's in the pamphlet now. *Cut by F. Eastwick.* He'd have—" The lawyer stopped, mastered whatever the sentence had been going to be, and settled for: "It was well done."

After he had gone, Felix stood at the case with her fingers resting on the sorts, the cold lead warming under her hands, and thought about justice.

It was not the shape she had imagined as a girl, listening to her father read the news sheets aloud. She had imagined law like a loom—that you fed truth in one side and wove justice out the other, the mechanism impartial, the pattern inevitable. What she had learned instead was that the loom belonged to the magistrates, that they could stop it or speed it or thread it with their own wool, and that when it failed—as it had failed Marta Osselin, as it had failed the child Ansel, as it had failed Corvel dying in his cell with his confession folded inside a psalm—then the work fell to hands. To fishwives whispering over herring crates. To coopers reading badly on a bridge. To sisters choosing blood over safety, one stitch at a time. To a compositor setting page after page through a winter night because forgiveness is what the powerful give themselves and verdict is what the people render.

Imperfect. Real. Unlawful, and truer than the law.

Outside, the light was failing, and down the hill she could hear the crowd beginning to gather, the low hive-hum rising, the first candles kindling.

* * *

That evening they read the names again, and this time Isandro and Livia stood together in the crowd, and this time Felix stood with them, the three of them shoulder to shoulder near the toll plinth while the cooper's successor—a schoolmistress, this time, with a clear carrying voice—worked through the October pages.

"*Marta Osselin. Jem Osselin. The child Ansel.*"

"Marta Osselin," said the bridge. Two thousand voices now, and the soldiers at the far end had stopped coming altogether; their officer had discovered that there was nothing left to guard the bridge against. The candles went down the span like a lit fuse. The river took the light and broke it and gave it back.

Livia's hand found Isandro's. Felix stood close enough that her shoulder pressed his, and none of them spoke, because the names were speaking.

When the litany ended, the schoolmistress lowered her paper, and for a moment the bridge was quiet enough to hear the water going by under the stones, dark and patient, carrying the winter's last ice down to the sea.

Then a voice from the back of the crowd—a child's voice, thin and bright—asked the question that had been waiting in the cold air all evening:

"Who wrote it? Who cut the names?"

And before Felix could move, before she could decide whether she wanted to be found, the crowd began to answer itself, the words passing up the bridge faster than the wind:

"*Eastwick.* They say an Eastwick cut it. The printer's daughter. F. Eastwick. It's signed—it's signed right on the last page—"

Faces turning. Candles tilting toward her. Somewhere in the press of bodies a stranger's hand found her sleeve and gripped it, not to seize her but to hold her the way you hold a railing, the way you hold something that keeps you upright.

"F. Eastwick," the bridge said, softly, all down its length, like the beginning of another name in the litany.

Felix stood very still in the middle of the sound, her father's name and her own braided together in two thousand mouths, and felt the weight of lead she had carried all winter change at last into something else—into wings, or into a door opening onto spring.

Beside her, Isandro leaned close, and she felt more than heard him smile.

"They'll want you to speak," he murmured.

"They'll be disappointed," she whispered back. "I'm a typesetter. We speak in other people's words."

"Not anymore," said Isandro, as the candles burned on down the bridge, and the river ran, and the century, newly born, listened.
