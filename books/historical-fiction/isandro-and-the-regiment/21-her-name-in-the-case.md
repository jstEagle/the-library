## 21. Her Name in the Case

The gates of Vellamont prison opened on a Tuesday, and Isandro walked out into a morning so bright it felt like an insult.

He had expected winter to have deepened while he sat in his cell. Instead the thaw had come early; the river ran loud and brown under the fort's walls, carrying broken ice toward the sea like a fleet of shattered plates. Snow slid from the rooftops in wet slabs. The whole city was dripping, groaning, waking—and he stood at the gate with nothing but the coat on his back, the coat she had worn across three checkpoints, smelling of another man's cell, blinking like something exhumed.

A guard had handed him a paper. *Released by act of amnesty, charges voided*, though someone had scrawled beneath it in a clerk's impatient hand: *Eastwick, F.* The name still wasn't his. He had worn it for eleven days, answered to it, slept inside it. Now the state took it back as casually as it had taken him, and Isandro folded the paper into his breast pocket anyway, because it was the only thing he owned that had her name written on it.

Livia was waiting.

She stood apart from the wives and mothers clustered at the gate, arms crossed against the cold, and when she saw him she did not run to him. She never had. She simply turned her face toward him fully, the way she did when she meant to see everything, and let him come to her.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been sleeping on stone."

"You look worse than that." Her voice cracked on the last word, and then her composure went with it, all at once, the way ice goes off a roof. She seized his collar with both hands and pressed her forehead to his chest and stayed there, breathing hard, while he held the back of her head and watched the breath of them both rise white into the cold air.

"I told you to stop," she said into his coat. "I begged you."

"I know."

"I wrote to them. I sat in Halden's anteroom for six hours with my hands in my lap like a good woman and I asked them what it would take." She pulled back and looked up at him, eyes red-rimmed and furious. "And then I read the pamphlet."

* * *

They walked home along the river path, past the ferry stairs where he had worked since his discharge, past the pilings black with water. Livia talked because he could not yet. She talked the way people do when they have been holding words too long and are afraid of silence.

The pamphlet had flooded the city in the first days of January. She described it to him: four pages, closely set, no title but a line from the ledger itself—*Being a true account of orders given and executed in the districts of Vellamont, Ansel, and Grey Water*—and beneath the last column, small and unmistakable, the signature: **cut by F. Eastwick**.

"They read the names at the bridge," Livia said. "Every evening. Hundreds of them, standing on the ice with candles. The soldiers just—stood there. Some of them took off their hats." She glanced at him sideways. "There was a woman with a voice like a bell who read the children first. They say Halden resigned the same night."

Isandro said nothing. He was watching the bridge grow larger ahead of them, its arches standing in the swollen river, and thinking of Felix crossing it alone in his imagination a hundred times while he sat in a cell counting stones.

"Betto has fled," Livia went on, quieter now. "Or gone underground. They came to our street asking after him and I told them everything I knew, which was everything, and my hands didn't shake until afterward." She stopped walking. "I want you to understand, Isandro. I chose. Nobody made me safe by lying. I chose blood over safety and I would choose it again, and if you ever lie to my face again I will drown you in the ferry pool myself."

"Livia—"

"Shut up and take the bread."

She pulled a wrapped loaf from her basket and pushed it against his chest: dark rye, still warm through the cloth, the smell of it hitting him with such force that his eyes stung. Bread from their mother's recipe, kneaded by his sister's hands.

"That's not all," she said. "The blessing part. You'll want to sit down for it, knowing you." She looked out at the river, jaw working. "I went to the Eastwick shop. Three days ago. I stood outside like a fool for half an hour before I went in. And I told her—" His sister's chin lifted. "I told her that whatever happens next, whatever this becomes, she is not to carry it alone. That my brother is a stubborn mule with a soldier's habit of walking into fire for strangers, and that if she means to keep him, she'd better keep him fed. And then I helped her hang punches for two hours and cried over her father's bench where nobody could see."

Isandro stared at her.

"Go," Livia said. "Wash the cell off you. Shave. Then go to her." She turned back toward the house and called over her shoulder, without turning her head: "And eat the bread on the way. You look like a scarecrow."

* * *

He ate it on the bridge.

It seemed right somehow, to stand on the span where he had been arrested wearing another man's coat under another man's name, and eat his sister's bread while the river ran loud beneath him. Eleven days in that cell. He had counted eleven mornings, and each one he had expected the amnesty to fail, some clerk to find an error, some magistrate to convert the old charge into something that would hold. Sedition, they had whispered. They wanted to make the name *Felix Eastwick* mean sedition, and let her swing for it.

Instead the pamphlet had done what no warrant could undo.

He looked down at the water and remembered the courier dying on the far bank with oilcloth clutched to his chest; remembered the ledger sewn beneath the sorts, the names, the dates, the neat signatures of Magistrate Halden. Remembered Felix's face the night she showed it to him, lit by a single lamp, looking younger and older than any living woman should look at once. He had thought then: *this will cost us*. He had been right. He had not guessed how much, or how strangely the payment would come due—not in blood, mostly, but in Corvel rotting in prison awaiting trial for crimes the pamphlet proved were committed by other men. In Betto vanished. In eleven days of his own life spent in a cell under a borrowed name.

But also: in crowds reading names aloud at the bridge. In a city that had woken up dripping and groaning like the season itself.

He finished the bread and went to find her.

* * *

The print shop smelled the way it always had, even after the raid—even after soldiers had scattered Aldous Eastwick's life work across the floor like a hand of losing cards. It smelled of ink and oil and the cold-metal sweetness of lead, and underneath that, faintly, of lamp smoke and lye soap. Isandro stood in the doorway a long moment before he knocked, because through the window he had seen her, and something in his chest had simply stopped working.

She was on a ladder.

Not a proper ladder—a kitchen chair dragged to the wall of the type case cabinet, and above the cabinet, mounted on pegs in careful rows, hung the punches. Her father's punches. Hundreds of steel letters, each one cut by hand over thirty years, each one the negative from which every word he ever printed had flowed. The raid had torn them down; she was hanging them back up, one at a time, sorting by eye and memory, and she was doing it with the particular ferocity of a woman rebuilding a cathedral out of matchsticks.

Her hair had come loose from its pins. There was ink on her cheek. She wore her father's apron, which she had never done before the winter began, and she was muttering to herself in the cadence of the trade—*lowercase a through e here, f through j there*—and Isandro realized he was memorizing her. That he had already begun. That some chamber in him that had been locked for eleven days had opened, and everything it had hoarded in darkness was flooding out at once.

The floorboard creaked under his boot.

She did not startle. She went still instead, all of her, the way she had gone still at checkpoints with a patrol officer's breath on her neck, and then she turned on the unsteady chair and looked down at him, and her face did something complicated that ended in neither smile nor tears but somewhere between.

"You're shorter than your reputation," she said, voice not quite level.

"So I've been told." He crossed the shop slowly, taking inventory the way a man takes inventory of a wound. The presses scarred but sound. The composing stone swept clean. On it, a fresh galley locked in place—the pamphlet, set again, proof pulled and pinned to the wall above. And beside the press, a case of type he did not recognize, its compartments empty, its frame new-cut wood still pale with sap.

"What's that?" he asked.

"Mine." She stepped down off the chair, refusing his hand with a look that said she would accept it later, when she chose. "I've been cutting new sorts since the raid. My own design. Smaller counters, tighter fit—it sets cleaner than Father's ever did, though I'd thank you not to repeat that to anyone hanging within earshot." She nodded upward at the pegs, the rows of steel. "His stay. Mine grows."

"Felix."

"Don't," she said, quietly, and turned away to straighten a punch that needed no straightening. "I've rehearsed this conversation for eleven days and none of the versions survive contact. So we'll just—begin badly and improve."

He set Livia's basket on the bench. From it he took the second loaf—he had carried it the whole way, warm once, now merely bread—and laid it beside the galley stone, among the tools of her trade, where it looked absurd and perfect.

"My sister sends her blessing," he said. "In her own words: you're not to carry it alone. Also she helped you hang punches and cried, which she will deny under oath."

That got him the almost-smile. "She told me she'd deny it."

"She denies everything. It's how she loves."

Felix picked up the loaf and weighed it in both hands like something to be set in type, and then she put it down again and faced him fully, and he saw that her hands were shaking—not with fear; he knew her fear by now, had watched it cross three checkpoints in her face. This was something else. This was the shaking of a woman holding still for eleven days so that the world would believe her calm.

"They kept you under my name," she said.

"Yes."

"For eleven days you answered to *Felix Eastwick*."

"It's not a bad name," he said. "Good bones. I answered to worse in the regiment."

"Isandro." Not loud. But the way she said it closed the distance between them more thoroughly than touch. "You took a beating at the customhouse for me. A cart accident at the toll gate. A musket ball at the millrace. And then my name." Her voice finally cracked, and she let it. "I signed that pamphlet so that the truth would have a maker. So the city couldn't pretend the words had fallen from the sky. And the price of signing was paid by you, in a cell, wearing my name like a coat."

"The amnesty—"

"The amnesty doesn't pay anything back," she said fiercely. "It just stops collecting. Don't you dare tell me the debt is voided."

He had no answer prepared. He had crossed the city with bread and his sister's blessing and a speech half-formed about gratitude and beginnings, and every word of it had died in the doorway of her shop, because she was not a woman you consoled. She was a woman you stood beside.

So he did the only honest thing. He reached into his breast pocket, unfolded the crumpled release paper, and laid it on the bench beside the loaf.

"Then keep it," he said. "Your name. I've no further use for it—you seem to need it more than I do. Consider it returned with interest: eleven days' lodging, one beating, and the worst gruel in Vellamont."

Felix looked down at the paper. Her name in a stranger's handwriting, in a prison clerk's careless ink. When she lifted her head her eyes were bright and wet and she was smiling the way he imagined she smiled at a finished page—as though she could already see the impression it would leave.

"You're standing in my shop," she said, "insulting the gruel, and you haven't touched the galley."

He followed her gaze to the proof sheet pinned above the stone. Four columns, close-set, the testimony of the ledger flowing in lines so clean they looked inevitable. And below the final column, in type finer than the rest—new type, he saw now, cut small and tight and true—

**cut by F. Eastwick**

"There's more," she said. "Read the colophon. All of it."

He leaned close. Beneath her name, in the same fine face, a second line he had not seen from the door:

*and set by I. — who wore the name a while, and wore it well.*

He stood very still. Outside, the river ran loud with the breaking ice; somewhere up the hill a church bell rang the half hour, off-key, thaw-warped. The lamp hissed. The punches hung above them in their hundreds, steel letters catching the light like a silent congregation.

"When did you cut that?" he managed.

"Three days ago. While you were being a martyr, I was being a craftsman." She picked up her father's smallest graver from the bench and put it in his hand and closed his fingers around it. "Livia says you're a stubborn mule. Good. Mules are useful. The publisher wants a second edition by spring—Corvel's confession appended, properly authenticated, and Livia's account of Betto if she'll allow it. It's four hundred pages of setting and I have exactly one pair of hands." She stepped closer, and now she did take his hand, the one holding the graver, and turned it palm-up as though inspecting a punch. "The pay is bread. My bread, once I learn to bake, which will be terrible for months. The hours are shameful. The work is the most important thing either of us will ever do."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you can stand at the ferry stairs for the rest of your life watching other men carry things across rivers," she said, "and wonder, every night, what the second edition would have said."

Isandro laughed—a rough, disused sound, like a press starting after long idleness—and turned his hand in hers until their fingers laced, ink and rope-callus, graver-worn and checkpoint-worn, both of them rebuilt around the same load-bearing truth.

"I accept," he said. "On one condition."

"Name it."

"When it's done—when spring comes and the bridge opens and the second edition is loose in the world—you teach me to cut a punch. One letter. My choice."

Felix studied him, head tilted, the lamplight moving in her eyes, and he understood that she was already composing her answer in metal, in her mind, arranging his condition into rows like sorts in a case.

"Agreed," she said. "Though I warn you—" and she nodded upward at the wall of her father's work, the congregation of steel, at the empty pegs waiting among them for what came next— "the first thing everyone cuts wrong. It's tradition. Your letter will be crooked and we'll hang it anyway, right there, where the whole shop can see it."

"Then make room on the wall," Isandro said, and reached for the apron hanging behind the bench.
