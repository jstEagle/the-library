## 22. First Impressions

The ice went out of the Vell in the third week of March, and the whole city stood at its windows to listen.

It was not a quiet sound. Those who had never lived through a river breaking had imagined it would be—a sigh, a softening—but it was a groan and a report like distant cannon, plates of ice grinding themselves to slush, the water beneath running brown and muscular with snow-melt. Felix Eastwick heard it from the doorway of the print shop at four in the morning, because she had not slept, because she rarely slept now except when the work put her down like a kind hand, and she stood there in her father's coat with the cold coming off the street and listened to the winter being dismantled.

The bridge reopened nine days later.

She was there for it, though she told herself she would not be. She meant only to walk past, to see, but half of Vellamont meant the same thing, and so instead of one woman passing there was a crowd pressed shoulder to shoulder at the bridge foot while the magistrate's men—fewer of them now, and slower to raise their voices—pulled the barricades apart plank by plank. Someone had nailed ribbons to the first rail. Someone else had chalked names on the stones of the approach, dozens of names, the letters large and uneven, written by many hands: the names from the pamphlet, the names from the ledger, read aloud all through January until the soldiers who had been ordered to disperse the readers simply stopped coming out for it. The names were still there. Rain had softened them but not erased them, and now a man with a brush and a pot of white paint was going over each letter carefully, renewing them, as a sexton renews an inscription.

Felix watched him for a while. Then she crossed.

The boards rang under her boots, that same hollow drumhead note she remembered from childhood, when her father would lift her onto the rail and tell her the bridge was the city's spine and you could feel it flex if you paid attention. She paid attention. The river ran beneath, high and loud and carrying the last ragged islands of ice seaward, and halfway across she stopped, because stopping felt necessary, and looked down at all that moving water.

Isandro had walked this deck in December wearing her coat, carrying her case of type, with her name folded into his pocket like a coin he expected to be arrested for. They had taken him at the fort gate within sight of the far bank. She had learned that afterward, in fragments, the way bad news always arrives—first a rumor on the ferry stairs, then Livia's face, then the letter that took three weeks to travel thirty miles. For fifty-one days he had been *the Eastwick girl* in the garrison books, a woman who did not exist, held on charges that were dissolving even as they wrote them down. On the second of January the amnesty burned everything pending, and the clerk who released him had looked from the papers to his face and back again and said nothing at all.

Now he came up the far slope of the bridge toward her, bread under one arm and a crate of delivered pamphlets—old ones, the last of the January printing, bound for a bookbinder in Tarrow Lane—swinging in his other hand, and he grinned when he saw her standing in the middle of the span like a girl who had never crossed anything before.

"You'll be late for your own press," he said.

"The press will wait."

"It's been waiting since dawn. I heard it complaining." He handed her the bread—still warm, from the oven Livia kept, and Livia's blessing baked into it in the form of an extra measure of butter—and they walked back across together, matching steps, the boards ringing their double note beneath them.

* * *

The shop had changed hands in the way that rivers change course: slowly, then all at once.

Corvel had died in the third week of February, in the prison infirmary, coughing out the last of what the damp cells of Vellamont had put into him. He had lived long enough to stand in the guildhall and authenticate the ledger before forty witnesses, his voice failing page by page, naming Halden's orders in his own cracked voice until he could no longer manage volume and the room leaned in to catch the words. He had lived long enough to see Halden resign—not tried, not ruined, the amnesty's broad shield covering the magistrate as thoroughly as it covered the courier's ghost—but to see the city turn its back on him in the streets, which Corvel, who had spent twenty years invisible behind his press, said was the only sentence he'd ever wanted passed. He had not lived long enough to reclaim his shop. It had come to Felix instead, through the strange arithmetic of debts and deathbed instructions and a lawyer who read the pamphlet eleven times before drafting a single line.

Her name was on the guild rolls now. *Eastwick, F., printer.* The clerk had hesitated over it, quill hovering, and Felix had waited without flinching until he wrote it.

The presses themselves had suffered in the raid. She had spent the winter rebuilding—the iron frame of the elder press straightened by a smith who refused payment and asked instead for twelve copies of everything she printed, forever; the platen reground; the friskin straps replaced; her father's punches, scattered across the floor that terrible night, gathered up one by one and sorted back into their cases. That work had taken weeks. Some nights she had sat among the drawers with a lamp and a jeweler's loupe, holding each steel punch to the light, reading the letter it cut, and wept without deciding to, the grief arriving on its own schedule now, less frequent and deeper, like the river's spring tide.

Tonight the shop smelled of ink and lamp oil and the wet wool drying by the stove, and Isandro stacked the empty crate by the door while Felix wiped down the composing stone with a rag gone stiff with old varnish.

"I have something," she said.

He looked up. He had learned, in the way of men who live with careful people, to recognize the tones of her voice, and this one made him set down the rag he'd picked up and give her his whole attention.

"It isn't finished. It needs polishing, and the depth is wrong on the left side of the stem—I can feel it when I draw my thumbnail across—but it's close." She took the lamp from the stone and carried it to the bench at the back of the shop, her father's bench, the graver drawer, the little forge with its cold charcoal, and Isandro followed the light.

On the bench lay a scrap of paper with a single letter drawn on it in ink, drawn large and confident, and beside it, on a square of soft steel no bigger than a thumbnail, the same letter rising from the metal in relief, still bright from the file, catching the lamplight along its edges.

It was an E.

"Your father cut a full fount," Isandro said. "Two founts. There are E's in those cases."

"There are." She turned the punch over in her fingers. "And every one of them was cut for another man's words. His hands made them, but they were sold with the shop, they printed other people's lies and other people's almanacs and God knows how many tax notices. This one—" she held it up between two fingers, small and hard and shining— "this one I cut myself. My own steel. My own letter. It doesn't belong to any fount. It belongs to whatever I choose to set with it, and I haven't decided yet what that is."

"What will you use it for?"

"First impressions." She smiled, and he saw the tiredness in it and the joy underneath the tiredness, layered like watermarks in good paper. "That's the trade term. The first pull off a new-cut face. You ink it and strike it and see what your steel has to say." She set the punch down gently on a clean cloth. "I thought tomorrow morning, before the deliveries, before anything. You'll pull the lever. My hands aren't steady enough tonight and I want it perfect."

Isandro looked at the letter for a long moment. Then he looked around the shop—at the two presses, one rebuilt and one repainted; at the racks of type re-sorted and gleaming; at the window where the shutter stood open a hand's width because the weather had turned and the night air came in smelling of thaw and coal smoke and the river; at the wall where a proof sheet hung framed, the very first broadside, its ink faded to the brown of old blood, *printed by F. Eastwick* in small defiant letters at the foot.

"Your father would have—"

"He would have pretended not to care," Felix said, "and then he'd have checked the depth of the stem with his loupe and said nothing for a full minute, and then he'd have told me the serifs were too generous." Her voice caught, just slightly, and steadied. "I'm going to make them too generous on purpose. Every letter I ever cut, for the rest of my life, is going to have his serifs."

* * *

Morning came up pale gold and cold, the particular cold of April that holds no threat in it, only memory.

By seven the stove was lit and the shop warming, and Livia arrived with more bread than any two people could eat and an announcement that she would stay to see the pulling of the first impression whether she was wanted or not, and that she had invited "anyone relevant," by which she meant, it developed, nearly a dozen people: the smith; the white-clad man who renewed the names on the bridge stones; two of the refugees from the snowbound inn who had found their way to Vellamont and work and each other; a boy of perhaps ten whom nobody introduced and everybody fed; and Betto.

Betto stood at the door as though unsure the door would bear him. He was thinner than the winter had left him—he had testified after Livia, giving Halden's men their route and their timing, giving up his own shame in open court where anyone might hear it—and he had come, he said, to ask for work, knowing he had no right to ask, prepared to be turned away.

Livia looked at Felix. Felix looked at the boy's hands, which were ink-stained to the second knuckle, stained the way only real apprentices' hands are stained, deep in the grain, and thought about inheritance, and what it was, and whether it could survive being refused.

"Composing stone needs cleaning before we start," she said. "Rag's by the sink."

Betto's face did something complicated. He went to the sink.

They gathered around the press at half past eight, an unlikely congregation smelling of bread and coal smoke and river water, and Felix laid the new-cut punch on the stone beside a piece of paper she had chosen with care—not fine paper, not paper for keeping, but a good honest sheet of the stock she used for pamphlets, the stock that had carried the ledger's testimony out of a dying man's type case and across a frozen country and into the hands of a city that had needed it the way a lung needs air.

Isandro inked the punch with a dab of the hand brayer, working the stiff ink across the steel face in thin passes, and set it in the press bed, and Felix laid the sheet over it, and for a moment everyone in the shop stopped breathing at once, the way crowds do.

"Pull," she said.

The screw came down. The iron spoke its short iron word. And then the lever rose and the platen lifted and Isandro drew the sheet out and held it to the window light, and there it was:

A single letter, black and crisp-edged on the pale sheet, its serifs generous beyond all propriety, its stem true, its counters clean. An E. Cut by F. Eastwick, pulled by the hand of a ferryman's son, witnessed by a sister, a smith, a traitor doing penance with a rag, a painter of names, and a child who stood on a stool to see.

Nobody applauded. It wasn't that kind of moment. But Livia cried freely and claimed it was the onions she'd theoretically brought, and the smith said *good depth* in the tone other men reserve for poetry, and Betto cleaned the composing stone until it shone like a promise.

Outside, the bridge stood open. Through the shop's front window Felix could see the traffic crossing it already—carts and cattle and children running the planks, a fishmonger ringing his bell, two soldiers walking their post with muskets slung and faces bored, ordinary men guarding an ordinary morning. The checkpoints were gone or unmanned or merely ceremonial now, and Isandro's delivery routes ran where they pleased, though he kept the old paths out of habit and affection, and greeted the toll keepers by name, and was greeted back.

Felix took the proof sheet from Isandro's hands and looked at it a long time.

One letter. That was all a language was, in the end, multiplied: one letter, then another, then words, then names, then testimony, then truth set solid in a chase and locked to the stone and given to the world at however many impressions the paper and the press could stand. Her father had taught her that a punch cutter's work outlived him by centuries—that somewhere in some case, decades hence, a letter of his cutting might spell a word in a sentence in a book that mattered—and she had believed him the way you believe the dead, completely and too late. Now she understood it differently. The steel didn't matter. The steel was only the beginning of the chain of hands: cutter, compositor, pressman, courier, reader. What mattered was the chain, and the willingness of ordinary hands to hold it taut across a frozen river when it mattered.

She hung the first impression on the wall beside the January broadside, driving the nail herself, and stepped back, and the two sheets hung together in the morning light—one a weapon, one a seed.

"Well," Isandro said, coming to stand beside her, his shoulder warm against hers. "What does the shop print first, Mistress Eastwick?"

Felix looked out at the bridge, at the river running free and brown and loud beneath it, at the city waking into its imperfect, unfinished, unamnestied truth, and she found that the answer had been waiting in her hands all winter, the way the punch had waited under the file, the way spring waits under ice.

"A history," she said. "Of this winter. Every name, every door, every hand along the chain. Set in my type, on my press, signed at the foot." She turned to the composing stone, where Betto had left the cases open and the morning sun lay across the brass like a blessing, and she began, with both hands, to sort the letters.
