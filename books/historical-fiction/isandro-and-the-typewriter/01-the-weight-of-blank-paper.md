## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

The map room smelled of linseed oil and cold iron, and by the third week of November it had begun to smell of Mirabel's breath as well — the small white ghost of it that hung in the air when she leaned close enough to read her master's marginalia. She had learned to breathe sideways, a habit of the dead season, turning her face from the paper so that no moisture would touch the ink. Master Corvus had taught her that in her first winter, six years ago, when she was eleven and all elbows and hunger for the work. *Paper remembers everything*, he had said, tapping the desk with one yellowed fingernail. *It remembers your hands, your weather, your carelessness. Be worthy of being remembered.*

She wondered, sometimes, whether the paper remembered him too.

The atlas lay open on the great oak table under its sheet of linen, forty-one plates finished and eleven waiting as blank copper, and Mirabel moved down its length the way she did every morning, plate by plate, saying the names of them aloud because the room was otherwise silent and silence, in Verenmoor, had begun to feel like an accusation. "The Vell marshes," she said. "The salt road to Haren. The river at flood stage, 1871." Her voice came out small and fogged. She said the numbers anyway. Numbers were safe. Numbers were the one language that never lied to you, though she was learning — everyone in Verenmoor was learning — that men could make even numbers lie if they held the pen.

Outside the tall north windows, the town was making its noise. Every year it was the same: the last carts rattling over the bridge before the ice crews closed it, the shouting of the ferrymen who would have nothing to do for four months but drink and mend rope, the hammering of boards over the tollhouse windows. Verenmoor sat on both banks of the Ald like a man straddling a fence he could not get off of, and every winter the river decided for everyone which half of the town belonged to whom. When the bridge chained shut, the east bank went dark and quiet and the west bank grew crowded and short-tempered, and grudges that in summer could be walked away from had to be carried instead, house to house, like firewood.

This year there was also the commission. The occupying commission had taken the customs house for its offices and the Blue Boar for its officers and the whole grey mood of the town for its own, and their clerks had been carrying crates into the tollhouse all morning, preparing to seal the bridge with a chain and a posted order and whatever ceremony Captain Reiner considered sufficient. Mirabel had watched them from this window while she waited for the stove to draw. She did not watch long. Watching the commission was a habit she was trying to break; apprentices were meant to be furniture, and furniture did not stare.

She turned back to the table and took up her catalog book.

*Plate XLII,* she wrote. *The Ald from the weir to the sea bend. Copper prepared, ground laid, coastline blocked in. Unfinished.*

Her hand paused over the last word. It always paused there. *Unfinished* was the word she had written forty times now, once for each abandoned plate, and each time it cost her something small and irrecoverable, like paying out rope hand over hand and knowing there was no anchor at the other end.

Master Corvus had died in the spring. That was the word the commission used — *died*, entered in their ledger between a confiscated horse and three sacks of meal, as though he were livestock. What they had actually done was stand him against the wall of the powder magazine on the fourth of April and shoot him, and what he had actually done to deserve it was draw maps: troop movements, supply roads, the positions of guns along the river, drawn small and true and passed across the water in ways Mirabel still did not fully understand and had promised herself she did not want to. He had been old and careful and beloved of no government, and he had believed that a map was a form of truth-telling, and they had killed him for it in front of eleven witnesses and a boy selling chestnuts.

Mirabel had inherited his atlas the way one inherits a debt. Nobody had handed it to her. She had simply found herself holding it, the way you find yourself holding a rope when someone falls.

She worked now while the light lasted. The cataloging was not only grief-work; it was strategy, though she would not have used that word. If the commission ever came through the door of the map room — and they came often enough, requisitions and inventories, clerks with cold hands and colder eyes — they must find an apprentice finishing her dead master's commercial atlas. Rivers and roads and elevations, the honest geography of trade. They must not find, tucked among the fair copies, the eleven blank coppers whose intended subjects lived only in Corvus's head and, now, in hers: the patrol schedules along the towpath, the gun emplacements at the ferry crossing, the little red pencilled dots where men had stood and men had fallen. Those drawings existed nowhere yet except as notes in a cipher of her own devising, hidden inside what looked like tide tables. Paper remembered everything. She was counting on it.

A knock at the street door — two raps, apologetic — and then the door itself, letting in a blade of air that smelled of coal smoke and river rot.

"Fräulein Galloway." It was Pieter, the commission's runner, fourteen years old and permanently out of breath. "Requisition for the garrison stores. Clerk Nystrom says bring it back by noon." He held out a single typed sheet, already stamped, already signed, and did not come past the threshold, because runners were not allowed in the map room and Pieter obeyed rules the way other boys obeyed hunger.

"Thank you, Pieter."

"He says—" The boy hesitated, glanced down the street, lowered his voice. "He says mind the 'e.' You know. Like always."

"I know."

The door shut. The cold stayed a moment longer than the boy did, pooling around her ankles before the stove argued it back.

Her uncle. Of course it was her uncle's office sending work; it was always her uncle's office, because Aldous Nystrom had arranged it so within a month of taking the clerkship, and Mirabel had never decided whether the arrangement was kindness or surveillance or the particular mixture of both that made up everything Aldous did. He had raised her since she was nine, since the winter her parents' boat went out onto the Ald with the ice still moving and never came back, and he had raised her well in the way that a man raises a thing he is afraid of losing: carefully, quietly, behind locked doors. Now he was the commission's clerk, keeper of their papers, signer of their receipts, and his niece was the girl with the typewriter who transcribed what needed transcribing, and neither of them ever said aloud how useful it was to each of them that the other existed exactly where he did.

She carried the requisition to the typewriter.

The machine sat on its trestle table by the south window where the light was best for reading keys: a battered upright, black enamel gone grey at the corners, its space bar worn smooth as a river stone. It had come with the commission's offices, secondhand from some ministry in the capital, and it had flaws the way old soldiers do — a heavy carriage return, a ribbon that fed crooked unless you coaxed it, and above all the 'e,' whose typebar had bent sometime in its long service so that every 'e' struck a hair high and a shade to the left, printing its letter like a man standing slightly off his mark in a lineup. Aldous had noticed it the first week. *Mind the 'e',* he'd told her, meaning: remember that our machine is identifiable, meaning: be careful, meaning, in the compressed language of their family: I love you, don't get us killed.

Mirabel rolled in a fresh sheet and set her fingers home.

The requisition was simple, insultingly simple: coal for the garrison, lamp oil, flour, salt pork, quantities listed in figures that made her eyebrows climb. Two hundredweight more coal than the garrison had men to warm. She copied it faithfully — that was the rule, the iron rule she had made for herself and never broken: *transcribe what is set before you, change nothing, add nothing, notice everything.* Her fingers moved with the small mechanical music of the keys, and the misaligned 'e' stepped high and left through every word that contained it — *stores, requisitioned, issued* — a tiny limp in the text, a signature nobody asked for and everybody got.

She was halfway down the page when she heard the bells.

Not church bells — those rang on the hour, patient and dull. These were the bridge bells, the two iron discs hung at either end of the span that were rung only when the crossing was about to close, and their sound was different: cracked, urgent, ancient, a sound like a door deciding. The town answered it at once. She could hear the change in the noise outside the window — the cart traffic quickening to a scramble, whips cracking, men shouting at horses, a woman's voice lifted in argument at the tollhouse. Everyone on the wrong side of the river suddenly discovering urgent business on the right side, or the reverse. The sealed vessel, closing its lid.

Mirabel's fingers stopped on the keys. A comma hung untyped in the air.

Every year the closing had meant the same thing to her: a smaller world, a quieter one, the map room and the stove and the atlas and Aldous's Sunday dinners, winter as a held breath. This year the breath caught differently. This year there were soldiers billeted in the granary, and a tribunal scheduled for the customs house whenever the commission pleased, and her master's name spoken in the taverns in the flat voice people use for the condemned. This year the chain going across the bridge would not keep the world out. It would keep everything in — every grudge, every secret, every piece of paper with a lie folded into it — until spring thawed the locks and let the river judge.

She finished the requisition. Her hands were steady; she made them be steady. *Paper remembers everything.* Let it remember a girl who did her work.

* * *

At noon she carried the finished copy across the square to the customs house, hunched into her coat, the paper inside her shirt where it would stay dry and, less practically, feel safer. The square was chaos in the ordinary key: carts backed up to the bridgehead, a sergeant waving his arms without authority, the ferrymen already drunk at ten in the morning and singing about it. The chain itself lay coiled on the cobbles by the tollhouse like a sleeping eel, black with grease, links thick as her wrist. She gave it a wide berth, the way you give a wide berth to anything that has been assigned a purpose.

Inside the customs house the smell was ink, wet wool, and the particular staleness of rooms where men make decisions about other men. Aldous's office was at the end of the corridor, and she knocked the way she always knocked — twice, softly, the family rhythm — and heard his voice say "Enter" in the tone he kept for subordinates and used on her only when others might hear.

He looked older than autumn had left him. Grey had colonized his temples entirely now, and there were new lines around his mouth that she suspected had been carved by documents rather than years. But his eyes, when they flicked up from his ledger and found her, did the thing they always did — softened, checked her over, counted her fingers and limbs as though the world might have taken one since breakfast — before settling back into their official blankness.

"The requisition," she said, laying it on his desk.

He read it. She watched him read it, and saw the small contraction pass through him at the coal figure — so he had seen it too, then, the arithmetic that didn't add — and saw him decide, as she had decided, to have seen nothing. He stamped it. He filed it. The machine of him performed its function smoothly, and only his hand, replacing the stamp, pressed a half-second too long, as if setting a seal on his own silence.

"You've been at the atlas," he said. Not a question. He knew her schedule the way she knew his.

"Cataloguing. Eleven plates remain."

"Eleven." He turned the word over. "Your master was ambitious."

"My master was thorough." She heard the edge arrive in her own voice a moment too late to blunt it.

Aldous looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment the clerk's face fell away and it was simply the man who had taught her chess and mended her boots and never once, in nine years, spoken her parents' names aloud. Something moved behind his eyes — worry, or its cousin, guilt — and was filed away as quickly as it had surfaced.

"There will be more typing this week," he said. "The commission is... active, with the bridge closing. Papers to prepare. Orders." He straightened the ledger that did not need straightening. "You'll come when I send for you."

"I always come when you send for me."

"Yes," he said quietly. "That's what worries me."

She almost asked him what he meant. Nine years of living with Aldous had taught her that he spoke in the margins — that the truth of him lived in the white space around his sentences, readable only if you knew his hand. But a bell rang somewhere in the building, an officer's boots went by the open door, and the moment closed like water over a dropped coin.

"Mind the 'e,'" he said as she reached the door. Their old joke, their old covenant.

"Always," she said.

* * *

The walk back took her past the bridgehead, and she let herself look — just once, just briefly — at the span itself. The Ald ran high and brown beneath it, carrying ice-floes down from the upper country like a fleet of broken plates, and the far bank looked farther than it had in October, the way a thing looks farther when you are told you cannot reach it. On the eastern side, small in the grey light, a lamplighter was already working the early dusk, going lamp to lamp along the quay as though the season were merely proceeding and not shutting like a trap.

That was when she saw the man on the wrong side of the river.

He stood at the eastern bridgehead, still as a post in all that scurrying, watching the western bank with the fixed attention of someone memorizing it. Not a townsman — she knew every coat in Verenmoor by now, occupation having made her a student of coats — and not a soldier either; he wore neither the commission's grey nor the particular slouch of a deserter trying to look civilian. He carried a canvas satchel slung across his body the way a man carries something he intends to keep, and he was looking, she realized with a small cold start, not at the bridge, not at the chain, not at the tollhouse —

— but at her.

Across thirty yards of freezing air and the brown rush of the Ald, across the coiled chain and the shouting ferrymen and the whole machinery of the closing season, the stranger on the far bank looked at her in the map room window's direction as though he knew precisely which window it was, and touched the brim of his hat. Not a greeting. An acknowledgment. The gesture of a man confirming a fact he had traveled a long way to verify.

Then the first link of the chain went up with a sound like a struck anvil, and the crowd roared, and when she looked again the stranger had turned away into the eastern streets, the satchel tight against his side, walking with the unhurried certainty of a man who knows the door is closing behind him and does not intend to be on the outside of it when it does.

Mirabel stood in the cold a long moment, the requisition copy warm against her ribs, the misaligned 'e' printed neatly on every page of her morning's work, and felt the winter settle over Verenmoor like a lid on a jar.

Eleven plates remained unfinished. She had thought that was the largest thing waiting for her in the dark months ahead.

She was wrong, though she would not know how wrong until tomorrow, when the stranger came to her door with a document to copy, and the sealed town began, quietly, to fill with everything the chain was meant to keep out.
