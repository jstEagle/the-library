## 2. Last Cart Across

The chain came at dawn, and it came on a cart.

Mirabel Galloway watched from the window of the map room as two commission soldiers unloaded it — a great coil of blackened iron links, each one thick as her wrist, rusted in places and greased in others, as though the occupation could not decide whether the bridge was a thing to be preserved or merely endured. The soldiers worked without hurry. That was the worst of it, she thought. Men who hurry are men who fear interruption. These two moved like farmhands laying out a harvest, unhurried and unwatched, because no one was coming to stop them.

The fog had not yet lifted off the Verl. It lay along the river in grey shelves, and the bridge rose through it in its stone arches the way a spine rises through skin — old, familiar, the one true bone of the town. For three hundred years the bridge had been Verenmoor's argument with the world: *you may come, you may go, but you must cross here.* Now the argument was being settled with iron.

She counted the carts before the chain went up. A habit from her master, Herran Voss, who had taught her that a mapmaker's first duty was not drawing but noticing. *Anyone can draw a river,* he used to say, tapping his temple. *Not everyone sees what travels on it.* Four carts crossed before eight o'clock: two loaded with peat, one with the commission's mail sacks, and one carrying a family — a woman, three children, a mattress lashed upright like a sail. They were the last civilians out. The woman did not look back, and Mirabel understood why. Looking back was a luxury for people who knew they would return.

By half past eight the chain was fastened to the northern abutment with a padlock the size of a hymnal, and by nine there was paper nailed above it.

She went down to read it, because someone had to, and because she had learned long ago that in an occupied town the person who reads the orders aloud becomes, briefly, important — and importance could be spent like coin. A knot of townsfolk had gathered anyway: Frau Behrens with her market basket pressed to her chest; the ferryman's widow, Katrijn, whose face had gone the colour of the fog; young Pieter Stoop, hat in hand, reading the words over and over as though repetition might change them.

Mirabel read silently and then, when Pieter looked helplessly at her, read it aloud.

*By order of the Occupying Commission, District Fourteen: the Verenmoor crossing is closed to all traffic until the resumption of navigable conditions in spring. No person shall pass the chain. No vessel shall put upon the river between the chain and the southern weir. Provisions will enter by commission barge only, on schedule to be posted. Violations are subject to tribunal.*

*Tribunal.* The word sat on the page with the fat confidence of all commission words, printed in the heavy type that Mirabel herself had set a hundred times on the battered typewriter in the commission house — though not this notice; this one had come from district headquarters, from machines that did not have her misaligned 'e'.

"Spring," said Frau Behrens, as if tasting it. "That's five months."

"That's if they open it in spring," said Katrijn.

Nobody answered her, which was answer enough.

Mirabel folded her hands inside her sleeves and let the crowd dissolve around her, and felt what she felt — not panic, not yet, but a pressure like water finding the seams of a wall. She had lived in Verenmoor through two winters already. But there is a difference between a town that winter closes and a town that is closed, and every soul standing in the mud before that chain could feel the difference in their teeth. Winter was weather. This was architecture.

* * *

Her uncle Aldous wore his good coat that day.

She noticed it at midday, when she carried the morning's transcriptions to the commission house on the Kirchgasse — a requisition for lamp oil, an inventory of requisitioned grain, a letter to some distant clerk confirming quantities of things that had probably never existed. Aldous stood in the doorway of his office with his collar brushed and his ink-stained cuffs turned back neatly, receiving the congratulations of Captain Reiner's adjutant as though he were being decorated rather than confirmed.

"—appointed recording clerk to the Commission, District Fourteen," the adjutant was saying, "for the duration of the closure and such time after as—"

"Yes, yes." Aldous waved this away, but Mirabel saw how his chin lifted as he did it, the small upward tilt of a man swallowing something sweet. He caught sight of her in the corridor and his whole face rearranged itself — softened, opened, became the face she had known since she was seven years old. "There she is. My niece. You've heard, Miri?"

"I heard the chain go up."

"The appointment," he said patiently, ushering her into the office and closing the door against the corridor's draught. The room smelled of sealing wax and cold tea. "Recording clerk. It means I keep the true ledger of the district — everything in, everything out, everything accounted. There has never been a proper accounting here, do you understand? Years of slovenliness under the old administration. I am to be the instrument of order."

He said *instrument* the way other men said *saviour*, and Mirabel loved him too much and knew him too well to say so. Instead she set the transcriptions on his desk and asked the question she had carried up the hill with her.

"How long is your post good for? If the armistice comes?"

Aldous's hands, which had been smoothing the lapels of the good coat, went still. In the pause she heard the stove tick, and beneath it, faint and constant, the river.

"If the armistice comes," he said, "there will be a great deal of paper, Miri. Treaties generate documents the way floods generate mud. Armistice commissions, repatriation schedules, claims, inventories. A man who knows where every file sits in District Fourteen is not a man they send home." He smiled, and for a moment it was her uncle's smile entirely, the one that had coaxed her through fevers and nightmares and the terrible silence after her parents' boat went out and did not come back. "I have made myself useful. Useful is the safest country in the world. I advise you to take out citizenship."

"You didn't answer me."

"I answered the part that matters." He took the transcriptions from her, thumbed through them, and paused at the top sheet. His finger came down on the page — on a word, on a letter within the word — and tapped once. "Your 'e' is drifting again. Third line. *Stores.* It prints low and left. Someone at headquarters will notice eventually and ask why the commission's copyist cannot align a common vowel."

"It's the machine. The 'e' pallet is bent. I've asked three times for a replacement font."

"And three times been refused, I know." He set the pages down and looked at her a moment longer than the conversation required — a look she would turn over in her memory many times in the weeks to come, though she could not read it now. It contained something. Caution, perhaps. Or apology. Or nothing at all but a tired man looking at the last piece of his family. "Go home, Miri. It's going to be a long winter, and the light is already going."

Outside, the fog had burned off and the sky was the white of unmarked paper. From the Kirchgasse she could see, two streets over, the gap where the bridge should have shown its arches, showing instead the black stitch of the chain.

* * *

The stranger came to her door at dusk, on the wrong side of the river.

That last part she learned only afterward, and it is the part that makes the memory burn: he had crossed that morning, on the last cart, or under it, or beside it in some fashion the chain was meant to prevent forever after — and so he had come into the town as the lid came down, sealed in with the rest of them, a man trapped in the very act of arriving.

But at the time he was only a knock, two knocks, a hesitation, a third knock — the rhythm of a man deciding, knock by knock, whether he meant it.

Mirabel set down her pen. Eleven plates waited on the map room table, each one pinned under its linen cover, each one a half-finished argument between Herran Voss's intentions and her own patience. Her fingers were cold. The stove needed feeding. Every reasonable instinct told her not to answer, and she answered, because in Verenmoor after dark a knock unanswered was a question the whole street would ask in the morning.

He stood back from the door in the way of a man who does not wish to be seen from the windows across the lane — hood up, collar high, a canvas satchel slung flat against his body so that it made him shapeless. Only when she had opened the door halfway did he lower the hood.

He was younger than his voice had promised. Dark hair cut short and growing out unevenly, a jaw scuffed with a week of beard, eyes that performed a complete inventory of the street behind him before they came to rest on her. When they did, they changed. Something in them deliberately went soft, went harmless.

"You're the copyist," he said. Not a question. "Galloway. The commission's typist."

"Who told you that?"

"A man at the ferry landing, before—" He stopped himself, and the stopping told her more than the sentence had. Before the chain. Before the closing. Before whatever manoeuvre had carried him across. "I'm told you also do private work. Copies. Fair copies. Accurate ones."

"My master is dead," Mirabel said. "His business closed with him."

"Then I'm fortunate the apprentice remains." He reached into the satchel slowly, telegraphing every movement the way one moves near a nervous dog, and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth. He did not offer it yet. He held it against his chest like correspondence from a dead man. "One document. Six pages, perhaps seven. To be copied clean — not summarized, not corrected. Copied exactly, including the errors."

"Including the errors." She repeated it flatly, because it was an odd instruction, and odd instructions were either traps or tests, and she could not yet tell which.

"There are errors worth keeping." His eyes went past her shoulder into the hall, to the map room door standing ajar, to the lamplight falling across the long table and its shrouded plates. Something moved through his face — recognition, hunger, grief — and was gone so quickly she might have imagined it. "You're doing maps."

"I'm finishing work."

"Whose work?"

"That's not your business."

"No," he agreed mildly. "It isn't." And then, softer, almost to himself: "Herr Voss always kept his plates covered. Said uncovered maps invited opinions."

The cold walked up Mirabel's spine on small feet. She had mentioned no names. The lamp behind her threw her shadow long across the doorstep, and in the silence she heard the river working at its banks, patient as a debt.

"Herr Voss has been dead eleven months," she said carefully.

"I know how long he's been dead." The stranger's voice did not rise, but it acquired weight, the way a beam acquires load. "I know it to the week. I carried something for him in the last month he was alive, and I've come a long way to finish carrying it, and now the river is chained and I'm on this side of it until spring. So." He held out the oilcloth packet at last. Up close his knuckles were split from cold, and there was a healing cut above his eyebrow, yellow-green at the edges, four or five days old. "My name doesn't matter tonight. Call me Vey. I need six or seven pages copied exactly, including the errors, by a hand the commission trusts, and I'm told yours is the one. I can pay in silver."

"We don't use silver here. We use ration marks and favours."

"Then I'll pay in favours. I'm told I owe several in this town already."

She did not take the packet. Later — in the cell, in the tribunal room, in the long nights that followed — she would try to reconstruct those thirty seconds at her own doorstep and understand why she hesitated, and she would never quite manage it. Partly it was caution: a stranger who knows Herr Voss's habits and Herr Voss's name and Herr Voss's death-date is a stranger wearing danger like a coat. Partly it was the simple animal wisdom that the chain had gone up that morning precisely to keep the world outside the world, and here was the world, knocking.

And partly — she admitted this only to herself, much later — it was that his eyes had done the thing eyes did when they looked at her master's map room. Most people saw paper. He had seen the man.

"What's in it?" she asked. "The document."

"Names," said Tomas Vey. "Dates. Quantities. What was taken, and by whom, and where it went." He turned the packet over in his cold-split hands. "Errors worth keeping, Fräulein Galloway. Errors with signatures attached."

Downriver, muffled by fog and distance, a bell buoy clanked once against the ebb tide — the sound Verenmoor had heard every night of its existence, the sound that said *the river is open, the river is open*. Tonight it sounded different. Tonight it sounded like a lock turning.

Mirabel thought of her uncle in his good coat, becoming an instrument of order. She thought of the misaligned 'e' printing faithfully on every page she gave the commission, her one small crooked signature on all that straight accountancy. She thought of eleven unfinished plates and five months of winter and the lid coming down on the jar.

"Bring it tomorrow," she said. "Daylight. The front step, not the door. If anyone asks, you want a fair copy of a shipping manifest for a cousin in Hallem."

"A shipping manifest," he repeated gravely.

"For a cousin. In Hallem. Learn it."

Something that was nearly a smile touched the edge of his beard and withdrew. He pulled up his hood, glanced once more down the empty lane — north toward the bridge, south toward the commission house, the inventory of a trapped man — and stepped backward into the dark.

"Tomorrow," he said, and the fog took him.

Mirabel closed the door and stood with her hand still on the latch, listening to the house settle and the river work and, far off, the chain clank in the wind off the water like a struck fence. In the map room the eleven plates lay sleeping under their linen, and on the table her pen had left a small blot spreading on the blotter, black opening outward, like a door into a room she had not known was there.

She fed the stove. She trimmed the lamp. She did not sleep for a long time, and when she did, she dreamed of chains and vowels and a cart crossing the bridge at dawn — the last cart, the very last — and of something hidden beneath it that all the iron in District Fourteen could not keep out.
