## 3. The Clerk's Niece

Aldous Galloway kept his ledgers the way other men kept their consciences: balanced nightly, in a hand so small and even that an auditor once mistook it for print. He was doing so now, at the commission office on the Kesselplatz, with the window shut against the river fog and the stove ticking through its ration of peat, when Captain Reiner set the paper down beside his elbow without knocking.

"The Vey matter," Reiner said. "It wants typing tonight. Clean copy. Your niece does that work, doesn't she?"

Aldous did not look up at once. He finished the figure he was carrying — forty sacks of barley, received in good order, the lie of good order being the whole grammar of his trade — and only then raised his eyes.

"She does transcription for the commission. Yes."

"Then have her do this." Reiner tapped the page. It was written in pencil, in a hand Aldous did not recognize, which was itself unusual; dictation ordinarily came to him by mouth, and he took a certain professional pride in knowing every voice that passed through the commission's business. "Read it first if you like. I know how you like to read things."

He did like. That was what they teased him about at table, when there was a table and company to sit at it: Aldous Galloway, who reads everything twice. He read it then, sitting very still, while the stove ticked and the fog pressed its grey face against the glass, and somewhere below in the town a cart wheel screamed on frost-hardened cobbles, one of the last carts, because tomorrow the bridge would close.

The letter accused Tomas Vey of theft of commission stores. It named dates, quantities, a warehouse key. It was precise in the way that accusations are precise when someone has taken trouble over them, and it would be typed on commission stationery, and it would hang a man.

* * *

That evening he walked home along the quay rather than take the upper road, though the upper road was shorter, because he wanted — he told himself — the air. The truth, which he did not permit himself to examine, was that he wanted to see Verenmoor whole before he did what he was going to do to it. The town lay along the bend of the Sere like handwriting on a ruled line: the tiled roofs furred with frost, the church spire, the coal wharves, and beyond them all the bridge, four arches of grey stone that had carried everything out of Verenmoor and into it for three hundred years. Tomorrow morning a chain would go across its throat and an order would be nailed to the tollhouse, and the season would shut like a book.

He had secured his position that afternoon. That was the phrase he used in his own mind, *secured*, as though he were cargo lashed against weather. Major Hessler had confirmed him as clerk of the occupying commission through the winter closure, with authority over requisitions, inventories, and correspondence, a signature with weight behind it now instead of merely ink. Twenty years he had been a clerk in this town — first to the shipping guild, then to the magistrate, then, when the army came, to the commission, because a clerk serves whatever is written above him; that is what a clerk is. But always he had been a clerk among clerks. Now he was *the* clerk, and the word had a taste to it, ironish, like water from a new pipe.

And Mirabel. He turned the corner by the salt shed and saw her window lit, the yellow square of it above the chandlery where they lodged, and felt the old ache that he had learned to live with the way one lives with a stone in the boot — by not stopping, by keeping the pace even.

Her parents had gone out on the river fourteen years ago, on the last week of October, ferrying survey instruments upriver for her father's work. Her father, Jonas, had been a mapmaker of the small meticulous kind that river towns produce: charts of channels, plans of drainage, nothing military, nothing dangerous. The boat had been found overturned below the weir. Nothing else was ever found. Mirabel had been six years old, standing on the quay in a coat too big for her, and Aldous, who had never married, who had a room with one bed and a ledger-line life, had simply picked her up and carried her home, and no one — not the magistrate, not the priest, not the gossips at the fish market — had ever suggested anyone else should have.

He had raised her badly or well, he could not decide which, by the standards available to him. He had taught her figures before stories. He had taught her that a document is a promise made visible, and that the man who keeps the documents keeps the town's memory, and that memory is a form of power, though he had not used that word to her. He had not taught her tenderness, because he did not have any surplus of it to teach, but he had discovered too late that children manufacture their own, that she had grown soft-hearted in the gaps between his rules, generous in ways that alarmed him. She gave away bread. She remembered servants' names. She wept at the strangest things — not at funerals but at a goose flying over in fog, calling, lost.

And she had gone to Corvinus, her master, and learned his trade, and loved him, and when they shot him in the market square in the spring for mapping troop movements for the resistance, she had not wept at all, which frightened Aldous more than weeping would have. She had simply gone back to the map room the next day and begun cataloguing the atlas, plate by plate, as if grief were a filing error she intended to correct.

*The atlas.* He thought of it the way a man thinks of a gun left loaded in a neighbor's house. Corvinus had died for those maps; they were evidence, they were treason rendered in ink, and the girl kept them in the cold room behind the chandlery as though careful cataloguing were the same thing as safety. He had told her twice, plainly, to burn them. She had looked at him with her mother's level eyes and said, *They're finished or nearly finished. You don't burn a thing that's finished,* and he had not pressed her, because pressing her was the one lever he had never learned to use.

* * *

At home she had soup on the stove and the battered typewriter on the table, its case open, its keys gleaming dully in the lamplight. She typed for the commission three days a week — neat, invisible, unremarkable work, exactly the sort of work he had arranged for her precisely so that she would remain neat, invisible, unremarkable. A woman alone in a garrisoned town is safest as furniture. He had built her into the walls of the commission's ordinary business like a brick, and bricks do not get questioned.

"Reiner brought something," he said, taking off his coat. "An accusation. It needs clean copy tonight."

She looked up. In the lamplight her face had the stillness she'd inherited from nobody he could name — her mother had been animated, quick as water. "What kind of accusation?"

"Theft of commission stores. A man called Vey."

"Tomas Vey." She said the name carefully, tasting it. "The stranger. The one who came across the day before the chain went up. He asked me to copy something, the day the bridge closed. A bill of lading, he said."

Aldous felt a small cold movement in his chest, like ice shifting in a barrel. "Did you?"

"I copied the bill of lading. It was a bill of lading." She frowned at him. "Uncle, why? What did he steal?"

"That's not for us to ask. We type what we're given to type." He set the penciled sheets on the table, squared their edges, and found his hands were being very clerkly, which was how he knew they wanted to shake. "Read it aloud as you go, if the phrasing gives you trouble. I'll be in my room with the Hessler correspondence."

But he stayed. He told himself he stayed to assist with the difficult passages, the technical language of stores and inventory, which was his province. He sat across the table with the lamp between them and listened to his niece's fingers begin to fall on the keys, that particular rain-like rhythm he had heard a thousand evenings and never once thought of as anything but comforting.

The letter began. *To the tribunal of the occupying commission, Verenmoor district. It has come to the attention of the undersigned...*

"It isn't signed," Mirabel said, pausing. "There's no name under 'undersigned.'"

"The captain will sign it after. Type it as it stands."

She typed. *It has come to the attention of the undersigned that one Tomas Vey, lately arrived in this district, did on divers occasions between the twelfth and twentieth of the month remove from commission stores at the coal wharf the following articles...*

Aldous watched the sentences assemble themselves in black and white, each keystroke fixing them a little further into the world. This was the thing about typing that he had tried once to explain to Mirabel and failed: handwriting confesses, handwriting is a face, but typescript is a mask. Any machine makes the same letters. Any hand can hold the same keys. A typed accusation floats free of everyone who touched it, and lands on the man it names.

He had checked the facts. That was what he kept returning to, later, in the dark of his narrow room, turning the night over like a coin to inspect both faces. He had checked them because checking was his nature, and the facts were — plausible. Stores *had* run short in November; the tallies showed discrepancies; a warehouse key *was* missing from the board. Whether those discrepancies pointed at Tomas Vey or elsewhere was a question Aldous had not been given time to pursue, and he noticed, with a discomfort he filed under *fatigue*, that the discrepancies also happened to touch the requisitions that passed through Captain Reiner's own signature.

No. He put that thought down firmly, the way one puts down another man's property. Reiner was a captain of the occupation, vain and quick-tempered, but he was not a thief; the army did not make thieves of men of his rank; and even if it had, that would not be Aldous's affair. His affair was this: a man had come to Verenmoor two days before the closing of the bridge, on the wrong side of the river, with papers that did not entirely explain him, asking quiet questions in the fish market about the late Master Corvinus. Mirabel had told him that much herself, in her flat way, not knowing it was damning. *He asked whether Corvinus had left any papers behind. I said the atlas wasn't finished and none of it concerned troop movements.*

A stranger asking after the papers of an executed traitor, in the same fortnight that commission stores went astray. Two wrongnesses in one man. Perhaps the second explained the first; perhaps the first explained the second; either way, the commission had decided, and the commission's decision needed typing, and typing was not deciding. That was the sentence Aldous built for himself, brick by patient brick, and stood inside: *typing is not deciding.*

He was a clerk. Clerks did not choose what was true; they recorded what was asserted. If the tribunal condemned an innocent man, the fault would lie with the tribunal, with Reiner, with the war, with the fog on the river — with anything in the world except the small even hand that had carried the pencil pages to the table where his niece sat, and the voice that had said, *have her do this work tonight.*

Except that he had chosen her. That was the splinter in the joint of the argument, the place where the whole construction creaked. Reiner had said *your niece does that work*, and Aldous could have said many things in reply. He could have said, *I will type it myself, my eyes are better at night.* He could have said, *the commission's typist is ill.* He could have said, *let me see the evidence first, captain, since I keep the stores ledger and the discrepancy may be mine to explain.* Any of these. Instead he had said, "She'll have it done by nine o'clock," because — he examined the reason honestly, holding it up to the lamp of himself, and found it small and true — because if he typed it, it was his. Under his hand, in his hours, it became Aldous Galloway's letter, Aldous Galloway's accusation, Aldous Galloway's hanging if it went wrong. Passed to Mirabel, it was commission work, typed by a typist, on commission paper, in commission ink. She was his wall, and he was hiding behind her.

The carriage return rang. She rolled the platen and began the final paragraph, and he realized he had not heard a word of it, and made himself listen.

*—and the undersigned respectfully submits that the said Tomas Vey be held for trial at the convenience of the tribunal, the bridge being closed and no flight being possible.*

*No flight being possible.* The town sealed like a jar, and the accusation typed the same night, and the trial to follow at leisure, because the accused could not leave and neither could doubt, neither could appeal, neither could winter end until the powers across the river permitted it. Whoever had drafted that pencil original — that hand he did not know — understood Verenmoor perfectly.

Mirabel pulled the last sheet from the platen and laid the pages in a squared stack, and read them through once, the way he had taught her to, hunting errors.

"There's a phrase in here that isn't commission style," she said. "*Divers occasions.* Nobody at the commission writes *divers*. And it says *stores at the coal wharf* — you always say *wharfside stores*, you've corrected me on it twice." She looked up at him across the lamp, and her eyes were her mother's eyes, level and clear, and for one terrible moment he thought she would ask him outright: *Who wrote this? Did you write this? Is it true?*

Instead she said, "Shall I leave it as written?"

Clerks record what is asserted. He heard himself say it — not the maxim, but its equivalent, its human translation: "As written. Copy means copy, Mirabel. It goes to Reiner in the morning."

She wrapped the pages in brown paper and string, and her fingers were steady, and he thought, with relief that tasted of ash, *she suspects nothing, she is safe, I have kept her safe.* And beneath the relief, so deep down that it registered only as a faint nausea he attributed to the peat smoke, another thought lay folded like a document in a drawer that would not quite close:

She had recognized his corrections. She knew his phrasing. And she had seen that this letter was not his.

Which meant that some part of his niece already knew that the man who had raised her had carried a stranger's accusation into their kitchen and set it on her typewriter, and had chosen, for the sake of a title and a signature and a wall to hide behind, to let her hands do the writing anyway.

Aldous went to his narrow room and did not balance the day's ledger. For the first time in twenty years, he left a column uncarried.

Across the landing he could hear her moving about the kitchen, washing the supper dishes, and then a silence, and then — unmistakable, though he told himself he must have imagined it — the sound of the typewriter again, one key struck softly, twice, three times, as if she were testing something.

In the morning the letter would go to Reiner. By evening, he knew without knowing how he knew, there would be soldiers at the fish market, and Tomas Vey would be in the cold cell under the tollhouse, and the sealed town would have begun to turn on itself like a clock with a bent spring.

He blew out his lamp and lay in the dark listening to the river, which he had lived beside for fifty years and which had never once told him anything he wanted to hear.
