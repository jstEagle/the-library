## 4. Ink Under the Fingernails

The commission's typewriter sat on the clerk's desk like a small black cathedral, and Mirabel Galloway had long ago made her peace with kneeling before it.

She had cleaned the platen that morning with a rag dipped in paraffin, working the rollers until they turned without a whisper, because a sticky platen produced uneven impressions and uneven impressions invited questions — and in Verenmoor that winter, questions were the one commodity more plentiful than river ice. Outside the commission house window, men were hauling the last of the season's coal down to the wharf under lantern light, their breath rising in columns, and the bridge across the Vell stood dark and dripping in the gray afternoon, already wearing its new chain like a collar being fitted.

She rolled a fresh sheet into the machine. Her uncle's handwriting lay on the desk beside her: *Aldous Galloway, Clerk to the Occupying Commission*, his neat, upright script, each letter standing at attention the way Aldous himself stood, the way he had taught her to stand when inspectors came through the map room. She had known his hand for eleven years. She could have drawn it as a font, every quirk of it — the way his *t* crossed with a flat, impatient stroke, the little hooks on his descenders. She had learned letters from this man before she had learned maps from anyone.

The draft was an accusation. It said so in its first line, in words she did not recognize.

*To the Commission of Occupation at Verenmoor: I make formal report that the man Tomas Vey, lately of the far bank, did on or about the fourteenth day of November take and carry off stores belonging to said Commission, to wit:*

Her fingers hovered. She read it again, thinking she must have misread.

It was not the content that stopped her — though the content was enough, theft of commission stores in a sealed town meant the tribunal, and the tribunal in a sealed town meant the cellar of the customs house where no one heard anything ever again. It was the sentences themselves. They were wrong. Not legally wrong; they were constructed with a lawyer's care, clauses stacked like sandbags. But they were not Aldous's sentences. Aldous wrote the way he spoke, which was to say in circles, qualifying himself into corners, unable to issue even a requisition for lamp oil without three sub-clauses of apology. *I regret to note, if the Commission concurs, though the matter is hardly mine to press.* His prose apologized for existing. This letter marched. This letter had been drilled by someone who knew exactly what a tribunal required to convict and had laid those requirements out in order, item by item, like a place setting.

Someone had dictated this to her uncle. Or her uncle had copied it from someone else's hand, which amounted to the same thing.

Mirabel looked up. The outer office was empty. Down the corridor, boots passed — Reiner's people kept later hours than the clerks did — and somewhere below, the great iron stove in the entry hall ticked as it cooled. She looked back at the draft and told herself what she had been telling herself since the bridge closed: that she transcribed, and what she transcribed was none of hers. That was the whole arrangement. That was why she was permitted to sit at this desk at all, an apprentice girl with no name worth entering in any ledger, useful precisely because she was nothing but hands.

She began to type.

The machine was a Continental, older than the war, older probably than Mirabel, with keys that required a full deliberate pressure, a confession of force in every stroke. Her master — she would not think of Herr Anselm, not now, not with this letter under her fingers — her master had taught her that a typewriter was honest in a way no pen could be, because it committed. A pen hesitated; ink could be blotted, a word half-formed abandoned. But a type-bar struck once and struck through, and there the letter was, punched into the page's very grain. Every document was therefore a kind of map of the moment it was made, showing exactly where the maker's hand had pressed and how hard, and Mirabel had spent six years learning to read such maps: which machines, which operators, which habits of spacing and emphasis betrayed themselves on paper like footprints in snow.

So she typed cleanly. Evenly. No hesitation marks. She was invisible even in her own impressions, or she had better be.

*...did take and carry off stores belonging to said Commission...*

The ribbon was old and the word *Commission* came out grayer than the rest, and she struck it again carefully, over the same impression, because a faded word was another footprint. Under her fingernails, always, no matter how often she scrubbed: the ghost of carbon black, the ink that never fully left. Some evenings she examined her hands under the map room's shaded lamp and thought they belonged to someone else entirely — a printer's hands, a counterfeiter's hands, hands marked forever by everything they had reproduced.

Outside, the chain went taut.

She heard it before she understood it — a groan of iron taking strain, carried across the water and up through the window glass, low and enormous, like the river itself clearing its throat. Then voices, then a whistle, three notes descending, the commission's signal. She turned in time to see, across the square of frosted glass, lanterns moving onto the bridge, and the shape of the workmen gathering at its far end where the great chain-link barrier was being wound shut around the stone piers. The last cart had crossed an hour ago; she had watched it from the window while cleaning the platen, a farmer with cabbages under tarpaulin, the wheels ringing on the frost-slick planks, and she had felt the peculiar grief of watching a door close slowly, all day, all week, all month, and only now arriving at the latch.

Verenmoor was sealed. Whatever came out of this winter would come out in spring, or it would come out in paper.

She turned back to the machine.

*I further state that the said Vey did act in concert with persons unknown, and did intend the said stores for the use of armed resistance—*

There. She stopped, her fingers resting on the keys, the sentence unfinished above them.

That was not in the draft. She checked. She set the sheet beside Aldous's handwriting and read the corresponding passage twice, three times, running her fingertip along the lines. The draft said *did act in concert with persons unknown.* Full stop. It went on to request detention pending investigation — politely, even. It did not say *armed resistance.* It did not need to; the tribunal needed no encouragement toward that conclusion, and a careful author would not offer it. But someone had offered it. Someone had wanted the word *armed* in the record, wanted it typed in a fair clerk's hand, unimpeachable, official.

And she was about to supply it.

*You are only the pen,* she told herself, and the words had a familiar ring, because her uncle had said something like them to her on her first day at this desk: *You're my pen, Mira. A pen isn't asked what it writes.* He had said it kindly, wrapping her in it like a coat against the cold of the commission house. And she had worn it all autumn.

But a pen didn't notice when a sentence wasn't in the draft. A pen didn't pause. She was pausing. Which meant she was already, in some small and dangerous way, no longer only a pen — and the noticing itself felt like a crack running through the clean ice of her routine, a hairline she could hear spreading in the silence.

She typed it. *Armed resistance.* The keys fell. The letters were in the page now, in the grain, and there was no blotting a type-bar's commitment. She signed nothing — transcription bore no signature; that was the point of her — and dated it as instructed and fed the second copy through, and when both sheets were done she squared their edges against the desk and sat looking at them while the lanterns outside finished their slow procession across the bridge and the chain groaned once more, final, like a coffin lid seating.

Then she took the copies down the corridor to the commission's out-tray, because that was the arrangement, and arrangements were the only solid things left in Verenmoor.

* * *

She found her uncle at home, in the rooms above the chandler's shop, doing what he always did on closing days: sitting by the stove with the ledger of the household accounts open on his knee and not reading it. The lamp was turned low. He had poured himself the finger of schnapps that appeared on nights like this and only nights like this, and he looked up when she came in with the expression of a man greeting a verdict.

"They closed it," she said. "I heard."

"Quarter past five." He closed the ledger. "The engineer wanted one more day. Reiner wouldn't give it to him." He said the name without inflection, which was how Aldous said names that mattered.

Mirabel hung her coat on the peg and stood warming her hands at the stove, and considered, for the length of time it takes water to decide to boil, whether to ask. Eleven years of living with this man had taught her the geography of his silences — the ones that meant tiredness, the ones that meant money, the ones that meant her parents, spoken of so rarely they had worn smooth as river stones. And then there was a fourth kind, rarer, which meant shame, and she had heard its particular weather in his voice tonight when he'd handed her the draft that morning without meeting her eyes.

"The Vey letter," she said.

"Mm." He did not look up from the ledger.

"Whose phrasing was it?"

For a moment the only sound was the stove's ticking and, faint and far, the river worrying at the pilings under the bridge it was about to be denied. Aldous turned a page he had not read. "It came through channels," he said. "Channels write better than clerks do. You noticed."

"I noticed it wasn't yours."

"That," said her uncle, "is a careful observation, and you should put it away." Now he did look up, and his face in the lamplight was the face she had known since she was seven years old and carried off a boat with nothing but a wet coat and her mother's compass, the face that had fed her and taught her letters and never once, in eleven years, raised his voice. "Mira. There is a man named Tomas Vey who came over the bridge two days ago on the wrong side of history, and there are stores missing from warehouse three, and there is a captain who wants the matter tidy before the freeze finishes. None of these things are going to change because a transcriptionist has opinions about syntax. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

She understood he was telling her the truth in pieces, the way he always did, and hiding one piece behind his back.

"What happens to him?" she asked.

"An inquiry." He said it the way men say *spring,* meaning eventually, meaning perhaps. "He'll be held while it proceeds. It may come to nothing." He rose, crossed the room, and put his hand briefly on her head, the old gesture from childhood, benediction and dismissal at once. "You typed it well. Go to bed."

She went to bed. She did not sleep. She lay listening to the sealed town — the absence of carts, the absence of the far-bank bell that had marked the quarter-hours all her life and was now silent on the other side of a chain — and she turned the letter over in the dark, sentence by sentence, the way she turned plates of her dead master's atlas, hunting for the error that would let her put it down. There was no error. That was what frightened her. The letter was perfect, and perfection in Aldous's office had only ever arrived from above.

Near midnight she heard him in the next room, walking. Floorboard, floorboard, pause. Floorboard, floorboard, pause. A man pacing out the dimensions of something he could not map.

* * *

They arrested Tomas Vey at dusk the following evening, and the whole town watched, because a sealed town will watch anything.

Mirabel was coming back from the wharf with lamp oil when it happened — she saw it from the corner of Fishergate, and could not have looked away any more than she could have refused to hear it. Two gendarmes, then Captain Reiner himself in his gray coat with the collar up, unhurried, almost courteous, and between them a tall man with river mud frozen gray on his boot-tops, whom she recognized even at forty paces because she had sold him bread that morning at the bakery stall without knowing his face was already in a document under her fingertips. Tomas Vey did not struggle. That was the detail that lodged in her like a splinter. He walked between the gendarmes with his head up and his hands loose at his sides, and halfway down Fishergate he stopped — the escort stopped with him, surprised — and he turned and looked directly across the square, past the shuttered fountain, past the coal sacks, at her.

She did not know him. There was no reason on earth he should know her. But his eyes found her among the watchers the way a needle finds north, and he held her gaze for one breath, two, with an expression she could not read — appeal, or warning, or recognition — and then Reiner said something quiet and the small procession moved on toward the customs house, toward the cells below it, and the crowd exhaled and began, in the manner of crowds, to pretend it had seen nothing.

Mirabel stood in the falling cold with the lamp oil growing heavy in her arm.

He had looked at her. Not at the gendarmes, not at Reiner, not at the townsfolk whose testimony might save or hang him. At her. As if he knew — impossible, but the knowledge sat in her chest like swallowed ice — as if he knew whose fingers had driven the keys that put him in the cellar, and was asking her something.

She walked home the long way, past the dark windows of the print shop and the boarded café, and by the time she reached the chandler's stair she had stopped telling herself the comfortable things. The phrasing was not her uncle's. The word *armed* was not in the draft. The letter was too good, and good accusations do not assemble themselves in clerks' offices, and clerks who are ashamed pace their floors at midnight. She climbed the stairs quietly, hoping her uncle would be asleep, and saw instead, through the door's gap, lamplight, and Aldous at the table with his household ledger open, and on top of the ledger — she saw it only a moment, only an edge of it before she made her presence known with a cough — a second paper, a commission envelope, addressed in a hand that was neither his nor hers nor any clerk's she knew: a hard, slanted, military hand she had seen once before, at the bottom of an order posting the bridge closure.

Reiner's hand. In her uncle's rooms. After the letter was filed.

Aldous covered it smoothly, greeted her, asked about the oil, commented on the cold. She answered. They ate the stew she reheated, and spoke of the price of salt, and beneath the table her hands were steady and her heart was not, and when she finally blew out her candle she lay in the dark with ink ghosts under her fingernails and one thought turning over like a coin that would not lie flat:

She had typed the letter. She could not untake that step. But the letter had a twin somewhere, a source, a hand behind Aldous's hand — and if she was going to live in this sealed town until spring, she was going to find out whose it was, starting with the paper her uncle was hiding under the household accounts, and God help them both with what she did after.

Down the hall, the floorboards began again. Floorboard, floorboard, pause. Like a man pacing out a cell he couldn't see the walls of yet.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would go back to the commission office. Tomorrow she would pull the filed copy and compare it against her own — impression by impression, letter by letter, the way her master taught her, the way no one in Verenmoor could do but her.

What she found when she did would break the winter open.
