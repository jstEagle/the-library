## 6. Tomas in the Cold Cell

The cell was not built for men. It had been a root cellar once — Tomas could see it in the shape of the ceiling, the low brick vault made for turnips and apples and the slow dark work of winter — and then some clerk with a stamp had decided that anything with one door and no windows would serve. A pallet. A bucket. A candle stub in a tin dish, burning with the apologetic flame of something meant to be forgotten.

He sat on the pallet with his back against the wall and counted what he still owned. His coat was gone; they'd taken it for the lice. His boots remained, minus the laces. In the lining of his right boot heel, folded to the size of a postage stamp, there had been a scrap of oilcloth and beneath the oilcloth nothing at all now but the ghost of where it used to be — because he had already delivered it, months ago, to a dead man's apprentice who didn't know what she'd been handed.

That was the arithmetic he kept returning to. Everything he had carried, everything he had crossed the river twice in winter for, every mile of frostbite and forged passes — all of it was sitting three streets away under the floorboards of a map room, or so he prayed, while he sat in a root cellar waiting to be shot for a theft he hadn't committed.

He laughed. It came out wrong, a dry click in his throat, and the candle guttered as if startled.

Outside, Verenmoor went about its evening. He could hear it through the stone — the clatter of the commission's lorries, a woman calling a child in by name, the groan of the bridge chain being tested one more time before the river froze it in for the season. That sound had followed him for four hundred kilometers. The chain across the bridge was the sound of his own trap closing, and he had walked into it anyway, and the strange part — the part he kept turning over like a coin worn smooth — was that he wasn't sorry.

* * *

Herren Geist had died on a Tuesday.

Tomas knew this the way he knew most things that mattered: secondhand, late, and in fragments. The news had reached the resistance cell across the river eleven days after the execution, carried by a fisherman who charged triple for news the way other men charged for eels. *The mapmaker. The old one on the Kesselstraat. They took him out behind the powder magazine.* No ceremony. No body released. Just an old man with ink-stained fingers standing in the slush at dawn, refusing the blindfold, and a volley, and then a town that pretended it hadn't heard.

Tomas had known him as Master Geist, though his name was printed in three languages on the atlas prospectus — *Hendrik Geist, Cartographer Royal by appointment,* a title that had stopped meaning anything the day the commission marched over the bridge. Tomas had worked for him for two winters, back when working for a mapmaker meant humping a surveyor's chain through frozen marshland and holding measuring poles straight in wind that came off the river like a knife. He'd thought he was hired for his shoulders. He'd been wrong from the start.

"You have a good memory," the old man told him the first time they crossed into occupied territory to take soundings near the weir. "Better than mine, and I've spent sixty years filling mine up. But that isn't why I keep you."

"Why, then?"

"Because you complain." Geist hadn't looked up from his field book, pencil moving in those quick precise strokes that Tomas later understood were two things at once — depth measurements on the left page, and on the right, coded in the margins among tide tables and barometric readings, the strength and position of every patrol, every emplacement, every gun the commission thought the fog concealed. "A man who complains is paying attention. Contentment is blindness. You grumble about the cold, which means you know exactly how cold it is, which means you notice when things are colder than they should be. Keep complaining, Vey."

It had been a joke. It had also been a recruitment, and Tomas hadn't understood until the night Geist pressed a sealed packet into his hands and said, "Across the bridge, before the sentry changes. Don't run. Men who run are remembered."

* * *

The last map. He'd called it that himself afterward, in his head, though Geist never named his work. It had been the thickest packet yet — not paper but plates, thin zinc sheets wrapped in oilcloth and strapped flat against Tomas's chest beneath his shirt, cold enough at first touch to raise welts on his skin. Geist's instructions had been simple: cross the river, deliver the plates to the print shop on the free side, wait for confirmation, come back if you can, stay if you can't.

"And you?" Tomas had asked. "When do you cross?"

"I don't." The old man had been sealing a letter, dripping candle wax onto it with the same unhurried care he brought to everything. "A mapmaker leaves last, boy. If I go early, they burn the map room. And everything in the map room—" he'd paused, wax hissing off the stick, "—is worth more than either of us. More than me certainly. Possibly more than everyone."

Tomas had crossed that night with the plates riding his heartbeat like a second ribcage. He had crossed past the sentries who nodded at him, because they knew him, because he was the mapmaker's man, because nobody looks hard at a man whose face is boring. He had delivered the zinc to the printer — a heavy, silent man named Brandt with flour-white hands and a daughter who watched from the stairs — and waited out the week, and then, because he was young and stupid and loyal in roughly equal measure, he had gone back.

He'd told himself it was for the rest of the archive. There were other plates, other papers, a whole atlas in progress — *Verenmoor and the River Country: A Complete Survey*, twelve years of Geist's life, the thing the old man guarded the way other men guard children. The resistance wanted it. The commission wanted it too, though they didn't know yet how much they wanted it, and the race between those two wants was going to kill people. Tomas had understood even then that it would kill people. He had simply believed — the way the young believe, cheaply, without receipts — that it wouldn't kill him.

Then Geist was arrested, and eleven days later he was dead, and the packet of his final effects — the things the commission found in the map room, catalogued, confiscated — included a list of names. Tomas never saw the list. He only knew that two weeks after the execution, a message reached him across the river: *They have your description. Stay on the free side.*

So he stayed. Four hundred kilometers of exile in a country that spoke a version of his language with flattened vowels, doing dockwork in a port city, sleeping in a bunkhouse with nine other fugitives who each told the story of their crossing differently every time they told it. He wrote letters through intermediaries. He received almost none. The war ground on around him like a millstone, and the armistice talks began, and stalled, and began again, and somewhere south of all of it Herren Hendrik Geist lay in an unmarked grave outside a town Tomas could draw from memory down to the weathervanes.

That was the thing about maps. You could leave a place, and it would not leave you. He could close his eyes and walk Verenmoor street by street: the Kesselstraat with its sagging gables, the print shop with its smell of lead and lampblack, the commission house with its lit windows at midnight, and the map room on its upper floor where a girl he'd met precisely twice sat transcribing requisitions on a battered typewriter.

Mirabel Galloway. The master's apprentice. He had thought of her more than was sensible for a man who had exchanged perhaps forty words with her, all of them about paper weights and drying times. She had the same habit Geist did — the tilt of the head when she checked a measurement, the way she held her pen like it might bolt. And she was still there, inside the walls, with the atlas. Every piece of intelligence that trickled across the river confirmed it: the apprentice had stayed, the map room was intact, the commission used her hands for clerical work without ever asking what else those hands could do.

Which meant the atlas was sitting there like a keg of powder in a house full of smoking men, and the only person guarding it was a girl of twenty-odd with ink under her fingernails.

* * *

He had argued with himself across the whole journey back, and lost in stages.

The first argument was practical: the armistice was coming, they said, weeks maybe, and when it came the commission would evacuate, and during evacuations things get burned. Records get burned. Evidence gets burned. If Geist's atlas fell into a brazier, twelve years of the old man's life would become smoke, and along with it the proof — the beautiful, damning, documented proof in Geist's marginalia — of what certain officers had been stealing from the occupied towns while writing reports about banditry. The old man hadn't just mapped troop movements. He'd mapped the ledger frauds too, quietly, in his tidy coded hand, because a man who complains is paying attention, and Geist had paid attention for six years.

The second argument was duty: the resistance committee had voted — narrowly, acrimoniously — that the atlas must be recovered and moved to the capital, where it would do its work at whatever reckoning came after the war. Someone had to go. Everyone else had reasons. Tomas had none that outweighed a dead man's legacy rotting in a cellar.

The third argument he never said aloud to anyone, because it was the weakest and therefore the truest: he wanted to see the town again. Not the war-town, not the occupied thing with its chains and checkpoints — the town underneath, the one that had fed him in his twenties, that had taught him the river's moods, that held a grave he owed a visit. And, if he was honest in the dark hours of the road, a girl at a typewriter whom he'd thought about with an embarrassing persistence for a man with no claim whatsoever.

He had crossed the bridge on the last open evening, on papers Odile's network had forged so beautifully that he'd felt a flicker of professional admiration. The sentry had glanced at the pass, glanced at him, waved him through. He'd made it as far as the Kesselstraat. He'd stood in the doorway of the map room and asked Mirabel Galloway to copy a document — cover business, legitimate enough — and watched her fingers find the keys, and thought, *I can be gone again by Thursday.*

Then a sergeant and two men with rifles had come up the stairs, and someone said the word *theft,* and the word had his name attached to it, and Thursday had turned into a root cellar with a candle stub.

* * *

The worst of it wasn't the cold. The cold was honest; it told you plainly what it intended to take and you bargained with it hour by hour, stamping your feet, doing the exercises a sergeant had taught him a lifetime ago. The worst was the letter.

They had shown it to him at his arrest — flashed it, actually, the way you'd flash a warrant, giving him half a second that was somehow worse than reading it. Typed. Commission stationery. An accusation in careful official prose: *Tomas Vey, described as follows, did on the night of the—* steal stores belonging to the occupying commission, stores that Tomas happened to know had never existed except in the ledgers of a man who was stealing them himself. He had read enough of Geist's marginalia to know exactly what kind of officer needed a thief invented, and why a stranger newly arrived over the bridge with forged papers would make such a convenient one.

But it wasn't the accusation that kept him pacing the four steps of the cellar. It was the typing.

He had seen Mirabel Galloway type. One page, one evening, but his memory was the good kind, the complaining kind, the kind that paid attention. Her rhythm had a signature to it — a hesitation before capitals, a light strike, the little pause-and-correct when a line drifted. The letter they'd flashed at him had none of that looseness. Or it had too much of something else. He couldn't articulate it even to himself; he only knew that something in the artifact was wrong, the way a compass is wrong near iron, the way a map is wrong when someone has faked a coastline.

And beneath that wrongness, fainter, like a watermark held to the light: the letter had been typed on a machine with a familiar defect. He'd seen pages from that machine pinned above Geist's desk for years. The old master's own typewriter, the one that vanished from the map room the day of the arrest.

Someone in Verenmoor had it. Someone in Verenmoor had typed his death warrant on a dead man's machine.

Tomas sat back down on the pallet and pulled the blanket — a single army-gray thing with the smell of other prisoners in its weave — around his shoulders, and made himself think it through, because thinking was the only property they couldn't confiscate.

If the letter was typed on Geist's machine, then whoever held the machine was connected to the master's arrest. Either a looter, or a friend who'd smuggled it out first, and friends don't frame strangers. Which meant the machine-holder and the framer were different hands, and the framer had borrowed — or was hunting — the other. Two threads, and somewhere between them the truth, tangled like wire.

If he was condemned on a forged letter, the forgery could be proven. Typewriters had fingerprints, Geist had taught him that much, holding two pages to the window one winter afternoon: *See? This 'e' sits low and left, always, like a tooth gone crooked. Machines confess, Vey. People lie, but machines confess.* If the exhibit letter and the machine could be brought together in front of the tribunal—

But the tribunal was the commission's tribunal. Bringing the machine forward meant exposing whoever had hidden it, and anyone hiding Geist's typewriter in an occupied town was guilty of something far worse than theft in the commission's eyes. Saving himself meant endangering whoever had kept faith with his dead master. And there was a girl in this town, an apprentice with ink on her fingers, who had no idea that the document she'd transcribed last week had been used to hang a target around his neck — or did she know? Had she typed it? Was her hand in it? He turned that question over and over until the candle burned down to a blue nub and the cellar went black, and still he couldn't answer it, and the not-knowing hurt worse than the cold.

Footsteps on the cellar stair, near midnight. Not the guard's shuffle — lighter, quicker, pausing twice on the worn treads. Tomas rose from the pallet, heart hammering with something that was not entirely fear, as a key scraped in the lock and the door swung open a hand's width, spilling a blade of lamplight across the floor.

Behind it, barely visible in the dark beyond the lamp, stood a figure holding the light steady — and a voice, low and urgent, that he recognized from a doorway on the Kesselstraat, from a better life, from a page he'd watched being typed.

"Master Vey," Mirabel Galloway whispered. "We haven't much time. Tell me exactly what you stole — and don't lie to me, because I've come to prove you stole nothing at all."
