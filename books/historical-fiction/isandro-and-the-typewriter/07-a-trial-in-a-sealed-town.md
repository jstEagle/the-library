## 7. A Trial in a Sealed Town

The tribunal convened in the wool exchange, because it was the only hall in Verenmoor large enough to hold a judgment and the town's appetite for one.

Mirabel arrived early, in the grey hour when the lamplighter was still working his way down the Kessinstraat with his long pole, snuffing the flames one by one as if the day could be put out before it began. Frost furred the iron railings. The river ran beneath the bridge in its winter voice — low, grinding, patient — and beyond it the far bank was a white smudge that might have been another country entirely, which in every way that mattered now, it was.

She had not slept. She had sat at her narrow window until the candles burned down to their brass dishes, turning over what she would do in the morning the way she turned a plate under her fingers before inking it, feeling for flaws. She had settled on nothing braver than this: she would go, and she would watch, and she would keep her hands folded like any other townswoman come to see justice done. Watching was safe. Watching was invisible.

The wool exchange had been stripped of its trade for the duration. The long trestles where bales of fleece had been graded were pushed against the walls, and in their place stood a single table draped in a commission-grey cloth, behind which three officers sat in a row. Captain Reiner occupied the center chair. He wore his grief like a decoration — the grave, downward cast of his eyes, the slow way he removed his gloves finger by finger, as though the business before him were too sorrowful to be hurried. At his right hand sat a lieutenant Mirabel did not know, young enough that his collar chafed him; at his left, taking notes in a small black book, sat her uncle.

Aldous did not look up when she came in. That was the arrangement between them, unspoken but ironclad: in public he did not know her any more intimately than a clerk knows the girl who types his papers. She found a place near the back, standing, because the benches had been claimed before dawn by women with baskets they did not open and men who smelled of stove smoke and anticipation. The hall filled the way a glass fills with dark beer — slowly at the edges, then all at once, froth and noise.

At half past nine, soldiers brought Tomas Vey up from the cell beneath the customs house.

He walked between two of them without being held, which somehow made it worse, as if he had agreed to this, as if the whole apparatus were something he had signed. His coat was the same coat — river-grey, patched at the elbow — and his hands were bare, and he flexed them once at his sides as though missing the weight of something he usually carried. He had been given no chair. He stood at a chalk line drawn on the floorboards, facing the table, and the hall went quiet in the particular way of towns: not silent, never silent, but lowered to a murmur, a collective held breath with coughs in it.

Mirabel's stomach had clenched itself into a knot the size of a walnut since the moment he entered, and it did not loosen. This was the man whose document she had copied on her first week — a bill of lading, nothing, she hadn't even remembered his face until the arrest — and then the man whose accusation she had typed with her own two hands, ten fingers moving over the keys in the cold map room while her uncle stood behind her left shoulder reading out the words.

*The undersigned witnesses depose that the party Tomas Vey did, on the night of the ninth of December, remove from commission stores at the granary wharf the following goods...*

Her typing. Her machine. Her hands.

"Proceed," Reiner said, and the clerk of the tribunal — a pale local man borrowed from the tithe office, who kept wetting his lips — read the charge aloud. Theft of commission stores. Goods to the value of four hundred marks. And then, in a voice that gathered weight as it went, *to the detriment of the occupying authority, in time of war*, which was the phrase that carried a man past a fine and a flogging and out toward the wall behind the powder magazine.

The evidence was produced.

There was only the one piece of it. A soldier carried it forward on a wooden board like a reliquary — a single typed sheet, laid flat, and the clerk of the tribunal held it up so the hall could see, though at twenty paces no one could read a word, and everyone craned anyway.

"The confession and inventory of the accused," the clerk announced, "found in the possession of the accused at the time of his apprehension, bearing—"

"Bearing his signature," Reiner said quietly, and the quiet carried better than a shout. "Which we will hear read."

Mirabel did not listen to the words. She had written them; she knew them the way she knew the shape of her own teeth. What she watched instead was the paper itself, tilted now toward the light from the high windows, and it was the light that did it.

The sheet caught the glare at an angle, just for a moment, as the clerk turned to hand it across the table, and Mirabel saw the type leap off the page in raised shadow — inked letters standing proud of the paper, each character struck hard, slightly unevenly, the way an old machine strikes when its escapement has begun to wear. And even at that distance, even blurred, something about the alignment snagged in her eye the way a crooked picture frame snags in a room.

She told herself she was imagining it. She spent the next quarter hour of testimony — the granary watchman, nervous; a carter who said he'd seen a figure on the wharf road and couldn't say who; the stores sergeant reading figures aloud in a voice like gravel poured slowly — telling herself exactly that. She was a mapmaker. She saw lines that weren't straight and called them crooked; it was her whole trade, her whole curse, to notice. Noticing was not knowing.

But her mind had already left the hall. It had gone back up the hill, through the frost, into the map room, and it had laid her memory of that letter beside this exhibit the way one lays a tracing over a base map, looking for the places they diverge.

* * *

She had typed the accusation on the commission machine, the Underwood bolted to the trestle table under the north window, serial-stamped and requisitioned and serviced by a corporal with oily thumbs twice a year. It had its habits. Every machine did; that was the first thing her master had taught her about documents, years ago, in another life: *a typewriter is like a walk, or a limp. Everyone has one, and hardly anyone can hide it.*

The Underwood's habit was a heavy strike and a wandering baseline — nothing you'd see without a loupe, but there, measurable, consistent. Its 'e' was fine. She would have sworn it on anything. She had typed hundreds of pages on that machine and its 'e' sat square in its box like a well-behaved child.

So why — why, when the clerk of the tribunal read the fourth line of the exhibit aloud, did the cadence feel wrong in her ears? Not the words. The words were hers, nearly hers, close enough that only she would feel the difference: a comma shifted, a *the* where she'd typed *that*. Small edits, made after her version. But underneath the words, the *sound* of them was wrong, the way a voice is wrong on a bad telephone line — same sentence, different throat.

And then the clerk turned the page again to smooth it on the table, and the light caught it once more, and this time Mirabel was ready for it, staring, greedy for the angle, and she saw it.

The 'e' leaned.

Not all the e's. That was the thing — that was the thing that made her breath go thin and her fingertips go cold against her folded hands. Most of them sat square. But here and there through the page, an 'e' rode high in its line, tilted a degree off true, its counter opening toward the upper right like a door left ajar. She counted without meaning to, marking their positions in her head the way she marked soundings along a river channel: third line, sixth word. Fifth line, second word. Ninth line, fourth word and last word both.

An intermittent fault. A worn segment, or a bent typebar, striking true most of the time and askew when the mechanism tired or the key was hit off-center. The commission machine did not do this. The commission machine had never done this. She could produce a ream of proof, dated and stamped, filed in her uncle's own cabinets, and every page of it would show a square 'e' in a straight line.

The letter she had typed did not match the exhibit. Someone had retyped it.

The hall was warm and close and smelled of wet wool and lamp oil, and Mirabel stood among her neighbors with her face arranged into mild civic attention while inside her skull a single thought rang like a dropped bell: *someone retyped my letter.*

Who? Who had the text? Only her uncle, Captain Reiner, and the file itself. Where? On what machine? There was no other typewriter in Verenmoor that she knew of except —

Except.

The old Corona. Master Eiland's Corona, the portable he had carried in its cracked leather case to every survey, every archive, every smuggled meeting in a back room above a bakery in the years when mapping troop movements for the resistance was a crime punished at the wall behind the powder magazine. The case had gone into the commission's inventory when they took him. Then the case had gone *out* of the commission's inventory, because she had checked, after, secretly, line by line down the seizure ledger in her uncle's cabinet, and the Corona was listed as *removed, disposition unknown*, and she had spent two years not letting herself wonder what those words meant.

If the exhibit bore the Corona's signature — the leaning 'e', the proud strike — then someone in this town had that machine. Someone had taken her words, meant to condemn one man, and set them down again in another machine's voice. And whatever else that someone intended, the fact itself was a rope thrown to a drowning man: the state's only evidence against Tomas Vey was a document that could be shown to have been manufactured twice, altered between workings, produced on a machine that had no lawful business existing.

Hope arrived the way a technicality always arrives — small, mean-looking, easily stepped on. It did not feel like rescue. It felt like a hairline crack in ice, running out from her boot.

"Tomas Vey." Reiner had risen. He stood with his fingertips resting on the grey cloth, head bowed, giving the room the full benefit of his reluctance. "Do you deny the signature?"

"I don't deny it's my name." Tomas's voice was hoarse from the cold of the cell, but steady. He did not raise it; he didn't need to. The hall had gone truly quiet now. "I deny everything else on the page."

"You concede the document issues from you."

"I concede I can't prove it doesn't."

A stir, quickly smothered. Mirabel felt her nails bite into her palms. He was doing the arithmetic of a sealed town — no witnesses on his side, no advocate, no road out — and choosing honesty as the only ground left to stand on.

Reiner let the silence work for him. Then, gently, terribly: "The tribunal notes the accused offers no defense." He looked down at the exhibit, and he smoothed it with one gloved hand, and Mirabel watched his palm pass over the leaning e's as if smoothing away a stain. "In view of the evidence, and the gravity of the charge—"

"On what machine," Mirabel said.

She had not decided to speak. Her voice simply left her, thin and clear across the hall, the way a sound leaves a struck glass, and afterward she would remember the sensation of watching herself do it from a short distance away, appalled and powerless.

Every face turned. Reiner's included. Up at the clerks' table, her uncle Aldous lifted his head for the first time all morning, and his face was the face of a man watching a dam develop its first crack.

"Young woman," the lieutenant began, "this tribunal does not take interruptions—"

"On what machine was the document typed?" She heard her own voice steady itself, gather its skirts, continue. "The commission keeps its machines under requisition. There are two in Verenmoor, both accounted for. If the tribunal will permit an examination of the type against the commission record—" she made her voice humble, technical, boring, the voice of a girl who copies papers for her living, nothing more — "it could verify the document's origin in a quarter hour. Surely the captain wants the evidence beyond question."

Silence. The kind that has weight and temperature.

Reiner studied her. It was the first time in two years of transcribing his requisitions and his memoranda and his lists of seized property that he had ever actually looked at her, and she felt the look travel over her like a lantern beam across a wall, searching for the seam in the wallpaper. She kept her face empty. She thought: *invisible, be invisible, be the girl who types.*

"And what would a copyist know of type examination?" Reiner asked, mild as milk.

"I align letters for a living, Captain." A ripple of something — not quite laughter, not quite approval — moved through the benches. The town was enjoying this, whoever won. "It's the same eye."

Reiner smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "The tribunal thanks the citizen for her diligence. The authenticity of the document is attested by the commission clerk." He did not look at Aldous when he said it; that was the cruelty of it, or perhaps the courtesy. "It bears the accused's signature. The tribunal is satisfied as to its provenance."

He turned to the lieutenant. Papers were gathered. The clerk of the tribunal began some formula of adjournment. And Mirabel understood, with the sick clarity of a woman watching a bridge close for the season, that the hearing was not paused for deliberation — it was being *wrapped*, tied like a parcel, finished. They meant to have the verdict this afternoon. Perhaps within the hour.

Tomas Vey was already being turned toward the door by his two soldiers, gentle as ushers. As they brought him level with her end of the hall — a moment, no more, the crowd packed and pressing — his eyes swept the faces the way a drowning man sweeps a shoreline, cataloging, dismissing, and stopped on hers.

He did not know her. Of course he did not know her; she was nobody, the clerk's quiet niece, the girl behind the typewriter. But she could not stop her face from doing the thing faces do, and whatever it did — some loosening, some grief, some guilty tell — he read it. His step hitched, half a beat. His brow came down, not in anger but in the concentration of a man memorizing something. And then the soldiers had him at the door, and the gray daylight took him, and he was gone down the steps toward the cell that in an afternoon might become only a holding room before the wall.

The hall emptied in a rising tide of talk. Mirabel stood still in it, letting it break around her, thinking fast and cold.

This afternoon. She had until this afternoon, and the only proof she had lived inside her own eye: a leaning 'e' that no one else in Verenmoor had seen, pointing like a compass needle toward a typewriter that officially did not exist, in the hands of someone unknown, somewhere in this sealed and grudging town.

Someone retyped her letter. Which meant someone had wanted Tomas condemned badly enough to forge the forging — or wanted something else entirely, something she could not yet see the shape of. Either way, that machine was real, it was here, it had recently been in use, and machines leave traces the way rivers leave banks.

She pulled her coat tight and slipped out the side door into the biting air, and she did not go home. She went up the hill toward the map room, walking fast, already composing in her head the test she would run: pull every page the commission machine had ever produced, compare the e's, build the proof page by page until the difference was undeniable even to a tribunal that did not want to be convinced.

Behind her, in the wool exchange, Captain Reiner was saying something to the clerk at the little black book, and her uncle Aldous was looking at the door she had gone through, with an expression she did not see and would not have recognized.
