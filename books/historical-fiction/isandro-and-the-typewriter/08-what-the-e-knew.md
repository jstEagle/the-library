## 8. What the 'e' Knew

The commission office kept its fire low — coal was requisitioned, counted, and signed for in triplicate, and Captain Reiner had made a small religion of triplicate — so Mirabel worked that morning with her coat on and her fingers stiff as the pencils in the jar beside her. The typewriter sat on its table under the window where the frost had grown feathers on the inside of the glass, and it looked, as it always looked, like nothing at all: an Unterhoff & Sons No. 4, black enamel gone grey at the corners, the ribbon spools worn to the shine of old coins.

Nothing at all. That was the trouble with machines. They committed no crimes and left no confessions. A typewriter was only a small iron town where letters lived in their rows like obedient citizens, and if one of those citizens stood crooked in his doorway — well, who would ever ask him what he had seen?

Mirabel had asked. All night she had asked, lying in her narrow bed above the map room, listening to the river grind ice against the pilings, going over the two documents in her mind until they wore grooves in her like a cart track. The exhibit letter, the one that had put Tomas Vey in the cold cell by the granary, and her own transcription of the accusation, the one her uncle had carried down to her three nights ago in his careful clerk's hand and asked her to type fair.

She had compared them by candlelight until the candles drowned in their own wax. The wording matched. The spelling matched. Even the peculiar comma her uncle used — too high, always, as though punctuation embarrassed him — appeared in both documents at the same places. And yet the letters themselves were strangers to each other. Not different hands exactly. Different *weights*. As if the same words had been spoken by two voices, one of them tired.

Now, alone in the office while Reiner took his morning inspection of the guardhouse and her uncle attended the tribunal's clerical preparations, she set a fresh sheet into the commission machine and began, very quietly, to interrogate it.

*The quick brown fox.*

She typed it once. She typed it again. She typed it a third time with her eyes closed, letting her fingers find the keys without her, because a forger works with his eyes and a machine betrays itself without them.

Then she drew the sheet out and held it to the frosted window, using the pale daylight as a lightbox, and laid over it — her heart going strangely slow and loud, the way it did when she inked a plate and could not yet know if the plate was true — the copy she had made of the exhibit letter from the tribunal file. She had copied it stroke for stroke, letter for letter, the way her master had taught her to copy coastlines: not approximately, not *in the spirit of*, but faithfully, faithfully, faithfully.

There. Lower right quadrant of the page, third line, second word.

The *e*.

On the commission machine, every *e* came off the platen sitting level, a good bourgeois letter, both arms of it equal, the crossbar honest and horizontal. But on the exhibit letter, every *e* leaned. Not much. A degree, perhaps two degrees, to the right, and the lower arm struck the paper fractionally harder than the upper, so that the letter seemed to be listening — cocking its head toward something said off the page. She had noticed it last night and told herself it was nothing, a worn type bar, a matter of no consequence to a man's neck.

It was not a worn type bar. She saw that now. A worn bar strikes unevenly; it does not lean consistently, every time, at the same angle, across eleven pages of testimony. This was a bent frame. Some blow, some fall, some long-ago accident of the road had tilted one segment of the machine's skeleton, and every *e* that had ever passed through that segment came out leaning east, listening.

She went through her own transcription. Her *e*s sat straight as fence posts, all forty-one of them. She counted. Counting was what mapmakers did; counting was how you knew a coastline had not lied to you.

Forty-one straight *e*s in the document she had typed. One hundred and six leaning *e*s in the exhibit.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake, and instead they moved with the particular calm she felt only in the map room, the calm of someone following evidence rather than leading it.

The conclusion arranged itself in her mind with the clean, terrible orderliness of a contour line closing on itself around a summit.

The exhibit letter had not been typed on this machine.

Whatever else was true about the letter that had condemned Tomas Vey — whoever had dictated its phrases into her uncle's ear, whatever grudge or greed had set the thing in motion — the physical fact was simple and could not be unsaid: the commission's own typewriter had never touched that paper. Which meant the letter had been written somewhere else, on some other machine, and then walked into this office and presented as commission work.

And there was only one other typewriter in Verenmoor. Only one other machine in all the world, so far as Mirabel knew, whose *e* listened.

* * *

Master Helderan's machine had been an older beast entirely — an Unterhoff No. 2, its enamel chipped at the carriage return where he had knocked it against the doorframe of the shop on the day they brought it home, twenty years before Mirabel was born. He had never repaired the chip. "A machine should carry its history," he used to say, running his thumb along the raw iron beneath the paint. "So should a map. Show me a map with no corrections on it and I will show you a liar."

She had been nine years old the first time he showed her the leaning *e*. She had come to the shop after school — she was forever coming to the shop, long before she was formally apprenticed, drawn up the outside stair by the smell of ink and the sound of the little bell over the door — and he had sat her on a stool beside the workbench and typed her name for her, MIRABEL, each letter separately, pronouncing it as he did, and then he had pointed at the fourth character with the blunt end of a burnisher.

"There," he said. "See how she leans? She's been leaning since before I owned her. Every *e* this machine has ever made leans the same way, like soldiers all looking at the same window. If you ever see a leaning *e* in a printed thing, girl, you'll know where it was born."

He had thought it a joke then, a game for a child, the sort of secret a man gives away because it is worthless. *Every e this machine has ever made.* He had died without knowing that he was teaching her to read a confession.

They had taken the machine when they arrested him. That was part of it — she remembered now, remembered with the sick clarity of things half-understood by a fourteen-year-old and fully understood only years later. Two men from the occupation authority had come up the outside stair with a canvas sack, and they had taken the typewriter, and his correspondence, and his drafts, and the plates from the drawer under the workbench — not all the plates, thank God, thank God, only the ones they found, and Master Helderan had hidden the rest so well that even she had not known where until the night before they shot him, when Odile's father had come to the house with a message scratched on the back of a printer's proof.

*The floor remembers what the walls forget.*

She had lifted the boards in the map room that same week, in the dark, and found the atlas — her master's life's work, the great survey of the river country, its final plates still uninked — and she had been hiding it, and hiding herself inside her usefulness to the commission, ever since.

But the machine they had taken. It had gone into the sack and down the stairs and into the back of a requisition wagon, and she had watched it go, nine days before the execution, and she had assumed — everyone had assumed — that it was inventoried, catalogued, crated, shipped to some archive in the capital where confiscated things sleep in rows.

It had not been shipped anywhere. It had not left Verenmoor at all.

Because the *e* was here. The leaning *e*, one hundred and six times over, sat in the tribunal's exhibit folder wearing the commission's seal, condemning Tomas Vey in a hand that belonged to a dead man's desk.

Mirabel took out a second sheet and typed the alphabet, slowly, all twenty-six letters, lowercase and capital, then numerals, then the punctuation marks. She did the same on a comparison sheet she constructed from memory of the exhibit — she dared not take the exhibit itself from the file; the tribunal clerk counted pages the way Reiner counted coal. When she laid the two sheets together at the window, the differences bloomed like a photograph developing.

The *e*, leaning. The *y*, its descender hooking left where the commission's hooked right. The numeral 3, flat-topped on the exhibit, round-topped here. And one more thing, small and damning as a splinter: on the exhibit letter, the space between words was irregular — a hair wider after certain letters, narrower after others — the rhythm of a machine whose escapement had a catch in it, a hitch at every seventh advance. She had heard that hitch. God help her, she had *heard* it, a thousand evenings of it, the tick-tick-tick-tick-tick-tick-*tick* of Master Helderan's No. 2 working late, the heartbeat of her childhood, and she had read the exhibit letter aloud in her head last night and the rhythm had been wrong and she had not known why, the way you can hear a wrong note in a song you've known all your life and not be able to name it until morning.

Someone in Verenmoor had that machine. Someone had kept it through two years of searches and requisitions and door-to-door inventories — hidden it the way she hid her atlas, which meant someone with something terrible to hide, or something precious. Someone had typed the accusation against Tomas Vey on it, deliberately, letter by deliberate letter, and then either they or another hand had carried the pages into this office and let them be entered as evidence.

And there the thought stopped her, stopped her cold, her breath fogging the frosted glass.

Because there were two ways to read it, and she could not yet tell which way the truth lay.

One: someone wanted the letter to look like commission work but not *be* traceable to any commission machine — a forger clever enough to use the dead man's typewriter precisely because its trail led nowhere, into a confiscated crate, into the past, into the grave of Anton Helderan.

Two: someone wanted the letter to be traceable — someday, by someone — to that machine. Someone typing a message into the evidence itself the way her master hid corrections in his maps, waiting for a reader with eyes.

Either way, the implications closed over her like river ice.

If the first — then whoever forged the letter knew about the machine's history, knew it had belonged to a man executed for resistance mapping, knew perhaps that his apprentice still lived in Verenmoor and worked at the commission's side. Knew enough to make the forgery a weapon aimed not only at Tomas Vey but, if it were ever discovered, at *her*. Because who else would they blame? Who else in this sealed town had loved that machine, had learned her letters on it, had ten thousand hours of it in her fingers? The forgery was a loaded gun pointing backward through time, and its muzzle rested against her name.

And if the second — if the letter was a message, a flare fired from inside the town — then somewhere behind one of these shuttered windows was a person who believed Tomas Vey innocent, who had risked the tribunal, the gallows, Reiner's attention, to say so sideways, in leaning letters, hoping for a reader. A person who might be an ally. Or a person who might have done it carelessly, not knowing the machine's signature was legible, and who would be destroyed — by Reiner, by the tribunal, by the mob of grudges this sealed town had become — the moment anyone else learned what Mirabel now knew.

Either way, she could not bring this proof forward. Not yet. Not like this. To stand up at the tribunal and say *the exhibit was typed on Anton Helderan's confiscated typewriter* was to answer the only question that mattered more than Tomas Vey's guilt: *where is that typewriter, and who has kept it?* And the answer would drag her master's name back into the mud of a hearing already decided in its heart, and worse — it would set Reiner hunting. Reiner, who had signed the inventory of Helderan's effects. Reiner, who would want to know, immediately and forcefully, whether the machine had vanished from the requisition wagon on its way to the capital, and whose ledger that disappearance stained. Reiner, who had everything to gain from the trail staying buried — which was, Mirabel realized with a slow cold turn of her stomach, exactly the kind of motive that turned a technicality into a second body.

She gathered her test sheets, folded them small, and considered the stove. Burn them. Burn the evidence and keep only the knowledge, the way her master had kept knowledge in his head when the plates grew too dangerous to keep on paper.

She did not burn them. She tucked them instead into the hollow spine of the 1811 hydrographic survey, where the binding had shrunk away from the boards, and pressed the covers shut, and stood a moment with her hand flat on the shelf as if taking an oath.

Outside, the frost on the window had begun, very slightly, to weep — the sun was up somewhere behind the clouds, and the long winter of the sealed town ground on toward the armistice everyone whispered about and no one announced. Somewhere down the street a shutter banged. Somewhere beyond the granary, Tomas Vey sat in the cold cell with one hundred and six leaning letters standing testimony against him, and none of them able to speak unless she gave them a voice.

Someone in Verenmoor had her master's typewriter.

Mirabel pulled on her gloves, wound her scarf to the chin, and went out into the white morning to find out who — beginning, because there was nowhere else it could begin, at the print shop of Odile Brandt, whose father had also been arrested, and whose father had also, she suddenly remembered, been made to open his premises for inventory two days after the wagon came down the hill.

The snow squeaked under her boots. The hunt had begun, and every door in town stood shut against the cold, and behind one of them something was ticking — tick, tick, tick, tick, tick, tick, *tick* — waiting for her to knock.
