## 9. The Printer's Daughter

Odile Fenner had grown up believing that ink was the most honest substance in the world. Her father had taught her so before she could read, holding her small hand over a composing stone, guiding her fingers across the cold brass sorts. *Letters don't lie, little bird,* he'd said, sliding the first 'O' of her name into its chase. *People lie. Letters just say what people make them say.* She had been six years old and had thought it a comfort. It had taken a war, an occupation, and a locked drawer beneath her father's bed to teach her that it was a warning.

The print shop above the tannery lane smelled of lamp oil, damp wool, and the ghost of solvent — the smell of her whole childhood, now tinged with the smell of absence, because her father had been gone eleven weeks and the shop had begun to forget him. Frost ferned the inside of the north window each morning; she scraped it with the flat of a palette knife so she could see down into the lane, because seeing was the first discipline of survival in a sealed town. Verenmoor under occupation was a bowl of held breath. Everyone watched everyone, and everyone pretended not to.

She lifted the lamp and went to the drawer.

It had come to her in March, three days before the gendarmes took her father — a parcel wrapped in oilcloth and tied with printer's twine, delivered by a boy who would not say who sent it and who was himself gone from Verenmoor by April. Inside: one typewriter, black iron, heavier than a sleeping cat, its keys worn to silver at the centers, its frame stamped with a maker's mark half filed away. And a note in her father's hand, four lines only.

*This is Brandt's machine. It must never be traced to us, and it must never stop being useful. The 'e' rides high. You will know your work by it. Burn this.*

She had burned it. She had kept everything else.

Her father had been arrested for printing — that much the town knew, and no more than the town knew, which was how he had wanted it. The official charge mentioned pamphlets. The truth, folded into the charge like arsenic into bread, was that he had printed *passports* — or things shaped like passports, movement permits bearing the commission's seal, its letterheads, its bureaucratic music. He had made eleven of them before they came for him. Eleven names, eleven strangers, eleven doors opening out of Verenmoor toward the coast while the bridge still stood open. Odile had set the type on four of them herself. She had been proud. She could no longer remember what pride had felt like, only that it had lived in the same part of her chest where the guilt now sat, a stone where a bird had been.

Because the twelfth permit had been hers, and she had made it too slowly.

*Not too slowly,* she corrected herself, as she corrected herself every night, kneeling at the drawer. *Differently.* The old ferryman, Janek, had waited four days for his papers while she practiced the commission clerk's flourishes, and on the fifth day the bridge closed, and Janek was still in Verenmoor, and two weeks after that the gendarmes had taken him from the fish market on suspicion of harboring — suspicion that was accurate, though they could not prove it, and Janek had not given them the satisfaction of confirming it. He was in the cold cell now. Or he had been. Cells in Verenmoor had a way of emptying without explanation, and no one asked where the water went.

She opened the drawer and took out the typewriter the way other women took out their dead children's photographs: with both hands, and not looking directly at it at first.

Brandt's machine. Master Brandt, the mapmaker — executed in the spring, before the frost, before the bridge chains, back when Verenmoor still believed in seasons. She remembered him only distantly: a tall stooped man who used to bring her father proofs to check and stand in the shop smelling of turpentine and river air, speaking softly, laughing rarely, always touching the paper on the racks as though greeting it. A man in love with accuracy. They had shot him for accuracy — for knowing precisely where troops moved and putting that precision into lines that found their way to the resistance. The commission had confiscated his instruments, his plates, his correspondence. Everything, the proclamation said. Everything but one typewriter, which her father had hidden in a bale of rag paper two days before Brandt's arrest, and which had crossed the town in a cart of laundry to arrive at her door like an orphan.

*You will know your work by it.*

She set the machine on the composing table, rolled in a sheet of commission letterhead — one of forty-three blanks her father had acquired through the clerk's office before Aldous Galloway's time, when the previous clerk had been a drunk and sellable — and pressed the keys with two fingers, testing. *Requisition. Requisition. Requisition.*

There. The 'e' rode high, its impression slightly tilted, a small crooked tooth in every word that contained it. A flaw invisible to anyone who did not know to look, and unmistakable to anyone who did. Her father had called such flaws *fingerprints of the machine*. Every press, every typewriter, every hand-set page carried them, he said; the craft of forgery was not making a document perfect but making it perfectly imperfect in all the right places. The commission's own clerks typed on machines whose flaws Odile had memorized the way other girls memorized songs. The requisitions she produced bore the correct smudged seal, the correct watermark, the correct tired phrasing of military bureaucracy — *authorized drawing of stores, per schedule annexed* — and the correct little errors. What they did not bear, anywhere, was the high-riding 'e' of Brandt's machine. That machine she used only for certain work, and even then she typed its output through two thicknesses of cloth to soften the impression, and she never used it twice in the same week, and she slept badly on the nights she did.

Tonight was one of those nights.

Down in the cellar, behind the paper store, under the false bottom her father had built for hiding banned type during the last censorship, seven people were sleeping. Seven was too many; she had said so until her voice gave out and Mireille Sauvan had taken her by both wrists and said, *And the snow is too deep, and the war is too long, and yet here we all are.* They were the overflow from the Sauvan loft, which already held nine, moved out after the fish-market arrests for fear of contagion — the occupational word for anyone caught breathing near a suspect. Two of them were children. One was a wounded deserter whose fever came and went like a bad conscience. All of them ate, somehow, from stores that existed on paper as spoiled, condemned, never delivered. Odile forged the spoilage. Odile forged the condemnation. Somewhere in a commission ledger a quantity of flour was officially rotting in a warehouse that did not exist, and unofficially it was becoming the thin gray bread that kept seven hidden people alive in a printer's cellar, and every loaf of it passed through the high-riding 'e' of a dead man's typewriter.

She had stopped praying about it in October. Prayer required either asking forgiveness or asking impunity, and she had decided she could honestly ask for neither.

* * *

The trouble with guilt was that it was articulate. Defiance was mute — it simply acted, night after night, hands moving in the dark with a craftsman's steadiness — but guilt talked. It talked in her father's voice, mostly, because guilt knew exactly which voice would reach her.

*Tonight you forge a requisition for forty pounds of flour,* it said, as she set the blank in the platen and adjusted the ribbon she kept inked to match the commission's violet-gray. *Tomorrow some clerk notices the arithmetic. Tomorrow some inspector counts heads in a cellar. And then your father's arrest becomes merely the first act, little bird, and the second act has your name in it, and the third act has those seven sleepers in it, and none of you will get to write the ending.*

*They'll starve otherwise,* she answered it, as she always did, feeding the first line in.

*Some do starve,* said the voice. *It is terrible. But you are manufacturing evidence, Odile. Evidence is a weapon left lying in the grass. Whoever finds it points it wherever they like.*

She typed. The keys struck with their familiar arthritic clatter, softened by the cloth she laid over the carriage — an old trick, it blurred the strike just enough to suggest a tired machine and a hurried clerk. The requisition grew line by line: flour, salt pork, tallow candles, medical gin. She signed it with the name of a quartermaster sergeant who had been transferred west in September and whose signature she had copied eleven times until her own hand dreamed in it.

Halfway down the page she paused, because the voice had changed.

*You know what else lies in the grass,* it said, gently now, which was worse. *You know what else carries that crooked 'e'.*

Yes. She knew. Everyone in Verenmoor who cared to know had heard by now — the news ran through a sealed town faster than through an open one, sealed vessels being what they were — that the mapmaker's apprentice, Galloway's niece, the quiet girl with the commission typewriter, had typed a letter accusing one Tomas Vey of theft, and that Vey was in the cell, and that the tribunal would sit before the ice broke. Odile had heard it from Mireille, delivered in the low flat voice Mireille reserved for the worst items, and Odile had felt something turn over in her chest like a boat shipping water.

Because she had seen the accusation letter. Not the original — a copy, circulating in the way documents circulated, handed from the commission office to the gendarmerie to the harbor master and back, each hand leaving its grease. She had seen it because Mireille had obtained it, because Mireille obtained everything, and Mireille had brought it to her and said only, *Look at the fourth paragraph. Tell me what you see.*

What Odile had seen, in the lamplight of the Sauvan kitchen, with her heart going like a trip-hammer, was a letter typed on a machine whose 'e' rode high.

She had told no one what that meant. Not Mireille, not the sleepers in the cellar, not the dark. Because there was only one machine in Verenmoor with that fingerprint, and it was under her floor, and if anyone else had produced that letter with it, then someone either had a second machine — impossible; her father had tracked Brandt's instruments like a priest tracking relics — or someone had used hers.

But the drawer was locked. The key hung at her throat. No one had entered the shop — she checked the flour dust on the stairs every morning, an inheritance of paranoia she no longer questioned —

Except.

Except that she had been away for two days in November, hauling the feverish deserter's medicine from the midwife across town, and the shop had stood empty except for the cellar sleepers, who swore no one had come, who would swear anything, who might have opened the drawer themselves to look at the strange machine, out of boredom, out of terror, out of the simple animal need to touch the thing that might save them —

Or. Another thought, colder. Or the letter had been typed *before*. Before her father's arrest. Before the machine came to her. Some old document, some draft, some discarded practice piece that her father had made and that had been seized with his effects, sitting in a commission evidence box for months, waiting for a clever officer to notice what it was and what it could be made to prove.

That was worse. That was so much worse, because it meant the letter condemning Tomas Vey might be her father's work — her father's crooked 'e', her father's crime, exhumed and weaponized — and the tribunal would not care that he had died eleven weeks ago. They would simply add his name to the tally and take Vey in his place, and the machinery her father built to open doors would close one on top of an innocent man.

She finished the requisition. Her hands had not shaken once; her hands never shook; it was the rest of her that shook, somewhere behind the sternum, in the place where pride had turned to stone.

* * *

She hid the machine, banked the lamp, and went down the cellar stairs with a candle and a plate of the gray bread, because the sleepers expected her at midnight and midnight discipline was the only sacrament left to her.

The cellar breathed. Seven sleepers arranged among the paper bales like commas in a long sentence — the deserter feverish again, flushed and muttering; the two children curled together under Mireille's good shawl; old Sauvan awake as always, nodding to her from the shadows with the courtesy of a man visiting his own grave. She distributed the bread. She checked the deserter's bandage. She listened at the coal hatch to the sounds of the town — sleet on the tannery roofs, a patrol's boots going up the lane at their unhurried interval, the river grinding its ice.

Mireille was awake, of course. Mireille was always awake; sleep, she claimed, was for people with clean consciences and heated rooms, and she had enjoyed neither since 1914. She drew Odile down onto the crate beside her and spoke with her lips barely moving, the occupied-town murmur that put the words directly into the listener's ear without troubling the air between.

"You've thought about the letter."

"I've thought about nothing else."

"Then you know what it means."

Odile looked at the candle flame rather than at the old woman's face. "It means someone in this town can produce commission documents that I cannot account for. It means that everything I have built — the requisitions, the spoilage ledgers, the whole paper architecture keeping these people fed — stands on ground that someone else knows how to shake. If the commission discovers a forger, they will not ask which forger. They will find one. Perhaps the wrong one." She heard her own voice go flat and careful. "Perhaps the niece of the commission clerk."

"Galloway's girl," Mireille said. "You think she's in danger?"

"I think she typed a letter against Vey on a machine that wasn't hers," Odile whispered. "Which means either she doesn't know what she typed, or she does. And if she doesn't know — if someone dictated it, if someone used her the way the commission uses everyone — then she is standing where my father stood, holding the instrument, trusting the hand that reaches for it." She swallowed. "And I know exactly how that ends."

Mireille was silent a moment. In the corner, the deserter cried out softly at something in his fever, and the older child stirred and murmured and was stilled.

"There's a hearing coming," Mireille said at last. "Tribunal, before the ice breaks. If Vey hangs for theft, the theft was real in the eyes of the law, and the law will start looking for stolen stores, and it will find our flour, Odile. Our nonexistent warehouse. Our rotting paper trail. Your father always said: every forgery is a door that swings both ways."

"I know what my father said."

"Then hear what I say." Mireille's grip on her wrist was sudden and strong, a bird-catcher's grip, and her eyes in the candlelight were the pale unblinking eyes of a woman who had buried a husband and two sons and outlived every authority that had tried to break her. "That girl with the typewriter is either our destruction or our shield, and we will not know which unless someone speaks to her. Not you. Your face is a confession; you couldn't hide it if you sewed it shut. But she comes to the harbor master's office for transcription work, yes? And the harbor master owes me for the winter herring. There are ways a message travels."

"And what would the message say?"

Mireille smiled, and it was the smile Odile hated, the smile that meant the old woman had already decided and was merely allowing the world to catch up. "It would say: *look at the fourth paragraph. Look at the e.* Nothing more. Let her be clever; they say she's clever. Let her follow the crooked letter wherever it leads. If it leads her to the truth about Vey, perhaps she saves him. If it leads her to us—" Mireille shrugged, a terrible loose shrug. "Then better we know now, while there's still one berth on the last barge and seven people who need to be on it."

"The berth," Odile said quietly. "You've heard."

Everyone had heard. The coal barge, the season's last, scheduled to run the frozen channel on the armistice tide — if there was an armistice, if the ice permitted, if the commission released it. One passenger berth, reserved by prewar treaty clause for documented civilian transit. One seat out of Verenmoor until spring, maybe until the war ended, maybe forever. Her brother Emil needed it more than any living person needed anything — Emil, hunted since the fish-market raids for the forgeries he'd sold to pay their father's legal costs, Emil with his burned fingers and his terrible laugh, hidden now in the tanner's loft with a price on his head that would double at the first hard frost of justice. One berth. Emil, or the fevered deserter, or one of the children, or — if the tribunal went wrong — perhaps no one at all, because a berth was useless to a fugitive without papers, and papers were her craft, and her craft was a weapon lying in the grass.

"I've heard," she said.

"Then make me a promise, printer's daughter." Mireille leaned close, and the candle guttered, and for a moment the whole cellar seemed to lean with her. "Whatever that mapmaker's girl turns out to be — friend, fool, or trap — when the moment comes and the crooked 'e' points its finger, do not hesitate. Hesitation is how they took your father. He hesitated to burn the plates, one night, one night only, and the night was enough."

Odile rose. She gathered the plate, trimmed the wick, performed the small liturgies of departure. At the foot of the stairs she stopped, one hand on the cold rail, and looked back at the breathing dark where seven lives persisted on the strength of her forgeries.

"I don't hesitate," she said. "I'm my father's daughter. I just carry the weight of what my hands have made."

Upstairs again, alone, she unlocked the drawer one last time and took out the machine and set it before her in the lamplight, this iron orphan of a murdered man, this instrument of her guilt and her defiance, its silver-worn keys waiting like patient teeth. She would type the message tonight — the harbor master's innocuous covering note, the four words that might save Tomas Vey or hang them all. She dipped the ribbon, fed the paper, and reached for the keys.

And froze — because in the lamplight, angled as she had never thought to angle it, she saw on the carriage frame a smear in the dust. A smear in the shape of fingers. Fresh. Days old at most.

Someone had touched the machine.

Someone besides her knew it was here.
