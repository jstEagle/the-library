## 10. Two Fugitives, One Berth

The press smelled of ink and cold iron, and Odile Verlinden had learned to love that smell the way other girls loved bread baking — as proof that something in the world could still be made rather than destroyed. She stood at the hand-press in the back room of her father's shop with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, though the room was barely warmer than the street, and fed another sheet under the platen. The impression came out crisp. A requisition for medical stores, commission seal and all, destined for a cellar on the Molendijk where six people who did not officially exist would eat tonight because the paper said they could.

Her father had taught her the trick of it when she was twelve: *the seal is nothing but patience, Odile. Wax, pressure, and the willingness to hold your breath.* He had taught her everything else too — kerning, burnishing, how to tell a good rag paper from a bad one by pressing it to your cheek — and then he had gone and gotten himself arrested, and left her alone in the shop with all his knowledge and none of his protection.

That had been in October. Six weeks now. No trial, no sentence posted, just an absence shaped like a broad man with ink-stained cuffs who used to whistle while he set type.

She wiped her hands on her apron and checked the sheet against the original pinned above the press. Good. The 'e's were perfect — her father's machine, her father's letters, even and honest. It was only when she thought about the *other* machine, the one hidden under the floorboards beneath the drying rack, that her stomach turned the way it always did.

Under those boards lay the master's typewriter, the one that had belonged to Hendrik Lammerts, the mapmaker, dead these fourteen months by firing squad. Her father had smuggled it into the shop two days before his own arrest, wrapped in oilcloth, carried through the dark like a sleeping child. *Keep it safe,* he'd said. *One day someone will come asking for it, and you'll know why by their face.* And then, harder, gripping her wrists: *And if no one comes — use it, Odile. Use it better than they ever used theirs.*

She had. God help her, she had.

* * *

The knock came at nine, three short and one long, and she blew out the lamp before she opened the door a hand's width. Snow came in with the visitor — fine, dry snow that hissed on the warm iron of the press — and behind the visitor, the river's darkness, the bridge's black spine, the distant lanterns of the commission house on the far bank.

"You're late," she whispered. "Miet said ten."

"Miet is optimistic." The woman in the doorway was old enough to be somebody's grandmother and careful enough to be nobody's; she had been a midwife in Verenmoor for forty years and knew every door, every cellar, every lie worth telling. She slipped inside and Odile shut the bolt behind her. "There's news. That's why I've come. The coal barge goes Saturday."

Odile felt the words land somewhere below her ribs. "Saturday. Before the armistice?"

"If there's an armistice. If there isn't, it goes anyway — Reiner's sold the coal south and he wants his payment before any peace makes the ledgers public." Miet unwound her scarf, and her breath hung between them in the cold. "It takes one passenger. One berth behind the coal, hidden past the customs skiff. After Saturday, the river freezes solid and nobody leaves Verenmoor until spring. Nobody. Whatever happens with the tribunal, whatever happens with the war — sealed."

"One berth." Odile sat down slowly on the stool by the press. "You told me last week there might be two."

"Last week I was wrong." Miet's face was kind the way a surgeon is kind. "I'm sorry, girl. I thought you should hear it sitting down."

For a long moment Odile looked at the requisition drying on the rack, its honest false 'e's, its careful forgery of a government she had never chosen, and thought: *arithmetic.* That was what this was. Not tragedy, not yet — tragedy could wait its turn — first arithmetic. Two lives, one berth, and the whole frozen winter on the other side of the equation.

"Tomas Vey," she said. "If he's freed."

"If. The tribunal sits Thursday."

"And Pieter."

"Your brother is hunted tonight, Odile. Reiner has men walking the Molendijk with lanterns. They found another forged chit in a raid on the tannery — a good one, everyone says too good, and they know it didn't come from the commission's machine because the commission's machine has that broken 'e.'" Miet paused. "They know there's a second typewriter in this town. They don't know whose."

*They will,* Odile thought. *Eventually they always do.*

* * *

Pieter had come to the shop at midnight twice that autumn, smelling of coal smoke and river water, laughing too loudly the way he did when he was frightened. He was twenty-three and had their father's hands, broad and clever, and he had taken to the forging like he'd been born to it — better than her, honestly, though she'd never say so. He could reproduce a signature after seeing it once. He could draw a commission seal freehand that fooled a man holding it under a lamp.

He had done it for the fugitives first — the ones the resistance moved along the river toward the coast, deserters and smuggled families and a wounded wireless operator whose name Odile never learned. Chits, travel passes, requisitions. Paper that turned hunted people into clerical errors.

Then, in November, he had done one thing more. One.

"He shouldn't have," she said now to Miet, and heard how thin her voice sounded. "I told him. One forgery for yourself, one time, and the whole thing unravels. That's what Father always—"

"That's not why they hunt him," Miet said gently. "They hunt him because the chit he made got a family of five out of the customs house on the north road, and Reiner can't abide being made a fool of. Your brother spent his crime saving five lives. Reiner has spent his covering the theft of a winter's worth of army stores. If it comes to weighing, Odile—"

"It won't come to weighing." She stood, pressed her palms flat on the cold press bed. "That's exactly what won't happen. Tribunals don't weigh. They count documents."

* * *

After Miet left, Odile did not sleep. She knelt on the cold boards of the back room and lifted the trapdoor, and took out the oilcloth bundle, and set the master's typewriter on the press bed where the lamp could reach it.

It was a beautiful machine, even now, even knowing what it was. Black enamel worn grey at the space bar where a working life of thumbs had rested. The keys were round as coins. She had typed on it perhaps thirty times since October — never anything kept, always drafts, practice sheets burned afterward in the stove — and every time, she felt like a thief in a church. This was the machine that had typed the master's maps' accompanying notes, his field reports, and — if the rumors that crossed the river were true — the coded coordinates that had gone to the resistance in the margins of shipping invoices. For that they had stood him against the wall of the powder magazine in the first week of December last year, and the town had watched, and the snow had come down the way it was coming down tonight.

And it was also, now, the machine whose fingerprints were all over the letter that had put Tomas Vey in a cell.

She had heard about that — the whole town had, by evening it was in every queue and doorway. The clerk's niece had typed an accusation of theft against the stranger who arrived with the closing of the bridge. Theft of commission stores. And Odile had read a copy of the accusation — Miet obtained things like that the way other women obtained eggs — and she had known within four lines that it had *not* come from this machine, whatever anyone believed. The 'e' was straight. The 'e' on the exhibit was straight as a soldier.

Which meant either the commission had found a second typewriter, or someone had retyped the letter elsewhere to make it look like it came from here. Or — and this was the thought that kept her kneeling on the cold boards past midnight — someone had written the original on this machine and then had it copied clean, so that when the trail led to the missing typewriter, the typewriter would be found wherever they wanted the trail to end.

Wherever they wanted *her* to be standing.

She ran her thumb along the edge of the carriage. Somewhere in this town, in the last day or two, someone else had touched this machine besides her. Someone had typed Tomas Vey into ruin, or prepared the ground for it. The thought made her want to wrap the thing in oilcloth and sink it in the river, and she almost did — she got as far as the door with it under her arm — and then she stopped, because sinking it would prove nothing except guilt, and because the machine was also the one thing that could unmake the frame. A typewriter testifies. Every broken letter, every uneven strike, every quirk of alignment is a fingerprint and a confession waiting to be read aloud. Her father had taught her that too.

*The document remembers what the liar forgets.*

She put the machine back under the floorboards and sat with her back against the wall and let the arithmetic have her.

* * *

Tomas Vey. She had seen him once, before the arrest — a tall, weathered man asking directions to the mapmaker's house, carrying nothing but a canvas roll slung across his back, and she had thought at the time: *another stranger wanting maps, wanting routes out, wanting what everyone wants.* Now she knew what the canvas roll had held, because Miet had pried it out of the interrogation gossip: the master's final map, carried across the river by the last courier the resistance trusted, brought back into the town where it was drawn. Tomas had run the master's last work through occupied streets to safety fourteen months ago, and then — this was the part Odile could not stop thinking about — he had come *back*. Back across a closing border, onto the wrong side of a bridge about to be chained, into a town full of men who would shoot him for the papers in his head. To find the atlas. To finish what a dead man started.

Who did that? Who spent his own life on a dead man's errand?

And Pieter — Pieter, who had held their mother's hand in the market square until the soldiers made her let go, who had learned forgery in six weeks to feed strangers in cellars, who was at this moment somewhere in the rafters or reed beds outside town with lanterns swinging along the Molendijk looking for him — Pieter, who was hers. Blood of her blood. Ink of her ink.

If Tomas went to the tribunal Thursday and the letter held, he would be shot. Then the berth was Pieter's; the arithmetic resolved itself cleanly, and she could spend the rest of the winter hating herself for the relief.

If Tomas was freed — and there were whispers, strange whispers, that the mapmaker's apprentice was stirring, that the quiet girl with the commission typewriter was asking questions about the letter — then the arithmetic broke open again, and she would stand Saturday morning at the coal quay with two names and one place behind the coal, and she would have to speak one name aloud and let the other hear it.

Could she put her brother on that barge and watch Tomas walk back into Reiner's reach? Could she put Tomas on it and let Pieter run the frozen marshes till spring, with a price on his head and the marshes full of soldiers? Was there even a difference between choosing and letting choose — if she simply said nothing, simply failed to act, would the world pick for her and absolve her of the picking?

"No," she said aloud to the empty shop, and her voice sounded like her father's. "That's cowardice wearing arithmetic for a coat."

There had to be a third way. There always was, her father said; you just hadn't set the type for it yet.

* * *

She worked until nearly dawn, because hands that are working cannot wring themselves. She printed fifty copies of a grain requisition that would move flour from a commission warehouse to a parish kitchen on the Molendijk. She set type for a funeral notice that was actually a signal to the network — the date of the barge encoded in the dates of the mourners. And all the while her mind kept returning, traitorously, to the mapmaker's apprentice.

Mirabel Galloway. Aldous Galloway's niece, the one who typed the commission's paperwork with her head down and her collar up, invisible as furniture. Except apparently she wasn't invisible anymore; apparently she had noticed something in the letter, something wrong, and instead of folding it away like any sensible person in a sealed town she had begun to pull the thread. Odile had watched her once from the shop window, crossing the frost-covered square with a portfolio clutched to her chest the way Pieter used to carry their mother's loaves — like something both precious and breakable. The atlas. Everyone knew the story: the apprentice finishing the atlas her master died for. What fewer people asked was what else lived in the map room, and whether the master's final map — the one Tomas Vey had carried across the river at the cost of everything — had found its way home.

If it had. If Mirabel had it. If the map showed what the resistance always claimed it showed — the troop positions, the supply routes, the thefts dressed up as requisitions — then it wasn't just an atlas. It was a verdict. It was the third way, the way out of the arithmetic, the document that could break Reiner and free Tomas and maybe, maybe, take the heat off a forger's brother, because tribunals might not weigh lives but they certainly weighed evidence.

But using it meant exposing the machine. Exposing the machine meant exposing her. Using it meant going to the commission's own apprentice — the clerk's own niece — and saying: *here is what I've done, here is what my father hid under my floor, here is my brother's life; help me.*

Trusting a stranger. Trusting a Galloway. Betting Pieter's neck on the conscience of a girl who typed letters for the men who shot Hendrik Lammerts.

Odile banked the stove, put out the lamp, and stood in the dark listening to the snow tick against the window like fingers trying the latch. Outside, far off, a lantern moved along the quay — the barge crew, loading early, coal dust ghosting up white under the light. Saturday. One berth. Two names.

She thought of her father's last instruction — *one day someone will come asking for it, and you'll know them by their face* — and realized, standing there in the cold, that she might already have seen that face, crossing a frosted square with an atlas hugged to her chest.

Tomorrow, Odile decided, she would find the mapmaker's apprentice. And whichever of the two names the Saturday barge ended up carrying, she would make sure first that the truth rode with it.
