## 11. Uncle Aldous's Ledger

The commission offices occupied the old customs house on the quay, a building that had spent forty years weighing grain and now weighed men instead. Aldous Galloway had been given the corner room, its windows fogged with river damp, and a ledger bound in green canvas that he was expected to keep in a hand so regular it might have been printed. He was good at this. He had been good at being invisible and indispensable for thirty years, first in his brother's shadow, then in his niece's life, and now in the commission's service, where a clerk who asked no questions outlived every captain who asked them.

That morning he was reconciling the coal requisitions against the transport manifests, a task dull enough to be a kind of prayer. Coal came upriver on barges; coal went down to the garrison, to the hospital, nominally to the town's relief fund. The numbers should have sung in unison. They did not.

He found it at first as one finds anything wrong — not as a fact but as an itch. A column that wouldn't balance by eleven tons. Then another page, off by nine. He went back through October, through September, his pen moving down the figures with the patience of a man following a bloodhound on a long scent, and by the time the church bells rang noon the itch had become a wound: some sixty tons of commission coal had left Verenmoor on paper bound for destinations that did not exist, and the signatures authorizing those shipments belonged, all of them, in their confident downstrokes and their too-even spacing, to Captain Elias Reiner.

Aldous sat very still.

The stove ticked. Outside, a dray horse complained at the slipway, and somewhere a woman was calling children in to dinner in the flat river accent he had heard for fifteen years without ever quite calling home. He looked at the ledger and understood two things simultaneously, the way one understands a fall only once the ground is already rising: that Reiner was stealing from his own commission, and that the theft required a thief to be found.

* * *

He should not have gone home at midday. There was no reason for it except that his hands wanted doing something other than lying flat on a desk, and the walk — across the frozen canal path, past the shuttered dye works, up the hill where the mapmaker's house stood with its chimney breathing thin smoke — was the walk of a man rehearsing a conversation he had no right to begin.

Mirabel was in the map room.

He stood in the doorway without announcing himself, which was his habit and his flaw in a single gesture, and watched her work. She had her sleeves pushed back despite the cold, her dark hair pinned carelessly so that strands escaped and caught the grey window light, and she was bent over not the atlas plates — he checked, quickly, automatically, the way he always checked — but over loose papers spread across the drafting table like the pieces of a disassembled clock.

Among them he recognized, with a lurch that arrived in his stomach before his head, the requisition forms he himself had carried home last week for typing. And beside them, in Mirabel's careful pencil hand, a comparison chart: dates, tonnage, signatures. She had drawn little boxes around the discrepancies and connected them with lines, and the lines converged, all of them, on a name written small and fierce at the center of the page as if she were afraid to write it larger.

She hadn't seen him. Her lips were moving, counting, and there was graphite on her cheekbone and ink — he knew the shade — under her fingernails, and for one unguarded second Aldous felt what he had felt standing over her cradle twenty years ago when his brother and his brother's wife walked out of history and left him a child and a silence: *I would burn any building in this town to keep this girl warm.*

Then he saw what she had circled twice, at the bottom of the sheet, beneath the name.

*Who dictated the Vey letter? Not U.A.'s phrasing. Whose?*

The warmth in him turned to ice so fast he tasted it.

"Miri."

She started badly, her elbow sweeping toward the papers, and then — he watched her decide, in the space of half a heartbeat, that hiding was worse than brazening it out — she straightened and turned to face him with her chin up in a way that was her mother all over, her mother whom he had loved like a second self and buried without a body.

"Uncle." Nothing in her voice. She had learned stillness from him; that was the bitter joke of it. "You're home early."

"The office is cold." He came into the room slowly, unhurried, a man with every right to look at his own kitchen table. His eyes moved over her papers once more, cataloguing, and he saw her register the catalogue. "Reconciliations."

"Mm," said Mirabel. "Mine are maps."

"They're ledgers," he said quietly.

The word hung between them. She didn't deny it. That frightened him more than a lie would have, because Mirabel lying to him would have meant she still thought of him as someone to protect herself from, and Mirabel telling him the truth meant she thought — God help them both — that they were on the same side of whatever this was.

"Aldous." She never called him Uncle when things mattered; she had dropped the title at eleven years old, the same week she'd learned he kept a locked drawer. "Tomas Vey didn't steal anything. You know that, don't you? The letter I typed—"

"The letter you typed was a sworn deposition based on testimony before the commission." His voice came out in the clerk's cadence, flat and load-bearing, each clause a brick. "It is not your place—"

"It isn't anybody's place!" She was up now, hands flat on the table, and the comparison chart slid and she put her palm over the name at its center as though she could take the word back into the wood. "They arrested a man on Tuesday. There's a tribunal forming. And the letter doesn't read like you, Aldous. I've typed four hundred pages of your correspondence. I know your sentences the way you know your own teeth. Whoever dictated that letter to me has never once used your constructions. So either you've changed how you think—"

"—or someone else thought it for me," he finished.

The room went very quiet. The stove settled. Down the hill a dog barked twice at the frozen river and gave up.

Mirabel's eyes were on his face, reading him the way she read contour lines — the shape of the land beneath the surface marks. He watched her arrive at something and watched her flinch from it.

"You didn't write it," she said, softer. "And you didn't know that when you gave it to me. Or—" and here her voice did something he had never heard it do, cracked open at the hinge, "—or you did know, Uncle, and you fed it through me anyway because it was safer coming out of my machine than yours."

He wanted to say: *the dictation came to me in Reiner's own hand, half-typed notes with instructions in the margin, and I retyped nothing, questioned nothing, because a clerk who questions dies in a cellar with his questions.* He wanted to say: *I told myself it was justice, that Vey was a smuggler anyway, that even an innocent man's misfortune is arithmetic next to the survival of everyone under this roof.*

What he said was nothing, and his nothing lasted four seconds too long, and in those seconds Mirabel's face closed like a gate.

"That's the man who carried Hendrik's last map across the river," she said. "You know that too. Whatever else Tomas Vey is, he was loyal to the dead. And you helped hang a word around his neck for a captain who—" She stopped herself. Looked at the chart under her hand. "Sixty tons, Uncle? Is that where we've landed? Sixty tons of coal worth a man's neck?"

"How do you know about the tonnage?"

"I count everything," she said. "You taught me. Every entry leaves a signature, you used to say, when I was ten and forging your handwriting for practice. You thought I was learning penmanship. I was learning that paper remembers."

*Paper remembers.* He had said that. He had believed it his whole professional life, and now the belief stood in front of him wearing his dead brother's chin and asking him to choose.

Because that was the shape of it, and he had known the shape since noon. If Mirabel pursued this — if she took her little chart with its converging lines anywhere near the tribunal — the inquiry would not stop at Reiner. It would climb the chain of custody the way water climbs wick. It would reach the clerk who accepted dictation without question, who certified the transcription, who initialed the routing slip that sent a forged accusation to the gendarmerie with his office's seal on the envelope. It would find Aldous Galloway's initials on every page of a frame for murder-by-tribunal, and it would ask him, in front of the town whose respect he had spent fifteen years earning in silence, whether he had been Reiner's fool or Reiner's partner, and both answers ended at the same dock: transport, at best. The gallows, if the tribunal needed an example and the war needed a lesson.

And if she stopped — if he could persuade her to stop — then Tomas Vey went before the tribunal with Aldous's letter as evidence and Aldous's silence as sentence, and died in the snow beyond the powder magazine some gray morning while the commission filed its paperwork, and Mirabel would live in this house with him knowing what he had chosen, and she would never again call him by anything at all.

* * *

That evening he sat in his study with the ledger copied out fair into a private notebook — a thing he had promised himself he would never keep, a second set of books, the clerk's original sin — and made, in the manner of a lifelong accountant, a balance sheet.

On the debit side: his position, his pension, his name. On the credit side: nothing he could enter without his hand shaking, so he wrote the entries anyway. *The girl.* Twenty years of her fevers and her mended pens and her terrible violin and the way she stood in doorways saying goodbye to people as if memorizing them. *Hendrik's daughter.* His brother, who had once, aged nine, taken a beating meant for Aldous behind the schoolhouse and never mentioned it afterward, not once in forty years, so that Aldous had owed him a debt the size of a life and repaid it, apparently, by delivering the son-of-his-house into the machinery of a court-martial.

There was a third column, and the third column was the worst. It contained the possibility — seductive, gray, reasonable — that none of this needed resolving at all. The armistice rumors grew daily. If the war ended within weeks, the commission would dissolve, tribunals would be voided or pardoned in the general amnesty, Reiner would slink off to some other province, Vey might walk free by administrative accident, and Aldous's ledger could go into the stove along with his knowledge and his shame, and the river would close over all of it.

That was the entry his pen hovered over longest. *Wait.* It was the most survivable answer and the least human one, and he recognized it, with disgust, as the exact reasoning of a man who had watched soldiers march his neighbors onto barges for fifteen years and told himself each time that intervention was arithmetically impossible.

From upstairs came the sound of Mirabel's typewriter — no, not the typewriter; she only transcribed for the commission downstairs at his office. It was the sound of her pencils, actually, but he had lived with her sounds so long he heard machines in them. Then the front door opened and closed, softly, the way she closed doors when she didn't want to say where she was going, and through the window he watched her cross the garden into the lane with her collar up and her satchel under her arm, heading not toward the commission offices but downhill, toward the quay, toward — he counted the turnings as her lamp bobbed past them — the direction of the cells.

Aldous sat with his private ledger open before him.

He thought: *if she goes to that cell, she becomes a witness, and witnesses in sealed towns float out of the river in spring.* He thought: *if I warn Reiner, I buy us both safety and sell the last thing she will ever forgive.* He thought: *I am fifty-eight years old and I have never once, in my entire careful life, been the man in the story. I have been the man who keeps the minutes of the story.*

He rose, took his coat and hat, blew out the lamp, and let himself out into the freezing dark, following the downhill lamp-glow of his niece toward the cells — carrying under his arm, wrapped in brown paper like a parcel of accounts, the only honest book he had ever kept.
