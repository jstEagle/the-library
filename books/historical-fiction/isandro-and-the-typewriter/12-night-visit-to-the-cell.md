## 12. Night Visit to the Cell

The guard's name was Pieter Brack, and his price was two things: money and silence.

Mirabel found him at the back door of the old customs house just after the lamplighter made his rounds, when Verenmoor settled into that particular winter hush that pressed against the windows like a hand. She had spent the better part of an hour convincing herself to come — standing in the map room with her coat half on, her hand resting on the cold brass of the door handle, telling herself that what she intended was not a crime but a courtesy, a visit of Christian charity to a man who would likely hang regardless. The lie had not held. She knew exactly what she was doing. She was going to offer a condemned man his life, and she was going to discover whether he could afford it.

Brack opened the door before she knocked twice. He was a broad man gone soft around the jaw, with the red-rimmed eyes of someone who drank to make his work bearable, and he looked past her into the dark street before looking down at her at all.

"You're the clerk's girl," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the commission typist," Mirabel said. "It isn't the same thing."

That earned her something like a smile. "No. I suppose it isn't." He stepped back to let her in, then held up one thick finger. "Money first."

She had wrapped the coins in a scrap of linen from her master's drafting kit — a square of the soft grey cloth he'd used to wipe ink from his pens, which she now regretted spending on this — and she put it in Brack's palm without letting their fingers touch. He weighed it the way a fishmonger weighs a fish, by feel alone, and nodded once.

"And the rest?"

"No one sent me. No one knows. If you're asked, you were paid by no one and saw no one, and if anyone presses you, you'll say you took pity on the Galloway girl because grief makes people strange."

"Your uncle will press me."

"My uncle," Mirabel said, "will be told nothing unless you tell him."

Brack studied her for a long moment. In the thin light of his shaded lantern, his face was all planes and shadows, and Mirabel thought she saw there the same arithmetic every man in Verenmoor performed now, nightly: what a thing cost, what it might fetch, what it would cost later if it went wrong. Then he grunted, pocketed the cloth, and picked up a ring of keys.

"This way. And mind the third stair. It sings."

* * *

The cell block had been a wine cellar once, back when Verenmoor had wine worth cellaring and merchants rich enough to buy it. Now the vaulted brick rooms smelled of wet stone and mildew and the sour ghost of barrels long since emptied or broken, and the cold down there was different from the cold above — older, patient, indifferent to season. Two cells remained occupied. One held a drunk who'd struck a gendarme during the grain riot in November; Mirabel heard him snoring as they passed, a wet rhythmic sound like oars in shallows. The other held Tomas Vey.

Brack unlocked the outer door, hung the lantern on an iron hook in the corridor, and retreated up the stairs without a word, leaving the door open behind him — a small mercy, or a small insurance, depending on how the night ended.

Tomas sat on the floor with his back against the wall, knees drawn up, and he did not startle when the light reached him. He had been listening to footsteps for days; Mirabel understood that hers simply registered as one more set, until they weren't.

He stood slowly. In the four days since his arrest, the stranger who had come to her across the frozen river with a document and a desperate errand had been reduced by the cell to something essential: cheekbones, wrists, eyes that adjusted to the lantern light with a kind of weary competence, cataloguing it and dismissing it in the same breath.

"The clerk's niece," he said. His voice was roughened but steady. "Or the typist. Which am I talking to?"

Mirabel's mouth went dry. So he knew. Of course he knew — a sealed town is a town of whispers, and even its prisoners hear them through the stones.

"Both," she said. "Neither. I came because I typed the letter that put you here."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise, she realized. Confirmation. He lowered himself back against the wall and gestured to an overturned crate near the bars, the cell's only other furniture.

"Then sit," he said, "and tell me what you've found out about my handwriting."

"It isn't yours." She stayed standing. Sitting felt too much like settling in, and she did not intend to stay long enough to settle. "I know the letter wasn't yours because I typed it — or I typed something. What was read at your hearing is not what I produced. The 'e' on the commission machine has been misaligned since October; it strikes low and left, and it leaves a faint double-impression where the type catches the platen twice. Every page I've typed for six weeks carries it. The letter they entered as evidence doesn't. Someone retyped it on another machine before it reached the tribunal."

Tomas was quiet for a moment. Down the corridor, the drunk turned over in his sleep and muttered something in a language Mirabel didn't know.

"You came all the way down here," Tomas said at last, carefully, "in the dark, in the cold, paying a guard out of your own purse, to tell me a technicality about a lowercase letter."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because it had kept her awake for three nights. Because she had lain in her narrow bed above the map room listening to the river grind its ice and seen, again and again, the moment at the hearing when the prosecutor had held up that sheet of paper and read the accusation in his flat commission voice — *the theft of stores entrusted to the occupying authority* — and had understood that the words were wrong. Not the content. The rhythm. She had typed those sentences; she remembered typing them, remembered her uncle standing behind her shoulder dictating in his dry, measured voice. But the exhibit bore her sentences in someone else's hands, arranged with a fluency that was not her own — and beneath the fluency, something else she couldn't name until she had sat up at midnight, lit a candle, and pulled her master's old correspondence from its drawer.

Habits. A typist leaves habits in a page the way a walker leaves footprints in snow. Her own were there in everything she produced: the em-space before a parenthesis, the way she broke lines short rather than hyphenate, the particular stubbornness of her margins. And the retyped letter — she had gone through it line by line, candle guttering, heart slowing with each discovery — carried habits that were almost hers. Copied from hers. Learned from hers.

Someone who had studied her transcription had forged the forgery. That was the thought that had brought her down into the earth tonight, more than justice, more than guilt: *someone has been learning to be me.*

"There's more," she said. "And it's the more that matters. Listen."

She told him about the machine. Not the commission's battered Underwood with its drunken 'e,' but another one — the Corona that had belonged to Master Aldwyn Hetch, the mapmaker, confiscated in theory when they took him, vanished in fact. She told him that the type slugs of the exhibit matched the Corona's known impressions, that she had compared them against letters Hetch had written her years ago, which she kept wrapped in oilcloth like the relics they were. That whoever had retyped the accusation against Tomas Vey had done it on a dead man's typewriter — a machine that should have been sitting in an evidence depot in the provincial capital, three hundred miles away, and instead was somewhere in Verenmoor, warm, in use.

"So someone in town has Hetch's machine," Tomas said slowly, "and used it to frame me for theft."

"To frame you for *his* theft." Mirabel heard her own voice drop, though there was no one to hear. "That's what I came to tell you, and what I need you to understand. The stores that disappeared in November — coal, quinine, forty tins of condensed milk meant for the garrison hospital — they were never stolen by any resistance courier. They were signed out under requisitions that don't exist and sold across the ice to the bargemen. Someone in the commission is stealing from the commission, and when the audit comes — and it will come, when the bridge opens — they needed a thief already caught. You were convenient. You were foreign, you arrived on the wrong side of the river, and you asked questions."

Tomas closed his eyes. When he opened them again, Mirabel saw that he had followed her further than she expected, faster than she expected.

"You can prove it," he said. "You can prove the letter forged. How?"

"The machine. Produce the Corona, show the tribunal its type matches the exhibit, and the evidence collapses. Show that the Corona belonged to a man executed for treason, and the tribunal must ask how it got off their own inventory and back into circulation. Ask whose hands have been on it. That question has only one honest answer, and it isn't mine, and it isn't yours."

For the first time, Tomas smiled — a brief, crooked thing, quickly folded away. "A dead man's typewriter testifying at trial. He'd have liked that. He always said paper outlives everyone; he just never guessed how literally."

"He was my master," Mirabel said quietly. "I know what he'd have liked."

The smile left Tomas's face entirely. He looked at her across the small cold cell, and Mirabel watched him arrive, visibly, at the thing she had not yet said — the reason she was still standing, arms crossed against the damp, why her speech had run ahead of her purpose like a cart rolling downhill.

"There's a condition," he said.

"There's a cost." She made herself say it plainly. "I can't produce the Corona. I don't know who has it. But I believe I know who *had* it last, and if I drag that machine in front of the tribunal, I won't be dragging it alone."

* * *

Odile Fenn. The printer's daughter. Mirabel had not spoken her suspicion aloud before, not even to herself in the dark, and saying it now — even obliquely, even to a prisoner — made it heavier and more real.

She laid it out for Tomas as she had assembled it for herself over three sleepless nights, in fragments. The printer Odile Fenn's father had been arrested in the same sweep that took Master Hetch — both men had moved maps and pamphlets for the resistance, both men had known each other since apprenticeship. Before his arrest, the printer had hidden things: type, plates, papers. It stood to reason he'd hidden the Corona too — Hetch's machine, entrusted to the one man in Verenmoor with clean hands and a workshop full of hiding places. And the workshop still operated, still printed the commission's proclamations, and Odile still ran it alone.

And there was more, the part Mirabel hated most, the part that felt like betrayal merely to think: the retyped letter had not been typed by a stranger. Its habits were learned from hers — which meant whoever ran that machine had also handled pages Mirabel had transcribed, had studied them, had taught themselves her hand. There were perhaps five people in Verenmoor with regular access to her transcriptions. Odile printed every one of them.

"I think Odile Fenn has the Corona," Mirabel said. "I think she retyped the letter — not to frame you, she may not even have known what she was finishing, only given sheets to retype, told it was routine copying. Or she knew, and refused, and someone took the machine from her and did it themselves. I don't know which. But if I stand before the tribunal and say *the exhibit was typed on Hetch's Corona,* the first question is *where is it,* and the second is *who has had it,* and every road from those questions runs through the print shop on Weavers' Lane. Through her."

The lantern flame shivered in some draft from the stairs. Tomas had gone very still.

"She's hiding people," he said.

Mirabel stared at him. "What?"

"Fugitives." He said it flatly, wearily, the way a man says a thing he has carried a long distance. "Deserters, escaped laborers, resistance couriers whose names went onto lists. The print shop takes them in, moves them out on the coal barges. That's part of why Hetch's machine would be there — a typist can forge travel passes, ration chits, requisition forms. A printer alone can't. He needs a machine that types, and hands that know commissions' paperwork." He rubbed a hand across his jaw. "I carried the last map Hetch made. I know how the network worked, and I know what happens to a house that gets named at a tribunal. Everyone in it hangs. Everyone sheltered in it goes to the wall with them. Including children, Mirabel, if children are hidden there."

The cellar seemed to contract around her. Mirabel thought of the print shop on Weavers' Lane, the smell of ink and hot metal, Odile's ink-stained fingers and her level gray gaze — *the printer's daughter, always tired, always precise* — and understood, all at once and far too late, that the town she had lived in all winter was a house of false walls and she had never once noticed the doors.

"So there it is," she said, and her voice sounded strange to her, thin in the vaulted dark. "I can save you. The proof exists, and I can carry it. But carrying it burns the print shop. It names the machine, the machine names the shop, the shop gives up everyone it's sheltered since the occupation began. I save one innocent man by delivering a dozen."

"Not a dozen." Tomas's voice was quiet. "Don't round it up to make it easier. Say the number when you know the number. That's what Hetch would say."

"Then help me find another way."

"There isn't another way. There's only the choice." He rose, unhurried, and came to the bars, and Mirabel made herself meet his eyes. Up close she could see how the days below had marked him — the hollows, the cracked lips — and how little the marks mattered against the steadiness underneath. "Listen to me. I came back to Verenmoor knowing the risk. Nobody dragged me. I crossed the ice in December with a dead man's map sewn into my coat because Hetch asked me to, and because the atlas mattered more than my skin, and I believed that then and I believe it now. If the price of my neck is a household of strangers going to the wall — people who never chose my risk, who took me in out of decency—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Don't buy my life with theirs. I'm asking you. Not as a favor to me. As a measure of what I am."

"That's not a measure of what you are," Mirabel said, sharper than she intended. "It's a measure of what they'll do to you. There's a difference, and the tribunal won't honor it."

"Probably not." Almost gently. "But I will."

She turned away from the bars because staying where she was felt like arguing with a saint, and saints are unbearable at close range. She walked the length of the cell, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the way she paced the map room when a coastline wouldn't resolve. The drunk snored. Somewhere above them, muffled by brick and frost, the town clock prepared to strike eleven.

"Then what do I do?" she asked the wall. "Let you hang? Type nothing, testify to nothing, go back to my atlas and let the tribunal do what tribunals do?"

"I didn't say that." She heard him shift, the rustle of straw. "I said don't spend other lives to buy mine. I didn't say stop looking. Find the machine without naming where you found it. Find who dictated the letter to your uncle — because your uncle didn't invent it, Mirabel, whatever you believe about him; clerks don't write accusations, they copy them. Find the theft underneath the theft. Reiner's ledgers, if it's Reiner. Prove the crime, not the machine. The crime convicts him and touches no one who's hiding anything." A pause. "And if you can't — if the only road runs through the print shop — then you let the twelfth of February come, and you let me hang, and you finish the atlas. That's the trade Hetch already made once. It's a fair price. I've had time to check the arithmetic."

The clock struck. Eleven strokes rolled down through the stone, each one arriving fainter than the last, like a message losing itself in translation.

Mirabel stood with her back to the bars, and let herself feel, fully and without flinching, the shape of what he asked: that she walk out of this cellar carrying either his death or other people's, and choose between them, and live with it, and keep making maps. It was the coldest thing anyone had ever requested of her, and the most honorable, and she hated him for it with a sudden fierce clarity that was, she suspected, very close to its opposite.

"I'll find another road," she said.

"Then you'll be the first person to find one in this town." She heard the smile in it, tired and real. "Go home, typist. It's late, and the guard's patience has a shorter life than mine."

At the top of the stairs, Brack was waiting with the lantern, and his face told her before he spoke that the night had grown teeth while she was underground.

"You'd best come quick," he said, keeping his voice low. "There's soldiers at the print shop on Weavers' Lane. Captain Reiner with them. They're turning the place inside out."
