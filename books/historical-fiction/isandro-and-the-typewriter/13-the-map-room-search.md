## 13. The Map Room Search

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which was how Mirabel knew it was not a knock of courtesy. Courtesy in Verenmoor arrived after breakfast and was preceded by an apology shouted through the door. This knock was three raps, evenly spaced, delivered by a man who had already decided he was coming in.

She had been awake since five, as she was every morning now, lying in the narrow bed above the map room and listening to the river grind its ice against the pilings. Since the night visit to the cell — four days ago, though it felt like a season — she had slept the way a candle burns: in small, guarded increments, ready at any moment to be snuffed. Every morning she descended the stairs half expecting to find the door already open, the room already turned inside out.

"Fräulein Galloway." Captain Reiner stood in the doorway with snow on his shoulders and his hands folded behind him, as though he were inspecting a regiment rather than a woman in a wool dress with ink on her cuffs. Two soldiers waited on the step behind him, breath steaming, rifles slung with the studied looseness of men told to look bored. "You'll forgive the hour."

"Captain." She kept her voice level by keeping it quiet. "The commission usually sends its requisitions by Corporal Hess. He knocks like a woodpecker."

"The commission isn't here for requisitions today." He stepped past her without being asked, and the smell of him came with him — wet wool, gun oil, and beneath it something faintly sweet, the cherry tobacco she had once seen him smoke on the bridge while the order closing it was nailed to the toll post. "We're looking for paper."

Her heart did not leap; it did the opposite. It went very still and heavy, the way a hare goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses the field. *Paper.* The word landed in her chest like a coin dropped down a well, and she listened for it to strike bottom.

"The commission has an entire office of paper," she said, following him in. "Two rooms. There's a cabinet that groans when you open it. Hess swears at it daily."

"I'm not looking for the commission's paper." Reiner removed his cap, shook the snow from it with two efficient strokes, and set it brim-down on the worktable as if he owned the table. "I'm looking for hers."

*Hers.* For one vertiginous second he could only mean Odile — but no. He crossed the room to the great flat drawers where the plates slept, and laid one gloved finger on the brass handle as tenderly as a man touching the cheek of a sleeping child, and she understood. The atlas. Her master's atlas. The thing that had been named in the tribunal's whispers, in Aldous's careful silences, in the way Reiner's eyes moved around this room whenever he came here, cataloging shelves the way other men cataloged faces.

"Whose?" she asked, and hated how the question bought her an extra heartbeat while telling her nothing.

"Come now." He smiled without turning. He had a good smile; that was the obscenity of him. "Verenmoor is a small town, Fräulein. Small towns keep few secrets, and those they keep are poorly wrapped. Your master — Master Corvin, the cartographer. Executed in October. A man who mapped troop movements for men who shot at our patrols from church towers. A man who left behind," and here his finger tapped the drawer, twice, a schoolmaster's tap, "*an unfinished atlas*. Where is it?"

"In the commission archive," she said. "Where it has always been. Confiscated with his effects. I would know — I cataloged the confiscation myself. You signed the manifest."

Something flickered across his face — not guilt, but the pleasure of a card player watching an opponent bet confidently into a trap. "I did. And the manifest says *atlas, unfinished, forty-one plates, condition damp*. Forty-one." He turned at last, and his eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who counted things for a living. "Master Corvin was a thorough man. Thorough men do not leave their life's work at forty-one plates. So either he stopped mid-thought, which I do not believe, or there is a forty-second plate. Perhaps more. And a plate that a resistance cartographer thought worth hiding from the commission — well." He spread his hands, presenting the room to her, the drawers, the shelves, the rolled charts in their pigeonholes, the smell of linseed and cold iron. "That is a plate I want."

"You think it's here."

"I think," Reiner said, drawing off one glove and then the other, folding them together with the same care he'd shown the cap, "that you have lived in this room since you were sixteen years old. I think your master trusted you before he trusted anyone. And I think that when a man knows the gallows is coming, he does not hide his treasures in archives controlled by the men who will hang him." He looked around slowly. "I think he hid them here. Under the eyes of a girl who types very well and asks no questions." His gaze came back to her face and rested there. "May I?"

He did not wait for permission. That was the point of asking.

* * *

What followed was the longest morning of Mirabel's life, and she spent most of it standing at the transcription desk with her hands folded on the battered typewriter, pretending to work, because the alternative was to hover, and hovering confessed.

Reiner searched the way she drew maps: systematically, patiently, with an artist's economy of motion. He began at the door and worked clockwise. He emptied the plan chest drawer by drawer, lifting each vellum sheet to the window light, holding it up the way she held proofs, checking not the drawings but the *thicknesses* — and she understood with a lurch that he knew what to look for because he had done this before, in other towns, to other mapmakers. He ran a fingernail along shelf backs. He weighed the brass weights in his palm and set them back in order. He unrolled every chart in the pigeonholes and rerolled them slightly wrong, and she had to watch him do it, had to stand there with her pen moving across a requisition she was not reading while a man who smelled of cherry tobacco mishandled eight years of her master's patience.

The soldiers, meanwhile, took the anteroom. She heard them through the partition: drawers opening, the clink of instruments, a low laugh at something. Every sound in her own body had narrowed to the space beneath the floor.

Because that was where they were. Eleven o'clock the night before, after the house above had gone dark, she had knelt on the cold boards in the corner behind the drafting table — the corner where the light never reached even at noon, where the boards had warped a little with the river damp so that a seam opened between the third and fourth plank — and lifted out the oilcloth bundle to check it, as she checked it every few nights, as a mother checks a sleeping child not because anything is wrong but because checking is love made physical. Forty-two plates. The forty-second was new since autumn, drawn in her master's hand during the last weeks, the weeks she had not known were his last: a chart of the commission's supply movements upriver, dates, tonnages, signatures — including requisitions signed *E. Reiner*, including tonnages that had never reached any depot, including enough diverted coal and grain, itemized in her dead master's neat italic hand, to hang a captain twice.

And beneath the plates, wrapped separately, the thing she had added herself: the key to the misaligned 'e,' the proof typed and dated, the thread that connected the forged letter to the machine that had made it. Everything she had. Everything Tomas was in that cell for. All of it eleven inches under her feet while the man it would kill stood six feet above it, weighing brass in his hands.

"Your master taught you this?" Reiner held up a coastal survey, examining the hachures. "The shading. It's very fine. Better than the commission's own people manage."

"He taught me everything," she said. It was safe ground; she let herself stand on it. "I have been finishing the atlas for the archive. As he asked. It is why the commission keeps me."

"It is why I permit you to be kept," Reiner said mildly, not looking at her. "There is a difference. Do you know what I find interesting about maps, Fräulein Galloway?"

"That they tell you where things are."

"That they *lie*," he said. "Every map lies. Scale lies, projection lies, the paper flattens what God made round. A map is a beautiful organized lie, and the only question worth asking of any map is — who paid for the lie, and what is it hiding?" He set the coastal survey down. "Your master understood that better than anyone. He made maps that looked like innocence and carried troop positions in the margins. Did you know that? Margins, Fräulein. In the decorative borders, in the little wind-roses and sea-monsters. We found three such maps in his studio. Our analysts spent a month on them." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, and it was worse. "So when I search this room, I am not only looking under floorboards. I am looking at wind-roses."

Cold moved through her then, slow as the river ice, because she had never thought of the margins. In eight years she had never thought to look at the margins, and she did not know whether that meant the margins held nothing or whether it meant her master had hidden things from *her*, too, and she was suddenly, absurdly, grieving again in front of the man least entitled to see it.

"Look wherever you like, Captain," she said, and heard her own voice as if from another room: steady, faintly bored. "But if you damage a plate, the loss comes out of someone's report. Not mine. Yours."

For a moment — just a moment — something honest crossed his face. Surprise, and beneath the surprise, a flicker of respect, the way a wolf might regard a dog that showed teeth. Then it was gone, filed away, and he said, pleasantly, "Corporal Brandt. The floor."

* * *

They started at the wrong end. That was the mercy of it, though Mirabel did not believe in mercy that morning and refused to call it mercy even afterward. Brandt and the second soldier pried up boards along the north wall first, where the old stove had been, and the sound of it — the shriek of nails, the crack of seasoned oak — went through her like the sound of her own ribs being counted aloud. Each board came up and was tilted to the light and put back crooked, and she cataloged each one automatically, helplessly, the mapmaker in her mourning the room even while the woman in her waited to be destroyed.

Reiner himself did not pry boards. Reiner walked. He walked the room's perimeter slowly, and then its interior, in a grid, pausing now and again to stamp one heel against the floor — listening. She understood the method with professional horror: hollow spots sing differently. He was sounding the room the way she sounded a frozen river before trusting it, and the room answered him in the language of joists and sleeper walls, and she stood at her desk transcribing nothing, the letter 'e' of the typewriter hanging crooked under her forefinger like a held breath.

He worked inward. Corner by corner, the grid tightened, and she began doing arithmetic she could not stop doing: twelve feet to the drafting-table corner. Eight. The stove's old footprint, cleared. Four feet. Three. He stopped near the window, tilted his head at some difference in the board-song, stamped twice more, and crouched.

Behind the drafting table. The warped corner. The seam between the third and fourth plank, which she had widened herself, in October, with a chisel, on the night after the execution, weeping and working by candlelight with her sleeves rolled up because grief and carpentry apparently used the same muscles.

Reiner put his palm flat on the third plank. He pressed, and rocked it, and it gave — a whisper of movement, an eighth of an inch, the soft complaint of wood shifting over cloth. She saw him register it. Saw stillness come over him the way stillness comes over water before it freezes, everything slowing and clarifying, and he looked down at the plank with an expression she would remember all her life: not triumph, not yet, but the pure, undistracted attention of a man who has spent months listening for exactly this note and has finally heard it under his hand.

One hand's width. His fingers were one hand's width from the edge of the fourth plank, and under the fourth plank lay her master's last testimony, and Odile's brother's life, and Tomas's acquittal, and her own gallows, all bundled in oilcloth, all silent, all waiting on the strength of a warped board and the patience of a murderer.

"Captain," she said.

He did not look up. His thumb had found the seam.

"The atlas is not under the floor," she said.

Now he looked up — annoyed at the interruption, curious despite himself. "No?"

"No." She rose from the desk. Every instinct screamed at her to sit, to be small, to let the moment pass; she stood anyway, because the moment was not going to pass, because his thumb was in the seam, because there was perhaps one sentence in the world left to spend and she had to spend it well. "It's in plain sight. It has been since October. You've walked past it a dozen times." She crossed the room — crossing directly over the corner, her heels landing on the fourth plank with deliberate weight, feeling through her soles the tiny give of the boards, trusting the bundle below not to speak — and took from the shelf above the drafting table the great gray folio labeled in her master's hand *GENERAL ATLAS — VERENMOOR DISTRICT*, the confiscation folio, the forty-one damp plates, the thing she had been "finishing for the archive" in full view of the commission for three months.

"You searched his studio," she said, laying the folio on the table between them like a dealt card. "You confiscated these yourself. You signed the manifest. And then you let me sit here and complete them, because you believed what everyone believes — that a treasure must be hidden, so you hunted for hiding places while the work itself sat on an open shelf with its name written on the spine." She let that settle. Let him hear his own doctrine handed back to him: *a beautiful organized lie, and the only question is what it's hiding.* "If there were a forty-second plate, Captain — and my master finished nothing early, so I grant you your arithmetic — do you imagine a man that careful would bury it under a warped floorboard in a room the commission visits weekly? He was a mapmaker. Hiding was his whole trade. If he wanted something lost, it would be *lost* — sunk in the river, burned, distributed page by page among fifty houses so that no seizure could ever gather it. What's under floors is what people want *found eventually*." A breath. "What he wanted never found, you will never find, because I am the only person alive who knows where it is, and I am telling you, as his apprentice and as a woman who has watched you search my home all morning: it is not in this room."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, the river ground its ice.

Reiner rose from his crouch slowly, knees cracking, and looked at her for a long time — and she bore it, bore the pale measuring of her face, while her heart beat so hard she felt it in her teeth and prayed the boards beneath her feet stayed quiet. He glanced down once more at the warped corner, at the seam, at the eighth-inch of give that had sung to him. Then he straightened his tunic.

"You type well," he said at last, "and you ask no questions, and you lie beautifully, Fräulein. Organized. Almost a map of a lie." He picked up his gloves from the table. "Almost."

He replaced his cap, nodded to the soldiers — the boards went back, badly, nails left proud — and at the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and delivered the parting shot with the pleasantness of a man laying a mine under a road:

"We'll continue this conversation after the tribunal concludes. Trials have a way of loosening tongues. Vey's, yours, your uncle's." He settled the cap. "Perhaps especially his. Good morning."

The door closed. The footsteps faded down the stair. Mirabel stood in the wreck of her room among the crooked boards and disturbed charts, and did not move for a long minute; then she knelt at the corner, eased up the fourth plank, and drew out the oilcloth bundle, and it was warm from the floor's trapped heat and entirely, impossibly intact.

It was only when she had reburied it and sat back on her heels, shaking, that she heard the second set of footsteps on the stair — lighter, hurried, familiar — and the door opened without a knock, and Odile came in white-faced with her coat still buttoned wrong and a printed slip crushed in her fist.

"Mira," she said. "It's Reiner. He's had a confession typed. Signed with Tomas's name. And Mira — it names me."
