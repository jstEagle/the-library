## 14. A Bargain in the Boathouse

The boathouse at the end of the coal stairs had not been built for secrets, but it had been built by a man who distrusted the water, and that amounted to nearly the same thing. Odile's father had told her so the winter before they took him: see how thick the walls are, girl, see how low it sits, how the door opens inward. Old Brandt Verhoeven had been afraid of the river rising, and so he had made himself a room that could hold back a flood. It would hold back other things too. Sound, mostly. Light, if she shuttered the lantern properly. A conversation, if both parties wanted it unheard — and only one of them needed to want it badly enough.

She arrived first. She always intended to arrive first, though tonight she suspected Reiner had left his quarters an hour before her and simply walked slowly, letting her believe she had chosen the hour. That was the shape of the man. He let you think you were holding the rope when all along he was coiling it.

Odile lit the lantern, turned its wick down until the flame was a blue seed inside the glass, and set it on the upturned skiff that served as a table. The boathouse smelled of tar, wet rope, and the coal dust that sifted down from the chutes above like black snow that never stopped falling. Her gloves were damp with river fog. She kept them on. She did not want to leave her fingerprints anywhere in this room warmer than the air around them.

In the pocket of her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, she carried two things. The typewriter's ribbon spool — the worn one, whose ink had printed a hundred false requisitions and ration chits, each one feeding someone through a winter that wanted them dead — and beneath it, folded into a square no bigger than a prayer book, the ledger page she had copied from the commission stores. Three barrels of salt pork signed out to a unit that had never existed. Forty blankets that had crossed the bridge and never come back. Captain Elias Reiner's initials at the bottom of every entry, written in a hand that bore down hard enough to emboss the paper.

She had stolen the copy fair. She had made the forgery fairer. And now she had come to spend both, because dawn was coming, and with it the barge, and with it the arithmetic that had kept her awake for four nights running: one berth, two fugitives, and a town sealed tighter than a jar until the ice broke in spring.

* * *

Reiner came in without knocking, which told her he considered the boathouse already his — or considered himself already safe. He wore his greatcoat open over a uniform that had been pressed more recently than any uniform in Verenmoor had a right to be. In a town of ash-grey faces and boots gone soft at the seams, Elias Reiner looked like a man who had found the one stove in the occupation that actually worked.

"Mistress Brandt." He used her father's name deliberately. Everyone in Verenmoor knew Odile as her mother's daughter; the printer had married into the name. Reiner chose the other one the way another man might choose a knife from a drawer. "You said you had something to sell."

"I said I had something to trade," Odile said. "Selling is what you do. Trading means we both walk out with our hands full."

He smiled at that — a thin, appreciative smile, the kind he spent on people he had already measured for coffins. He did not sit. He stood at the edge of the lamplight with his hands clasped behind him, a man inspecting a warehouse, and let the silence do his talking for him. She had watched him do this at the tribunal hearings, standing at the rail while witnesses twisted themselves dry. She made herself count the drips off the boom above the water. Seven. Eight. Nine.

"You know what I've come about," she said.

"I imagine you've come about Tomas Vey."

"I've come about your ledgers."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. The look of a man who has finally seen the shape of the thing that has been following him, and finds it smaller than he feared, and therefore more dangerous, because small things are easier to dismiss and harder to kill.

"Sit down, Captain," Odile said, "or stand. But listen."

She took the oilcloth packet from her pocket and unwrapped it on the skiff. She left the typewriter ribbon where it was, coiled like a sleeping adder, and laid the ledger page flat beside it, turning it so the writing faced him. Then she struck a match and held it near the corner of the paper — not touching, just warming it, the flame close enough that the paper began to glisten.

"Three barrels of salt pork," she said. "Forty blankets. Eleven crates marked *machine parts* that my father's boy swears were rifles going the wrong way across the bridge, sold to smugglers before the ice closed the river. All of it under your initials. All of it while you sat in a warm room and let a courier take the blame for stores that walked out of your own compound."

"Those entries have explanations."

"I'm sure they do. Explanations written in a hand that isn't yours, by clerks who don't remember, in books that have conveniently lost pages." She tilted the match, and the corner of the paper curled just slightly, browned, went out when she pinched it. "But here is what explanations can't survive, Captain: copies. I learned copying at my father's knee. He could reproduce a customs stamp so well the customs men paid *him* to check their work. Before you arrested him."

Reiner's face did not change. That frightened her more than anger would have. She had expected anger; she had built the whole scene on anger, planned to ride it, to push him while he was hot. Instead he pulled the stool from beside the skiff, turned it, and sat down slowly, like a man settling in for a story he intended to enjoy.

"You're threatening me," he said, "with paper."

"I'm offering you a trade in paper. There's a difference, and it matters."

"Then make it."

* * *

She had rehearsed this part on the walk down, mouthing the words into her scarf until the breath froze in the wool. Now she heard her own voice as if from across the room — steady, low, pitched the way her father pitched his when he talked men into paying for printing they couldn't afford to admit they needed.

"Tomas Vey walks free. Tomorrow morning, before the tribunal reconvenes. You produce a reason — evidence came to light, the letter's provenance is questioned, anything. You're the ranking officer; nobody in this town will ask you twice. In exchange—" she touched the ledger page, "—this original stays buried, along with my copy, and along with everything else I know about where those *machine parts* went and who signed for them. You keep your career. He keeps his neck. I keep quiet."

"And the machine?" His eyes went to the ribbon spool, then past it, as if he could see through the oilcloth to the typewriter itself, hidden somewhere he hadn't yet found. "You mention a machine in your note. Where is it?"

"The machine stays where it is. Consider it insurance. If anything happens to me — anything, Captain, an accident on the stairs, a fever, a fall through the ice — the machine and the papers go to the tribunal with my compliments. If nothing happens to me, you'll never hear of either again."

"That's not a trade," Reiner said. "That's blackmail with better manners."

"It's the only kind of trade anyone makes with an occupying officer." She let herself lean forward, let the lantern light catch her face. "You want to know why I'm doing this? Not for Vey. I barely know the man. For my father, who printed three thousand resistance leaflets on press number two and got a cell in his own cellar for it. For every person in this town who watched you hang Master Corvin against the mill wall and call it justice. I'm doing it because for once the paper cuts the other way."

She had said too much. She knew it the moment the words left her — she could feel them hanging in the cold air like breath, like evidence. But there was a pleasure in it too, a hot wire of pleasure down her spine, saying aloud what she had swallowed for two years. Reiner regarded her for a long moment, and then he did something worse than threaten her.

He agreed.

"All right," he said quietly. "Vey goes free."

Just that. No haggling, no wounded pride, no conditions about the ledger beyond a nod toward its burial. He stood, buttoned his greatcoat with unhurried fingers, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch.

"One question, Mistress Brandt." He didn't turn around. "The confession we found — the one typed on that missing machine, confessing in Vey's name to thefts he never committed. If your machine is such honest insurance, tell me: who do you suppose taught it to lie?"

And before she could answer — before she could even begin to understand that the question was the answer — he lifted the latch, and the cold came in around him like water.

"I'll have the release drawn up by morning," he said. "Trust me."

The door closed. The lantern guttered in the draft and steadied itself.

* * *

Odile stood alone in the boathouse for a long time after the sound of his boots had faded up the coal stairs. The drips from the boom counted out the silence — seven, eight, nine — and she listened to them because counting meant not thinking, and not thinking meant not feeling the thing that was uncoiling slowly in her stomach, cold and bright and certain.

She gathered the ledger page. Folded it. Wrapped it. Her hands were steady — printers' hands, hands trained to be steady over live type at three in the morning — and it was precisely their steadiness that convinced her.

Because here was what she knew, and what she had known since the moment he asked his one question and walked out without waiting for her answer: Reiner had produced a confession typed on her father's machine. On *her* machine. The machine that had been hidden in the print shop wall since before his arrest, that no living soul but Odile and her brother had touched in two years. Which meant either someone had taken it and returned it without her knowing — impossible; she checked the wall cavity the way other women checked a purse — or Reiner had access to it still, through someone, somehow, and had used it to forge Vey's confession, which meant the confession was worthless, which meant—

Which meant he had no need to trade at all. He had sat down on that stool knowing the exhibit was his own forgery, knowing the tribunal would collapse or wouldn't on its own, and he had agreed to free Tomas Vey the way a man agrees to give you back the money he stole from you yesterday.

*Trust me,* he'd said. And she had heard it, God help her, she had heard it in her ear all the way home along the frozen quay: the way her father used to say it when a customer asked whether the pamphlets could be traced. *Trust me.* And her father would smile, and the customer would leave lighter, and two weeks later the soldiers would come to the shop asking questions that only that customer could have asked.

Hope built on Reiner's word was a bridge built on ice, and spring was not coming soon enough.

She banked the lantern, climbed the coal stairs, and stood a moment in the falling dark looking at the print shop window, where a light was already burning — her brother's lamp, the signal lamp, lit an hour early because he had seen a soldier at the end of the street taking notes in a small book.

By morning, she knew — the way you know weather in your teeth — the release order would exist, and it would be a trap dressed as mercy, and somewhere between the boathouse and dawn, Reiner would move against the print shop, and the machine, and everyone who had ever fed from her forged hand.

There was no one in Verenmoor she could warn with words. Words traveled through Reiner's ears.

But there was a girl in the map room who read paper the way priests read scripture, and who had already found the truth in a misaligned letter of the alphabet — and Odile had a press, and ink, and until sunrise, one job left worth doing.

She went inside, and began to print.
