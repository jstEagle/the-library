## 15. The Typed Confession

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, in the form of a boy with a red armband and breath like boiled turnips, who would not wait for Mirabel to find her coat.

"Captain Reiner wants the clerk's niece at the customs house. Now."

*The clerk's niece.* Not *the typist*. Not even her name. She pulled on her coat over her nightdress, pinned her collar in the cracked hallway mirror without seeing it, and followed the boy down through streets still blue with frost. Smoke stood straight up from every chimney in Verenmoor — the wind had died in the night, and the whole town seemed to be holding its breath along with her. At the corner by the baker's she passed Odile coming the other way with a basket of type-metal slugs from the shop, and neither of them looked at the other, which was itself a kind of speech. *We have not spoken. We are strangers. Whatever happens, we were never—*

The customs house smelled of cold iron and old tobacco. A sentry took her name without writing it down, which frightened her more than if he had. She was shown not into Reiner's office but into the long hearing room where the tribunal sat, its rows of benches empty at this hour, the high windows letting down a grey light that made everything below it look already judged. Captain Elias Reiner stood at the far end beside the clerk's table, arranging papers with the neat, patient movements of a man laying a table for guests he intends to poison.

He was alone. That was the first wrongness. The second was that Aldous was not there; Aldous was always there for anything involving paper, hovering, straightening, his whole soul bent toward the proper filing of the world. The third wrongness she did not yet understand, but felt arrive in her body like a change of pressure, the way one feels weather before the river shows it.

"Fräulein Galloway." Reiner did not look up from his papers. "You will sit."

She sat, on the front bench, her hands folded where he could see them. She had learned long ago, in her master's map room, that hands are read like documents and must be composed accordingly.

"You have attended the tribunal's sessions," Reiner said. "As transcription support. You know the case against the prisoner Vey."

"I transcribed the requisitions," Mirabel said carefully. "I attended as instructed."

"And you are familiar with machines of this type." It was not quite a question. He lifted a sheet from the table at last and held it up between two fingers, the way a fishmonger holds something he suspects of smelling. "Then you may be of use to me. This document came into my possession last night."

He laid it on the bench beside her rather than handing it to her — a small cruelty, making her lean toward it, making her want it visibly.

It was a confession. She saw that before she read a word of it; confessions have a shape, a shortening of the spine, and this page had it. Then she began to read, and the words assembled themselves in her mind with terrible clarity, because she knew this vocabulary, this grammar of accusation, the commission's house style drilled into her through three winters of requisitions and inventories:

*I, Tomas Vey, do confess that on the nights of the ninth and seventeenth of November I did remove commission stores from the customs warehouse at Verenmoor with the knowledge and assistance of an accomplice resident in this town, namely the daughter of the printer Johann Brandt, said goods having been conveyed to persons unknown...*

She stopped reading. Not because she could not continue, but because her eyes had snagged on something above the words, in the physical country where words live — the ink itself, the impressions, the machine that had struck them.

She knew those impressions.

Not the commission typewriter. Its 'e' sat crooked, tilted two degrees east of true, a flaw she had mapped as thoroughly as any coastline because her livelihood depended on knowing her instrument. This page's 'e' stood upright. But its 't' — the crossbar struck slightly low and left, so that every 't' wore its stroke like a hat pulled over the eyes. Its 'y' descended with a faint double-bite at the baseline, as though the type slug were chipped. And the 'm' carried a ghost of ink at its right shoulder, a small black freckle repeated down the page like a birthmark.

That was Herr Corvus's machine. Her master's typewriter, missing since the morning they took him. The machine on which — she now understood, with a cold that started in her stomach and climbed — Odile had forged her small mercies, and on which someone had now forged a noose for two people instead of one.

"There is a signature," Reiner said. His voice was mild, unhurried. He was enjoying this, she realized; enjoyment was precisely what his mildness was for. "You will note it matches the hand on Vey's intake papers."

She made herself look. *Tomas Vey*, in a careful, slightly cramped script. She had seen his handwriting once, on the document he'd asked her to copy the day the bridge closed — a rounder hand, looser, the descenders curling back on themselves like fern fronds. But she had copied that document; the copy existed; anyone who wished could practice against it. A forgery of a signature was the cheapest lie on any page.

"The date," Reiner continued, "is the fourteenth of December. Three days ago. Before the tribunal reconvenes tomorrow." He came around the table at last and stood over her, and she kept her eyes on the page because looking up would have been surrender of a kind she could not afford. "You will observe, Fräulein Galloway, that the document names an accomplice. The printer's daughter. Odile Brandt."

"I observe what the document says," Mirabel said. Her voice sounded distant to her own ears, level and thin, like wire drawn through a die.

"What the document says is about to become what happened," Reiner said. "Unless, of course, it is shown to be false. And here is my difficulty." He drew out the word *difficulty*, savoring it. "The tribunal has been given to understand that all commission correspondence passes through one machine, under the supervision of your uncle. If this document was produced on another machine — a stolen machine, in private hands — then either it is a forgery, or there exists in this town an apparatus of false documentation operating for months without detection." He let the silence do its work. "Either finding damages me, Fräulein. I do not enjoy being damaged. So I am offering you a professional consultation, as the town's most qualified reader of typescript. Tell me what you see."

*Tell me what you see.* As though he had not chosen the question the way one chooses a weapon.

She understood the trap in full, then, and admired its construction even as she hated it. If she declared the confession authentic, Odile went to the cell beside Tomas, and the hidden fugitives lost their forger and their advocate in a single morning. If she declared it forged, she confirmed the existence of a second machine — and led Reiner directly along the trail to the printer's shop, to Odile, to everything Odile had built and everyone Odile was hiding. Either answer was a door with him standing behind it.

There was a third answer. It arrived in her mind unbidden, wearing her master's voice: *Read the page, girl. All of it. Pages confess more than their authors know.*

"May I examine it properly?" she asked.

Reiner inclined his head, one degree.

She lifted the sheet with both hands, as if it might bruise, and gave herself permission to be, for one measured interval, nothing but a technician. The ribbon was old — older than the commission's, dry in places, the letters graying unevenly at their left edges where the fabric had worn thin. The line spacing drifted: the fourth gap between lines was a hair wider than its sisters, the signature of a platen whose ratchet skipped under a certain rhythm of return. She knew that skip. She had heard that skip. On winter evenings in the map room, her master's fingers walking the carriage back, the little mechanical hiccup on the fourth line like a stammer in the machine's speech —

And then she saw it. The thing that stopped her breath and turned the room, briefly, dark at its edges.

At the phrase *with the knowledge and assistance of*, there was a hyphenated break across lines — *know-ledge* — and the space around that hyphen was exact. Symmetrical. One em of air on each side, set with the unconscious precision of habit.

She made that space. Not Reiner, not Odile, not Herr Corvus's ghost. Herself. It was her tic, her fingerprint, drilled into her by a master who had insisted that a hyphen is a bridge and bridges need approaches of equal length on both sides. No other typist in Verenmoor spaced broken words that way. She had watched herself do it a hundred times without thinking, the way one watches one's own handwriting grow identical year upon year. And here it was, reproduced on a page written by someone else's hands on someone else's machine — reproduced deliberately, studiedly, twice more further down: *ac-complice*, *ware-house*.

Someone had taken her habits and worn them like a coat.

She read on with a sensation she had no name for, worse than fear — a violation of the interior kind, as if a stranger had learned to reproduce not her handwriting but her breathing. The spacing after periods: single-and-a-half, her compromise between her master's airy doubles and the commission's cramped singles. The numeral style: she wrote dates with the month spelled out and the year abbreviated, *14 December '18*, and there it was, faithful as a portrait. Even her small lazinesses were present — the abbreviation *art.* for *article*, which she alone used among the commission clerks, because Herr Corvus had used it and she had inherited it along with his atlas and his enemies.

Whoever had typed this confession had studied her work. Had sat, perhaps, with pages of her transcription spread open, measuring her rhythms the way she measured coastlines, until they could produce a document that would swear under oath it came from her machine, her hands, her conscience.

Her craft — the one clean thing she owned, the thing that had kept her fed and invisible and sane through three winters of occupation — had been turned inside out and made to lie in her voice.

"Fräulein Galloway." Reiner's voice came from very far away and then very near; he had crouched to bring his face level with hers, and she made herself meet his eyes, grey and patient as river ice. "You seem distressed. Take your time. What do you see?"

*He doesn't know.* The realization surfaced slowly, through the shock, like something heavy rising through dark water. Reiner had commissioned this forgery — who else? Who else needed Odile silenced and Tomas buried under a second shovelful of paper? But he had not typed it himself. He had handed it to someone else, given them the stolen machine and the samples of her work, and he had never read the product closely enough to see whose fingerprints it bore. To Reiner it was simply paper that said what he wanted said. He could not read typescript the way she could. Almost no one alive could.

Which meant the page in her hands was a bomb with the fuse pointing backward. If she named her own habits aloud — *this spacing, this abbreviation, this is my hand disguised* — she proved the document forged beyond argument, for no forger would incriminate himself so elaborately unless the forger intended the incrimination to fall elsewhere. On her. The confession was built to hang Odile first and Mirabel second: *the typist who produces false documents for fugitives, the clerk's niece, the accomplice.*

If she stayed silent, the trap would close on Odile tomorrow, and when it closed, the investigation would follow the paper home to the map room, exactly as designed.

He was watching her face. She felt the weight of his attention like a hand flat between her shoulder blades, and understood that some part of him — the part that had searched her map room and come away empty — suspected already, and was waiting to see whether her distress would resolve into guilt or into usefulness.

"I see an old ribbon," she said at last, and was astonished at the steadiness of her own voice, the way one is astonished to find oneself still standing after a blow. "Dry on the left margin. The machine that struck this has seen hard service."

"Go on."

"The platen skips on the fourth return. The 't' strikes low. The 'e' is upright." She paused, and chose her next words the way one chooses footing on river ice, testing each before committing weight. "The commission machine's 'e' leans east. This document cannot have come from the commission office."

"No," Reiner agreed softly. "It cannot." He straightened, retrieving the page from her hands with two fingers, and she watched her own hyphens leave her. "Which presents us both with a problem of interpretation, Fräulein Galloway. A forged confession naming a known smuggler and her accomplices — or a genuine confession produced on stolen equipment by enemies of the commission. Tomorrow the tribunal will choose an interpretation. I would prefer that you assist us in choosing the correct one." He smiled then, briefly, without warmth, the smile of a man closing a ledger. "Consider what your testimony will cost, and whom. You have until nine o'clock."

He turned back to his papers. She was dismissed without another word.

* * *

Outside, the cold hit her like water and she was grateful for it. She walked without direction, past the shuttered customs shed, past the coal yard where the barge *Marten* lay low and black against the quay, taking on the season's last cargo for the run downriver before the armistice — if there was an armistice; the town argued about it nightly in the taverns, hope and rumor changing hands like counterfeit coin. One berth. She had known it for days, the arithmetic of it sitting in her chest beside everything else. Now the arithmetic had grown new terms.

She found herself at the sea wall below the church, where the river ran fast and green-grey under its skin of drifting ice-floes, and she stood there gripping the frozen iron rail while the truth arranged itself in her mind with the pitiless clarity of a well-drawn chart.

Someone had her sample pages. Not the commission copies — those lived in the office safe under Aldous's key, and Reiner could have had those, yes, but the *habits* he'd had reproduced were not the sort one learns from a week's study of official requisitions. The *art.* abbreviation. The equal approaches to a hyphen. Those were intimate details, the sediment of years.

Or — and here the cold entered somewhere deeper than the weather — they were details available to one person who had watched her type for three winters. Who had sat across the office from her every working day. Who had access to the safe, to the samples, to the stolen machine's whereabouts if it had passed through resistance hands before Herr Corvus's arrest — hands that had carried his final map across the river, hands connected in ways she was only beginning to chart.

No. She rejected it, and it returned. She rejected it again, and it stood its ground like a fact.

Because there was one more thing on the page, and she had not told Reiner, and she barely dared tell herself. Beneath the signature line, invisible to any eye that did not know to look, the paper held an indentation — a faint impression, caught by her fingertips as much as her eyes, of text typed and then removed. A practiced eye can read a ghost page by raking light across it, and she had done so instinctively, out of habit, before she'd known why. Most of it had been struck over too thoroughly to recover. But one line survived, half-legible, typed earlier and discarded:

*A. G. to confirm distribution —*

A. G. Aldous Galloway. Her uncle's name, drafted onto this very page and then erased — erased clumsily, or carelessly, or left deliberately, a thread trailing from the forgery back toward the customs office. Either the forger had drafted the confession with Aldous's participation and cut him out. Or the forger had wanted a trail leading to him, and had left it where only a forensic reader would ever find it — wanting *her* to find it.

Wanting her to know that whatever she did next, the road forward ran through her uncle.

Mirabel stood at the rail until her hands went numb, watching the ice come down the river in slow, silent procession — plate after plate, each one carrying its burden of frozen spray toward the bridge, toward the sea, toward places she would never see — and thought about the atlas hidden beneath her floorboards, and the berth on the *Marten*, and the sound her uncle's keys made when he locked the office safe at night, and how well he slept afterward, and how she had never once, in nineteen years, asked herself whether that sleep was innocent.

Nine o'clock tomorrow, Reiner had said. The tribunal at ten. And somewhere in this town, in possession of her dead master's typewriter and her living habits, sat a person who could write her name onto any crime in Christendom — and had already begun practicing the signature.
