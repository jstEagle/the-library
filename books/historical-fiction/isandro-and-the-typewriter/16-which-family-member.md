## 16. Which Family Member

The summons came at dusk, folded once and slid under the map room door the way official paper had learned to travel in Verenmoor — quietly, without a name attached, as though the building itself were embarrassed by what it carried.

Mirabel let it lie on the floorboards for a full minute before she picked it up. Outside, the river was doing its winter voice, that low grinding mutter of ice against the pilings, and somewhere down the street Odile's father's old press — now Odile's press — clattered through a late run of proclamations nobody would read twice. She unfolded the paper under the lamp.

*Fräulein Galloway. The tribunal will reconvene tomorrow at the ninth hour. You are requested to attend as a witness in the matter of the document known as Exhibit A. Bring any materials relevant to questions of authorship.*

*Authorship.* The word sat on the page like a trap set with care. Someone — Reiner, or the tribunal's cold-handed clerk, or whoever drafted this — wanted her to stand up in front of the commission and say whose fingers had struck those keys. And there was only one answer left to give. She had followed the misaligned 'e' like a blood trail through every document the commission had produced since October, and the trail did not stop at Captain Reiner's door. It passed through it. And standing just inside, holding the ledger open, checking the columns, initialing the transfers with his neat, upright signature —

Aldous.

She had known it since morning, if she was honest, since before morning, since the night she'd laid her own typed requisitions beside the forged transfer orders and seen the same hand of instruction moving beneath both: her uncle's phrasing, her uncle's habit of writing *stores* where everyone else wrote *goods*, her uncle's punctilious little flourish after every numeral. He hadn't typed the forgery — that had been done on her master's stolen machine, by Reiner or at Reiner's order. But Aldous had signed off on the inventories that made the theft invisible. He had countersigned the accusation against Tomas Vey. He had brought her the dictation himself, set it on her typewriter platen with his own hands, and told her it was justice.

And when she had confronted him — an hour ago, in his office, with the stove ticking and the smell of ink and wet wool between them — he had not denied it. That was the part she kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. He had not lied to her. He had only looked old.

---

She found him still in the commission office when she went back after midnight, because she knew he would be there. Aldous Galloway slept badly at the best of times, and these were not the best of times; he kept a cot behind the filing cabinets but rarely used it, preferring to sit upright at his desk with his collar unhooked and his hands flat on the closed ledger, like a man keeping a coffin lid pressed down.

"You should be asleep," he said, without looking up.

"So should you."

"The tribunal sits at nine."

"I know what it says. I'm the one they summoned."

That made him look up. In the lamplight his face was all planes and hollows, the face from her childhood stretched over newer bone. She had a sudden, useless memory of being seven years old and feverish, and Aldous carrying her down three flights of stairs to the doctor with her head bumping against his shoulder, counting the steps out loud so she would have something to hold onto besides the heat. *Forty-one, little one. Forty-two.* He had counted her through the fever. He had counted her through everything since her parents went into the fog of the western road one autumn and never came back out of it.

"Sit down, Mirabel."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down anyway." There was no command in it. It was almost a plea dressed in the clothes of one.

She sat, because refusing cost more than obeying, and for a while neither of them spoke. The stove ticked. A shutter somewhere banged in the wind off the river, once, twice, then gave up.

"If I testify," she said finally, "about the machine, about the 'e', about who dictated the letter and who countersigned the transfers —"

"Then I stand where Vey stood." He said it evenly, the way he read shipping manifests. "I've done the arithmetic. I taught you arithmetic. Don't look surprised that I can still do it."

"It isn't arithmetic." Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated it. "Uncle Aldous, you didn't steal anything."

"No," he agreed softly. "I only made the stealing possible, and then made it deniable, and then helped hang a stranger for it. Which in a sealed town amounts to the same crime, wearing a cleaner coat." He opened the ledger at last, and turned it toward her, and she saw he had been writing in it — not figures, but a statement, dated, signed, witnessed by no one. A confession. "I've been composing this since supper. If you testify, I will submit it. I'll say the dictation came from Reiner and that I knew it and let it pass. They'll try us together or they'll try me after him; either way the gallows has room."

"Reiner will hang and you'll transport," she said. "If you turn evidence against him first. That's what they gave Halloran last spring — cooperation for commutation. Transport isn't —" She stopped. Transport was a coal ship and a colony and very likely death by degrees, but it was not the rope, and they both knew which side of that line mattered tonight.

"Transport," Aldous repeated, tasting it. "To the other end of the world, on a boat, in irons, at my age." Something flickered across his face that was not quite a smile. "Your mother's father was transported, did you know? Before you were born. For printing. It runs in the family apparently — the paper crimes."

"Don't joke."

"Somebody has to. You never learned how, and I've had more practice."

She put her hand flat on the ledger, over his confession, as if by covering it she could unread it. "There's another way."

"There is always another way. That's what makes this town unbearable — the ways out are always visible and never open." He closed the ledger gently, taking it back from under her hand. "Say it anyway."

"The barge." Her mouth was dry. "The last coal barge goes at dawn, armistice or no armistice. One berth. One person, out, before the river locks and the tribunal does whatever tribunals do when empires sign papers over their heads. I have the berth, Uncle. My name is on the manifest. I could put your name there instead. Tonight. Before anyone checks."

The silence that followed was long enough to have weather.

"You'd give it to me," he said at last. Not a question. An inventory.

"I'd give it to you."

"And leave yourself here, with Reiner furious and a confession in my desk that names you as my accomplice whether you confess or not, and a tribunal sitting at nine that wants to hear about authorship." He shook his head slowly. "No."

"It isn't yours to refuse."

"Everything is mine to refuse. I raised you precisely so that you would learn to refuse things." But his voice had gone rough, and he turned his face toward the dark window, toward the river he could not cross, and for a moment Mirabel saw him not as the clerk of the occupying commission but as the man who had stood at a train window eleven years ago, holding a child's hand through the gap, promising her parents — to their faces, to the empty air, it made no difference — that nothing would happen to her. Promises made to empty air were the only kind that lasted, in Verenmoor.

"If I go," he said to the window, "and you stay, and Reiner decides to make an example — and he will, Mirabel, he's cornered now and cornered men make examples — then everything your master died for burns with you. The atlas. The plates. The truth about the troop movements, the resistance routes, all of it. It dies in a map room floor because I couldn't bear to take a boat ride." He turned back. "Whereas if I stay and stand up and tell the tribunal what Reiner did —"

"They might hang you."

"They might. Or the armistice comes tonight, or tomorrow, and the whole commission dissolves into paperwork nobody enforces, and I spend a year sweeping someone else's streets until the new authorities decide what to do with clerks of the old ones." He said it lightly, but his hands, flat on the ledger, had gone white at the knuckles. He was afraid. He had been afraid for weeks, probably for months, and he had typed and countersigned and smiled at Reiner across the mess table with that fear sitting in his stomach like swallowed glass, and he had done it all, she realized, so that the household stipend would keep coming, so that the map room would keep its coal ration, so that she could finish an atlas that was itself a hanging offense. His cowardice and his love had been the same act, performed in different rooms, and she did not know how to weigh them separately anymore.

"Aldous." She used his Christian name deliberately, the way their family used it — rarely, and only across a table where something true was about to be said. "Odile Ferreol has a brother. Emile. He's been hiding in the print cellar since Reiner's men started pulling printers apart. If the forgeries are traced — and they will be traced, tomorrow, when I testify about the machine — he hangs. Not transports. Hangs. He's nineteen." She heard her own voice steady itself with effort. "One berth, Uncle. You or Emile. That's the whole of the arithmetic you're so fond of. And whatever I choose, someone in this town wakes up at dawn either saved or condemned by me."

"Then don't choose," he said quietly.

"There's no not choosing. Silence chooses too. You taught me that — you used to say the blank page takes sides by staying blank."

He laughed, briefly, terribly. "Did I say that? I was a fool when I said it." He rose, walked to the window, stood with his back to her, a narrow silhouette against the snow-light coming off the river. When he spoke again his voice had the careful evenness of a man reading from a document he has rehearsed.

"When your parents disappeared, I made a vow at the rail of the customs house — an ugly place for vows, but it was the only high ground in town. I vowed that I would keep you alive, and that I would keep you honest, and that if ever the two vows conflicted I would choose the second, because a living liar is a worse inheritance than a dead honest woman." He turned around. "They conflict tonight, Mirabel. They have conflicted for months, and I chose the first vow every single time, and look what it bought. An innocent man in a cell. A printer's boy in a cellar. A niece about to destroy herself to patch what I broke." He came back to the desk and laid his hand on hers, and his fingers were cold as the ledgers. "Take the berth."

"No."

"Take it, get the atlas out over the river, deliver it to the capital, to the Geographical Society, to anyone with a printing press and a conscience —"

"The atlas stays hidden until the armistice settles who owns the presses. We've been through this."

"Then take the berth for yourself and live, and be honest, and let me pay for both of us. That's the trade I'm offering. It's the only trade I have left."

* * *

She walked home through the frozen streets at two in the morning, and the town was so quiet she could hear her own boots thinking.

Verenmoor under snow was a map she knew better than any sheet in the atlas — every roofline, every drain, every shortcut through the tannery yard — and tonight the familiarity felt like a cage drawn in her own hand. This was what her master had tried to teach her, back when the lessons were easy and the river was open: that a mapmaker's deepest knowledge is also her deepest loyalty, and that loyalties, unlike rivers, do not fork cleanly. They braid. They run together under the ice and come up who knows where.

The berth. She turned it over and over as she walked. One berth, dawn, the last barge. Three claimants now instead of two, which was somehow worse than the original arithmetic — herself, her uncle, and a nineteen-year-old boy she had met exactly twice, once in a cellar smelling of ink and once as a name on a forged pass. Any of them could be saved. Two of them would not be. And the tribunal sat at nine regardless, indifferent as a tide table.

At her door she stopped. Snow had drifted against the frame, and she broke it with her boot heel, and in the broken whiteness she saw, absurdly, contour lines — the way her master would have drawn them, fine and close together, a steep place. A scarp. The edge of something.

Inside, she did not light the lamp. She knelt at the floorboard in the corner, the loose one, the one that had learned her secret the way everything in Verenmoor eventually learned everything, and lifted it, and looked down at the oilcloth packets lying in the dark. Fifty-one plates. Fifty-one finished maps of a war that no one was supposed to see from above. And the fifty-second, half-inked, waiting on her bench upstairs: the final sheet, the one her master had died mid-stroke on, the one that showed the supply convoys and the dates and the quantities, Reiner's convoys, Reiner's quantities, the thefts rendered in cartographic fact that no clerk's countersignature could unmake.

She could finish it tonight. Four hours' work, maybe five. And walk it into the tribunal at nine, and lay it beside Exhibit A, and let the misaligned 'e' and the convoy totals and her own sworn testimony do what no bargain could: break Reiner, free Tomas, save Emile — and bury Aldous under the same collapse, because his initials were in the margins of the very ledgers the map contradicted.

Truth, her master used to say, is not a weapon you get to aim. It's a flood. You open the sluice and it goes where it goes, and you had better love the valley before you drown it.

She knelt there a long time, her hand resting on the oilcloth, feeling through it the faint ridges of engraved lines — the coastlines and contours and the small precise towns of a country that had stopped existing, drawn by a dead man who had believed maps could outlive regimes. Behind her eyes, two vows fought in the dark the way her uncle had described: keep her alive, keep her honest. And underneath both, quieter than either, a third thing she had no clean name for — the knowledge that whatever she decided before dawn, she would be deciding it alone, and that being needed was not the same as being spared, and that Verenmoor, sealed and snowbound and drowning in its grudges, had become the only map she would ever truly live inside.

Upstairs, on the bench, the fifty-second plate waited under its cloth.

Mirabel closed the floorboard, stood, and went to light the lamp over her workbench. Whatever she chose, it would be inked before morning — and the tribunal, the barge, and her uncle were all going to learn her decision at the same hour, in the same room, at the point of a pen.

She uncapped the ink, drew the cloth from the plate, and began.
