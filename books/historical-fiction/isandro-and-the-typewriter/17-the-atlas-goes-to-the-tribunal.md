## 17. The Atlas Goes to the Tribunal

Mirabel had always believed that finishing a map would feel like setting down a weight. Her master had said otherwise, once, in the warm season before the war came to Verenmoor — she had been sixteen, inking her first coastline under his eye, and he had told her that a finished map was not a burden released but a burden made permanent. *Ink is a promise you cannot take back, Mirabel. Every line you draw, someone will believe.* She had nodded the way apprentices nod, hearing without listening.

She listened now.

The map room was dark except for the single lamp she allowed herself, its shade hooded with a towel so no light escaped the shutters. Outside, Verenmoor slept under a sky the color of wet slate, and the river moved in the dark with its cargo of ice. Dawn would bring the tribunal. Dawn would bring Reiner's new confession, the one typed in her own hand's ghost, the one that would hang Odile beside Tomas if no one stopped it. Dawn would bring everything, which meant that tonight — this last, thin sliver of night — was the only hour in which Mirabel Galloway could decide what kind of person she intended to be when the sun came up.

She unrolled the final plate across her worktable and weighted its corners with the brass blocks that had been Master Corvin's, worn smooth by thirty years of his palms and two years of hers.

It was not, strictly speaking, her work. That was the first lie she would have to stop telling herself. The plate had been his — begun in his hand, in his careful stippled shading, in the weeks before the commission took him to the courtyard behind the customs house and shot him for the crime of knowing where things were. The troop movements he had mapped for the resistance were only one corner of what he had known. He had known the roads. He had known the fords. And in the last month of his life, working in stolen hours, he had known something else entirely: the exact arithmetic of Captain Reiner's thieving.

She lowered her loupe over the plate and began.

The river first. Always the river, her master had taught her; the water is the skeleton on which everything else hangs. She drew the Ver with a steadiness that surprised her, the north bank with its coal staithes and the south bank with its reed beds, the bridge rendered not as the schematic arrow of the commission's official surveys but as it truly was — eleven spans, the third cracked since the flood of '15, the fifth rebuilt with timber too green and too thin because the contract had gone to a cousin of the garrison quartermaster at half the price of honest wood. She inked that flaw precisely. A map that flattered was a map that lied.

Then the warehouses. Here her hand slowed, because here was the heart of it, the thing Master Corvin had died carrying out of Verenmoor in the shape of six inches of copperplate.

Reiner's requisitions said the commission had burned four hundred sacks of grain in the autumn — spoiled, the paperwork claimed, unfit for issue. But spoilage leaves a pattern, and patterns are a cartographer's native language. Corvin had tracked the smoke. He had walked the towpath at odd hours, sketching in a pocket book disguised as a birdwatcher's diary. He had recorded which warehouse chimneys gave smoke and which did not, on which nights, in which winds — and the chimneys of Warehouse Three, the one where the "spoiled" grain was supposedly destroyed, had never smoked at all. Four hundred sacks of requisitioned grain, drawn out of the occupied town's mouth during its hungriest winter, sold across the river through neutral hands, the proceeds vanished into an account Reiner believed no one could read.

But Corvin could read anything written on the land. That was his genius and his death. He had converted ledgers into geography: the barge moorings marked with dates, the cart tracks to the neutral ferry measured and timed, the empty warehouse plotted beside the full granary ledger-entry like a wound laid open beside the knife.

And in the margin — Corvin's margin, small and savage beneath the dispassionate neatness — the second truth: *Stores taken 12 Oct., 3 Nov., 21 Nov. Blamed on rivermen. Rivermen absent. See Vey, T., alibi witness statement, ref. customs house log.*

Tomas Vey had been on the wrong side of the river on every date of the alleged thefts. Corvin had proven it months before Mirabel ever met him. The atlas had been defending Tomas all along, waiting in the dark under the floorboards like a patient witness, while the town prepared to hang him for a crime committed in its name against its own stomach.

Mirabel sat back from the plate. Her eyes were burning. She let them, briefly, and then she dipped her pen.

There was still the legend to write — and here was the decision, the true one, the one she had been circling all night like a moth around a lamp it knew would kill it.

She could sign the plate *Elias Corvin.* His name alone. Let the dead man carry it into the tribunal, untouchable, unimpeachable; who would cross-examine a corpse? If the map were discovered to be hers, if Reiner survived long enough to seek revenge, her uncle would stand beside her and they would hang together or transport together, and everything Aldous had spent two years building — his position, his caution, his careful gray invisibility inside the machine of the occupation — would be ash.

Or she could sign both names. Hers beneath his, the way an apprentice signs beneath a master on a completed chart, which was honest, and which was also, she understood with a cold clarity that felt almost like fever, the only signature that could save anyone.

Because a lone dead man's map could be dismissed. Forged after death, planted, the fantasy of a loyalist faction — Reiner would say the words and the tribunal would nod, because tribunals are machines for converting doubt into verdicts. But a living witness, a commission transcriber, a clerk's own niece, standing in the hall and saying *I finished this plate, I verified these soundings, I will swear to every line* — that was not a document anymore. That was testimony. It put her body between the map and the machinery, and it put Aldous's face in the gallery where Reiner could see exactly whose family had done it.

Her uncle. She set down the pen and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Aldous, who had found her at nine years old standing in the doorway of an emptied house, holding a wooden horse and asking no questions because she had already learned that questions got children left behind too. Aldous, who had fed her, taught her letters on commission scrap paper, bought her the typewriter ribbon before he bought himself a winter coat. Aldous, who had typed an accusation against an innocent man because a captain told him to, and who had — she had seen it, in the way his hands moved when he thought no one watched — begun, in these last weeks, to understand the size of what he had helped build.

If she walked into that hall with the atlas, she did not save him. She condemned him alongside Reiner, unless he turned first, unless he chose, unless she gave him the smallest and cruelest gift of trusting him to be the man she needed him to be for one morning.

*I am sorry,* she said silently, to the dark, to him, to the boy she had been and the girl she was about to stop being. *I am sorry. Ink is a promise you cannot take back.*

She wrote the legend.

*Survey of the Town of Verenmoor and Its Stores, Oct.–Nov. 1918. Executed by E. Corvin, Master Cartographer. Completed by M. Galloway, Apprentice. Verified against customs house logs, garrison requisitions, and personal observation.*

Then, beneath, because Corvin had earned it and because she wanted whoever read it to feel it: the marginal note, expanded in her own smaller hand. *No destruction of stores occurred at Warehouse Three between 1 Oct. and 30 Nov. 1918. No chimney smoke observed on any night. Charges of theft against T. Vey are unsupported by geography.*

Unsupported by geography. Her master would have laughed at that, a short dry laugh, delighted. Only a mapmaker could have written such a sentence and meant it as proof.

She dusted the ink, blotted it, and held the plate up to the hooded lamp. The light came through the paper in soft gradations, the river luminous, the warehouses solid, the little annotations precise as stitches. It was beautiful. It was the most dangerous object in Verenmoor. She held it a moment longer than she needed to — letting herself feel, just once, the pure apprentice's joy of a finished thing well made — and then she took it apart.

Not the plate itself; the plate must survive intact, must be seen whole. But she wrapped it in the oilcloth Corvin had used for his field instruments, rolled it tight, and bound it with cord, and then she went down on her knees on the cold boards and lifted the loose plank and looked, one more time, at the rest of them. Forty-one plates. Coastlines and roads and granaries and, along the northern edge of the folder, in Corvin's hand, the resistance routes he had died for — the courier paths, the safe houses marked as innocuous buildings, the whole quiet nervous system of a town that refused. Evidence against her. Evidence for everyone. She had hidden them for two years the way you hide a heart under floorboards, and now she took the folder out and held it against her chest and felt it beat.

She could not bring them all. The tribunal would seize the folder entire if she brought it entire, and half those plates would become gallows-ink for people still breathing. So she made the choice her master had never had to make in full: she took Corvin's final plate, the stores survey, and beneath it — folded flat, hidden in the false bottom of her document case among blank commission forms — three supporting pages. The chimney observations. The barge timings. And the customs house log extract proving Tomas Vey's whereabouts on the theft dates, copied in Aldous's own clerk-hand, which would mean, when it surfaced, that Aldous had possessed exculpatory evidence and buried it.

That page was the knife she was putting in her uncle's ribs. She slid it in among the blanks and closed the case, and her hands were steady, and she hated how steady they were.

* * *

The walk across town took eleven minutes in daylight and twenty in the dark, picking her way around the ice-slicked cobbles past the shuttered bakery, past the printer's shop with its windows crossed by boards, past the cold cell block where a guard she had bribed twice now stood with his collar up and his eyes fixed carefully on the middle distance. She did not go to the cells this time. There was no time, and nothing left to say that Tomas had not already refused — *don't buy my life with theirs* — and she honored him best, tonight, by ignoring him completely. Some mercies had to be taken by force.

At the corner by the salt shed she stopped, once, and looked back at the town.

Verenmoor lay gray and sealed under the last dark, rooflines stacked against the river, chimney smoke standing up straight in the windless cold. Two hundred yards away the bridge showed its broken teeth against the water. She had spent her whole life in this town wanting out of it — wanting the capital, the great libraries of maps, the wide world that Corvin described in winter evenings like a man describing a lover. Even after his death, the wanting had stayed, transmuted into the atlas itself: finish it, send it out, and somehow leave with it, be carried out of Verenmoor inside your own work.

Now she stood in the dark holding the thing that could free an innocent man and destroy her only family, and she understood, with the terrible simplicity of decisions already made, that she had never once been leaving. She had been staying the whole time. Every plate she inked was a vow to this place. You do not make a true map of a town you mean to abandon.

The tribunal convened in the customs house, in the long room with the tall windows where the commission held its hearings. By the time she reached the steps, the east had gone the color of dirty pewter, and sledges were arriving, and soldiers stood at the doors checking papers with the elaborate boredom of men who know the outcome already. Word had run through Verenmoor overnight the way word does in sealed towns: the Vey trial, this morning, quick and final before the armistice made everything awkward. Half the town seemed to be pressing toward the doors — faces she had transcribed requisitions for all winter, the fishmonger's widow, the ferryman, old women with shawls over their heads, come to watch a riverman die.

Mirabel joined the press of them. Her document case hung at her side, heavier than any object she had ever carried. Inside, folded among blank forms, a dead man's last argument waited to speak.

At the door a lieutenant put out a hand for her papers. She gave him her commission transcription pass — *M. Galloway, transcriber, authorized presence at commission proceedings* — and he glanced at it, at her, at the case.

"Blank stock," she said, in the flat voice she used for clerks' errands. "Requisition forms for the record."

He waved her through. She felt the lie pass through him without catching, and understood that from this morning forward she would never again be invisible, and found — to her surprise — that she did not miss it.

The hall was cold enough that breath showed. At the front, behind the trestle table draped with the commission's gray cloth, sat the three officers of the tribunal, and at the center, immaculate, unhurried, wearing authority like a tailored coat, Captain Reiner. On the table before him lay documents in ordered stacks. Among them, she saw it at once — she could no more help seeing it than help seeing her own name — the exhibit: a typed letter, crisp, damning, and beside it a newer sheet, the confession, *signed Tomas Vey*, typed on a machine whose lowercase e sat crooked as a hanged man.

Odile was there, in the gallery, white to the lips, her father's arrest hanging over her like the blade it was. Aldous sat at the clerk's table to the side, ledger open, pen ready, doing his job, being furniture — and as Mirabel took her place among the townspeople his eyes came up, found her, and held. He looked at her document case. He looked at her face. Something moved through his expression like current under ice, and she realized he knew — perhaps not everything, perhaps not the pages in the false bottom, but enough. Enough to warn her, with one long look, to get up and walk out.

She did not walk out.

The tribunal called its first witness. The bailiff's voice rang off the tall windows. Somewhere beyond them, faintly, the river moved, carrying ice toward the sea, and Mirabel Galloway sat very still with her hands on her case and waited for the moment — Reiner would produce the confession soon, soon — when she would rise from among the shawled and the silent, cross the cold width of that room, and lay her master's map upon the table where everyone could see it.

The captain's voice, smooth as oiled leather: "The prosecution produces, as its final exhibit—"

Mirabel stood up.
