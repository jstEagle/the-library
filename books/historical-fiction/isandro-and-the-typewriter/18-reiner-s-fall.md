## 18. Reiner's Fall

The tribunal convened in the customs house at nine o'clock in the morning, in the long room where grain tariffs had once been argued by men who never imagined the town would be sealed like a jar. The windows were rimmed with frost that no one had scraped away; light came through them gray and thick as river water, falling across the trestle tables in bars. Captain Reiner sat behind the tallest table with his hands folded, wearing his best coat, the one with the brass buttons he polished himself. He looked, Mirabel thought from her seat at the back, like a man attending the wedding of someone he disliked.

She had not slept. Her fingers still smelled of ink — she had washed them three times and the smell would not go, as if the night's work had soaked through the skin into something deeper. Beneath her coat, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, lay the final map of her master's atlas: Verenmoor, its bridge, its warehouses, its hidden stores, the movement of commission supplies traced month by month in her master's careful hand and finished in hers. It was the truest thing in the room, and it was going to save a man's life or end several others, and there was no longer any way to tell which.

Aldous sat two rows ahead of her. He did not turn around. He wore his clerk's collar straightened to the last millimeter, and his ledger lay on his knees, closed, like a Bible at a funeral. Last night he had stood in her doorway with his hat in his hands and said only, *If you do this, I will stand when they ask me to stand.* She had not known until that moment how much a sentence of eleven words could weigh.

Tomas Vey was brought in between two gendarmes. Three weeks in the cold cell had carved him down to angles; his coat hung on him the way laundry hangs in winter, stiff and shapeless. But when his eyes found Mirabel across the room, something in his face eased, and he inclined his head a fraction — a bow, or thanks, or simply the acknowledgment that she had come, that he was not alone in a room full of enemies.

Reiner opened the proceedings himself. "The accused, Tomas Vey, stands charged with theft of commission stores and with conveying intelligence to enemy partisans. The evidence is before you." He lifted the typed letter — *the* letter, the exhibit, the document that had put Tomas in the cell — and held it up with the satisfaction of a man holding a winning card he had already counted twice.

Mirabel rose.

"Captain," she said, and her voice surprised her by being steady, "I am the transcriptionist of record for the occupying commission. I request leave to examine the exhibit."

Reiner's eyes narrowed. For a moment the frost on the windows seemed to thicken, the whole room to hold its breath inside the ice. Then he shrugged — a small, careless gesture, the shrug of a man who believed the game already won. "The clerk may approach."

*The clerk.* He meant Aldous. But Mirabel was already walking forward, oilcloth bundle under her arm, and Aldous rose beside her so that they arrived at the table together, uncle and niece standing shoulder to shoulder for the first time since her parents vanished, and perhaps understanding, both of them at once, what it meant that they were standing there now.

* * *

Tomas had expected the cell to be the end of things, and had made his peace with that somewhere around the ninth day, when the water bucket froze solid and he stopped being able to feel his toes. A man can carry a map across a frozen river with bullets singing past his ears, but there is no heroism in freezing quietly in a stone room while a war decides your town's business without you. What he had not expected — what kept him upright through the nights — was the memory of the old cartographer's face in those last minutes by the river, pressing the rolled map into his hands: *Take it across. Tell them the atlas is not finished. Tell my apprentice—*

He had never learned what to tell her. The sentence had been cut off by the first shot, and Tomas had carried the unfinished clause across the ice along with the map, and it had weighed more than the paper ever did.

So when the gendarmes marched him into the customs house and he saw Mirabel Galloway rise from the back row with something bundled against her chest the way one carries an infant, or a wound, he understood before anyone spoke that the unfinished sentence had finally found its ending, and that she was delivering it herself.

The girl walked to the front of the room like a woman walking a plank she had chosen. The clerk went with her. And Captain Reiner — Tomas watched him carefully, because he had spent years learning to read officers — watched her come with the particular stillness of a man whose arithmetic has just gone wrong.

* * *

Odile had never intended to testify. Testimony was what got fathers arrested; she knew the shape of that door from the inside. She stood at the back beside the stove, her father's apron folded in her pocket like a talisman, and told herself she had come only to see it done.

Then Mirabel laid the typed letter on the table, set a magnifying lens over it, and turned the exhibit toward the tribunal members with her voice pitched clear as a bell across cold air.

"You will observe the letter 'e' in every instance. The lower bowl is misaligned — it sits a half-millimeter high, and strikes too hard on the left. This is the commission typewriter's defect. I have typed four hundred documents on it; I know its faults as one knows a stammer." She drew a second sheet from the oilcloth — a page typed fresh that morning, each line a duplicate of lines from the exhibit. "Here are sample lines I produced yesterday on the commission machine. Compare them."

The tribunal members bent over the pages like priests over scripture. One of them, a grizzled major with a glass eye that never quite tracked, held the lens to the light and moved it slowly, twice, three times.

"They do not match," the major said at last.

"No," Mirabel said. "They do not. The exhibit was not typed here. Note the even impression, the centered 'e,' the slightly worn serif on the 'r' — these belong to another machine entirely. An older one. A *Rhenia* model, discontinued in 1913." She paused, and Odile saw her gather herself the way one gathers breath before diving. "There is such a machine in Verenmoor. It belonged to Master Corvin Halle, the cartographer, executed in March of this year. His typewriter disappeared at his arrest. The exhibit letter, Captain, was typed on a dead man's machine — by whoever has kept it for eight months. And I would ask the tribunal who that might be, and why a document accusing Tomas Vey was manufactured rather than written."

The silence that followed had texture. Odile could feel it press against her skin.

Reiner laughed. It was a good laugh, practiced, the laugh of a man who has laughed down subordinates for years. "The transcriptionist is imaginative. Grief does strange things to the young. Her master was a traitor; perhaps loyalty to a traitor has—"

"Then let the machine be found," Mirabel said. "Or let its owner speak."

And Odile discovered that her feet had carried her forward three steps into the open floor, and that her mouth was already opening, and that some door in her that had been locked since her father's arrest had simply come off its hinges.

"I can speak to that," she heard herself say.

* * *

Aldous had rehearsed his part a hundred times in the dark: rise when asked, state what the ledger showed, accept whatever followed. It was a clean plan, and like all clean plans it dissolved the moment it touched the world — because Odile Marchetti stepped forward, and then another man from the warehouse rows stood up to confirm the requisition discrepancies, and then Mirabel unwrapped the oilcloth on the tribunal table and unrolled the map, and Aldous understood that his small, orderly confession was about to be trampled by a stampede of truths he had only partially owned.

The map. God help him, the map. He had seen Corvin Halle's plates a hundred times in the map room and thought them an old man's vanity — rivers and roads, elevations and bridge loadings. He had not known that the same patient hand had recorded every wagon of commission stores diverted to the warehouse on Tanner's Lane, every date, every signature — including, on the last plate, in ink still sharp, the initials *H.R.* beside a column of missing coal and flour that reconciled to nothing in any ledger but the captain's own.

Reiner saw it too. Aldous watched the color leave the man's face in stages, like a tide going out — first the confusion, then the calculation, then the terrible arithmetic of a cornered officer counting the exits and finding the doors bricked up.

"The ledger," Aldous said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he made himself go on. "I am the commission's clerk. I have audited the stores at Captain Reiner's instruction and found the records altered to conceal diversion. The thefts laid against Tomas Vey correspond, item for item, to entries the captain ordered me to... adjust." He set the ledger on the table beside the map, opened it to the marked pages, and stood there with his hands empty, feeling lighter than he had any right to feel at the moment of his own destruction.

"You old fool," Reiner said softly. "You'll hang beside me."

"Perhaps," Aldous said. "But I will hang for what I did, Captain. You will fall for what you did. There is a difference, even at the gallows. Especially there."

It was at that precise moment — Aldous would remember this forever, the absurd timing of it — that the bells began.

* * *

Tomas heard them first as a mistake. Bells rang for fires, for floods, for Sunday — and this was none of those. All the bells, all at once, the church of Saint Aubin and the chapel across the water and even the little cracked bell on the customs house itself, all of them loosed together into the frozen morning like birds from a netted cage.

The doors banged open. A gendarme stood in them, hatless, his rifle dangling forgotten from its strap, his face wearing an expression Tomas had not seen on an occupying soldier in three years and had nearly forgotten existed.

"It's signed," the man said, half sobbing, half laughing, entirely undone. "The armistice. It's signed. The war is over. It's *over.*"

For a heartbeat the tribunal room simply refused to believe him. Then the room broke — not into chaos but into something stranger, grown men weeping openly at the tariff tables, the major with the glass eye removing his cap, someone in the back beginning a hymn and abandoned halfway through the first line because no one remembered the words, everyone only remembering that there had been words, once, sung together.

In the middle of it all stood Captain Reiner, and no one embraced him, and no one congratulated him, and the armistice that forgave nations did not reach him where he stood. The tribunal major, restored to himself by procedure the way drowning men are restored by driftwood, rapped the table for order and pronounced, amid the bells, the findings: the exhibit forged, the charges fabricated, the thefts substantiated. Tomas Vey was released with the tribunal's apology, read aloud in a voice that shook. Captain Reiner was taken into custody pending transport to the capital for trial before the commission's superior court — stripped of his buttons, almost gently, by his own sergeant, who did not look at him while he did it.

And Aldous Nystrom, clerk of the commission, confessed accessory after the fact and turned evidence, was sentenced to transport — not the gallows, the major said, because the court weighed cooperation and because, the major added, glancing at Mirabel, *the truth had been delivered to us by this man's own hand*. Transport to the capital. Years, perhaps. Perhaps fewer. A bill, presented and accepted, and Aldous bowed his head to it the way he bowed to everything, precisely, without complaint.

Tomas walked out of the customs house free, and found that freedom felt exactly like cold air and bell-noise and Mirabel Galloway standing on the steps with the empty oilcloth under her arm, looking not triumphant but hollowed, the way a vessel looks when the fire has done its work and the clay holds.

"We won," he said to her.

She looked at the bells swinging madly in their towers, at the strangers embracing in the square, at her uncle coming down the steps behind them with his ledger under his arm and his eyes red and his spine straight as a surveyor's line.

"Ask me again tomorrow," she said. "Today I can't tell the difference."

* * *

Odile stood in the square until her feet went numb, watching the town perform joy. It struck her, somewhere in the third hour of bells, that joy was a performance here, that grief had worn the costume so long that happiness had to borrow its gestures — the embraces too tight, the laughter arriving a beat late, like an echo unsure of its cue. Her brother was still hidden under the printer's floor. The barge went at dawn. None of this had changed because bells were ringing; the arithmetic of one berth and two lives sat exactly where the war had left it, untouched by the armistice, waiting for someone to solve it.

She found Mirabel at the edge of the square, apart from the crowd, holding the oilcloth as though it still contained something.

"My brother," Odile said, without preamble, because the day had burned away her talent for circling. "And your uncle. And one berth. And dawn." She swallowed. "I promised Reiner a trade and he took my typewriter and gave nothing. So now it's only us, and the arithmetic hasn't changed."

Mirabel was quiet for a long moment. The bells fell away around them, or perhaps the two of them fell away from the bells.

"No," Mirabel said at last. "It hasn't changed. But I've done my sums." And she looked up the street toward the docks, where the coal barge lay black against the gray water, its single passenger cabin already lit against the coming night — and Odile saw in her face something settled and terrible and tender, a decision already made, waiting only for the morning to be spoken aloud.

"Dawn, then," Odile said.

"Dawn," Mirabel agreed, and turned toward the map room, where the atlas waited to be crated for its journey — and toward whatever it was she meant to send on that journey in place of herself.
