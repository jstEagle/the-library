## 20. Armistice

Tomas heard the bells before he understood them.

He was in the coal yard behind the commission depot, stacking crates that no longer needed stacking, when the sound came rolling up from the river — not the single cracked bell of the town hall that rang for curfew and executions and other varieties of official grief, but all the bells at once. The church of Saint Aldric, whose bell had been silent since the second winter of the occupation. The chapel on the marsh road. Even the small tarnished thing above the fish market that had rung only for fires. They were ringing together, out of rhythm, ragged and overlapping, the way a crowd laughs or weeps.

He stood with a crate in his arms and let the sound pass through him like cold water.

Then the yard boy came sprinting through the gate, hatless, his voice already ruined from shouting. "It's signed! It's signed! The eleventh hour, they said — it's done!" And he ran on toward the square, and Tomas set the crate down very carefully on the stack, as if it were something living that might be startled, and walked out into the street.

* * *

Verenmoor in November was a town made of mud. The autumn rains had chewed the cobbles loose, the carts had churned them deeper, and now every street was a brown riverbed that took your boots to the ankle and kept a claim on them. Tomas had crossed half the occupied territories in four years and had never known mud like Verenmoor's. It had character. It held grudges.

The square was filling faster than he could have believed the town held people. Fishwives still in their aprons. Commission clerks with their collars undone, their armbands torn off and lying in the gutters like shed skins. Old men who hadn't left their doorsteps since the bridge closed were out on the cobbles in slippers, faces turned up to the bells. A woman was kissing a total stranger, a barge hand, both of them laughing so hard the kiss failed repeatedly and had to be attempted again. Two soldiers of the occupying garrison stood at the fountain's edge, unsure whether they were men or uniforms today, until an old woman — Tomas recognized her, the one who sold chestnuts outside the prison, who had never once looked at him in all his weeks of coming and going — pressed a paper cone of roasted chestnuts into each of their hands and said something he couldn't hear, and one of them began to cry.

Tomas moved through it the way he had learned to move through everything: quietly, at the edge, reading exits.

Four years of habit did not dissolve at the sound of bells. He had walked out of a tribunal three days ago a free man, and still his body did not believe it. When a stranger clapped him on the shoulder — a dockworker, shouting something joyful about the eleventh hour — Tomas's hand went to where the weight of the cell's silence still sat against his ribs, and he had to make himself smile instead of flinch.

Free. The word was a room he hadn't yet found the furniture for.

He found the edge of the square, near the apothecary's awning, and stood there with his hands in his pockets, watching. This was what he knew how to do: watch. He had watched troop trains for her master. He had watched the commission's stores dwindle while Captain Reiner's private shipments grew. He had carried the old cartographer's final map across the frozen river with it sewn into his coat lining, and he had watched, from the far bank, the smoke of the prison where that same cartographer was shot for the crime of drawing the truth too well.

And he had come back. That was the part he still could not explain cleanly, even to himself. Not for the map — the map was safe. Not even, in the end, for the atlas, though he had told himself that was the reason on the long road back: find the plates before the commission does, finish the dead man's work, keep faith. All true. But underneath the reasons there had been something simpler, something the cell had stripped everything else away to reveal: he had nowhere else that he was *from*, and one place where he had once been *needed*, and he had chosen the need.

"Tomas."

She was coming across the square toward him, and the crowd seemed to open for her without knowing it did — Mirabel Galloway, the mapmaker's apprentice, ink-stained to the second knuckle as always, wearing her master's too-large coat against the cold. She had ink at the corner of her jaw where she'd pushed her hair back with wet fingers. He had noticed that three days ago across the tribunal floor, when she had stood up with the map that broke Reiner, and her hands had not shaken, and he had thought: *there is more courage in that one pair of hands than in the whole garrison.*

"They're saying the barges start again within the week," she said, stopping beside him under the awning. Her breath fogged. "The bridge first. Engineer teams from the capital. They'll want a survey of the spans before anyone crosses."

"Will they?" he said. "A survey?"

"There's only one person in this town who's surveyed those spans," she said. "Me. My master started it. I intend to finish it." She looked out at the square, at the strangers embracing, at the chestnut seller and the weeping soldier. "It's strange," she said. "I've been waiting for this day the way you wait for a thaw. And now it's here, I keep thinking I should feel lighter."

"Do you?"

"Ask me tomorrow." She glanced at him. "Where will you be tomorrow?"

The question landed where all his questions landed now: on the open account of his life. Because there was a barge coming — the next one, within the week, the first free boat of the new age — and a man with no country worth the name, no family remaining above ground, no trade but carrying things secretly across rivers, could board it and be somewhere south by the following dusk. Somewhere the war was a story told by others. Somewhere no one would look at his hands and see a courier, a prisoner, a fugitive twice over.

"I don't know," he said, which was true, and then, because the bells were still ringing and lies felt heavier than usual, "South. Away. That was always the plan."

"The plan," Mirabel said, "was made by a man who thought he was going to die in that cell."

* * *

They ended up walking, because neither of them wanted to stand still and neither wanted to join the dancing in the square, which had begun around the fountain with a fiddle produced from somewhere and played badly and enthusiastically. The mud pulled at their boots. The rain had stopped an hour before the bells and the whole town smelled of wet stone and coal smoke and, improbably, of the chestnut carts doing a roaring trade along the marsh road.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" Tomas said, as they came out onto the levee with the river below them, brown and swollen, carrying branches and ice and the debris of four years toward the sea. "The night I carried the last map across. Your master told me — the last thing he ever said to me — he said, 'The river doesn't care whose side you're on, it only cares whether you can read it.'" He looked down at the water. "I've been reading rivers my whole life. And I never once asked myself what I'd do if there wasn't a war telling me which bank to run to."

"And now there isn't."

"Now there isn't." He turned his palms up, empty. "I'm a courier, Mirabel. That's all I've been since I was seventeen. Someone hands me something precious and tells me where to take it, and I take it, and I don't ask what happens after, because asking is how you get killed. Now nobody's handing me anything. There's nothing to carry." He heard the shape of it as it left him — the confession of a man whose usefulness had expired — and hated it even as he said it.

Mirabel was quiet for a long moment. Below them the river worked at its pilings. Out on the water, the hulk of the closed bridge stood black against the gray sky, its mid-span broken since the retreat two years ago, its chain strung with pennants today because some children had decorated it, absurdly, for the armistice — rags of red and white fluttering off the iron like the world's poorest celebration.

"Can I tell you what I see," she said, "when I look at you? Since you won't say it yourself." She didn't wait for permission. "I see a man who came back into a sealed town where he was already hunted, for a dead man's atlas. I see a man who sat in Reiner's cell for weeks and gave up nothing. I see a man who asked me *not* to save him, because saving him would have cost other lives. That's not a courier. That's just what the war made him wear." She looked at the bridge. "The war's over. You're allowed to find out what's underneath."

"And if there's nothing underneath?"

"Then you build something," she said simply, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world, as if she herself had not spent the entire winter learning exactly that lesson, plank by plank, plate by plate. "That's what building is, Tomas. It's what you do when there's nothing underneath yet."

* * *

That evening the whole town crowded into Saint Aldric's for a service of thanksgiving, and Tomas stood at the back among the strangers and the standing-room and did not sing. He watched Mirabel halfway down the nave beside Odile, the printer's daughter, whose brother lurked near the pillar in a borrowed coat, still not quite safe, still needing to stay hidden until the tribunals finished their work. He watched Aldous Galloway sit alone near the front, upright and gray, a man sentenced to transport who would sail on the first barge with the atlas under his arm — the courier at last, taking the precious thing to the capital, doing the one useful act left to him. Mirabel had not forgiven him. Tomas doubted she ever fully would. But she had put the atlas in his lap with her own hands, and there are kinds of love that look exactly like duty from a distance, and only like love up close.

Afterward, in the churchyard, in the blue dark with the bells finally silent and the stars sharp and cold overhead, Odile found him.

"There's a berth on the first barge," she said, without preamble. "Word went round tonight. Passengers, finally. South, then connections anywhere." She studied him with her father's frank printer's eyes, eyes that read people the way her press read type. "Half the town's talking about leaving. Starting over. You must have had that berth in your pocket since the tribunal."

"It crossed my mind," Tomas said.

"And?"

He looked past her, through the churchyard gate, to where Mirabel stood under the bare linden with a lantern, showing something to the fish-market children — a folded sheet, he realized; a map, a rough sketch of the town, and she was tracing streets with her finger while they crowded in, wide-eyed, as though she were showing them treasure.

"I've spent four years crossing bridges," Tomas said. "Burning them behind me, mostly. I don't think I've ever stayed on one side long enough to learn its streets." He looked back at Odile. "There's work here. The bridge needs rebuilding — real rebuilding, years of it. Somebody has to carry the timber accounts past the profiteers this time, and somebody has to know every smuggler's path on that river so the town never gets sealed like a bottle again. Somebody has to stand in the square and remember what the cell was like, so it never happens here again, not to anyone." He heard himself and almost laughed. "Listen to me. I'm making speeches. Your brother rubbed off on me."

Odile smiled slowly. "You could go south," she said, kindly, giving him the door one last time. "Nobody in Verenmoor would blame you. Nobody owes you anything here."

"That's the point," Tomas said. "Nobody owes me anything. Which means whatever I am here, I'll have chosen it." He blew out a breath, watching it cloud. "Belonging that's given to you — family, birthplace, all of it — you can lose it and it isn't your fault. But belonging you choose—" He shook his head. "You have to keep choosing it. Every day. That's harder. That's also mine."

Odile put her hand briefly on his arm. "Then welcome to Verenmoor," she said. "Fair warning: the mud is permanent."

"So I've heard."

* * *

He found her at the levee an hour later, because some instinct — new, unpracticed — told him where she would be. She was standing at the rail above the broken span with her collar up, looking at the black water, and when his boots crunched on the frozen gravel she didn't turn, only said, "I thought you'd be packing."

"I thought about it."

"And?"

Tomas came to stand beside her at the rail. Below them the river ran, indifferent and eternal, and beyond it the bridge stood broken in the dark, its torn span reaching toward the far bank like a sentence that had been cut off mid-word.

"I'm staying," he said.

She turned then. In the dark he couldn't read her face properly, only the catch of her breath, the way her hands went still on the rail.

"The bridge first," he said. "The engineer teams will need someone who knows both banks, every ford, every smuggler's landing, every place the ice jams in February. Then the survey — your survey. I'm told the mapmaker's apprentice needs someone to hold the measuring line on the far side, and I happen to be very good at being on the far side." He paused. "And after that, I don't know. That's the whole point. I'd like to find out what it's like to not know and not be afraid of it."

"Tomas—"

"You don't have to say anything," he said quickly. "I'm not staying *for* — that is, I'm staying for the bridge. For the work. For—"

"Tomas." Mirabel was smiling, the small crooked smile he had seen perhaps four times all winter and hoarded every one. "You're rambling like a man reading a document he hasn't finished transcribing."

"I'm out of practice," he said. "With honesty."

"Then practice." She turned back to the river, and her shoulder came to rest against his, light as falling snow, deliberate as a signature. "Because if you're going to help rebuild that bridge, you should know it'll take years. And I should warn you — I finish what I start. Ask anyone."

Out on the water, the current carried the last of the ice toward the sea, and somewhere in the town a single bell — the little cracked thing above the fish market — rang once, twice, as if testing whether peace could really be rung on it, and then fell quiet, satisfied.

"Years," Tomas said, and found that the word, for the first time in his life, sounded like a promise instead of a sentence.

Above them on the levee path, a lantern bobbed — Odile, coming to find them, a rolled sheet of fresh proofs under her arm, moving fast. Even at that distance Tomas could see the paper trembling, and hear the words she was already calling down into the dark:

"Mirabel — you'll want to see this — the atlas proofs came off the press tonight — and there's a letter with them. From the capital. It's about the atlas — and about who's asking for the author."
