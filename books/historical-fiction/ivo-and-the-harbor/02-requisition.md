## 2. Requisition

They came at the turn of the tide, which was how Sebastian knew they had consulted a chart.

No one local would have chosen that hour. The turn of the tide was when the harbor worked—when every fisherman still afloat was hauling in against the ebb, every hand on the quay busy with lines and crates and the small urgent mathematics of the morning catch. If you wanted to land soldiers without an audience, you came then. If you wanted to land them *unremarked*, you came then. Captain Rennick's transport had ridden in on the flood with its guns run out and its rails lined with men who did not wave, and it had come to anchor exactly where the deepwater channel began, precisely, elegantly, at the one berth in Vellamare that could take her draft.

Sebastian stood in the maproom window with the last plate drying under their hands and watched the pinnace drop from the ship's side, and thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water: *he reads water the way I do.*

There were eight men in the pinnace, and the man at the tiller wore his coat differently from the rest—not better, but more deliberately, the way a man wears a thing he has decided other people should notice. He came up the quay steps without hurrying while his sergeant bellowed the fishermen aside, and he stopped at the head of the stairs and looked out over the harbor for a long moment, turning his head slowly, as if comparing what he saw to something he carried.

Then he looked up—at the windows of the maproom, directly, without searching—and Sebastian stepped back from the glass before they understood they had done it.

* * *

By noon the town crier had read the proclamation from the customs house steps, twice, in a voice flattened by repetition: by order of the Provisional Authority, the port of Vellamare and all its works, records, vessels, and revenues were received into the keeping of the Army of the Coast. All ledgers, registers, charts, correspondence, and writings of every kind held by any inhabitant, guild, office, or trust were to be surrendered for inventory within three days. The penalty for concealment was not named. It did not need to be.

Sebastian listened from the back of the crowd the way they had learned to stand in crowds twenty years ago—shoulders down, face empty, positioned so that no one standing behind them could see anything worth describing later. Around them the town made the sound a town makes when it is deciding whether to be afraid: a low murmur with laughter caught inside it, half-swallowed jokes about soldiers and paperwork, the defiant economics of people telling each other this would pass.

Old Tomas the rope-maker said, "Records. What army wants a rope-maker's records?"

"Every army," said his daughter, not laughing at all.

Sebastian walked home the long way, along the harbor wall, counting. The transport's boats ran without pause all afternoon: officers to the customs house, clerks to the harbormaster's office, a squad to the church where the parish registers lived in an iron chest that three generations of sextons had pretended was locked. They worked in a rhythm Sebastian recognized because it was the rhythm of surveying—establish your stations first, then run your traverses, then fill in the detail. By evening the army had occupied the customs house, the harbormaster's office, the parish chest, the guildhall archives, and the two taverns with sightlines to the mole.

They had not come to the shop.

That was wrong, Sebastian knew. That was the worst of it. A requisition that missed the mapmaker's shop was not an oversight; it was a list, and somewhere on that list the shop sat in its place, waiting its turn, which meant someone had written the list knowing what belonged where—which meant someone had described Vellamare to Rennick before Rennick ever raised the coast.

Master Aldous had taught them to fear incomplete maps. *The danger is never in what's drawn,* he used to say, tapping the table with his pipe stem. *It's in what's drawn wrong on purpose.*

* * *

They came the second morning, at the hour Sebastian had always privately called the ink hour—the gray interval before full light when the lamp on the workbench still mattered and the harbor below was only a rumor of sound. A knock, measured and civil. Two soldiers on the step, and behind them the officer from the pinnace, hat under his arm like a caller.

"Master Oyelaran," he said.

"Apprentice," Sebastian said. "The master is dead."

"Yes." He said it the way a man confirms a column of figures. "Captain Rennick's compliments. The shop's holdings are to be inventoried. You'll admit us, or we'll admit ourselves; the captain prefers the former, and so, I think, will you."

He introduced himself only as Lieutenant Fess. He was younger than his manner, mid-thirties perhaps, with the careful hands of a man who had been a clerk before he had been anything else, and he entered the shop the way no one entered the shop—as if it were already his. He walked the length of the room once, reading the shelves the way Sebastian read a coastline, and Sebastian followed two steps behind and performed the oldest skill they owned, which was being uninteresting.

It was a performance built on true foundations, which was why it held. The shop really was mostly a shop: trade charts of coasts from here to the Corvane Strait, harbor plans sold to shipping offices, school maps, a drawer of obsolete city plans nobody had bought in a decade. Fess went through it all with two fingers, lifting, glancing, setting down. One soldier carried crates. Another stood by the door with the particular stillness of a man posted to watch the person, not the room.

"The atlas," Fess said, halfway through the second shelf. "The harbor atlas. The great folio. Where is it kept?"

"On that bench." Sebastian did not have to pretend the pause. "Under the weights."

Fess crossed the room and lifted the linen cover the way a priest lifts cloth off something that might be a relic or might be bread, and there lay the atlas—twenty years of Sebastian's life open under lamplight, the finished plates gleaming faintly, the unfinished ones ghostly in their pencil underdrawing. The final plate, the one drying when the transport anchored, lay under its sheet of blotting paper, and Sebastian's heart performed a slow, deliberate act of treachery by beating faster as Fess's hand approached it.

But Fess was not interested in the plates. He turned the folio's front board, checked the spine, riffled the guard of stubbed binding edges at the back where volumes had been removed, and nodded, once, to himself.

"And the companion volumes," he said. "Aldous Oyelaran kept working notebooks. Day-books. The harbor surveys exist in fair copy—" he touched the atlas's edge without reverence, "—which means they exist in rough somewhere. Field books. Name indexes. Where are they?"

"They were confiscated," Sebastian said. "Twenty years ago. At the arrest."

Something moved behind Fess's eyes—not surprise; confirmation. He wrote nothing down, which frightened Sebastian more than writing would have. "You were the apprentice at the arrest."

"I was."

"You saw the books taken."

"I saw everything taken. They took the counterweights off the press." It was true. It was all true, and Sebastian let the truth carry the weight, because truth is the best timber for a lie to be built from. "I rebuilt the trade from what wasn't worth taking."

Fess looked around the shop again, slower now, and Sebastian understood that the slowness itself was the question. *Where does a dead man keep his papers?* The room answered him with solvents and blotters, with the smell of gall and gum arabic, with thirty years of another man's habits preserved like a fly in amber because the apprentice had never been able to bear changing anything. Fess saw a shop that mourned its master. Sebastian prayed he saw nothing else.

The false wall was nine feet from where Fess stood.

Behind Aldous's memorial portrait—the charcoal likeness the town had subscribed for after the execution, so that the shop might hang the face of a traitor honestly, since honesty was cheaper than defiance—there was a cavity framed in the old stud wall, plastered to match, holding four wrapped bundles and a locked drawer of papers that had survived twenty years by being part of the grief. No one searches inside mourning. Fess's gaze crossed the portrait twice and did not pause, and Sebastian stood with their hands loose at their sides and their pulse hammering in their throat and thought, absurdly, of Ilse Marchetti at twelve years old, swearing over the unfinished atlas with her hand flat on the cover like a small sworn official of some republic of two.

"We'll take the atlas for inventory," Fess said at last. "Receipted. You'll have it back when the survey of holdings is complete." He produced a printed form, filled it in against the bench with a neat hand: *one folio volume, harbor atlas, incomplete.* His pen hovered. "Incomplete," he repeated. "How long has it been incomplete?"

"Twenty years," Sebastian said. "Since they killed the man who could finish it."

Fess looked up at that—looked properly, for the first time, at Sebastian's face, and Sebastian gave him back a mild, tired, truthful expression, because it was true: no one could finish it but the one who had, secretly, plate by plate, year by year, in the hours after the lamps of Vellamare went out.

"Captain Rennick will want to speak with you eventually," Fess said, wrapping the atlas himself, refusing help, folding the linen cover with unnecessary precision. "About the master's methods. About what he was working on at the end." He tied the string with a clerk's knot. "You'll make yourself available."

"I've made myself available for twenty years," Sebastian said. "I'm easy to find. I'm always here."

"Yes," said Fess, and for a moment the careful clerk's face showed something almost like sympathy. "So the record indicates."

* * *

They took more than the atlas.

Through the rest of that day and into the next, the army's inventory swallowed Vellamare's paper the way the tide swallows a sandcastle: not violently, not all at once, but completely, and with the same terrible smoothness. Sebastian watched from the shop and from the quay and from the high window, playing the unremarkable apprentice, cataloguing what left and—more carefully—what did not.

The customs ledgers went, forty years of them, crated and carried down to a lighter that ferried the boxes out to the transport. The harbormaster's tonnage books went. The pilots' examination records went. The guild rolls, the tide tables' original manuscripts, the church registers, the correspondence files of every merchant house too frightened or too prudent to object—all of it flowed down the quay steps into army crates stenciled in white, and soldiers with lists checked each crate against each list, and the lists themselves went into a leather case that never left Lieutenant Fess's hand.

On the third day they came for the shop's remaining stock—all of it, trade charts and school maps and the drawer of obsolete city plans, receipted and crated, until the shelves stood bare as ribcage. Sebastian signed each receipt in a steady hand. *Dread disguised as compliance* was a discipline, and the discipline was in the hand: a frightened man's signature trembles; a guilty man's hurries; so Sebastian wrote slowly, evenly, like a bored tradesman signing away goods he could reorder.

The crates went down to the quay in a drizzle. Sebastian walked behind them at a respectful distance, one more shopkeeper seeing his livelihood onto the boat, and stood at the rail of the harbor steps as the lighter pulled out.

That was when the arithmetic finished itself.

The companion volumes were gone. Not seized—gone. Sebastian had told Fess the truth: the army had taken Aldous's field books and day-books twenty years ago, at the arrest, and Sebastian had seen them go into evidence chests and had assumed, for two decades, that they sat in some provincial archive gathering the slow dust of forgotten cases. But Fess had not written anything down. Fess had known to ask. And now the whole machinery of the requisition had rolled through town collecting every scrap of writing Vellamare possessed except the one category it had already collected elsewhere, long ago, and had come back to collect again: everything Aldous Oyelaran had ever put his hand to.

Which meant the purge had woken up. Which meant somewhere in some sealed room, the old evidence chests had been opened, and someone had found the day-books, and found the day-books *cut*—Sebastian had cut them, or rather Aldous had, with Sebastian holding the lamp, the night before the arrest, razor and rule, excising every name from every page with the care of a surgeon and the haste of the damned—and someone had decided that the missing names were a problem worth solving.

And there was only one place those names existed now. Only one place in the world where the erased families of Vellamare still had faces and streets and burial grounds and the little facts of their lives: in the final plates of the atlas, which Sebastian had spent twenty years finishing in secret, and which had just been carried down these very steps in a string-tied crate, receipted, inventoried, *incomplete*, into the hands of an army that had hired clerks to ask precisely the right questions.

The lighter reached the transport. A davit groaned; the first crate swung out over the rail and vanished into the hold.

Sebastian stood on the wet steps with the rain coming fine and gray off the sea, and did the only arithmetic that mattered. Somewhere in that hold, in a crate marked with white stencil, lay the atlas's body. Somewhere behind a false wall nine feet from where Lieutenant Fess had stood, lay its soul—the wrapped bundles, the locked drawer, the plates with the names. Twenty years of silence had rested on one assumption: that no one alive cared anymore. The assumption was dead. The army had come to collect Vellamare's memory entire, and it would not stop at memory; memory pointed at people, and people were the cargo the whole expedition had been fitted out to find.

A gull screamed once over the masthead and was answered by nothing.

Sebastian turned and climbed the steps slowly, a shopkeeper going home to an emptied shop, and at the top paused—just a moment, the pause of a man taking a last look at his trade—to watch the next crate swing aboard.

Then they went home, unlocked the door with a steady hand, and stood in the doorway looking at the memorial portrait on the far wall, at the mild charcoal face of a hanged man who had trusted one person in the world enough to hand them a razor and say *hold the light steady.*

"Hold it steady," Sebastian said aloud, to the empty room, and went to check the false wall's plaster for cracks.
