## 3. What the Ledgers Say

Captain Edran Rennick did not read the ledgers on the first night.

He let them sit.

The crates had come up from the mapmaker's shop in the late afternoon, six of them, each one stenciled with the customs seal of Vellamare and carried by soldiers who handled them like ammunition. His quartermaster, a precise man named Voss, had inventoried them twice and reported: forty-one bound volumes, eleven loose portfolios, one sealed tin of instruments, and a quantity of paper stock sufficient for a decade of survey work. Voss had asked where the captain wanted them stored. Rennick told him the requisitioned customs hall, second floor, the room with the good lock and no windows facing the water.

Then he had gone to supper, eaten half a plate of fish that tasted of the harbor it came from, slept four hours without dreaming, and risen before the gulls.

This was his method, learned across nineteen years of service in places where memory was contraband: never touch a thing the day you take it. Let it sit overnight like meat after slaughter. Whatever is alive in it will settle, and whatever is dead in it will begin to smell.

At the fourth bell after midnight, with a single lamp lit and Voss asleep down the hall, Rennick unlocked the room and began to read.

* * *

The ledgers were beautiful. He had expected that, but expectation did not blunt the effect.

Aldous Oyelaran had kept accounts the way other men kept prayers. Every volume was ruled in a fine hand, margins exact as gunnery. Soundings recorded to the finger-width. Tidal observations logged against lunar tables so consistent they might have been copied from the moon itself. Marginal notes in two hands—one old and confident, one young and careful—annotating everything from the migration of eel grass to the shifting of a sandbar off the north mole over what must have been fifteen unbroken years.

The apprentice's hand, Rennick thought, studying it. Neat, disciplined, and deliberately plain. A hand that had been taught not to draw attention to itself.

He turned pages steadily, making no marks of his own, letting the ledgers tell him what kind of man had made them. Oyelaran had been thorough to the point of obsession and honest to the point of danger: there were entries here that contradicted the crown surveys, corrections to charts that naval offices three hundred miles away had been printing wrong for a generation. The harbor's depths, its hidden channels, the seasonal behavior of its currents—all of it laid down with the patience of a man building something meant to outlast him.

And then, at intervals, the gaps.

Rennick found the first one ninety minutes in. A ledger dated twenty-two years back, a section headed *Registry of Harbor Licenses*, and beneath the heading—a rectangle cut out with a razor or a very thin knife. Clean edges. Not torn, not gouged. Excised the way a surgeon excises, taking exactly what was needed and nothing more.

He set that volume aside and kept reading, and now he found them everywhere once he knew to look. Cut rectangles in the license registers. Cut strips from the crew manifests of the fishing fleet. An entire signature page removed from the record of a property transfer, leaving only the witnesses' names, both of which proved on cross-reference to belong to men dead within two years of signing. In the oldest volumes the cuts were fewer; in the volumes covering the years of the purge, they multiplied like holes in cloth eaten through.

Rennick did the arithmetic as he went, because arithmetic was what he trusted when all else was theater.

Forty-one volumes. Ninety-three excisions that he could find without better light. Perhaps thirty more that a careless search would miss. The cuts clustered around certain dates—the purge years, the resettlement years, the years when Vellamare had been ordered to forget that certain families had ever paid harbor dues, owned quay rights, buried their dead in the town cemetery under their own names.

Every cut fell where a name should be.

Sometimes a name alone. Sometimes a name with an address, a berth number, a debt owed. Aldous had not simply mutilated his records; he had performed a kind of negative cartography, mapping the erased by the shapes of the holes left behind. Anyone reading these ledgers honestly would learn nothing. But anyone reading them the way Rennick was reading them would learn something far more dangerous: that the town's memory had not faded naturally. It had been harvested. Collected. Carried somewhere by a man with steady hands and twenty years to hide it.

He closed the last volume near dawn and sat for a while in the gray light, listening to the harbor wake below the window—hooves on wet cobbles, the first gulls arguing, a winch somewhere groaning into motion.

Voss appeared at seven with coffee, took in the stacks of sorted ledgers, and said, "Damage?"

"Excisions," Rennick said. "Systematic. Predating the arrest."

"Stolen, then."

"No." Rennick wrapped both hands around the cup, which was too hot, and held it anyway. "Preserved. There's a difference, and the difference matters. Stolen documents leave. Preserved documents wait for someone to come back for them."

"You think someone will."

"I think," Rennick said, "that a man spends twenty years cutting names out of ledgers, and the army seizes those ledgers within a week of my arrival, and the same morning the man's apprentice opens the shop as though nothing has happened. I think about what that apprentice is waiting for." He drank. The coffee was terrible. Everything in this town tasted of salt. "Have the garrison carpenter build me shelving. Cataloguing begins tomorrow. And send a runner to the shop—politely—to inform the mapmaker's apprentice that I'll want assistance identifying the volumes. Their expertise, freely offered. Nothing coercive."

Voss hesitated. "You expect them to help us read what the master hid."

"I expect them to refuse convincingly," Rennick said, "and I expect to learn a great deal from how they do it. That's the trouble with silence, Voss. It's a language. Everyone speaks it with an accent."

* * *

He spent the middle of the day walking the town, because a captain who stays among his papers learns only what the papers say, and papers in Vellamare had been edited for twenty years.

The requisition was holding. That surprised him; he had expected more friction. Proclamations were posted at the market cross, the customs house, the chapel door. The garrison had billeted itself in the disused barracks above the fish yards without incident. The harbormaster, a heavy man named Corvo with a face like a dropped loaf, had surrendered the tide logs and gate keys with an obedience so complete it circled around to suspicious—though when Rennick mentioned the name Oyelaran, Corvo's hands developed a sudden interest in the ledger in front of him, straightening it, squaring it against the desk edge, aligning it with things already aligned.

"You knew him," Rennick said.

"Everyone knew him." Corvo's voice was flat as a becalmed sea. "Good mapmaker. Quiet man."

"Quiet men are the ones who hear everything. What did Aldous Oyelaran hear, Master Corvo?"

"Heard the tide, mostly." Corvo looked up then, and for one unguarded moment there was something in his face Rennick had seen a hundred times in a hundred towns: not defiance, not fear, but the specific exhaustion of a man carrying something heavy that he could neither put down nor show anyone. "If there's nothing else, Captain. The morning boats won't log themselves."

They logged themselves, of course. Corvo logged them. But Rennick let him go, because a man like Corvo was a door, and doors were best approached slowly, from the side, after you had learned which way they swung.

He walked the quays instead. Watched the fish markets open. Counted faces—noting who met the red coats with curiosity, who with indifference, who with the studied blankness of people performing the absence of an opinion. He stopped at the chandlery, bought rope he didn't need, and listened to the owner talk about weather for six minutes without once saying anything. He stood outside the mapmaker's shop for a full minute, unannounced, watching through the glass.

The apprentice was at the counter, inking something. Sebastian—that was the name in the employment records, just *Sebastian*, surname blank, origin blank, entered twenty years ago as if the paperwork itself had agreed to look away. Rennick had read that blank line three times last night and found it more eloquent than anything in the ledgers. A town does not leave a blank on a form unless the whole town has conspired to hold the pen loosely.

Through the window, the apprentice worked with the same plain, disciplined hand Rennick had seen in the ledgers' margins. No glancing toward the door. No stillness of guilt. Just work, steady as a clock, in a shop that had every reason to be shut.

That, too, was an accent.

* * *

Back at the customs hall, he laid out the excised ledgers on the long table in a sequence only he understood, arranged not chronologically but geographically—the harbor districts the missing names had lived in, reconstructed from tax rolls, parish fragments, and a cargo manifest from the purge year that some clerk in the capital had failed to sanitize.

By lamplight he built the shape of the hole.

Ninety-three excisions resolved, under patient cross-referencing, into something like thirty households. Fishing families. Boatwrights. A chandler. Two pilots. Names that appeared nowhere in the current town because they had been made to disappear, but which surfaced here and there in distant archives like bodies rising briefly in warm water: Marchetti. Ferren. Ossolin. A dozen others, fragmentary.

One name recurred in his reconstruction more than any other, attached to a house on the drowned lane, a berth in the inner basin, and a girl of six listed in a parish register that had survived by accident in the capital: *Marchetti, Ilse. b. —*. The birth date itself had been inked out by a different hand than Aldous's. Someone had gotten to the parish records too. The town had been editing itself for decades, Rennick thought, long before the army arrived to do it professionally.

And the intelligence reports said a woman using that name had landed in the western ports two months ago, traveling under papers that fooled nobody worth fooling.

He wrote the connection down, considered it, and did not act on it. Not yet. Acting on it today would flush the bird before he knew the size of the covey. If the Marchetti daughter had come home, she had come home for what Aldous had preserved—and she would go to the one person in Vellamare who had spent twenty years keeping it.

Everything in this town funneled toward the maproom above the harbor. Rennick simply needed to keep the funnel intact and watch what poured through it.

He drafted orders in his flat, unhurried script. House searches, beginning in ten days, beginning in the harbor district—but scheduled loosely, dates vague, so that anticipation would do his work for him and the nervous would start moving, and movement would reveal them. A census of harbor workers, framed as administrative routine. A public notice offering clemency for unregistered persons, worded gently enough to be refused and precisely enough to make refusal a confession.

Then, at the bottom of the day's page, he wrote the item that mattered most:

*Apprentice. Summon for consultation re: cataloguing. Courteous. Extended. Observe: sleep, appetite, hands.*

He looked at the sentence a long moment, then added a final line to his private notebook, where the real record lived:

*Ninety-three holes in the paper. One person in this town who knows what was cut out and where it went. They will lie to me beautifully. I will enjoy it, and then I will take the truth from them the way their master took the names—cleanly, with a thin blade, along lines already drawn.*

Rennick blew out the lamp and stood at the dark window a while, looking down at the harbor, where the tide was turning and the channels below the green water were rearranging themselves according to rules written down in a stolen atlas by a dead man who had outthought him once already.

In the morning, he would send the runner.

He hoped, in a professional way, that the apprentice would prove worthy of the master. It made the work better, when they were.
