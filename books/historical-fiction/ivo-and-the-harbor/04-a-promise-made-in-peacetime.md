## 4. A Promise Made in Peacetime

The rain came in off the water that morning the way it always did in Vellamare—sideways, patient, as if it had all the time in the world to wear the town down to its bones. Sebastian Oyelaran stood at the long window of the maproom and watched it silver the harbor, and for a while did nothing at all.

Twenty years of doing nothing at all, in this room, at this window. Twenty years of watching weather instead of making it.

Below, the shop was open because it was Tuesday, and on Tuesdays the shop was open. That was the whole of the reason now. Once there had been customers: captains wanting fair charts of the shoals off Gull's Teeth, merchants wanting their warehouses drawn into the tax rolls as larger than they were, children sent with coppers to buy a printed map of the known world so they could pin their fingers to places they'd never see. Once there had been Master Aldous at the drafting table by the north light, sleeves bound back with cord, saying *ink is a promise, boy—the moment you set it down you owe someone the truth of it.*

Once. The word sat in Sebastian's chest like a stone worn smooth by handling.

They still came, occasionally—a fisherman needing a chart mended, a widow wanting her husband's plot in the churchyard measured for a headstone—and Sebastian served them with the old courtesy, took their coin, gave them change. But the truth was that the shop had become a shell around a single purpose, the way a whelk's shell outlives the animal that made it. Everything Sebastian did now was done for the atlas.

And the atlas was finished. Had been finished, plate by plate, coast by coast, sound by sound, for six days now.

Sebastian turned from the window and looked at it where it lay closed on the great table, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, tied with the same kind of cord Aldous had used to bind his sleeves. Six days of not opening it. Six days of finding small tasks—the grinding of ink that needed no grinding, the straightening of pens already straight—to avoid the hour when the last page would be turned and there would be nothing left between Sebastian and the question that had waited twenty years like a debt collector on a doorstep:

*Now what do you owe?*

* * *

The memory came the way memories come in Vellamare—on the tide, unasked for.

It was summer, the summer Sebastian was twelve, and the maproom smelled of beeswax and cut vellum and the salt that got into everything in this town, that got into the grain of the wood and the hair and the lungs, so that people who left Vellamare said they could never quite wash the harbor out of themselves. Ilse Marchetti had climbed the outside stair two steps at a time the way she did everything, burst through the door without knocking because knocking was for strangers, and stopped dead.

She had seen the atlas before—everyone in the shop's orbit had seen it, the great half-made thing that occupied the table like a sleeping animal—but she had never seen it open to the last plates. Aldous was out; he'd gone up the coast road to survey the cliff paths and wouldn't be back till dark, and he had left strict instructions that no one touch the atlas, which meant, in the grammar of twelve-year-olds, that it must be touched immediately.

"It's not finished," Sebastian said, hovering, hands behind their back like a small official. "You can't look at unfinished work."

"Who says?"

"He says."

Ilse leaned over the table anyway, braids swinging, and studied the final pages—the ones Aldous kept covered even from customers, the ones with the coastal soundings that went deeper than any sounding had a right to go, past the harbor's honest depths into something else. Even at twelve, Sebastian understood there were two atlases living inside the one book: the one the world would see, and the one stitched beneath it, page under page, name under name.

"What are these?" Ilse asked, tapping a column of small careful letters along a margin. Not place names. Not depths.

"I don't know," Sebastian said, which was true then, and both of them knew it was a lie, which was also true then.

Ilse looked up. She had a way of looking at people that made them feel surveyed, charted, fixed upon some future map. "When it's done," she said, "whoever finishes it has to finish it for both of us. You and me. Because I'm going to be gone by then—I'm going to see all of it, every coast in this book—and when I come back, you'll have to show me. All of it. Even the parts nobody's supposed to look at."

"That's a stupid promise."

"It's not stupid, it's binding." She spat in her palm, having learned the gesture from her father's deckhands, and held it out. "Swear it."

Sebastian looked at the offered hand, at the atlas beneath it, at the harbor beyond the window going gold with late sun. Twelve years old, and the purge was four years past and spoken of in the town only in the grammar of weather—*before the bad years, after the bad years*—and neither of them understood yet what the margin names were, whose they were, what had been paid to keep them written down. Neither of them knew that Aldous checked the street twice before climbing his own stairs, or why the shop's shutters had new iron bars, or why the master sometimes stood at this very window at dusk counting boats with an expression Sebastian would only learn to read much later, and at terrible cost.

Twelve years old, and the worst thing that had ever happened was that Ilse was leaving in the autumn, her family bound for the capital, her father's fishing berth sold.

Sebastian spat in their palm and shook.

"There," Ilse said, satisfied, wiping her hand on her skirt. "Now you're stuck with me forever."

*Forever*, it turned out, was nine years. Then the war came, and the Marchetti name became a thing said quietly or not at all, and the last letter Sebastian ever received from her arrived in a hand grown unfamiliar, formal, the handwriting of someone writing across a border rather than across a garden wall. *I hope the atlas goes well. I hope you are well. Your friend, I.* Not even the full name. As if the name itself had become contraband.

Sebastian had kept the letter. It lived in the same drawer as the chalk stub and the spare nibs and the flat tin of Aldous's last tobacco, things too heavy to use and too sacred to throw away.

* * *

Present-day Sebastian pulled the drawer open now, without quite deciding to, and looked at the letter a long moment before closing it again.

Outside, the rain had thickened. A navy cutter stood in the roads where no navy cutter had stood in years, gray hull riding at anchor with the patience of a thing that intended to stay. It had been there three days. Three days of soldiers ashore measuring streets, counting doors, asking the harbormaster questions in the courteous voice of men who had not yet been given permission to stop being courteous. Requisition, the papers said. The army required the town's records. Its ledgers. Its lists.

Its memory, though the papers didn't say that part. The papers never said the true part aloud. That was the whole art of them.

Sebastian had watched the first crates go yesterday—Aldous's shipping manifests, the customs duplicates, the parish rolls—carried down the outside stair on soldiers' shoulders and loaded onto a wagon under canvas. Watched from this window with the stillness of a man who had spent twenty years learning to be furniture. And felt something turn over in the pit of the stomach, slow and cold, like a body rolling in deep water.

Because everyone in Vellamare knew what the army wanted with records. Records were how you found people. And the people the army would be looking for—the families scattered by the purge, the names that survived nowhere but in Aldous's hidden margins—those people had spent two decades becoming invisible. Borrowed surnames. Moved households. Children raised never to ask about grandmothers. The whole town had conspired in a silence so complete that most of the town had forgotten it was a conspiracy.

But the ledgers. The ledgers were the skeleton key. Somewhere in those confiscated volumes were the old entries, the pre-purge entries, the tax rolls and berth registries and baptismal records that named the families as they had been named before the erasure. Cross those against the present, and the borrowed names would peel away like wet paint.

Unless the ledgers had gaps.

Sebastian crossed to the table and untied the cord, and opened the atlas at last to the final plates, and looked down at what Aldous had died protecting—not the maps themselves, which were beautiful but innocent, but the margins, the interlinear script, the second atlas living inside the first. Names. Forty-one families. True names, old names, the names the purge had tried to make into no-names. And beside each name, in Aldous's tiny cipher, a location: the loft above the chandler's, the farm behind the salt marsh, the boat registered under another man's name. Hiding places. Twenty years old now, most of them stale, some of the families scattered again or dead or simply dissolved into the general sea of the town—but written down, all the same. Proof. Testimony. The thing the army would burn the town to find.

Aldous had cut the corresponding pages out of the official ledgers before they took him. Sebastian knew this because Aldous had told him, once, in the last week, in the flat calm voice of a man distributing his estate: *the ledgers will be seized, boy, they seize everything, so the ledgers must say nothing. What I cut out lives here.* He had tapped the atlas then, the way Ilse had tapped it at twelve, and Sebastian had not understood until later that the tap was a bequest.

So let Rennick—yes, Sebastian knew the captain's name now; the whole town knew it within a day, the way towns know—let Captain Rennick read his seized ledgers from cover to cover. He would find maps of grain shipments and mooring fees and forty years of weather. He would find holes where pages had been razored out with a craftsman's care, and if he was clever—and soldiers who reached captain in these times were clever—he would understand that the missing pages were the point, and that somewhere in Vellamare was a person who knew where they'd gone.

And there was exactly one such person. There had only ever been one.

Sebastian closed the atlas and retied the cord, and their hands were steady, which surprised them. Twenty years of practice. Fear, like ink, could be trained to hold a line.

* * *

In the afternoon the rain slackened and the light came low and coppery through the breaking cloud, and Sebastian went down to the shop floor to close up, and found the harbormaster's boy waiting with a folded paper.

"For the mapmaker," the boy said. "From the garrison. He says to say it's a courtesy."

Everything from the garrison was a courtesy now. Sebastian unfolded the paper. Fine hand, military brevity: *Captain Rennick requests the attendance of the mapmaker's apprentice at the customs house, Thursday next, regarding the records of the late Aldous Oyelaran. No cause for concern.*

No cause for concern. Four words that were themselves a kind of map, and every line of them led somewhere dark.

Sebastian thanked the boy, gave him a copper because that was what one did, and watched him run off through the puddles. Then Sebastian stood in the doorway of the shop a while, looking out at the harbor coming alive with lamplight, at the cutter's riding lights green and red on the black water, at the town that had kept its silence for twenty years and was about to learn whether the silence had saved anything or only postponed everything.

Thursday. Three days. Enough time to decide what to hide, what to show, what to lie about. Sebastian had lied to customs men and tax assessors and once, memorably, to a colonel of the old regime, and had never once been caught, because the best lie is mostly true: *yes, Captain, I was his apprentice; yes, I continued his work; no, Captain, I've never seen any pages cut from any ledger; the master was meticulous to a fault.* Twenty years of practice. The net was drawing in, but nets have meshes, and meshes have measurements, and measurement was the one trade Sebastian had ever mastered.

It was while standing there, working the problem like a chart, that the other thought surfaced—the one underneath, the one that had been surfacing for six days now, every time the atlas was opened, every time the drawer with the letter was pulled.

*Whoever finishes it has to finish it for both of us.*

The promise had seemed like a child's game once. Now it stood revealed as what it had been all along: a contract, signed in spit and sealed in summer light, and contracts come due. Ilse Marchetti was somewhere across the water—alive, the last rumor said, though rumors across borders were worth less than ballast—and the promise said that when the atlas was done, Sebastian owed her the whole of it. Every page. Even the parts nobody was supposed to look at. *Especially* those.

And the army, arriving with its courtesies and its crates, had just made itself the collector on the same debt. They wanted the names. Ilse, if she still lived, might want them more—for her mother's name was surely among the forty-one, written in Aldous's careful hand in a margin, existing nowhere else on earth. Two parties, then, converging on one book: one that would use the names to finish the purge, and one that—if the promise meant anything—had a claim older than any requisition paper.

Sebastian went back upstairs, lit the lamp, and sat down at the table with the atlas between their hands.

Three days. If the promise was a debt, then it was time to start paying it—and payment, Sebastian understood suddenly, clearly, with the cold clarity of a sounding line touching bottom, would not be made in this room. The names could not stay here, waiting under a memorial portrait for the knock of soldiers. They had to move. Which meant channels, and tides, and the routes only Aldous had ever charted—which meant, before Thursday, before the summons, before the courteous trap closed around the maproom, Sebastian would have to walk the harbor at low tide and remember what the master had taught them about water that hides.

The lamp guttered. Out in the roads, the cutter's bell rang the first watch, thin and clear over the rain-washed water.

Sebastian blew out the light and began, in the dark, to plan.
