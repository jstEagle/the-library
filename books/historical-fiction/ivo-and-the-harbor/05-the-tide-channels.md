## 5. The Tide Channels

Sebastian had learned the tide channels the way other children learned prayers—by repetition, in the dark, under threat.

Aldous Oyelaran had taken them out at first light and last light, when the harbor dropped its skirts and showed its bones. *Learn what the water hides, apprentice,* he would say, standing in the bow of the leaky skiff he refused to replace, his bad leg braced against the gunwale. *Charts tell you where the land is. They never tell you where it isn't.* Sebastian had been nine, ten, eleven, cold to the wrists, holding the sounding line while the master called depths in a voice like gravel poured slowly. Twelve years of soundings. Twelve years of watching Aldous pencil figures into a separate book—a slim one, oilcloth-bound—that never appeared on any shelf, that lived inside the master's coat, against his heart, where the soldiers found nothing when they took him.

The army had seized ledgers. Ledgers were paper. The tide kept its own accounts, and Sebastian had spent twenty years keeping the water's.

Now, three days after the crates went down the quay into Captain Rennick's custody, Sebastian walked out onto the flats at low water to see whether the old knowledge still held.

Dawn came gray and salt-stained, the sky the color of unprimed linen. The ebb had pulled back past the mooring stakes, and Vellamare's harbor lay exposed like a patient turned for examination: the mudflats shining, the oyster beds rank and glistening, and between them, threading dark through the brown-gold, the channels. The Pilgrim's Cut, which looked like nothing from the seawall but ran four fathoms at flood, deep enough to bring a boat of consequence past every watch-post in town unseen if you entered on the right slake of tide. The Serpent, which doubled back on itself twice and killed a customs pinnace in Aldous's boyhood, so the story went, and had been quietly left off every official chart since. And the Narrows below the customs house itself—the joke Aldous had never quite stopped enjoying—that a boat could lie under the customs house windows, close enough to hear the excise men snoring, in six feet of water, and no chart in the crown's possession would tell you it was there.

Sebastian walked the flats with boots off, trousers rolled, a sounding pole over one shoulder, looking exactly like what the town expected: the mapmaker's odd apprentice, grown strange and solitary, measuring mud. Nobody watched a person doing that. That was the beauty of it. Twenty years of being invisible had taught Sebastian that invisibility was not a cloak but a trade, practiced daily, perfected in small transactions—the bowed head in the market, the mumbled answer about tide tables, the deliberate dullness offered to anyone who looked too long.

But the survey this morning was not for any chart. It was reconnaissance.

*Begin,* Sebastian thought, planting the pole at the mouth of the Pilgrim's Cut, *where he ended.*

The channel had shifted, as they always did. A winter's storm two years back had pushed a bar of shingle across the outer third, and the cut now entered farther east than the master's secret book recorded—Sebastian knew the book by heart, every figure, having copied it once from memory onto nothing, having burned even the memory-copy's memory, in a manner of speaking, folding it into the place where names lived. Four feet seven inches here, at an hour past low water slack. Five feet at the bend where the old mooring chain lay buried—still there; the pole rang against it. Six feet and running hard at the second bend, where the current scoured a hole the locals called the Drowned Man's Chair.

It would do. At half-flood, entering with the tide, a shallow-draught boat could come up the Cut in the dark, hug the eastern bank where the pilings of the ruined herring shed threw a shadow the lamplight never reached, and pass within thirty yards of the garrison's new sentry post—which was, precisely because it was a sentry post, watching the sea and not the ground beneath it. Soldiers read water the way Rennick read ledgers: for what was written. The channels were what was unwritten.

Sebastian worked east along the Serpent, wading the shallows, sounding the bends, and found the second truth confirmed: the channel still connected to the fish-market culvert, and the culvert still ran beneath the customs warehouse, dry at three-quarters ebb, wide enough for a man to walk upright for most of its length if he didn't mind the smell and the rats. Twenty years ago Aldous had shown them that culvert and made them swear never to speak of it, and Sebastian had sworn, and had kept the oath so completely that sometimes, in the shop at night, it seemed the oath was the only thing left of the master that hadn't been confiscated.

People or papers. The thought arrived with the clarity of the morning light hardening over the water. Both. The channels could carry both.

And then the second thought, arriving slower, heavier, the way cold water finds its way through clothing: *for whom?*

Because Ilse Marchetti had come back—or the rumor of her had, moving through the harbor the way such rumors moved, in the pauses of conversation, in the way certain fishermen suddenly needed to check their lines when soldiers passed. Because the families were still here, or some of them, folded into borrowed names for twenty years, and the army had their ledgers now, and Rennick had eyes that missed nothing, and somewhere out in that gray water was whatever remained of the truth, waiting for someone to decide it was worth drowning for.

Sebastian stood in the middle of the flats with the pole planted and let the fear come, because it always came, and the only way through it was to let it pass like weather. Twenty years ago, Aldous had gone to the scaffold rather than give up a set of names, and everyone in Vellamare had learned the lesson written in his death: silence is survival. Sebastian had built a life on that lesson the way you build a house on pilings—carefully, deliberately, knowing the water was underneath everything.

But houses on pilings still flood. And promises made in peacetime do not expire; they wait.

*Whoever finishes it finishes it for both of us.*

Twelve years old, Ilse's finger on the unfinished plate, ink on her chin. Sebastian could still feel that moment the way you feel a scar in cold weather.

The tide had turned. Sebastian felt it before seeing it—the faint hesitation in the water pooling around their ankles, then the slow reversal, the harbor beginning to fill like a lung. Time to go. A person standing on the flats at flood tide got noticed, drowned, or both.

Sebastian took one more bearing—the entrance to the Narrows, directly below the customs house—and that was when the light changed.

Not the sun; the sun stayed behind cloud. Something on the customs house door.

Sebastian waded ashore, wrung out the hem of the coat, and walked up the ramp with the unhurried gait of a man with wet boots and no opinions, taking the long way along the seawall, passing the customs house the way one passes any building one has passed ten thousand times.

The door was old, black oak, iron-banded, and it had been repainted—army gray, badly—over the royal blue of the customs service. On the gray, low, near the hinge where a casual eye would slide past it, someone had drawn a mark in white chalk.

A circle. Broken at the top. With a single stroke drawn downward through the gap, like a keel, like a plumb line, like a name being sounded into deep water.

Sebastian did not stop walking. Did not turn their head. But every nerve in their body woke at once, the way the harbor wakes when the wind swings suddenly north—everything the same, everything changed.

It was old code. Older than the purge, older than the atlas. Herring-boat code, the chalk marks fishermen used before most of them could read, scratched on doorposts and bollards: *safe landing here*, *price of mackerel*, *skipper owes me drink*, *keep away*. Aldous had collected the old signs the way he collected everything forgotten, and he had taught them to his apprentice as a curiosity, a game, and then—one night, after the arrests began—he had taught them again, differently, without the smile.

The broken circle with the plumb stroke had meant one thing only, in the master's revised lexicon. Not a fishing sign. An invented one.

*I am here who was believed gone. Tell me how to reach you.*

Freshly drawn. The chalk's edge was still crisp; rain had fallen in the night, heavy until the second bell, and the mark must have gone up after—within hours. Someone had stood at this door in the dark, under the noses of the garrison, and written *I am here who was believed gone*, and walked away, and no one had seen, because no one in Vellamare saw anything anymore except what they were afraid of.

Except, apparently, whoever held the chalk.

Sebastian walked on to the end of the seawall and stood looking out at the roads as if checking the weather, which was even true, and thought: *This is a trap. Of course it is a trap. Rennick knows the ledgers have gaps; a man like that will know the gaps have a language. He'll put a mark on a door and wait to see whose face changes.*

That was the sensible reading. The sensible reading said: walk home, close the shutters, be invisible another twenty years, let the dead keep their own names.

But the chalk was wrong for a soldier's trap. Sebastian had studied it for half a heartbeat and the study was instinct now: the stroke was drawn bottom-to-top, the way left-handed people pulled a line, the way Ilse had always pulled hers—Ilse, who had broken her left wrist at eleven falling from the herring shed, whose writing had never straightened out afterward. And the mark was placed low, near the hinge, at the height and angle of a person standing casually at the door as if waiting for it to open—not the placement of a guard drawing bait, which would be centered, visible, proud.

Small evidence. Evidence that would hang you in a courtroom and mean nothing anywhere else. But the heart does not weigh evidence; the heart recognizes handwriting.

Sebastian turned from the seawall and walked home through the waking streets, past the bakery where the ovens had just been lit, past two soldiers leaning on the fountain who did not look up, past the shuttered shop of the sailmaker who had once, twenty years ago, sewn oilcloth covers for a slim secret book, and whose shutters, Sebastian now noticed for the first time in years, hung loose by a finger's width.

The maproom smelled of ink and rain when Sebastian climbed to it. Below, the harbor was filling, the channels swallowing themselves back into secrecy one by one—the Pilgrim's Cut going dark, the Serpent closing, the Narrows sliding under the customs house windows as though none of it had ever been exposed, as though knowledge of it existed nowhere outside a dead man's coat and a living apprentice's skull.

Sebastian sat down at the workbench and did not touch the atlas. Instead, they took a stub of chalk from the drawer—the same soft French chalk Aldous had used for blocking out plates—and held it, and understood that the day of choosing had arrived not with trumpets but with a piece of chalk on a gray-painted door.

Tonight, at full dark, Sebastian would walk past the customs house again. If the mark remained, it was a trap and would be ignored, and the silence would close over everything like water. If the mark was gone—if someone had returned to collect an answer—

Then Sebastian would add a second mark beside where it had stood. A circle, whole. A horizon.

*Answer received. I see you.*

Out in the roads, the fog was lifting off the water in long rags, and the tide crept up the flats, erasing Sebastian's footprints one by one, patient as everything else in Vellamare that served two masters.

* * *

Night came early that time of year, and it came raining again, fine and persistent, the kind of rain that emptied streets and filled gutters. By the second bell the town was dark except for the garrison lanterns at the customs house—one at the door, one at the corner, both hissing in the wet.

Sebastian went out at the change of tide, when sentries grow drowsiest, wearing a fisherman's oilskin and carrying a lantern with a shuttered flame, the universal license of a man going to check his boat. The route to the customs house ran along the seawall, and the seawall ran empty, and the rain fell on the harbor with a sound like static, like the sea whispering behind its hand.

The lantern at the door was manned by a young soldier with his collar up, stamping his feet, looking at the rain and not at the wall beside him. Sebastian passed within ten feet of him, nodded the nod of the incurious, and saw—in the half-second the passing allowed—a smudge of pale dust on the gray paint near the hinge, and the ghost of a wet thumbprint dragged across it.

Gone. Collected.

Sebastian did not stop. Stopping was for people with something to decide. The decision had been made somewhere between the flats and the seawall, made perhaps twenty years ago and only now collected, like the chalk. Sebastian walked on to the dark stretch past the fish market, set down the lantern, took out the chalk, and waited for the next patrol to pass and fade.

Then, quickly, in the rain, at the corner of the fish market wall where the customs house door could be seen from it—answer posted where the questioner would look—Sebastian drew a circle, whole, unbroken, and beneath it a short horizontal stroke. Old code, revised lexicon, the master's voice rising clear through twenty years of silence:

*Seen. Midnight tomorrow. Come by water.*

Below the fish market, the Narrows lay black and smooth, six feet deep, unwritten on every chart in the crown's possession. A boat could lie there tonight. A boat could lie there tomorrow night.

Sebastian shuttered the lantern and walked home through the rain, heart hammering not with fear, for the first time in twenty years, but with something older and more dangerous—the feeling of a chart's first bearing laid down on clean paper, when the course is set and the coast is still unknown.

Behind them, at the corner of the fish market, the rain had already begun to soften the chalk.

By midnight it would be gone. By midnight, if the harbor was what Sebastian had always believed it to be—full of the drowned and the hidden and the patiently waiting—it would already have been read.

And if it had been read by the wrong eyes, then the net was drawing closed around them both, and there was no longer any chart back to the safety of silence.

Sebastian climbed the stairs to the maproom, lit the lamp, and unlocked the drawer where the master's oilcloth key-chain lay coiled like something sleeping. Tomorrow, the tide would turn at half past eleven.

It was time to find out who else remembered how to read the water.
