## 6. Enemy Waters

The boat that carried Ilse Marchetti home was called the *Mercy of Sorrano*, and it smelled of nine days of mackerel, and she loved it more than she had ever loved any house she had lived in.

She stood at the rail in the gray hour before dawn, wrapped in a fishwife's shawl with her hair bound up in a kerch and her hands—her traitor, elegant, foreign-educated hands—stained to the wrist with fish blood so that no one would look twice at their softness. The manifest below declared her *Lise Corver*, widow, of no particular importance, returning to her sister's household with a crate of needles and a debt unpaid. The manifest was good work. She had paid too much for it. She had paid too much for everything on this voyage, because everything made abroad for the purpose of coming home had to be made by strangers who could not afford to know her.

Ahead, Vellamare sat against the dark like a mouthful of lost teeth.

She had forgotten—or had let herself forget—how the harbor light stuttered. Not turned: stuttered, a rhythm broken in the middle, because the old lamp's gearing had been damaged in some storm twenty years ago and never properly mended, so that every fourth beat came late, like a heart skipping. As a child she had fallen asleep to that rhythm thinking all the lights of the world did it. In Marvosa the harbor beacons were steady as arithmetic. She had lain awake in rented rooms listening for the skip and hating herself for missing it.

*Every fourth beat comes late,* she told herself now, watching it. *You are almost home.*

Almost. The word had teeth.

Nine days at sea, and before that four years of accumulated distance, and before that the night she had left—which she did not let herself think about at sea, because the sea gave you nothing to do with grief but pour it overboard, and she needed all of it, had carried all of it across two borders and an ocean like ballast, because it was the only thing keeping her pointed toward Vellamare instead of away from it.

The fishermen worked around her without speaking. That was the arrangement: her fare paid partly in coin, partly in her hands gutting catch, and entirely in silence. Old Tomas, who owned the boat, knew what she was—not her name, but her category, which in these waters amounted to the same thing—and had taken her aboard anyway, because his own brother had been on one of the lists once, back when lists were still being read aloud, and men like Tomas repaid debts across decades without ever naming them.

"You'll want to go down when we make the mole," he'd said the first night, spitting over the side. "Harbor's got soldiers in it now. Requisitioned, they're calling it."

"I heard."

"Heard where?"

"Where everyone hears things." She had smiled, and he had not smiled back, and that had been the end of the conversation, and she had understood from his face that he had decided to bring her anyway.

Now the mole came up out of the dark, black stone slicked with spray, and beyond it the town rose in its crooked tiers, and Ilse counted chimneys the way other returning exiles counted stars. There. The Marchetti roofline—no, not Marchetti, nothing was Marchetti anymore, the house had been sold within the year of the purge to a rope merchant whose name she refused even in her thoughts to learn. There, the customs house, squat and blind. There, the steep lane that switchbacked up to the maproom window she had once known better than the window of her own bed.

And there—her stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like something adjusting itself in deep water—the garrison flag above the harbor office, new-hoisted, hanging dead in the windless morning.

Soldiers on the quay. Lanterns. Crates.

*They've taken the ledgers already.* She had known it would happen; she had come home knowing it. Knowing a thing and watching it happen were different animals, and the second one bit.

* *

They came aboard at first light with the boredom of men doing routine work, and Ilse learned in the space of ten heartbeats exactly how much of her four years abroad had been spent learning this: how to be looked at by power and be found empty.

The soldier flipped through the manifest. "Corver."

"Lise Corver." Her voice had changed over the years—roughened by sea-languages, flattened of the town's lilt. She let the roughness do its work.

"Sister in town?"

"By the salt yards. I'm expected."

"Expected" was a beautiful word, she had found. It implied a whole architecture of witnesses without naming a single one. The soldier glanced at her hands—fish-stained, cracked at the cuticles—and then at her eyes, and here came the dangerous half-second, the moment when a person stopped seeing the shawl and started seeing the posture underneath it. She dropped her gaze to the manifest and let her shoulders round. Four years of practice said: you are nobody's question, only somebody's answer, and the answer is tedious.

He waved her through.

She stepped onto Vellamare soil carrying one crate, one bundle, and a name she had crossed an ocean to find, and the name was not her own.

That was the thing she could never explain to the safe people in Marvosa cafés who told her she was brave to have survived, brave to have rebuilt. They thought exile meant missing your mother the way anyone misses the dead. But her mother had been taken from her twice. Once by the purge itself, when the soldiers came in the spring of Ilse's twelfth year and the town discovered, in the space of a single week, that it had never known any Marchettis at all—only people wearing the name temporarily, removable, erasable. And once afterward, slowly, by silence: because when she went looking, abroad, among archivists and smuggled papers and old men paid to remember, she found that her mother had been unwritten so thoroughly that even the records of her destruction were gone. Trial registers: blank. Burial rolls: blank. The purge had not merely killed; it had unmade the killing, folded the crime closed like a map, and put the map away.

Ilse Marchetti the daughter existed nowhere in any document on earth.

Except one.

* *

Aldous Oyelaran had died for those plates, and she had known it even at twelve, in the wordless way children know things they are never told. She remembered the execution less as an event than as a weather change over the whole town—a pressure, a hush, shutters closing along the harbor road like eyes. She remembered Sebastian that same season, thin and watchful at the edge of things, suddenly an orphan of a different kind, inheriting not just grief but a job: finish the atlas. She remembered standing with Sebastian in the maproom while the light went amber through the windows, and the promise—

No. Later. The promise was a live coal and she would carry it carefully or not at all.

What mattered was this: somewhere in Vellamare, in whatever hiding place Aldous had built to outlast his own death, existed plates bearing the true names of the erased families. Names of the dead restored to the record. Places they had hidden. Perhaps—she allowed herself the hope only in daylight hours, never after dark, never alone—the names of survivors. People who had gone into the earth of the coastline and stayed there, breathing under borrowed identities for twenty years.

Her mother's name might exist in two places in the world: in Aldous's stolen plates, if they still existed; and in the mouths of whoever had survived to speak it.

So her work in Vellamare was twofold, and she laid it out in her mind the way she had watched engineers lay out survey lines in Marvosa: run one line from the paper, one line from the living, and trust them to cross.

The paper line belonged to Sebastian. She did not yet know if Sebastian still lived in the town, still kept the shop, still—after twenty years of surviving by silence—remembered a promise made by two children over an unfinished atlas. Exile taught you that the people you left behind were ghosts until proven otherwise. She would find out. But the finding had to be slow. A stranger asking after the mapmaker's apprentice, in a garrisoned town, three weeks after the army seized the ledgers? She might as well walk into the customs hall and announce herself.

Which left, for now, the living line. The taverns. The fish markets. The salt yards. Twenty years of borrowed names, and somewhere in that sediment, people who flinched at their own birthdays, who never answered to anything spoken too loudly, who had habits of sitting facing doors.

She would know them. She had grown up among people who became them overnight.

* *

Her "sister's" household by the salt yards consisted of one ancient woman named Petra who took one look at Ilse in the doorway and said, "God help us, you have her chin," and then fed her bread and oil and wept with her face absolutely still, the tears simply falling, as though crying were manual labor she had long since perfected.

Petra had been her mother's friend. Petra was the reason the manifest's address would hold up under questioning. Petra was also terrified.

"They ask questions now," Petra said, when the bread was gone. "Not like before. Before they took people. Now they ask. A captain here—Rennick—he believes in paperwork. He thinks everything true can be written down." A bitter pause. "He's not wrong often enough."

"The ledgers," Ilse said quietly. "Oyelaran's ledgers. Is it true they took them?"

"Two days ago. Crates on the quay like salt fish." Petra's hands twisted in her apron. "Lise—" warningly, the false name deployed like a shield between them, *don't say real names in my kitchen* — "whatever you came back for. Whatever you think is waiting. The town buried this once. We helped bury it. You dig, you don't dig alone, and the ones beside you get filled in too."

"I understand."

"You don't. You've been away. Away, everyone's brave." Petra looked at her then, really looked, with the full terrible tenderness of the old. "You have her way of listening. Did you know that? She used to listen like the words might be the last of something."

Ilse set down the cup very carefully, because her hands had begun to shake, and said, "Tell me one thing. Just one. Then I'll sleep, and tomorrow I'm only a widow with needles."

"Ask."

"The graves. When they took them—there was never a place. No roll, no marker, nothing. Did anyone—did anyone mark it privately? Some stone? Some—"

"No." And then, softly, as if the word cost money: "But the water remembers. Ask the old ones where the current runs wrong past the point, and they'll tell you it runs wrong because it's carrying names." Petra wiped her face with finality. "Superstition. But superstition is memory that found a place to hide."

* *

She slept three hours and woke with the harbor light stuttering through the shutter-cracks, every fourth beat late, and lay in the strange bed composing herself into Lise Corver: the rounded shoulders, the flat vowels, the widow's patience. Then she rose and went out into the fish market at dawn, because markets were where a town told the truth while pretending to haggle.

Vellamare's market had doubled in size since her childhood—no. Had not changed at all, and she had doubled, and the difference felt like the change. She moved through it buying nothing, selling nothing, being nobody: a woman with a crate of needles and a widow's face. She heard the garrison's presence everywhere without seeing it, the way you hear weather through a roof. She heard something else too, fainter, threaded under the fish-seller cries like a second current.

Silence. Specific silences. The gap where a family's stall should have been. The way conversation bent around the subject of the requisition the way water bends around a stone—never stopping, always noting.

And once—once—she saw it. An old man mending nets at the market's edge, and when two soldiers passed behind him he did not look up, did not pause in his work, but his hands went still for precisely the length of their passing, resumed only when the boots had faded. Twenty years of survival written in a pair of knotted hands.

She marked him. She marked the door of his house, later, walking past it once at the correct pace of a woman with errands. She marked three others in the market the same way, small tells, the grammar of the hidden. She did not approach anyone. Approach came later, after trust, after pattern, after she knew which silences were fear and which were loyalty worn so long it had become flesh.

Toward noon she let her feet carry her, innocently, along the harbor road, and past the foot of the lane that climbed to the mapshop.

She did not climb it. She stood at the bottom like a pilgrim at the base of a mountain and looked up at the building—shutters open, sign hanging, the old brass plate with OYELARAN still legible beneath two decades of salt fog. Someone had kept it open. Someone had kept it open for twenty years.

Smoke rose from the chimney. So someone was alive in it.

She stood there perhaps a minute—too long, some part of her noted coolly, move, *move*—and as she turned away she saw, chalked low on the customs house door across the lane, so low it might have been a child's scribble, a mark.

She knew the mark.

Every child of the purged families had known the mark; it had been chalked on *their* doors once, before the doors were painted over or sold. She had thought it extinct, a fossil, a thing that existed now only in archives. And here it was, fresh, the chalk dust still caught in the grain of the wood, drawn within days—hours?

Someone in Vellamare was signaling. Someone was awake.

The mark meant, in the old code her mother had taught her along with prayers: *the water remembers. I am listening.*

Ilse walked away from the customs house at a widow's pace, her heart going like a bird in a closed room, and began, methodically, joyfully, terrifyingly, to plan how a woman named Lise Corver would make contact without either of them dying for it.

Behind her the harbor light stuttered on into the morning, every fourth beat late, faithful as a wound.
