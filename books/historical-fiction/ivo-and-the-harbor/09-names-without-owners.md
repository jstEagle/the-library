## 9. Names Without Owners

The fish market opened before the sun did, and Ilse learned its rhythms the way she had once learned the rhythms of her mother's kitchen: by standing in the way until someone corrected her.

"Mind the gulls, girl." A woman with forearms like mooring rope shoved a crate of silvering mackerel against Ilse's hip. "You want work or you want to stand there being pretty at my fish?"

"Work," Ilse said, and took the crate.

She had been three days in Vellamare under the name Lena Voss, a cousin from up the coast come to earn winter wages. The false manifest that had carried her across the water was already ashore somewhere in Rennick's ledgers, one more line of ink among hundreds, unremarkable. She had spent six years abroad learning how to be nobody—how to hold a stranger's accent in her mouth, how to answer questions with questions, how to let men at customs look at her papers and see exactly what they expected to see, which was nothing. Six years of practice for this: hauling fish before dawn in the town where she was born, listening.

Because the survivors were here. She had crossed an ocean on the certainty of it. The purge had not killed everyone it meant to erase; some of the erased had simply been folded into Vellamare like letters hidden in the pages of a book, living under borrowed names in borrowed houses, so quiet for so long that they had half convinced themselves the other lives had never happened.

Ilse had come home to find them, and she was discovering, hour by hour, that finding them was not the same as being welcomed by them.

* * *

By midmorning the market thinned and the taverns along the quay began their slow waking—the Sweeping Gull first, then the Anchor's Rest, then the narrow place with no sign at all above the door that everyone called the Back Room because no one would give it another name. Ilse worked them all. She hauled barrels, scrubbed floors, cut lemons, carried beer. She kept her head down and her ears open, and she said the names softly, when she said them at all, the way you'd offer something fragile to a person you suspected might strike it from your hands.

The barkeep at the Gull was called Doss now. Ilse knew—he remembered, she was almost sure he remembered—that he had been born Tomas Ferreiro, second son of a family that had kept the harbor lights for three generations before the purge decided three generations of lighthouse keepers made convenient conspirators.

She said the name on her fourth evening there, low, while he drew her a cup of small beer she hadn't asked for.

His hand did not shake. That was the terrible part. His hand went perfectly, deliberately still, the way a man's hand goes still when he has stepped onto ice and heard it speak. Then he finished pulling the beer, set it down, and leaned close enough that she could smell the hops on his breath.

"You say that word again in this room," he said, barely above the noise of the taproom, "and I will put you out on the street myself, and I will sleep fine afterward. Do you understand me?"

"Doss—"

"That man died. He died twenty years ago and I buried him and I dug the grave deep, and you don't get to dig him up because it suits you." His eyes were wet and furious at once. "There are soldiers on the quay. There is a captain in the customs house with lists. And you come in here with your mother's chin and your father's name in your mouth like it's a gift. It isn't a gift. It's a lit match."

Ilse held his gaze. "Your father trimmed the south lamp every dusk of his life," she said. "I watched him do it when I was seven. Somebody should remember that."

"Somebody remembering it is what killed him." He straightened, wiped his hands on his apron, and became, in the space of a breath, only a tired barkeep again. "Drink your beer and go home, girl. Whoever you are."

But his hand had gone still. She turned that stillness over all night, walking back to her rented room through streets smelling of salt and tar. He had not forgotten. None of them had forgotten. That was the discovery that both heartened and appalled her: the purge had never worked the way its authors wanted. It had not erased anything. It had only taught a whole town to flinch.

* * *

The flinching had shapes. Once she knew to look for them, she saw them everywhere.

A cooper on Fish Lane who always, without fail, glanced over his shoulder before saying his own name aloud to a customer—a small tic, a half-second hitch, as if checking that no older self was following him. An old woman who sold cockles from a blanket near the seawall and who, when the church bell rang the hour, moved her lips silently through numbers that didn't match any calendar Ilse knew; counting, perhaps, anniversaries of things that officially had never occurred. Two brothers who ran a net-mending shed and who had not spoken each other's true surname in so long that when one of them slipped once, drunk, on a feast night—said *Vasquez*, just once, into his brother's ear—the other brother left the table and was sick in the harbor.

Ilse collected these small tremors the way her mother had once collected recipes: patiently, precisely, with love. And everywhere she went she asked, carefully, in the accents of a cousin from up the coast, about the atlas.

*The old mapmaker's book,* she'd say, wiping down a table or weighing cockles. *My grandmother used to talk about it. Said Master Oyelaran could chart a channel blindfolded. Whatever came of all those ledgers?*

And the town closed around the question like a fist.

At the Anchor's Rest, a stevedore with grey in his beard told her the ledgers had burned, then told her they'd been sold to collectors in the capital, then told her to mind her own business—all in the same quarter hour, which told her he knew none of it was true and all of it might be dangerous. At the Back Room, a woman who gutted mackerel in the alley behind the tavern looked at Ilse for a long moment when the atlas came up, and then said, very quietly, "Ask instead about the people the book was for. Ask why nobody asked after them for twenty years." And went back to her knives.

That one stayed with Ilse longer than the rest. Because the answer, of course, was that people had not asked because asking was death, and because shame keeps its own silence even after the fear fades—and because the accusation in the woman's voice had landed somewhere true. Ilse had spent six years away learning to be nobody. She had not sent a single letter home. She had not asked after anyone either.

* * *

It was at the cockle-seller's blanket, on the eighth morning, that the wall finally cracked.

The old woman's name was Marta now—it had been Marta for so long that Ilse suspected even she answered to it without thinking—but her hands told a different story. They were a scribe's hands gone coarse, knuckles swollen from cold water, but they handled the small brass scale with a delicacy that no fishwife ever learned. Ilse crouched beside the blanket pretending to sort cockles, and watched the old woman weigh out portions for a queue of customers, and saw her lips move again when the bell rang.

"My mother counted the days too," Ilse said.

The old woman did not look up. "Did she."

"Not the way the church counts. Another way."

The queue moved. A man bought a pint of cockles and shuffled off. When the blanket was momentarily empty, the old woman gathered her skirts and sat back on her stool and looked at Ilse properly for the first time—not at the borrowed face but somehow past it, the way you look at a painting you suspect hangs crooked.

"I know whose daughter you are," she said. "You've had your mother's walk since the day you stepped off that boat. You think a false manifest hides that?"

Ilse's heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs. "Then you understand why I'm here."

"I understand it better than you do." The old woman's voice was flat as slate. "I've watched you three days. The Gull. The market. Asking your soft little questions. Do you know what happens if Captain Rennick hears even half of what you're stirring? He doesn't need proof. He needs a reason to start searching, and searching finds things, and found things hang people. People who have kept their heads down for twenty years. People with children."

"They've already lost everything worth keeping. Their names—"

"Their names are *theirs*." For the first time the old woman's voice rose, and a passing customer glanced over, and she lowered it again to that flat slate whisper. "You think we want them back like lost coins? Serafina. Is that what you want to hear me say it? Serafina, yes, that was me, thirty years ago, a girl with good hands and a foolish tongue. That girl got her husband taken and her house sealed and her name struck, and the woman you see sitting here survived because she buried Serafina deep enough that even I stopped hearing her at night. And now you come digging with a spade made of grief, and you'll bury us all twice."

"Serafina," Ilse repeated, softly.

The old woman flinched. Actually flinched—a full-body shudder, like a horse feeling a whip it couldn't see—and Ilse understood in that instant what she had really come home to find. Not survivors. Hostages. A whole town of people held captive by their own survival, guarding the graves of themselves.

"Stop digging," the old woman whispered. "Whatever you're planning, whoever's helping you, stop. Take your boat and go. There is no version of this that ends well. There wasn't twenty years ago and there isn't now."

"There's one," Ilse said. "One where it's written down. One where nobody can be erased again, because the record exists somewhere Rennick can't burn it."

"And who keeps that record safe?" The old woman laughed, a dry terrible sound. "Paper burns, child. Men burn faster. Go home."

Ilse stood, brushed the grit from her knees, and turned to go. Behind her she heard the small brass scale chime, the next customer served, the ordinary morning resuming. She had reached the mouth of Fish Lane when footsteps caught her up—quick, uneven—and a hand pressed something into hers, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from the old woman's pocket.

"Don't come back here," Marta said, not meeting her eyes, already turning away. "And don't thank me. I didn't give you anything. Someone dropped it. Someone careless. God forgive me."

Then she was gone, back to her blanket and her cockles and her borrowed name, and Ilse walked until she found the shelter of the sea wall, and unfolded the oilcloth with fingers that would not quite stay steady.

It was a fragment of a copper plate. No bigger than her palm, edges sheared rough where it had been cut away from something larger, the surface scored with the mirror-script of engraving—letters reversed, waiting for paper and pressure to make them readable. Aldous Oyelaran's work; she knew it now from Sebastian's descriptions, the fine confident burin lines, the little anchor mark the master hid in every flourish. She tilted it toward the light.

Three partial words ran down the fragment's edge, cut deep and then gouged at, defaced by someone in a hurry—but the gouges had missed the deepest strokes. She read them slowly, reversing each character in her mind the way she had learned to read engravings abroad, in a printer's shop in a free port where such things were still taught:

*...osa ...archett...*

Rosa Marchetti.

Her mother's name. Cut from a plate, mutilated by a soldier's blade or an informer's knife, surviving anyway—in the pocket of a cockle-seller who had carried it for twenty years like a stone she could not swallow and could not spit out.

Ilse sat down hard on the sea wall. The gulls screamed. The tide came in below her, patient and enormous, filling the channels her mother had never lived to see mapped.

Somebody had stolen this plate the night the ledgers were seized. Somebody had broken it, and scattered it, and one piece had found its way here—to a woman who had sworn she wanted nothing found. Which meant others might exist. Fragments, scattered through the town like seeds, carried in pockets and floorboards and prayer books by people who despised what they carried and could not destroy it.

Her mother existed nowhere else on earth. Not in parish rolls, not in tax records, not in any ledger the army held. Only here, in copper, in reversed letters, in the keeping of the frightened.

Ilse wrapped the fragment again and pressed it against her chest, and made herself a promise colder and harder than the one she had made at twelve: she would find the rest. Every shard. Every name.

Behind her, up the lane, a garrison patrol turned the corner in their grey coats, and the market went quiet the way water goes still, and Ilse stood and walked slowly, innocently, toward the quay—toward the maproom, toward Sebastian—with her mother's name hidden against her heart and the whole sleeping town between her and the soldiers who would burn it if they ever learned it existed.

She did not see the young soldier at the corner who watched her go, and marked the direction, and did not yet know why.
