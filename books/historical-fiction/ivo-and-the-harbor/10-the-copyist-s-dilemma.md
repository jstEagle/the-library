## 10. The Copyist's Dilemma

The sailcloth had to be treated before midnight.

Sebastian had learned that much from Aldous in the first year of apprenticeship, when the master was still alive and still whole, when the lessons came easy as breathing: *cloth takes ink differently than paper, child. Paper forgives. Cloth remembers every hesitation.* They had been stretching a chart of the northern shoals in those days, and Aldous had run his thumb along the treated weave and said, half to himself, that a map made on sailcloth could survive salt water, and a map made on paper could not, and there was a lesson in that about what was worth making.

Sebastian had thought it only talk then. A master's habit of turning everything into instruction.

Now, twenty years later, alone in the maproom with the shutters drawn and one shutter's gap open toward the harbor, Sebastian boiled the treatment over the small spirit lamp and understood that everything Aldous ever said had been instruction, and some of it had simply taken two decades to arrive at its purpose.

The mixture was beeswax and linseed oil, a whisper of turpentine, and—this was the part no guild manual recorded—a measure of the same gum arabic used to size the atlas's finest plates. Aldous's own formula, written nowhere, held now only in Sebastian's hands. When the cloth came out of it, cooled and stretched taut across the old surveying frame, it took a nib like vellum and shed water like a gull's back. Rolled tight around a spar of seasoned ash, sealed with pitch, a chart could cross an ocean without losing a single name.

A chart could also be hidden where no soldier would think to look, because soldiers did not search masts. They searched desks. They searched walls. They searched people.

Sebastian stretched the second length of cloth, pinned its corners, and let it dry, and while it dried, sat down in front of the false panel behind the memorial portrait and did not move for a long time.

* * *

The originals were still there. Of course they were there. Sebastian had checked eleven times since the requisition, sometimes telling themselves it was inventory, sometimes admitting it was prayer. The portrait of Aldous—painted by a harbor artist who charged three silver and captured exactly the tired iron of the man's gaze—swung out on hinges Sebastian had installed at nineteen, and behind it, sunk into the wall cavity between the joists, lay seven plates of hammered copper, each one engraved in Aldous's hand with the thing the purge had tried to make unmakeable: names.

True names. The Marchetti name among them, though Ilse had never needed the plate to know who she was. But there were others—the Kesselink fishers, the Ovidi family from the lamp quarter, the Ansel family whose bakery on Rope Lane had become, in the years after, a chandler's, then a ruin, then nothing at all. Families erased so thoroughly that the town's own memory had been taught to skip over them like a needle skipping a groove. Aldous had spent the last decade of his life recording them: their houses, their boats, the tide channels they had used to slip the harbor on the night of the purge, and above all their names, spelled out letter by letter in his severe, upright engraver's script, so that no fire or soldier or convenient forgetting could ever claim they had not existed.

And Captain Rennick held the companion ledgers. Had held them for six days now. Somewhere in the garrison house those mutilated volumes sat under lock, their cut pages gaping like missing teeth, and somewhere in Rennick's methodical mind the question was surely forming that Sebastian had been dreading since the crates left: *if the names are not in the ledgers, where are they?*

Every answer pointed here. To this room. To this wall. To the apprentice who had kept a dead man's shop open for twenty years out of, everyone assumed, grief.

Grief, yes. But grief keeps things. That is one of its functions, and one of its dangers.

Sebastian closed the panel, swung the portrait back into place, and turned to the drying cloth. It was time to stop guarding the truth and start moving it.

* * *

Copying an engraved plate onto cloth is not transcription. It is translation between languages, and the two languages disagree about almost everything.

Copper takes the graver's line clean and permanent; cloth takes ink soft and slightly bleeding, so every stroke must be made as if signing your own name in front of a magistrate—once, correctly, without correction. Sebastian began with the coast itself, because the coast was safe. No one hangs for a coastline. The headlands of Vellamare went down first, then the soundings in the outer roads, then the beacon and the mole and the white church tower that served every fishing crew as a transit mark. Hours passed. The spirit lamp burned low and was refilled. The harbor light outside the shutter-gap shifted through its slow colors—gold to white to the deep blue of the small hours—and Sebastian worked the way Aldous had taught working: shoulders loose, breath even, the whole body converted into patience.

It was only when the geography was done that the dilemma arrived, and it arrived not as a thought but as a physical event: the hand stopped. Mid-air, mid-stroke, hovering over the space where the first name would go.

Because here was the arithmetic Sebastian had been avoiding all evening, the sum that made the stomach clench like a fist. The plates existed in exactly one place. Once copied, they could exist in two. A copy rolled inside a spar and carried out through the tide channels meant that if the garrison found the originals—if Rennick's men tore down the wall, if the reader reconstructed what the knives had cut, if any of the thousand catastrophes Sebastian had rehearsed for twenty years finally arrived—the truth would already be at sea, beyond reach, multiplying.

But copying was not hiding. Copying was exposure. Each hour bent over these plates was an hour the door downstairs could open, boots on the stair, and Rennick's voice saying in that courteous gray tone, *Master Oyelaran. Working late.* Every candle-end burned was evidence. Every drop of gum arabic on the floorboards was a confession written in a language any mapmaker could read.

And there was worse, if worse were needed. There was the fact that Sebastian could not copy fast enough. Seven plates, each dense as scripture. At the pace the coastline had demanded, the work would take nine nights. Nine nights of lamplight behind shutters in a town full of informants and a garrison full of men who had been told, explicitly, that records and memory now belonged to the army.

Nine chances, each night, to lose everything—including Ilse's mother, who existed nowhere else in the world except in Aldous's copper and, soon, God willing, in rolled sailcloth bound for open water.

Sebastian looked at the hovering hand and heard Aldous, the way the dead speak, which is to say in one's own voice wearing a borrowed gravity: *A map is a promise, child. You do not make promises halfway.*

The hand came down. The first name went onto the cloth in careful strokes: **ANSEL, TOBIAS**, and beneath it, in smaller letters, *baker of Rope Lane, taken 14th of the Harvest Month*. Then his wife. Then his daughters. Sebastian wrote them all, and writing them discovered something that no amount of preparation had foreseen: it was not like copying at all.

It was like saying them aloud. Alone, at three in the morning, in a room where no living person could hear—but the room heard. The walls heard. Twenty years of silence broke its own seal one syllable at a time, inked instead of spoken, and Sebastian found tears falling onto the stretched cloth and had to lift each bead away with a corner of linen before it could bloom the wax sizing, because the cloth remembers every hesitation, and it must not remember weeping. Not on these pages. These pages had to be perfect. Perfection was the only armor testimony gets to wear.

By dawn, one plate was done. Three families. Eleven names. Eleven people whom the official record insisted had never lived.

Sebastian blew out the lamp as the harbor turned gray, then rose-gold, and stood at the shutter-gap watching the fishing fleet put out under the eyes of a garrison picket on the mole, and felt something unfamiliar settle into the chest—not peace, nothing like peace, but resolve of a particular density, the kind that comes from having started something that cannot be unstarted.

The first copy existed. Which meant the first copy could be found. Which meant it had to leave soon, through the channels, into hands that could carry it away. Which meant Ilse. Which meant risk stacked upon risk, and no way to sort them anymore into separate piles. Everything touched everything now.

* * *

The second night, the knock came at half past ten.

Not on the street door—on the alley door, the one that opened onto the stair, the one only three living people knew the use of. Sebastian's heart performed a complete catastrophe in the space of a single second: the cloth, the lamp, the ink, the frame. There was no time. There was never time. The cloth went under the bottom drawer of the plan chest, beneath a false lining Aldous had built for charts he'd wanted out of sight, and the lamp was snuffed and the frame covered with its canvas apron, and Sebastian crossed the room counting heartbeats, trying to make the face go smooth, and pulled open the door.

Ilse stood on the landing with rain in her hair and a wrapped parcel of smoked fish under one arm, and she read the whole of it off Sebastian's face in the time it took to step inside.

"You're doing it," she said quietly. "You've started."

"I started last night." Sebastian latched the door. "Eleven names are on cloth already."

"Show me."

"Are you followed? Is anyone—"

"Show me, Sebastian."

So Sebastian lifted the false lining, drew out the roll of treated sailcloth, and unrolled it across the plan chest in the dark, working by feel and by the thin wash of harbor light through the shutter-gap, and heard Ilse's breath change when her finger found the third family. The Ovidis. Her mother's sister's line, surviving in ink when it survived nowhere else.

She didn't cry. Sebastian had expected crying, had braced for it, and instead Ilse went very still, the way water goes still over depth, and when she spoke her voice was flattened by effort into something almost ordinary.

"It's good work. He'd say it was good work." A pause. "How long for the rest?"

"Eight nights. Maybe seven if I don't sleep."

"That's too long."

"I know how long it is, Ilse."

"Do you?" She turned, and in the dimness her eyes caught what little light there was. "Rennick has a reader coming—I heard it in the fish market today, from the wife of one of his clerks. Someone who reads ink impressions, knife marks, whatever they call it. He can pull names out of those cut pages like pulling splinters out of skin. When he arrives, Rennick won't need the plates at all. He'll just need the people the names belong to. And he'll start with anyone who ever stood in this shop."

"Then I'll work faster."

"Faster is sloppier. Sloppy is dead." Ilse put her hand flat on the unrolled cloth, over the Ovidi name, as if taking a pulse. "Listen to me. I can get a spar aboard the *Cormorant's Grace*—she warps out on the spring tide, four days. If you can have the finished copies ready—"

"Four days." Sebastian looked at the chest, at the six untouched plates waiting behind the portrait, and did the terrible division. Four days. Half the work, carried half-done, or all of it carried too late. "I can't promise four days."

"Then promise me you'll send what's finished and keep the rest hidden, and not stay in this room dying by inches to save one more name." Her hand closed, gathering a fold of the cloth. "You're not the only one who can carry things through the channels. And you're not the only one they'll come for."

They stood a moment in the cold dark of the maproom, hand and hand on the cloth, over the inked dead, and Sebastian understood that this was the shape of the dilemma after all—not cloth versus copper, not speed versus care, but this: that love had become logistics, and every hour spent protecting one person was an hour stolen from protecting everyone.

"I'll work tonight," Sebastian said at last. "All of it. Come back tomorrow after the market, and take whatever exists."

Ilse nodded once, rewrapped the fish, and left it on the plan chest—cover, alibi, supper, all three—and went down the stair into the rain.

* * *

Alone again, Sebastian relit the lamp, stretched fresh cloth, and set the graver's translated lines flowing: **KESSELINK**, and the fishing family's names, generation under generation, the dates of taking and the dates of birth that the purge had made mutually exclusive. Outside, the garrison patrol passed below the windows, boots measured on wet cobbles, and Sebastian worked through the passing of their footsteps and through the ones that followed, and did not stop when fingers cramped and did not stop when the lamp guttered, and was reaching for the fifth name on the plate when the sound came.

Boots on the alley stair.

Not Ilse's—wrong rhythm, wrong weight, two sets, heavy. Sebastian's blood went to ice water. The cloth came off the frame in one motion, rolled hard, into the lining, down flat, the apron over the frame, the lamp pinched to darkness, and Sebastian stood in the black room listening to the steps climb and climb past—

Past. Up, and up, to the loft above the maproom, the empty tenant's floor, where a key scraped and a voice muttered about roof leaks, and the boots crossed overhead and crossed back and settled, eventually, into silence.

Soldiers billeted. One floor up. Directly above the work.

Sebastian stood in the dark a long time, listening to the strangers breathe through the ceiling, and understood that the room had just shrunk to the size of a held breath. Above, a man coughed, rolled over, slept. Below, under the false lining, half a plate waited unfinished, and six more waited behind a dead man's portrait, and somewhere across the sleeping town Captain Rennick's reader was already on the road.

Four days until the spring tide. Two floors between the truth and the army.

Sebastian sat back down at the frame in the dark, found the nib by touch, and kept writing.
