## 12. What Loyalty Weighs

The Oyelaran sons—no, the Voss sons now, she corrected herself; they had taken their father's new name along with his new life—drank at the Anchor's Rest on Thursdays, after the second watch changed. Ilse had learned this in three days of listening, of carrying trays of smoked fish and small beer through the fish market while her eyes did other work. The older one, Darian, sat with his back to the wall. The younger one, Piet, sat facing the door, because he had not yet learned to be afraid of it.

She remembered them as boys: two dark-haired children who had run the quay like gulls, shouting, always shouting, while their father counted cargo and their mother's brother disappeared one autumn without a boat and without a name anyone would say aloud. That uncle had been a Marchetti cousin. That was the whole of her claim on them, and it was thin as sailcloth, and she meant to test every thread of it.

She dressed the part first. A widow's grays, a shawl that swallowed her hair, a basket of dried herring bought from a woman who owed the Marchetti name nothing but who took her coin readily enough. She let the basket swing at her hip like an anchor and walked into the Anchor's Rest at the hour when the lamplight was kindest to strangers, because it made everyone look like someone's lost daughter.

The tavern smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular staleness of men who slept in barracks and missed their own beds. She found a stool at the end of the bar and ordered nothing for a long time, which made her invisible in a way that ordering never did. A woman who buys nothing is either waiting or grieving, and no soldier wants to interrupt either.

Piet saw her first. She felt it—the sudden stillness of recognition, the way his hand stopped halfway to his cup. He was twenty-three now, maybe twenty-four, broad through the shoulders from drilling, but he had his mother's eyes, and those had not changed since he was nine years old and she had pulled him out of the tide pools behind the customs house, laughing, both of them soaked to the ribs.

*You were heavy*, he had said. *You're heavier now.* And she had said, *That's the water. Water weighs nothing until you carry it.*

He carried something now. She watched him weigh it across the smoky room.

Darian noticed his brother's stillness and followed his gaze, and Ilse watched the second recognition arrive like cold water—faster, harder, because Darian had been eleven that summer, old enough to remember what happened to families who recognized the wrong people in public places. His face did the thing she had come to study these past weeks in tavern mirrors: the flattening, the careful blankness, the face becoming a shut door. He turned back to his cup.

Good, she thought. *Be afraid. Fear means you remember.*

She waited until they rose to leave, and then she did the simplest thing, the thing no training counters: she said their names into the room, softly, in their mother's voice—not their mother's actual voice, which she barely remembered, but the voice mothers used in Vellamare, low and salted, half-song.

"Darian. Piet."

They stopped in the doorway like two men hit by the same wave. Then Darian took his brother's elbow and steered him out, and Ilse put coins on the bar for beer she hadn't drunk, and followed them into the dark.

* * *

She caught up with them at the mouth of Cooper's Lane, where the lamps ran out and the town gave up pretending to be watched. They stood beneath the leaning eave of the ropewalk, and the three of them formed a triangle of held breath.

"You shouldn't be here." Darian said it first, flat, quiet. Not a question. A man stating weather.

"Where should I be?" Ilse kept her voice gentle. "Tell me where I should be, and I'll go there."

"Ilse—" Piet started, and Darian's hand came up, not touching him, just near, the way you'd steady a mast.

"Don't say it," Darian told his brother. "Don't say the name on the street."

"It's not a street," Piet said. "It's Cooper's Lane. Nobody's—"

"Everyone's listening everywhere. You'd know that if you'd been in the garrison longer than one thaw." But Darian looked at her then, really looked, and she saw the war begin in him. He had her mother's cheekbones too—all the cousins had shared that bone-deep map—and seeing them on a declared enemy's face in his own town was doing something to him that his uniform could not order away.

"I'm not here to endanger you," Ilse said. "I know how this works. I've lived abroad six years knowing how this works. I want ten minutes of your time in a place with no ears, and then I'll vanish so completely you can tell yourselves I was never real."

"And if we refuse?" Darian asked.

"Then you'll spend the rest of your lives wondering what I wanted, and whether it mattered, and whether the ten minutes would have cost less than the wondering."

Piet laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly bitten off. "Gods. She talks exactly like Aunt Reva."

Something moved through Darian at the name. Ilse marked it, filed it, and pressed before it could close.

"The ropewalk loft. Now. Ten minutes."

* * *

The loft smelled of hemp and pigeon droppings, moonlight coming through gaps in the slatted walls in pale stripes. Darian stood by the ladder with his arms folded, guarding the way down rather than the way up, and Ilse understood that too: he had not decided yet which direction he was facing.

"I'll say my piece once," she began. "Your father betrayed Aldous Oyelaran. I know it. Half the harbor knows it, though half the harbor has known it silently for twenty years, which amounts to the same thing as not knowing it. He did it to save you. To save his sons. No one blames him—least of all me. I'm not here about the past."

"Then why are you here?" Darian's voice stayed level, but his arms tightened.

"Because the past is coming back regardless of whether anyone is here about it. Captain Rennick has the master's ledgers. He has hired someone to read what was cut out of them. When he finishes reading, he will have names—families who have lived under borrowed identities for two decades, neighbors of yours, men who drink in the Anchor's Rest, women who sell you fish every morning. And when he has the names, he will have the houses. And when he has the houses—" She let it hang. In the striped moonlight, their faces went young again.

"That's not how it works," Darian said, but his voice had thinned. "The purge ended. There was a proclamation. There was—"

"There was a purge," Ilse said, "and then there was a silence, and now there is a captain who reads silences professionally. Ask yourself why the army requisitions records in peacetime, Darian. Records are only dangerous to people planning to use them."

Piet had gone very still on a coil of rope. "What do you want from us?"

Him first, she thought. *Always the younger one first; the older one is already armored.* "Nothing tonight. I want you to think about a question, that's all. One question, and I'll never ask you anything else." She let the pause stretch until both brothers leaned toward her without meaning to. "If your father's crime saved you, and the army comes for the families your father failed to save—who are you loyal to? The blood that bought you? Or the uniform that spent it?"

Silence. Somewhere below, a cat knocked over something glass, and the sound broke and scattered.

"You don't know anything about us," Darian said finally, but softly now, which was different.

"I know your uncle's name was Tomas and he played the fiddle badly and the whole quay pretended not to notice when he was gone, including your father, especially your father, and that pretense is the debt I've come to collect." She rose. "I'm not asking you to betray your captain. I'm asking you to decide, before the decision is made for you, whether you're the kind of men who watch a second erasure happen in the same harbor where the first one happened outside your windows. That's all. Decide. Quietly. Even deciding nothing is deciding—I just want you to know you decided."

She climbed down the ladder. At the bottom she paused, one hand on the rail, and said, without turning, "Your mother kept a blue jar on the sill above the stove. Tomas painted it for her the winter before he vanished. If it's still there, tell me. It would mean something to me."

Then she was gone into Cooper's Lane, heart slamming, hands cold inside her sleeves.

* * *

Two days passed. Two days of herring and small beer and watching doors, of chalking nothing anywhere, of being the most boring widow in Vellamare. On the third evening, a scrap of paper appeared inside the empty flour barrel behind the baker's on Cooper's Lane—a dead drop she had named aloud in the loft, deliberately, so that using it would itself be an answer.

The paper held four words in a hand she didn't recognize: *Jar broke years ago.*

No signature. No name. But the ink was military issue, black and precise, and the sentence was not a refusal. It was grief wearing a fact. Someone had checked. Someone had gone home—or gone near home—and asked after a blue jar, which meant someone had heard her, and carried her question around for two days like a stone in a boot, and finally needed her to know the truth of it even though the truth closed the subject forever.

Piet, she thought. It had to be Piet. Darian would not have answered at all; Darian's silence was armor, not conversation.

She stood behind the bakery with the paper in her fist and allowed herself, briefly, dangerously, to hope. One son wavering. One thread of blood loyalty bent toward the living instead of the dead arrangement that had kept everyone safe and no one honest. It was more than she'd had a week ago. It might be enough to move a man, a gate key, a warning, a single crucial hour.

* * *

On the fourth morning she learned how wrong hope measures things.

She was at the fish market before dawn, buying nothing, watching everything, when she saw Darian Voss cross the square toward the customs house in full daylight, in uniform, unhurried. He did not look at her. He did not need to. He walked past her stall at the precise distance of a man who does not know a person, and he did not glance back, and it was the not-glancing-back that told her, the way a missing current tells a swimmer the tide has turned.

By noon she had confirmed it through the fishwives' web, which is faster than any army's: Darian Voss had requested a private word with Captain Rennick's adjutant the previous evening. What was said, no one knew. What was ordered afterward, everyone soon felt: patrols doubled along the waterfront, the customs house gate chained an hour early, and a new sentry posted at the head of Cooper's Lane with orders, the fish seller whispered, to note every face that came and went.

Ilse stood amid the gutting tables with herring ice melting through her basket and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like relief, what she had done. She had tested loyalty and found the seam—and Darian Voss had chosen duty over blood, or fear over both, or perhaps simply chose his brother's safety the only way he knew: by burning the connection before it burned them all. Maybe the report had even protected Piet. Maybe that was the kindness in it. She would never know, and she had to keep working as if she didn't care.

But one thing she did know, standing there while the market shouted and gulls screamed over the scales: Rennick would now have a description of her. Widow's grays. Basket of herring. Accent softened by six years abroad. A face with Marchetti cheekbones, asking after a blue jar.

And above the harbor, in a maproom with a false wall, was a man who had no idea the net had just been drawn one knot tighter around them both.

She left the basket on the table among the melting ice and walked, unhurried, a boring widow among boring widows, toward the one door in Vellamare she had promised herself she would not bring danger to—and brought it anyway.
