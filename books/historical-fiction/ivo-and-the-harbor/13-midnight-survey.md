## 13. Midnight Survey

*Sebastian*

The plates went into the oilcloth an hour before moonrise, rolled tight as wounds and bound with tarred cord. Sebastian counted them three times by feel alone—no lamp, no window, nothing the garrison's watchers on Bell Row could mark. Nine rolls. The sailcloth copies had taken eleven nights, each letter traced onto treated canvas in ink mixed with salt and glycerin so it would not run if the sea found it. Aldous would have approved of the medium. He had always said the harbor kept its truest records in things meant to be soaked.

Sebastian wrapped the bundle against their chest with a length of fishing net, the way eel-men carried their catch through the shallows, and let themselves out through the shop's back door into the smell of low tide.

The town was quiet in the particular way that frightened. No dogs on the quay. No lamplight at the harbormaster's window, though Corvane sat awake behind it most nights now, a man aging visibly under the weight of two betrayals. Even the garrison's patrol had passed early, boots loud on purpose, performing vigilance and then abandoning it to the cold. Sebastian stood in the alley behind the maproom and listened until the silence resolved into its ordinary parts: water against pilings, the creak of moored hulls, wind coming off the flats carrying the mineral stink of exposed mud.

Then a soft whistle from the direction of the cooper's yard. Two notes, rising.

Ilse.

She detached from the shadow of the wine barrels the way she had learned to move in whatever port had taught her—not Vellamare's walk anymore, something quicker, flatter, a walk for streets where being recognized was fatal. She wore a fishwife's hooded cloak and rubber boots to the knee, and beneath the cloak Sebastian could see the outline of the waxed satchel holding her half of the cargo: the fragment of plate the old woman at the fish market had surrendered, wrapped like a relic, along with the manifest pages that would explain them to the smuggler waiting beyond the point.

"You're late," she breathed.

"You're early."

"One of us has to be." Her eyes went over Sebastian's shoulder to the alley mouth, held there a long moment, came back. "Bell Row's dark. The corporal who watches your door fell asleep around nine—I saw his lamp gutter and not relight. Either he's lazy or Rennick wants us to think he is."

"Rennick doesn't leave things to laziness."

"No," Ilse said. "He leaves them to arithmetic. Come on."

They went down through the tannery lane, past the shuttered smokehouse where the racks hung empty since the requisition, down the stair cut into the cliff that only appeared at this tide—eleven steps, then the shelf of rock, then the mud. The channels opened before them in the dark like veins of a thing still living: black ribbons between silver-brown flats, running out toward the point where the smuggler *Cormorant* rode at anchor beyond the harbor light's reach, pretending to be a wreck too poor to salvage.

Aldous had charted these channels in the years before the purge, when charting anything honestly was already dangerous. He had walked them at every tide for six years, pole in hand, sounding depths that no customs survey ever troubled to find, because he understood before anyone else did what a map really was: not a record of where things were, but a decision about what deserved to be found.

Sebastian knew the channels the way other people knew their mother's voice. Left fork at the old anchor chain, keep to the seaward bank where the mud holds, past the drowned skiff, until the channel bends north and deepens all at once, like trust given after long refusal.

"Left at the chain," they murmured, and felt rather than saw Ilse nod.

The mud took their boots to the ankle and held them with a sound like slow kissing. They moved in the rhythm Aldous had drilled into a twelve-year-old apprentice decades ago—three steps, listen, three steps—and somewhere in that rhythm Sebastian's fear changed shape. It stopped being about the patrols, the lamps, the arithmetic of Captain Rennick. It became simpler and older: the wish to arrive, together, at a place neither of them had ever reached.

Halfway down the long channel called the Seam, where the water ran thigh-deep and faster, Ilse caught Sebastian's sleeve.

"Listen."

They froze. Out past the point, faint as a struck match, a light bloomed—the patrol boat's lantern, sweeping the harbor mouth in long deliberate arcs. It wasn't due. The army's boats worked the outer roads at slack water, and slack water was hours away.

"They've changed the schedule," Ilse whispered.

"Or they're looking for something else."

"Or they're looking for us." Her grip on Sebastian's arm tightened, not pulling, just anchoring. "There's a cut behind the old oyster beds. Deep enough to lie flat in, narrow enough they'll sweep over us. If we go now—"

"If we lie down in that cut with these plates," Sebastian said quietly, "we drown or we lose them. The tide's making. In an hour the cut is under a fathom."

Ilse was silent. The lantern swept again, farther off now, arcing toward the breakwater. Then her voice came, very close, amused and terrified at once: "You've thought about this. You've been thinking about this for twenty years."

"I've been thinking about you for twenty years," Sebastian said, and had no memory of deciding to say it, and could not take it back, and found—to their astonishment—that they did not want to.

The lantern died behind the breakwater. They went on.

* * *

*Ilse*

*You've been thinking about you for twenty years.* Ilse turned the words over in the dark like coins she suspected were foreign currency but hoped weren't. She had rehearsed a hundred conversations with Sebastian Oyelaran across seven years and four ports, and none of them had contained that sentence. None of them had needed to. It had been waiting here, in the mud and the cold water of home, the way everything true about Vellamare waited—patient, submerged, intact.

She followed Sebastian's shoulder through the dark. That was the remarkable thing, she thought; she had crossed borders with forged papers and false names, had learned three cities well enough to vanish inside them, and here she was reduced to a child's trust, following one shoulder, one sure hand pointing left at a rusted anchor chain she couldn't see.

"Here," Sebastian murmured. "Deep step. Give me your hand."

Ilse gave it. The hand was ink-stained even now, days after the last copying session, the calluses of a pen worn into the fingers like rings. She had held that hand once before—at twelve, pressed flat against the atlas's unfinished page while they swore the oath over it. Whoever finishes it finishes it for both of us. She remembered the exact weight of Aldous Oyelaran watching them from the doorway, saying nothing, letting children believe promises were simple.

Her mother's name existed nowhere in the world except on stolen plates in a captain's strongroom. Ilse had spent seven years learning that sentence's every syllable. In Marannsheim archives, in a refugee house in Sallow Port, in the letters of a dead woman's cousin who wrote back twice and then stopped writing forever—everywhere, the same absence, the same smooth official paper where Marchetti should have been. Her father had rebuilt his life on the erasure like a man building on sand, drinking himself quieter each year, unable to say why the family trade name shamed him. Her mother had gone into the purge's ledgers as a number. A confiscation line. *Goods and persons disposed per directive.*

Aldous Oyelaran had written her name in ink and hidden it in the atlas like a seed. And Sebastian had spent twenty years keeping it alive.

"Talk to me," Ilse said, because the silence was getting large enough to hold fear. "While we walk. Tell me what I'm standing in."

"The Seam," Sebastian said. "Aldous charted it in his own hand—never the survey copy. Six feet at mean low water at the mouth, shoaling to three at the bend, then dropping to nine where it meets the Cut. The army's charts show it as mudflat. Unnavigable." A pause, boots sucking free of the bottom. "He drew it wrong on purpose, forty times, in forty documents. Every official map in this harbor lies about this channel. That's how he hid the families' boats during the purge. Nobody looked for people in water that didn't exist."

Ilse laughed—a small, dangerous sound, muffled against her collar. "God. He must have loved it. The joke of it."

"He didn't laugh," Sebastian said. "But he kept redrawing it. Every year. I used to think it was obsession. Now I think it was defiance—he wanted to be caught doing it eventually. He wanted someone to have to look at forty maps and understand."

"And nobody looked."

"Nobody looked," Sebastian agreed softly, "until me."

The channel bent north. Ilse felt the current change against her legs, the sudden deeper pull Sebastian had promised, and ahead of them the sky opened slightly where the flats ended at the last shingle bar. Beyond it, invisible but present as a held breath, the sea. And on the sea, at the edge of the lightless horizon, a single riding lamp burning dim green at a masthead—the *Cormorant*'s signal, lit for ten minutes only, exactly as arranged.

"There," she said.

They climbed the shingle bar and lay flat among the stones, catching breath, and from this height Ilse could see the whole dark geometry of the harbor she had hated for leaving and loved for taking her back. The town above it, blind-windowed. The garrison's new barricade across the quay, pale as a scar. And the customs house, squat and certain at the harbor mouth, its windows dark—but as she watched, a lantern moved inside it. Slowly. Methodically. Someone walking the ground floor with a light, room by room, at midnight.

"Sebastian."

"I see it."

"Customs house is staffed. At this hour. That's not routine."

Sebastian was quiet for a moment, lying on their back among the stones, and Ilse watched their face resolve itself into the expression she was beginning to know—the cartographer's expression, the one that measured disaster in terms of scale and distance. "The gate," Sebastian said finally. "The tide gate under the customs house. It's the only way into the inner channels from open water without passing the quay. If Rennick's posted men there—"

"Then the *Cormorant* can't come to us. And we can't go to it."

"We can still get the plates out. Not tonight by water." Sebastian sat up slowly, and the green riding lamp out on the water began, at that moment, to die—ten minutes gone, the arrangement honored, the ship sliding away toward the horizon with its hold lighter than it should have been. Ilse watched it go and felt something in her chest go with it, some version of the night they'd planned, some version where this was hard but clean.

"So what do we do," she said. "Row out and knock?"

"No." Sebastian was unwinding the netting from the bundle, checking the plates with their fingertips, counting nine, then wrapping them again with quick, sure hands. "We do what Aldous did. We use water that doesn't exist." A pause. "There's a stone culvert under the customs house, older than the gate. Eighteenth century. It drains the tanner's race—it's dry at low water for maybe ninety minutes, and it comes out inside the harbor wall below the guard room. It's not on any map."

"Not on any map," Ilse repeated. "Except yours."

"Except mine."

She looked at this person she had sworn an oath with at twelve and found unrecognizable and recognizable in equal measure, kneeling on a shingle bar in the dark with nine rolled plates bound to their chest, proposing to walk a drain beneath the army's own fortress because it was the one road in Vellamare that existed only in truth. She thought about her mother's name in ink on sailcloth, eight inches from Sebastian's heart. She thought: *I could still turn back. I could still be nobody's daughter, in nobody's ledger, safe in the enormous anonymity of the sea.*

She thought: *No.*

"When's the next low water?" she asked.

"Four hours. We wait here, or—" Sebastian hesitated, and in the hesitation Ilse heard the whole weight of the next thing before it was said. "Or we go back to the maproom. There's a place. You'd be safe until the tide turns."

"Safe," Ilse said, tasting it. "In your cellar. Like the plates."

"It's not—"

"It's exactly that." But she said it gently, and she reached out and found their hand among the stones, and held it, the second time in their lives and the first time as adults, and the cold of it, the ink-callused reality of it, undid something in her that seven years of careful traveling had built. "Yes. The maproom. Yes."

They lay back down among the stones to wait for the tide, shoulder against shoulder, because walking back through the channels was no safer than waiting and the flats gave nothing away. Above them the stars came and went behind fast cloud. The patrol boat's lantern woke once more, far off, and swept the shallows they had crossed, and passed over, and Ilse felt Sebastian go rigid against her and then, deliberately, muscle by muscle, relax.

"They didn't see us," she whispered.

"No."

"How can you be sure?"

"Because Rennick is sure of everything," Sebastian said. "And when he's sure, he sleeps. And when he stops sleeping—" they turned their head, and Ilse felt more than saw their eyes, dark in the dark— "then we'll know it, the whole town will know it, the way you know weather."

* * *

*Sebastian*

The patrol lamp swept the shallows a third time, and Sebastian lay among the cold stones with Ilse's breath audible beside them and understood, with the terrible clarity of the tide-flats, that the lamp was not searching. It was pacing. A man who walks his rounds at the wrong hour is a clock showing the wrong time, and clocks show wrong for reasons: wound by another hand, set to another purpose. Rennick had moved the schedule tonight because tomorrow—or the day after—he intended to move something larger, and the harbor was being taught to accept his hours as natural.

Which meant the culvert plan was already dying. Which meant the *Cormorant* would not wait past the second attempt. Which meant—

"You've gone quiet in a different way," Ilse said.

"I'm redrawing the map."

"In your head."

"That's where I keep the real ones."

She made a sound that was almost a laugh. Then, after a while, in the voice people use for things said to the ceiling rather than the listener: "At the fish market, old Mother Ansa told me the purge families used to say a word for this. For moving at night through water that isn't on the maps. She wouldn't tell me the word. She said the word got people killed twice—once when they were found using it, once when they were found remembering it."

"Aldous had a word too," Sebastian said. "For the channels. He called them the honest lines. Everything else in the harbor was drawn to lie; these were the only drawings that told the truth, so they were the only ones that had to stay secret." They shifted the plates against their chest. "I used to think that was clever. Cartographer's wit. Now I think he was describing the whole town. Everyone honest had to go underground to survive being so."

Ilse's hand found theirs again in the dark and stayed.

They waited out the tide in the company of the stars, and when the water had fallen far enough they rose and went back the way they had come, through the Seam, past the drowned skiff, up the eleven steps cut in the cliff, two shadows carrying nine names and one mother's name and twenty years of unfinished business into the sleeping town.

At the top of the stair, before the lanes swallowed them, Sebastian paused. From Bell Row, one lamp had come alight in the night—the watch post at the corner, where the corporal was supposed to be asleep. It burned steady and unhurried, and beside its glow, briefly, silhouetted against the glass, a figure crossed the room. Tall. Uniformed. Not moving like a corporal.

Moving like a man taking measurements.

Sebastian stood in the dark and watched Captain Rennick's shadow cross the lamp, once, and return, and depart, and thought: *he knows the channels exist. He just doesn't know yet that they're ours.*

"Tomorrow," Ilse breathed.

"Tonight first," Sebastian said. "The tide won't wait, and neither will he. Low water's at four. The culvert gives us ninety minutes. We put the plates in the wall cavity below the guard room and signal the *Cormorant* on the next dark tide."

"And if he's already found the culvert?"

Sebastian looked at the lamp on Bell Row, at the town stacked above the harbor like a held breath, at the woman beside them who had crossed the world to ask a map for her mother's name.

"Then tomorrow," they said, "we learn what Aldous never had to: what it costs to be caught telling the truth."
