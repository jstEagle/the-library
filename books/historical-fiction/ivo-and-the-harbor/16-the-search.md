## 16. The Search

They came at the hour when the harbor fog thins and everything hidden feels briefly exposed—mid-morning, which was worse than dawn. Dawn at least would have had the decency of violence about it. This was procedure: six soldiers in garrison gray, boots on the stairs, and Sergeant Voss with a folded paper he did not bother to read aloud.

"By order of Captain Rennick," he said instead, tucking it back into his coat like a man returning a wallet he'd only borrowed to count, "these premises are subject to search for seditious materials."

I stood in the doorway of the shop with the day's first customers' silence still hanging around me—the shop had no customers anymore, not since the requisition, but I opened the shutters out of habit, and habit is its own kind of lie—and I said, "Of course," because what else does a mapmaker's apprentice say? What else have I said for twenty years?

*Come in. Look. There is nothing here but work.*

Voss posted two men at the door and sent the rest up. I followed them up the stairs the way you follow a coffin you're not permitted to carry.

* * *

The maproom has not changed substantially since Master Aldous died in it—or rather, since he died in the square below and I carried his last unfinished plate up these stairs with my hands shaking so badly I left a thumbprint in wet ink that is still there, on the northwest corner of the harbor survey, under glass now, the way relics are kept. Twenty years of my life are arranged in this room with cartographic precision: the flat files with their brass corners, the drying rack where plates hang like flayed skins, the long south window through which the harbor light comes in the afternoon and turns everything the color of old honey.

And behind all of it—the wall.

I will describe the wall once, here, because after this morning I will never be able to think of it calmly again. It is the north wall, load-bearing, stone older than the town charter. Against it stands the flat cabinet where the original copper plates sleep in their felt slots, all forty-one of them, each one carrying names that exist nowhere else on earth. In front of the cabinet hangs Aldous's memorial portrait—painted by Ilse's mother, as it happens, though she signed it with another name and was dead within the year, which is the kind of arithmetic this town forces you to do whether you want to or not. The portrait shows him at fifty, spectacles pushed up into gray hair, one hand resting on an open atlas, looking slightly past the painter as if the future were standing just over her shoulder.

The portrait is hung on a hinge. Aldous installed the hinge himself, years before the purge, back when it was only ever going to hold charts of tide tables. He showed me the mechanism when I was fourteen and swore me to secrecy with a solemnity I mistook for play. *A mapmaker's first loyalty*, he said, *is to the blank spaces.*

The soldiers did not know about the hinge. But they knew how to look at walls, and I learned in those forty minutes exactly how much terror a body can hold while standing perfectly still.

* * *

They began, professionally, with the flat files.

Corporal Dane—I knew his mother; she buys her oil from the chandler on Harrow Street, and his father drowned off the point when he was four—pulled each drawer and rifled it with the exaggerated thoroughness of a man who wants his sergeant to see him working. Charts slid out in fanned cascades. He checked the margins for writing, held a few up against the window light looking for watermarks, and put them back wrong, always wrong, the coastlines of my twenty years shuffled like a deck he didn't know how to read. Every chart misfiled was a small wound. I catalogued the wounds to keep from screaming: the Sound survey filed under outer banks, the estuary soundings reversed, the 1809 coastal plate laid face-down beneath a stack of decorative commissions Aldous took to pay for ink.

"You do beautiful work," Dane said, not unkindly, holding up a pen-and-wash of the harbor at dusk that I had made the winter I turned nineteen, when I still believed finishing the atlas would feel like triumph.

"The master's work," I said. "I only finish it."

"That's what they say." He set it down carefully, more carefully than he'd taken anything else out. "That you finish things."

I let that pass through me and out the other side, the way I let the cold of the harbor pass through me on winter surveys. Twenty years of practice. But my heart was doing something ugly and arrhythmic against my ribs, hammering out a message I could not let my face receive: *behind the portrait, behind the portrait, behind the portrait.*

Private Okafor was at the drying rack, flipping through the hanging proofs with two fingers like a man shuffling through laundry. Another was going through the desk drawers, finding Aldous's nibs, my burnisher, the tin of Chinese ink Aldous guarded like scripture and which I have used sparingly for two decades so it would last until the final plate was done. It is nearly empty now. I finished with it three nights ago. I did not know then what a mercy that timing would come to seem.

And Voss stood in the middle of the room with his arms crossed, turning slowly, the way a man turns when he is not looking for something so much as listening for it.

"Big room," he said.

"It's a workshop."

"Workshop above a shop above a harbor." He turned another slow quarter-circle. "Master worked here twenty years before you, yes? And you twenty after. Forty years of paper in one room." He looked at me for the first time since coming up the stairs. "Where's the rest?"

"There is no rest. What was seized—" I kept my voice level, kept it in the register I use with customers asking why charts cost what they cost, "—was the ledgers. You have the ledgers. Everything else is what you see."

"Mm," Voss said, in the tone of a man filing something away, and gestured at the wall. "What's behind that?"

*Behind the portrait.* My heart did not hammer then; it stopped, entirely, a held breath in the shape of a man. "Stone," I said. "Load-bearing. Older than the charter—the whole row shares it. Master Aldous wanted to hang the survey plates on it once and couldn't get anchors to bite; the mortar crumbles like biscuit. That's why the cabinet stands off it." A gesture toward the cabinet, casual, the gesture of a man with nothing behind any of his words. "Damp ruins copper. We keep everything off the north wall."

It was true. Every word of it was true, which is the best way to lie. Voss crossed to the wall and rapped it twice with his knuckles—stone answered, dense and dead—and then he looked at the portrait.

He looked at it for a long time.

I watched him look at Aldous's face and I thought, with a clarity that felt almost like hallucination: *he is deciding whether a dead man's eyes can testify.* The painted Aldous gazed past Voss's shoulder at nothing, at the future, at me, and Voss studied him the way he might study a suspect, and the room held its breath with me.

"Who painted it?" he asked.

"A local artist. Dead now."

"Name?"

"I never knew her true name. She signed with a mark." This too was true. Ilse's mother had signed with a small chalk-white spiral, the old symbol, in the lower corner where only someone cleaning the frame would find it. I found it the year after her death and understood, finally, that she had known exactly what she was painting and for whom the room would someday need to keep secrets.

Voss touched the frame. Lifted it half an inch on its wire, testing the weight—

—and the hinge took the weight silently, as it was built to, the portrait swinging not at all, giving nothing, and Voss saw only an ordinary heavy picture on an ordinary nail over an ordinary cabinet, and set it down.

"Copper plates," he mused. "You said damp ruins copper."

"Felt-lined. Cedar. The cabinet is thirty years old and airtight as a coffin." I smiled. I actually smiled, some muscle in my face betraying me into craft. "Would you like to see inside? They're mostly blanks and rejects. The army already took anything worth reading."

He considered it. I watched him consider it—watched the calculation move behind his eyes like weather—and I understood that if he opened that cabinet I would have perhaps ten seconds of watching before the shouting started, and that whatever came after ten seconds would end every plan Ilse and I had built in the maproom's lamplight, would end the spar waiting in the smuggler's hold, would end Ilse's mother's name in whatever place names go when they are never spoken.

He waved a hand. "Leave it. If it's in there, it'll keep."

*If it's in there.* He said it looking at me.

* * *

They took the desk apart. They took the floorboards up in the corner where the damp had warped them—found nothing but ballast stones and a dead mouse, mummified, which Okafor held up by its tail and regarded with philosophical disgust before dropping it back into the dark. They went through the loft above the drying rack, where I slept, such as I slept, and shook out my blankets and read my single letter from a chart-seller in Corvane and put it back folded differently. They tapped every panel. One of them—new, young, cheeks still holding their training-ground color—knelt and peered up the chimney with a lantern and reported, faintly apologetic, "Birds' nests, Sergeant."

Through all of it I stood by the south window with my hands folded, the posture of a man patiently inconvenienced, and inside I was a bell that had been struck once and could not stop ringing. Not fear exactly—or yes, fear, but fear with a strange clarity inside it, the way panic at sea sometimes arrives with perfect vision. I could see the room as they saw it and as it was simultaneously: the surface of the chart and the depths beneath it, and myself standing on the boundary line I had drawn and lived on for twenty years, the line between what a room shows and what it keeps.

Ilse had been gone from the maproom for six days. Six days, and the felt slots in the cabinet still held the ghost-shape of the three plates we had copied onto sailcloth, rolled, and sealed in wax. Six days, and the chalk mark I had redrawn on the customs house door was either washed away by rain or read by soldiers, I didn't know which. Six days, and somewhere out past the molehead a smuggler named Corvo waited on a schedule that did not wait, and Rennick's proclamation was drying on the customs house wall, and half the doors in town wore the spiral, and none of us knew which neighbor had drawn which.

Voss's men finished the way professionals finish, badly satisfied, the way dogs finish with a hole that smells of old burrow and no rabbit. They stacked my charts wronger than they'd found them. Dane paused at the door and gave me a nod that might have meant anything—might have meant *sorry*, might have meant *I know*, might have meant nothing at all but a young man's discomfort with what his uniform makes him do to rooms where people live—and then they were down the stairs, boots receding into the street, and the shop door closed, and the room was mine again and utterly changed.

* * *

I did not go to the wall at once. That is a discipline Aldous taught me without knowing it: never rush to confirm your treasure; the first thing a watcher learns about you is what you look at first. So I began righting the charts, refiling the Sound survey, setting the 1809 coastal plate face-up where it belonged, and my hands shook only a little, and only at the edges.

Then I crossed the room, took down the portrait, and opened the hinge.

The cabinet breathed out its cedar smell. Felt slots. Copper sleeping in felt. All forty-one plates, undisturbed, their engraved lines catching the window light like the veins of leaves. Names in the negative—cut away into metal, the letters hollow, so that when ink is rolled across the plate and paper pressed down, the names rise up out of the whiteness as if surfacing from underwater. *Marchetti. Ferro. Oyelaran.* I had spent three nights engraving the last of them and I had wept while I worked, quietly, the way you weep at sea, without letting it interrupt the stroke.

I closed the hinge. Hung the portrait. Aldous looked past my shoulder at the future, and for the first time in twenty years I looked directly back at him and thought: *we are running out of future to hide in.*

* * *

Rennick came at dusk. Not with soldiers—with a corporal and a lantern, and alone up the stairs, which was somehow more invasive than the six-man search had been. He entered the maproom the way he enters every room: as though it had been awaiting him and was slightly disappointed by the standard of its preparation.

He walked the perimeter once, unhurried. Touched the flat files. Ran a finger along the drying rack. Stood before the portrait for a moment—less time than Voss had, which frightened me more—and then turned to me, and the lantern light made his face half-lit and half-dark, a coast at dusk.

"They found nothing," he said. It wasn't an accusation. It was almost admiring. "Six men, four hours, and a room that has been the center of this harbor's memory for forty years, and they found a dead mouse." He picked up a nib from the desk, examined it against the light, set it back precisely where it had lain. "You know, in the capital they teach us that towns like this are simple. Ledgers, harbors, fish. It's the quiet ones who turn out to be complicated." He set the nib down. "Your master cut the names out of his ledgers with a razor, stitch by stitch, before they ever took him. Do you know what that costs a man? To sit in his own shop, night after night, mutilating his own life's work with steady hands? It takes either a fanatic or a craftsman." His eyes moved over my face the way a reader's eye moves over a page. "Or both."

"Captain," I said, "if there's a question in there—"

"There isn't. Not today." He smiled, thin as a coastline on a small-scale chart. "Today I only came to tell you that I'll be back. Perhaps next week. Perhaps with the same men, perhaps with better ones. Perhaps with a reader." He let the word sit in the room. *Reader.* The harbormaster's warning, spoken aloud in the voice I had dreaded hearing it in. "Ink remembers what knives cut out, apprentice. Pressure and paper. Everything your master carved away is still pressed into the pages underneath, and there are men who can raise it, the way you raise a name off a plate." He moved toward the stairs, then paused at the landing, half-turned, the lantern throwing his shadow long across my charts.

"One more thing. The Marchetti girl." He said the name the way you'd drop a stone into a well, to hear how deep it went. "When she surfaces—and she will surface; people always come home eventually—you'd do well to remember whose shop sits between her and the sea."

Then he was gone, and the stairwell swallowed his footsteps, and the harbor light died by degrees outside the south window until the room was lit only by the lamp I hadn't dared light yet.

I stood in the dark beside the wall with my hand flat against the portrait's frame, feeling through wood and hinge and cedar and felt the cold weight of the copper, and I understood with total simplicity what the day had been. Not a search survived. A countdown begun. Survival was never survival—it was postponement, and postponement has a rate of interest, and Rennick had just named the currency.

Three plates were already at sea. Thirty-eight still slept behind Aldous's painted gaze.

I had one night, maybe fewer, and a copyist's hands, and a promise older than either of the men who wanted to collect it.
