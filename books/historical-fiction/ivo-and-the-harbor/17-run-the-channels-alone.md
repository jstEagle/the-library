## 17. Run the Channels Alone

Ilse counted the soldiers on the quay the way her mother had once counted coins at the end of a market day—by touch, by habit, without looking directly at them. Four at the customs house door. Two more lounging against the fish racks with the studied idleness of men paid to loiter. A patrol boat riding at anchor in the roadstead, its lantern doused but its silhouette unmistakable against the last grey of evening.

And across the square, in the upper window of the mapshop, one small lamp burning. Sebastian's lamp. The garrison had let it burn because they wanted it to burn; two of Rennick's men had taken up residence in the cooper's shed across the lane three days ago, and their attention never wavered from that window, that door, that stooped shadow moving behind the glass.

They thought the atlas lived in the maproom. They were right, or right enough. And so they watched the maproom, and the maproom was watched, and every plan Ilse and Sebastian had made together had narrowed to a single channel of possibility.

"You're going to say no," she had told him that afternoon, in the low voice they used now even when no one could hear, the voice of people who had learned that walls in Vellamare had grown ears. "You're going to tell me to wait."

"I'm going to tell you to wait," Sebastian had said, not looking up from the sailcloth spread across the drafting table, the copied plates in Sebastian's careful hand, names rising out of the treated fabric like things surfacing from deep water. "The run needs two people. It was always meant for two. One to sound the channel, one to carry."

"The run needs one person who knows the water," Ilse said. "I've run it four times now. I know it the way you know it—because you taught me, and because I listened." She had put her hand flat over his, over the sailcloth, over the name of a woman she had crossed the sea to find and found instead here, in ink, in Aldous Oyelaran's patient script: *Marchetti, Liora — taken 14th of the Frost Month. Her line survives.* Her line survives. She was the line. That sentence had carried her across two oceans and it would carry her through one more night of cold water. "You're watched. I'm not. Not yet. Rennick doesn't know my face, only my name, and half this town still thinks the Marchettis drowned."

Sebastian had looked up then, and what was in that face was worse than fear. It was arithmetic. Twenty years of survival had taught Sebastian to weigh lives like cargo, and Ilse could see the scales working—the plates, the testimony, the smuggler waiting one more tide at the far anchorage, the whole fragile machinery of their plan balanced against the risk of letting her go alone into channels that shifted with every storm.

"If anything goes wrong—" Sebastian began.

"Nothing will go wrong," Ilse said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and knew too that some lies are load-bearing, that you build the night on top of them and hope they hold.

* * *

She went down to the water before moonrise, along the route Sebastian had drilled into her until it lived in her feet: out the yard, through the widow Brenner's garden where the fence post leaned at exactly the angle of a sleeping man's shoulder, down the stair cut into the cliff behind the smokehouse that no map but Aldous's ever showed. The tide was three hours past full and falling fast, and the harbor exhaled around her—that long, low sigh of water draining off the flats, the sound Sebastian called the harbor breathing out, the sound that meant the channels were opening.

At the bottom of the stair the mudflats stretched black and gleaming under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Somewhere out there, invisible in the dark, lay the seam she needed: the deep channel that ran behind the breakwater, past the old powder hulk, out through the gap in the outer rocks where the smuggler *Kestrel's Mercy* waited with its hold ready to swallow forty years of stolen truth. The copy was finished. Rolled in waxed canvas, wrapped in oilskin, lashed inside a hollowed spar that would float even if the boat sank—it was already loaded in the skiff she'd hidden two nights ago among the pilings below the ruined net-dry. All she had to do was take the skiff through the channel at slack-low water, when the patrol boat drew too much to follow, and make the far anchorage by the third hour after midnight.

Simple. Sebastian had drawn it simple, in the clean confident lines of the master's style, and Ilse had believed the drawing, because believing Sebastian's maps was the oldest faith she had.

She stripped to her shift, bundled her clothes into the oilskin with the plates' duplicate manifest, and waded in. The water took her breath the way it always did, first as shock and then as something almost like recognition—the sea remembering her, and she it. She had been swimming these waters at seven years old, before the purge, before her mother vanished into a ledger's cut page, before her father put her on a boat and told her that forgetting was a kind of inheritance too. Her body knew the cold and moved through it without asking permission.

She reached the skiff, hauled herself aboard, and lay low between the thwarts while she counted the lights of the town. The mapshop lamp still burned. The customs house lanterns made a yellow smear on the fog coming off the water. No movement on the patrol boat. She slid the spar into place along the keel line, checked the lashings twice, and took up the oars.

The channel announced itself before she saw it—a change in the water's texture, the flat black of the shallows giving way to a slow, muscular ripple where the ebb ran deep. She shipped the oars and used the pole, as Sebastian had taught her, reading depth by feel the way Aldous must have done, thirty and forty years ago, alone in a rowboat with a lead line and a notebook full of names he would die rather than surrender. *Six feet,* she thought, pressing the pole down. *Seven.* The old powder hulk rose against the sky on her left, its ribs open like a whale's, and she slipped past it into the stretch Sebastian called the Throat, where the current quickened and the rocks stood close enough on either side that she could have touched them with an oar.

Here, in the dark, in the cold, with the harbor's whole history draining out to sea around her, Ilse felt something she had not allowed herself to feel since she'd come home: joy. Fierce, terrified, entirely unreasonable joy. Because this was her mother's water. Liora Marchetti had fished these channels before they were erased from every chart in Vellamare, and tonight her daughter was running them, carrying her name back out toward the world in Aldous Oyelaran's hand. The purge had taken everything except the water. The water remembered.

She was through the Throat. The gap in the outer rocks opened ahead of her, a corridor of starlit foam, and beyond it the open sea breathed long and slow, and somewhere out in that darkness rode the answer to twenty years of silence.

That was when the lamp came on.

It swept from the customs house—not the harbor side but the seaward gallery, where no light ought to have been—and caught her mid-stroke, pinned her to the black water like a moth to a board. For one heartbeat she sat frozen, and in that heartbeat she understood three things with terrible clarity. First, that the lamp had not been searching; it had been waiting. Second, that the skiff was too heavy on the ebb to turn before the current carried her into the rocks, and if she turned anyway she would be smashed on the outer ledges within sight of the garrison. Third—

Third, that the customs house door had opened, and men were coming down the seaward stair, and there were more of them than chance would ever have gathered.

They had known. Not guessed—known. The channel, the tide, the hour. Someone had given her to Rennick, and the trap had not been built for the plates at all.

The first soldier's boot hit the lowest step and sent up a sheet of spray, and Ilse did the only thing left that was hers to do. She pulled hard for the gap, once, twice, driving the skiff toward open water, making them chase her, making them see her flee—so that every eye, every lantern, every report filed under Captain Rennick's methodical hand would say that the girl in the boat had been trying to escape with contraband, that whatever she carried was in the boat, that nothing remained ashore. She let them see her run. It was the last gift she could send back up the channel, through the Throat, past the powder hulk, to the lit window above the harbor: *they are watching me, so they are not watching you.*

A rope snaked out from the rocks—there were men *in* the rocks, men who had waited in the cold black water like herons—and caught the bow. The skiff slewed. Hands seized her arms, cruel and competent, and someone wrenched the spar loose from the keel and cracked it open against the stone like a lobster.

It was empty. She had moved the roll an hour ago, at the last minute, in a fit of caution she had not examined until now: re-lashed inside her bundled clothes, which she had stowed not in the skiff but wedged beneath the net-dry stairs where she'd changed. The plates were not in the boat. The soldiers stared into the broken spar, and then at her, and their faces changed as understanding dawned in them slower than it had in her.

"Where is it?" the sergeant said.

Ilse Marchetti, standing in a seized skiff in her wet shift in the freezing dark, smiled at him with her mother's smile—the one the ledgers had cut out of existence and that no captain's men had ever seen.

"In the sea," she said. "Where all your ledgers will end up."

* * *

They walked her up the seaward stair with her hands bound, and the strangest part, she would think later, was how ordinary the customs hall looked from the inside. She had passed it a hundred times since coming home, had sold fish across its very threshold, and had never once imagined its interior: the long counter scarred by a century of cargo hooks, the wall of pigeonhole shelves where manifests slept in their hundreds, the iron stove ticking in the corner, and above it all the portrait of some forgotten commissioner glaring down at the room with painted disapproval. Now there were tables set across the middle of the floor, and papers on them, and lamps, and Captain Rennick seated behind the papers as though he had been sitting there for years and merely awaiting the arrival of the evening's business.

He looked at her for a long moment. Water ran off her shift and pooled on his floor, and he let it pool, and she understood that the discomfort was deliberate, a first question asked without words.

"Ilse Marchetti," he said at last, not as a greeting but as an entry in a ledger. He wrote it down. She watched him write it, watched the letters of her erased name take shape in a government hand, and felt the vertigo of seeing the impossible happen twice: once when Aldous had restored her mother to ink, and now when the enemy did the same, for opposite reasons. "Daughter of Liora. You've come a very great distance to drown in a harbor channel."

"I came home," Ilse said.

"Yes." He set down the pen and folded his hands, and his voice stayed level and almost gentle, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "So did the woman on the fishing boat with the false manifest, three weeks ago. So did the person who has spent those three weeks copying stolen documents onto sailcloth in a maproom I have generously allowed to remain open." He let that sit in the ticking quiet of the stove. "You see, I knew about the channels before you ever swam them. Master Aldous surveyed them; therefore they exist in his notes; therefore they exist in my custody. I have simply been waiting to learn which direction you would run them. Tonight you were kind enough to demonstrate."

The cold she had stopped feeling on the water came back all at once, from the inside. But her mind kept working beneath the fear, cold and clear as the channel itself, and it found the flaw in his certainty and pressed on it. If he had known everything—the channels, the copying, the plan entire—he would not have needed to catch her. He would have taken the maproom while she swam. He had let the lamp find her because catching her served another purpose, and she watched his face and understood it one heartbeat before he spoke.

"The plates you copied," Rennick said. "The originals. I want them, and I want the person who made the copies, and I am prepared to be generous about the order in which I receive them." He drew a paper toward himself, unhurried. "You will remain my guest. And tomorrow morning, your friend the apprentice will receive a message proposing terms. I mention this now so that you may spend the night considering how you'll feel, should your silence cost them everything they've built."

"They won't trade," Ilse said, and hated how thin her voice came out.

"No," Rennick agreed mildly, returning to his paperwork. "I suspect they won't. People rarely do, until the price is standing in front of them wearing fewer clothes than yours and dripping on my floor." He did not look up. "Take her down to the holding room. Dry clothes. She is to be treated well—she is worth a great deal."

They led her past the counter, past the pigeonholes with their sleeping manifests, and at the door she turned, once, to look back through the tall windows toward the harbor. Fog had closed over the water. The mapshop lamp still burned in the upper window, small and stubborn, and she thought of Sebastian bent over the drafting table at this very moment, waiting for her return, counting the hours toward a rendezvous that would never be kept.

Let them believe the plates were lost in the channel, she prayed, to no god in particular, to the water itself. Let them search the sea. Let every eye stay fixed on me.

Above the harbor, in the window that had burned all her life like a promise, the lamp went dark.
