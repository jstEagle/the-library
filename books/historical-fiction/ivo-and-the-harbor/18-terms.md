## 18. Terms

They brought Sebastian to the customs hall at dusk, which was a courtesy, and that courtesy frightened them more than manacles would have.

No escort gripped their arms. No one called out the charge. A corporal simply appeared at the maproom door as the light failed, cap in hand, and said, "The captain asks for you," in the tone of a man inviting someone to dinner. Sebastian set down the pen they had been holding—set it down carefully, parallel to the rule, because their hands wanted to shake and precision was the only lie left available to them—and went.

The customs hall had been transformed in ten days. Where harbor ledgers had once lain open on long tables for any clerk to consult, there were now crates. Rennick's crates, army-stamped, the same ones Sebastian had watched carried out of the shop like coffins. Sconces had been hung at intervals along the walls so that the whole room burned with even, indifferent light, the light of an empire that did not need shadows to work. At the far end, beneath the old harbor master's seal—a compass rose worn nearly smooth by two centuries of thumbs—Captain Rennick sat at a desk that was not his, reading.

He let Sebastian stand for a while. It was not cruelty; it was measurement. When he finally looked up, his face was exactly as it had been at every encounter: mild, attentive, almost kind, the face of a country doctor.

"Apprentice Oyelaran." He pronounced it correctly, which meant he had practiced. "Sit, please."

There was one chair, placed opposite him, angled to catch the light. Sebastian sat. Under the desk, out of Rennick's sightline, their knee pressed against solid oak, and they used the small pain the way Aldous had taught them to use a fixed point at sea—to hold position while everything else moved.

"You're wondering why no soldiers came to your workshop tonight." Rennick closed the ledger before him—not one of Aldous's; something administrative—and folded his hands on it. "I'll tell you plainly, because I find plainness saves everyone time. I know what you are. Not the story the town tells itself about the quiet mapmaker who keeps the shop open out of sentiment. I mean what you actually are, and have been, for twenty years."

"And what is that?" Sebastian asked. Their voice held. Twenty years of practice, and their voice held.

"The keeper," Rennick said, "of everything I was sent here to find."

* * *

Rennick had spent the afternoon with the ink-reader, and the afternoon had gone badly, and he did not intend for anyone in this room to know it.

The man's name was Corvus, and he worked like a surgeon performing an autopsy on a body that refused to confess its cause of death. He had lamps, magnifiers, raking light, a solution of gall that lifted ghost-ink from paper the way a dog lifts scent from stone. Three names in nine days. He had promised six by the month's end, but Rennick had watched the sweat on the man's temples and understood the arithmetic: the deeper cuts yielded nothing. Aldous Oyelaran had not merely sliced the names from his ledgers. He had razored through three sheets beneath each one, dampened the pages, pressed them under weight until the wounds healed into scars indistinguishable from ordinary wear. The master had known, in the end, exactly how much time he had, and had spent every hour of it well.

Three names. One of them Marchetti—Serafina Marchetti, reconstructed letter by agonized letter from the impression her name had left in the paper below. Ilse Marchetti's mother, sitting in a crate fifty feet from where Rennick now offered tea to her daughter's oldest friend.

That was the other thing Rennick knew and did not say: he knew the woman taken at the customs house two nights ago had come off a fishing boat under a false manifest; he knew the manifest was false because he had written the proclamation that made her false; and he knew—had known since the chalk mark appeared on the customs house door, old purge-code drawn fresh—that the apprentice and the enemy were braided together somewhere deeper than coincidence could reach. He did not say it because saying it would spend the knowledge, and he had learned, over thirty years of soldiering, that some coin is worth more unspent.

What he had instead was an offer. He had drafted it four times. The first three drafts had been threats wearing offers' clothes. The fourth was honest, and honesty, he had found, disarmed people who had spent lifetimes expecting the opposite.

"Before we speak of terms," he said aloud, "you should understand your position accurately. Not the position the town imagines—the brave keeper of secrets facing down the empire. The actual one." He turned the mutilated ledger around and pushed it across the desk. "Do you know what this is, from where I sit? It's a lock. And locks only have value if what's behind them still exists somewhere. Your master died believing it existed nowhere—in his head, or nowhere at all. But I don't believe in nowhere, Apprentice. I've searched too many houses. Somebody always kept something."

Sebastian looked at the ledger without touching it. The wound where a name had been was visible even in this light, a pale seam in the page like an old scar on skin.

"You've had my reader working nine days," Rennick continued. "He has recovered three names. He believes there were—" he consulted a card, though he did not need to; he knew the number the way he knew his own rank "—forty-one families erased in the Vellamare purge. Forty-one. Some of their descendants are in this town right now, living under borrowed names, and every day those borrowed names hold is a day I choose not to test them. Do you follow me? I am telling you that I have been patient, and that my patience is a resource, and resources are subject to requisition like everything else in this town."

"You requisitioned the bread ovens last week," Sebastian said. "And the church bell. And Mrs. Ferro's second floor."

"I did." No shame in it; he offered none. "War doesn't ask permission. That's rather the definition." He rose, walked to the window that faced the harbor, and stood with his hands clasped behind him—a man showing his back deliberately, another form of courtesy, or theater, and after twenty years of Master Aldous Sebastian knew the two were often indistinguishable. "Here are my terms. Listen all the way through before you answer, because I will not make this offer twice."

* * *

The terms were these:

The original plates—the true plates, the final pages of the atlas with every restored name, every erased family, every hiding place, delivered intact into army custody. In exchange: Ilse Marchetti released within the hour, passage on any neutral vessel she named, papers under whatever name she chose, and a written guarantee—Rennick's own signature, witnessed—that she would never be pursued. And more than that. More, Rennick said, turning from the window with the mild face of a man offering a reasonable price for a horse: protection. For the hidden families. If the atlas came to him quietly, there would be no searches, no house-to-house, no testing of borrowed names. The knowledge would rest in army archives, sealed, and Vellamare could go on pretending its silence was innocence.

"They will be safer in a sealed archive than they are in your memory," Rennick said. "Memories get people killed. Archives gather dust. Think of it as"—he paused, selecting—"a lighthouse. The light exists whether anyone looks at it or not. What matters is that the rocks are marked."

"And the forty-one families," Sebastian said slowly, "learn their true names exist in a military archive, indexed, retrievable, one decree away from the next purge. You're asking me to build the instrument of the next erasure and call it safety."

"I'm asking you to buy a life," Rennick said. "One life, that I know you value, plus the lives of everyone you've spent twenty years protecting. Versus the alternative, which is that I keep Corvus working, recover the names myself, take the plates anyway when I eventually search your workshop properly, and hang the Marchetti woman on the customs house quay as an enemy courier. Those are the two futures available to you. There is no third future where you outwit me, Apprentice, because I have read your master's file, and I know how this ends. It ended for him exactly this way. He also believed he had a third future."

The sconces hissed. Somewhere below, in the cells, Sebastian thought they could hear the sea—or imagined it, which amounted to the same thing; they had lived their whole life within earshot of that water, and their heart had learned its rhythm so thoroughly that silence itself now broke like a tide against them.

"One night," Sebastian said.

Rennick's eyebrows rose a fraction.

"A night to decide. You gave Master Aldous no night. You gave him a trial that lasted forty minutes." They kept their voice level, kept their eyes on his, kept the fixed point. "You know what I am. Then you know what I am worth only when my work is finished. Give me the night, and in the morning you'll have your answer and the plates—whichever answer that is. Refuse me the night, and you'll have nothing but a locked workshop and a girl in a cell and three names out of forty-one, and Corvus sweating over ghosts until the war ends and someone else signs your orders."

It was insolence dressed as logic, and both of them knew it, and both of them let it stand.

Rennick considered. Not long—he was not a man who performed deliberation—but long enough that Sebastian saw, in the lamplight, something move behind the mildness. Recognition. The look of a chess player finding, in an opponent he had dismissed, a familiar style of play. A dead man's style.

"One night," Rennick said. "You'll be escorted home. The workshop will not be entered. On my word—which you have no reason to trust, and will have to trust anyway, because it's the only currency I deal in. Sunrise, Apprentice. Come to me at sunrise."

* * *

The walk home took eleven minutes and lasted a year.

The corporal fell back half a street behind, out of courtesy or surveillance, and Sebastian moved through Vellamare as if through the pages of their own atlas: the cooper's lane, the cutwater, the stairs above the fish market where the smell of brine and tar had been the smell of childhood. Every doorway was a name. That was the thing Rennick would never understand, would never be able to confiscate: twenty years of walking this town had been twenty years of reading it, and every shuttered window belonged to somebody whose grandmother's name lay razor-cut from a ledger and re-inked in Sebastian's hand on a plate behind a false wall. Mrs. Ferro, who sold mussels and sang off-key. Old Tam, the netmender, whose father had been a Vellamare-born pilot before he became no one. The boy who swept the customs steps. The names rose up around Sebastian in the dark like buoys marking a channel, and every one of them was a reason, and every one of them was a weight.

Ilse was one of the weights too. That was the unbearable part—that she was not separate from the rest. If love had pointed one clean direction and duty another, the choice would have been hard but legible, a coastline you could chart. But Ilse *was* the atlas; she had put her twelve-year-old palm flat on the unfinished map and sworn it. Her mother's name existed in one place in the world, and that place was Sebastian's making. To save her by surrendering the plates was to hand her mother's name to the men who had erased her. To protect the name was to leave her in a cell until Corvus found it himself and used it against them both.

Sebastian climbed the stairs to the maproom, lit one lamp, and stood a long time among the smells of linseed and old paper, listening to the harbor breathe.

Then they began to work.

Not packing. Not deciding. Working—the only prayer they had ever learned. They pulled the memorial portrait from the wall, opened the false panel, and lifted out the original plates one by one, laying them across the drafting table like a spread of cards, like a life dealt face-up. Forty-one families. Dates of the purge. The channels. The names, restored in Aldous's hand and continued in their own, twenty years of a single continuous line of ink.

They looked at the plates for a long time. And then, with the terrible calm that comes at the center of a storm, Sebastian reached for a fresh sheet of treated sailcloth, wet the pen, and began—not to copy.

To finish.

If Rennick wanted the atlas at sunrise, Sebastian thought, dipping the pen, watching the first new stroke of ink take the light—then Rennick would have the atlas. All of it. Every name, every date, every crime, bound in the master's style and signed in the apprentice's hand.

He had asked for the wrong version. By sunrise, Sebastian intended to give him the right one.
