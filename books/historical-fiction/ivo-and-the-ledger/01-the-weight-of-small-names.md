## 1. The Weight of Small Names

The morning report arrived at seven o'clock, as it always did, folded twice and smelling faintly of the Colonel's tobacco, and it lied at once, as it always did.

I had learned to read the thing the way one reads a fortune in tea leaves: not for what it said but for what it had been made to say. *Ward stable. Fevers diminishing. One death recorded, Private Hollis, cause: putrefaction of wounds.* All true, in the way that a portrait is true—it captures the nose and misses the man entirely. Because three beds down from Private Hollis lay a boy of twenty-two named Ellery Finch, who had died at half past four that morning with his hand in mine and his last word so small I nearly missed it, and there was no mention of him anywhere in those neat columns. Not his name. Not his regiment. Not even a discreet dash where a number might have been. As far as the War Office was concerned, Ellery Finch had never been born, never enlisted, never bled out on a table while I counted his pulse down like a clock running backwards.

They had struck him from the roll before his body was cold. That took organization. That took ink and intention.

"More water over here, Miss Dunmore?" asked Tilly, my junior, who was seventeen and still believed that most problems were failures of hydration.

"In a moment." I tucked the report under my arm and smiled at her, because the alternative was to explain something I did not yet have words for, and Tilly's faith in water was one of the few unspoiled things in this building. "Yes. Bring the pitcher."

Our hospital had been a wool merchant's warehouse in some luckier decade, all brick and tall windows, and when the war swallowed it whole the army had bolted sixty cots between the pillars and called it a ward. Attached to its eastern wall, sharing our chimney like a greedy sibling, stood the printing workshop—Fenwick's establishment—which produced drill manuals, ration notices, and the elegant official fictions of the morning report itself. Some days the wall between us grew warm, and I liked to imagine that if I pressed my ear to the bricks I could hear the type being set, each letter dropping into its groove like a soldier into ranks. On other days I thought about how easy it was to make a machine that repeats whatever you feed it. A press does not know it is lying. It only knows how to multiply.

Ellery Finch's cot had already been stripped by the orderlies. That was the custom: bed linens washed before grief could settle into them, as though sorrow were a stain and laundry a cure. I stood looking at the bare mattress for longer than was useful, and then I went to do my work, which was not nursing—not this particular hour's work—but the other kind, the secret kind, the kind that would have seen me dismissed without character if anyone knew and possibly worse than dismissed if certain people knew.

My sewing basket sat on the shelf in the linen room, between a stack of pillowcases and a jar of laudanum spoons. Beneath the basket's false bottom—a clever arrangement of wood and canvas that Fenwick had built me two winters ago, saying only, *For your threads, miss,* with a face that told me he knew precisely what threads I meant—lay my ledger.

It was an ordinary enough object. Bound in gray cloth, quarter-ruled, the sort of book any quartermaster might use for counting sacks of oats. I had bought it for twopence at a stationer's in town precisely because it looked like nothing. Inside, in my own careful hand, were the names.

I turned to the page marked with a pin through its top corner—the pin meaning *unfinished business*, which in wartime is nearly all business—and wrote:

*Ellery Finch, private, 4th Company, attached garrison hospital. Died 14th October, four-thirty in the morning. Wounds received at Harrow Ford, six weeks prior. Age approximately twenty-two. Mother living, address unknown; he spoke of a plum orchard.*

And beneath that, because I had developed the habit, the way one develops a limp or a taste for gin:

*Omitted from morning report of same date. Entered by no officer. Struck by no signature I can find.*

The ledger held forty-one names now. Forty-one men who had died in this ward or passed through it on their way to dying elsewhere, whose deaths existed nowhere in the official record except as arithmetic that didn't add up—if one were crude enough to check the arithmetic, which nobody was, because checking was the one activity the entire apparatus of the war had been designed to prevent. I did not yet know who did the striking. I knew only that it happened with suspicious tidiness: a name here, a name there, gone like chalk marks after rain. And I knew that whoever kept the true count would be in trouble for it, should the keeping ever come to light, and so the ledger lived under a sewing basket and I lived behind a face that was very good at appearing to think about nothing but bandages.

*Why?* Tilly would ask, if she knew. Or more likely she wouldn't ask why at all; she'd ask *what good does it do them*, the dead, to have their names in a hidden book? And that is the question, isn't it, the one I turn over at night like a coin worn smooth. What good?

None, perhaps. The dead are past needing anything, which is the whole cruelty and mercy of death. But I had watched an officer's pen move through a name the way a scythe moves through wheat—without pause, without appetite, without anything so human as malice—and I had understood that the erasure was not for the dead man's benefit or detriment. It was for ours. A war that admits its true count becomes a different war. A public that reads forty-one names instead of thirty-nine begins, dangerously, to imagine faces. So long as the arithmetic stayed tidy, the war stayed abstract, and abstraction is the great enabler of everything I had spent three years trying to keep at bay with carbolic and clean linen.

So: I wrote the names. Not because it helped them. Because it helped *me* remain a person who noticed, in a place built to ensure that noticing became extinct.

"You're doing your mending," Tilly observed, returning for the second pitcher. She glanced at the ledger's gray cover protruding from beneath the basket. "That's a lot of mending."

"I'm very behind," I said. "The war is hard on stockings."

She laughed and went off with her water, and I felt the familiar small shame of the deceiver, and the larger, colder satisfaction of the undetected. Both feelings at once—that is the whole biography of a certain kind of resister, the quiet kind, the kind history rarely bothers to hang or celebrate. We are neither brave enough nor foolish enough to be interesting. We just keep books.

* * *

The afternoon brought its usual inventory of miseries, administered in the usual doses. Sergeant Dalrymple, both feet gone green with gangrene, demanded his boots back and threatened to walk to the front to complain personally. A lieutenant with a fever wept for his horse, whom he described in terms he had never once applied to his sweetheart. And Colonel Vane himself swept through on inspection, silver-tipped cane clicking on the flags, followed by his adjutant and his reputation.

Vane was not cruel in the way of storybook villains; cruelty requires attention, and attention was the one expenditure the man abhorred. He looked at our ward the way a merchant looks at stock—assessing depreciation. His eyes crossed the cots in rows. They did not stop at faces. When he reached the stripped bed where Finch had lain, he paused, however, and consulted a paper his adjutant held out.

"This bed was occupied by...?"

"A private, sir," I said. "Finch."

"Finch." He said it the way one says a figure that has failed to reconcile. "Not on my list."

"He was on my ward, sir. For six weeks. He died this morning."

Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt, nothing so warm—but the flicker of a man discovering an irregularity in a column. "Then the report will be amended in due course," he said, and moved on, cane clicking, and the adjutant made a small note, and I understood with perfect clarity that the amendment would consist of nothing at all, and that Vane knew I knew, and that neither of us considered this worth a scene.

That is the strange etiquette of these places. The lie is not defended. It is simply maintained, the way a wall is maintained, and you may stand next to the wall and observe its mortar, and everyone pretends you cannot see it because acknowledging the wall would require discussing what it keeps in and what it keeps out.

After rounds I carried slops down the back stairs, which is the privilege of rank among nurses—I had earned the right to my own errands—and passed through the yard between our building and the workshop. The presses were running. Through the high window I could see apprentices moving in the lamplight, arms rising and falling with the rhythm of the platen, and Fenwick himself at the stone, inking a form, his gray head bent over the type like a priest over scripture. A good man, I had always supposed. A careful man. Careful men survive in the printing trade, same as in nursing.

He saw me through the glass—he always seemed to see me—and lifted one inky hand in something between greeting and warning, though of what, or against what, he gave no sign.

I stood in the cold yard a moment longer than my slops required, listening to the presses. *Multiply,* they said. *Multiply. Multiply.* Every lie in this war traveled on those machines, stamped a thousand times over until repetition wore the rough edges off falsehood and left something smooth enough to file. And I thought—standing there with my hands going red around the pail—an unwelcome thought, the first of what would become a great many unwelcome thoughts:

What else could a press multiply?

I put the thought away. I want to be honest about that. I put it away the way one puts away a knife—carefully, wrapped in cloth, telling oneself it is for cutting bread.

* * *

Night shift in a military ward is mostly listening. The wounded talk in their sleep; the dying talk less and mean more. At two in the morning I made my slow circuit with the lamp, adjusting blankets, noting breaths, and when I came to the empty bed where Finch had been, I stopped, absurdly, as though the mattress might have news for me.

Tilly slept sitting up in the duty chair, chin on chest, young enough to sleep anywhere. I envied her and did not.

Back in the linen room, by the light of a candle stub I was technically stealing, I opened the ledger and reread the last week's entries. And it was there, in the rereading, that the evening curdled—because reading one's own handwriting back is a dangerous practice; it invites comparison, and comparison invites pattern, and pattern is where safety ends.

Fourteen entries. Fourteen names since the first of the month, which was high but not unprecedented for autumn campaigning. But look closer, Mirabel, I told myself, and I did, and there it was: of the fourteen, six had been omitted from the reports. Six struck in nineteen days, when the usual rate was one struck per month, maybe two, like a leaky roof dripping. And of those six—here my finger stopped on the page, and the candle flame leaned as if reading over my shoulder—of those six, every single one had belonged to the 4th Company.

Finch's company. The company that, according to the garrison rolls pinned in the adjutant's office, had ceased to exist eleven months ago. Disbanded, the roll said. Men redistributed. Paper trail neat as a hem.

Except its men kept turning up in my ward, dying of wounds dated months after their company had been dissolved, and now they kept failing to appear in the reports, and nobody—no clerk, no colonel, no adjutant with his little pencil—seemed troubled by the contradiction.

I sat in the dark with my stolen candle and my dangerous little book and felt the ground shift beneath the ordinary. For three years I had believed myself a record-keeper of random cruelties, a witness to the war's general wastage. But this was not general. This was specific. Somebody had a reason for making six particular men disappear twice—once from the world, and once from the memory of the world—and reasons, unlike soldiers, are never struck from any roll. Reasons leave traces. Reasons have authors.

I closed the ledger, blew out the candle, and sat listening to the presses next door fall silent one by one as Fenwick's crew ended their night. In the silence afterward the ward breathed around me: sixty men breathing, or failing to, and somewhere out in the rain-dark town, presumably, the person who had decided that Ellery Finch should not exist, sleeping soundly on a better mattress than any in this building.

I am not, I want to say clearly, a brave woman. I have never tested well on that score. But I did something that night that I would later recognize as the first step of the road that ended in fire, and it was this: I took the pin from the top of the page—the pin that meant *unfinished business*—and I moved it to mark the whole cluster of 4th Company names together.

A small gesture. A rearrangement of stationery.

But I had begun to believe that the six dead men were not scattered accidents of bureaucracy. They were a list. And lists have makers, and makers have motives, and somewhere in this garrison town a motive was walking around in boots, unaware—as yet—that a nurse with a sewing basket had started doing sums.

The morning report would arrive at seven o'clock, folded twice, smelling of the Colonel's tobacco.

For the first time in three years, I found I could hardly wait to read it.
