## 2. Instructions to Forget

The rain began at dusk and did not stop so much as settle in, the way bad news does. By nine o'clock it had worked through every seam of the hospital roof and was dripping onto the ward floor in a rhythm that one of my convalescents, a corporal with a musket ball lodged somewhere philosophy could not reach, insisted was "God keeping time." God, if He was keeping time that night, was doing it badly. The rhythm dragged. It hesitated. It kept stopping altogether, as though even Heaven had grown bored of this war and wandered off to find a more entertaining one.

I made my rounds with a candle because the lamps had gone out again and because I preferred, in honesty, the small circle of light. A candle tells you only what is near you, and what is near you is generally all you can help. The rest—the endless rest, the wounded lying in rows like typeset waiting for a press—is more than a person can look at straight on for long.

I had finished the last of the laudanum doses and was returning down the corridor that connects our building to Fenwick's workshop when I heard the knock.

Not a knock, precisely. A scratching. The kind of sound a mouse makes, or a woman who does not wish to be heard.

Now, I should explain something about doors in a military hospital at night. They are not supposed to be knocked upon softly. They are supposed to be locked, and guarded, and answered by orderlies with lanterns and suspicion. Ours had a guard once, at the start of the war, when everyone still believed the enemy might come to us instead of us going to him. Four years later, the guard had been reassigned, desertion had thinned the pool of candidates, and the door answered to whoever cared enough to try it. I had complained about this to the quartermaster twice. He had written it down both times, which I have since learned is what quartermasters do instead of anything.

I opened it.

Rain came in first—a fistful of it, flung sideways by wind—and behind the rain, a woman.

She stood under the shallow eave where the hospital wall meets the workshop's, in that mean strip of yard between the two buildings where we keep the coal and the broken wheelbarrows and other things nobody has the heart to bury properly. She held no umbrella. Her shawl was soaked through, dark wool clinging to her shoulders, and her hood had fallen back, and I saw her face plainly in the spill of my candle: young, perhaps thirty, handsome in the way of portraits rather than people, and white to the lips. Not cold-white. Frightened-white. There is a difference. Cold comes gradually. Fear arrives all at once, like a debt collector.

"Are you the nurse," she said. Not a question. She had been told.

"I am the nurse," I said, which was true, though on paper I was variously a matron, an assistant, and—on the quartermaster's books—"female laborer, general." Wars are generous with job titles for women. They simply decline to pay them.

"You'll take him." And she stepped aside, and I saw the boy.

He was standing against the workshop wall as though he had been placed there, which of course he had, the way one places a parcel. Seven years old, maybe eight, small for either. Dark hair plastered flat by rain. He wore a coat far too big for him, cut down from a man's, and he was not crying, which frightened me more than tears would have. Children cry when they believe someone will come. This child had the stillness of one who has already concluded that no one is coming, and has organized his face accordingly.

"He's ill?" I asked, because illness I knew how to hold. Illness fit in my hands. Whatever this was, it did not.

"He's wet and he's hungry and he hasn't spoken since Tuesday," the woman said. "Other than that, he's perfect."

There was something in her voice—not wit exactly, but the wreckage of it. Wit after a shipwreck. I have heard that voice in the ward, from men who joke while the surgeon's saw is being carried past, and I have never decided whether it is courage or its opposite.

"Whose is he?" I said. "What's his name? Where—"

"You're asking questions," she said, "in a doorway, in the rain, loudly."

I closed my mouth. She was right, and she had the particular authority of someone who has spent recent days learning exactly how much noise is too much.

She came closer, into the candlelight, and looked at me with an intensity I found almost indecent. It is not often a stranger looks at you as though your soul were a document she was checking for forgery.

"Forget my face," she said.

"That seems unlikely," I said, before I could stop myself.

"Forget it anyway. That's the price of the boy. You forget me, you forget tonight, and you never ask him questions about how he came here. Can you do that?"

"No," I said honestly. "But I can pretend to, until pretending wears through."

Something moved across her face then—I want to say gratitude, but it may have been despair; at that distance and in that light they wear the same coat—and she reached out and took my wrist. Her hand was freezing. Through her fingers I could feel a trembling that she had otherwise mastered completely, the way one masters a horse by sitting very still and praying.

"There will be men," she said quietly. "Sooner or later there will be men asking after him. They'll be polite. One of them may be charming. If you tell them the truth, he dies. If you tell them a lie well, he lives. That's the whole of it. I'm sorry to put it so plainly, but I haven't time to dress it up."

"Madam—"

"Don't." She released my wrist. "Don't madam me. Every minute I stand here, I spend something I can't replace."

And then she did the thing I have replayed a hundred times since, usually at three in the morning: she knelt down in the mud and the rain in front of the boy, took his face in both wet hands, and kissed his forehead, and said something to him that I could not hear—that she made sure I could not hear, turning her head, cupping her mouth against his ear as though sheltering a flame. Then she stood, turned, and walked out into the rain, and the dark folded her up like a letter.

She did not run. That was the worst of it. A running woman flees *from* something. This woman walked away *toward* something, unhurried, her shoulders square, and I understood—I understood with a lurch, the way one understands a thing one has been refusing to read—that whatever she was walking toward, she had chosen it, and that the choice was the kind with no return ticket.

* * *

The boy did not move.

I stood in the doorway holding a candle over the yard like a fool, and the boy stood against the wall being rained on, and between us lay four feet of mud and the entire problem of what to do next.

"Well," I said. "You can't stay out there. You've gathered that, presumably. You look like a boy who gathers things."

Nothing. Rain. From inside the ward, the corporal called out something about God's timing having improved.

I put out my hand. He looked at it the way one looks at a proffered umbrella in a hurricane—as a nice thought, but insufficient to the occasion. Then he took it anyway, and I learned the second frightening thing about him: his hand was steady. Mine was not.

I brought him in through the corridor, past the linen store, past the dispensary with its lock that has never once kept anyone out, and into the small room behind the ward that serves as my office, mending room, and occasional chapel of despair. It contains a desk, a chair, a stove, a sewing basket, and a great deal else that I will come to.

"Sit," I said. He sat. On the floor, back against the wall, hands in his lap, as though he had been instructed in the posture by someone with strong views on how boys await their fates.

I found bread—there is always bread; bread is the one thing wars produce abundantly—and cheese gone hard at the corner, and set them near him. He ate. He ate carefully, evenly, without haste and without pleasure, the eating of a boy who has known hunger long enough to have stopped performing about it, and I watched him and felt the ground begin to shift under my professional calm.

Because here is what I had already noticed, in the yard, in the candlelight, and had been declining to think about: children do not arrive at hospital doors by themselves. Children are delivered. Someone chose this hospital, this corridor, this nurse—or, failing knowledge of the nurse, chose the place and gambled on what came out of it. And that someone had looked at me and seen something worth gambling a child on.

Which meant that somewhere in the world there existed information about me.

I did not care for that. I am, by constitution and by policy, a woman whom nothing concerns. I rise early, I do my work, I write letters home to a sister who does not answer them, and I am of interest to no one—which is, in a garrison town in the fourth year of a war, the safest of all the possible conditions a woman can enjoy.

Then I saw the paper.

It was in his right hand. Had been the whole time, I suppose—his left hand had accepted the bread, but the right stayed closed in his lap, and I had taken it for a habit, a fist, a boy's small hoarding. When he finished eating he wiped his fingers deliberately on his coat, one at a time, and then opened the fist to check on his treasure, and there it was: a scrap of printed paper, perhaps the size of a prayer card, soft at the edges from handling.

He saw me see it. And he did the opposite of what I expected—instead of hiding it, he held it out to me. Flat on his palm, an offering, an instruction. As if he had been told: *give this to the woman*. As if this, not the bread, not the bed, not the boy himself, were the actual delivery.

I took it.

The printing was fine and even, done on good stock—not a scrap at all originally, but a piece cut down from something larger. Most of it had been trimmed away. What remained was a fragment: the tail end of one line of type, and beneath it, larger, a name.

*Company records amended per order —*

and below that:

*Pvt. E. MOURVANE*

I have sat with dying men and kept my hands steady. I have written letters beginning *It is with profound sorrow* forty times in an afternoon and remained a functioning person. But I confess that when I read that name, the candle in my hand shook, and the shadows in the little room lurched like passengers on a coach.

Because I knew it. Of course I knew it. I knew it in my own hand, in my own ink, on the page that lay folded in oilcloth at the bottom of my sewing basket not four feet from where this child sat: *Private Édouard Mourvane*, entered in my ledger on the twelfth of March, two years gone. Entered because Private Mourvane had died of fever in my ward, had been buried in the plot behind the chapel with eleven others whose names I also kept, and had appeared in no official report—struck, omitted, unwritten, as though a man could serve a war for three years and then be excused from it retroactively, like a bad debt.

The company he'd belonged to, I might add, did not officially exist. More of that later. There is always more of it later; that is the nature of ledgers.

The boy watched my face while I read. I want to be precise about this: he watched my face the way a sailor watches weather. He was reading me for the storm. And whatever he saw—whatever crossed me and could not be recalled—he nodded, slowly, once, as though a question posed days ago had finally been answered.

"You know who that is," I said.

Nothing.

"Édouard Mourvane," I said, watching him. "That's a name. Whose name?"

His mouth opened. I saw the word gather behind his teeth like water behind a dam—and then he shut his mouth, and swallowed, and looked at the floor, and I understood that he was not unable to speak but had *decided* not to, which for a seven-year-old is either madness or the most sophisticated act of self-preservation I have ever witnessed.

"All right," I said. "All right. Not tonight."

I gave him blankets from the convalescent store—I have stolen more blankets from that store than any thief in the province, and I regret none of it—and made him a bed behind the screen by my desk where the fever cases used to lie, back when we had room for fevers to be particular. He lay down obediently, curled toward the wall, the paper scrap tucked under his cheek like a coin under a pillow.

"Forget my face," she had said. I banked the stove and sat in the chair and looked at the sleeping shape of him and thought: *madam, forgetting is the one commodity I do not stock.*

My whole trade, you see—my secret, shameful, necessary trade—is remembering. Remembering what reports omit. Writing down what the War Office has unwritten. Every name in that ledger is a small act of insubordination, committed in ink, in a basket, under a mending pile, by a woman whose official title is *female laborer, general*. I had believed, until that night, that this was defiance enough for one lifetime: to notice, and to write it down, and to tell no one.

But the boy breathed, and the rain kept its bad time against the glass, and the name on the scrap dried slowly in the stove-warmth—*Pvt. E. MOURVANE*, printed, typeset, *printed*—and a colder thought arrived and would not leave.

Fever does not print. Grief does not print. Somebody had set that name in type, and struck it from record, and cut the evidence into scraps—and somebody had given one of those scraps to a child, along with instructions to deliver himself to strangers.

Which meant the ledger in my basket and the boy behind my screen were pages of the same book. And somebody, somewhere, was holding the pen.

Near dawn the rain stopped, as rain eventually does even in the worst of years, and the boy slept, and I did not. And at first light, crossing the yard to the ward, I saw the thing that turned dread into arithmetic: fresh hoofprints in the mud by the gate, and among them, half-drowned but legible, a printed bill, the kind posted on garrison walls by the hundred—

*NOTICE. Inquiries concerning missing persons to be directed to Captain Rook, by authority of Colonel Vane.*

The paper was dated yesterday.
