## 3. The Boy Who Wasn't There

By morning the rain had stopped, but the boy had not, and I mean that in every sense. He was still at my door, still damp, still holding that scrap of paper as though someone might pry it from his fingers with crowbars. And he had not, in any official capacity, arrived. No coach record, no garrison pass, no name on any list. In a war where men were reduced to columns of figures, this child was a blank space, and blank spaces frightened me more than bullets did.

I got him upstairs before the day shift came, moving through the grey half-light like a thief in my own hospital. This is not a figure of speech. I actually carried him, one arm under his knees and one behind his shoulders, and he weighed about as much as a sack of flour with opinions.

"You'll be Tobias," I told him on the stairs. "Can you be Tobias?"

He considered this with the gravity of a general reviewing troops. "Tobias," he agreed. Then, after a pause that cost me something: "That's not my name."

"I know," I said. "It's yours for now. Borrowed names are like borrowed coats. They keep you warm until you get your own back."

He nodded, satisfied by the logic, which is more than most officers have ever managed with mine.

The ward below was already stirring — coughs, groans, the particular complaint of Sergeant Hollis whose leg had been amputated above the knee and who nevertheless insisted, daily, on telling everyone he could still feel his toes and they were cold. The convalescents were arranged in two rows of twelve, and among them sat the men nobody asked questions about: fevers half-burned out, minds gone soft from shell-shock, faces too ruined to invite conversation. A boy sitting beside such men would be invisible. Grief makes its own shadows, and the ward was full of shadow enough to hide a child in.

I installed him in the bed nearest the stove, next to Private Wrenn, who had taken a piece of shrapnel through the skull at Kessel Ford and now spent his days folding scraps of paper into birds with astonishing patience and terrible accuracy. Wrenn looked at the boy. The boy looked at Wrenn.

"He's my nephew," I announced to the ward at large, in the voice I use for changing dressings — brisk, no appeal permitted. "His mother's taken ill. He'll be here days."

Nobody challenged this. That is the mercy of hospitals: everyone assumes everyone else's misery has paperwork behind it. Sister Cade raised one eyebrow at me, which from her constituted a full cross-examination, but she said nothing, and brought the boy porridge with an extra spoonful of honey she pretended she hadn't.

I told myself it would be for a few days. Three at most. Just long enough to learn what name was on that scrap of paper and why the sight of it had made my hands go cold in a way I hadn't felt since my first year of nursing, when I'd realized the dead outnumbered the beds.

Here is what the scrap said, because I read it while he slept that first morning, prying it gently from fingers that gripped even in sleep:

*E. MARCH — 3rd Co. — ask for the Clerk.*

And here is why my hands went cold: E. March appeared in my ledger. Fourth entry from the top of the autumn pages. *Died of wounds, 14 October.* Except that according to the morning reports — the ones signed, stamped, and filed by men paid to sign, stamp, and file them — Private E. March had never existed at all. His company, the 3rd, appeared nowhere in the rolls. His death appeared nowhere in the returns. When I had copied his name into my ledger eleven weeks ago, I had done so because Corporal Denny, dying slowly and lucidly of a wound in the belly, had gripped my wrist and made me swear to write it down: *March, Eli. He was real. Write it down, miss, or nobody ever will.*

So I had written it down. And now the man's son — if that is what he was, and God help us all if it wasn't — was asleep in my ward, holding instructions that said *ask for the Clerk*, while somewhere in the world there lived a Clerk who presumably wished to remain unasked-for.

I put the scrap under the false bottom of my sewing basket, where the ledger lived, and I stood there a long moment listening to the presses thudding away in the workshop next door, feeling the floor tremble faintly with each impression. Paper everywhere in this building. Paper going out into the world with proclamations and casualty lists and prices of flour printed upon it. And one small piece of paper, hidden in a basket, that could hang us all.

The boy turned in his sleep and murmured something. It sounded like *don't let go of my hand*, so I didn't, for a while, though I had dressing changes waiting and a matron with eyes like audit.

This is how it begins, I thought. Not with grand decisions but with holding a hand a little longer than strictly necessary.

* * *

The man called the Clerk had hands like a churchmouse's feet. Small, quick, quiet, always moving along edges.

That's what I remember best. Not his face. His face was nothing, a door nobody painted. But his hands I remember.

We went in coaches. First there was Mother's friend, the one with the crooked hat, and she took us down stairs that smelled of cabbage and we waited and waited and I counted the cracks in the ceiling until Mother said stop counting, Ivo, counting won't help. But it helped me. Forty-one cracks.

Then the first coach. We rode inside a space with crates and Mother put her coat over me and said whatever happens, be furniture. Be a table, be a chair. So I was a chair. Chairs don't hold anyone's hand. That was hard.

At the river there was a boat and the water talked underneath us the whole way, saying things I couldn't understand, and Mother cried but quietly, the way she did everything quietly near the end, like she was practicing not being there.

Then the man called the Clerk met us on the far side. He knew Mother's name before she gave it. He gave her papers and money folded very small and he said, "The boy goes alone from here. That was the arrangement."

And Mother said, "The arrangement was wrong."

They argued in whispers, which grown-ups think children can't hear, but whispers are just shouting wearing slippers. The Clerk said names must match papers and papers must match and there was a place where the boy would be safe but only if no thread connected him to anything, not even her. Especially not her. He said it like arithmetic. Two and two and you take away everything and the answer is zero and zero is safe.

Mother held my face in her hands then, both hands, cold from the river air, and she looked at me so hard I thought her eyes might leave marks.

"Listen to me," she whispered. "Whatever happens, forget my face. Do you understand? If anyone asks, if anyone ever asks — you never saw this face. You never had a mother named what you're named. Forget the face and they can't follow it. Forget the face and I stay alive."

"That doesn't make sense," I said.

"No," she said. "It isn't supposed to. It's supposed to work."

She kissed my forehead and my nose and then she walked away up the towpath without turning around, which is a thing I know now takes enormous strength, because I have tried it in games and I always turn around.

After that there was another coach, and a woman who smelled of lavender and fear, and another coach, and bread with sand in it, and a long time being furniture. And then a door, and rain, and a lady with tired eyes who held my hand longer than necessary.

I forgot her face. Mother's, I mean. Mostly. I kept the shape of it, like when you fold a paper bird and unfold it and the creases stay. I'm keeping the creases.

But I didn't tell the tired-eyed lady any of this yet. She asked questions like *where does it hurt* instead of *who are you*, and those are different kinds of questions, and I decided she could be trusted with the second kind later, maybe, if the creases held.

* * *

Three days passed, and I learned the following: that Tobias ate like a small wolf and thanked you for it like a small bishop; that he helped Sister Cade carry water until she ran out of pretexts to refuse him; that Private Wrenn had taught him to fold paper birds and the boy's were better than the man's, which Wrenn found hilarious each time; and that he watched the door. Every footstep in the corridor, his head came up. A child learning the sound of pursuit. It broke something in me slightly, watching it, and I bandaged the break with work, which is what nurses do.

I also learned that asking him direct questions got me nowhere. *Where did you come from? Who gave you the paper? What is your true name?* — he would look at me politely and answer with weather. So I stopped asking and started simply being present, which is the older and more effective interrogation. Children talk sideways, when they trust you. You have to stand where the talking flows.

On the third evening, mopping the ward floor while he folded a bird, I said, casually, to the mop: "Whoever wrote that paper you carry wanted you to find someone safe. Someone who keeps records."

The folding stopped.

"My mother said forget her face," he said to the bird.

"Yes," I said. "Mothers say wise things at the worst possible moments."

"If I forget her, she stays alive." He looked up. "Is that true?"

I knelt down beside him, wet rag forgotten, and chose honesty, because he had the eyes of a child who had been lied to expertly and recently and would recognize the craft. "No, love. Forgetting protects her from being followed, not from harm. They're different protections. But both are real."

He absorbed this. Then: "There was a man. At the river. With mouse-hands. He said I had to be zero." A pause. "I don't want to be zero anymore."

The presses thudded next door, steady as a heartbeat, and I thought: neither do I, love. Neither do I.

That night I took out the ledger and added beneath Eli March's name, in my smallest hand: *a son survives.* It was not information. It was testimony. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.

And then, as I closed the basket, Sister Cade put her head round my door, unannounced, at an hour when nobody visits, and said the sentence I had been dreading since the rain:

"Nurse Dunmore. There's an officer downstairs asking after your nephew. Says he's been sent by Colonel Vane's office — says the colonel likes to know about everyone under army roofs." She paused. "He's very charming. I don't like him at all."
