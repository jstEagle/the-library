## 4. Colonel Vane's Arithmetic

There is a particular pleasure in arithmetic done well, and I have never understood why more men don't cultivate it. Numbers do not flinch. Numbers do not write letters to their mothers. A number, properly arranged, becomes whatever you require of it, and it will hold that shape for a hundred years if you ask it to.

I had been asking a great deal of my numbers that winter.

The office I kept at garrison headquarters had once belonged to the paymaster, and still smelled faintly of coin and complaint. I had the desk moved to face the window—overlooking the yard, the hospital's low slate roof beyond it, the workshop chimney smoking its patient smoke—and I had the ledgers brought up in crates. Eleven crates. War, in its essence, is eleven crates delivered on time.

That morning's report lay before me, and I will confess it gave me satisfaction. The Third Battalion, engaged at the causeway, losses as follows: killed, forty-one; wounded, one hundred and six; missing, none. A clean column. A tidy sum. The War Office would read it over breakfast and feel the particular comfort that comes from bad news that arrives in an acceptable quantity.

The true figure was fifty-three.

I did not think of the twelve as dead men, because that sort of thinking is where bookkeeping goes to die. I thought of them as variance. Twelve men who had been in the wrong place with the wrong names at the wrong hour, and whose deaths, entered honestly, would have raised questions I had spent three years ensuring could not be asked. Forty-one was not a lie. It was a *selection*. There is a difference, and the difference matters, though I grant it matters mostly to me.

My clerk—no, forgive me, my *assistant*, Corporal Hewitt, a young man with ink-stained cuffs and the moral flexibility of warm wax—knocked and entered with the morning's correspondence.

"Anything of interest?" I asked.

"A requisition from the hospital, sir. Lint. And this." He set down a single sheet. "Forwarded from the adjutant's office. It seems there was some confusion in last week's returns."

There is no phrase in the military vocabulary more dangerous than *some confusion*. I took the sheet and read it twice. A sergeant in the hospital's administrative chain—it hardly mattered which; they rotated like weather—had noted that the ward's burial returns did not reconcile with the battalion's reported dead. One private, name of Ellery, deceased of his wounds on the Tuesday, appearing nowhere in the Wednesday report. The sergeant had flagged it, dutifully, in the small copperplate hand of a man who believes the system exists to be accurate rather than useful.

Ellery. Yes. I remembered the name the way a merchant remembers a debt. He was one of mine—one of the twelve, or adjacent to the twelve, the categories blurred pleasantly at the edges. He had died at the causeway wearing the wrong company's number, and so he had been subtracted, and now some hospital clerk with too much time and too much conscience had noticed the subtraction.

"And who writes this?" I asked.

"Sergeant Odum, sir. He's since rotated out. But the note says the discrepancy was—" Hewitt squinted— "'first observed by the nursing sister on duty.'"

*Observed.* What a word. Not *recorded*, thank God. Not *reported*. But observed, which meant noticed, which meant it had lived in somebody's head long enough to grow opinions.

"Hospital nurses observe many things," I said. "It is what we pay them for. Observing is not the same as keeping accounts."

"No, sir."

"Find out," I said, and then I stopped, because the sentence I had been about to construct was far too large for its container. Find out. An inquiry. Words like that have a way of growing legs, and legs have a way of walking back to your own door. I started again, more carefully, the way one crosses a frozen river.

"Do nothing," I said. "Simply make it known to me. Who she is. How long she has been there. Whether she writes letters, and to whom. Quietly, Hewitt. If anyone asks why you're interested in a nurse, tell them you're interested in lint."

Hewitt smiled at that, pleased to be trusted with a small sin, and went out. I returned to my figures and found, for the first time in three years, that they would not sit still.

* * *

I want to be honest with you, whoever you are, whoever eventually reads this—and someone always does; that is the whole tragedy of paper—because honesty between professionals is a courtesy I extend even to enemies. You believe, perhaps, that a man who falsifies the dead must hate them, or hate the truth, or wake at night with ghosts standing at the foot of his bed counting aloud. Nothing so dramatic. I wake at night, yes, but what stands at the foot of my bed is a column of figures that refuses to balance, and I confess I find that far worse.

I came to this work the way most men come to most work: by being good at the thing nobody else wanted to do. Twenty years ago I was a subaltern who could add. This sounds like nothing. It was everything. In every army there are men who can charge a hill and men who can count what the hill costs, and the second kind live longer and die less often and are remembered worse, and I chose my category early and without regret.

The war made the rest simple. Wars are expensive, and governments are squeamish, and somewhere in the middle between expense and squeamishness a space opens up, and men like me are poured into it like lead into a mold. When a battle goes badly, someone must decide what the word *badly* shall mean in the official record. When a strategy fails, someone must translate failure into a dialect acceptable to ministers who have invested their reputations in it. I did not invent this service. I perfected it. And for years the work was innocent, or as near to innocent as arithmetic ever gets: rounding, smoothing, the gentle massaging of catastrophe into inconvenience.

Then came the commission I do not speak of, and the company that does not exist, and the morning when fifty men marched out under numbers they had never worn before, and the afternoon when none of them marched back, and the evening when I sat with a pen and performed the only act of creation available to a bureaucrat: I unmade them.

Nine names, in the final accounting. Nine men of a company that had never been mustered, dying in a battle that had never been fought, and their widows receiving—nothing, because you cannot pension a woman whose husband never served. Do you know what I felt, setting down that final line? Relief. Clean, structural relief. The plan held. The load-bearing wall bore the weight. Anyone who tells you that power feels like triumph has never held it; it feels like a building that stays up when you expected it to fall, and you walk around inside it for weeks afterward, waiting for the crack.

I tell myself—and here is where you may laugh, if you are the sort who laughs—that the alternative was worse. Those nine men, honestly recorded, would have pointed at the order that sent them, and the order would have pointed at the man who wrote it, and that man had friends in rooms I will never be invited into, and the friends would have pointed at the war itself, and the war pointing at anything is a thing neither I nor the country could survive. My falsehoods were mortar between stones. Remove them and the whole edifice comes down on everyone's head, including the heads of people who never asked to be stones.

This is what I believe on Tuesdays. On other days I believe other things, and I have learned not to keep a ledger of my beliefs, because beliefs, unlike casualties, cannot be revised downward without consequence.

* * *

Hewitt was as good as his small sins required. Within four days I had a page on the woman.

Her name was Dunmore. Mirabel Dunmore. Six years at the hospital, arrived—of all ironies—in the same season as the workshop's expansion next door, recommended by a chaplain since dead of fever. No family in the district. No letters flagged by the censor, which either meant innocence or competence, and after twenty years in my profession I no longer believed in the first. She tended the surgical ward, kept the linen counts, taught convalescents their letters on slow afternoons—which the matron described, in her flat way, as "harmless," a word I have come to regard as the sound innocence makes just before it becomes a problem.

And she kept a notebook. Sewing basket, false bottom, Hewitt's report said, with the modest pride of a man quoting someone else's burglary. One of the ward orderlies—a boy who owed gambling debts to people I could introduce him to—had seen her writing in it at night. Names, he thought. He hadn't read it; he couldn't, being unable to read at all, which is a quality I look for in witnesses.

Names.

I sat with that for a long while, looking out at the hospital roof through the winter light. Let us assume the worst, because assuming the best is a luxury I gave up around the time I unmade nine men before supper. Assume she writes down what the reports omit. Assume she has done it for years. A private here, a corporal there—the small dead, the administratively inconvenient, gathered up in a woman's needlework basket like so much mending.

What did it amount to? Almost nothing. That was the honest answer, and I made myself sit with it as one sits with an uncomfortable chair. A notebook of omissions proves nothing. It proves discrepancies, and discrepancies are the weather of war; every hospital in the province could produce one. Her notebook was an itch, not a wound. In peacetime it would have been a curiosity, in a museum case someday, *The Sentiments of a Military Nurse, 1790–1796*, purchased by a gentleman collector and read by no one.

But this was not peacetime, and beneath her notebook, if she had one, lay the ground I had salted. Because a woman who notices one missing private is a woman who might, given time and grievance, notice a missing company. And nine names written in her careful hand would not be an itch. It would be a fuse with very neat handwriting.

Here is the trouble with my position, and I record it plainly: I could not investigate her. An investigation announces that there is something to find. Inquiries have staffs, and staffs have mouths, and mouths in a garrison town operate at the speed of scandal. Every question asked aloud about Sister Dunmore would travel faster than any answer, and questions, unlike answers, cannot be recalled and rewritten. I had built my edifice on the principle that nothing must ever appear to have been hidden. The moment the army began searching a nurse's sewing basket, the basket became interesting forever.

No. Whatever was done would have to look like nothing. A visitor. A courtesy. A charming officer with no visible purpose, paying the hospital his respects because officers do that, in winter, out of boredom. Someone who could sit at a ward bedside for an hour and let a woman talk herself into visibility. Someone who could look at her hands, her eyes, the pauses in her sentences, and report back not facts but *texture*—whether she was a woman who kept names out of grief or out of intent.

I had such a someone. Captain Rook. A decoration with a pulse, the men called him, half in envy and half in affection, and both halves were correct. Rook had the rare gift of being liked immediately and suspected never. He visited hospitals the way other men visited churches, sincerely, and sincerity is the finest camouflage yet devised. I would send him with instructions so mild they would embarrass a tourist: pay your respects, take an interest, notice everything.

And if it came to the worst—if the texture came back wrong—then I would need to think about paper. Not hers. Mine. About how a fire starts in a printing workshop, how accidents prefer buildings full of oil and lamp wicks, how a town forgets an accident in a season but never quite forgets a scandal. These were not new thoughts. They were thoughts I had already thought, three winters ago, when I hired a forger through three intermediaries to print papers for a company that did not exist. I remembered the workshop then. I had driven past it twice and allowed myself not to look at it, which is a discipline.

There was one other item in Hewitt's report, tucked at the bottom like a bill you hope will be lost. The night before last, a woman had come to the workshop door in the rain, after hours. Carried a child. Stayed less than a minute. The watchman—half-blind, half-honest, entirely for sale—described her as tall, veiled, and frightened, and described the child as "a boy, seven or so, quiet as a debt." She had left the boy and gone into the dark, and the watchman, having no category for the event, had filed it where the poor file such things: nowhere.

A child, left at the door of a print shop, in the rain, by a frightened woman who wished to be forgotten.

I am not a romantic. I want that on the record alongside everything else. But twenty years of arithmetic had taught me to recognize when two columns were converging on the same sum, and every instinct I owned stood up at attention. A nurse who records the unrecorded. A mother who surrenders her son to strangers. A workshop that prints things at night. Separately, nothing. Together—

Together, a story. And stories, unlike soldiers, cannot be struck from the rolls.

I wrote two lines to Rook that afternoon, sealed them, and gave them to Hewitt with instructions to deliver personally. Then I sat a long time in the grey failing light, doing sums no one had asked of me: how long a typesetter needs to set a page; how fast paper burns; how quickly a broadsheet travels from a garrison town to the capital when the wind is right and the news is true.

The figures would not stay still. For the first time in my career, I could not make them.

Somewhere across the yard, under the hospital's slate roof, a woman was writing names in a book hidden beneath her sewing, and each name was a small stone placed against my arithmetic, and she did not yet know—how could she—that I had already begun choosing the date.
