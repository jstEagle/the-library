## 5. What the Presses Know

There is a particular hour in a printing workshop when the ink smells honest. It comes just after the morning's first run, when the type has been washed, the rollers dressed, and the presses sit idle in the grey light like horses waiting to be told what they think. I have loved that hour for thirty years, which is either a testament to my character or a confession of how little else I've loved.

That morning the hour was spoiled by a scrap of paper the size of my palm, sitting on my workbench like a small accusation.

I should explain how it came to be there. Mirabel Dunmore brought it. The nurse from the hospital next door — the one with the steady hands and the look of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't enjoy — appeared at my door before the presses were warm, holding the boy under one arm and the scrap under her thumb, and asked me, in the tone of a person who has already decided, what I made of it.

What I made of it, I said, was that it was paper.

What I made of it privately, the moment my eyes touched the watermark, was considerably more complicated, and I said nothing about that, because I am a coward with excellent posture.

* * *

Let me tell you about paper, since no one else will, and since paper is the closest thing I have ever had to a confessor.

Paper remembers. That's the whole trade secret. Cloth remembers the hands that broke it down to rag; water remembers the mould it drained through; the wire of the laid mould leaves its fingerprint in every sheet, fine as a hair, and no two moulds leave the same fingerprint, not ever, not if the maker tried. A sheet of paper is a confession written so small that most people go their whole lives without learning to read it.

I learned to read it at nineteen, under a man named Corvus — not his name, obviously, but he collected names the way other men collect debts, and I never learned which of his was real. Corvus taught me forgery the way the Church teaches catechism: thoroughly, joylessly, and with the constant threat of fire. He said, and I have never forgotten it, that a forger who worries about the words is already caught. The words are the last thing anyone checks. Worry about the paper. Worry about the ink. Worry about the wear of the type, the slant of the hand, the little human stutters that no honest clerk would think to fake and no dishonest one can bear to reproduce. Perfection is the signature of the devil, Aldous. Be imperfect in the right places.

I was good. I want that on the record, wherever the record ends up, because I have spent twenty years pretending otherwise and it has chafed like a wet collar. I was very good. I could age a document in a smokehouse and a week of patient handling until a bishop would have sworn he'd christened on it. I could cut a watermark out of one sheet and inlay it into another so cleanly that the paper's own mother couldn't have told them apart.

And then, one winter, I stopped. Not out of virtue — let's not be vulgar about it. I stopped because a man came to Corvus with a commission I won't describe, and I saw what the finished work was for, and I understood that my hands, which I had considered instruments of craft, were instruments of something else entirely, and that the something else did not care about my craft at all. Corvus called it a change of heart. I called it a change of stomach. Either way I bought my way out with the one thing Corvus wanted — my silence, and my absence — and came to this town, where the war grinds along a hundred miles east and a man can set type for broadsheets, playbills, and the parish register, and sleep at night.

Mostly sleep at night.

* * *

The scrap on my bench was a corner of a printed form. Part of a muster roll, by the look — the column headings half visible, a name in the entry line, and a stamp I knew the way you know a scar in the mirror.

Three winters ago — I count winters the way misers count coins, so I'll say it precisely: the winter the river froze hard enough to skate, and the town held its little festival on the ice, and I skated badly and was happy — a client came to the workshop. Not to me directly; he came through a broker in the capital whose name I never learned, only his handwriting, which slanted left like a man walking into wind. The commission was for a private printing: muster rolls, pay ledgers, ration chits. A full administrative kit for a company of soldiers. Two hundred sets.

Now, you will say — and Mirabel said, in her flat way, when I finally told her — that printing muster rolls is honest work. Armies are made of paper. Every regiment is a ream and a half of forms with men attached. And I told myself the same, standing at my press in the cold, feeding sheet after sheet through for a company whose officers I never met, whose men I never saw, whose colonel's signature I reproduced forty times until my wrist ached and the forgery had worn smooth and natural as a river stone.

But there were things wrong with the commission, and I knew them at the time, and I did the work anyway, which is the sentence I would like you to weigh most heavily.

First: the paper. The client specified my own stock — the laid mould I'd bought secondhand from a bankrupt stationer in the capital, with its distinctive watermark, a fleur and a broken stem. Why would a military clerk specify a watermark belonging to a provincial printer? He wouldn't. Unless the point was that the paper be traceable to nowhere, or — worse — that it be traceable to me, in which case the trace was a leash, not a signature. I told myself it was thrift. I was very good at telling myself things that winter.

Second: the numbers. The rolls were for a company of one hundred and twenty men, and the pay ledgers were drawn up for a company of one hundred and twenty, but the ration chits were printed for ninety. Thirty men's worth of food, missing. I mentioned it in my letter to the broker — I did mention it, I want that recorded too, my one flicker of conscience, brief as a match in a cellar — and the reply came back with no explanation at all, only a second commission, and a payment generous enough to make a man decide not to have asked.

Third: the name of the company. It had none. Every company has a number, a colonel, a home garrison. These forms said only what was required to be said and nothing else. A company with no name is like a man with no face; it can go anywhere and be accused of nothing.

I printed it all. I washed my type. I skated badly on the frozen river and let the town's bells make me feel like a citizen, and I did not think about thirty missing rations, because thinking is a door and I had learned to keep it shut.

* * *

The scrap on my bench was from one of those rolls. I knew it before I touched it, the way you know a voice through a wall. The watermark — fleur, broken stem — caught the lamplight when I tilted it. The type was mine; I could show you the exact nick in the *e* of the column heading, a flaw from a dropped sort that I never replaced because it made every line it touched honest, in its way. And the name in the entry line —

The name. I read it three times. It meant nothing to me. A private soldier, one of a hundred and twenty names I set in type over eleven days and never once thought of as men. That is the trick of printing, you understand — it is also the sin of it. You set names all day and they are lead. They are not people until someone with a different trade makes them people. I set the names of that company the way I set the names of horses for a livery bill.

Except now one of those names had come back to me in the hand of a child, folded small, carried through rain, clutched the way children clutch the one thing they were told to keep.

I sat with it a long while. The presses ticked as they cooled. Somewhere next door the hospital woke up — I heard the clink of the pails, the low murmur of the ward, a man coughing the deep wet cough that I have learned, over these years of sharing a wall with the wounded, to hear in my sleep. Mirabel had gone back over the wall with the boy hidden somewhere among her convalescents, and had left me with the scrap and one instruction: *Tell me what you see. Nothing else. Just what you see.*

What I saw was my own sin, printed small and returned to sender.

* * *

Here is the thing about guilt that no sermon gets right: it does not arrive like a storm. It arrives like damp. It gets into the walls of you over years, and you don't notice it, and then one cold morning the plaster bubbles, and you press your thumb against it, and the whole face of the wall comes away soft in your hand.

I had kept the door shut on that commission for three winters. And now a boy of seven was sleeping in my neighbor's hospital with a piece of my type folded in his fist, and the damp had come through.

Because here is what I could not stop working out, sitting there like a man doing sums he hates: a child does not tear a corner off a muster roll for a keepsake. Children keep locks of hair, buttons, the ribbon off a hat. They keep things that belonged to someone. That scrap had been torn from a roll — torn, not cut, torn in haste — and it had been *kept*, and it had been *given*, and the name on it was the name of a man, and the man was almost certainly the boy's father, because the world is not subtle, whatever the novelists say. And the roll was mine. My type, my paper, my press. Which meant that whatever had happened to that father — and a boy carried from door to door in the rain by a woman who says *forget my face* does not have a father who died honorably in a report anyone read — whatever had happened, my hands had touched the paperwork of it.

Corvus used to say: the words are the last thing anyone checks. He was wrong about one thing. The words are the last thing anyone checks — but the paper checks you. Sooner or later, every sheet comes home to the hand that made it.

* * *

I did what a man does when the wall comes away in his hand. I made tea I didn't drink, and I went down into the cellar where I keep the archive — every job I've printed in twenty years, one proof copy of each, dated, filed, because a printer without his archive is a man without a memory and a memory is the only collateral I have ever had.

The file was where it should be. Third shelf, winter of the frozen river. I untied the string and there it all was, neat as a hymnal: the muster rolls, the pay ledgers, the ration chits, the colonel's signature forty times over, smooth and natural as a river stone. I had even kept the broker's letters — I keep everything; it is my disease and my defense — and I read them again by cellar lamplight, and the left-slanting handwriting walked into the wind across the page, and I noticed for the first time what I had been too well paid to notice three winters ago.

The second commission, the one that came after I asked about the missing rations. It was not for more rolls. It was for a single printed notice — a death notice, a form of bereavement, the kind sent to families — one hundred and twenty of them, blank in the name line. Blank. A hundred and twenty letters of condolence with no name filled in. I had printed them and thought it an eccentricity. Now, holding the boy's scrap, I understood it as something else: forms made ready in advance, the way an undertaker keeps coffins on the shelf. You do not order blank death notices unless you expect the names to be needed soon, and you leave the names blank if you intend to fill them in later — or if you never intend to fill them in at all, and the notices are for a family that will never be told anything, because officially there is nothing to tell.

I put the file back. I tied the string. My hands were steady, which surprised me; the hands are always the last to be told anything.

Then I went and stood in the yard between the workshop and the hospital wall, in the smell of ink and lye and the cold coming off the mill stream, and I asked myself the question I had been avoiding since dawn.

If a woman risks her life to bring a child to your door — not to the magistrate, not to the garrison, not to the church, but to the one building in this town full of paper and presses — does she know what you are? Has she read you the way you read paper? And if she has, what does she think you are: the man who can help, or the man who made the thing she's running from?

And under that, quieter, the question I have been outrunning for twenty years: *which are you, Aldous?*

* * *

I did not sleep that night, which was not unusual, but for once the insomnia had a shape. I lay in the loft above the workshop listening to the presses tick as they cooled, and I thought about thirty missing rations, and a hundred and twenty blank condolences, and a company with no name, and a boy who was not supposed to be anywhere, and I understood — the way you understand a thing in the dark that you will have to find the courage to say in the light — that the commission had never been for paper at all. The paper had been the weapon. Someone had built a company out of my type the way you build a man out of wax, for the purpose of burning it, and the burning had already happened, three winters ago, somewhere east, in a battle that never officially occurred, to men whose names I had set in lead and never once thought of as men.

And one of those names was folded in a child's fist one wall away.

Toward morning I got up, dressed, and took down the file again. I untied the string and laid the proofs out across the workbench, all of it, muster to condolence, and I stood over them like a man standing over a body.

Then I heard the small sounds from next door — the hospital waking, the pails, the coughing — and beneath them, fainter, a child's voice asking something, and Mirabel's voice answering, low and level, the voice of a woman who has already decided.

I took a fresh sheet of my own stock, the fleur and the broken stem, and I dipped my pen.

If she was going to ask me what I saw, she was going to get all of it. The paper, the broker, the missing rations, the blank condolences, the winter, the whole confession, written so small that most people would never learn to read it — but she would. I had the sudden, terrible certainty that she was the one person in the world who would read it.

I wrote the date at the top of the page, and under it, the first honest sentence I had set down in twenty years: *Three winters ago I printed a company of soldiers who did not exist, and I believe I know now what they were made for.*

The ink was barely dry when someone knocked at the workshop door — twice, soft, the knock of a person checking whether the house was asleep.

Through the shutter I saw the uniform before I saw the face. A captain's coat, rain still beaded on the shoulders, and a smile already arranged, the way you'd arrange type in a stick: everything in its place, nothing accidental, and every letter of it set by someone else.
