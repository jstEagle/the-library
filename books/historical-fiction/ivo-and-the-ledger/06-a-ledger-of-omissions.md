## 6. A Ledger of Omissions

There is a particular hour in a military hospital that I think of as the honest hour. It comes somewhere between three and four in the morning, after the last rounds, before the first carts. The officers are asleep and dreaming of promotions. The orderlies have found corners in which to fold themselves. Even the dying seem to consent, for an hour, to do it quietly. And the ward belongs to the wounded and to me, and we tell each other the truth in the dark — or they moan it, which is nearly the same thing.

It was in that hour that I sat down with the boy's scrap of paper and my ledger, and began, properly, to be dangerous.

I had put Tobias — I did not yet know his name, so in my head he was *the boy*, which is a poor name for anyone — to sleep in the linen room, wedged between two stacks of blanket rolls like a misposted letter. He had gone down without protest, which frightened me more than crying would have. Children who cry want something. Children who lie down where they're told and close their eyes on command have been taught by someone exactly what happens when they don't. Three winters of war has made me an expert in the grammar of silences, and his said: *I have practiced this.*

The workshop next door was quiet too, though I had heard Fenwick moving about past midnight, and someone had knocked — softly, twice — at his door before that. I had seen, through my own window, the shape of a captain's coat in the rain. I filed this away the way I filed everything: under *later*. Later is where I kept most of my life.

Now. The paper.

Tobias slept with it clenched in his fist, and prying it loose had taken both patience and a biscuit. It was a printed slip, cheap stock, the kind used for handbills and broadsheets — but this was no handbill. Someone had cut it down with a knife, roughly, leaving a margin ragged as a torn hem. Across the top, in type so ordinary it was almost obscene, ran a single line:

**PVT. ELEAZAR VOSS — C COY — SETTLED IN FULL**

And beneath it, smaller, a date. The eleventh of March, three years gone.

*Settled in full.* As though a man were an invoice.

Here is what you must understand about my ledger, because I have never explained it to anyone and may never get another chance. It is not a diary. Diaries are vanity; diaries assume someone will care what you felt about the weather. My ledger is arithmetic. Under the false bottom of my sewing basket — a gift from my late husband, who bought it from a peddler and overpaid, which was his way with everything — beneath the buttons and the darned stockings and the whole domestic theater of my supposed grief, there is a book bound in grey board, unremarkable as a hymnal. In it, in my smallest hand, I write the names that the morning reports decline to contain.

Private Hollis, dead of fever on a Tuesday, entered in the ward book as *discharged*. Discharged to where, I wonder? Heaven, presumably; the army rarely specifies.

Gunner Pell, dead of a shell wound, recorded as *returned to duty* — which, given that he left us in a box with his name painted wrong on the lid, struck me as optimistic bookkeeping even by military standards.

Sergeant Amory, dead, listed as *deserted*. That one made me angry for a week. Dead men make convenient deserters; they never come back to contradict the report.

Twenty years of nursing will teach you many things, chief among them that paperwork is the last battlefield, fought after everyone has stopped shooting, and that it is fought precisely because the shooting is over and the winners must now be decided. Men die in battle. On paper, they are *managed*. And I had begun, some years ago without quite admitting it to myself, to keep the count the reports would not keep. Not out of principle — let me be honest, since no one is listening — but out of a nurse's stubbornness, the same reflex that makes you count the spoons. Something is missing. Something was here. I saw it go.

So that night, in the honest hour, I set the boy's paper beside my ledger and looked up Eleazar Voss.

He was not there. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have. Voss was not among the names I had recorded as omitted, because Voss had never needed omitting. He had been erased upstream, before the water ever reached me. There are deaths that arrive at my ward and are quietly reclassified, and those I catch. And then, apparently, there are deaths that never appear anywhere at all — men who simply stop being on any list, so thoroughly that even my small rebellions never touch them.

That frightened me. I sat very still and listened to the ward breathe, sixty men breathing in their sleep, each one currently alive, each one currently counted, and thought: somewhere there are men nobody breathes for.

But the date survived. Whoever printed that slip had been careless or hurried, and the date remained: the eleventh of March, three winters past. And dates, unlike men, cannot be struck from rolls retroactively without someone noticing the ink.

I turned to the back pages of my ledger, where I keep — God help me, I keep everything — a chronological index, and I ran my finger down the third winter to March. The eleventh itself yielded nothing. But the week around it —

Ah.

Here is the thing they never teach you about ledgers, the thing that makes them more treacherous than rifles: patterns live in them whether you invite the patterns or not. A ledger is a net, and time swims through it, and eventually something big comes through and tears the mesh.

Seven names. Within nine days of the eleventh of March. All of them omitted — that word again, *omitted*, as if the men had left their names at home on the hall table — all of them recorded nowhere in the official rolls, all of them known to me only because they had passed through my ward first, or because a comrade had whispered them to me over a dressing change, drunk on morphine and honesty in equal parts.

Corporal Hett. Private Dunlow. Private Sarey. Private Meech. Private Ostrand. Lance-Corporal Pyle. And Private First Class Eleazar Voss.

Wait, I thought. Not seven. Eight. Because three days after Voss's date there was another entry, one I had nearly forgotten, a man brought in delirious with a leg wound that had gone to rot, who gave his name twice — once as Tam Rutter and once, in his fever, as *Rutter, C Company, third platoon, don't write it down* — and died anyway, and whom I had written down precisely because he asked me not to, which tells you everything about my character that you need to know.

Eight names. One week. And every one of them, when I checked my marginal notes — I make marginal notes the way other women make pies, constantly and with more filling than necessary — every one of them belonged to the same company. C Company, Two Hundred and Ninth Foot.

And the Two Hundred and Ninth, as any clerk in the War Office could tell you, does not exist.

I do not mean it was disbanded. I mean it was never formed. I mean there is no such regiment, no such company, no muster roll, no pay book, no existence whatsoever in the great paper universe where armies actually live. I had assumed, over the years, that these men were deserters using a fictional unit, or spies, or madmen. Fevered soldiers say all manner of things. *C Company, Two Hundred and Ninth,* one of them had insisted, gripping my wrist hard enough to bruise. *We were there. Tell them we were there.* And then he had wept, and I had thought: concussion.

I was no longer thinking concussion.

I sat back in my chair in the honest hour and looked at the eight names and the paper scrap and the sleeping ward, and I performed the little exercise that has gotten me into every difficulty of my life: I followed the arithmetic to its end.

Men die. That is war's first law and its only reliable one. Men die and are reported, and sometimes the reporting is generous and sometimes it is cruel, but it is done in ink that can be read. These eight men had died — I was sure now, sure in my bones, surer than I have ever been of any diagnosis — and had not merely been misreported but *unreported*, expunged before the fact rather than after, which requires forethought, and forethought requires a reason, and the reasons available to a man in power are short. Cowardice. Theft. Or something done that must never appear in any account, something so heavy that eight men's entire existences were worth less than the cost of recording it.

They had been there. *Tell them we were there.* Somebody had made sure there was no *there* to tell.

And then, because my mind is a cruel instrument that never stops working even when I beg it to, I took the final step. The boy. The woman in the rain with her terrible instructions — *forget the face* — and the paper clutched in a child's fist like a talisman, or a debt. A printed slip bearing the name of a man erased from history. Who gives a child a thing like that? Only someone who believes the name is worth more than safety. Only a mother, or a widow, or whatever you call a woman whose husband has been deleted and who alone remembers the original text.

The boy was not abandoned. He was *archived*. Placed with me — why me, how did she choose me, did someone tell her about the nurse and her sewing basket? — the way you deposit a document you cannot keep and cannot destroy.

Someone knew what I did at night.

I looked up, absurdly, at the dark windows, half expecting the rain itself to be taking notes.

Outside, the sky was going the color of dishwater, which is dawn's way of apologizing for the night. In the ward, Private Callum turned over in his sleep and murmured a woman's name. Somewhere in the linen room a small boy slept between blankets, holding in his dreams a face he'd been ordered to forget. And in my lap lay eight lines of my own small handwriting, arranged in a column neat as graves in a soldier's cemetery, and for the first time in twenty years of quiet counting I understood that I was not keeping a record anymore.

A record waits. A record is patient; it assumes someday someone will ask. What I held was not a record. It was a question with weight and edges, and it would not sit quietly under a false bottom forever, and neither — I admitted, setting my jaw against my own better judgment — would I.

Nine names, I thought. If Voss made eight, and the company had a fourth platoon I hadn't yet touched, there would be nine. Companies run to nines; misery is orderly that way. Nine men of a company that never existed, all unmade in a single week, three winters ago.

I wanted those nine. I found, sitting there in the grey light, that I wanted them the way I have wanted water in a fever ward — completely, and without much regard for consequence.

Which was, of course, exactly when Sergeant Bracken lurched into my thoughts, because Bracken had been one of the convalescents the previous autumn, a drinker with a ruined shoulder and a tongue that loosened further with every tot, and once — only once, and I had filed it under *later* along with everything else — he had said to me, apropos of absolutely nothing, while I changed his dressing:

"You've got honest hands, Sister. Waste of honest hands, this place. There was a whole lot of us once, and now there's me, and I'm not even sure about me."

I had asked him what he meant. He had looked at me with the peculiar terror of the very drunk who have said too much, and asked for his shirt, and signed himself out against advice the next morning.

Bracken. Who had served — if the mumbled fragment in his file meant anything, and I now believed it meant everything — with the Two Hundred and Ninth.

The honest hour was over. The first cart rattled into the yard, and the lamps went pale, and the day began assembling its lies. I closed the ledger, hid it beneath the buttons and the theater of my life, and stood.

There is a tavern by the mill race called the Drowned Bell, and Sergeant Bracken drinks there from opening to closing, or did. Today I would find out what he knew. Today, apparently, I was the sort of woman who did things.

I put on my shawl, and I went to work, and I waited for evening the way one waits for a surgeon: dreading the knife, and knowing it is the only thing that will take the ball out.
