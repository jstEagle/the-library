## 7. The Company That Never Was

There are three kinds of men who drink in the Sable Hart at eleven in the morning: men who have lost everything, men who have lost nothing and are bored, and men like me, who have lost something so specific and so improbable that no one would believe us if we told them. I was on my third gin when she walked in, and I remember thinking—there's a woman who has never once in her life been asked to leave a tavern before noon.

She was a nurse. You can tell. Nurses walk into a room the way a surgeon walks up to a table: assessing, sorting, already deciding which part of you is broken and whether it's worth the trouble. She looked at me sitting there in my coat that had once been a uniform and was now mostly stains and memory, and I watched her decide I was worth the trouble.

God help her.

"You're Sergeant Bracken," she said. Not a question. She sat down across from me without asking, the way you'd sit down at the bedside of a man who couldn't object.

"Depends who's asking," I said. "If it's the regimental pay office, Bracken's dead. Died beautifully. Very heroic. There's paperwork."

"I'm Mirabel Dunmore. I work at the hospital." She set a small leather ledger on the table between us, and I will tell you honestly, gentlemen, that my stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept going. A ledger. Of all the objects in God's wide world to set down in front of a man, she chose the one that had ruined me.

"I don't know anything about ledgers," I said, and reached for my glass, and found my hand wasn't steady enough to make the lie convincing. It never is. That's the trouble with drink—it doesn't steady you, it just makes the shaking interesting.

"It's a list of names," she said quietly. "Men who died and weren't reported dead. Nine of them died the same week. All from the same company. A company that, according to every record in the War Office, never existed."

The gin in my glass had gone warm and ugly, but I drank it anyway, because my throat had closed up and something had to go down it. Around us the Sable Hart conducted its morning business: two carters arguing about oats, an old woman asleep in the corner with her chin on her chest, the barman polishing a glass he'd been polishing since the war began, probably. Nobody was listening. Nobody ever listens to Sergeant Bracken. Perhaps that's why she chose me.

"Where did you get those names?" I asked her.

"From the dead," she said. "Or nearly. Men dying in the ward say things they wouldn't say standing up. One of them gave me a name, then another, then I started looking." She leaned forward. "Sergeant. I think you were there."

* * *

Let me tell you about the Ninth Provisional Company, because no one else will, and because I've spent four years waiting for someone to ask, and now that someone finally has, I find the telling is like passing a kidney stone. Slow. Painful. But afterward, by God, you feel lighter.

We were formed in the spring, three winters back. That should have been the first warning—nobody forms a company in spring when everyone knows campaigns start in autumn. We were drawn from scraps: men transferred from garrison duty, a few recovered wounded, some recruits straight off the farms with their boots still smelling of cowshed. Our orders came not through the usual channels but through a civilian—a grey man, dry as a church pamphlet, who appeared at our first muster with papers signed by people whose names meant nothing and whose signatures looked, if you studied them long enough, suspiciously alike.

We called him the Clerk. He didn't mind. I don't think he heard it as an insult. To a man like that, being called what you are is just efficient bookkeeping.

"We're not on any roster," Lieutenant Hesse said to me one night, going through our supply requisitions. "I wrote to Division asking for our number to be confirmed. The reply said no such company exists."

"And yet we eat," I said.

"And yet we eat," Hesse agreed, and we left it there, because soldiers learn early that questions about paper lead nowhere good and questions about food lead somewhere worse.

For six weeks we marched. That's all we did. South along the river roads, then east, then south again, always following orders that arrived sealed and strange, always camped apart from the main army, always moved at night more often than day. The men joked that we were a ghost company, sent to haunt the enemy. The men joked about a lot of things. That's what the men are for.

Then, in the last week of harvest, we stopped marching.

* * *

I want to be careful here, because this is the part where a man starts drinking again, and Mrs. Dunmore has only bought me the one gin, and I've a reputation to maintain.

The valley was called Mereholt. There's nothing in it now. There was nothing much in it then—a village, a mill, some forty or fifty families, and a detachment of enemy irregulars, maybe sixty men, badly supplied, half of them boys, using the village as a winter staging point. Our orders described them differently. Our orders described them as an artillery position threatening the entire southern line, fortified, numbering three hundred. Our orders instructed us to take no prisoners because none could be spared for escort, and to report the engagement as defensive.

You understand what that means? It means somebody needed a battle. Needed one on paper. An enemy thrust in the south, repelled heroically by forces that did not exist, at a location chosen for its inconvenience—far from witnesses, far from roads anyone important might travel. And we were the brushstrokes. We were the ink.

We did it in under an hour. I won't dress it up. Sixty irregulars against a full company with fresh muskets and the advantage of surprise, and the rest—the villagers, the ones who ran toward the mill, the ones who didn't run fast enough—we were ordered to consider them combatants. Some of my lads did. Some of them fired high all morning and wept while they did it. Hesse walked through the aftermath with his sword drawn and his face white as flour, and when a boy of maybe twelve crawled out from under a cart, Hesse stood over him and shouted, in front of everyone, "This one is a combatant! Do you hear me? This one is a *combatant*"—and let him run, and no officer corrected him, and the boy vanished into the treeline, and that was the only mercy performed in Mereholt Valley that day, and it cost Hesse his career and very nearly his neck.

Three days later we were dissolved. Not disbanded—dissolved, the way you dissolve sugar. Orders came down: the Ninth Provisional had never been formed; its personnel were returned to their original postings; its existence was an administrative error and should not be referenced in future correspondence. The Clerk himself came to read it to us. He stood on a wagon bed with his grey coat and his grey face and read out that we had never existed, and I swear to you, Mrs. Dunmore, half the men believed him by the end. That's the power of paper. Tell a man he was never born and he'll spend the rest of his life apologizing for taking up space.

But nine of us didn't go back to any posting. Nine of us knew too much, or were judged unreliable, or were simply surplus—and you can't have surplus lying around after a massacre dressed as a battle. They died over eight days. Private Wren, "killed in a fall from a transport." Corporal Ames, "fever." Two drowned crossing a ford in dry weather—the report actually said *in dry weather*, someone's little joke, or someone's little test to see if anyone was reading. Hesse took a pistol ball behind the ear in a town where the war had never come. Each death ordinary. Each death alone. Each death recorded nowhere, or recorded wrong, until we survivors learned not to stand near windows and not to trust men bearing transfer papers.

There were eleven of us who saw and understood. Nine went into the ground unrecorded. I ran—I deserted, yes, put it in your ledger too—and Tom Quill, the last besides me, wrote to me last autumn from the capital, saying he was going to petition the Judge Advocate, saying truth was owed to those men. His letter came back undeliverable the second time I wrote. I've drunk to him every night since, which is every night.

* * *

Mrs. Dunmore hadn't interrupted once. I'll grant her that. Most people, when you start a story like mine, interrupt either to stop you or to hurry you, and she did neither. She just listened with those nurse's eyes, and when I finished she opened her ledger and turned pages until she found what she wanted, and read me names.

"Wren," she said. "Ames. Hesse. Cotter. The Vane brothers—not the colonel, privates, cousins of his apparently, though I don't know that yet for certain. Pell. Ostrer. Maddox. And a lieutenant listed as 'H——', cause of death blank."

"Quill made it ten," I said. "He just hasn't stopped breathing yet."

She wrote that down. In front of me. Added it to her book as though a drunk sergeant's word were a thing worth preserving in ink, and I felt something crack in my chest that had been holding steady for four years, and I had to look at the ceiling of the Sable Hart for a good while, at the smoke stains shaped like countries nobody's ever conquered, until I could speak like a man again.

"Why?" I asked her finally. "Why dig this up now? War's ending. Everybody's tired. The dead are dead."

"Because somebody is still trying to unmake them," she said. "And now they're trying to unmake a child."

I must have looked at her sharply, because she went on, low and quick—a boy left at the hospital door, carrying a printed scrap with one of the nine names on it, Wren's name, and instructions to forget a face. I listened, and the gin went cold in me, and four years of practiced stupidity fell away all at once, which is a feeling I don't recommend.

"The Clerk," I said. "If your boy is connected to Wren—if he's Wren's blood—the Clerk won't stop. Paper is his religion, and loose paper is his devil. He burned a company to keep a lie tidy. He'll burn anything."

"I know," she said. "That's why I need to know everything you remember. Names, dates, the seals on the orders, the faces. All of it. Can you do that?"

"For sober?" I said. "No, madam, certainly not. Sober I'm a coward with a headache. But for a price—" I tapped my empty glass, and she looked at me for a long moment with something that wasn't quite contempt and wasn't quite pity, and pushed the bottle across the table herself.

"One condition," she said. "Every name you give me gets written down exactly right. Theirs already weren't."

So I talked. Through noon, through the afternoon, through the carters leaving and the old woman waking and the lamplighter coming round, I talked, and she wrote, and for the first time since Mereholt I felt like a witness instead of a fugitive. Testimony is a burden, they say. Nobody tells you it's also a rope—you hold one end, and someone holds the other, and for a while you're not drowning alone.

It was full dark when she closed the ledger, and that's when I saw it—the thing that turned my belly over worse than any memory of the valley. Through the window, across the street, a man stood in the shadow of the cooper's shop. Grey coat. Still as furniture. He wasn't watching me; I'd have been flattered. He was watching her.

"Don't look," I murmured. "Grey civilian, dry as dust?"

Her hand went flat against the ledger on the table, slow, the way you'd calm a spooked animal.

"Sergeant," she said quietly, "how long has he been there?"

"Long enough," I said, "that I'd best teach you the only lesson the Ninth ever taught me worth keeping." I drained the glass and stood, and the room did its familiar slow tilt, and I gripped the table like an old friend. "When the paper comes for you, madam—run first, and write afterward."
