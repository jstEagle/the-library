## 8. Visitors After Dark

He came at the hour when the lamps are trimmed and the ward settles into that particular hush where the only honest sounds are breathing and the tick of cooling stoves—the hour when a nurse is supposed to be invisible, and usually manages it without even trying.

I knew before he introduced himself. I had known since morning, if I am truthful, when Sister Quill mentioned with elaborate carelessness that an officer would be calling on routine matters, *nothing for you to fuss over, Dunmore*, which in the grammar of hospitals means precisely the opposite. Routine matters do not require warning. Routine matters do not make a senior sister polish the receiving room brass twice.

So when the knock came—courteous, unhurried, two raps exactly—I was already three moves ahead of him on a chessboard he didn't know we shared. Or I told myself I was. Fear dresses itself up as foresight; it's cheaper that way.

"Captain Rook," he said to Sister Quill, and his voice carried the way good voices do in sickrooms, pitched low enough not to wake anyone, warm enough to seem like kindness. "Adjutant to Colonel Vane. A matter of records, nothing more."

*A matter of records.* There are four words I have come to dread more than any description of wounds. Wounds at least announce themselves honestly.

I was folding bandages at the far table, and I did not look up. This is a skill. You would be astonished how much of nursing is the art of not looking up—of making your hands so occupied, your posture so unremarkable, your whole self so thoroughly absorbed in lint and linen that a man can stand six feet away describing his business and never once think to wonder about you. Invisibility is not a cloak. It is a craft, practiced daily, and I have been apprenticed to it for six years.

* * *

Let me tell you what I moved that afternoon, and how, because the mechanics of fear deserve recording as much as its effects.

The ledger lived beneath my sewing basket in the linen press at the end of the ward—a false bottom Fenwick cut for me in October, fitted so well that Sister Quill had once sat her whole weight on the basket lid while complaining about her feet, inches from nine names she was paid not to know. But a hiding place is only as good as the day it is tested, and today, I judged, was testing day.

At four o'clock I sent Tobias down the back stair with a plate of bread and the story that Matron wanted help sorting type in the workshop cellar. He went willingly; the boy has developed a passion for the composing stick that Fenwick encourages with criminal enthusiasm, teaching him letters by having him set nonsense words. Tobias believes he is being given a treat. This is the great mercy of children: you can convert your lies into their games and feel almost innocent about it.

The ledger I moved at half past five, during the change of shifts, wrapped in a shawl as if it were laundry, carried across the yard in the ten minutes between the day nurses leaving and the night lamps being lit. The yard between hospital and workshop is eleven paces wide. I know because I counted them that evening, twice, once going and once coming back, and both times they felt like crossing a parade ground under review.

Fenwick met me at the door without a word. He had cleared a space behind the paper store, among the reams of rag stock that smell faintly of chalk and old rain, and he took the bundle from me and looked at me with those tired compositor's eyes and said only: "You'll want the bottom shelf. Damp rises."

That was all. That is Fenwick entire. A man who once forged documents for money and now spends his guilt in floorboards and discretion.

Then I washed my face, pinned my cap straight, and went back to be nobody.

* * *

Captain Rook conducted his inspection of our records between eight and ten, and I will grant him this: he did it charmingly, which made it worse. A blunt officer you can hate. A charming one you must perform for, hour after hour, while your heart keeps its own irregular count beneath your ribs.

He sat in the receiving room with the ward's admission book open before him, and I brought tea because that is what nurses do, and because a woman carrying a tray is furniture, and furniture is permitted anywhere.

"You've been here long?" he asked, on my third delivery of tea. He has a face built for friendliness—brown eyes, a crooked tooth when he smiles, laugh lines already starting—and he used it the way Fenwick uses ink, deliberately and in quantity.

"Six years, sir."

"Six years. And you remember everything, I expect." He said it lightly, tossing it, the way a man tosses a ball to see if a dog will catch it. "Names, faces. All these poor fellows who pass through."

"I remember what's written down, sir. Memory is a poor clerk. That's why we keep books." Which was, I grant you, a bold thing to say to a man hunting a book, but boldness is sometimes the best camouflage. Guilt makes people careful; innocence makes them tedious. I gave him tedious.

He laughed—genuinely, I thought—and turned a page. "Colonel Vane is very thorough about the rolls. Names in, names out. Everything accounted for." His finger ran down a column of admissions. "Everything squared."

"Yes, sir. Very satisfying, arithmetic."

Something crossed his face then, quick as a draft under a door. I couldn't read it—amusement or discomfort or recognition—but I logged it, because noticing things is the one habit I cannot break even when breaking it would be safer. He was too good at this to be comfortable with it. That was worth knowing.

"Private Halloway," he said suddenly. "Admitted the ninth of March. Discharged—" he checked the book, though I'd have wagered my last coin he already knew what it said, having read it before ever entering the building, "—discharged to garrison duties, is that right? You'd recall him perhaps. Young fellow, red hair."

I set down the teapot with the steady hands of a woman thinking about nothing but the teapot. Halloway. Not one of my nine. One adjacent—one whose name I had recorded on a wet Tuesday in March because the burial detail was three men short and the chaplain's ledger had no entry where an entry belonged.

"Red-haired private, March admission," I said, frowning mildly, the frown of retrieval, not resistance. "They all blur, Captain, if you'll forgive me saying so. Red hair, brown hair, no hair. What I'd recall is a wound or a fever. Was there something particular?"

"He died," Rook said, watching me. "In March. Here, according to some papers. But there's no burial record, no effects returned, no discharge. The man simply—" he smiled his crooked smile, "—evaporated."

"Then someone's arithmetic is off," I said, and passed him the sugar. "It's usually off, Captain. War runs on paperwork the way a mill runs on water, and the paperwork is always silting up. If you find the error, do let us know. Matron despises discrepancies worse than gangrene."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere down the ward a man coughed in his sleep, and both of us turned our heads toward the sound, and for a moment we were just two people in a hospital at night listening to a soldier cough, and I think he forgot to be an inquiry and I nearly forgot to be furniture.

"You don't ask what I'm doing here," he said at last, quietly.

"Should I, sir?"

"Most people do. Most people get nervous when the colonel sends someone."

"Do they." I collected the cups. My hands were perfectly steady; I had instructed them to be. "Nervousness is a luxury in a ward, Captain. We haven't the strength for it. If you find something wrong in our books, you'll find it was wrong before I could spell. Goodnight, sir—the night sister will bring you whatever else you need."

And I walked out of the receiving room at exactly the pace of a tired woman with trays to wash—not fast, not slow, not looking back, though every nerve in my back was a lit window facing him.

* * *

But the evening was not done with me.

At half past eleven, long after Rook had gone to his lodgings—or claimed to—the side door of the ward whispered, and I turned with a lamp to find Fenwick standing in the corridor with a face like a dropped proof sheet.

"He's still in the town," Fenwick said. "Rook. Took a room at the Ram's Head, facing the yard. My boy saw him settle in at ten with a bottle he isn't drinking."

"Watching the workshop or the hospital?"

"With a view of both doors? He's watching the space between them." Fenwick rubbed his ink-stained thumb along his jaw. "Mirabel. The boy. If that captain knocks on doors tomorrow instead of reading books—"

"He won't. Not tomorrow." I heard myself say it with more certainty than I owned. "Men like that work in layers. Today was the records; tomorrow he'll talk to the sisters, the orderlies, the laundress. He'll be thorough, because he's capable, and slow, because he isn't sure. We have days. Perhaps a week."

"And if we have less?"

I looked down the dark ward, at the shapes of sleeping men, at the lamp I held throwing its small stubborn circle against all that dark. Somewhere below us, under forty feet of workshop floor, a child was asleep on a bedroll among the paper reams, dreaming—I hoped—of toy soldiers and letters he was learning to set.

"Then we go faster," I said.

Fenwick lingered. He is not a man who says comfortings; he builds them instead, out of oak and hinges. "There's a second way out of the cellar," he said. "Through the old coal chute, into the mill stream culvert. It's foul and it's tight, but a boy could pass it, and so could a satchel of papers. I'll clear it tomorrow. Say nothing. Just—know it's there."

He left me then, and I stood my last hour of watch doing sums I did not want to do: how many steps from ward door to yard, eleven; how long a captain sits over a bottle he doesn't drink, hours; how many names in my ledger, forty-one; how many of them could be proven, if pressed, by anyone brave enough to dig—perhaps nine; and how much time a capable, uncomfortable man in a lodging-house window would need before he found the seam where my two buildings joined and pulled.

Near dawn, the ward door opened softly and Sister Quill came in for the early shift, and I handed over my report—routine, uneventful, nothing to fuss over—in the voice of a woman who had spent the whole night doing nothing but fold bandages and pour tea.

Which was, in a sense, true. It is simply that the bandages I folded were around a secret, and the tea I poured was poured for a man who had been sent to find it.

I went down the back stair into the grey light, crossed my eleven paces, and knocked twice on the workshop door—his knock, his rhythm, courtesy returned as warning. Fenwick opened it, read my face in an instant.

"Well?" he said.

"He's taken a room at the Ram's Head," I told him. "Facing the yard."

Below us, faint through the floorboards, I could hear Tobias laughing at something in his sleep.
