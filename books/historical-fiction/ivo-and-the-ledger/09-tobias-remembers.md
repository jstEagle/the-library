## 9. Tobias Remembers

The soldiers lived in a wooden box under the workshop stairs, and I found them on a Tuesday, though I did not know it was Tuesday then. I did not know much of anything then except that the cellar smelled of ink and oil and that Mirabel's hands were always cold in the way that river water is cold—surprising at first, then something you got used to.

There were thirty-one of them. I counted twice to be sure, because counting was the one thing I was allowed to be good at. Twenty-nine marched in red, two in blue, and one had lost his head entirely and stood for nothing at all, which Mirabel said made him a colonel.

She laughed when she said it. Fenwick did not laugh. Fenwick never laughed at jokes about colonels, which should have told me something even then.

"Mind the blue ones," Fenwick said, coming down the stairs with a lamp and a loaf cut into pieces that were mostly crust. "Those are ours."

* * *

I had been in the cellar four days by then, or five. The days down there were soft at the edges, like bread left in milk. Above me the hospital went about its business—I could hear it through the floorboards, the coughing and the boots and once, memorably, a man singing a song about a girl from Antwerp with all the wrong words in all the right places—and below me the presses slept like great iron animals that had been fed and were content.

Mirabel brought me down after the captain came. She didn't tell me why. She just knelt in front of me in the linen room, very close, so close I could see the small scar near her eyebrow that I later learned came from a kettle and not, as I had hoped, a bayonet, and she said, "Tobias. There is a game we're going to play. In this game you live downstairs."

I said, "Is there a winning?"

And she said, "Yes. Every morning you wake up, that's the winning," and then she held me hard enough that I understood the game was more serious than she'd made it sound.

* * *

Fenwick set the soldiers out on a plank across two barrels, red on one side, blue on the other, and he arranged them with the care of a man who had done real arranging once, in another life, with real soldiers.

"Now," he said. "You came here from somewhere. Everyone comes from somewhere. Show me."

"I'm not supposed to say."

"Who told you not to say?"

"Mama."

"And does your mama strike you as a foolish woman?"

I thought about it seriously. Mama read more books than anyone I knew and could mend a net and argue with a fishmonger simultaneously, so no, she did not strike me as foolish.

"No," I said.

"Then she told you not to say because saying is dangerous. But look—" He moved one of the blue soldiers along the plank, walking it with two fingers, little tin hops. "This one is you. And he has already done the dangerous part. He already traveled the whole road. What I am asking is only where the road went. The road doesn't mind being talked about. Roads can't be arrested."

It seemed true when Fenwick said it. Most things seemed true when Fenwick said them; that was his trouble, I learned later. He had spent years making untrue things seem true for money, and now the skill worked backwards on himself.

I looked at the soldiers for a long time. Then I took the blue one with the chipped base, because he was lightest, and put him far away at the end of the plank.

"That's home," I said. "The mill."

"The mill." Fenwick didn't write anything down. I noticed that. His hands stayed flat on the barrels like a man keeping them honest. "What kind of mill?"

"Grain. My papa worked the sluice gates before the war took him." I stopped. The word *took* did something in my chest when I said it, like a door closing on a draft. "He's coming back."

"Yes," Fenwick said, in the voice grown-ups use, and I hated him a little for it, and loved him a little too, because he didn't say *of course* or *certainly*. He said *yes* the way you say yes to a child who has asked if the soup is hot, meaning: yes, and also, let us not speak of the soup anymore.

* * *

Here is what I remembered that night, lying on the pallet among the flour sacks, listening to the presses tick as they cooled. Here is what came back to me in pieces, the way a plate comes back from the kiln in pieces, each fragment sharp in a different place.

We left at night. That was first. Mama woke me with her hand over my mouth before I was properly awake, so that I dreamed I couldn't breathe for a while before I actually couldn't, which is an interesting thing to have happen to you. She had a bag already packed, which meant she had been expecting the night for some time.

We went in a coach with a driver who never turned around. Not once. I watched the back of his head the whole journey and decided he was blind and navigated by smell, and Mama didn't correct me, which meant either that I was right or that the truth was worse.

Then a river. A boatman with a lantern he kept hooded, and Mama paying him with coins she took from inside her glove, and the boatman looking at me too long and saying, "Boy's got his father's chin," and Mama going very still beside me, stiller than I had ever seen anything go still, and saying, "My brother's chin," in a voice like ice over water.

Then a town I don't remember the name of, and a man called the Clerk.

* * *

The Clerk was not what I expected, which is most of what I remember about him. I expected the important men to be large, because in my experience largeness and importance went together like bread and butter. But the Clerk was small and dry and neat, with fingers like pencils, and he sat in the back room of a chandler's shop surrounded by paper—paper in stacks, paper in rolls, paper hanging from strings like laundry.

"You'll be the boy," he said.

I didn't answer. Mama had a rule: never confirm what a stranger already knows.

He smiled like I'd passed a test. "Good. Your mother raised you right, which is more than can be said for most of this country." Then he looked at Mama and his smile changed into something else, something flatter, and he said, "The pension arrangement stands. The widow's portion. Provided the name stays buried."

"He isn't dead," Mama said.

"On paper he's been dead three years," the Clerk said, and picked up a stamp, and stamped something, and the sound of it went through me like a nail through board. "Everything else is sentiment."

That was the phrase I carried out of that room like a stone in my shoe: *everything else is sentiment.* I didn't know what sentiment meant. I asked Mama on the next coach and she said, "It means love, when powerful men want to dismiss it," and then she cried, quietly, facing the window so I would only hear it and not see, and I learned what sentiment meant after all.

* * *

But the middle of the story—that was the part the toy soldiers pulled out of me, days later, on the plank across the barrels. The middle was Papa.

Because before the coach and the river and the Clerk, there had been the company. Papa's company. Nine men, and I knew every one of their names the way you know the names of the months, because they came to our house and ate our bread and once they carried our piano up a hill for Mama's birthday, complaining magnificently the entire way, and Corporal Voss played the fiddle badly enough that dogs attended from a mile around.

They were supposed to be a supply company. That's what the boys at school said when I mentioned them—*supply company*, said with a curl of the lip, the safe ones, the ones who never fought. I didn't know then that none of the other children were wrong and none of them were right.

Fenwick laid out nine blue soldiers in a line. He didn't ask me anything. He just set them up and waited, and something in the arrangement—the neatness of it, nine small men standing where nine real men had stood—loosened the thing in my chest that had been locked since the night of the hand over the mouth.

"They went out in October," I said. "Papa wrote Mama it was a supply run. Wagons and such. And then—" I moved the nearest blue soldier forward, off the plank edge, into the dark between barrel and wall. "And then the letter came back. About the ambush. Enemy action. All nine."

"All nine," Fenwick repeated softly.

"All nine at once. Papa used to say you'd sooner lose nine coins than lose nine men in one spot, unless somebody put them all in one spot on purpose." I looked up at Fenwick. He had gone a strange color, the gray of old paper. "Was it on purpose?"

Fenwick was quiet for a long time. Down in the cellar the lamp hissed and somewhere above us a patient laughed in his sleep.

"Tobias," he said finally, "there are things I know how to do with paper that honest men can't undo. And I think—I think someone did one of those things to your papa's company. I think those nine men were sent somewhere they were never meant to come back from, and then the sending was erased, and the erasure was signed." He gathered the nine blue soldiers into his palm very gently, the way you'd gather eggs. "Your mother didn't run from grief, son. She ran from whoever did the grieving math."

* * *

I cried then. I am not ashamed to report it, though I was ashamed then, seven years old and furious at my own face for leaking. Fenwick didn't tell me to stop. He sat down on the flour barrel and let me cry against his apron, which smelled of lead and lamp oil, and when it was over he said only, "Your mother is cleverer than all of them put together, do you understand? Clever women hide well. If she got you out, she got herself out."

"She left me," I said, into the apron. "At the door. She said forget the face."

Fenwick was quiet again. When I looked up he was staring at the nine soldiers in his palm as if they were letters in a language he'd invented and never meant anyone to read.

"Forgetting the face," he said slowly, "is what you do to keep the face alive. It's a terrible instruction and it was the correct one. But Tobias—" and here he took my chin, gently, and turned my face up to the lamplight, "—she is alive. Do you hear me? Dead mothers don't arrange coaches and hooded lanterns and blind drivers. Dead women don't leave sons at doors with instructions. Living, hidden women do that. Which means somewhere within a week's ride of here, your mother is hiding, and hating every day of it, and planning."

"Planning what?"

"Getting you back. That's all any of this is, from her side. Everything else is sentiment." He smiled, thin and crooked. "And I've recently decided sentiment is the only thing worth printing."

* * *

It was Mirabel who understood the rest, when Fenwick told her that evening, standing at the bottom of the stairs with the lamplight making shadows of both of them while I pretended to sleep on my pallet and listened through the gap between sack and wall.

"The mill," Mirabel said. "He said the mill. Fenwick, there are perhaps forty grain mills within three days' ride, but a widow in hiding needs work no one questions and a room no one visits, and millers' wives always need—"

"A widow's portion," Fenwick said. "The Clerk said it himself, if the boy heard right. They're still paying her. Someone delivers money to her, regular as rent, which means someone knows exactly where she is, which means—"

"—which means she's not hiding from Vane," Mirabel said. "She's being kept. Like a receipt." A pause, and then her voice dropped into something harder than I had ever heard it, harder than I would ever hear it again until the night of the fire. "They haven't killed her because she's the proof they control. And the moment we print the names, the proof stops needing to exist."

Above them, in the dark, I lay with my heart going like a drum, and I understood two things at once, the way you understand thunder and lightning are the same event seen in different places. I understood that my mother was alive and findable. And I understood that finding her might be the very thing that killed her.

I did not sleep that night. I lay among the flour sacks and rehearsed the road in my head—the coach, the river, the chandler's shop—walking it backwards, mill-ward, and somewhere past midnight I made the decision that a seven-year-old probably shouldn't be allowed to make.

In the morning I climbed the stairs before anyone was awake, found Mirabel at her table with her ledger open before her like a bible, and said:

"The mill had a red door. And a willow on the stream side, and a wheel that only turned half the year. And Mama said, in the coach, when she thought I was asleep—" my voice shook, and I steadied it the way Papa would have, "—she said, *we'll see the water again, the water with the half-wheel.* So you don't need to search forty mills. You need to find the one with the half-wheel and the willow. And you need to hurry. Because the Clerk stamps things, and everything he stamps stops existing."

Mirabel looked at me for a long moment. Then she pulled the ledger toward herself, dipped her pen, and on a fresh page, above the names of the dead, began to write the coordinates of the living.
