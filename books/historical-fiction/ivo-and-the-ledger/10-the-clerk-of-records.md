## 10. The Clerk of Records

I have commanded men who charged cannon with nothing but a sabre and a bad attitude, and I have never once seen them look as afraid as Colonel Hollis did when he said the word *civilian* in my office that October morning. He said it the way one says *tumor*. He had reason to. In my experience, soldiers kill by accident and apologize; civilians kill on purpose and send an invoice.

The Clerk arrived at four o'clock, which was tactically clever of him, because at four o'clock every officer in the garrison is either drunk, pretending not to be drunk, or writing letters home about how much they miss plum preserves. No witnesses. He came without escort, without uniform, without even a decent coat — a small grey man in a small grey coat, the sort of person you would step around in a corridor and never remember stepping around. His name, if he had one, I never learned to my satisfaction. Everyone called him the Clerk because he looked like what happens when a filing cabinet learns to walk.

"Colonel Vane." He set a leather satchel on my desk with the tenderness of a father laying down an infant. "You've been busy."

"I've been thorough," I said. "There's a difference, though I understand it's not fashionable in your profession."

"And what is my profession?"

"Arson," I said, "with correspondence."

He smiled at that. It was the first and last genuine thing between us for some hours.

* * *

Let me be honest, since no one is auditing this particular ledger: I did not invent the Ninth Company. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, in the privacy of my own skull, where I keep all my best work. The order came down through channels so clean that following it backward would take you to God, or at least to someone with a similar relationship to accountability. A company exists on paper. It draws pay, rations, boots. It fights no battle anyone can visit afterward. And then, when its purpose is served, it dissolves like sugar in wet ink.

The purpose, in this case, was arithmetic. The Ministry required a victory at Kolvary that the field could not provide, so the field was adjusted. Three hundred enemy dead became nine hundred. The surplus six hundred needed a source, and the Ninth Company — gallant, doomed, wiped out holding the ford against impossible odds — was the source. Very moving. I wrote part of the dispatch myself, and I will confess that the line about the colours never falling gave me some satisfaction. You may judge me. You were not there. Neither, notably, were the six hundred men I killed with a pen, but that has never stopped anyone from judging.

The Clerk's role was the paper architecture: muster rolls, pay ledgers, a chaplain's letters to grieving widows written in a hand so beautifully generic that three different women swore they recognized their husband's loops. He built the Ninth Company the way a carpenter builds a staircase — something you climb, something that leads somewhere, something that does not care who falls down it.

And then, apparently, something had gone wrong with his staircase.

* * *

"There is a woman," he said, when we were seated and I had poured him wine he did not touch. "At the garrison hospital. She keeps notes."

"Nurses keep notes. Temperatures, bowel movements, the usual poetry."

"She keeps names." He opened the satchel and produced a single sheet, copied in a clerk's fair hand — his own, I presumed, since I doubted the hospital employed anyone with handwriting that neat. It was a list. Nine names. I recognized all nine before I reached the fourth, which is the kind of recognition that arrives in the stomach rather than the mind.

"Where did you get this?"

"That is not the relevant question."

"The relevant question—"

"The relevant question," the Clerk said, with the patience of a man explaining arithmetic to a dog, "is whether she has nine names or eleven names or forty names, and whether she has them alone or with witnesses, and whether she understands what she has. So far she appears to understand very little. That is her advantage. Ignorance is slow, but it is also quiet."

"They're common soldiers' names," I said. "Dead men have no advocates."

"Dead men have mothers," he said. "Mothers have priests. Priests have bishops, and bishops have debts, and debtors talk. Paper does not stay where you put it, Colonel. That is the entire tragedy of my profession. A soldier who deserts can be shot. A rumor can be drowned in brandy. But a piece of paper with a true thing written on it — that survives fire, flood, indifference, and occasionally its author. Loose soldiers are a discipline problem." He tapped the sheet with one grey finger. "Loose paper is a verdict."

I looked at him for a long moment. Small man, mild face, hands like folded documents. And underneath, the same thing I had always suspected: fear. Not of me. Of paper. A man who builds houses out of words spends his whole life listening for wind.

"You came a long way to tell me a nurse writes things down."

"I came a long way," he said, "because three winters ago I commissioned a printing job from a workshop in this town. One job, paid in advance, client anonymous, plates destroyed — or supposed to be. Last month a boy walked into a village two counties east carrying a printed scrap that should not exist, and the woman who found it should not exist, and now the scrap is missing and so is the boy and I do not know where either of them is, and I am a man who knows where everything is. Do you begin to see the shape of my week?"

* * *

I did begin to see it, and I hated seeing it, because the shape of his week was rapidly becoming the shape of mine.

We sent Captain Rook into the hospital — charming fellow, good at making women feel listened-to, a talent I have never possessed and have learned, on the whole, to survive without. Within days Rook reported back that yes, there was a nurse named Dunmore, quiet, competent, unremarkable, who kept a sewing basket, and whose sewing basket, according to an orderly Rook bribed with two shillings and a compliment, went everywhere she went including places sewing baskets have no business going.

Two shillings and a compliment. The empire runs on less.

"A basket," the Clerk said, when I told him. "She carries her evidence in a sewing basket."

"Would you prefer a safe?"

"I would prefer," he said, "not to still be having this conversation in a month. Colonel, let me explain how these things end, because I have watched several end and survived more than I can say. They end one of two ways. The first way: the inconvenient person is arrested on some unrelated charge — theft, sedition, immorality, we have a full catalogue — the papers are seized, the matter is contained, and ten years later someone finds the papers anyway in a storeroom during an inventory and everything burns twice. The second way—" he paused, and I watched him decide to trust me, or decide to make me complicit, which amounts to the same transaction wearing different clothes. "The second way is faster."

"Fire," I said.

"Fire," he agreed, with the satisfaction of a physician whose diagnosis finally gets said aloud.

Here is what nobody tells you about burning things down: it is the most honest act available to a bureaucracy. Everything else we do — the falsified rolls, the invented companies, the dispatches describing battles that happened only in the Ministry's morning room — everything else pretends to be something it isn't. Fire does not pretend. Fire says: this existed, and now it must not, and I lack the patience for subtlety. When a government burns a building, it is confessing in the only language it speaks fluently.

But I am getting ahead of myself. At that point I was still pretending to consider alternatives, because a colonel must be seen to consider, even when considering alone.

"An investigation," I said. "Quietly. We could bring her in, search her effects—"

"And find what? If she has already hidden the ledger — and she has, women like that hide things the way other people breathe — then we have arrested a nurse and taught her that we are frightened. Fear is fertilizer, Colonel. She will plant everything she has."

"And your alternative?"

"Accidents," the Clerk said mildly, "are underappreciated."

* * *

I dismissed him at dusk and sat with the lamp and thought, which is the activity I am actually paid for, whatever the paperwork claims.

Here is the trouble with alliances between a soldier and a civilian: the soldier thinks in territory. Take the hill, hold the bridge, retreat in good order. The Clerk thought in documents, and documents do not have terrain. You cannot flank a ledger. You cannot take its surrender. Every solution I proposed, I proposed in the grammar of battle, and every one of them he batted away with that grey patience until I understood that we were not speaking the same language at all — that he was not trying to win anything. He was trying to return the world to the state it had been in before the nurse picked up her pen. He wanted silence. Not victory. Silence.

And I realized — sitting there with the lamp hissing at me — that I wanted something else entirely, and that this made us more dangerous to each other than any tribunal ever could.

Because the Clerk was right about one thing and wrong about everything else, and the thing he was right about was this: loose paper is a verdict. I had spent three years building the Ninth Company out of nothing, and I knew precisely how flimsy nothing is. One ledger in the wrong basket. One broadsheet off the wrong press. One boy carrying one scrap across one river. The entire edifice — my promotions, my dispatches, the very respectable reputation I had assembled with such industry — stood on paper, and paper stands on nothing at all.

You cannot court-martial a rumor. You cannot hang a widow's memory. But you can burn a building, and buildings contain presses, and presses contain type, and type contains — if you are unlucky, if you have been careless, if some forger three winters ago kept his plates out of sentiment or greed — the whole truth, arranged letter by letter, waiting for thumbs to screw it tight.

I called for my horse. My adjutant appeared with the remarkable speed of a man who listens at doors.

"Ride to the workshop district," I told him. "Find out who supplies the printer Fenwick with lamp oil. Find out how much he keeps on the premises. Find out what his insurance situation is, who his creditors are, and whether he has family." I pulled on my gloves. "And find out whether the nurse Dunmore has been seen entering or leaving the premises after dark."

"Sir?" said the adjutant, who was young enough to still believe orders should make sense.

"Lamp oil," I said, "is a famously careless commodity. Stands to reason a print shop full of paper and flame will come to grief eventually. Stands to reason it might come to grief sooner rather than later, given how careless everyone is these days." I settled my cap. "Stands to reason."

He rode off to reason, and I returned to my desk, and I began — because I am thorough, as I told the Clerk, and thoroughness is its own reward — to draft the report I would eventually send to the War Office concerning the tragic fire at the Fenwick printing workshop. It is easier to write such things in advance. The facts settle better when you lay them down fresh and let them age.

I was three sentences in — *at approximately the eleventh hour of the night, fire was discovered in the lamp-oil store* — when the adjutant returned, earlier than any sensible errand allowed, and stood in my doorway breathing like a landed fish.

"Well?"

"Sir," he said. "The printer's apprentice says the nurse has been coming most evenings. And sir — the master, Fenwick — he's been setting type. Whole pages of it. By lamplight, behind locked doors."

I put down my pen. Somewhere outside, a church bell rang the hour, indifferent as arithmetic.

"What pages?" I asked, though of course I knew. I had known since the woman at the hospital first picked up her pen, maybe since the day I first picked up mine. Some questions you ask only to hear the shape of the answer.

"He wouldn't say, sir. But the apprentice says they've ordered paper. A great deal of paper. Enough, sir, for hundreds of copies."

Hundreds of copies. Hundreds of little verdicts, folded and flying on the wind like the world's most patient birds.

The Clerk had warned me about loose paper, and I had nodded along like a man being warned about weather. But the Clerk thought in documents, and I think in territory, and suddenly — horribly — I saw the terrain at last: a town, a garrison, a capital, doors and doorsteps and market stalls, every flat surface in the country a place where a broadsheet could land and be read and be remembered, and no army on earth able to unring that particular bell.

It would have to be tonight, then. Not within the fortnight. Tonight.

I reached for my cloak, and I gave the order that I would spend the rest of my life, however long that proved to be, insisting I had no choice about.
