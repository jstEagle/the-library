## 11. Ink Under the Floorboards

There is a particular smell to a lie that has been kept warm for thirty years. It smells like lamp oil and lead dust and the inside of a man's own mouth when he swallows the same sentence every morning. I know this because I have been swallowing mine since before Mirabel Dunmore was old enough to hold a needle, and on the third of December, in the year our long war finally began to cough itself toward its end, I put down my composing stick, looked at the woman who had been hiding a child in my cellar for eleven days, and decided I could not swallow it one more time.

"You've been staring at that paper scrap like it owes you money," she said.

"It does," I said. "In a manner of speaking."

She was sorting bandages at the workbench we'd cleared for her, because the woman could not sit still any more than she could stop noticing things. That was her whole trouble, of course. Noticing. In a garrison town, noticing is a disease with a short course and a fatal prognosis. I had survived thirty years precisely by cultivating blindness—a professional affliction of printers, who see everything that passes through their hands and admit to seeing nothing.

"Mr. Fenwick." She set down the bandage. When Mirabel used your full name, you knew you were about to be noticed whether you liked it or not. "The boy's scrap. You recognized it the night he came. I watched your face do something complicated and then pretend it hadn't."

"My face," I said, "is a press of considerable age. Things shift in it. Gears slip."

"Whose watermark is it?"

I could have lied. I want that on the record, whoever keeps records after us—probably no one, which was rather the point of what I did next. I could have lied, poured myself the last of the gin, and gone on being a respectable master printer until respectable men came with torches and made the question moot.

Instead I knelt down on the cold flags of the workshop floor, in full view of God and Mirabel Dunmore, and pried up the board beside the big Stanhope press.

* * *

You should understand what a printer's cellar actually is, because the army never has. Every workshop in this quarter sits atop a second workshop—the one that exists between midnight and dawn, where paper is aged in ovens and seals are pressed from moulds carved by men with steady hands and flexible consciences. Mine was better than most. For six years, in my youth, I had been one of the best forgers on the continent, and I do not say that out of vanity but out of arithmetic: everything that happened afterward, good and ill, flowed from how good I had been.

Under the floorboards: forty reams of stock, three sets of official seals, a plate for excise stamps that had hanged two men already (not my doing; the men who commissioned it hanged themselves, in the ordinary way of commerce), and a small hand-press I called the Widow, because it had outlived everyone who ever knew its whole history.

Mirabel looked at all of it for a long moment. The lamp guttered and steadied.

"You're a criminal," she said at last, and there was something in her voice—not horror. Relief, if anything. I have thought about that relief a great deal since. It is a terrible thing when the discovery that your friend is a forger comes as good news.

"I was," I said. "I retired. Respectability is only crime with a shopfront, anyway. Half the officers who buy my hymnals forged their commission papers. The other half forged their courage."

"The scrap in the boy's hand—"

"Is mine." I took it from the tin where she kept it wrapped in linen like a relic, which in fairness it was. "Look at the chain lines. Look at this—" I turned it toward the lamp. "A printer's flaw. A nick in the laid mould, here, top left corner. Every sheet from that mould carries it. My mould. I made this stock three winters ago, four hundred sheets, for a client who paid in advance and never came back twice. Clients like that don't come back twice. Clients like that are buying something they intend to burn or bury."

"And the printing on it?"

"That I didn't do. Someone else used my blank stock. Which means someone else has access to my materials or bought surplus off the man I sold to, and either way—" I stopped, because the next part was the part I'd been chewing on for eleven days. "Either way, the paper that tells us who this boy is can be traced to this shop. To me. The trail doesn't start with the child, Mrs. Dunmore. It starts under my floor."

She sat down slowly on the bench. Above us, faint through the joists, Tobias was asleep in the loft among the empty paper bales, and I confess we both glanced upward at the same moment, the way people do when the silence overhead suddenly feels precious.

"Tell me about the client," she said.

"There is no client to tell about. Paid through a factor. But the job itself—I printed nine hundred documents. Company muster rolls. Commission sheets. A complete administrative history of a military unit that did not exist." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the way it does when I am setting type I don't agree with. "I told myself it was paperwork for some quartermaster's scheme. Ghost soldiers on the rolls so someone could draw rations and pay for men who weren't there. Common enough. War is mostly accounting with occasional interruptions of noise."

"But?"

"But ghost companies get dissolved, Mrs. Dunmore. They get struck. Nobody prints *nine hundred* documents for a company meant to vanish. Nine hundred is a company meant to be *found*—found, inspected, believed in. You build a paper soldier when you need him to have died somewhere specific." I looked at her and saw that she had already arrived where I was going, because she had a ledger under her sewing basket and had arrived everywhere months before the rest of us. "Someone needed a unit that had officially existed, officially fought, and officially lost a certain number of men. And then needed the same unit erased, so no one could ask the men's families what really happened."

"Nine names," she said quietly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"My ledger. Nine names, struck in the same week. All belonging to a company that appears in no other record I've ever seen." She laughed, one short syllable with no humor in it at all. "I thought I was keeping a list of oversights. Clerical cruelties. Officers too proud to report their losses."

"And instead you were keeping evidence."

* * *

We did not speak again for an hour. There are silences that are absences and silences that are presences, and this one was the second kind. I cleaned the Widow. She wrote. At some point she brought out the ledger—that forbidden little book—and set it on the workbench between us, open, as though it had been waiting thirty years for a floorboard to open too.

"I need to say the thing I brought you down here to say," I told her, "and I need you to let me finish it, because if you interrupt me I will lose my nerve, and I have very little left. It went into the gin."

She folded her hands. Waiting. She was good at waiting; nurses always are.

"A broadsheet," I said. "One page. Your nine names, the company's true history, whatever Bracken gave you in testimony, the dates, the discrepancies in the casualty returns. Set in my best type, run on the big press, five thousand copies in a single night. Before the year turns." I wiped my hands on my apron, though they were clean. "Before the war ends, Mrs. Dunmore. Because the day peace is signed, every record in the War Office gets boxed and sealed and carted away to be forgotten by a new administration with new lies. Paper dies of storage. But a broadsheet—paper that travels, that lands on tables and doorposts and chapel doors in a hundred towns—that kind of paper doesn't die. It breeds."

"You're proposing treason," she said.

"I'm proposing *publication*. Treason is merely the government's opinion of it."

"And the presses can do it? Truly?" Her voice cracked slightly on *truly*, and I understood then that she wanted this with a hunger that frightened her, and that she had come down here every night for eleven days hoping I would offer and praying I wouldn't.

"The press can do it. The man operating the press can do it. I still set a clean page, whatever else I am." I sat down across from her. "But before you answer, you should hear the cost, and you should hear all of it, because I've sold documents to desperate people for thirty years and I know how the transaction ends."

"Tell me."

"Printing makes you visible forever. Ink is the opposite of forgetting—you said yourself that's why you keep the ledger. Well. The ledger is private. One copy, hidden in a basket, deniable. A broadsheet is a confession with five thousand witnesses. The moment that page leaves my press, you are no longer a nurse who noticed too much. You are the woman who published the count. They will not wonder who did it. They will *know*. Your name, or mine, or both, on every officer's desk from here to the capital within a fortnight." I held up one finger. "First: Vane. If he is half the bookkeeper I think he is, he already suspects you. Printing confirms it. He will not need to search anymore. He will only need to punish."

Second finger. "Second: the boy. Today he is a rumor in a cellar. Afterward he is the surviving son of a murdered man, and there are men whose careers depend on murdered men having no sons."

Third finger. "Third: me. I will not run. I'm sixty-one years old, my feet are flat, and my life is in these machines. Fenwicks do not evacuate. We go down with the shop. It's a family tradition of stupidity."

I put my hand flat on the workbench, next to the open ledger.

"Now hear the other side of the arithmetic," I said. "Because I've done it, over and over, these eleven nights. Five thousand copies. Say half are burned by frightened innkeepers. Say half of what remains is read by people who do nothing. That still leaves hundreds of hands, hundreds of memories, hundreds of kitchens where someone reads nine names aloud. The War Office can unpublish a report. They cannot unpublish a *town*. And when the century turns, Mrs. Dunmore—when everyone is drinking to the new year and the new era and forgetting the old one—your names will still exist, somewhere, in ink, in the hands of strangers. That is all immortality anyone gets. It's the only kind I can manufacture."

For a long time she looked at the ledger. Then she looked at me, and her eyes were wet and furious, which I have learned is what courage looks like in the best people—it certainly never looks like that on officers.

"If I say yes," she said, "I stop being able to protect the boy by staying invisible. Invisible was his armor."

"Invisible is also his erasure," I said gently. "Vane doesn't need to find him, Mrs. Dunmore. He needs only to wait. Boys in cellars grow into boys on streets. You cannot hide a child for fifteen years. But you can make it so that when he walks out into the world, he walks out under his father's true name, into a country that has read the truth. That's armor too. Different weight."

She pressed both palms against her eyes. Above us the house ticked and settled. Somewhere down the street a patrol's boots kept their stupid perfect time.

"There's a middle path," she said finally, muffled. "Copies in trusted hands. Quiet distribution. No name on the masthead. We could—"

"—we could whisper," I finished. "Yes. And whispers die in a week. Forgive me, but you've read the ledgers of this war. Everyone whispered. Everyone chose the safe version, the delayed version, the version with fewer risks. Nine hundred documents' worth of someones chose it, and the result is a company that never existed and nine graves without names." I leaned forward. "You once told me the officers strike the dead because remembering costs too much. You've been paying the cost alone for three years, in secret, in pencil. I am offering you the chance to send the bill to the men who ran up the debt."

Silence again. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"I'm afraid," she said at last, simply, the way you'd report a symptom.

"Good," I said. "Only fools print unafraid. Fear is what keeps the register clean." I rose, knees cracking like green firewood, and laid my hand on the Stanhope's frame, cold iron, my oldest colleague. "You don't have to answer tonight. Think on it. Pray on it, if you still do that sort of thing. Talk to the boy's mother when you find her. But answer by Friday, because type takes two days to set properly and I'll not rush the composition of the most important page I've ever—"

The bell above the workshop door rang.

At half past midnight. With three soft, unhurried strokes, the way a man rings when he wants you to know he is not in a hurry because he does not need to be.

Mirabel was on her feet with the ledger in her hands before the third stroke faded. I blew out the lamp. In the dark, under the smell of ink and lamp oil, we listened to boots cross my front yard—two pairs, maybe three, and a lighter step behind them, courteous almost, the step of a man who had been raised to knock politely before arresting people.

"Through the cellar," I breathed, guiding her shoulder in the blackness. "Down, and stay down. The floorboard goes back over the Widow. The ledger goes with you."

"Mr. Fenwick—" she whispered.

"Go. And Mrs. Dunmore—" I couldn't see her face, only the shape of her hesitation, and I found, rather to my surprise, that I meant what I said next completely. "Whatever you decide about Friday—decide it as a woman who has already counted the dead. Don't let them teach you to stop."

The boards went back over the light. I climbed the stairs to my door, straightened my collar, arranged my face into the expression of a tired old tradesman woken by idiots, and opened it before they could knock twice.

"Master Fenwick." Captain Rook lifted his hat, smiled his charming smile, and stepped past me into my shop as if he owned the lease. "Forgive the hour. We're making inquiries, and I find I do my best thinking around printing presses. May I ask what you keep under your floorboards?"
