## 12. Rook's Kindness

I had been a soldier for eleven years and a liar for ten of them, and I want it understood from the outset that I was good at both. Good soldiers make the best liars. Both trades require you to keep your face still while something important dies behind it.

My orders regarding Saint Verity's Hospital were simple, the way all dirty work is simple when someone else has written it down. Captain Elias Rook, charming, unremarkable, fond of drink he did not finish and opinions he did not hold, was to frequent the hospital ward attached to Fenwick's printing workshop. He was to make himself liked. He was to ask small questions with large meanings folded inside them, like notes sewn into bread. And if — Colonel Vane had paused delicately on this point, as though the possibility pained him personally — a certain woman of the ward kept certain papers of an unusual character, then Captain Rook was to confirm it and withdraw, and other men with less charm and more boots would handle the rest.

"A nurse keeping notes," Vane had said, tapping one gloved finger on his desk as if the finger itself were the accusation. "Women keep notes on everything, Rook. Recipes. Grudges. It is their way of arithmetic." He smiled at his own joke, and I smiled back, because that is what I was paid to do.

So I began visiting Saint Verity's in the manner men visit hospitals: carrying oranges, wearing my best coat, letting the matron believe what matrons prefer to believe about captains.

* * *

The ward smelled of carbolic and wet wool and, underneath, the honest animal smell of men healing or failing to heal. Two long rooms joined by an arch; forty beds, give or take the ones they set up in the corridor when the trains came in; and along the far wall, windows that looked out onto the workshop yard, where through the glass one could watch the print-shop boys hauling paper like men hauling snow before a thaw.

She was there on my first morning, of course. Mirabel Dunmore. You cannot mistake her unless you are determined to, and I had arrived determined to. She was neither pretty in the way that forgives a man for staring nor plain in the way that releases him from it. She was *attentive*, which I have since learned is rarer than either. When she bent over a soldier's dressing, she bent the whole of herself, as if the wound were a letter she was reading closely for fear of missing a postscript.

"Captain." She straightened when she saw me, and took inventory of my oranges, my coat, my smile, and filed all three somewhere behind her eyes where I suspected things went to be forgotten properly. "Are you wounded?"

"Not yet."

"Then you're lost. Wards are at the back, past the arch. The well-meaning visitors stand by the stove and get in the way."

"I'll stand by the stove," I said, "and try not to mean well too loudly."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not a smile, but the ground where a smile might build, should the survey come back favorable. It did not, that day. But she let me hand her the oranges, and she counted them without appearing to count them, and I thought: *Vane is wrong about this one, and being wrong about her will cost him, and God help whoever is standing nearest when the bill comes due.* I did not yet understand that the person standing nearest would be me.

* * *

Here is what no one warns you about infiltration: to gain trust you must be trustworthy, and trustworthiness is not a costume you can remove at night without the help of a lady's maid and considerable moral anesthesia. By my fourth visit I knew the names of thirty men in those beds. I knew Private Hollis kept a beetle he called the Provost under his pillow. I knew young Tammas, both arms splinted to the shoulder, needed his face shaved every third day and pretended to sleep through it because he could not bear to be seen needing so small a kindness. I knew the convalescents played cards with a deck missing the queen of hearts, and that they'd voted — actually voted, with raised hands — that the queen had deserted rather than died.

And I knew, in the way one knows weather before rain, that the ward loved her. Not sentimentally. Soldiers love practically: they love what works. When Mirabel Dunmore told a dying man he would live, he lived, or he died believing it, and both outcomes counted as victories in the only ledger that mattered on that ward.

*Ledger.* The word was already finding its way into my thoughts. That should have warned me.

"You're staring, Captain," she said one afternoon, catching me at it over the rim of her tin cup. We stood at the window, watching sleet fail convincingly at being spring.

"I'm observing. It's a professional habit."

"And your profession?"

"Presently? Standing near stoves and meaning well quietly."

She almost laughed. Almost. The almost was worth more to me that week than the colonel's entire salary was worth to him, and I noted this with a professional habit of my own: alarm.

"They say you were at Corvane," she said. Casual as passing the salt.

"I was near Corvane. There's a difference the size of a river."

"Were your orders to be near, or to be in?"

"Madam," I said, "you interrogate like a provost's clerk."

"You flatter me. Clerks ask questions they intend to answer."

We looked at each other across a silence with edges on it, and I understood two things simultaneously. First: she knew precisely why I was there. Second: she had known for days, and had decided I was more useful curious than gone. Which meant the hunter had been hunted since his second visit, and had spent a week preening in a trap thinking it sunlight.

I finished my tea. It tasted of tin and humility.

* * *

Vane summoned me at the fortnight's end, and the interview went as interviews with Vane always went: he spoke in figures.

"The hospital returns," he said, "show eleven dead last month. My own arithmetic suggests fourteen." He let the number sit between us like a coin on a table. "Paper drifts, Captain. Paper is blown about by any wind. One wonders what becomes of the drifted pages."

"One wonders," I agreed.

"You wonder nothing else? You've seen nothing? No little books. No lists. No woman writing after hours?"

"I've seen a woman who writes letters home for men who can't write," I said, which was true, and therefore the most effective lie available to me.

"Hm." Vane looked at me the way an auditor looks at a column that won't sum. Then he dismissed me with the figure of a salute — the shape of it, none of the warmth — and I walked out of that office knowing I had just chosen a side, or been chosen by one, and that the paperwork of my soul had been amended without my signature.

* * *

It was Tammas who found it. Or rather, Tammas's blanket.

The boy — nineteen, both arms ruined, forever apologizing for the trouble — had twisted in his sleep, and the wool had dragged off the cot and taken with it, from beneath the mattress frame, a folded square of paper that skittered across the floorboards and stopped against my boot like something asking to be stepped on.

I should have stepped on it. Let me be clear-eyed about my own failures. A good liar steps on things. A good liar knows that found paper is a loaded weapon with no safety catch, that ignorance is the only uniform that never gets searched.

I picked it up.

It was a page torn from a larger book — cheap paper, ruled in a fine, disciplined hand, the sort of hand that keeps accounts or prayers. And it was a list. Names, ranks, regiments, dates. Nine entries in a column, and beside each, in smaller script, a note. *Struck from morning report, 14th. Widow notified by regimental letter, false date. Struck again from casualty roll, 16th — twice erased, once is error.*

Nine names. I read them once and felt the floor of my profession tilt beneath me, because I recognized the company designation. Everyone in the garrison who could read recognized it, or rather recognized its absence: a company recorded as disbanded before ever seeing action, its number retired like a racehorse broken in the gate. Except here were nine of its men, with dates of death that clustered like flies on meat, all in one week, all struck from rolls that officially contained them.

I folded the page along its existing creases, exactly, carefully, the way you'd fold a flag.

"Captain Rook?" Tammas, awake, watching me with the flat, ancient eyes of boys who have seen supply wagons full of worse things than ammunition. "That'll be Nurse Dunmore's. She keeps... she writes things. For the men."

"For the men," I said.

"Yes, sir. She remembers everybody." He said it simply, the way you'd say *the sun rises*, and it was somehow the most damning testimony I had heard in eleven years of hearing testimony. She remembers everybody. In an army whose entire machinery ran on the principle that nobody need remember anybody, this woman had made herself the opposite of an institution.

I put the page back beneath the mattress frame myself, tucking the blanket as Tammas's mother might have, if his mother had been a captain in a compromised cavalry regiment.

"Why didn't you step on it?" I asked myself later, in my quarters, with the lamp turned low and the drink I did not finish sitting reproachfully by my elbow. I am trained to answer such questions honestly; it is the one luxury of my trade. And the answer was this: because the list had names on it, and names are the one thing I have never learned to treat as arithmetic. Vane saw numbers where I see faces. That was the whole distance between us, nine names wide, and I had crossed it picking up a piece of paper off a floor.

* * *

I went back the next day. Of course I went back. A spy who stops visiting is a confession in a good coat.

The ward was quiet, that hour between dressings when even the stove seems to rest. She was at the far table, writing — letters home, presumably, for the men who couldn't. I stood at a respectful distance and watched her pen move, and I confess I was looking for the ledger the way a man looks for a scar on a face he's decided to trust anyway.

"You have questions," she said without turning around. "You've had questions since the second week. Ask one and I'll decide whether to lie."

"Just the one, then." I sat down across from her, uninvited, and she permitted it, which from her amounted to an embrace. "Why do you do it? The letters. The remembering. Nobody pays you. Nobody thanks you. Half these men will forget your name by autumn."

"Then half of autumn will be mistaken," she said, still writing. "And I do it because someone must keep the count, Captain. Wars end. Armies disband. Paper burns wonderfully. If no one holds the numbers, then the dead become whatever the living find convenient." Her pen paused. "You know that already. You've known it longer than I have. That's why you haven't reported whatever it is you found under Tammas's bed."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the yard a print-boy shouted about ink.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, and we both enjoyed the fiction, briefly, like sharing a cigarette.

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't."

I rose to go, and at the arch I stopped, because there is a limit even to a liar's discipline. "Nurse Dunmore. Whatever you're counting — count it carefully. There are men above my rank who think numbers are the enemy, and they fight dirty."

She looked up then, fully, and for the first time I saw her afraid — not of me, which would have been flattering, but of what I represented, which was merely the weather coming in.

"Thank you, Captain," she said. "That was almost kind."

"Don't tell anyone," I said. "I have a reputation."

* * *

That night I wrote my report to Vane, and here is what it said, in its entirety and in my best staff-hand: *Hospital routine unchanged. Ward orderly. Nothing of note.*

Nothing of note. Nine names, a woman who keeps the count, a boy I'd glimpsed twice moving between beds like a small ghost the ward had collectively agreed not to see — nothing of note. I signed it, sealed it, and understood as the wax cooled that I had just committed treason against the clearest order of my career, and that I felt, in place of guilt, something suspiciously like relief.

But I am a professional, and professionals know that relief is the feeling most likely to get you killed. So I will note, for the record I keep in the only place left to me — the inside of my own skull — what I did next. I checked the creases of my conscience, folded it along its existing lines, and slid it beneath the mattress frame of my soul.

And then I asked myself the question that has kept me up every night since: how long does a thing stay hidden under a blanket, in a war, in a garrison town, in the last winter of a long war — before somebody else finds it?

I had a terrible suspicion I already knew the shape of the answer. It wore gloves, and it counted everything twice.
