## 13. The Widow at the Mill

The boy drew maps the way other children drew monsters—badly, obsessively, and with more truth in them than anyone wanted.

He had taken a stub of pencil to the back of a spoiled proof sheet Fenwick had given him to scribble on, and produced a winding line that was either a river or a very ambitious snake, two squares he called *the mill* and *the oven house*, and an X placed with the solemnity of a boy who understood exactly what X meant. Below it all, in letters that leaned like tired soldiers: *MAMA IS WHERE THE WATER TURNS THE WHEEL.*

"He draws it every morning," I told Fenwick, keeping my voice low over the clatter of the workshop. "Same map. Same crooked wheel."

"Children repeat what they can't say," Fenwick said, not looking up from his type case. His hands were steady among the letters; his eyes weren't. "You're going to follow it."

"I hadn't planned on admitting that aloud."

"You never plan anything aloud, Mirabel. You just come back with soot on your hem and a new name for the ledger." He set a line of type down with unnecessary care. "Vane's men are still asking after you at the market. Rook was seen at the customs house Tuesday. If you walk out that road, you walk out as someone worth following."

"I've been worth following since a woman put a child in my arms and told me to forget his face," I said. "The only question is whether anyone's been clever enough to do it."

Fenwick didn't argue. That was the trouble with Fenwick—he'd been a forger long enough to know when a thing was already written and only needed copying out.

* * *

I went on a Thursday, because Thursdays the hospital received its linen delivery and Matron counted sheets with the ferocity of a woman avenging a personal insult, and no one noticed whether I was among the living or the laundered. I wore my greyest dress and carried a basket of mending, which made me precisely as interesting as a fence post. The mending was even real. I have found that the best disguises are simply true things arranged in the wrong place.

Tobias's map, translated into geography by Fenwick—who knew every road within thirty miles the way a sinner knows his scripture—put the mill at Harrow's Bend, where the Lor stopped being a stream and started having opinions. It was a four-mile walk along the towpath, then a half mile of lane between hedgerows that had given up on elegance and settled for thorns.

I will not pretend I walked there intending only to look. A person who intends only to look does not bring bread, a shawl, and a lie prepared about visiting a cousin. But I did intend, sincerely, to be sensible. To stand at a distance. To confirm the woman existed and could be reached later, when the roads were quieter, when Vane's inquiry had grown bored, when the ledger was safer than it was—which is to say, never, and I knew it, and I walked anyway.

The mill announced itself before it appeared: a low groan of timber and water, the sound of something old being asked to work past its retirement. Then the lane bent and there it was—a squat stone building straddling the river, wheel turning with the patience of geography, sacks of grain stacked by the door like fat brown sentries. Beside it, the oven house Tobias had drawn, its chimney breathing thin smoke into a sky the colour of dishwater.

And beside the lane, leaning against the hedge as if he'd grown there, a man with a soldier's slouch and a civilian's coat.

I did not stop walking. Stopping is confession; the whole art of invisibility is to move at the speed of boredom. I passed him at twelve feet, close enough to see he was chewing a grass stem with the theatrical patience of a man being paid by the hour, and I catalogued him the way I catalogue symptoms: boots army-issue, coat borrowed, eyes doing arithmetic on me while his face did nothing. There was a second man somewhere—I felt him rather than saw him, the way one feels weather change—and when I reached the bend in the lane I risked the oldest trick in a nurse's book and glanced back as if checking the sky.

There. By the towpath gate. Watching the mill, not watching me, which was worse. They weren't hunting nurses.

They were waiting on Livia Dunmore—or whatever her true name was—and they were patient about it.

* * *

The miller's wife was deaf in one ear and generous in both, which is the finest combination a stranger can hope for. I presented my cousin story; she believed roughly none of it and helped me anyway, because I mentioned a sick woman needing quiet lodging and she had buried three children and a husband's temper, and women like that do not ask which war drove you to their door. She set me to sorting apples in the yard—an occupation that explained an hour of loitering better than any lie—and it was there, between a windfall and a bruised pippin, that the woman came out to fetch water.

I knew her before I saw her face. I knew her by the way she moved through the yard like a guest in her own life, shoulders drawn in, eyes sweeping the hedges in the same pattern Tobias's swept the ward. Children inherit their parents' fears the way they inherit chins.

She saw me looking and the bucket went still in her hands.

"Livia," I said, very quietly, and used the name from Tobias's fragments, and watched her go white as milled flour.

We stood in the apple yard with a basket between us and a deaf woman humming twenty feet away, and I told her the truth in the smallest number of words I have ever managed: *Your son is alive. He is warm, fed, and sleeping above a printing press four miles from here. He draws your mill every morning. He calls the oven house by name. He has told us everything a seven-year-old can tell, and some of it is about a man called the Clerk, and there are men in this county who would very much like that story to end with the boy silent.*

She did not cry. I had expected weeping; instead she set the bucket down with exaggerated care, as though grief were something that spilled, and said, "Is he well? Truly? Not just fed."

"He has opinions about porridge and a hatred of buttons. He is flourishing in every direction except patience."

That almost made her smile. It died halfway, the way smiles do in wartime.

"They told me to leave him with you," she said. "A woman came to me—she said the hospital nurse kept names. Said you wrote down the ones they erased. I thought—" Her hands twisted in her apron. "I thought anyone who writes down dead men's names might keep one live boy safe."

"She told you to forget his face."

"She told me forgetting was the only way either of us survived." Livia looked toward the lane, toward the hedge, toward the man chewing his grass stem with such unconcern. "She was right, wasn't she. You've come to tell me I was right."

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I performed in that yard, standing among apples, while the wheel turned and turned and turned:

If I took Livia back with me that afternoon, we would walk four miles of open towpath past at least two watchers, carrying the single piece of evidence—the mother—that connected Tobias to the phantom company and the phantom company to Colonel Vane's tidy books. If they took us on the road, they would not merely have a nurse and a widow. They would have confirmation. Everything Vane suspected but could not prove would walk up to him wearing our faces.

If I left her, the watchers would eventually grow tired of waiting, or they wouldn't, and either way the woman who had trusted a stranger with her child would remain in a building her son could draw from memory, one careless question away from discovery. The miller's wife was kind. Kindness is not armour.

And beneath both options, quiet as a coal under ash: the ledger. Nine names, the massacre, the dates, the signatures Bracken had given me—all of it copied now, thanks to Fenwick, into forms fit for the press. Every hour I spent being visible on the Harrow's Bend road was an hour someone might trace back to the workshop, and the workshop held not just my ledger but Fenwick's whole confession in ink, and the presses that could turn confession into a thousand copies.

One woman against the last chance of making nine dead men matter.

That is how these choices arrive. Not as grand dilemmas with trumpets, but as a basket of apples and a bucket going warm in the sun, and a woman looking at you with her son's exact eyes, asking without asking whether you have come to save her or to count her among the costs.

"The men on your lane," I said. "How long?"

"Since the frost. Two of them, sometimes three. They ask the miller about travellers. I keep to the oven house." She paused. "There's a third now. Newer. He watches the road more than the mill."

Newer. Since Tobias arrived at my door. Since Bracken talked in a tavern. Since the inquiry sharpened from curiosity to knife.

"If I take you today, they follow us home," I said. "They find the boy, the ledger, the press. Everything burns together."

"And if you don't?"

"I don't know. I truly don't. That's the honest answer and I apologize for it."

Livia looked at the wheel a long time. When she spoke again her voice had gone flat and practical, a voice I recognized—I heard it in myself every time I opened the sewing basket.

"My husband's name is in your book," she said. "One of the nine."

It was not a question, so I did not insult her with one. I nodded.

"Then listen to me, nurse, because I have had four months in an oven house to think clearly, and clear thinking is all I own." She stepped closer; the smell of woodsmoke came off her. "Do not spend the nine names to buy one widow. If they take me, I am a woman hiding under a false name, and that is a crime, and crimes get trials, and trials are slow. But your book—they can't trial a book. They have to burn it. So keep the book out of the fire and let them waste their winter watching a mill."

"That is the most self-destructive bargain I have ever been offered."

"It is the only one on the table." And then, softer, with the flatness cracking just once: "Tell him I watch the water turn the wheel. He'll know what it means. Tell him I count the turns."

She picked up her bucket. I picked up my apples. Between those two ordinary motions we concluded the entire negotiation, and I walked out past the man at the hedge feeling his gaze slide across my back like a hand searching pockets, and I did not stop, and I did not look at the mill again, because looking is confession and I could not afford to confess to anything except fruit.

* * *

Fenwick met me at the workshop door before I'd cleared the threshold, which meant Tobias had told him something, which meant the boy read my face better than most generals read maps.

"You found her," he said.

"I found her, and two watchers, and a third who's new, and a bargain I hate." I set the basket down among the type cases, absurdly domestic amid the machinery. "She stays. For now. She gave the order herself, and she's right, and being right has never tasted worse."

Fenwick studied me. Then, gently, the way he handled worn type: "And the road? Did they mark you?"

"One of them looked at me the way a clerk looks at a figure that won't sum. Not alarmed. Interested." I pulled off my gloves finger by finger. "Aldous. If they're posting a third man on a mill lane, Vane isn't investigating anymore. He's collecting. He knows there's a shape to this and he's gathering the corners."

"Then we've less time than we thought."

"We had no time to begin with. We had a fiction of it." I looked toward the stairs, where a small creak announced a small boy pretending not to listen. "How soon could the presses run? Truly. Not comfortably—truly."

Fenwick was quiet for the length of three wheel-turns' worth of silence, though no wheel turned here, only my heart performing its arithmetic.

"With help, day and night?" he said. "Four days to set the full sheet. Two more to print enough copies to matter."

"Then tonight we begin," I said, and upstairs the creaking stopped, and somewhere across town a colonel was no doubt also deciding how to spend his four days, and only one of us intended to use them for counting.

The knock came at the workshop door an hour after dark—two soft raps, then one, the signal Bracken used when he had news worth the risk of bringing it.

From the sound of his knuckles, the news was bad.
