## 14. A Bargain in the Chapel

### Mirabel

The chapel had been a chapel once, before the army annexed it for the storage of things the army did not wish to look at: broken wheelbarrows, mildewed tents, crates of hymnals nobody could account for and nobody dared burn, because burning hymnals was the sort of thing that got chaplains writing letters. Now it smelled of dust and tallow and the particular melancholy of furniture that has outlived its purpose. I went there because it was the one room in the garrison where no officer had a reason to be.

I should have known better. Captain Rook was a man whose entire profession consisted of having reasons to be places he ought not.

He was sitting in the third pew when I came in, hat off, hands folded, looking so much like a penitent that my first thought was that he'd come to pray, and my second — arriving hot on the heels of the first — was that no one who looked that much at peace with God could possibly mean me any good.

"Sister Dunmore," he said. He used the honorific loosely; I was no more a sister than he was a saint, but nurses collected titles the way wounds collected lint. "You'll forgive the ambush. Your ward is a public place. This one is merely abandoned."

"Then we're well matched," I said. "I'm merely a nurse."

"Merely." He smiled at that, and it was not an unkind smile, which troubled me more than a cruel one would have. Cruelty I knew how to answer. Kindness from an inquiry was like warmth from a fire in a powder store — you appreciated it and you backed away all the same. "Sit down, Mirabel. Please."

Not Sister. Not Dunmore. *Mirabel.* I sat, because sitting gave me time to think, and because my knees had opinions of their own lately. The pew creaked under me like an accusation.

"You've been asking after a company," Rook said. "The Ninth Consolidated. You spoke to Sergeant Bracken twice last week, once in the Sable Arms and once outside the widow Cetch's house, where you thought no one saw you."

The cold that went through me then was not fear exactly. It was something older than fear, the sensation of a door opening somewhere far off in the house and not knowing which room it leads to. "Sergeant Bracken fought," I said. "Men who fought are permitted conversation. I wasn't aware the army had begun rationing that too."

"The Ninth Consolidated doesn't exist."

"Then we've had a very dull pair of conversations, he and I."

Rook leaned forward. In the grey light through the boarded windows his face lost its charm entirely, and what remained underneath was younger than charm and more tired. "Mirabel. I am going to say several things now, and you may believe none of them, but I would ask you to hold them all. First: Colonel Vane keeps a file on this hospital, and your name appears in it three times, each time in worse ink. Second: within the month, perhaps within the fortnight, Vane intends to submit a final report closing the matter of the Ninth Consolidated permanently, and matters closed by Vane stay closed the way graves do. Third—" he paused, and here the weariness showed its seams— "I was sent here to find whatever you're hiding. And I have found it. Or enough of it."

The ledger weighed nothing against my hip. It always did, when I thought about it. Four years of names, sewn into oilcloth, small as a psalter, heavy as a quarry if you believed otherwise. I kept my hands still in my lap and let him finish, because a man talking will tell you everything if you give him silence enough to fill.

"There's a boy," Rook said quietly. "Seven years old or thereabouts, staying among the convalescents, whom nobody remembers admitting. There's paper — printed stock, very fine work, better than it ought to be — that connects him to a name that was struck from the rolls in the same week nine other names were struck. And there is a ledger. I don't know what's in it, but Vane suspects, and Vane's suspicion is the most dangerous weather in this province."

"And what does Captain Rook suspect?" I asked.

"I suspect," he said, "that I am standing in a chapel asking a nurse for help, which is either the beginning of wisdom or the end of my career, and I've long since stopped believing those are different things."

### Rook

She looked at me the way soldiers look at maps of country they've been ordered to take: measuring, unromantic, already costing it out in lives. I respected her for it. I'd spent two weeks in her hospital learning what she was worth, and most of what I'd learned had come from watching her hands. Steady with the wounded. Steady with the officers. Steady, I noted, even now, though I'd just told her — obliquely, delicately, the way one tells anyone anything that might get them shot — that she was being hunted.

"You propose to help me," she said at last. "Officers of inquiries don't help. They conclude."

"Officers conclude when concluding is safe. I've read Vane's figures. Nine men of a company that never existed, killed in an engagement that never happened, in a valley the engineers never surveyed. Do you know what struck me first, reading it? Not the lie. Lies are cheap; the War Office prints them by the ream. What struck me was the *tidiness*. Every number correct. Every column balanced. Somebody loved those numbers, Mirabel. Somebody counted them the way you count children — by heart, at night. Men who falsify carelessly leave loose ends. These were tied off with affection. And a man who loves his work that much will burn a workshop to keep it beautiful."

Her face didn't move, but something behind it did. I had learned to read her the way one reads weather — not the words but the pressure. She knew about the workshop. Whatever she and Fenwick were planning, they were planning it among the type cases.

"So here is my bargain," I said. "Give me the original ledger. Tonight, tomorrow, whenever you can bear to part with it. Not copies — the original, every page in your hand. With it I can delay Vane's report. A man in the Paymaster's office owes me a debt I intend to collect; a discrepancy raised in the right quarter freezes a final accounting for weeks, months if we're lucky. While it's frozen, you move the boy. Move the mother too, if you know where she is — and I think you do. When they're beyond reach, when the report comes down and the fire follows it, the ledger survives. Held by someone with rank enough to keep it breathing."

"And what," she said, "does Captain Rook gain?"

"Insurance," I said. "If Vane falls, I want to be holding the reason. If Vane rises, I want to be holding the only copy of the thing that could hang me alongside him. Either way, I live. That is my entire ambition, and I confess it without shame. I've seen what happens to honest men in this army. They make excellent examples."

"That's not insurance," Mirabel said. "That's ransom."

"It's both. Most things are."

She was quiet a long while. Somewhere beyond the boarded windows a wagon passed, and the whole ruined chapel shivered with it, a fine grey sifting of dust coming down on us like snow that had given up on being clean. I watched her decide, and I want to set down honestly, because honesty is the one luxury left to me, that I did not know which way she would go. I half hoped she'd refuse. Refusing would have meant she was smarter than me.

"You misunderstand what that ledger is," she said finally. "You think it's evidence. It's not. Evidence is dead paper; it sits in files and waits for tribunals that never sit. That book is a debt. Thirty-one names in it — thirty-one men the army declared alive who are not, or dead who were never allowed to have died — and every one of those names was written by me because some clerk above me was paid to write *nothing*. If I put it in your hands, Captain, it becomes leverage. Yours. And leverage gets traded. Leverage gets *spent*."

"Yes," I said. "That's generally what happens to evidence."

"And who spends it? You? You've just told me your ambition is survival. Survival makes narrow spenders. Vane's report is delayed — for how long? A month? Two? Long enough for me to move two people and hide a book. And then what? The report lands. The workshop —" she stopped herself, too late, and I saw her register the stop, and I saw her decide to let me see it, which frightened me more than anything else she'd said. "And then the matter closes. Graves stay graves. Your insurance sleeps in some strongbox until it's worthless or until someone more ruthless than either of us finds it. That isn't a bargain, Captain Rook. That's a slower version of hanging."

"Then counter," I said. "You clearly came to trade."

She almost smiled. It was the first true thing her face had done all evening and it undid me somewhat, which I record here as a warning to whoever reads this: never negotiate with a tired woman over the bodies of the dead, because grief has better arithmetic than duty and she will beat you every time.

### Mirabel

Here is what I knew that he did not know I knew: there was no original ledger to give. Not anymore. There was a ledger, yes, four years of my hand, but for six nights it had been lying open on Fenwick's composing stone, and Tobias — Ivo — had been sleeping above the presses, and every name in my book now existed in lead, locked in chases, ready to meet paper. The original was becoming a copy even as we sat in God's abandoned furniture. By the end of the week there would be hundreds of originals, and they would be everywhere, and nowhere, and that was the whole terrible point.

So I could afford to bargain with him. That is a strange thing to learn about oneself in a chapel: that safety, real safety, arrives not when you hide the dangerous thing but when you make it impossible to contain. I had spent four years guarding my little book like a relic. I was done with relics. Relics get burned along with the workshops that house them.

But Rook didn't know that. And a man offering ransom for a treasure he believes unique will pay nearly anything — including, I suspected, the thing he hadn't yet offered.

"Here is my counter," I said. "You delay Vane's report. Not for me. For yourself — you said survival; very well, survive. In exchange I will give you something worth more than my ledger." I leaned forward. "I will give you the *count*. Not the book — the arithmetic. The nine names of the company that never was. The date. The valley. The order of battle that puts Vane's signature three removes from the massacre, if you know how to read it. Enough, Captain, for a man in the Paymaster's office to raise holy hell, and enough for you to hang Vane with if it ever comes to that — but not enough to hang me. No handwriting. No witnesses. Just numbers, and numbers have no mothers."

"And the original ledger?"

"Stays where it is until the broadside — " I caught myself again, and this time I did not pretend to be sorry. "Until it's safe. You see, Captain, you've made one error in all your cleverness. You assume there is one book. There isn't. There is going to be paper on every door in this garrison town before the year turns, and when that day comes, your insurance will be worth exactly what truth is worth, which is everything and nothing, depending entirely on which side of the door you're standing."

I watched him absorb it. He was quick — quicker than Vane, I'd have wagered, though I'd have lost that bet inside a fortnight, as it turned out — and I watched him arrive at the future I was describing: the count published, unpublishable, Vane's tidy columns read aloud in markets. I expected anger. What I got was worse. I got relief. It crossed his face like light through a crack and he shut it away fast, but I'd seen it. He wanted to be beaten. Some part of him had walked into that chapel praying I'd hand him a way to lose gracefully.

"The broadside," he said slowly. "Fenwick's presses. You're going to print it."

"I'm going to do more than print it. But printing will do for now."

"If Vane learns —"

"He already suspects. You told me so yourself, not ten minutes past. His suspicion is weather, you said. Then we'd best build roofs."

For a moment neither of us spoke. The dust settled around us. In the pew behind mine, one of the forgotten hymnals had fallen open, and I read upside-down a line about the Lord knowing the number of our days, and thought that the Lord had kept sloppy books compared to mine, and that somebody ought to audit heaven.

"Terms," Rook said at last, and his voice had changed — flattened, professional, the flirt buried at last under the soldier. "I freeze the report through the Paymaster. I give you warning of any movement against the hospital or the workshop — a day, if I can buy a day; an hour if that's all there is. In return you give me the nine names tonight, written, unsigned. And Mirabel—" he stood, and up close his youth showed fully, and it looked a great deal like my own exhaustion— "when it goes wrong, and it will go wrong, you take the boy and you run and you do not send back for me. That is not generosity. That is the price. I won't be rescued by a nurse. I have a reputation as a scoundrel to maintain."

"Agreed," I said, and we shook on it, his hand dry and cold around mine, and I felt the bargain settle between us the way a splinter settles: small, deep, certain to fester before it ever heals.

Because here is what I did not tell him, though he will read it someday if these pages outlive their author: I gave him nine true names. But I held back the tenth thing — the signatures, the chain of command, the proof that reaches up past Vane to the civilian who built the phantom company out of nothing but ink and appetite. The Clerk, Fenwick called him. Rook bought the fingers of the truth. I kept the wrist.

Not because I distrusted him. Distrust would have been simpler, and cleaner, and kinder to us both. I held it back because I had learned — from Vane, from the war, from four years of watching men trade other men's deaths like currency — that everyone spends leverage eventually. Even the kind ones. *Especially* the kind ones; kindness just delays the transaction until it costs double.

I walked out of the chapel into the dark with the nine names gone from my keeping and the rest of it still warm beneath the floorboards of my conscience, and I remember thinking: this is how betrayals are seeded. Not in malice. In bargains. Two frightened people agreeing to trust each other in installments, each installment smaller than the last.

It was snowing when I came out — early, wrong, the winter arriving like everything else that year ahead of schedule. And halfway across the yard I saw the thing that turned my stomach cold and made me walk, not run, back toward the workshop lights: a lamp moving along the lane beyond the wall. Not one lamp. Three. Moving slowly, methodically, the way men move when they are measuring a building for something.

Measuring it, perhaps, for lamp oil.
