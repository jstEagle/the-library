## 15. What Burns First

There is a particular pleasure in arithmetic done properly, and I have spent thirty years cultivating a taste for it. Columns that balance. Totals that agree. A war, at bottom, is nothing but a very long sum, and I had always prided myself on being the man who carried the ones correctly—carried them, where necessary, out of sight.

So you will understand that it was not cruelty that brought me to the window of my office that grey Tuesday morning. It was bookkeeping. A ledger somewhere in this garrison town did not balance, and I have never in my life been able to sleep beside an unbalanced column.

Rook was late. That was the first figure out of order. Captain Elias Rook was a great many things—handsome in the way that makes widows forget their grief and their purses equally, charming enough to be sent into drawing rooms rather than ditches—but he had never once been late to me. Punctuality is the homage vanity pays to ambition, and Rook paid it religiously. When the clock on my mantel ticked past the quarter hour and his boots were still absent from my corridor, I found myself looking at the clock the way a moneylender looks at a debtor who has begun, suddenly, to smile.

He arrived at last with rain on his shoulders and an apology so smooth it might have been planed by a cabinetmaker.

"Colonel. The roads."

"The roads," I said, "have not moved since the Romans paved them. Sit down."

He sat. He crossed his legs. He arranged his face into its customary expression of intelligent deference, and I will grant him this: it was very well done. Most men, when they are concealing something, overcorrect. They become too relaxed, too agreeable, like furniture upholstered by someone terrified of lumps. Rook was better than that. But he was not perfect, because no one is, and I had trained myself for decades on imperfection—the smudged total, the name spelled two ways in one dispatch, the death recorded in the wrong month because some clerk could not be bothered to ask the widow when exactly her husband had stopped breathing.

"Your report," I said.

"You'll have it by Friday."

"I have your preliminary findings now, Captain. You wrote to me eleven days ago that the hospital woman kept *unusual notes*. Those were your words. Unusual notes. I have been chewing on the phrase for a week and a half, and I find I am still hungry. What notes? Whose names? How many pages?"

Rook looked at the fire. It is astonishing how many men believe that looking away from you conceals anything, when in fact it only tells you precisely where to look—in their case, at whatever the fire has reminded them of.

"There are notes," he said carefully. "She keeps a record of ward business. Fevers, dressings, dietary complaints. Very dull reading."

"And?"

"And I don't think it amounts to anything, sir."

There it was. Not the lie itself—I could have forgiven the lie, expected the lie, budgeted for the lie the way one budgets for candle wax—but the *shape* of it. He said *I don't think* where a loyal officer says *it doesn't*. Thinking is what I pay him not to do in my presence. An officer who thinks near a superior is an officer rehearsing an argument.

"You've read them," I said.

"I've glanced—"

"Elias." I used his Christian name the way one uses a key. "You have been in that hospital for five weeks. You have drunk tea with nurses until I'm surprised you don't float. You have charmed the matron, complimented the surgeon's hemorrhoids, and been seen weeping—actually weeping, do not deny it, a corporal told me—at the bedside of a boy from the Fusiliers. In five weeks you have given me four reports totaling perhaps six hundred words, and every one of them has the flavor of a man writing with his left hand while his right hand does something else entirely. So let us not spend the morning insulting each other. What did you find, and why have you decided, all on your own, that it doesn't matter?"

For a moment—and I want to be fair to him here, because fairness is simply accuracy applied to people—he almost told me. I watched the impulse move through him like weather. Then it passed, as weather does, and what remained was the captain: correct, regretful, closed.

"There may be a page or two of names," he said. "Deaths she's recorded that differ from the returns. It's likely sentiment rather than sedition. Grief makes women keep lists, Colonel. It gives them somewhere to put things."

"Grief," I said, "has toppled more governments than ambition ever did. Ambition wants a chair; grief wants a reason. Give me ambition over grief in any century."

He left soon after, promising Friday, and I stood at my window watching him cross the yard with his collar up, and I did the arithmetic I had been avoiding since he walked in.

A loyal officer would have brought me the notes by now. A frightened officer would have brought me the notes and lied about how he got them. Rook had done neither. Which meant Captain Rook had found something worth keeping, and had decided—for his own reasons, which I would extract eventually, the way one extracts a tooth, slowly and with appropriate tools—that he would rather hold it than hand it over. Insurance, probably. Men always imagine paper will insure them. It is the great romance of our age: that a document can protect you. Documents do not protect anyone. Documents merely wait, patient as graves, for whoever is strong enough to come collect them.

The problem was not that Rook hesitated. Hesitation can be managed. The problem was what his hesitation told me: that there was, in fact, something in that hospital worth hesitating over. Nine names. One company that had existed for eleven days in the spring three years past and had never, in any return since, existed at all. A company whose deaths had been distributed among other regiments' losses, diluted like poison in a well, until the massacre at Harrow Ford became simply *an engagement*, then *an incident*, then nothing but a bad mile of road that travelers avoided without knowing why.

I had not designed that solution. I want that understood, if anyone is ever in a position to understand anything about me. The Clerk came to me with the problem already shaped—a surplus of dead that could not be reported and could not be buried quietly, owing to the unfortunate presence of witnesses—and I merely provided what I provide: order. Structure. A way for the numbers to come out even. If the method I chose was paper instead of powder, that is because paper is cheaper, quieter, and does not leave marks on the floor. Every man at Harrow Ford died once. That they were made to die twice—once in the flesh, once in the record—was regrettable. It was also, and I say this with neither pride nor shame but with the flat honesty of an accountant, necessary. Wars are not lost by the dead. They are lost by the *counted*, when the count comes out wrong.

And now a nurse with a sewing basket—yes, I knew about the basket; Rook's corporals are observant even when their captain wishes them otherwise—had decided to un-carry three years of careful figures.

* * *

The Clerk arrived at dusk, as he always did, materializing in my office with the apologetic air of a man collecting rent. He was smaller than his reputation, greyer, with ink permanently ghosted into the creases of his fingers like a stain of character. He never used his name. He had a dozen, presumably, and treated them all as disposable stationery.

"She's still there," he said, without preamble, settling into the chair Rook had warmed. "The nurse. And now there's a boy."

My hand paused over the decanter. "A boy."

"A child of seven, more or less. Appeared at the workshop door in the rain six weeks ago. She's hidden him among the convalescents. Fenwick—the printer—is feeding him at his own table."

"Whose child?"

The Clerk smiled. It was the least pleasant thing about him, that smile: it contained no warmth whatsoever, only recognition, the satisfaction of a man confirming a figure he'd already penciled in. "You know whose."

Dunmore. Of course. Private Edmund Dunmore, Company K, the company that never was. The widow Livia Dunmore, who had declined to accept her arithmetic with the docility the War Office expected of her, and had vanished south with the one piece of evidence no falsified return could neutralize: a living face with his father's jaw.

"You've known about the boy," I said slowly, "and you're telling me now."

"I wanted to be sure of him first." He accepted the brandy I poured, held it to the light as though checking it for impurities. "He's been to the mill. To Livia Dunmore. Your captain followed her there—or someone did; my information is imperfect on that point. The family is reconnecting, Colonel. Slowly, clumsily, but reconnecting. And the nurse has been asking questions in taverns. Sergeant Bracken's taverns, specifically."

"Bracken is a drunk."

"Bracken is a drunk who remembers everything," the Clerk said mildly. "That's why we left him alive. A sober man who remembered would have to be silenced, and silencing creates questions. A drunk who remembers creates nothing. No one believes him; he barely believes himself; he wakes each morning with the whole thing reassembling in his skull like a puzzle he can't put down. It's almost elegant." He drank. "But elegance frays. And now the fraying has reached your hospital."

I sat down across from him. Outside, the rain had resumed, drumming on the glass with the patience of something that intended to continue long after both of us were gone.

"Tell me what you propose," I said.

"Propose?" The Clerk set down his glass. "Colonel, I don't propose. Proposing is what men do when they still believe events can be steered. I *observe*. And what I observe is this: loose soldiers are manageable. Loose soldiers can be posted, promoted, transferred, demobilized, decorated into silence or drunk into oblivion. Loose *paper* is another animal entirely. Paper copies itself. Paper travels. Paper sits in a drawer for thirty years waiting for someone with a grudge and good eyesight. There is a document in that hospital—possibly two, counting whatever the printer has in his cellar—and documents, unlike soldiers, cannot be reasoned with. They can only be present or absent."

"Burn them, then," I said. "A search. Confiscation."

"A search creates a record of searching. A confiscation confirms the value of the thing confiscated. No." He shook his head with genuine regret, and I realized, with a small cold movement in my chest, that he was not enjoying this. That was what made him dangerous. Men who enjoy this work make mistakes, eventually, out of appetite. "The paper must cease to exist *inconclusively*. Fire is the only verdict that closes the question forever. Ash cannot be cross-referenced."

"The workshop," I said. I do not know why I said it before he did. Perhaps I had already seen it, the way one sometimes arrives at a total before finishing the addition. "Lamp oil."

"Lamp oil," he agreed. "Old buildings, open flames, a tired master working late. Tragic. Common. Nobody investigates a printer's fire beyond the insurance, and Fenwick carries none, having reasons to avoid insurers. The hospital stands twenty feet away—you'll want the wind studied first, of course. We are not barbarians."

"When?"

He considered. "A fortnight. I'll need to arrange the oil man, confirm the watch rotations, ensure your Captain Rook is conveniently occupied. Speaking of which—" and here the regret in his face deepened into something almost like sympathy, which I found I hated worse than anything else he'd brought me that evening— "the captain is a complication. He's hesitating today. Next week he'll be choosing. You should decide whether he chooses with us or against us before the fire, not after."

"He's mine to manage," I said.

"Of course." The Clerk rose, retrieved his coat, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch. "One more item of arithmetic, Colonel, since you appreciate the discipline. The year ends in six weeks. With it ends the mobilization ledgers—everything gets rebound, recatalogued, shipped to the capital archives, and misfiled by clerks who have already forgotten the war. Time is doing half our work for free. Whatever that woman intends, she must print it, carry it, and distribute it before the doors close. She has a deadline whether she knows it or not." He pulled his hat low. "Make certain she never learns what hers is."

Then he was gone, and I was alone with the fire and the rain and the brandy, and I confess I sat for a long while doing nothing but listening to the clock.

* * *

People imagine that a man in my position feels remorse, or else feels nothing, and both camps would be surprised to learn that what I felt that night was *hurry*. A fortnight. Six weeks until the ledgers closed. Somewhere across the yard, in a cellar that smelled of ink and lamp black, a printer and a nurse were setting type by candlelight—I had no proof of it yet, but I have rarely needed proof; pattern is faster—and every hour they worked was a figure added to a column I could not permit to reach its total.

I wrote the orders that night. Not orders, precisely—suggestions, inquiries, routine postings, each one innocent alone, each one a stone placed in a wall whose shape only I would see until the wall was finished. Captain Rook to be detached to the capital on the twelfth, escorting dispatches. The fire brigade's winter inspection scheduled for the fifteenth, ensuring the equipment would be conveniently under review. A requisition of lamp oil for the workshop district, placed through a factor who owed the Clerk his freedom and his silence.

At the bottom of the page, before sealing it, I paused. It is an old habit of mine, this pause; I have taken it before every entry that mattered, and I have never once, in thirty years, failed to write after it. Some men pray at that pause. I check my columns.

Nine names. One boy. Two documents. Fourteen days.

The figures balanced. I sanded the page, sealed it, and set it by the door for the morning rider, and if my hand was steadier than my pulse, well—that, at least, is what the army trained me for.

Across the yard, through the rain, a single light still burned in the workshop. I watched it the way one watches a fuse one has not lit yet: with a strange, unhurried tenderness.

Burn first, I thought. Everything does, in the end. The only question worth asking—the only question I have ever been paid to answer—is what burns *first*, and who is standing close enough to feel the heat when it goes.

On the twelfth day, I would learn, the answer was: not fast enough.
