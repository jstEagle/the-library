## 16. Setting the Type

There is a particular silence that descends on a workshop when everyone in it knows they are about to do something unforgivable. It is not the silence of concentration, which is warm and busy and hums a little at the edges. It is the silence of church, or of the moment before a surgeon's saw. Fenwick called it "the quiet before the ink," and he said it the way other men say grace.

We had been at it since the lamps were lit, and I will confess something now that I confessed to no one then: my hands did not shake until the third hour. For two hours they were steady as a compositor's, and I took a foolish pride in that steadiness, and then Tobias turned over in his sleep above us — I heard the floorboard sigh through the ceiling, one small creak like a question — and after that my hands shook for the rest of the night, and I set type anyway, because shaking hands can still hold letters if you ask them politely enough.

"You're holding your breath again," Fenwick said without looking up. He could tell from across the room. He claimed he could hear a held breath over the sound of the press, which I never believed until that night, when there was no press running at all and he still heard mine.

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing the way a woman breathes when she's pretending not to be afraid. Shallow, up here—" he touched his own collarbone— "instead of down here." He put a hand on his belly. "Breathe down here. Fear lives high. Courage lives low."

"That's nonsense."

"Probably. But it's nonsense that keeps a man working." He slid another line of type into the composing stick with his thumb, each letter going home with a small click, click, click, like rain beginning on a window. "Besides, if we're hanged for this, I'd rather be hanged having breathed properly."

I have decided, in the years since, to forgive him everything, including that sentence.

* * *

The broadsheet was Fenwick's design, and it was better than anything I had imagined, because I had imagined a document and he had imagined a weapon. One large sheet, printed on both sides, foldable into quarters small enough to slip inside a coat. On the front, in letters tall enough to read across a street: THE TRUE COUNT. Beneath it, the nine names — Bracken's nine, my nine, the men of the company that never was — each with his rank, his village, and the date the War Office struck him from the rolls. Beneath those, the account of the massacre as Bracken had given it to me in the tavern, word for word where the words were fit to print, and marked plainly where they were not. And beneath *that*, the signatures: Bracken's laborious soldier's hand, Fenwick's cramped forger's loops, my own name, which I wrote last and which felt less like signing a document than stepping off a cliff and finding out, too late to matter, whether the air would hold me.

"We should print your ledger entry beside each name," Fenwick had said earlier, when we planned it. "Date received, source, the officer who signed the omission."

"They'll know it's me within the hour."

"Yes," he said, and went on setting type.

That was the whole of the argument. I have thought about it often since — how the most consequential conversation of my life lasted four words and a nod. There is something obscene about that, and something clean. The big decisions are never argued. They are only arrived at, the way you arrive at a river: one moment you are walking on solid ground and the next you are in it, and all the deciding happened somewhere back along the road without your permission.

* * *

By midnight we had the front side locked in the chase, and Fenwick pulled a proof, and we stood over it together while the ink dried, and I read my dead men's names in mirror image first and then, when the proof came, right way round, and I want to tell you that reading them made me feel triumphant. It didn't. Reading them made me feel as though I had exhumed them. Private Hollis, who owed me for two pairs of socks. Corporal Ames, who sang badly and constantly. Sergeant Oduya, whose name I had written in my ledger on a Tuesday and who had been, by every official account, never born, never enlisted, never shot in a beet field outside Marren and left there while his regiment was dissolved into other regiments like sugar into water.

"They look small," I said. "In print. Nine names on one sheet. Like it cost nothing."

"Ink always looks cheap," Fenwick said. "That's its genius. Paper and ink are the cheapest things in the world and the hardest to take back. Vane can burn this workshop to the cellar stones. He cannot burn four hundred sheets once they've left the yard. That's why he's coming, Mirabel. Not because we're dangerous. Because we're the last copy."

"He isn't coming yet."

"He's coming." He said it without drama, the way you'd say *it's raining*. "A man like Vane doesn't investigate for three weeks and then write a polite report. Rook's questions stopped being questions. The mill road has watchers on it — Livia saw them, you told me so yourself. We have days. Maybe fewer. So we don't sleep tonight, and tomorrow night we don't sleep, and when the run is done, we scatter these sheets wider than his fire can reach."

"And you?" I asked, though I already knew, and knowing it made my throat close. "When the run is done?"

Fenwick lifted the proof to the lamp, squinting at it the way he squinted at everything, as if all the world were a document with a forgery hidden in it somewhere. "Somebody stays with the presses. If they come before the run finishes, somebody works slower and louder and gives the rest of you the yard."

"There are ways out the—"

"There are," he agreed pleasantly. "For three of you there are. Now hand me the quoins, and stop arguing with an old man who has already decided the shape of his evening."

I handed him the quoins. I am not proud of how easily I let him win. I am only telling you what happened.

* * *

Somewhere past two, Tobias came down the stairs.

He was carrying his wooden soldiers — all six of them, cradled against his chest the way other children carry a cat — and he was wearing the expression of a boy who has done his sums on the situation above stairs and concluded that nobody up there believes anyone's going to bed.

"It's too quiet," he announced. "I can't sleep when it's too quiet. At the farm there were dogs."

"There are no dogs here," Fenwick said, "but there are biscuits in that tin, and if you sit by the stove and don't touch anything metal, you may keep watch."

"What am I watching for?"

"Lamp oil," Fenwick said gravely. "Fire loves lamp oil. Also rats, colonels, and bad luck. In roughly that order."

Tobias considered this job offer and accepted it, seating himself on the upturned crate by the stove with his soldiers drawn up in ranks on his knees. And I will say this for fear: it makes children of us all, but it also makes sentries of us all, and there are worse arrangements.

He watched me work for a long while. Setting type is hypnotic even to watch, I think — the pick-and-place of it, letter by letter, the case's little pigeonholes like a city seen from above. Finally he said, "Is that Papa's name?"

My hands did not shake. I had asked them not to, and for once they obeyed.

"Yes," I said. "It's there. Second line."

"Good," said Tobias, and offered no further comment, and ate his biscuit, and arranged his soldiers into a square — the kind Bracken had described to me, the kind that holds — and stayed awake with us until nearly four, at which point Fenwick carried him back up to bed the way you carry something you have decided, privately and permanently, to protect.

* * *

I want to record the labor itself, because it deserves recording. The world remembers fires and escapes; it does not remember the hours of picking letters out of a case. But that night was mostly that. Fenwick at the stone, setting the body text with forty years of skill in his fingers, faster than I could read it. Me at the second case, setting the names, because he said — and this I believe he meant — "You kept them. You should be the one who sets them." The names took me twice as long as they should have, because I checked each one against the ledger twice, and against my memory a third time, and spelled Oduya out loud twice because I refused, absolutely refused, to let a compositor's error be the last thing that happened to that man.

The signatures were the hardest. Bracken's mark, cut into a block of boxwood by Fenwick's own knife from the paper Bracken had signed. Mine, likewise. Fenwick declined to cut his own; I did it for him while he looked away and made jokes about vanity.

At some point near dawn-but-not-yet, he stopped, wiped his hands, and said: "Read it to me. All of it. Straight through. If we're going to commit the crime, we ought at least to know what the crime says."

So I stood under the lamp with the galley proof in my hands and I read the true count aloud in an empty workshop at three in the morning: nine names, one field, one order signed by a man who had never seen the beet fields of Marren and who had written *engaged the enemy with regrettable losses* in a report composed, I do not doubt, in a warm room with a glass of something at his elbow. My voice held. I had expected it not to. When I finished, neither of us spoke, and the lamp hissed, and upstairs a child breathed in his sleep, slow and even, like a tide that hadn't heard any of the news.

"Well," said Fenwick at last. "It reads well."

"It reads terribly."

"Yes," he said. "It reads exactly as terribly as it should. Terrible is the product. Come along — we have a forme to lock and perhaps two hundred sheets to pull before someone brings us our lamp oil."

And I laughed. God help me, standing over the evidence of a massacre with my name signed to its bottom edge, I laughed, and Fenwick laughed, and it was the laughter of two people on a ledge, and it was also, I maintain, entirely appropriate. Some things are too heavy to carry solemnly. You have to swing them.

* * *

We hung the first wet proof on the drying line by the window, where proofs always hung, and Fenwick looked at it swinging there — nine names turning gently in the draft, black against grey dawn — and said, quietly, without any joke in him at all:

"When this is over, whatever 'over' means, someone will ask why we didn't do it sooner. Or why we did it at all. They'll want it to have been complicated." He took down the proof, squared its edges with a tap of his palm. "It wasn't complicated. A woman kept count. A coward remembered. That's the whole machinery."

"You're not a coward."

"I forged documents for money for eleven years, madam. I am precisely the authority on my own character." He hung the proof back up. "But I'm a coward with a press, which is the next best thing to a brave man."

Through the window, over the rooftops toward the garrison road, I thought I saw — or told myself I saw, the distinction mattered less than I wished — a light where there should have been no light. A lantern, moving along the mill road, stopping. Moving again.

Fenwick saw it too. He didn't say anything. He simply began banking the stove, checking the ink fountain, laying the dampened paper in stacks with the unhurried economy of a man clearing a deck for weather. Above us, the floorboard sighed once more.

"Dawn in an hour," he said. "Sleep if you can. Tomorrow night we pull the run."

"The lantern—"

"Will still have been there whether you sleep or not. Sleep, Mirabel. Tomorrow we find out what it wanted."

I did not sleep. I sat beneath the hanging proof with my ledger open on my knee, reading my own handwriting in the grey light — all those nights of stolen minutes, all those names — and below the window, far off, the lantern on the mill road stopped moving altogether, and stayed stopped, like a held breath.
