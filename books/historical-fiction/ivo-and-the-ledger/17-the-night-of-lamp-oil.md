## 17. The Night of Lamp Oil

They came early. That was the insult of it, more than the fire itself—Vane had promised himself a fortnight, and a man who cannot keep his own promises to his own conscience will certainly break them with strangers.

I knew because the dogs started. Garrison dogs bark at everything: carts, cats, their own reflections in disgrace. But these three began in unison, a low rolling complaint from the yard behind the workshop, and then stopped all at once, as if someone had paid them. Nobody pays dogs to be quiet except men who intend to be heard by no one.

I was in the cellar with my sleeves rolled to the elbow and ink to the second knuckle, feeding the small press while Fenwick worked the large one, and Tobias slept above on a pallet of compositors' felt, which is, I am told, the most comfortable bedding in the world if you are seven years old and have spent a season sleeping on floors. Fenwick's apprentice Joss pulled the two-hundredth sheet and held it up to the lamp, and I read my own handwriting rendered in metal—nine names, one company, one night at Vasser's Ford, and beneath it the signatures of officers who had signed a report swearing none of those men had ever existed.

"There," Fenwick said quietly. "That's a true thing now. It can't be untrue again."

I have thought about that sentence many times since. It is the closest I have ever come to theology.

The dogs went silent. Fenwick's head came up like an old soldier's, which he was not, having been a forger instead—a profession with better hours and worse company.

"Mirabel," he said. "How many lamps did we light coming in?"

"One."

"Then why," he said, with the terrible calm of a man reading his own death notice in fine type, "can I see torchlight through the floorboards?"

* * *

Captain Elias Rook, being a man who survived on charm and would die of it eventually, had spent the evening doing arithmetic he could not show anyone.

Colonel Vane had summoned him at four o'clock, fed him brandy, and told him with a smile that the workshop's lease was being terminated early due to "structural concerns regarding lamp oil"—a sentence so transparently murderous that Rook had actually laughed, hoping the colonel was joking, and seen in the colonel's eyes that he was not. The order was simple: keep away from the hospital tonight, take no notes, remember nothing. It was delivered the way favors are delivered, which is how threats travel among gentlemen.

Rook walked the long way back past the hospital, past the workshop, past the mill stream where the ice was coming loose in plates. He thought about the ward. He thought about Private Hollis, who had shown him how to fold paper boats with one hand because the other was gone, and about the way nurses' shoes sounded on stone floors at three in the morning—softly, always softly, as though grief could be woken. He thought about a scrap of ledger paper he had found behind a supply cabinet three days ago, written in a hand too disciplined for a woman who was supposed to be nobody, listing nine names in a column, and about the fact that he had put it in his breast pocket instead of the colonel's tray.

He was carrying contraband against his own heart. That does things to a man.

At half past ten he stood in the lane behind the tannery and watched six men move toward the workshop yard without lanterns, which meant they were not thieves, and with cans, which meant they were worse. And Rook discovered the exact size of his soul, which turned out to be neither heroic nor obedient but some awkward middle measure, like a coat cut for someone else.

He could not warn the workshop openly—that was treason, and Rook, whatever else, intended to survive this war with his neck. But there were bells in this town. There was the fire watch. There was the ancient municipal ordinance, older than the war, that every licensed premises must sound its alarm bell upon sighting open flame after curfew, on penalty of fine.

There was no flame yet. Rook drew his pistol and fired once into the air over the tannery yard, then shouted, at the top of a voice trained to carry across parade grounds:

"FIRE! FIRE IN THE TANNERY LANE! BELLS!"

There was no fire. Not yet. There would be. The bell of Saint Aldhelm's began, and then the chapel bell, and then every dog in town remembered it had opinions. Six men with oil cans paused in a dark yard, and one of them said something Rook did not catch, and the whole machinery of a small town at night—watchmen, neighbors, the incurably curious—began to grind toward the workshop district, forcing the men to either be seen or hurry.

Rook walked away in the opposite direction, trembling, telling himself he had merely discharged his weapon in error, and knowing perfectly well that errors do not choose their targets so precisely.

He had bought them minutes. He did not know if minutes were enough. He touched the folded paper in his pocket once, like a talisman, like an accusation, and kept walking.

* * *

"Out," Fenwick said. "Joss, take the boy through the coal hatch. Mirabel—the sheets. Take the sheets."

"The press—" I began.

"The press weighs nine hundred pounds and I have carried it up those stairs twice in my life and both times I swore." He was already pulling the large press apart with his bare hands, throwing levers, jamming the screw. "Go."

I want it recorded that I argued. I want it recorded that I said something about all of us going together, and that Fenwick looked at me the way a master looks at an apprentice who has confused enthusiasm with skill.

"Miss Dunmore," he said. "I spent eleven years forging other men's names. Every one of those documents helped somebody lie. This—" he put his palm flat on the stack of broadsides, still faintly warm, smelling of oil and truth— "this is the first honest work of my life, and it is finished, and a finished thing only needs keeping. Keeping is your trade. Mine is presses. Go now, and when they ask who ran this place, describe a fat old man with bad knees, and make him frightening."

Joss went up for Tobias and came down with the boy half-asleep, wrapped in felt, clutching—I noticed even then—one of the toy soldiers Fenwick had used to coax his memory out of him, a little lead sergeant whose paint was worn away at the shoulders. We went through the coal hatch into the mill race's outflow, which was exactly as pleasant as it sounds, and I heard, above us, the door of the yard give way, and voices, and then Fenwick's voice, loud and theatrical and magnificent:

"Gentlemen! Gentlemen, this is a house of God's own trade, and I'll thank you to wipe your boots!"

And then the first smell of lamp oil, sweet and wrong, threading down through the floorboards into the dark water.

* * *

Colonel Augustus Vane arrived at the edge of the square at midnight because he was a man who inspected his own work, and because somewhere under the medals there remained a clerk's instinct that numbers must be watched to the last digit.

What he found was disorder. Bells. Watchmen with buckets. A crowd gathering the way crowds do, drawn by fire the way moths are drawn by everything. His men were supposed to have been finished by now, gone, anonymous, the workshop an accident already cooling into its official story. Instead the bells had roused half the garrison town, and Captain Rook was standing at the corner of the chandlery looking appropriately alarmed, and Vane thought, with the cold clarity that had won him every promotion since Vasser's Ford: *too many eyes.*

A fire before an audience is a spectacle. A fire before an audience *and then a search for bodies* is an inquiry. He had planned for ash and silence, and instead he had theater.

"Rook," he said.

"Sir." A crisp salute. Too crisp. Vane noted it the way he noted all discrepancies, in a ledger inside himself that never closed.

"You fired your weapon in the tannery lane this evening."

"I heard movement, sir. Thought it was looters ahead of the fire. It seems I was mistaken." Rook's face did what it always did, which was anything it wanted. "Fortunate, really. The bells brought the watch quickly. If the fire spreads to the chandlery, half the street goes with it."

Vane looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere across the square, flames took the workshop roof, and the crowd made its low sound, the sound people make when beauty and horror arrive wearing each other's clothes. Vane felt nothing whatsoever, which he had always considered a professional advantage.

"Where is the nurse?" he asked.

"I couldn't say, sir."

"Couldn't." Vane turned back to the fire. "You understand, Captain, that in a week's time this will be an accidental blaze, mourned by the town, investigated by no one, and that the woman who worked next door will be a footnote, if she is mentioned at all. You understand that the difference between a hero and a traitor in this army is generally a matter of paperwork." He smiled at the fire, at his fire. "I have done the paperwork."

"Yes, sir," said Rook. And then, because he was Rook, and because even doomed men deserve one honest sentence: "So has she."

Vane chose not to hear it. That choice would cost him everything, but he did not know it yet; no one ever knows the moment their ledger tips. He watched his problem burn and gave the order to withdraw before the crowd could memorize faces, and if he lingered one extra second at the corner—looking not at the fire but at the dark mouth of the mill race, where the water ran fast and cold out of sight—well. Even clerks have instincts.

* * *

We came up out of the culvert behind the mill, soaked to the ribs, Tobias shivering in my arms with the toy soldier still locked in his fist, and from the rise above the tannery we could see the workshop burning.

I made myself look. It seemed owed—to Fenwick, to the presses, to every sheet of paper that had ever passed through that building carrying someone's lie or someone's truth. The roof fell inward with a sound like the end of a sentence, and sparks went up in a column, and the crowd below gasped, and Joss began quietly to cry, and Tobias asked me, in the terrible reasonable voice of children, whether Mr. Fenwick had gotten out.

"No," I said. Because I had decided, somewhere in the black water, that I was finished lying to this child. "He stayed with his machines."

Tobias considered this. Then he held up the little lead sergeant toward the fire, an offering, a salute, whatever a seven-year-old's heart knows to do that the rest of us have forgotten.

I had the bundles. Three oilcloth packages of printed broadsides, two hundred sheets each, tied with the same string that had once bound surgical dressings. They were heavy. Everything true is. Behind us the mill stream ran silver in the firelight, running toward the river, running toward the capital, running everywhere at once the way water and news do.

"Can you walk?" I asked Tobias.

"I'm not a baby," he said, with his chin up, and I nearly laughed, kneeling there in the mud with the world burning behind me, because of course he wasn't. He was the son of a struck name. He was the reason for all of it. He was, though I did not yet know it, Ivo.

Somewhere behind us, on the road we would have taken, a horse moved in the dark—and stopped, and waited, as if someone out there had been told to watch the mill road and was now hearing bells and doubt arguing inside his own chest. I pulled the boy close and turned the other way, toward the water.

"Then walk fast," I said, "and don't look back at the fire."

He looked back at the fire. Children always do. I hope to God they always do—that they look, and remember, and write it down.

We went along the mill race in the smoke and the sparks, three bundles of the true count against my chest, a dead man's son against my side, and behind us the workshop burned bright enough to read by—which, I thought, was the only eulogy Aldous Fenwick would have wanted, and the last light by which anyone would ever check my figures.

They would check them. Sooner or later, in every kitchen and barracks in the country, they would check them.

The paper was already moving.
