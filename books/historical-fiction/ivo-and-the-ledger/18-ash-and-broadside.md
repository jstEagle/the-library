## 18. Ash and Broadside

There is a particular sound a printing press makes when it is finishing its work—a kind of satisfied sigh, iron settling into iron—and I will hear that sound in my ears for the rest of my life, because it was the last thing Aldous Fenwick ever said to me.

Not said with words. Said with the press. He put his hand flat on the frame as the last broadsheet came through, and the machine groaned and settled, and he looked up at me with ink on his knuckles and soot already beginning to fall from the rafters like the first snow of a very bad winter, and he said—wordlessly, absolutely clearly—*Go.*

I did not go. Not immediately. I am not made for going when I am told, which is the entire reason any of this happened.

"Come with us," I said. "Fenwick. There's time."

He was already turning back to the press, laying the fresh sheets out on the stone with the tenderness of a man tucking in children. "There's time," he agreed, pleasantly, as if we were discussing the weather rather than our deaths. "Time for you. A man my age runs slower than paper burns, Mirabel. And someone has to stay with the machines. They're frightened of fire, you know. Terrible thing, watching your life's work go up and being unable to look away." He smiled at me over his shoulder. "Besides, if they find ash and no body, they'll look for a body. Give them one they can stop looking for."

"That's obscene logic."

"I was a forger for eleven years. All my logic is obscene. It merely *looks* respectable." He took my wrist—not roughly, never roughly—and pressed something into my hand: the copper plate, small and warm, of the ninth name. Tobias's father. "Carry this. Paper can be burned. Copper has to be melted by someone who means it."

Then he turned back to his presses and I understood, finally, that I had been dismissed not by a man sacrificing himself but by a craftsman completing an order. The order was mine. The ledger, printed true. He had taken the commission at last, and he meant to see it delivered.

I went. God forgive me, I went.

* * *

The yard behind the workshop had gone strange and bright, torchlight sliding across wet cobbles, and the first window blew outward in a bloom of orange as I came through the door. Livia was waiting where I'd told her—by the water butt, Tobias bundled inside her cloak so that only two eyes showed, enormous in her pale face. She had been at the mill when I found her, three days ago, and she had come down the dark road to us without hesitation once she understood what her son had carried in his fist all those miles. A name on a scrap of paper. Her husband's name. Struck from the rolls by men who thought paper was obedient.

"The stream," I said. My voice came out wrong, flattened. "The mill stream runs behind the timber yards. Vane's men are watching the road and the bridge, but nobody watches water at night."

"Nobody but us," Livia said quietly. "I grew up on this river. I know where it's shallow enough to carry a child and deep enough to hide a woman." She looked past me at the workshop, where the flames were now finding their voice, roaring low and hungry through the ground floor. "The printer—"

"Staying."

She closed her eyes for one breath. Then she opened them and became, before my sight, a different creature—not the hunted woman from the mill with flour in her hair, but someone older and harder, someone who had been making these calculations since the day her husband's company was invented and destroyed. "Then we don't waste what he bought us," she said. "Take the bundle on your left shoulder. Tobias walks between us. If I say duck, you put him under the water and you go under with him. Understood?"

"Understood," I said, and marveled, even then, even in the firelight, at how quickly terror can be refashioned into competence when there is no other material left to work with.

The bundles were heavy—fourteen reams of printed truth, still smelling of Fenwick's ink, wrapped in oilcloth against exactly this night. Nine names. One massacre. The signatures of four officers who had signed the false reports, and beneath them all, set in type half an inch high, the sentence Fenwick had insisted upon composing himself, because a forger knows better than anyone that a document lives or dies by its closing line:

*Here is the count. Count it.*

We went down behind the cooperage, into the black throat between the buildings, and behind us the light grew at our backs like a pursuing sun.

* * *

I have crossed that stream a hundred times in my life. I have never crossed it like that.

The water came to my waist and held the cold of the whole winter in it. Tobias did not cry—that was the terrible part, the part I keep returning to. A boy of seven, wading through night water while his whole known world burned behind him, and he made no sound at all. He simply kept his chin up and his eyes on Livia's cloak ahead of him, moving the way soldiers move, I realized—the way men move who have learned that noise is death. His father's training, passed down in blood instead of letters.

At the second bend Livia said "duck" and we all went under, the three of us, crouched to our necks beneath the overhang of a timber wharf while a lantern swept the bridge above. I held Tobias's head above the surface with one hand and pressed the broadside bundle against my chest with the other, and in the darkness I could feel his heart going like a bird's, fast and small and stubborn.

"They're arguing," Livia whispered, when the lantern had passed. "Listen. They're supposed to be watching for runaways and they're watching the fire instead. Even Vane's men are men tonight."

Even Vane's men are men. I have wondered since how many of them knew what they were burning. How many suspected. Whether any of them, standing in that yard with their torches, felt the specific shame of a man destroying arithmetic. It does not excuse them. Nothing excuses them. But Fenwick taught me late in our acquaintance that forgery is a trade of details, and cruelty is too, and somewhere in that yard was surely some corporal who would read the broadside when it reached him, and read his own guilt set in other people's type, and understand at last what he had illuminated.

We came out of the stream at the mill race, soaked and shaking, and stood a moment in the reeds to look back.

The workshop was gone past saving. Fire had found the paper stores and the varnish and the lamp oil they'd planted—it had everything it needed and it sang with having it. From the ridge above the town you could see the glow reflected in the clouds, and I knew that by morning every garrison within forty miles would have a version of the story, and every version would be wrong, and every wrong version would be official.

Tobias stood between us, dripping, and watched the fire, and then he did the thing that broke me—not crying, not trembling, but raising one small hand, solemnly, the way you wave goodbye to a coach carrying someone you love to a place you cannot follow.

"He liked the presses," he said, to nobody, to the fire. "He let me work the lever."

"Yes," I said. My voice held. I do not know how; some debts are paid in voice. "He did."

* * *

We walked all night. That is the plain fact of it and I could dress it up, but the truth is three hours of numb legs and one hour of terror at a crossroads and the rest just walking, walking, the bundles growing heavier as exhaustion made everything heavier. We went north along the mill stream toward the ruined chapel where Rook had promised—promised, God help us, a captain's promise—to leave a horse if he could, and no word of himself otherwise.

The horse was there. Tied to the lych-gate with a soldier's neatness, and beside it, weighted under a stone, a folded note. I expected orders, or warnings, or a confession. It was none of those. It was a single line in a careful hand:

*The count stands at nine. I checked it myself against the muster rolls. Forgive me the rest.—R.*

Livia read it over my shoulder and asked, "Is he friend or enemy?"

And I found I honestly could not answer, and said so. "He's a man," I said finally, "who checked the count. In this war that may have to be enough."

* * *

Dawn caught us in the beech wood above the river villages, and we stopped because we had to—because Tobias was asleep on his feet, because Livia's hands were blue, because even grief must yield to physics. I built no fire. We sat against the trunk of an old beech with the bundles between us like a fourth traveler, and as the light came up grey and thin through the branches, Livia unwrapped the topmost sheet and we read it together for the first time in full daylight.

It looked so ordinary. That was the shock of it. Set beside the enormity of what it contained, the broadside was a humble object—foolscap, two columns, the nine names ranked plainly, the massacre described in Fenwick's dry compositor's prose with none of my fury left in it (he had edited me; I had never noticed until that moment, and I loved him for it). At the bottom, the closing line. *Here is the count. Count it.*

"It doesn't look like anything," Livia said. Her fingers trembled on the paper. "All of it. Everything. And it looks like a handbill for a lost dog."

"That's the whole art of it," I said. "Fenwick told me once: the most dangerous documents in the world are the ones that look like they belong in the world. A proclamation gets torn down. This—" I touched the edge of the sheet. "This gets tucked in a pocket. Passed hand to hand. Read aloud in taverns by men pretending to read something else. Officers can burn a proclamation. They cannot burn every pocket in the capital."

"And us? Can they burn us?"

"Yes," I said, because she deserved the truth and had paid for it dearly. "They can. But it won't matter the way they think. That's the joke of print, Livia. You can kill the author and the printer and the smuggler, but the paper keeps talking after everyone who could vouch for it is dead. It becomes evidence without a witness. Vane spent his whole career making men disappear. Now he has to make paper disappear, and paper is patient, and paper multiplies."

Tobias stirred against my side and woke, and looked at the sheet in his mother's hands, and pointed one small finger at the fifth name in the column.

"That's Papa," he said. Simply. As a child points at a name on a shop sign.

"That's Papa," Livia said, and gathered him in, and over the crown of his head her eyes met mine, and whatever was in them I could not hold and did not try.

* * *

We buried nothing and burned nothing and kept moving, because that is what survivors do, though I have since decided that "survivor" is a word invented by people who want the living to sound luckier than they feel. By mid-morning we reached the house of a widow Bracken had trusted—a midwife named Corrag with sharp eyes and a stove that had cooked many things off the books. She took one look at the three of us, dripping, ash-smudged, hauling oilcloth bundles like the world's most dangerous laundry women, and stood aside from her door without a question asked.

Later, when Tobias slept in a real bed for the first time in a week, Corrag poured something hot into three cups and said, "Half the town saw the fire. The soldiers are saying lamp oil, careless storage, tragic. The rest are saying the colonel's men were seen in the yard before it started. Both stories are walking around together buying drinks." She sipped. "Which do you want to be true?"

"Neither," I said. "I want the third story. The one on the paper."

Corrag looked at the bundles stacked by her door and then at me for a long moment. "How many have you got?"

"Fourteen bundles of fifty."

Her eyebrows rose, and then—this is the part I want to set down accurately—she began, very quietly, to laugh. Not mockery. Something closer to relief, the laugh of a woman who has been carrying fear alone in a small house for years and has just learned the fear has an opposite. "Fourteen bundles," she said. "Child, I can put one of those in the hands of every laundress who works inside the garrison walls by Sunday. And laundresses go everywhere. We are the most invisible army in this war." She held out her hand. "Give me two bundles. Keep the rest moving. Where next?"

That was the question, wasn't it. North to the capital was the obvious road—Bracken had contacts among the discharged men, and discharged men are the one population no officer counts and every officer should fear. But there were other roads. South, where the mills were restless. West, where the chaplains had grown honest out of sheer boredom with lying. The broadsides needed hands and roads and weeks, and we had perhaps days before Vane understood that the fire had produced not silence but an infestation.

"Where next," I repeated, and looked at Livia, and Livia looked at the sleeping boy in the next room, and neither of us said what both of us were thinking: that we had already lost the only home either of us had, and the printer who had given it warmth, and that everything we carried now—all of it, the papers, the plate, the child, the count—had to arrive somewhere, had to take root somewhere, or Fenwick had stayed at his presses for nothing.

Outside, far to the south, the sky over the garrison town had cleared, and the smoke from the workshop had thinned to a smudge on the horizon like a thumbprint on a clean page.

Corrag was still waiting for her answer. I gave her the only one there was.

"Everywhere," I said. "Next, we send it everywhere."
