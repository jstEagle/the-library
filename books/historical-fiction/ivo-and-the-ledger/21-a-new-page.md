## 21. A New Page

The river at Wethering Slough—for that is the village's name, though no one in it can explain the *slough* and no one has ever seen a slough there—runs green in April and smells of mud and thawed winter. It is not a beautiful river. It is a working one, turning the mill wheel two fields downstream and carrying away everything the village would rather not look at, which is, I have come to believe, the primary function of most rivers and several governments.

We came to Wethering Slough in the second week of spring, eleven months after the workshop burned, and I will confess that my first act upon arrival was to check the roads behind us. My second was to count our money. The third was to notice that Tobias had already found the water and was negotiating with two local boys over ownership of a stick that could not possibly have interested anyone. By the third day he had a fishing line of dubious legality and three friends; by the end of the month, Livia said, he had stopped waking in the night.

I did not stop waking in the night. But I have made peace with being a woman who sleeps lightly. Some of us are built as sentries.

* * *

The school began, as most ruinous enterprises do, with an accident of kindness.

The miller's youngest, a girl named Prue with a lazy eye and a mind like a steel trap someone had carelessly left baited, attached herself to Livia in the market and refused to be detached until Livia had taught her her letters on the back of a flour sack. Prue then taught her brothers. Then the cooper's children appeared at Livia's door, then the ferryman's, and within a fortnight there were nine children sitting on our floor every morning, and the village faced the unprecedented problem of a literate population.

"They'll want a schoolhouse next," the ferryman said to me darkly, as though literacy were a flood.

"They'll want a schoolmistress," I corrected him. "The schoolhouse they could build themselves."

I regretted it before the words had finished leaving me, because I saw the exact moment the village decided I had volunteered. There is a lesson in there about the dangers of precision in speech, but I have never learned it.

So: the disused wool store by the lane became a school, through the combined carpentry of four fathers and the ferocious organizational will of Livia, who has discovered in rural motherhood the same executive talents that once kept her hidden for a year from Colonel Vane's entire apparatus. She organized the benches. She organized the stove. She organized me, which is no small feat, and she did it while pretending it was all for Tobias's benefit, which we both knew was fiction, and which we permitted each other, because some fictions are load-bearing.

I teach them letters. I teach them numbers. And when the inspector from the county association came—sent, I suspect, because a woman with no references teaching children within twenty miles of a garrison town raised exactly the kind of quiet question I had spent a year earning the right to dread—I showed him my curriculum and my ledgers of tuition and my hands, which are a nurse's hands and a printer's assistant's hands and could belong to nobody dangerous, or so I hoped.

He stayed for tea. He left satisfied. I washed his cup three times.

* * *

Here is what I did not tell him, because it was none of his business and because I am still, at bottom, a keeper of secrets:

On the first of May, I opened a new ledger.

It is a proper one this time—not a sewing basket, not stolen hours, not entries made in the dark with my heart hammering like a press. Fenwick's last gift to me, smuggled out with the broadsides in that final terrible week, was a bound book of good rag paper, blank, with his initials tooled into the cover. I had thought, in the months after, that I might never write in it. There are objects that carry too much of their maker. But paper is patient, and so eventually was I.

The first page took me an hour.

Not because the names were hard to come by. Because my hand kept wanting to do the old thing—the old careful, guilty, clandestine thing—writing small, writing slanted, compressing each name as if to hide it even from the page it sat on. Nine hundred days of recording the dead teaches you a hand you cannot simply put down. I wrote *Prudence Miller* in letters the size of pins and then looked at them with something close to anger.

She is seven years old. She is alive. She deserves letters she can read across a room.

I ruled fresh lines and began again, large and upright, the way Fenwick set type: confident, legible, meant to be seen. *Prudence Miller.* Below her, *Jonas Cooper. Elsie Ferryman. Tobias Dunmore* —the surname is ours now, mine and Livia's both by arrangement of silence; the law asks fewer questions about a widow's ward than about a boy with no papers, and I have had enough of papers to last me. His true name lives where it should live, written once in Livia's hand inside his own copybook, and when he is ready to carry it he will.

That is the whole difference between this ledger and the other one. The dead needed only to be recorded; the living need to be *recorded well*. They need room on the page. They need margins wide enough for corrections, for growth, for the whole untidy continuation of a life. I leave generous margins now. It costs more paper. Paper is cheap.

By midsummer the ledger held forty-one names. Every child in the school, every teacher—which is to say Livia, me, and a rotating honor guard of older girls who supervise the infants—and, in a section I did not plan but could not refuse, the adults. The ferryman came to me in June, hat in his fists, and asked in a roundabout way whether the school might take a grown man who had never learned his figures and who was, he admitted, "being robbed blind at cards every Saturday." I said Saturday evenings were available. Within a month there were six men learning arithmetic under the pretense of improving themselves, and one of them, the smith, quietly asked me whether I was the one from the broadsides.

"The broadsides covered a great many things," I said.

"The counting," he said. "The woman who kept the count."

I looked at him for a long moment. The forge hammered somewhere behind us; the river went on being a river.

"Yes," I said. "That was me."

He nodded slowly, the way men nod when they have decided something and wish to appear to have decided it long ago. "My brother was in the Fourth," he said. "Killed at Kessel Field. Officially wounded and discharged. My mother wrote to him for two years at an address that had no beds in it." He picked up his slate. "Thank you for the arithmetic," he said, and went inside, and that was the whole of it. That is how it goes now. Not exposure. Not the torches coming up the yard. Just men and women who read a broadside four winters ago—or heard it read aloud, which is how most truth traveled in those days—and file the knowledge away, and one day set it down in front of you like a cup of tea, and ask nothing except that you go on keeping the books.

* * *

I should say something about Vane, since he is owed at least a paragraph, poor devil.

The trial was in the capital, in the autumn after the burning, and we did not attend. We learned what there was to learn from the newspapers, which printed the proceedings with the relish of men who had been forbidden to print anything for years and intended to make up the deficit. Stripped of rank. Struck from the rolls—it pleased me beyond measure, I admit it freely, that the sentence was written in his own grammar: erased, omitted, made into an absence in an official book. Scapegoated, in the end, by the very machinery he had serviced so faithfully. The Clerk was never named. The Clerk, I am reliably informed by Rook—one letter, unsigned, forwarded twice across the country before it found us—is a respected man now with a new patronage and a fine house, and the war is over, and no one wants to open that particular drawer again.

Rook writes once a year. His letters are short and awkward and contain, buried in the middle under reports of weather, the closest a man of his training can come to an apology. Last spring he enclosed a pressed flower from the field at Kessel, which I did not know what to do with, and so Livia put it in the ledger, between the smith's name and the ferryman's, where it has stained the page very faintly violet. Even the hunter wavers. Even the hunter, given years and distance, becomes a man pressing flowers and mailing them to women who owe him nothing. I keep his letters. I have not answered the last two. Perhaps I will. Forgiveness, like literacy, may yet reach the villages.

And Fenwick. Fenwick stays with his presses, in whatever parish the dead are assigned to, and I miss him at strange moments—at the smell of ink, at the sound of any machine working rhythmically, at the exact hour of dusk when the light turns the color of lamplight through cellar doors. Grief, I have found, is not a wound that closes. It is a name in a ledger. You do not stop carrying it. You simply learn to write it in a large enough hand.

* * *

But this chapter is meant to be about healing, and I am wandering, which is the privilege of the healed.

In September, Tobias caught his first trout, entirely by accident and entirely against regulations, and brought it home with the triumphant cruelty of all boys toward fish. We cooked it badly and ate it anyway. Afterward he sat on the step beside me while Livia mended by lamplight, and he was quiet in the particular way he has when he is assembling something, and then he said:

"Missus Harrow's son died in the war."

Missus Harrow keeps the toll house upstream. Her son died two years ago, at a place whose name I will not write here, and the letter she received misspelled his name, which she told me once at market with a steadiness that frightened me more than weeping would have.

"Yes," I said.

"Is he in your book?"

I set down my mending. This is the moment, I thought. This is where the ledger becomes what it becomes, the moment it passes out of your hands and into the next pair of hands, and you discover whether what you built was a weapon or a lamp.

"Not yet," I said. "What was his name?"

"Willem," he said. "Willem Harrow. She says everyone gets it wrong. They wrote *William*."

"Then we shall write it correctly." I fetched the ledger and the good pen, and he watched, deadly serious, supervising every letter, as I ruled a line and wrote, in large upright characters with generous margins, *Willem Harrow — beloved son of the toll house — counted.*

"Counted," he read aloud, testing the word. "That's a funny thing to put."

"It's the most important thing to put," I said. "The whole war was fought by people trying to make other people uncounted. Everything after is just deciding to count."

He considered this with the gravity of seven going on eight, which is to say completely and for eleven seconds, and then asked if he could have the trout's tail bone, because Josie Ferryman didn't believe he'd caught it. He could. He went to bed. I sat on the step a while longer with the ledger open on my knees, watching moths batter the lamp, and felt—for the first time since a rain-soaked woman whispered *forget the face* at a door I still dream about—that the weight in my lap had changed direction. It no longer dragged me backward into the dark. It pulled forward, gently, the way a current pulls a boat it approves of.

Livia blew out her lamp and stood in the doorway behind me. "Come in," she said. "The past can mind itself for one evening."

"The past," I said, closing the book, "has been minding itself beautifully lately. It's the future I'm keeping hours on now."

* * *

Which is, I suppose, the honest ending of my part of this story, except that stories, unlike ledgers, refuse to balance.

Because in October a traveler came through Wethering Slough—a printer's agent, he said, touring the provincial towns, buying stock and stories, a cheerful grey man with ink-stained cuffs. He ate at the inn, he bought paper at the mill, and he asked, in the idle way of a man making conversation rather than history, whether anyone here remembered the affair of the broadsides. The famous ones. The true count. There was talk, he said, of a commemorative edition. The war was over; the century was turning; people wanted to *remember* now, safely, in gilt editions, at a profit.

The village, bless it, knew nothing. The village had never read anything in its life before my school.

But the agent, paying his bill, paused at the door and looked directly at me—the way one professional recognizes another across a room, by the ink ghosts on the hands and the habit of counting exits—and smiled, and said, "They say the woman escaped with a child. They say the child carried the last copy out of the fire himself."

"Do they," I said.

"They say a great many things," he agreed, and touched his hat, and went down toward the river road, walking, I noticed, without once looking back.

That night I took out the ledger and turned past forty-one living names to the back pages, where I had begun—without ever deciding to—a different kind of entry. A record, brief and factual, of everyone who has come asking. In case. Memory repurposed as care, yes. But memory, I have learned, must also keep its sentry posted.

I dipped the pen and wrote the date, and the description, and beneath it, in a large upright hand with generous margins:

*A grey man, ink-stained cuffs, asked after the child. Watched too long. Counted.*

Some pages, it seems, are never entirely new.
