## 1. The Weight of Names

The ink froze in the well by the window, so Rafael kept the bottle under his coat, against his ribs, where his body heat kept it liquid and where it stained him faintly blue at the collarbone like a bruise he had chosen.

It was a small vanity of craft in a place that had no use for craft. The garrison hospital at Vesterhavn had been a wool warehouse before the war found a use for it, and it still smelled of lanolin and tar beneath the sharper smells that came with December — carbolic, gangrene, the sweetish rot of lungs giving out. Three hundred cots stood in rows under a roof of blackened beams, and along the east wall the frost climbed the windows each night in ferns and feathers, beautiful in the way that only useless things could still be beautiful here.

Rafael moved down the rows with the lamp held low, the way one carried a candle into a room where someone slept — not to wake them, but to see them. Most were asleep. Some were past sleep and into something else, a country with no ambassador. A few watched the lamp come and turned their faces toward it the way plants turn, without deciding to.

At the fourth cot from the stove, a boy — no, a man, though the war had mislabeled him somewhere between enlistment and now — lay with his right arm wrapped to the shoulder. His name was Anders Vik. Rafael knew this because Anders Vik had told him on the first night, and then again every night since, as if afraid the name might be taken from him while he slept, the way his boot had been taken, and his coat, and eventually, the surgeons assured him quietly, the arm itself.

"Vik," the boy said now, half-waking. "You've got it? You've got my name written?"

"I have it," Rafael said. "A-anders Vik. With two letters where most men would be satisfied with one."

"Satisfied." The boy smiled with his eyes closed. "My mother spelled it wrong for years. She'd write *Anders* and I'd say, Mother, there are two of them, you're shortchanging me. She said names are like bread, you can't skimp on the middle or the whole thing falls flat."

Rafael wrote it down later, that too — not the name, which was already entered, but the mother's saying, because the ledger was not strictly a ledger. The ledger was a cloth-bound book, sewn rather than glued, its cover made from a piece of grey blanket that had once belonged to a cot in the fever ward, and inside it Rafael recorded what the army's own books declined to hold.

* * *

The official casualty list was a single sheet, posted each Friday outside the commandant's office, and it was a masterpiece of subtraction. It gave numbers. It gave units. It gave, when the mood was generous, initials. *P. Halvorsen, 3rd Rifles, died of wounds.* As if the wound had done the dying and the man had merely been present. As if P. Halvorsen had arrived in the world as an initial, been christened by a clerk with a cold, and departed the same way.

Rafael had begun the ledger in October, on a night that would keep its own chapter later, and by the second week of December it held ninety-one names. Ninety-one men who had passed through these cots and out through whatever door waited for them, and whose full names existed nowhere else — not in the regimental rolls, which listed deserters more carefully than dead men; not in the telegrams home, which went to *next of kin* as a category rather than to mothers who spelled names wrong for years.

Each entry took what the man could carry. That was the rule Rafael had set, though no one had asked for rules. A button off a tunic, described: *brass, regimental crest worn smooth, thread green.* A photograph, if there was one, noted but never taken — *tintype, woman seated, child standing on chair behind her, corner burned.* A name, always, spelled as the man himself spelled it, even when the spelling was wrong, especially when it was wrong, because a man's errors were his own and no clerk's business. And last, a sentence. One sentence in the man's own voice, or as near to it as Rafael could carry out of the ward without dropping anything.

Tonight's work began at the far end, where the worst cases were kept nearest the stove and furthest from hope, an arrangement nobody discussed.

Halvard Ness was dying. Everyone on the row knew it, including Halvard, who had stopped pretending otherwise around the feast of St. Lucia and now spent his hours dictating, in a voice like paper tearing, instructions for a farm he would never see again — which field to fallow, which mare to sell and which never, never to let his brother touch. He was fifty-one, the oldest man in the ward, conscripted late because the war had run through the young the way fire runs through dry grass and had begun, out of hunger, on the green wood.

"Nurse," he said, as Rafael bent to check the dressing. "Nurse Halloran. Come here. Bring your book."

Rafael brought the book. There was no point denying its existence; half the ward knew about it, and the other half suspected, and the officers — the officers did not look closely at nurses, which was the whole architecture of Rafael's survival and everyone else's.

"Read me mine," Halvard said. "I want to hear how I sound."

Rafael opened to the page. *Ness, Halvard. Farm called Solvang, above the lake at Brekke. Wife, Astrid. Two sons living, one daughter dead of scarlet fever, 1887.* And then the sentence: *"Tell Astrid the mare stays. Tell her I said the mare stays and she'll know what I mean."*

Halvard listened with his eyes shut, and when it was done he was quiet a long moment, and Rafael thought he had slept. Then: "She'll know," he said. "Good. That's the important part accounted for." He turned his face to the wall. "The rest is just farming."

Rafael sat with him until the tearing-voice slowed, and then rose, and moved on, because the ward did not permit grief to sit down anywhere; it had to be carried, walking, like everything else.

* * *

Near midnight the snow came back. It came the way it had come all week, without wind, straight down, in flakes so large they seemed to have intentions, and through the high windows the lamplight caught them so that the whole dark outside appeared to be slowly filling with feathers. Rafael paused at the end of the row and watched, hands around the cooling lamp, and allowed — as one allows oneself one small dishonesty per day — to imagine that the world beyond the glass was gentle.

"You'll ruin your eyes."

Sister Margit, the head nurse, materialized the way she always did, soundless despite her size, a woman built like a dock crane and twice as reliable. She looked at the ledger's outline under Rafael's apron — everyone saw the outline; nobody named it — and then at the window, and then at Rafael with an expression that managed to be simultaneously disapproval and blessing.

"The colonel's aide came by at nine," she said, lower. "Asking after the ward count. Asking whether the nursing staff kept *independent records.*"

"And what did you tell him?"

"That our records were the army's records, kept faithfully, and that nurses can barely spell." Margit's mouth did something at the corner that was not quite a smile. "He believed me. Men always believe a woman is stupid if she offers it to them first."

"He wants the list Friday."

"He wants everything Friday. They want the town Friday, if it comes to that." She said it lightly, but the lightness had edges. For three days the requisition carts had been coming through the gate — flour, coal, harness, and yesterday, absurdly, the church bells, pried from the tower and hauled off on a sledge while the priest stood in the snow with his hat in his hands. Paper next, people said. All the paper in the district, inventoried and seized. What an army feared, apparently, was not bullets but writing.

"Then we'd best be quick," Rafael said.

Margit held the look a moment longer. Whatever she thought about the ledger — and Rafael had long ago concluded that she thought everything about it — she had decided, sometime in November, that her thoughts would remain her own property. "Cot sixty-one has a fever spike," she said instead. "And the new intake from the mill road is lying in the corridor because we've no beds, and one of them keeps asking for his brother. Go and tell him something true, Nurse. It's the only medicine left in stock."

* * *

The corridor smelled of wet wool and iodine. Six men lay on stretchers along the wall, their boots lined up beside them like sentries relieved of duty, and Rafael knelt first at the one who was asking.

His name — given freely, eagerly, the way men gave their names here, as though a name were a rope thrown across water — was Tomas Brandt, and he was nineteen, and he had a shell fragment sitting somewhere near his spine that the surgeon had looked at and looked away from. His brother Eirik had gone down in the same action, up on the ridge, and no one could tell Tomas whether Eirik was in another hospital, or a prison camp, or a grave with a number on it, or a grave without.

"He's at the depot," Rafael said. "They're holding the walking wounded at the depot for exchange." This was true. It was also, in Eirik's case, probably irrelevant, and Rafael hated the arithmetic of sentences like that — how much truth a sentence could hold before it broke.

"Will you find out?" Tomas said. "When you can walk again, will you go and look? He's got a scar through his eyebrow, from when we were boys, off a skate. He'd deny it was me that did it. He tells it different every time."

"I'll remember the scar," Rafael said. "And the story."

And then, because the ledger was within reach of conscience if not of hand, Rafael asked the question that had become ritual: whether there was anything — a token, a word, a thing that could be carried.

Tomas thought. His hands moved over the blanket, patting pockets that weren't there. "My button," he said finally. "The top one. Mother sewed them on with red thread, all the rest are regulation thread, but the top one's red, she ran out halfway, or she wanted—" He stopped. His throat worked. "If I don't— if they take my kit, the button goes in the kit, and the kit goes in a crate, and the crate goes—"

"I know where the crates go," Rafael said gently.

So the button came off — Rafael cut the red thread with the small scissors kept for dressings, and the small ceremony of it made Tomas laugh, a cracked, surprised sound, as though laughter had been waiting all day for a lawful excuse — and the button went into the ledger's pocket at the back, a flap of oilcloth sewn in for exactly this, and the entry was made by lamplight while Tomas watched, lips moving, checking the spelling of his own name like a schoolboy.

*Tomas Brandt. Brother Eirik, missing at the ridge, scar through left eyebrow, blames his brother for it, tells it differently every time. Button, top, red thread — his mother's doing.*

"There," Rafael said. "Now you exist twice. Once here, and once wherever they keep the originals."

Tomas looked at the ceiling. "Which one's the copy?"

* * *

Rafael finished the round at three in the morning, by which time the ink had frozen twice more and been thawed twice more against Rafael's body, so that the entries of the night were written in a hand that grew shakier as the pages turned, and Rafael resolved — as every night — to copy them fair in the morning, knowing — as every night — that the morning would bring forty dressings and no ink.

Ninety-two names now. Tomas Brandt had made ninety-two.

Rafael stood at the east window with the ledger open on the sill, reading backward through the month, and the frost feathers on the glass made halos of the lamplight so that each page seemed lit from beneath. Ninety-two men. Somewhere in the army's arithmetic they were a fraction of a percentage, a rounding error, a column closed and carried forward. Here they were a mare that must not be sold, a button sewn with red thread, a story told differently every time, a mother who skimped on the middle of a name and flattened the bread.

The book weighed almost nothing. Rafael had weighed it once on the dispensary scales, out of some private joke against the universe: eleven ounces. Eleven ounces against ninety-two lives, and the scale had tipped toward the lives, and Rafael had understood then — standing in the cold dispensary with the little brass weights gleaming — that this was the whole secret of the thing. Memory was not heavy. It was the lightest cargo in the world. It was the only cargo that could be carried through any checkpoint, any confiscation, any century, provided one person agreed to bear it.

Provided, said a colder voice, one person was permitted to.

Because the requisition carts were coming more often now. Because the church bells had gone out on a sledge. Because Sister Margit had used the word *Friday* twice in one night, and because the colonel's aide had asked, with a clerk's terrible innocence, whether the nursing staff kept independent records.

Rafael closed the book. Eleven ounces. Under the apron, against the ribs, next to the ink.

Outside, the snow went on falling with its vast patience, covering the roads, the sledge-tracks, the whole occupied town, erasing everything equally — and Rafael turned back to the ward, where ninety-three cots held ninety-three chances, and began the rounds again from the beginning, because the beginning was the only direction the night allowed.

At the far end, Halvard Ness had begun, very softly, to dictate to the dark: *the mare stays, the mare stays.* Rafael wrote it down a second time, in case the first had not held.
