## 2. Ivo in the Ward

The dying spoke to Ivo more honestly than the living ever had.

They called her by every name but their own — *mother, brother, boy, boy* — and she answered to all of them, because a voice that answered was what they needed, and names, she had learned, were things you could lend out like coats. She had been lending hers for eleven years. Ivor Halloran, orderly, third class, a narrow-shouldered man with a soft voice and no history anyone could check, because the man who might have checked it had drowned in a ferry accident off the southern coast before Ivo ever put on his name along with his trousers.

She moved down the ward with the basin and the lamp, and the snow ticked at the windows like a clerk counting something in.

"Boy." Halvard Ness had surfaced again from wherever the mare lived. His hand came up out of the blanket, pale as something washed ashore. "Boy. Is she tended?"

"She's tended," Ivo said. "Stalled, fed, warm. You told me yourself she's the finest animal in the county."

"I did?" He frowned at the ceiling, working at it. "I did. She is." The hand sank back down. "Then that's all right. That's all right."

It was never all right. It was only ever arranged.

Ivo set the basin on the floor and wrung the cloth, and while her hands worked her eyes went, as they went every hour of every shift, down the ward to the small table under the lamp where Rafael sat with the ledger open and the ink bottle stoppered with a cork bitten soft at one end. Her brother bent over the book the way other men bent over cards or drink — wholly, ruinously, with everything else forgotten around the edges of the thing itself.

She knew what was in it. She had known since the first week, when she'd found the cloth-bound volume hidden inside the false bottom of the biscuit tin and read three pages standing in the cold pantry with her heart going like a fist on a door. *Ness, Halvard, private, 2nd battalion. Not listed in Tuesday's return.* And beneath it, in Rafael's upright hand: *carries a photograph of a mare; speaks of her constantly; afraid.*

Not numbers. Not statistics. Rafael wrote them down the way other people said prayers — name, then the small human weight of them, the button or the photograph or the sentence they'd repeated forty times before the end — and Ivo understood that this was either the bravest thing happening in the garrison town or the most fatal, and that the two categories had lately stopped being distinguishable.

What she had not known, until tonight, until an hour ago, was that her own names were in it.

*O. Vessel,* the page had said. *Orderly, civilian attachment. No next of kin given. Diligent. Never eats in the mess. Watch him.*

She had read it four times before the meaning assembled itself, and then she had stood in the pantry among the frozen apples and laughed once, a short ugly sound she strangled immediately, because there was no one to laugh with and nothing that was funny. Her brother had written her down. Written *him* down. O. Vessel, who did not exist, recorded with the same grave care as Ness and his mare, as though a fiction entered into evidence became a fact, as though the ledger's whole dangerous magic were to make even a lie permanent.

And underneath the laugh there was something else, something that had kept her awake through the dead middle hour of the shift, holding the basin on her knees in the dark of the linen room: that if Rafael died tomorrow — if the fever took him, or worse, if worse was coming for them all the way it came down the telegraph wires these days — then O. Vessel would be all that remained of her. A name in a book. A diligent orderly with no next of kin. The woman she actually was — Ivo, who had skinned eels at seven and argued theology with her mother at twelve and buried her own girlhood in a coffin she had not been permitted to weep over — would go nowhere. Would never have gone anywhere. There would be nothing left in the world to say she had existed, except a page of her brother's handwriting describing a man who had never been born.

Invisible was safe. Invisible was also gone. She had spent eleven years learning the first truth so thoroughly that the second had managed to sneak up on her in middle age, arriving not as revelation but as a slow cold, like water finding the seam in a boot.

* * *

"You're favoring the left side," Rafael said, without looking up. "The basin. You've been carrying it against your hip since midnight. Your hip will thank you to switch."

Ivo came down the ward and set the basin on the table, deliberately, on top of a loose sheet of casualty figures the medical officer wanted copied by morning. "My hip can speak for itself."

"It usually does." Rafael glanced up then, and the lamplight caught the exhaustion there, the fine grain of it, someone running on wick-trimmings. "You should sleep. Your shift ended an hour ago."

"So did yours."

"My shift is different from yours."

"That's true," Ivo said. "Yours ends when they shoot you. Mine ends when they notice me." She hadn't meant to say it. It came out with an edge on it, honed somewhere in the linen room in the dark, and she watched it land, watched her brother go very still the way he did — stillness as armor, stillness as the family trade — and she wished, briefly and uselessly, that words could be unspoken the way letters could be burned.

"Ivo," Rafael said, low. The ward was quiet; the nearest sleeper was six cots away and breathing through cotton wool. "Ivo."

"Don't." She picked up the empty basin. "I didn't come in here to quarrel."

"Why did you come in here?"

To see it again. To stand close enough to the table that the page wasn't a memory but a fact, that she hadn't invented the small horror of seeing herself rendered into someone's careful record. But she said none of that. She said: "Ness wants his boots on. He won't settle without them."

"Ness has no feet to speak of."

"He doesn't know that, does he." She heard her own voice flatten into the orderly's voice, the borrowed instrument, pitched low and slightly bored. "So I'll put his boots where he can see them, and he'll believe his feet are in them, and he'll sleep. That's the work. That's the whole of the work, most nights."

Rafael closed the ledger. It was such a small gesture and such a complete one — the book shut, the hand flat upon it, the whole archive of ninety-three lives pressed under five fingers — that Ivo felt its weight across the ward, felt it like weather.

"If it troubles you," Rafael said quietly, "what I write—"

"It doesn't trouble me."

"—then you should know that I write everyone. Everyone who comes through this door. That is the entire point. No one is omitted. Not the ones the officers don't want, not the ones who—" Rafael stopped. Chose the door carefully, the way Rafael chose all doors now. "Not anyone."

*Everyone,* Ivo thought. *Except the one person in this ward who isn't in it.*

Because that was the arithmetic that had her by the throat, wasn't it? Rafael wrote everyone — and had never once written *Ivo*. Eleven years, and her brother, the great witness, keeper of names, had stood at family gravesides and holiday tables and the mouths of dark pantries saying nothing, writing nothing, keeping her erasure with the same terrible diligence that strangers' wives kept lockets. She understood why. Understanding changed nothing. To write Ivo's name anywhere — in a ledger, a letter, a parish roll — would be to hang a sign on the whole disguise, and the disguise was the wall between Ivo and the tribunal, the prison hulk, the kind of death that began years before the shooting part. Rafael had protected her the only way the century allowed: by agreeing, daily, silently, that she did not exist.

And now the ledger said *O. Vessel, no next of kin*, which was Rafael doing the only thing Rafael could do — recording the shadow while denying the body — and Ivo could not decide whether it was love or cowardice or simply the century itself, pressing down on them equally, insisting that everyone choose between safety and self, between the wall and the name.

She put Ness's boots at the foot of his cot where his failing eyes would find them in the grey half-light. He murmured thanks to somebody named Anders, who was possibly dead and possibly a horse. She pulled the blanket straight.

* * *

Toward four in the morning, the ward did its trick of going silent all at once, the way water goes still before ice, and Ivo stood at the window with the lamp turned low and let herself look at the town. Snow on the barracks roof. Snow on the customs house. Down at the harbor, the masts of the transport ships stood against the darker sky like needles drawn through black cloth, and she counted them out of habit — two, and a third half-rigged — and hated herself for counting, because counting ships meant thinking about berths, and thinking about berths meant admitting that the arithmetic had already started somewhere in the world whether the family consented to it or not.

Behind her, a cot creaked. Young Sørvik, nineteen, both lungs going, the one whose letter home sat unsent in his breast pocket because he couldn't decide how to spell *forgive*. His eyes were open.

"Vessel," he whispered. "Hey. Vessel."

She went to him. "Here."

"They say the ships are for officers first."

"They say a lot of things."

"And after that, ranks." His breath made a thin whistling, a kettle heard through a wall. "Where do orderlies rank, Vessel? Where do I rank?"

Ivo looked down at him — the fever-bright eyes, the letter rising and falling under the nightshirt with each ruined breath — and she felt the question go through her like a splinter finding its depth. Where did she rank? Nowhere. On no list. In no column. The ships would load the living by rank and by paper, and she had spent eleven years making sure she appeared on neither, and now the century was turning over like a page, and she could hear, in the creak of the harbor ice and the chalking of numbers on doors, that the new page intended to demand of everyone the same question: *name, rank, and proof.*

"You rank," she said, and heard her voice crack on it, and covered by turning up his blanket, "you rank exactly where you're lying, and I'll be here."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed softly. "It isn't."

He slept, or subsided. She stood a moment longer, and across the ward the lamp still burned over Rafael's table, and her brother was writing again — of course writing, always writing, pen moving with that patient terrible faith — and Ivo understood with sudden clarity that whatever was coming down the wires toward this town, whatever requisition or confiscation or reckoning the last weeks of 1899 carried in their pockets, it would land on the ledger first. Everything the army wanted would want the book. Everything the book held would hold them all.

And she saw, too, with the same clarity, the thing she would not be able to unknow: that when the day came — and it was coming, she could feel it the way sailors feel weather — she and Rafael would stand somewhere with the book between them, and the question would not be *where to hide it* but *which of us is it worth more than*, and she already knew, the way you know your own hands, that Rafael would answer wrong. Would answer with her. Had probably already answered, in some unwritten page, some decision made at four in the morning over ninety-three names.

She picked up the basin. She switched it to her right hip, for her brother's sake, and walked back down the ward past the boots and the blankets and the breathing, and behind her, faint under the wind, she heard the harbor bell strike the hour, and beneath it — or thought she heard, or dreamed she heard — the sound of hooves and wheels in the snow, many of them, coming into the town before dawn.
