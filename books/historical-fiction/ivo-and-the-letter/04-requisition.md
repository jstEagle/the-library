## 4. Requisition

They came for the town on a Tuesday, in the grey hour when the lamps had burned down to their wicks and the frost had not yet decided whether it would be fog or snow. Ivo heard them before she saw them — the particular sound of many boots moving without hurry, which is the sound of an army that already owns what it is walking toward.

She was at the hospital window with a basin of water going cold in her hands. Below, out on the Kastel road, the column unspooled like something being poured: infantry first, then wagons draped in canvas, then officers on horses too fine for the weather, then more wagons, and last a flat-bed drawn by six mules and carrying what looked, absurdly, like a printing press lashed under tarpaulin.

"Paper," said Rafael beside her, though Ivo had not heard him come up. He said it the way other men said *artillery*.

"What?"

"They brought a printing press." His breath fogged the glass. "You don't bring a press unless you intend to print."

"Print what?"

"Everything." Rafael took the basin from her hands, because that was his habit — taking the heavy thing so she could stand there lighter — and carried it down the ward to the man in the third bed who had asked for water twice in the night and would ask again before noon and would not live to see either.

Ivo stayed at the window. She had learned, in nine years of living as a man, to read a street the way sailors read weather, and what she read now was this: no one was running, therefore no one needed to run, therefore it was already done. The requisition had happened somewhere in a tent weeks ago, signed by men whose boots had never touched this mud, and today the town was merely being informed of its own new ownership.

By eight o'clock the soldiers were chalking numbers on the doors.

She watched a corporal do their own street. He was young, red about the ears, methodical as a schoolmaster. At the widow Brandt's house he drew a square and wrote *14* inside it, and beneath the square a smaller mark Ivo could not make out until he reached their door and made it again: a tally, two strokes and a slash, five souls within. The corporal did not knock. He chalked *15* on their lintel, added the tally — though they were three, not five; he counted the house, perhaps, or counted generously, or simply could not be bothered to ask — and moved on down the row, his coat swinging, his breath trailing him like a scarf.

Five souls. Ivo stood very still and felt the number settle over her like a coat thrown on from behind. She had spent nine years learning to be uncounted, and here was a boy with a stick of chalk counting her in four seconds without ever seeing her at all.

That was the thing about inventories, she would think later. They did not need to know you to own you.

* * *

The stores went first.

At the granary by the mill race, soldiers stacked sacks in pyramids while a lieutenant with a ledger book — a real one, leather-bound, brass-clasped, the kind that closed with a click — walked the rows calling figures to a clerk. *Wheat, 340 sacks. Rye, 112. Oats, spoiled portion, deduct.* The clerk wrote it all down in a hand as neat as Rafael's, and Ivo, hauling a crate of dressings past on a handcart, thought: so that is what an honest ledger looks like when it serves a lie. Numbers do not care whose side they are on.

They took the salt. They took the lamp oil and the tallow and, with a thoroughness that made the chandler laugh until an officer looked at him, the candles. They took the doctor's second cart and the farrier's tools and the entire contents of the stationer's shop on Kirkegata, every ream and quire and roll of it, carried out into the slush by men who handled paper the way sappers handle powder.

Ivo stopped in front of the stationer's with her handcart blocking the lane and watched. A sergeant sat on an upturned crate amid the shop's emptied shelves, sorting. Letters, he made one pile. Ledgers and account books, another. Printed matter — newspapers, pamphlets, almanacs — a third. Loose paper of any kind, a fourth. Each pile went into a different kind of crate, and each crate got stenciled, and Ivo read the stencils upside down: *RECORDS — HOLDING. RECORDS — HOLDING. RECORDS — BURN.*

"Burn," she said aloud, before she meant to.

The sergeant glanced up. He had a face like a folded map, all creases and no expression, and he looked at her — at him, the orderly in the hospital's grey jacket — with the flat professional gaze of a man cataloguing rather than seeing. "Requisition order," he said. "All written material in the district. Public and private." He held up a sheet of his own, printed on the army's press already, apparently, somewhere south of them. "You can read it yourself if you've got a mind to. It's the last private paper in this town that hasn't been boxed."

Someone behind her laughed. Ivo did not. She was thinking of the drawer in their kitchen where their mother kept the letters from their father, dead eleven years, tied in ribbon gone the color of weak tea. Private. Every word of it, soon, in a crate with a stencil on it, weighed and assessed by a sergeant with folded-map eyes who would decide — *burn* or *hold* — with the tip of one finger.

"Something wrong with your cart?" the sergeant asked, because she had been standing too long.

"No, sir," said Ivo, in the low flat voice she kept for men like this, and hauled the dressings on toward the hospital, and did not look back at the crates.

But she counted, the way she counted everything now. Four piles. Four kinds of paper. And somewhere in the fourth pile, if the order reached as far as it claimed, would go cloth-bound books kept under floorboards by nurses who wrote down names that officers omitted.

* * *

The proclamation was nailed to the chapel door by noon and read aloud by the priest, who read it in the voice of a man reciting his own diagnosis:

*By authority of the military governor, the town of Vardal and its district are hereby placed under requisition for the duration of operations. All stores, stock, vehicles, livestock, premises, and goods are subject to assessment and seizure as required. All written records, public and private, are to be surrendered for inspection within seventy-two hours. Households will receive ration cards per the numbers marked upon their doors. Movement beyond the town boundary is prohibited except by written pass.*

Seventy-two hours. Ivo stood in the crowd with her cap pulled down and her shoulders rounded into the shape she wore in public — narrower than they were, apologetic — and did the arithmetic she could not stop doing. Seventy-two hours until every letter, every diary, every christening record and debt note and love token in Vardal passed through soldiers' hands. Seventy-two hours until some clerk leafed through the parish rolls and found the entry she knew was there, the one their mother had paid a fat sum to have recorded carelessly, *Ivar* Halloran, born — and the date smudged, and the godfather's name misspelled, small corruptions bought with their mother's savings against exactly this kind of inspection. Carelessly enough? Seventy-two hours to find out.

And underneath that arithmetic, colder, the other one. Because the proclamation did not only take paper. It numbered doors. It issued cards per soul. Which meant that sooner or later — and she had known this day would come, had felt it coming for years like weather in her knees — someone official would set Ivar Halloran's card beside Ivar Halloran's person and ask questions in a tone that expected answers.

The world was shrinking. She could feel it contract around her like ice forming on a pond, outward in from the edges, and everything that could not fit inside the shrinking circle would be pushed up and broken. Stores. Candles. Letters. Names.

Her own name above all.

* * *

They inventoried people that afternoon.

It was done gently, at first, in the way that made it worse. Soldiers went door to door with the tallies off the lintels and a printed form, and householders stood in their own kitchens while strangers counted spoons and beds and bodies. The widow Brandt wept quietly and was noted. The baker argued about his second oven and was noted louder. At the houses where only women and children remained, the soldiers were almost courteous, and Ivo understood that courtesy precisely: it was the manner of men handling goods already sold.

At the hospital they came at four o'clock, three of them plus the clerk with the brass-clasped book, and Dr. Møller met them in the corridor with his hands clasped behind his back like a man presenting a museum wing.

"Ambulatory wounded, forty-one," the clerk read from Møller's list. "Non-ambulatory, sixty-six. Staff?"

"Four nurses, two orderlies, myself."

"Names."

Møller gave them. Ivo stood at the ward's far end folding bandages that were already folded, and when the clerk's pen scratched *Halloran, I., orderly,* she felt the scratch travel up her spine like a splinter working loose. There it was. In a book. In a soldier's book, this time, with a brass clasp and the weight of an army behind it. *Halloran, I.* One initial between her and the parish rolls, between her and the crates stenciled *BURN*, between her and whatever came after inspection.

The clerk looked up from his page, and for one heartbeat his eyes crossed the ward and touched her — just the ordinary glance of a man matching entries to bodies — and she gave him back nothing. A tired orderly. Grey jacket, chapped hands, eyes down. Nine years of practice compressed into the space of one look passing over her and finding nothing to stop on.

Invisible, she told herself, is safe. Invisible is alive. She had repeated it ten thousand times and it had worked ten thousand times.

But standing in the ward that evening, after the clerk had gone, she found the sentence had developed a crack in it. Because across the room Rafael knelt at the bed of the man in the third position — the water-drinker, the one who would not see noon — and Rafael was writing. Not in any army book. In the cloth-bound ledger he believed she did not know about, though of course she did; she had known since spring, since she'd seen the way his hand drifted to his breast pocket whenever a man died, the way a man touches a wound that aches before weather changes. He was writing the soldier's name. The true one, the full one, the one the army's clerk had taken down wrong an hour ago and would carry wrong into every report and every pension file until it ceased to be anyone's name at all.

*I. Halloran, orderly.* That was what the army would keep of her.

*Rafael,* writing by lamplight in a stolen hour, was what her brother would keep of everyone else.

She thought: one of us has understood something the other hasn't. She thought: to be invisible is to survive, and also — the crack widened — to survive as no one. The army's book and Rafael's book were both lists of names, and only one of them lied.

* * *

That night the family sat in the kitchen with the stove banked low and their mother's good candles confiscated that morning, so that they saw one another by the light of one oil lamp turned down to a thumb's flame. The requisition notice lay on the table between them where their mother had put it, smoothed flat, her flour-sack hands pressing its corners down as though it might otherwise blow away and take the whole world with it.

"Seventy-two hours," their mother said. "For paper."

"Paper," Rafael agreed. Too quickly. Too evenly. Ivo watched him say it and knew he was already three moves ahead, already walking through the house in his mind, opening drawers, weighing floorboards.

Their mother looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she filed away; Ivo could tell by the small settling of her jaw, the same motion she made putting dough aside to rise. "They took the Hendersons' Bible," she said instead. "Mrs. Henderson told me at the pump. Took it out of her hands and gave her a receipt for it. A receipt." She laughed once, without any laughter in it. "A receipt for the Word of God. So we'll know where to collect it after the war."

"After the war," said Ivo, "we'll collect a great deal."

Their mother turned to her then, and the lamplight caught the grey at her temples and the deep lines the last year had cut, and Ivo braced herself, because their mother had a way of looking at her that skipped the disguise entirely — that had always skipped it, from the very first night nine years ago when Ivo had stood in this kitchen in her dead father's coat and their mother had looked at her for a full minute and then said only, *you'll want shorter sleeves.*

"Iv—" their mother began, and stopped on the letter, the way she always stopped, catching the name half-formed and folding it back into *Ivar* before it could escape into the room where the walls had been counted and the souls tallied. "Boy," she finished. "Your papers. Will they do?"

"They've done for nine years."

"That was before there was a clerk on every corner with a book." Their mother's hands flattened the notice harder. "Before they started counting."

Rafael said nothing. He was looking at the table, at a point just left of the notice, and his right hand rested on the table edge — and Ivo saw the fingers move, once, toward the breast pocket, and stop.

The world shrank another inch. She felt it close.

Outside, down the street, chalk scraped on wood. Another door. Another number. Another handful of souls reduced to strokes and a slash, counted by a boy with cold ears who would never know what he was counting, and would write it all down neatly, and would burn what he was ordered to burn, and would sleep well afterward, because numbers do not care.

Ivo sat in the failing lamplight between her mother and her brother and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only late and low-lit, that everything they were — every letter in every drawer, every alias, every careful corruption in the parish roll, every name in a cloth-bound book under the hospital floor — had just been given seventy-two hours to become something that could be carried, or cease to exist.

She looked at Rafael's hand, resting on the table, guarding the pocket that wasn't there.

"Brother," she said lightly, into the silence, in the tone one uses for talking about the weather, "what exactly have you got under those floorboards?"
