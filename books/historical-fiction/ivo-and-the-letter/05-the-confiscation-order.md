## 5. The Confiscation Order

The order came on a Thursday, pinned to the hospital door with a bayonet's point driven through the paper and into the oak, as though the army feared even the announcement of its arrival might be disputed.

Rafael read it twice in the gray light before dawn, before the orderlies had lit the stoves, before the first moans of the waking ward rose up through the floor. The paper was heavy stock, the kind used for proclamations, and the ink had been applied by a machine — a printing press somewhere in the occupied capital, turning out thousands of identical sentences that would be nailed to ten thousand doors across ten thousand towns. That was what made it terrible, Rafael thought. Not the words themselves but their multiplication. Somewhere a clerk had set the type once, and now every written thing in the province was condemned by the same stroke of his pen.

*By order of the Provisional Military Authority: all written records, public and private — ledgers, correspondence, parish registers, diaries, bills of sale, medical charts, and papers of every description — are to be surrendered for inspection no later than Sunday, the seventeenth day of December, at the garrison offices. Failure to comply will be treated as espionage under Article Nine. Citizens are reminded that possession of undocumented writing constitutes concealment of intelligence.*

Undocumented writing. Rafael stood very still, breath fogging in the cold corridor, and let the phrase turn over. As if paper could be illegal simply for existing outside an officer's knowledge. As if a grandmother's recipe, a child's copybook, a love letter folded into a prayer book were all, by decree, intelligence against the state.

And beneath the floorboards of Ward Three, wrapped in oilcloth, lay two years of names.

* * *

Rafael went through the morning as though walking on the surface of a frozen lake, listening always for the crack beneath. The ward rounds proceeded. Broth was ladled. Bandages changed. Private Okafor, feverish, gripped Rafael's wrist and asked twice whether his mother had been written to, and Rafael said yes, yes, this week, and felt the small familiar lie settle into place like a stone in a wall. It was not entirely a lie. The letter existed. It lived in the ledger, along with the promise Rafael had made at the man's bedside: *I will tell them who you were.* But the letter had never traveled farther than the floorboards, and now the floorboards were about to be pulled up.

Because they would be pulled up. Rafael understood this by midday, watching the soldiers move through the town with their requisition chalk, marking doors. An inspection was not a reading. An inspection was an excavation. The colonel had already come once, in October, demanding casualty lists, and had accepted the official version with the bored courtesy of a man who wanted the paper more than the truth. But that had been before the evacuation orders began circulating among the officers' quarters like a sickness. Now the army wanted everything written down, because a town about to be emptied was a town whose paper trail had to be controlled. History, Rafael had come to understand, was not what happened. History was what survived, and in whose hand.

At the noon ration distribution, Rafael counted the soldiers in the yard. Fourteen. Twice the usual number. One of them was measuring the hospital's outer cellar stairs with a length of string, making notes on a clipboard.

They would search systematically. Not today, perhaps, but before Sunday. Cellars first — everyone hid things in cellars — then walls, then floors, working room by room with bayonets and crowbars, and the hospital, being full of paper by its nature, would be searched last and therefore most thoroughly, because by then the searchers would have learned where people hid things.

Rafael carried the supper trays and did the arithmetic of panic, and by evening the panic had been refined into something colder and more useful.

* * *

After lights-out, Rafael knelt in the dark of Ward Three with a candle stub shielded behind an enamel basin, and lifted the board.

It came up easier than it had gone down — two years of nervous hands had worn the edges smooth. The oilcloth parcel sat in its nest of dust and dead flies, and Rafael drew it out and held it against the chest the way one holds a wounded bird, feeling the ridiculous small weight of it. Two hundred and thirty-one names. Some had only that: a name, a regiment number, a date. Others had more. A lock of hair wrapped in waxed paper. A button from a civilian coat, worn by a boy who'd claimed to be nineteen and couldn't have been more than sixteen. A photograph of a woman standing stiffly beside a bicycle, sent by a wife who would otherwise never have known her husband had died asking for her.

Rafael unwrapped the ledger and read by the guttering stub, not because reading was necessary but because one does a thing for the last time slowly. *Halloran, R., nurse, keeps this record against the day someone asks properly.* His own hand, younger-looking somehow, from the first entry. *Marek, Josef, private, 3rd Regt., died 14 March of wounds to the belly. He spoke of an orchard. He had a brother, Tomas. Someone must find Tomas.*

Someone must find Tomas. Rafael closed the book.

Where?

The well had been Ivo's first suggestion, weeks ago, when the requisitions started — drop it down Saint Anne's well, weighted, and let the water keep its secrets. But wells got dredged; thirsty armies dredged everything. The chapel wall: a cavity behind the loose stone near the confessional, dry, dark, undisturbed for a century. Better. But chapels were being inventoried too — Rafael had seen the surveyor's men there Tuesday, tapping frescoes with knuckles, listing the silver. And there was a third possibility, the one Ivo had raised last and defended hardest, so quietly that Rafael had understood even then it was the one she believed in: a dead man's coat.

Private Andersson, who had died in November with the orchard-man's gentleness and no family anyone could locate, had left behind a greatcoat, thick wool, army-issue, already tagged for return to the depot stores with the rest of the effects of the unclaimed dead. Depot stores meant the supply carts, and the supply carts meant movement — out of the town, past the checkpoints, into the great anonymous river of army property where nothing was inspected because everything belonged to the inspectors already. Who searches a coat that the army itself is shipping?

Who searches a coat full of dead men's names, worn by a dead man, going where the dead go?

Rafael rewrapped the ledger, replaced the board, pressed the dust back into the seams with a flat palm, and put out the candle. In the darkness the ward breathed around him — forty men breathing, some rattling, some shallow as birds — and Rafael knelt a moment longer in the cold and understood that the dread had changed shape. Since dawn it had been a stone in the stomach, formless. Now it had edges. Now it was a schedule: three days, one coat, one needle and thread, and a sister whose hands were the steadiest in the garrison.

Resolve, Rafael thought, rising stiffly, is only fear that has found its work.

* * *

He found Ivo at midnight in the laundry, where she went sometimes when she couldn't sleep, folding the endless linen in the light of the banked boiler. She looked up as he entered — tall in her orderly's coat, hair bound flat under her cap, that face she kept deliberately plain and unreadable in company — and something in Rafael's own expression must have shown, because she set down the sheet she was holding before he said anything at all.

"The door," she said. "What was on the door?"

Rafael told her. He gave her the words whole, the way he'd memorized them on the walk over, and watched her take them the way she took everything — without flinching, without moving at all except in the eyes, where the calculation ran fast and far behind the stillness.

"Article Nine," she said at last. "Espionage." A short exhale that was almost a laugh. "My recipe for barley soup makes me a spy."

"Ivo."

"I know." She turned back to the linen, folded one sheet with unnecessary precision, laid it down. "You're thinking about the coat."

"I'm thinking about everything. The coat most of all."

"Andersson's coat." She nodded slowly. "It goes to the depot Monday with the unclaimed effects. If the ledger is inside the lining by Sunday night—"

"—then it leaves the town inside army property, and if it's ever found, it's found as a stranger's coat, far from us."

"If." She faced him fully then, and in the boiler-light her face was suddenly, unbearably young — the face under the disguise, the sister who had taught him to swim in the mill pond before either of them knew the world was shaped like this. "If it's sewn well enough. If the cart isn't searched at the bridge. If whoever wears that coat next doesn't notice four hundred pages adding weight to his shoulders." She paused. "And if we're both gone by then, or gone from here, or — Rafael, if it goes wrong, it doesn't just hang you. Article Nine hangs the name on the cover too. And the name on the cover is Halloran."

The silence of the laundry pressed in — steam-heat and lye and the low tick of the cooling boiler.

"Then we sew it so well," Rafael said, "that no one finds it until finding it is safe."

Ivo studied him a long moment. He knew what she was weighing: not the plan but the price, running the arithmetic she was always running, the invisible arithmetic of a person who had spent years making herself smaller than her life. Then she rolled up her sleeves.

"Andersson's coat is in effects storage," she said. "Third shelf, tagged. I have the key till six." She picked up her needle case from the windowsill, where it lived among her mending. "Bring the ledger. Bring your thread count — you always sew too loose and pretend it's speed." And then, softer, with the ghost of the old teasing that predated the war, the disguise, the century: "We'll do the lining tonight. The floorboards will just have to learn to sleep alone."

But at the door of the laundry, pausing with the lamp, she looked back, and the teasing was gone.

"Sunday they collect the papers," she said. "Monday the coat leaves. That's hours, Rafael. Hours, not days." Her voice dropped. "And Ivo Halloran's name is written in that book. Whatever alias they know me by — whatever the clerks think they've counted — the true one is in there, in your hand. If they find it while we're still in this town, they won't need Article Nine. They'll only need to read page forty."

She named no page. There was no page. But Rafael heard the truth beneath it as clearly as a bell struck in an empty church: the ledger held her, and the ledger was now the most dangerous object in the garrison, and in three days' time it would ride out of town stitched into a dead man's shoulders — or it would kill them both.

They climbed the dark stairs together toward effects storage, two sets of footsteps trying to sound like one, and above them, through the frost-furred windows, the first gray of a Friday that left only two days to make a coat carry the weight of two hundred and thirty-one lives.
