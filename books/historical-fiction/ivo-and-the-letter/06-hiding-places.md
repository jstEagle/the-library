## 6. Hiding Places

The confiscation order came at dusk, and by lamplight our kitchen had become a war council of two.

Rafael stood at the table with the ledger in their hands, that cloth-bound book no bigger than a psalter, and looked at it as though it had grown heavier since morning. Outside, the snow was coming down again — the third snowfall since the requisition began — and somewhere down the street a soldier's boots squeaked in a slow patrol rhythm, unhurried, proprietary. The town belonged to them now. Even its silence.

"The well," Rafael said. "Wrapped in oilcloth, weighted with a stone. Nobody dredges a well in winter."

"Nobody *has* to dredge it," I said. "They only have to suspect it. A well is the first place anyone looks. It's where every story ever told hides its letters and its jewels. You've handed them a map."

"And where would you put it, then? The chapel? There's a loose stone behind the saint—"

"The chapel is being used as a storehouse now. I carried crates past the altar on Tuesday. They've stacked biscuit tins where the font was." I pulled my coat off the peg, still wearing my orderly's cap because I had not yet stopped being an orderly even inside my own house; taking it off had begun to feel like something that required permission from a world that would never grant it. "And the wall stone is loose for every child in this parish. Half of them have hidden cigarettes there. If a corporal leans against it wrong, your ledger comes tumbling out at the feet of men whose orders are to burn paper."

Rafael set the book down flat on the table, palms over it, protective, the way Mother used to lay a hand over the bread when we were small and hungry and she wanted us to wait. "Then you tell me, Ivo. Where does one hide forty names?"

Forty-one, I almost said. Forty-one, counting the alias that was mine — *Ivar Halloran, orderly, fevered three nights, recovered*. My name written in my brother's careful hand between a farm boy's and a farrier's, indistinguishable from theirs, which was the whole point and also the whole wound. "You don't hide them," I said. "You move them."

* * *

We had argued like this before, years ago, the night I first put on a man's collar and bound myself and walked out into the world under a name that wasn't mine. That night Rafael had argued against it too — not because they thought it wouldn't work, but because they hated what it cost. *You'll disappear*, they'd said. *You'll go into that disguise and one day you won't come back out.* And I had answered with the arithmetic of survival: a girl alone in a garrison town during a war has two choices, and one of them isn't a choice at all.

We never resolved that argument. We simply set it down, like a tool mid-task, and picked it up again whenever the light was bad.

Now the light was very bad indeed.

"The depot sends a supply cart Thursday," I said. "Wounded men who are fit to be moved — the ones they're sending back to work, not home to die. Their kit goes with them. Greatcoats folded on the cart, unexamined, uninspected, because who searches a dead man's coat going *out* of a war zone?"

"Dead man," Rafael repeated. They'd caught it, of course. They caught everything. That was the trouble with having a sibling who kept records for a living.

"Sergeant Brakk," I said quietly. "The one who died on Monday. His effects went to the paymaster, but his coat — his coat nobody claimed. Too big for the stores list, too worn to reissue. It's hanging in the ward's back room right now with his name inked inside the collar, and in four days it rides east to the depot with the convalescents' baggage."

"You want to sew a ledger of executed and erased men into the coat of a dead sergeant and send it to a depot full of strangers."

"I want to sew it into a coat that has *already passed inspection once*. Look at it, Rafael." I crossed to the window, checked the street — the boots had squeaked past, gone around the corner — and came back to the table. "The order says all written records surrendered for inspection. So we give them everything they can see. Your official lists, the dispensary log, Mother's letters from Aunt Sanna, the children's copybooks. Let them drown in the ordinary paper of an ordinary town. But the true book — the book that matters — travels out of their reach entirely, stitched into wool, under a name they've already finished accounting for."

Rafael didn't answer at once. They turned the ledger over in their hands, and I watched their thumb move across the cloth cover the way it did when they were frightened and refusing to say so. My brother. My sister. Whatever the world allowed us to call each other tonight, which was nothing, which was everything.

"If the cart is searched—"

"It won't be."

"—if it is, some quartermaster's clerk opens a dead sergeant's coat and finds forty names the army specifically ordered destroyed, and he reads enough of them to know what they are before he burns it. Then he asks whose needlework this is. And the stitching, Ivo—" Rafael's voice dropped, not for drama but because the truth had gotten too heavy for a full voice. "The stitching will be yours. Fine work. Orderly's hands. They'll come to the hospital. They'll come to *you*, and you'll be standing there in a man's coat with papers that won't survive one careful reading."

There it was. The thing beneath the thing.

"You're not afraid of losing the ledger," I said. "You're afraid of losing me."

"I can be afraid of both. I've got room."

* * *

Mother went to bed early with a hot brick at her feet and her rosary murmured low, pretending — kindly, deliberately — not to hear us. She had gotten very good at strategic deafness these past months, our mother, but I saw the candle burn behind her door long after she should have slept, and I knew she heard every word and was praying over the parts she couldn't fix.

We worked through the evening by lamplight, and the argument changed shape the way arguments do when neither party will yield: it stopped being about the well versus the coat and became about everything else.

"You could leave," Rafael said suddenly. It came out of them sideways, the way dangerous things do. "Thursday. On the cart. There are women who take laundry to the depot, there's transport, there are papers that could be made—"

"There are papers that could be *forged*, you mean, and I'd be a fugitive in a man's clothes one checkpoint away from ruin for the rest of my life."

"Which is different from now how?"

The words landed harder than Rafael meant them to, or perhaps exactly as hard as they meant, and for a moment neither of us breathed. The lamp hissed. Snow ticked against the glass.

"That was cruel," I said.

"That was *honest*." Rafael pushed both hands through their hair, and for a second they looked nineteen again, the night before I left, standing in this same room saying *you'll disappear.* "You think I haven't noticed? You get to be invisible, Ivo. You get to walk through all of this in a borrowed name and be no one, safe inside your disguise, while I'm the one whose name is on the casualty lists, whose handwriting is on the ledger, whose neck is the one the colonel will stretch if he ever learns what I've been writing in the dark. You chose invisibility and it *worked*, and part of me has hated you for four years because it worked, because you vanished into safety and left me standing here in plain sight holding everything that couldn't vanish."

I stood very still. There is a particular pain in being accused of your own survival, and I felt it go through me like cold water — not because it was false, but because it was half true, and halves are worse than wholes.

"You think it was safety?" My voice came out lower than I intended. "You think binding my chest every morning and answering to a man's name and flinching every time a doctor's hand came near — you think that was *safety*? I traded one kind of death for another, Rafael. The visible kind was quick. This kind takes decades. But I did it so someone in this family would be alive at the end of the war, and I never once — *never once* — asked you to carry my secret for me. You volunteered. You wrote my aliases into your ledger like they were any other soldiers'. So don't stand there and tell me I left you holding everything. I *am* everything. I'm the heaviest name in that book, and I have been walking beside you the whole time, sewing bandages with these fine orderly's hands, waiting for the day the weight came due."

Silence. The lamp flame bent and straightened.

Rafael looked at me for a long moment, and I watched the anger drain out of their face and something else arrive in its place — not forgiveness exactly, but recognition, which is forgiveness's older, sterner sister.

"That was honest too," they said finally.

"Yes."

"We're good at this, aren't we. Cutting each other precisely."

"It's the family trade. Mother does it with a single raised eyebrow."

And Rafael laughed — a short, broken sound, more exhaustion than humor, but real — and sat down heavily at the table, and pulled the ledger toward themselves, and after a moment pushed it across the wood until it rested in front of me.

* * *

Sergeant Brakk's greatcoat hung on its nail in the back room of Ward Two like a man who'd stepped out of himself and forgotten to return. It smelled of him still — pipe smoke and liniment and the cold iron smell the dying leave in wool. I had dressed him for the dead-cart on Monday. I knew every tear in this coat, every stain, the button missing from the second cuff because I'd cut it free for Rafael's ledger, one small token among tokens.

Rafael held the lamp while I worked.

We'd settled it the way most things are truly settled — not by one side winning but by the other side running out of alternatives. The well was too obvious. The chapel was occupied by biscuits. The floorboards had already been lifted once and could be lifted again. And there was, I confess, a stubbornness in me now beyond strategy: I wanted the ledger to *go somewhere*. I wanted those forty-one names riding east through the snow in a dead man's wool, out past the checkpoints, out past the requisition lines, arriving at a depot where someday, somehow, a stranger might unfold a coat and find witnesses instead of ashes.

"You stitch like a surgeon," Rafael said quietly, watching my needle travel through the lining seam.

"I stitch like a woman pretending to be a man pretending to be invisible. It makes one precise."

The ledger fit perfectly once I'd wrapped it in the oilcloth Rafael insisted on anyway — compromise, our native language — flat against the coat's tail between the layers, thin as a psalter, silent as a psalter. I worked the seam closed with thread pulled from Brakk's own repair kit, so the wool and thread matched, so the mending looked like mending. Around us the ward slept its uneven sleep: men breathing, men moaning, one man by the window breathing in the slow deliberate way of someone pretending to sleep and listening hard.

I noted him. I'd note him later, if there was a later.

"Thursday," Rafael said. "The cart leaves at seven."

"Six-thirty. They always load early and blame the delay on whoever's last."

"And you'll carry it out."

"I'll carry it out." I tied off the final knot and bit the thread, and ran my palm flat along the seam, feeling nothing — which was the entire art of it. Nothing to feel. Nothing to find. "And you, brother, will spend Wednesday surrendering every scrap of innocent paper in this house so thoroughly that no lieutenant alive will believe there could be more."

"Lieutenant," Rafael repeated, and grimaced. "Voss was here again today. Third time this week. He stands at the desk and watches my hand move when I write."

"Does he now."

"He's young. He has that terrible earnestness the young carry instead of experience. He asked me yesterday where I'd learned my penmanship." Rafael shook their head slowly. "Ivo, I don't frighten easily anymore. But that boy reads the way I write. Line by line, looking for what's underneath."

I looked down at the coat on my lap, the dead sergeant's name ghost-white on the collar tag, the seam closed over forty-one lives, and felt the strange double weight of it — lighter than air in my hands, heavy as a coffin in my chest.

"Then Wednesday we feed him paper," I said. "And Thursday the coat goes east. And we hold our breath until the century turns."

Rafael nodded. But at the door, pausing with the lamp, they looked back at me with an expression I hadn't seen before — or rather, one I had, years ago, on the night I first put on the disguise: grief and love braided so tight you couldn't tell which strand was which.

"Ivo," they said. "If the inspection goes wrong tomorrow — if they come for the ledger and don't find it, and start asking who might have taken it — promise me you'll let me answer. Not you. Me. My name survives a question. Yours doesn't."

Before I could find an answer, boots sounded on the front step — two sets, heavy, unhurried — and the knocker fell against the door like a fist on a table, and Rafael's face in the lamplight went white as the snow falling past the window.
