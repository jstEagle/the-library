## 7. The Inspection

The order came at dawn, folded twice and carried by a boy who wouldn't meet my eyes. *By command of the occupying authority: all written records within the hospital precincts — official, private, or otherwise — to be assembled in the east ward for inspection at ten o'clock this morning. Non-compliance constitutes aiding the enemy.* The ink was the flat blue of government forms everywhere, and beneath it a signature I did not recognize, which somehow frightened me more than a name would have.

I stood in the corridor with the paper in my hands and listened to the building wake around me: the creak of stretchers, someone coughing in the third bay, water being carried. Ordinary sounds. But I heard them differently now, the way one hears a house differently after a thief has been through it — every floorboard suddenly a question.

Ten o'clock gave me four hours.

* * *

The ledger was not under the floorboards where I had told the colonel it wasn't, because there was no ledger for him to find. That was the fiction I had built my life upon in the past three weeks: that the cloth-bound book with its columns of names did not exist, had never existed, that my hands which wrote it were only ever writing what the army permitted me to write. Lies are load-bearing structures. Remove one and everything above it comes down.

I went to the laundry room first, because the ledger lived there now, sewn — no. I will say it plainly, even in my own memory. The ledger lived inside the ticking of a mattress in the convalescents' ward, stitched in by Ivo two nights ago while I kept watch at the door and my heart kept watch against my ribs like something trying to escape. She had done the work in twenty minutes with a curved needle and waxed thread, and when she finished she pressed her palm flat against the mattress and said, "There. Now it sleeps with the sick. No one searches a dying man's bed with any enthusiasm."

She was right, mostly. But an inspection is not a search. A search looks for a thing; an inspection looks at *everything*, and it was everything they wanted now. Paper. All the paper in the town. I understood then what requisition had been building toward — the chalk numbers on the doors, the inventories, the clerks with their lists of blankets and barrels and souls. You cannot erase people until you have first erased what was written about them.

Four hours. I made myself walk slowly down the ward, because a nurse who hurries is a nurse with something to hide, and made myself do the small honest work of the morning: changing a dressing on Corporal Engh's foot, spooning broth into a boy from the coast whose name I had written three weeks ago in a hand much neater than the army deserved. His lips moved around the spoon without waking. I counted his breaths the way I counted all their breaths now, out of habit or prayer, I no longer knew the difference.

At half past eight Ivo appeared with a basket of linens, which was our arrangement for speaking. She set the basket down, folded a sheet with those quick precise movements she had learned from our mother and then improved upon, and said, without looking up, "They're at the chapel already. Two of them. A sergeant who sneezes and a lieutenant."

"A lieutenant?"

"Young. Gloves too clean for this weather. He asked the priest how many registers the parish kept and whether baptisms counted as records." Her hands never stopped moving. "Rafa. Baptisms."

"I heard the order."

"Baptisms have names in them. Mothers' names." She meant: names like ours, the ones we had buried so carefully, the aliases layered over aliases like coats in winter. Ivo could pass as a man in a doorway, but paper was patient, and patience was the enemy's whole profession.

"They're not inspecting the parish," I said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"No," she agreed. "Today."

She lifted the basket, paused, and added in a voice pitched for me alone: "The mattress stays. If you move it, you move it in front of God and everyone, and then we've lost. Let it sleep."

And she was gone down the corridor, walking like a man, shoulders square, invisible in the way she had made an art of, and I stood there aching with the old paradox of her: that her survival depended on being unseen, and mine had come to depend on seeing, on writing down, on refusing the comfort of looking away.

* * *

They arrived at nine minutes past ten, which told me they wanted me to watch them be late.

There were four of them. A major who smelled of cologne applied over the smell of the train, two clerks with satchels, and the lieutenant Ivo had described. He was perhaps twenty-five, tall without being broad, with the kind of face that hadn't yet decided what it believed. His gloves were indeed too clean. He carried no satchel. The others carried the instruments; he carried, apparently, himself.

"Gentlemen." The major addressed the ward as if it were a market stall. "All documents to the central table. Patient files, admission registers, dispensary logs, correspondence held on the premises. Nothing leaves the building until the review concludes."

The clerks began. I watched them work the way I had once watched surgeons work — with that double vision, the procedure proceeding calmly on the surface while underneath you tracked every vessel it might cut. They took the admission book first, of course. My official hand was in it, the hand I showed the world: dates, regiments, wounds, outcomes. *Died* written in the tidy abbreviations the forms required, as if men were weather events. The clerks flipped pages. The major glanced, nodded, moved on.

The lieutenant read.

That was the difference, and I felt it before I understood it. The clerks handled paper like cargo. The lieutenant stood at the table and actually read, line by line, and while he read he looked at nothing else, and while he looked at nothing else I could feel him looking at everything.

"Matron Halloran."

I crossed the ward. Up close he was younger than I'd thought, and there was ink on the edge of his right hand, the shadow of it that never quite washes off a person who writes constantly. A fellow creature of paper. I didn't know yet whether that made him safer or infinitely more dangerous.

"Lieutenant Voss," he said. "You keep the registers?"

"I keep what I'm given and write what I see."

"Yes." He turned the admission book toward me, one finger resting on a page as lightly as if the page might bruise. "Your hand changes."

The ward went very quiet around us, or I imagined it did; either way the silence had edges.

"Does it?" I said.

"Here." His finger moved down the column. "These entries — February through March — firm hand, heavy pressure, someone writing fast because there were too many to write slow. Then here—" the finger slid another inch, "—the same alphabet, but slower. Considered. And after April, faster again, but differently. Looser. As though the writer had decided something."

He looked up at me. His eyes were pale gray, unremarkable, and completely awake.

"Handwriting reflects fatigue," I said. "We were shorthanded in spring. We are shorthanded now."

"That's true," he agreed pleasantly. "It usually is."

He let the sentence sit there between us like a coin on a table, and I could not tell if it was payment or bait. Behind him the clerks had begun on the dispensary log, and one of them called out a number discrepancy — sixty vials of morphine recorded received, fifty-four accounted for — and the major grunted and told me to explain it, and I explained it, truthfully, because the truth of the morphine was simple: six vials had gone into men instead of onto forms, and I would make that trade again at gunpoint. The major wrote *irregular, noted* in his own immaculate hand, and moved on to the linen stores.

Voss did not move on. He turned back to the beginning of the admission book and started reading again, from the first page, and this time he read aloud, very quietly, not to me exactly and not to himself but to the space between, the way one reads in order to measure something.

"'J. Aldous, private, gunshot, left thigh.'" Page-turn. "'T. Merek, private, fever.'" Page-turn. "Do you remember them?"

"I remember all of them."

"All of them," he repeated. It was not mockery. That was the unsettling part. "Then you'll know how many men passed through this book and don't appear in the regimental returns. I've compared. There are forty-one patients in these registers whose deaths are recorded nowhere else. Not in the returns, not in the casualty wires, not in the depot archive. Forty-one men who, according to the army, were never here at all."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree and right itself. Somewhere behind me a man moaned in his sleep, and I clung to the sound like a rail.

"Paperwork goes astray in wartime, Lieutenant."

"It does." He closed the book gently, almost tenderly, and I understood that he loved paper too, in his own damaged way, and that this made him worse than the major, not better. "It goes astray, or it's carried astray, or it's copied before it goes somewhere useful. Those are the three fates of paper, Matron, in my experience. I've often wondered which fate suits a given document best."

"And which fate do you recommend?"

For the first time, something moved behind the gray eyes that might have been respect, or might have been the recognition of prey.

"The useful one," he said. "Eventually. When the war is over, someone should know those forty-one names. It seems a shame to burn the only copy of a man." He straightened his gloves. "But today I'm instructed to inventory, not to judge. Today I count what exists."

*What exists.* The words walked down my spine. I kept my face arranged in its nursing expression, the one that has absorbed ten thousand kinds of bad news without changing, and I thanked him for his thoroughness, and I walked back down the ward at the same unhurried pace, and only my hands knew — cold, in my pockets, curled around nothing.

* * *

They worked through the ward for two hours. The clerks were thorough in the way of men following a list: cupboards opened, desk drawers run through with a finger, the chaplain's shelf of devotional books shaken spine-up to dislodge loose papers. One of them found letters under a dead soldier's pillow — three of them, unsent — and the major put them in the confiscation crate without reading them, which struck me as its own kind of obscenity. Words a man had spent his last strength choosing, filed unread between requisition forms and a list of blankets.

At noon the lieutenant did something none of the procedures required. He began at the far end of the ward and walked the beds.

He spoke to the men who could speak. He asked the boy from the coast his name, and wrote it down — wrote it in his own little notebook, not the official forms — and asked where home was, and whether there was anyone to notify, and the boy, half-lucid, gave a village and a mother's name, and Voss wrote both with the same careful pressure he'd used reading my registers. Bed by bed he moved down the ward collecting what the army had declined to collect. Names. Homes. People to grieve.

He was doing my work, I realized. He was keeping a ledger.

I did not know what to do with that realization. It sat in me like swallowed glass. An officer who inventories the dead by choice is either a better man than his uniform or a more dangerous one, and the two categories overlap more than anyone wants to admit.

He reached the last bed but one — Corporal Engh, asleep, his bandaged foot propped on a stool — and then he reached the convalescent row, and the mattresses, and I felt my pulse move into my throat and stay there.

Voss stopped at the second bed. The occupant was a sergeant with a chest wound who slept eighteen hours a day and had not been conscious in four. Voss stood over him a moment. Then, without ceremony, without even glancing in my direction to see whether I was watching — and I was watching; I have never watched anything harder in my life — he pressed his palm flat against the mattress, near the pillow, and held it there.

Three seconds. Four. Feeling for what? Weight? Lumps? The particular thickness of a mattress with a book sewn into it?

Five seconds.

He withdrew his hand. He wrote something small in his notebook. He moved to the next bed, pressed the mattress there too — thinner, ordinary — and continued down the row as if checking for lice, which was presumably what any observer was meant to assume.

I busied myself at the water stand. My hands poured water for a man who was asleep. Some part of my mind detached and began doing arithmetic I did not want it to do: how long to unstitch, how far to the well, how many steps from the ward door to the courtyard, whether the major would notice a nurse leaving, whether Ivo was anywhere in the building, whether—

Voss finished the row. He closed his notebook, and as he passed me at the water stand he slowed, just slightly, and said, without turning his head, "Very comfortable mattresses in this hospital, Matron. Better made than the ones at the depot."

"Local wool," I said. "We're lucky in some things."

"So I see." And he smiled — a small, correct, terrible smile — and followed the major out into the gray light, and the doors closed behind them, and the ward exhaled.

* * *

Ivo found me twenty minutes later sitting on a stool in the linen closet with a sheet pressed over my mouth and nose, breathing through it, though there was nothing wrong with the air. She pulled the sheet down, looked at my face, and read it the way she had read my face across twenty years of shared winters.

"How close?" she asked.

"Hours," I said. "Maybe less. He touched the mattress, Ivo. He knows it's heavier than it should be, or he suspects, or he's guessing, and his guess is better than most men's certainty. He gave me until — I don't know. Until he comes back with permission to open things instead of look at them."

She sat down on a pile of folded sheets, pulling her coat — his coat, the orderly's coat — around her, and for a moment my sister looked very young, and very tired, and entirely visible, which is rare for her and always frightens me.

"Well," she said at last. "Then the mattress is done. Tonight it moves."

"Where? The well? The chapel wall? Tell me you haven't—"

"No." She rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. "The well floods in thaw. The chapel has a sergeant counting baptismal registers as we speak. No, Rafa. There's one place in this town where no clerk will ever look, because no clerk has ever cared what happens to it." She dropped her hands and looked at me, and there was something in her face I couldn't name yet — resolve wearing the mask of mischief, or the other way round. "Sergeant Aldous's greatcoat. The one folded in the mortuary with his effects, waiting for the supply cart to take it to the depot. Dead men's coats go where dead men's coats go, and nobody reads them."

"You want to send forty-one names to the depot in a dead man's pocket."

"I want to keep them alive," Ivo said, "and that coat is the only vehicle in this town nobody inspects. The cart leaves Thursday." She stood, smoothed the orderly's coat, became a man again in the space of a breath. "So we have tonight, and one argument about it, and you're going to lose the argument, brother, because I've already threaded the needle."

She left me among the linens with my heart going like a drum at a retreat, and through the frosted window I could see, far off at the gate, Lieutenant Voss pausing on his way out — pausing, and looking back at the hospital, at my window, at me or at the idea of me — before he turned up the road toward whatever he intended to do with what he knew.

Thursday, the cart would leave.

I doubted we had until Thursday.
