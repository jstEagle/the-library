## 8. Berths

The notice went up on the chapel door on a morning so cold the paste froze before the clerk could smooth it flat, and so the paper stood away from the wood at one corner, lifting and settling in the wind like something trying to speak.

Ivo Halloran read it twice, once as an orderly of this garrison and once as no one at all, a person who existed on no paper anywhere, and both readings told her the same thing: there was a ship.

*By order of the Army Command, evacuation of civilian and non-essential personnel will commence 31 December. One vessel. Berths allocated by rank, family status, and lottery. Lists to be posted at the harbourmaster's office within seven days.*

One vessel. Before the century turned or not at all.

She stood in the small crowd gathered before the door — women with shawls drawn to their eyebrows, an old man leaning on a boy's shoulder, two soldiers who had no business reading civilian notices and read them anyway — and she felt the arithmetic begin in her chest before her mind had consented to it. That was how it always worked now. Some part of her had become a counting-house, tallying everything against the day it would be needed: tins in the pantry, candles, morphine tablets, names. And now this. One ship. How many berths. Who counted as family, and under what name.

The boy holding the old man's shoulder said, "Grandda, we'll go together, won't we," and the old man said, "Of course, of course," in the voice people used for children and for lies, and Ivo turned away because she could not watch it and keep her face orderly.

* * *

At the hospital the news had arrived ahead of the paper, the way bad news did, carried in the mouths of orderlies and cooks until it was everywhere at once. Rafael met her in the corridor between wards, and she knew from ten paces that something had already been decided inside him, some column added up, though he only handed her a stack of bandages to fold and said nothing for a long moment.

"You've seen it," he said.

"The chapel door."

"One ship." He said it the way he read casualty figures aloud, flat, each word set down separately so none of them could lean on another. "Matron says perhaps four hundred berths. There are better than two thousand in the town still standing."

Ivo folded a bandage into thirds, then thirds again, until it was a hard white square. "Families?"

"Split by lottery and rank. Not even whole families go together. Mother has already been to the harbourmaster's office twice this morning. She came back with chalk on her skirt."

Their mother had a habit, when the world became a ledger, of putting herself bodily into the columns — queueing at dawn, arguing with clerks, standing her ground before men with lists until the lists bent. Ivo had spent years being grateful for it and ashamed of the gratitude, since every hour their mother spent fighting officialdom was an hour Ivo herself could not spend, standing at any counter, offering any papers.

"Rafael," she said. "Read me your number."

He understood at once. Hospital staff were essential personnel; his name would be near the bottom of any list, or not on it at all, held back to nurse whoever was left behind when the gangway came up. He gave her his lottery figure without flinching, and she worked it against hers, and the sum came out wrong in every direction.

"And yours?" he asked.

"I'm listed as T. Halloran, orderly, second class." She kept her voice level. "Thomas has a berth in the third drawing. Thomas also has papers that would not survive a clerk looking too long at the fourth letter of his surname."

It was the first time she had said it aloud so plainly, and saying it changed nothing and everything. Rafael looked at her the way he looked at a wound that had finally shown its true depth — not with horror but with the immediate, practical attention of a man deciding what to do next.

* * *

That evening the harbourmaster's office became the centre of the town's life, more than the chapel, more than the wells. A line formed before dawn each day and stayed past dark, townspeople waiting to confirm they existed — because that was what it had come to, Ivo realized, watching from across the square while she pretended to carry a crate of dressings. The lists were posted in the office window, and everyone in that queue was waiting to learn whether the name they had lived under for forty years had been spelled correctly by some clerk in the capital, whether their marriage certificate from a parish that had since burned would be judged sufficient, whether they were, on paper, real.

A soldier walked the line with chalk and marked numbers on shoulders and coat collars, and Ivo watched a woman beg him to mark her children and not herself, and watched him refuse, because the numbers were not kindnesses, they were inventory.

Inside the window, three sheets of paper in a glass case. *List A: priority embarkation, military dependents. List B: general berths, by lot. List C: retained personnel.* She found Halloran, T., on List B, position 214. She found Halloran, R., on List C, along with every doctor and matron and trained nurse in the garrison, which meant Rafael would stand on the quay on the last night and load other people onto the ship and stay.

And she found, on List B between Halloway and Halvorsen, a gap where a name should have been — a smudge, a correction, someone's ink failing. Position 213 was blank. She stared at it through the glass until her eyes watered with cold, and she thought: *that is what I am, if they look closely. A smudge. A correction.*

* * *

Her papers lived in a oilcloth packet sewn into the lining of her coat, and that night, alone in the room she shared with two other orderlies' widows — the fiction required that she lodge apart from family, another wall built brick by brick over nine years — she took the packet out and laid its contents on the bed like a surgeon laying out instruments.

A birth certificate. An army work permit. A letter of character signed by a priest who had since died. A medical exemption card. Each document was genuine in its way, salvaged, adapted, reissued over years to a boy named Thomas Halloran who had grown steadily more solid in the world's records while the girl he had replaced grew less. Nine years of careful maintenance. Nine years of never standing closer than arm's length to a checkpoint clerk, never letting a document leave her hands, never answering a question directly when a mumble would do.

The new checkpoints changed everything. Evacuation meant queues, and queues meant clerks, and clerks meant documents passed hand to hand under lamplight while the holder stood exposed in full view, waiting, while the clerk compared the photograph — she had none, photographs were for officers — or asked questions designed to catch exactly her kind of discrepancy.

She spread the papers out and read them as an enemy would.

Name: Thomas Halloran. Age given: thirty-one. Her hands were thirty-one; her face, if you knew to look, was not quite. Sex: male. Occupation: orderly. Next of kin: R. Halloran, brother.

There it was. The trap folded inside itself. If she boarded as Thomas, Rafael was her brother — fine, natural, unremarkable. But if anyone ever pulled the thread, if any clerk ever checked the parish rolls or wrote to the old priest's successor, then Thomas Halloran was a fraud, and worse, his sister Ivo was a woman living undocumented in a requisitioned town under martial law, and the penalty for that had been posted on walls all autumn in language that did not use the word *woman* because the law preferred euphemism the way wounds preferred bandages.

Every berth on that ship came with a checkpoint between here and it. Every checkpoint was a place where nine years of careful stitching could be picked open in under a minute by a bored man with good eyesight.

She repacked the papers, sewed the packet shut with small neat stitches, and lay in the dark listening to the widows breathe, and did the arithmetic again, because it was the only prayer she knew.

Four hundred berths. Two thousand souls. Rafael held back as essential staff. Herself conditional on a lie surviving scrutiny. Their mother, whose own documents were impeccable and whose age placed her high on the priority lists — a widow of a veteran drew preference, and their father's name still opened doors he had died behind.

So the mathematics, done honestly, came to this: their mother could leave. Rafael could not. And Ivo could leave only if nothing went wrong, and something always went wrong, she had nine years of evidence that something always went wrong.

Which meant the truest accounting was simpler and crueller: there was, functionally, one berth in this family that would survive contact with reality. Their mother's.

Unless.

She lay still a long time, listening to the wind find the loose shutter.

Unless Rafael gave his place — but Rafael had no place to give; List C held him fast unless a manifest was altered, and manifests were altered by officers for reasons of state, not mercy. Unless someone struck a name and wrote another. She had seen the manifests once, through the harbourmaster's doorway: heavy ruled pages, columns for corrections, a clerk's initials required beside every amendment.

Corrections were possible. Corrections happened. Position 213 proved it.

She fell asleep mid-calculation and dreamed of the chapel notice, its corner lifting and settling, lifting and settling, trying to say the thing the printed words would not.

* * *

Morning brought their mother to the hospital steps, waiting for the change of shift, and Ivo saw at once that the night had done its work on her too. Ana Halloran had the look of a woman who had spent hours with a harbourmaster's clerk and won concessions and trusted none of them.

They walked home the long way, along the frozen canal, because the main streets were choked with requisition carts, and it was on the towpath, between the ice and the dead rushes, that their mother said what she had clearly rehearsed all night.

"There will be a choice," she said. "There's always a choice, whatever the notices pretend. Lists are paper. Paper can be argued with. But we will have to choose, and I'd rather we chose it ourselves than let a clerk choose for us."

"Mother—"

"I'm talking." Her voice was quiet and terrible. "I am sixty-one years old. My papers are perfect. Your brother cannot leave — he knows it, I know it, the whole hospital would collapse without him and Command knows it best of all. You can leave, if your lie holds. So the question is whether your lie holds well enough for me to put my own safety behind it, because I will tell you now, Iva—" and she used the true name, softly, the way you'd touch a scar, "—if you board that ship and they pull you off at the gangway, I am not staying here without either of my children. Do you understand? I go where you go. On the ship or back up the hill. There is no version of this where I wave from the rail."

Ivo looked at the ice. Underneath it the water moved, black and patient.

"Then we wait for the lists to correct themselves," she said. "Position 213 is blank. Someone made an error somewhere, and errors get corrected, and sometimes corrections go the other way."

"That isn't arithmetic. That's hope."

"It's what I have instead."

Their mother stopped walking. On the far bank a patrol went by, four men and a dog, breath steaming, and neither of them turned to watch it, both of them expert by now at stillness.

"Nine years," her mother said. "Nine years I have written *my son* in letters and *Mr. Halloran's boy* to the neighbours, and never once, not once, have I asked you to take it back, because taking it back meant dying, and I wanted you alive more than I wanted you my daughter. But I want you to hear me say it before the century turns, in case the century doesn't give us another chance. If we get one berth between the three of us, and the choice comes down to my life or your exposure—" her jaw set, the exact jaw Rafael had, the exact jaw Ivo hid beneath a bound chest and a man's coat, "—then I will stand on that quay and make a scene such as this town has never seen, and no clerk in the King's army will move you an inch toward that gangway. Remember that. Carry it. When the moment comes, and it is coming, I want you to know it was decided here, on the ice, in December, by a stubborn old woman who loves you."

Then she walked on, chin up, a small straight-backed figure against the gray water, and Ivo followed, and neither spoke again, and the canal ice creaked beneath them like a ship taking on cargo.

* * *

Two days later the corrected lists were posted, and the town crowded the harbourmaster's window in the falling snow, and Ivo, pressed among strangers' wet wool coats, read the amendments through the glass with her heart going like a fist on a locked door.

Position 213 had been filled. A clerical error, the amendment slip noted, duplicate allocation resolved. And there, in the space where the smudge had been, in the clerk's careful hand, were two names where the rules allowed one:

*Halloran, R.*
*Halloran, T.*

Both listed. Both marked. One berth.

Behind her in the crowd someone laughed bitterly and said what everyone was thinking: two names, one berth, and the army would sort it out in its own time — meaning it would strike one name at random, or worse, inspect both, and inspection was death coming quietly up the gangplank wearing a clerk's cap.

Ivo stood in the snow and felt hope curdle in her chest into something harder and colder and more useful. Because an error was now on record. A manifest carried their names side by side, and manifests could be amended, and somewhere in the town tonight there must be a man with the authority to hold a pen over that page — and she had three days, maybe fewer, before the correction was corrected for them.

She turned toward the hospital to find Rafael, pushing through the crowd, and behind her the harbour bell rang out through the snow, once, twice, counting down.
