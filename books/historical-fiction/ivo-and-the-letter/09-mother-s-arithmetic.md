## 9. Mother's Arithmetic

The lottery notice went up on the customs house door at noon, and by three o'clock the whole town had memorized it the way the drowning memorize the position of the moon.

*One vessel. One departure. Berths allocated by rank, by family unit, by lot. Evacuation complete before midnight, December 31st, 1899.*

Rafael read it twice, then a third time, because the third reading was the one where the arithmetic finally landed in the chest like a dropped stone. He stood in the crowd with his hands in his coat pockets, feeling the ghost of the ledger's weight even though the ledger itself lay sewn into a dead man's greatcoat, somewhere in the occupied quarter, on a cart he had paid a week of morphine to find and had not yet found again. The notice was printed in the army's tidy type. Nothing about it was negotiable. Nothing about it even pretended to be.

Beside him, a woman with a shawl over her head said to no one in particular, "By rank. By *rank*." She said it the way people said the names of diseases.

Rafael walked home through streets that had become someone else's inventory. Chalk numbers on the doors. Soldiers at the corners with lists of their own, official ones, the kind that got to exist. The bakery was closed, requisitioned. The schoolhouse had a number chalked on it now too, as if children were a commodity that could be counted and carried off. He passed the mill bridge without looking at it. He had looked at it too much already this week.

The house on Vester Lane had the number 44 chalked on its door, and his mother had scrubbed at it once with a wet cloth and then stopped, as if she had decided the number was beneath the dignity of resistance.

Inside, the stove was lit and the room smelled of boiled turnips and coal smoke, and Ivo was sitting at the table with his boots off — a thing he only did indoors, a thing that was itself a small act of disguise, because boots made the man and bare feet made the brother — and their mother, Sigrid Halloran, was standing at the window with her arms folded, watching the street the way she had watched it through two wars and one occupation, which was to say: without hope, but with attention.

"You've seen it," she said, without turning.

"We've seen it," Rafael said.

"Then sit down." She turned. Her face in the stove light was the face Rafael had known all his life and still could not fully read: the strong jaw, the gray coming through the dark hair like frost through a field, the mouth that had delivered more hard sentences than any magistrate in the province. "Both of you. Sit down. We are going to settle this tonight, because I have watched the two of you circle it for three days like dogs around a bone, and I will not have us spending our last week in this town performing politeness."

Rafael sat. Ivo did not move at first. There was a quality to his stillness that Rafael had learned to read the way sailors read weather — the particular motionlessness of a person calculating exits. Then he pulled out the chair across from Rafael and sat, and the three of them made a triangle around the table, and their mother laid her hands flat on the wood as if to hold it, and them, in place.

"Three of us," she said. "One berth."

"Mother—" Ivo began.

"Let me finish. I have been doing sums since the notice went up, and I have done them faster and more honestly than either of you, because you have both been doing something else. You have been doing *performance*. Rafael has been performing the martyr. Ivo has been performing the pragmatist. And I have been standing at that window watching you perform, and I am tired, and the stove is expensive, and we are going to speak plainly for once in this family's history."

The turnips steamed. Somewhere down the street a soldier's boots rang on frozen cobblestones, unhurried, the walk of a man who owned the ground he walked on.

"By rank," Sigrid continued, "your brother has a claim. Hospital staff are evacuation-priority — I read the annex posted at the customs house, the one nobody reads because they are too busy weeping at the main notice. Nurses and physicians go with the wounded. Rafael, you are on the manifest by profession. You don't even need the lottery."

"I'm not going," Rafael said.

"You see?" Their mother spread her hands toward Ivo, an attorney presenting evidence. "There it is. Day one of the martyrdom. He has decided he isn't going, and he has decided it in that tone of voice that means he has already written the scene in his head where he stays nobly behind, and he is waiting for one of us to argue him out of it so he can refuse beautifully."

"That isn't fair," Rafael said.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair, which is why I am done being fair and have moved on to being *correct*."

Ivo made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. He was staring at the table, at his own bare hands, and Rafael saw — with the particular cruelty of attention — how carefully Ivo had learned to hold those hands. Flat on the wood. Fingers spread. A man's hands, or what passed for one in the bad light of a stove-lit kitchen. Twenty-two years of practice in that single gesture.

"Then let us be correct," Ivo said quietly. "The manifest lists Rafael. It does not list me, because officially I am not a person with a claim; I am an orderly attached to the hospital, and orderlies are allocated *after* the wounded, and there will be no berths left after the wounded. Rafael goes by profession. That is the arithmetic. It is simple."

"Except that your papers could unravel at any checkpoint," their mother said. "Except that the name on your travel document is a name you borrowed from a boy who died of fever in '81, and if one clerk reads one letter too carefully, you will not be put on a ship, you will be put in a room with a lamp and a man with questions. So the arithmetic is not simple. The arithmetic is: Rafael can leave safely. Ivo can leave *dangerously*. And I—" she paused, and for one moment the magistrate's mouth wavered, and Rafael saw the woman beneath, sixty-one years old, who had buried a husband and outlived an empire's patience, "—I am an old woman with no profession and no rank, who takes up a berth that either of her children could use."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Rafael felt it settle on his shoulders like snow.

"No," he said.

"You didn't let me finish—"

"You're not staying. That is not — Mother, that is not a thing we are going to discuss as though it were on the table."

"It is on the table. I put it there. That is what tables are for."

* * *

The argument, when it finally came apart into its true components, was not about berths. Rafael understood that within the first ten minutes. It was about a debt twenty-two years old, and it arrived the way old debts always arrive: suddenly, with interest.

It was Ivo who broke the dam, which surprised Rafael; he had expected their mother to do it. Ivo had been quiet through the first exchange of positions — Rafael refusing to leave, their mother refusing to let Rafael refuse — and then he had said, in a voice so level it was almost gentle:

"You want to know why Rafael performs the martyr? Because martyrdom is the one role in this family that was never offered to me."

Sigrid went very still.

"I was given the other role," Ivo said. He still wasn't looking up. "The disappearing role. Do you remember, Mother? Do you remember standing in this kitchen — this kitchen, these tiles, I can show you the exact tile — and telling me that the world had no place for what I was, and that the only mercy the century offered was a pair of trousers and a new name and silence? I was fourteen. You cut my hair yourself. You burned my dresses in the stove — this stove, or its predecessor — and you told me it was a kindness." He looked up then, and his eyes were wet and furious. "And it *was* a kindness. That is the trap of it. It was a kindness, and it worked, and I am alive because of it, and I have never once in twenty-two years been able to decide whether to thank you or to grieve."

"Ivo," Rafael said softly.

"No. Let me. Because Rafael wants to stay behind with his ledger and his names and his beautiful useless heroism, and Mother wants to stay behind because she has decided her life is the cheapest coin on this table, and nobody — *nobody* — is asking the question underneath, which is: who got to be seen in this family, and who got to be *hidden*?" His voice cracked on the last word and he pressed on through the crack. "Rafael keeps a book of names because the army erases people. I *am* the book of names, Mother. Every name Rafael writes down, some version of it has been mine. I have been Anders. I have been Mikkel. I have been a dead boy's name for two decades so that this family could keep a daughter it was never willing to acknowledge it had. And now there is one berth, and the family's instinct — its *loving* instinct — is to put me on it, because putting me on it means I go on being the hidden thing. The thing that is sent ahead. The thing whose survival is arranged rather than chosen."

"That is not—" Rafael started.

"Isn't it?" Ivo turned on him, and there was twenty-two years in his face. "You gave me your coat when we ran from the first checkpoint. You gave me your bread in the winter of '94. You are giving me your berth. Do you know what all three of those gifts have in common, Rafael? In every one of them, you stayed *visible*. You stayed the son, the brother, the nurse with the honest name and the honest face. And I went ahead, disguised, alone, into whatever came next. You have spent your whole life giving me survival and keeping the *self* for yourself. And I love you. God help me, I love you, and I am so tired of being loved in a way that requires me to disappear."

The stove ticked. The turnips went cold. Down the street, the boots had stopped somewhere, and a door closed.

Sigrid Halloran had not moved through any of it. Her hands stayed flat on the table, and Rafael watched his mother — the woman who had cut a child's hair with sewing shears and called it mercy — absorb her son's grief the way a seawall absorbs a wave: without visible motion, but Rafael could see the cost of it in the tendons of her neck.

"You are right," she said at last.

Ivo blinked. Of the three of them, he had perhaps expected this least.

"You are right, and I have no defense, and I am not going to insult you by building one now." Her voice was steady, but it was the steadiness of a rope under load. "I cut your hair because the alternative was watching them take you somewhere worse than a name. I would do it again. That is the truth, and it is an ugly truth, and it does not make your grief smaller. But hear me, Ivo, and hear me carefully, because I am only going to say this once." She leaned forward. "I did not hide you because I was ashamed of you. I hid you because the world was armed. And there is a difference, and someday — maybe not in this century — you will believe me about the difference."

"And now?" Ivo's voice was raw. "Now that the world is still armed? Now there's one berth, and you've already done your sums, and your sums end with you staying. That's not arithmetic, Mother. That's the same mercy. The ugly kind. The kind that works."

"Yes," she said simply. "It is."

"No." Rafael heard his own voice come out harder than he had intended, and did not soften it. "No. I'm ending this. Both of you have spent this evening competing to be the one who stays, and I have sat here listening to you make love sound like a bidding war, and I am done." He stood. His knees ached — he had been on his feet in the ward for fourteen hours and had forgotten until now. "Here is what is actually true. I am on the manifest by profession. That is not negotiable, it is *typed*. Ivo's name is on the lottery roll — the clerk's error, the duplicate entry, whatever it was — and that gives us three days before someone corrects it for us. Three days. And in three days, no one in this kitchen is going to win this argument by being the most willing to be lost. I refuse. I refuse all of it. Nobody stays out of nobility, nobody stays out of penance, and nobody—" his voice did break here, and he let it, "—nobody gets sent ahead to survive in a disguise ever again. Not in this family. Not in the last week of this century. We have three days. We are going to use them to find a third option, and if there is no third option, then we are going to stand at that quay together and decide as equals, with everyone's whole name in the room. Do you understand me? *Whole names.*"

The silence rang.

Ivo was looking at him with an expression Rafael had never seen before — something stripped and young, the fourteen-year-old surfacing through the orderly's careful face like a body through ice. And their mother had closed her eyes, and her hands had finally come off the table, and she was sitting in the chair like an old woman, just an old woman, at the end of a hard year at the end of a hard century.

"Three days," she said quietly. "And what do we do with them, Rafael? You've refused everything. What do you propose?"

And Rafael, who had an answer for dying soldiers and an answer for colonels and an answer for the dark at three in the morning, found he had no answer at all — and was spared the humiliation of saying so, because that was the moment the knock came at the door.

Three knocks. Measured, military, wrong for the hour.

Ivo was on his feet first, barefoot and silent, moving to the wall out of the window's sightline out of two decades of habit. Sigrid's eyes had gone hard again, the seawall rebuilt in a breath. Rafael crossed the room and opened the door a hand's width, and the stove light spilled out onto the step, onto the snow, onto the uniform.

The young lieutenant from the inspection stood on their doorstep, gray-faced, one hand pressed to his side where his greatcoat had gone dark and stiff, and in his other hand — Rafael saw it and felt the floor tilt — he carried a bundle of letters, tied with string, the confiscated letters, the ones he had been ordered to burn.

"Forgive the hour," the lieutenant said. His voice was not steady. "I've been shot, and I find I don't want to die in the barracks. And I need to speak with you, Nurse Halloran. About the manifest. About what I can still change."

Behind Rafael, in the stove light, he heard his mother rise, and heard Ivo's breath catch — and the three-day arithmetic of the Halloran family tilted, all at once, on the weight of a bleeding stranger holding a bundle of other people's words.
