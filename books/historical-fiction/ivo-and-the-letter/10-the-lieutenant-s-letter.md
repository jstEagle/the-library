## 10. The Lieutenant's Letter

He bled on our doorstep the way a gentleman spills wine — apologetically, one hand pressed to his side as though he could hold the evening in.

I had seen my brother bring men through that door before. I knew the choreography of it: the shoulders under the arms, the voice pitched low and certain, *you're all right, you're here now*, even when it wasn't true. But I had never been the one to catch the weight. When Rafael stepped aside to bar the door behind him, the lieutenant swayed, and some instinct older than my disguise moved me forward, and then his arm was over my neck and all his careful officer's posture came apart in my hands like wet paper.

He was lighter than I expected. That is what I remember thinking, absurdly, as we got him down the hall — *he's light, they starve them too* — this man whose neat questions at the inspection had followed me into sleep for weeks. Lieutenant Ashe. I had memorized his face the way one memorizes the face of a dog that might bite. Now that face was gray, and the dog was dying on our kitchen table.

"Not the table," my mother said, already moving. "The cot by the stove. Rafael, the good lamp."

"The barracks surgeon—"

"Is three streets away and drunk," Rafael said. "Coat off. Scissors."

I cut the uniform because it seemed right that someone should. There is an intimacy in scissors that no confession matches; I opened the jacket down the seam and saw the wound beneath, high in the left side, a small ugly mouth that welled as I pressed around it. Through-and-through, or near enough. He had bound it himself, badly, with a handkerchief gone rust-brown, and beneath the handkerchief, against his ribs, something stiff that crinkled when I lifted him to turn him.

A satchel strap. Leather, army-issue, buckled flat to his body so it wouldn't swing.

"Leave it," he said. His eyes found mine and held them with a focus that cost him visibly. "Orderly. Leave the bag."

"You're shot, sir. The bag can wait."

"It has waited." A breath, drawn thin. "It can wait until I can watch you open it."

*Watch.* Not *let*. As if the letters inside were prisoners he could not afford to lose custody of for even the length of an unconsciousness. My hands went still on his chest. I looked at Rafael across the stove light, and Rafael gave me the smallest shake of the head — *later* — and I let it be later.

We worked. My mother boiled water and did not ask questions, which is her particular genius; she has the gift of making silence itself into assistance. Rafael probed, and the lieutenant took it the way soldiers take pain, jaw set, eyes fixed on a knot in the ceiling beam, and only once did he make a sound, and that sound was not a cry but a word, half-swallowed: a name. A woman's name, maybe. Or a mother's. It didn't matter which. I have learned that the dying call out names the way drowning men clutch driftwood, and the name matters less than the reaching.

By two in the morning he was stitched and feverish and alive, which surprised me more than it should have. Men like him die easily, I thought, and then corrected myself, because I did not actually know how men like him died. I knew how orderlies died, and privates, and boys who lied about their age. Officers were a country I had only seen from its borders.

Rafael went to lie down for an hour. Mother stayed in her chair by the window, knitting without looking at her needles, a sentinel in wool. And I sat on the stool beside the cot, changing the compress when it needed changing, watching the firelight move on a face younger than rank.

It was nearly four when he spoke.

"You wrote the list."

Not a question. I kept my voice level, my voice that was a man's voice, dull and serviceable. "I copy what Nurse Halloran writes, sir. He has the handwriting worth copying."

"No." He turned his head on the pillow with effort, and his eyes were clear now, fever-bright but clear. "I've seen your hand before tonight. At the inspection. On the ward ledger — the official one. Your letters lean left at the ends of words, like a person writing against wind." He paused. "And I've seen it somewhere else. On a casualty return that didn't come out of any army office."

My heart performed a small cold maneuver. "Sir, I don't—"

"Halloran's ledger." He said it softly, almost kindly. "The real one. Don't trouble yourself denying it. I'm not here for it. If I were, you'd already know — I'd have brought men."

I sat very still. Outside, snow ticked against the shutter. In the window's black glass my mother's reflection had stopped knitting.

"Why would you know that?" I asked. "Why would you care?"

He was quiet a long moment. Then he said, "Help me sit up," and when I did, wincing at what it cost him, he reached down with his free hand and unbuckled the satchel at last, and drew it onto the blanket between us, and opened it.

Letters. Dozens of them. Perhaps a hundred. Tied in bundles with regulation twine, each bundle tagged in a clerk's precise hand: *undeliverable — hold for disposition.* Confiscated mail. The harvest of every checkpoint and requisition since autumn, the words the army had taken out of the world along with the ledgers and the parish rolls and the shop inventories.

"My orders," he said, "were to burn these. All written matter of unverified provenance, to be destroyed after cataloguing. I catalogued them." His mouth twisted. "That's the trick of it. You catalogue a letter — sender, recipient, date, summary of contents — and you have already read it. You have already stood in another man's kitchen at midnight while he tells his wife the war is nearly over and he is keeping well, which is a lie, and that she shouldn't worry, which is impossible. You burn the paper afterward. The reading has already happened. You cannot unread a hundred fathers."

The fire cracked. Neither of us moved.

"There's a woman in the third bundle," he went on, quieter. "She writes to a schoolteacher in the occupied quarter every week. She describes her garden. She has never once mentioned the war except to say the cabbages did poorly. I have read eleven of her letters. I know her cabbages better than my own mother's roses." He looked at me, and there was something raw under the officer's steadiness, something that had been bleeding longer than the wound in his side. "And on Tuesday I am supposed to carry them all to the incinerator behind the quartermaster's store, and I find — Orderly — I find that I cannot."

"Why tell me this?" My voice came out lower than my disguise required, closer to its own weather. "You held my brother's ledger over us once. Those questions at the inspection—"

"I was doing my duty then," he said. "Badly, perhaps. Thoroughly, certainly." He exhaled. "But a strange thing happens when you spend three months reading other people's last words. You begin to notice who isn't on the manifests. Who gets recorded as *civilian male, unspecified* — as if the army had swept up the town and forgotten to count the people in it. I noticed a name on a casualty return that appeared nowhere else in any file I processed. Appeared twice, in fact. Under two spellings." His eyes rested on me, mild and terrible. "One of those spellings was on the evacuation lottery list. Berth twelve. I altered the manifest myself, you see. I know exactly how many Hallorans there are."

The room tilted very slightly. Across the room my mother's needles had gone silent altogether.

"I'm not threatening you," he said. "God knows. I'm telling you what I am, since you're wondering. I'm a man who was ordered to erase a town and instead learned it by heart. We are—" he gestured vaguely, at me, at the satchel, at the wall that hid nothing anymore, "—colleagues, I think. Of a disreputable profession."

"What profession is that?" I asked, though I knew.

"Keeping accounts," said Lieutenant Ashe, "that no one has commissioned us to keep."

* * *

Toward dawn his fever climbed, and for two hours I was simply a nurse again, and glad of it — glad of the plain honest work of cool cloths and measured water, work that asked nothing about who I was. Fever makes strangers of everyone equally. He spoke in fragments: a river with the wrong name, an order given twice, *burn them, sir, all of them, yes sir* — and once, clearly, terribly: *"I'll read them aloud, Mother, I promise, I'll read every one."*

When the fever broke, the winter light was coming up thin and blue through the shutters, and he was weak as a convalescent child, and something had shifted in the room's arrangement of trust. It does that, sickness. It levels the ground between the bed and the chair.

He asked for the satchel. I brought it.

He untied the first bundle with fingers that trembled and would not admit to trembling, and chose one letter, and held it out to me.

"Read it," he said. "Please. I want — if these are going to survive, someone besides me should know them while they're still paper. In case Tuesday goes wrong."

"Why me?"

"Because your brother writes down the names of the dead that no one else will write." He said it simply. "I assumed the household trade ran in the family."

I took the letter. The paper was soft from handling — he had handled them all, I understood, more than once, in the lamplit privacy of whatever room they'd given him, a young officer alone at night with a hundred strangers' voices. The hand inside was round and unhurried, a workingwoman's hand.

*Marta writes:* *Dear Alva — the frost took the last of the kale but I have saved seed and I will try again, as one does. Tell your pupils the story of the fox and the moon, the one I told you, so that somewhere it keeps being told. I am well. Do not believe anything else you hear.*

I read it aloud, as he asked. My voice — the false one, the orderly's flat instrument — cracked halfway through on *as one does*, and I let it crack, and did not repair it, and Ashe watched me with an expression I recognized because I had worn it in wards for two years: the look of a person witnessing on behalf of everyone who couldn't be present.

"There are ninety-three more," he said when I finished. "Some of them are just lists, you know. One woman wrote nothing but names — forty names, neighbors, with dates. No commentary. She understood the assignment better than the army did." He retied the bundle with exaggerated care, as though the twine were a tourniquet and the letters the limb. "If I refuse to burn them, I end my career at best. At worst—" he touched his bandage lightly, "—well. I've had a preview of the worst."

"Then why keep them?" I heard myself say. "Burn them and stay safe. Isn't that what you people do? Paper burns easier than people."

"Yes," he said, without offense, as if I'd stated a law of physics. "It is. I've done it eleven times — burnings, I mean. I was thorough." He looked into the stove. "But here is what I learned at your hospital, during the inspection. I stood behind your brother's desk for twenty minutes while he pretended to search for a file, and on the corner of the desk there was a tray of buttons. Odd buttons. Uniform buttons mostly, different regiments, and I thought: souvenir-taking, sentiment, nurses collect things. And then I saw that under each button was a slip of paper with a name on it, in a left-leaning hand, and I understood that Nurse Halloran had built a museum of the erased on his desktop and dared the world to notice." He turned back to me. "I noticed. And I said nothing. And I have been saying nothing ever since, loudly, in triplicate. This satchel is the first time I've managed otherwise."

Outside, the garrison bugle sounded reveille through the snow. Somewhere a cart groaned past, loaded — I thought of coats, supply carts, a dead soldier's greatcoat riding toward the depot with ninety names sewn against its lining. My whole family's survival hung on paper hidden in cloth, and here was a stranger with his life hung the same way, and between us on the blanket lay a hundred letters that only wanted what all of us wanted: to arrive.

"Marta," I said. "The cabbage woman. What happens to her letters if you burn them?"

"They stop existing," he said. "She'll keep writing. Her words will go into the fire weekly, forever, and eventually she'll die believing herself unanswered, and she will be right."

I looked at his side, at the neat stitches Rafael had set, and thought: *someone shot him for this already, or for something like it, and he came here.* To us. To the one house in the requisitioned town where keeping names was the family business.

"Tuesday," I said. "What do you need?"

He smiled — the first time, tired and crooked, taking years off his face. "I need the incinerator to have an accident. And I need these letters dispersed before then, quietly, to the addresses they were always meant for. There's a woman in the third bundle who deserves to learn her cabbages arrived." He drew out the bundle and set it in my hands, all of it, the whole weight of a hundred voices, lighter than I expected and heavier than anything I had carried. "And I need one more thing. The manifest. Three days remain before the correction comes down from division. I can strike one name honestly — a clerical mercy, one berth freed, no questions. But I have to know it goes to the right person. So I need to speak with your brother."

As if summoned, Rafael's step sounded on the stair.

"And Orderly," Ashe added, as the door opened, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle, "when he comes — you may as well stop performing. In this house, at four in the morning, over other people's letters—" his eyes moved over my face, cataloguing it, keeping it, "—there is no one left in this room to hide from."

Rafael stopped in the doorway, sleep-rumpled, taking in the scene: the open satchel, the bundles in my lap, the lieutenant upright and pale and resolved.

"Lieutenant," Rafael said carefully. "You wanted to discuss the manifest."

"I did," said Ashe. "But first — Nurse Halloran — I've brought you ninety-four letters, and a proposal, and I think your sister already knows what I'm going to ask."
