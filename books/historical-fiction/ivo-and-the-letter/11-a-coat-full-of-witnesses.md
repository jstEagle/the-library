## 11. A Coat Full of Witnesses

The cart left at dawn, and I did not see it go.

I learned of it the way one learns most disasters in a garrison town — sideways, in fragments, from a man who did not know he was carrying bad news. Private Osei, his arm bound in a sling of my own making, mentioned it while I changed his dressing: that the supply convoy had come through before light, that the quartermaster's men had cleared the dead-room of everything not nailed down, blankets, boots, greatcoats, and that the whole lot was bound north for the depot at Vellmark.

North. Away from the quay. Away from the ship. Away from Ivo and the single berth we had not yet found the courage to divide.

"Greatcoats," I said. My hands kept working the bandage. I had taught them long ago to continue when the rest of me failed. "All of them?"

"Every scrap of wool in the building, Sister. Orders." He said *orders* the way everyone said it now, a small flat stone set down between himself and responsibility. "There's a lad outside still loading. You could ask him, but he won't know more than the manifest."

I finished the knot, patted his shoulder, and walked out into the grey morning with my heart going like a fist against a locked door.

The coat was Corporal Anders Brandt's. He had died on the ninth of December, and I had written him into the ledger myself: *Brandt, Anders, corporal, 3rd company; carried a photograph of two girls in a garden; called for his mother in a voice like a boy's.* It had been Ivo's idea — her fierce, impossible idea — to sew the ledger into the lining of a dead man's coat, because no quartermaster inventories a dead man's pockets twice, because wool swallows paper the way earth swallows seed, because a coat bound for the depot would pass every checkpoint that would strip a nurse or a woman or a sister to the skin.

She had sewn it herself, by candlelight, her needle moving with the same patience she brought to tending the dying, and she had said what she always said when I doubted her: *Trust me, Rafael. I have been hiding in plain sight my whole life. I know how it's done.*

And now the coat — the ledger, twenty-two pages of names the army had decided never existed — was on a cart rolling north to Vellmark, where stores were sorted, burned, redistributed, lost.

* * *

The loader was a boy of perhaps seventeen with chilblains on his ears. He confirmed it in four words: "Vellmark depot, first light."

"Those coats," I said. "Some of them came from the dead-room. They should be marked. For the families."

He looked at me with the particular blankness of a soldier who has been told that families are someone else's department. "Manifest says field stores, Sister. Depot sorts 'em. Nothing personal goes north."

Nothing personal. As if a name stitched into a lining were not the most personal thing in the town.

I stood in the cold and watched the cart's tailboard disappear around the powder magazine, and I understood, with the clarity that comes only after the door has already shut, exactly what I would have to do.

* * *

Morphine in the garrison hospital was counted like sin. One grain per dose, recorded in the controlled-substances book, countersigned weekly by the medical officer. We had, by hoarding and small mercies and the deaths of men who no longer needed it, accumulated a reserve — eleven ampoules beyond the week's issue, kept for the nights when the wounded came in screaming and the official supply ran dry before the screaming did. Eleven ampoules meant perhaps seven men eased through their worst hours. It meant the difference between agony and sleep for boys whose legs had been taken by shell-fire.

It also, as I had learned two days earlier, had a price on the occupied quarter's black market that nothing else in the town could match.

Sergeant Danya Reyes kept the transport pool. She was a broad, weathered woman with a face like a closed ledger herself — appropriate, I always thought — and she controlled which carts went where and, more importantly, which drivers could be spoken to. She owed me nothing. Three months ago I had refused her morphine for her own suffering and reported her attempt to take it as theft, and I had not regretted it then, and I did not regret it now, because regret is a luxury and she had been trying to steal medicine from men worse off than she was.

But she loved horses, and two of her draught animals had gone lame in the frost, and I knew things about lameness that army farriers did not.

I found her in the stable behind the transport yard, wrapping one of the lame mare's legs in sacking, and I laid out my offer the way one lays out cards in a losing hand.

"A week of my morphine ration," I said. "Seven ampoules. And I'll show you how to blister and bind that leg so she's pulling again inside ten days instead of being destroyed."

Her hands went still on the mare's fetlock. "You reported me."

"I did."

"You'd bribe me now."

"Yes." I made myself say the word without flinching. It sat between us, ugly and honest. "Yes, Sergeant. I would. There is something on that Vellmark cart that I cannot lose, and I will pay anything I have to get it back."

"What thing?"

I had rehearsed a lie. I discovered I didn't want to spend it. "Names," I said. "A list of names the army confiscated. Men who died whose deaths were never reported."

She studied me for a long moment, and something moved behind her closed face — not warmth, exactly, but recognition. A woman who keeps transport ledgers knows what a record is worth. "If the depot finds contraband in field stores," she said slowly, "the whole lot gets burned. Paper especially. You understand that."

"I understand that."

"And you understand that if I help you, I'm not doing it for free out of gratitude, because I don't feel any."

"The morphine is payment. The leg is a separate kindness, or none at all. Your choice."

She unwound the sacking, looked at the swollen joint, and sighed like a woman surrendering a position. "Show me the blister treatment first. Then we'll talk about your cart."

* * *

We talked about it over the mare's bandaged leg, in low voices, while a groom curried the next stall and heard nothing but horse-talk. There were, Sergeant Reyes told me, three carts on the northern road: the powder wagon, which traveled fast and alone; the grain cart, which would halt at Mill Bridge for the ferry toll; and the stores cart — mine — driven by a conscript named Pell, who was slow, timid, and terrified of the depot's receiving sergeant, and who therefore stopped wherever stopping felt safer than arriving.

"He'll overnight at the burned mill outside Torv," she said. "Conscripts don't drive the mill road after dark if they can help it. Too many wrecked wagons on it, too many stories. He'll camp and go in at dawn."

"Then I have until dawn tomorrow."

"You have until dawn tomorrow," she agreed, "if you can keep up with him, which you can't on foot, and you can't ride, because nurses don't requisition horses and a nurse galloping north alone is a story that reaches the colonel by supper." She straightened, dusted straw from her knees, and looked at me with something that was almost, dangerously, sympathy. "But a laundry cart goes to the forward aid post at Torv every second day, and today is the second day. The laundress is a woman called Maret who owes the hospital for her son's fever. You could ride in the laundry. Under it, if it comes to checkpoints. Nobody searches laundry going toward the front — they search it coming back, looking for deserters."

"When does it leave?"

"Noon. And Sister—" she caught my sleeve as I turned, her grip hard, her voice lower still— "whatever's on that cart. If it's worth seven ampoules of morphine, it's worth more than your life, and the depot receiving sergeant hangs thieves, not loses them. Think about whether the names are worth a hanging."

"They're already hanged," I said. "Every man on that list. That's rather the point."

* * *

Noon came grey and windless, snow beginning in fine grains that hissed against the laundry cart's canvas. Maret was sixty, iron-spined, smelling of lye and smoke, and she asked me exactly one question — "Is this about your sister?" — because of course Ivo had mended her son's shirt without being asked, back in October, when asking permission was still a thing people did.

"It's about everyone's sisters," I said, and folded myself into the basket of linens, and we went north.

Riding under wet sheets through an occupied country does strange things to the mind. The world narrowed to white cloth and the creak of axles and slivers of sky. At the checkpoint before the river, boots stopped beside the cart, and a voice asked Maret her business, and she answered with such bored fluency — *aid post linens, third time this week, look if you like but you'll be folding them after* — that the soldier laughed and waved us through, and I lay among other people's washing and thought: this is what Ivo has done every day of her adult life. Turned herself into something too ordinary to examine. Made invisibility a craft.

I had hidden names in a book. She had hidden herself in a life. And I had let her do it, all those years, because it was easier than watching the world refuse her.

* * *

The burned mill at Torv was a skeleton against the darkening sky, its wheel long since collapsed into the millpond, its walls open to the weather like a ribcage. Pell had done exactly what Reyes predicted: camped in the lee of the south wall, cart backed up to the broken doorway, a small fire going, a rifle across his knees that he clearly hoped never to need.

Maret halted the laundry cart a quarter mile short, at a stand of birches, and would come no farther. "My son needs his mother sensible," she said. "Go carefully. And Rafael—" she pressed something into my hand, a heel of black bread wrapped in cloth— "eat something. Fainting in a snowfield helps no one."

I waited for full dark. I had learned patience in wards, waiting for fevers to break and hemorrhages to clot, but this was a different species of waiting, every minute a stone in the stomach. The snow thickened. The firelight at the mill shrank to a smudge. Somewhere north, the century was running out its last days, and here I was crouched in a birch wood preparing to rob a frightened boy of a coat.

Not rob, I corrected myself. Recover. Though I had seven ampoules of stolen-from-my-own-hands morphine in my pocket, and the difference between recovery and robbery was mostly a matter of who was telling it.

I approached the fire openly. In war zones, the openly walking figure is less alarming than the lurking one; soldiers shoot at shadows and question faces. Pell started up, grabbed his rifle, saw the nurse's grey cloak, and sagged back onto his crate with relief so naked it near broke my heart.

"Sister. God. I thought you were partisans." He was nineteen, maybe, with a farm boy's big knuckles and a city boy's unlined face. "What's wrong? Anyone hurt?"

"No one's hurt, Pell." I sat down across the fire from him, palms out, unhurried. "I've come about the cargo."

His face did the thing faces do when they are innocent and afraid of being otherwise. "The coats? They're just coats."

"Some of them came from the dead-room at the garrison hospital. Families will want to know where their men's things went. I need to look through them before the depot does. One coat in particular."

"Look through—" He glanced north, as if the depot's receiving sergeant might materialize out of the snow to catch him agreeing to something. "Sister, there's a manifest. Sergeant Hoss counts everything. If a coat's missing off the manifest, it's my pay, my rations, maybe my back. I can't—"

I set the seven ampoules on the crate between us. Firelight turned the glass to little amber jewels. His eyes went to them and stayed, and I understood then that someone at home was in pain, or he was, or both, and that the army's medicine reached depots even more slowly than it reached wards.

"That's a month of quiet evenings," I said. "For ten minutes with the coats, and your silence after."

"Which coat?" he whispered, as though the manifest itself might hear.

"Corporal Brandt's. Grey wool, 3rd company button, a repair on the left elbow."

He knew it. Of course he knew it — conscripts memorize their cargo the way sailors memorize weather, looking for the flaw that will sink them. "Third bale from the tailboard," he said miserably. "Under the blanket rolls. Sister, whatever you take out — put it back exactly. Exactly."

I promised. I would break that promise within the hour, and I have never regretted it, and I think that says something true about promises.

* * *

The coat was where he said, cold as the night itself, smelling of another man's sweat and the straw of the cart. I carried it away from the fire, behind the mill's broken wall, and worked by feel and by the occasional mercy of cloud-shredded moonlight. My fingers found the seam along the left lining panel, the slight ridge of thread that was not factory thread, the small hardness beneath — and my breath went out of me all at once, twenty-two pages' worth of held breath.

Then I felt the other thing.

The seam on the right panel was cut. Not unpicked — cut, raggedly, recently, the lining gaping. I pushed my fingers inside and found paper. Not my ledger. Letters — a bundle of them, tied with string, perhaps thirty, perhaps forty, addressed in half the handwritings of the town to half the addresses of the world. Confiscated letters. Someone had hidden letters in this same coat, in the same way, and then cut them free — or begun to — and left them.

And the ledger's own seam—

I turned the coat to the moon. The left panel had been slit too. Carefully, expertly, and resewn with different thread. Someone had opened the lining, taken the ledger out, looked at it — or read it — and sewn it back.

My knees nearly went. I crouched in the snow behind a ruined mill with my brother's-and-sister's names in my lap and understood that I was not the only keeper. Someone had found the ledger, known precisely what it was, and chosen silence. Chosen to send it onward, hidden still, rather than report it or burn it.

Pell? No — Pell was terrified of manifests, not fluent in seams. Reyes? She had never touched the coat. Which left whoever had loaded the dead-room at first light, or someone at the hospital itself, someone with needle skills and midnight access and the same terrible arithmetic I carried.

I stood in the snow with the coat over my arm and felt less alone than I had since the confiscation order came, and more afraid, because a second keeper means a second person who can be caught.

I put the coat back in its bale, exactly, as promised. I put the bundle of letters inside my cloak, against my ribs, because some things you do not leave in straw. I gave Pell his ampoules and told him the coats were exactly as I'd found them, which was now a lie wearing a truth's clothes, and he took the morphine and did not meet my eyes, and we were both of us thieves that night, and both of us keeping faith with someone.

Maret was asleep under the canvas when I got back to the birches, but she woke at my step and asked nothing, only handed me the bread I'd forgotten to eat and turned the cart south.

Halfway home, with Torv's darkness behind us, she spoke into the snow-hush without turning around. "You found it, then."

"Yes."

"And it was still there."

"It was still there."

She nodded, as if that settled some wager she'd been running with the century itself, and drove on, and I rode the last miles with forty unsent letters against my heart and one question beating time with the wheels:

Who else had read the names — and why had they sewn them back?

By the time the garrison's chimneys rose out of the dawn-grey ahead of us, I had stopped asking *who*. There was only one person in that hospital who could stitch a seam so that a quartermaster's eye would never find it. Only one person who had argued, a week ago, for the coat in the first place — and who had since gone very quiet whenever the ledger was mentioned, quiet the way people are quiet when they are carrying something heavier than they have admitted.

The laundry cart rolled through the gate as the sun came up red on the snow.

Ivo was waiting at the hospital steps, dressed for work, face pale, eyes finding mine over the heads of the morning detail — and the first thing my sister said, before good morning, before hello, was:

"Don't be angry. I have to show you what I did to the coat."
