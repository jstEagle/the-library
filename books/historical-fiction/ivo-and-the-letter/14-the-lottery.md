## 14. The Lottery

The list went up on the customs house door at nine o'clock in the morning, and by half past the square was a field of backs — hundreds of them, shoulders hunched against the wind off the water, every face turned toward a single sheet of paper nailed to the wood at eye level. The paper was not large. It was the size of a ration card, and it held the names of everyone in the garrison town who would be leaving on the *Meridian* before the century turned.

Ivo stood at the edge of the crowd with her cap pulled low and her hands in her coat pockets, and she did not move toward the list for a long time. She had learned this discipline years ago, in another town under another flag: never be first to anything, never be last, never be the one who pushes or the one who lingers. Be the man in the middle of the crowd, unremarkable, forgettable. She let the miller's sons go ahead of her. She let the widow Brandt go, and the two schoolteachers, and the boy from the bakery who had lost two fingers to frostbite and wept openly when he found his name.

Only when the press of bodies thinned did she drift forward, sideways, the way a man drifts toward a shop window he has no intention of entering.

The clerk's hand was small and precise. The names ran down the page in columns, sorted by district, then by household, then — where the army's arithmetic required it — by rank and lottery number. Ivo read the way she had been taught to read documents that might kill her: quickly, completely, without letting her eyes betray interest.

She found her mother's name in the third column. *Margrethe Iversen — berth 41 — deck class.* She felt her chest loosen and then immediately tighten again, because a berth was not safety, a berth was only a place to stand on a ship, and ships sank, and ports closed, and the world beyond the water was only a rumor. But her mother's name was there. That was something. That was more than they'd had yesterday.

Then she found Rafael's name.

*Rafael Iversen — berth 17 — medical staff, priority.*

And beneath it, three lines down, in the same small precise hand:

*Ivo Iversen — berth 17 — duplicate entry — see correction notice.*

Ivo read it twice. Three times. The wind came off the harbor and lifted the corner of the page and slapped it back against the door, and still the words did not change. Two names. One berth. A note in the margin, faint as an apology: *clerical error pending resolution.*

She looked up. Across the square, past the customs house, the masts of the *Meridian* rose above the warehouse roofs like winter trees, bare and black against a sky the color of dishwater. Three days until New Year's Eve. Three days until embarkation. Three days until some clerk in some office noticed what the whole square could see if they cared to look — that one berth had been promised twice, and that one of the two names attached to it belonged to a person who did not, strictly speaking, exist.

Because that was the thing about the name *Ivo Iversen*. It was real now. It had papers, a service record, a pay book, a place on a hospital roster. It had eleven years of being spoken aloud in barracks and wards and queues. But underneath it, older than all of that, was the girl who had cut her hair behind the chapel the week their father died, and the girl did not appear on any manifest the army kept. If anyone pulled the thread — one letter of one name, one date of birth checked against a parish register — the whole garment would come apart, and Rafael's name would come apart with it, because siblings were recorded together, always, everywhere, in every ledger the world had ever kept.

*Duplicate entry,* said the margin, in its faint apologetic hand.

Ivo thought: *they have no idea how right they are.*

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the quay, because the long way was emptier and she needed empty. The ice had crept further out overnight; the harbor pilots were saying the *Meridian* would need an escort cutter to break a channel, and the cutter was coming from the south, and the south was slow. Everything was slow now except the danger, which moved at the speed of paperwork.

At the mouth of Fish Lane she passed the requisition officer's men chalking numbers on doors — they had reached the lane at last, working street by street, cataloguing the town down to its hinges. A soldier stood outside the widow Brandt's house writing in a ledger of his own, and Ivo thought: *everyone keeps ledgers now. The whole war is a contest of who writes faster.*

Rafael was home before her, which meant Rafael had run, which meant Rafael had seen the list and understood it instantly, because Rafael understood documents the way other people understood weather. Their mother sat at the kitchen table with her hands folded, very still, in the way she became still when she was frightened — not the stillness of calm but the stillness of a woman holding a full cup while the floor tilts.

"You saw," Rafael said. Not a question.

"I saw."

"Seventeen." Rafael laughed, once, without any humor in it at all. "Of all the berths on that ship, they gave us the same one. Seventeen. As if the universe itself is keeping a ledger and has decided to balance its accounts."

"Or as if a tired clerk copied from two different lists and his tea went cold." Ivo hung her coat on the peg by the door, deliberately slowly, deliberately ordinarily. "Errors happen, Rafi. Errors get corrected."

"That's what frightens me." Rafael stood at the window with his back to the room, and even through the disguise of the nurse's plainness — the flat voice, the squared shoulders — Ivo could see the shape of the thought moving through him. "The correction won't be a debate. It'll be a pen stroke. They'll strike one name and keep the other, and they'll keep mine, because medical staff are priority and orderlies are—" He stopped.

"Are nothing," Ivo finished for him. "Orderlies are furniture."

"I didn't say—"

"You didn't have to." She sat down across from their mother. Up close, their mother's stillness resolved into details: the whitened knuckles, the prayer book she wasn't reading, the way her eyes went from one child to the other and back, doing arithmetic she refused to speak aloud. "So we have three days to decide which name gets struck. Us, or them."

"There's no decision," Rafael said too quickly. "It's obvious."

"It's never obvious. That's the trap of it." Ivo laid her hands flat on the table, palms down, the way she'd seen officers do when they wanted a room to quiet. "Listen to me. My name is the fragile one. One inquiry, one records check, and I'm not just off the ship — I'm arrested, and you're interrogated, and Mother loses both children in the same afternoon. Whereas your name is iron. Priority, medical staff, clean papers. If one of us leaves, it should be—"

"No." Rafael turned from the window. His face had gone the color of the ice in the harbor. "No. You don't get to do this. You don't get to stand in my kitchen and volunteer yourself for erasure like it's arithmetic. Like you're a line item."

"It *is* arithmetic—"

"It is *not*—" And then Rafael stopped himself, jaw tight, because their mother had raised her hand — not to strike, just to still the air, the old gesture from childhood, from the years of short rations and long silences.

Their mother spoke quietly. "Your father used to say that a family is a boat. Everyone bails, or everyone drowns. He never said anything about what happens when the boat itself must be divided." She looked down at her folded hands. "Three days. We will not spend them shouting. We will spend them thinking. All of us. Separately, if we must."

But Ivo saw her mother's eyes flicker — just once, just briefly — toward Rafael, and away. And Ivo had spent eleven years learning to read the things people did not write down, and she knew what that flicker said. It said: *not my son. Anyone but my son.* And beneath it, guiltier, quieter: *and God forgive me, I know which child I mean.*

* * *

That night Ivo could not sleep, and so she did what she always did with sleeplessness: she worked. She went down to the ward for the night shift, though she wasn't rostered, and moved among the beds with the lamp turned low, refilling water glasses, straightening blankets, listening to the breathing of forty men and sorting it in her head into the categories that mattered — easy, labored, wet, absent.

Private Lund was awake. He was sixteen and had a hip wound that was healing clean, and he lay staring at the ceiling with the particular brightness of a boy who has just remembered he wants to live.

"They posted the list," he whispered when she came near. "My name's on it. Berth sixty-two. Did you see? Did you see my name?"

"I saw it," Ivo said, which was true; she had seen every name on that list, twice, and carried most of them in her head now alongside the ones from Rafael's ledger, the two lists braided together like rope.

"My mum doesn't know yet. There's no time to wire her. Do you think—" He stopped, embarrassed. "Do you think the ship will actually come? They're saying the ice—"

"The ice will break," Ivo said. "Ships have come through worse than this. Men have come through worse than you." She adjusted his blanket, tucking it the way their mother used to tuck hers, before everything. "Sleep, Lund. You'll want your strength for the gangway. It's steep, and everyone's pushing."

He grinned and closed his eyes, and within minutes his breathing joined the easy category, and Ivo stood a moment longer in the dark between the rows of beds, listening to the ward breathe, and thought about what it was to be a name on a list. Lund's name meant a mother at a dock somewhere, scanning a crowd. Her mother's name meant a daughter — one daughter, the visible one — watching her go. And her own name, *Ivo Iversen*, berth seventeen, duplicate entry, meant nothing to anyone in the world except the woman asleep in the nurses' quarters down the hall, who had spent eleven years helping her brother wear it.

A name was such a small thing to hang a life on. A few letters, a clerk's pen, a margin note. And yet the whole machinery of the century — the ships, the borders, the registers, the wars — turned on those letters, ground people fine between them, and called the grinding *administration*.

She thought about the ledger hidden in the greatcoat, sewn into the lining by her own hands, riding somewhere in the occupied quarter on a cart full of dead men's belongings. Somewhere out there, dozens of names slept in cloth, waiting. Some of them were on the customs house list. Most were not. The list on the door decided who left. The ledger decided who was *known* to have existed. And Ivo was beginning to understand, standing in the dark with the lamp turned low, that these were not the same thing at all — that a person could board a ship and still be erased, and stay on shore and still, somehow, remain.

* * *

On the second morning, the correction notice appeared.

It was smaller than the original list, a strip of paper pasted beside it, and the crowd around it was quieter this time — quieter the way a room goes quiet when the doctor comes in. Ivo did not go near it. She sent a baker's boy she trusted with a coin, and he came back breathless and recited it: *"Re: berth seventeen. Duplicate assignment due to clerical error. Corrected manifest to be posted December thirty-first, noon. Households affected will present themselves for verification."*

Verification.

The word sat in Ivo's stomach like a swallowed stone all the way home. Verification meant papers on a table. Papers meant dates of birth, parish entries, the whole paper trail of a life — and her life's paper trail ended, abruptly, in a chapel yard eleven winters ago, where a girl had taken scissors to her hair and buried her own name along with her father.

Rafael met her at the door, and she knew from his face that he'd heard.

"Present themselves for verification," he said hollowly. "They make it sound like a formality. Like showing a ration card."

"For you it is a formality."

"And for you it's a gallows with a queue." He shut the door behind her and stood with his hand on it, as if the door were the last honest thing in the town. "Three days. Noon on the thirty-first. That's when they correct it *for* us, Rafi — do you understand? We don't even get to choose anymore unless we choose first. Every hour we wait, the pen moves closer to the paper."

"Then we choose tonight," Ivo said. "All three of us. At the table. No walking out, no silence, no sacrificing ourselves heroically in the doorway like martyrs in a bad play." She pulled off her cap and ran a hand through her cropped hair, a gesture she allowed herself only indoors, only here. "Mother's right about one thing. This family has spent eleven years not saying things. We can't afford it anymore. Not with three days left."

Rafael looked at her for a long moment. In the gray light from the window his face was young and old at once, twenty-six years carrying forty of them, and Ivo saw that beneath the fear there was something else gathering — resolve, or grief, or both wearing each other's coats.

"There's something else," Rafael said quietly. "Something I haven't told you. About the lieutenant. About what he asked me, after the inspection. I think — Ivo, I think there may be another way. A worse way, maybe, or a better one. I don't know yet."

Outside, far off across the frozen harbor, a bell buoy clanged once against the ice and fell silent, like a word begun and abandoned.

"But first," Rafael said, "we choose tonight."
