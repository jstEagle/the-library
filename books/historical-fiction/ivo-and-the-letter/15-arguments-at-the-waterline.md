## 15. Arguments at the Waterline

The harbor had frozen in layers, like a thing growing in secret, and by midnight the quay was a shelf of black ice glazed with the town's breath. Bonfires burned along the mole — the army's, for once, lit for warmth rather than destruction — and their light lay on the ice in long orange tongues that never quite reached where we stood.

We had come down to argue, and we argued the way we had done everything else that winter: quietly, because loudness invited ears.

Mother walked ahead of us with her shawl drawn over her head so that she looked from behind like a girl, like someone's daughter instead of our mother, and I hated her a little for it. Hate is what fear calls itself when it wants to feel noble. Ivo walked beside me with her collar up, her hands jammed in the pockets of the greatcoat she had not yet surrendered to the supply carts, and I could feel her thinking — I have always been able to feel Ivo thinking, a kind of weather off the sea.

The *Vestland* rode at anchor beyond the ice field, black against the black water, one row of portholes lit. One ship. Two hundred and sixty berths. Four thousand people in the requisitioned quarter who had heard the same arithmetic we had.

"Two names," Mother said without turning. She said it the way she said everything now, flat as slate. "One berth. And of all the clerks in this garrison, God gave us the one honest mistake of his career."

"It wasn't a mistake," Ivo said. "It was an accident. There's a difference. A mistake can be corrected. An accident is just fate being clumsy."

"Then fate has until Thursday."

* * *

We found shelter from the wind in the lee of a fish shed whose door had been chalked with a number — 114, stores, priority two — and there, between the wall and a stack of empty crates, with the bonfire throwing shadows through the cracks, we began the argument properly. Though *began* is wrong. It had begun three days ago when the list went up, and perhaps it had truly begun years before that, in a smaller house, in a quieter winter, when Ivo first vanished into a boy's name and I stayed visible enough to be counted.

"I'll stay," Mother said.

"No," I said.

"Rafael—"

"No." I heard how it came out of me, flat and final, a door closing. "You are fifty-nine years old. You have a cough you've hidden from me since October because you knew I'd put it in my ledger under your alias and then you'd exist twice, which you apparently consider extravagant. You are not staying in a garrison town through a siege winter so that your children can sail away comfortable."

"My children will not sail away at all if one of them doesn't take that berth, which brings me to Ivo—"

"Ivo goes," I said.

"You don't get to decide that," Mother said. "That is precisely the sin of this family, Rafael, that everyone decides for everyone else out of love and calls it sacrifice afterward. Your father decided. He decided to keep the farm and send us nothing. You decided to stay invisible at that hospital writing your little book of the dead while Ivo decided to stop being a woman so the war wouldn't notice her. And now you decide she sails. Who asked her?"

The fire cracked somewhere down the mole. A sentry coughed. In the pause I turned to Ivo, expecting — wanting, maybe, in some shameful corner — for her to say it herself: *I go.* Because Ivo going meant the ledger's most dangerous entry removed from the town, meant my own conscience amputated cleanly at the wrist.

Ivo looked at the ice for a long time. Then she looked at me with an expression I had not seen since we were children hiding in the hayloft from Father's temper — an expression of complicity, almost tenderness, the look of someone about to lie to you for your own good.

"I'll tell you what I want," Ivo said mildly. "I want to hear Mother say why she stays. Not why she must. Why she *stays*. There's a difference and we're all of us fluent in it."

Mother's jaw worked. The shadow of the crates fell across her face in bars.

"Because," she said finally, "there are forty-three people in this quarter who know me as the woman who carries soup to the houses the soldiers won't enter. Mrs. Brandt's boy has fever and no mother. The old Jew on Tanner's Lane — don't look shocked, Rafael, you wrote him into your book yourself, I've seen the page — he won't survive evacuation and he knows it, and someone has to sit with him at the end so he doesn't die inventoried. Numbered. Someone has to be unnumbered here. That is why I stay. Not because I'm braver than either of you. Because I'm the only one of us they've never once suspected of being anyone else."

It was the longest speech she had made since the requisition, and the wind seemed to hold itself quiet to let it land.

"No," I said again, but softer, because softness was all I had left against it.

"Yes," she said. "And here is my counteroffer, since you're so fond of deciding: I stay, Ivo takes the berth, and you, Rafael — you take the second problem."

"There is no second problem. There is no berth for me."

"There is whatever the lieutenant offers," Mother said, and watched me the way she used to watch me over mending, knowing already where the tear was. "Don't think I haven't seen it. This officer who lingers over your handwriting. This offer you mentioned last night and then swallowed. Whatever price he's named, you mean to pay it alone and tell us after. So. Decide tonight, decide in front of witnesses. What does he want, Rafael? What is the book worth?"

* * *

I told them. All of it, there in the fish shed with the firelight striping the walls: the lieutenant's wound, the letters he carried and had not burned, and the bargain he had set out in the hospital corridor in a voice barely above breath — the manifest altered, any name struck clean, any name added, if I surrendered the ledger openly. Honestly, he had said. He wanted to save one person honestly rather than everyone corruptly, and he wanted it done before the century turned, because he was young enough to believe a new century might begin with a clean act.

Ivo listened without moving. When I finished, she was quiet a moment, and then she laughed — a small, terrible laugh with no amusement anywhere in it.

"He wants the book," she said. "He doesn't want you. Do you understand what happens to a witness who hands over the evidence? You become a name in someone else's file. You become — Rafael, you become one of your own entries. Written down by another hand and filed."

"He gave his word the names would be read."

"They gave word at the inspection too. Word is paper, and paper is confiscated."

"And what would you have me do?" My voice cracked on it, and the cold took the sound and scattered it. "Keep the book forever? Bury it under a floorboard until the floorboards rot? Ivo, I have four hundred and eleven names in my memory and four hundred and eleven buttons and photographs and letters in a dead man's coat, and every day I carry them I am one search away from erasing all of them at once. He is offering the only version of survival this book has. Read aloud. Copied. Scattered into other memories. Isn't that — isn't that the entire point? That remembering isn't keeping? That it's giving away?"

I hadn't known I believed it until I said it. Saying things, my whole life, has been how I find out what I think, and I stood there in the fish shed newly convinced, waiting for them to strike it down.

Mother struck it down first, but gently, which was worse.

"That's a beautiful speech," she said, "and you rehearsed it, which means you've already decided, which means this argument is theater. So let me play my role. If you give him the book, you give him Ivo. Every alias. Every page where my child appears as a man named Ivor Halloran, orderly, third company. You hand my son to the army with your own hands, and no promise about reading names aloud covers that page."

Silence. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"She wouldn't be here," I said weakly. "She'd be on the water."

"Papers are checked at landfall. Descriptions travel faster than ships."

I looked at Ivo to deny it, and Ivo did not deny it, and that non-denial told me everything, because Ivo had known — had known since the lieutenant first made the offer, had weighed it in those quick clever hands and said nothing, the way Ivo always said nothing, invisibility perfected even among family.

"You were going to let me do it," I whispered. "You were going to stand at the gangway and watch me hand them your name."

"I was going to let you save four hundred other names," Ivo said. "Mine included. It seemed a fair exchange rate." A breath. "But Mother's right, and I've thought of something better anyway. I've been thinking of it all evening while you two performed."

* * *

What Ivo had thought of, Ivo would not say outright — Ivo never says anything outright; Ivo arranges things and lets you discover the arrangement like furniture moved in the dark. But I saw my sibling pull a folded paper from inside the greatcoat, glance at it by firelight, and fold it away again, and I saw that the handwriting on it was not Ivo's usual careful male hand but something looser, older, learned from our mother at a kitchen table twenty years ago.

"What is that?" I asked.

"A letter of resignation," Ivo said, "from the post of Ivo Halloran."

And before either of us could reach for it, my sibling stepped out of the lee of the shed onto the open ice and was gone toward the quay stairs, quick and silent, a figure the sentries' firelight touched and dismissed, as it always had.

Mother and I stood in the cold and looked at each other across four feet of frozen air.

"Follow," she said.

"Not yet." I caught her arm. "Mother. If I give him the book, I'll copy it first. Every name, twice, in two hands. The original goes to the lieutenant with the pages concerning Ivo cut out and burned in front of him — he'll accept that, he's decent, decency is exploitable — and the copies go into the world however the world allows. Nobody trades Ivo. Nobody trades you. I trade only myself, which is the one currency I actually own."

"And what does trading yourself buy? One berth. Which Ivo refuses, I'd wager."

"Which Ivo—" I stopped. The ice shifted somewhere beneath us, a groan like a struck bell, and far out on the dark water the buoy clanged again, patient, idiotic, faithful.

"Rafael," Mother said, and took my face in both her chapped hands, the way she had not since I was small, "listen to me. I have buried a husband and given away a daughter and gotten back a stranger I love anyway, and I have watched you carry the dead of this town in a cloth notebook like a man carrying water in his cupped hands, losing it faster than you can walk. I am staying. That is settled, it was settled before we left the house, and you will not insult me by reopening it. The only question left is which of my children I put on that ship, and I have chosen, and my choice is leaving this quay in that direction—"

"—and mine," said a voice from the top of the quay stairs, "is already booked."

We turned. Ivo stood at the head of the steps with the lantern from the harbor office lit in one hand, and behind my sibling, emerging from the office door with his cap in his hands and the sheepish posture of a clerk caught mid-favor, stood young Søren, the manifest clerk, the very man whose honest accident had started all this.

"I traded," Ivo called down cheerfully. "While you two were rehearsing speeches. My berth for a signature — Søren's cousin's discharge papers needed a witness, and I witnessed, and now the manifest reads one berth, one name, and the name is being changed as we speak. To whom, Søren?"

The clerk lifted his cap higher, revealing a bald patch and pure misery.

"To a Nurse R. Halloran," he said. "Effective tonight. I'm sorry, ma'am. The lady was very persuasive."

* * *

The argument that followed lasted until nearly four in the morning, and I will not record it all here because some things even a keeper of names must leave unwritten. We wore grooves in the ice going in circles: Mother declaring she would simply refuse the shore-leave pass and vanish into Tanner's Lane; I pointing out that refusal meant arrest and arrest meant papers, and papers meant questions, and questions were the one thing this family could not afford; Ivo standing apart with the lantern held high like a lighthouse operator with opinions, contributing only, at intervals, devastatingly, "The manifest is signed."

Love, in our family, has always expressed itself as stubbornness. We are not a household of embraces. We are a household of refusals — I refuse to leave you, I refuse to let you refuse, I refuse your refusal — each one a stone laid carefully in a wall against the dark, and by dawn the wall we had built on that frozen quay was this:

Ivo had signed away the berth and could not un-sign it; the clerk, terrified and honest, would not forge it back. Mother would stay, and no power on the ice could move her, and I had extracted from her one concession only: that when Tanner's Lane ended its work she would present herself at the hospital, where I could at least guard her with proximity. And I — I stood at the rail of the dark quay holding a berth I had not asked for, a ledger I could not safely keep and could not bear to burn, and a bargain half-made with a wounded lieutenant whose decency I was about to test in ways he had not imagined.

The east was graying. The bonfires guttered low. Somewhere beyond the mole the *Vestland* rang four bells, and the sound crossed the ice thin as wire.

"Three days," Mother said. "Then they correct the lists, or the ship sails, or the century turns whether we've managed it or not. Go home and sleep, both of you. Even martyrs sleep."

Ivo walked ahead of us up from the water, lantern swinging, and I watched my sibling's shadow stretch and shrink across the snow and thought: *Ivo arranged all of this before we ever reached the quay. Ivo decided hours ago and let us argue with a closed door.*

And it was there, halfway up the hill with the cold in my bones and my sister-brother-sibling silhouetted against the graying sky, that I understood what I would have to do — not the surrender the lieutenant had asked for, but something stranger, something that would need his help and his honesty and would cost him more than he knew he was offering.

I would need to find Lieutenant Ash before morning muster.

Because I was not going to give him the ledger.

I was going to ask him to steal it.
