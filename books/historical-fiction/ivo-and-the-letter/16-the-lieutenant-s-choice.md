## 16. The Lieutenant's Choice

He came back on the second-to-last day, limping worse than before and carrying a leather satchel that he held against his chest the way a man holds a wounded bird.

Ivo saw him from the dispensary window, crossing the yard between drifts of ash where the requisition office had been burning its surplus all week — forms, manifests, the paper carcass of an army's memory. The lieutenant walked through that falling grey with his collar up and his cap pulled low, and she thought: *he looks like a man coming to confess.*

She met him at the door because Rafael was at the quay arguing with a harbourmaster, and Mother was inside packing what could not be packed, which was nothing.

"Lieutenant Voss." She kept her voice level, orderly to officer, though they had long since stopped pretending that was what they were to each other. "You shouldn't be walking. The wound in your side—"

"Is closed. It itches. That's how I know it's healing." He looked past her into the corridor, the empty ward with its stripped beds. "Is your brother here?"

"No."

"Then I'll wait." He did not sit. He stood in the doorway with snow melting off his shoulders and dripping onto the stones, and after a moment he said, quietly, "I've been ordered to burn them."

Ivo closed the door behind him. "The letters."

"The letters." He lifted the satchel slightly, as if presenting evidence at a trial only he would attend. "Forty-one of them. Confiscated under the records order. Private correspondence, most of it — wives, mothers, a man who wrote to a woman who turned out to be another man's wife, which is apparently why his letter was flagged. None of it matters. None of it is intelligence. You know that. I know that. But the order says burn, so every clerk in this garrison has been feeding stoves for a week like it's a sacrament." His mouth twisted. "I couldn't do mine."

"You kept them," Ivo said. It wasn't a question. She had known since the night she'd pulled the shrapnel out of him and found the satchel beneath his coat, heavier than any dispatch case had a right to be. She had said nothing then. Neither had he.

"I kept them." He finally sat, on the nearest bench, as if the admission had taken the last of his legs. "And now I'm going to ask you for something, Miss Halloran — or whatever name you're carrying this month; don't look at me like that, I've read enough papers in this town to be fluent in fiction — and I want you to hear all of it before you answer."

* * *

She made tea because there was nothing else to make, and because her hands wanted occupation while her mind ran ahead of her like a horse that smelled fire. Outside, the garrison was dismantling itself by daylight. Wagons groaned down toward the water. A squad of engineers was pulling copper wire off the chapel wall, and the sound of it carried across the yard — a thin metallic keening, like something being unspooled from the world.

Voss wrapped both hands around the tin cup and did not drink.

"There are two manifests," he said. "The one the public sees, posted on the customs house, and the one my colonel signs tonight and seals until embarkation. Berth allocations, final. Families split, priorities assigned, the usual arithmetic of abandonment." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "The error with your family's names — two listed where one berth exists — will be corrected tomorrow morning when the sealed copy is opened. Corrected by whoever opens it. It could be corrected generously or it could be corrected with a red line through both names, depending on the mood of the man holding the pen."

"And you hold the pen," Ivo said.

"I hold *a* pen. I'm junior. I'm wounded, which makes me sympathetic, and I'm honest, which makes me useless, and between those two things I have been given the manifest duty because no one senior wants to spend New Year's Eve doing sums." He set the cup down. "I can strike any single name from that manifest and substitute another. One. The paperwork allows it — compassionate substitution, it's called, God help us — provided the substitution is documented and the officer making it signs his name beside it. Signs his own true name, knowing what he signed."

Ivo stood very still. Through the window she could see the wire crew working, the smoke rising off the burning office, the frozen road to the mill bridge where a clerk had once read her papers aloud and paused, for one endless breath, over a single wrong letter.

"You can put Rafael on that ship," she said.

"I can put anyone on that ship. Your brother. Your mother. You." He looked up at her, and his eyes were the flat pale grey of the sea beyond the breakwater. "That's not the price. Here is the price." He drew a breath. "The ledger."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Ivo heard it falling the whole way down.

"You know about—"

"I know about the ledger, yes. I've known since the inspection, when your brother's hand shook for exactly one second over a casualty list written in a hand too careful to be casual about anything. I know your family keeps names the army omits. I know the greatcoat that went out on the supply cart last week was carrying more than buttons and a dead man's smell, because I signed the requisition for that cart and I watched your sister—" he caught himself, corrected without blinking, "—watched *you* follow it halfway across the occupied quarter before you lost it at the depot gate." He spread his hands. "I am not clever, Miss Halloran. I am simply a man who reads things, which in this town amounts to the same crime."

"So you'll trade." Her voice came out colder than she intended. "One berth for one book. And when you have the book — what? You burn it? File it? Read it aloud to the colonel as proof that a nurse in this garrison spent a war committing treason with a pencil?"

"I want him to surrender it openly." Voss said it steadily, as if he had rehearsed it on the walk over, and perhaps he had; she saw now the exhaustion under his composure, the way a man looks who has argued with himself for days and finally lost to the better argument. "Not smuggled. Not hidden in a coat or a floorboard or a dying man's pocket. Handed to an officer, in daylight, with witnesses. Surrendered under the confiscation order, which means it becomes army property, which means it goes into the record, which means—" he leaned forward, and now the weariness cracked and something hotter showed underneath, "—every name in it enters the official history of this garrison. Not as rumour. Not as a family's private grief. As *fact*, attested by a commissioned officer who will state, on the record, that the names in that book were omitted from prior reports and are hereby restored."

Ivo sat down slowly on the bench across from him.

She had expected many bargains in these weeks. She had imagined soldiers demanding the ledger at gunpoint, clerks discovering it by accident, informers selling it for passage money. She had never imagined this: a young man offering to make her family's secret *true* rather than merely safe.

"You'd ruin yourself," she said.

"I'd sign my name next to forty-one unsent letters' worth of negligence and however many hundred names are in that ledger of omitted deaths. Yes." He almost smiled. "There would be an inquiry. There might be a trial. My career would end at approximately the age it began." He picked up the tea and drank it this time, cold as it was. "My father was a schoolteacher in Drammen. Forty years, one schoolroom. When he died, the town knew the name of every child he had ever taught, because he had kept a register his whole life — attendance, marks, little notes in the margin. *This one whistles beautifully. This one lies but weeps when others suffer.* When he died, those registers were read at his funeral. All of them. Six hours. People came from three counties." He set the cup down again. "I have spent this entire war learning that the army believes a man exists only if his name appears twice — once on a muster roll and once on a report. Your brother believes a man exists if someone remembers him. Only one of those beliefs survives contact with the century that's coming."

"That's very fine," Ivo said, and hated the tremor in her voice. "But you're asking him to choose between his mother leaving safely and every name in that book becoming ammunition. Because that's what it is, isn't it? Once it's official, it's evidence. Evidence against the officers who wrote the false reports. They will not thank us. They will come looking for everyone who ever touched that book."

"Yes," Voss said simply. "They may. I've considered it. I've considered it every night for a week, lying in the infirmary listening to the stoves eat paper." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked nineteen instead of twenty-five. "Do you know what I did before the inspection? I burned eleven letters. My first assignment. Orders were orders, and I fed them to the stove, and one of them was from a girl to her brother and had nothing in it but weather. I remember a line of it still. *The apples froze on the tree and Father says leave them for the birds.*" His jaw worked. "I have carried that sentence through two inspections and one surgery. So no, Miss Halloran, I don't want to save everyone corruptly. I tried that arithmetic — strike a name quietly, falsify a line neatly, save one person with a lie and sleep badly forever. I want to save *one person honestly.* One signature. One truth on the record, even if it costs me everything, because it's the only kind of saving left in this town that doesn't rot the saver."

* * *

Rafael came back at dusk, smelling of pitch and harbour ice, and Ivo told him in the pantry with the door shut, in as few words as possible, because she had learned that long explanations gave people time to build refusals.

She watched her brother's face go through it — the offer landing, the pen, the signature, the open surrender — and she watched something move behind Rafael's eyes that she hadn't seen since they were children, some door swinging on an old hinge.

"Openly," Rafael said.

"He insists. It's the whole point of it, for him."

"It would end him." Rafael's fingers found the counter edge and gripped it. "An inquiry. Possibly a tribunal. For us. To put one of us on a boat."

"It would put the names in the record," Ivo said. "Forever. No one could burn them afterward without burning their own history. You've said it yourself a hundred times — the ledger only lives if someone else carries it. He's offering to carry it into the middle of the army's own archive. With a salute on top."

"And if the colonel reads it and decides the tidy solution is a fire and a ditch and three fewer Hallorans?" Rafael turned, and in the pantry's single candle their face was half shadow, half light, and Ivo thought suddenly how tired Rafael looked — not old, but worn smooth, the way stone wears when water has been running over it for years. "One berth, Ivo. He can strike one name. There are four of us and one berth and he's asking for the book *now*, tonight, before the manifest closes. He's asking me to trade our mother's life for a principle with somebody else's name signed at the bottom."

"He's asking you to stop hiding," Ivo said quietly. "Both of us. Our whole lives. You with the ledger under the floorboards, me with my name changed more times than I can count. He's the first person who's ever offered to make what we did *count* instead of just making it *safe*."

The silence in the pantry was long. Somewhere above them Mother's footsteps crossed the ceiling, back and forth, back and forth, pacing out the perimeter of a home that would belong to strangers by spring.

"I need to think," Rafael said.

"We have until the manifest closes."

"I know how long we have." Rafael pinched out the candle between finger and thumb, and in the sudden dark their voice came low and strange. "What I don't know is whether I've been keeping that ledger to give the dead their names back — or to keep myself from having to say mine."

* * *

They gathered in the kitchen near midnight. Mother sat with her sewing basket on her lap although she was not sewing; her hands lay folded on the wools and buttons as if holding a vigil over them. Voss stood by the stove with his cap in his hands, out of uniform in spirit if not in fact, and did not flinch under Mother's stare, which Ivo noted with a kind of respect — few men survived that stare on first acquaintance.

Mother heard the offer through once, without interruption. Then she said, "Which name would you strike, Lieutenant?"

"That's not mine to choose, ma'am."

"Everything is yours to choose; you said so yourself, you hold the pen." Her voice was mild and terrible. "So let me save you the suspense. Strike Rafael. My eldest stays with me, and I stay regardless — I've buried a husband in this town and I'll not have my children bury me in someone else's. Or strike Ivo, whose papers are a house of cards waiting for the first breeze at the first checkpoint, and we all know it. Or strike Rafael and send the book with them, and to hell with your honesty, young man — honesty is a luxury for people whose mothers aren't standing in kitchens."

"Mother," Rafael said.

"Don't." She didn't raise her voice. She never needed to. "You've been carrying other people's names for a year. Carry mine a moment longer: I have watched both my children disappear — one into a uniform, one into a lie — and I have spent every day since deciding which disappearance was my fault. I will not watch a third."

Voss cleared his throat. It was the first time Ivo had seen him uncertain. "Ma'am, the substitution must be documented as compassionate grounds. Family hardship. It cannot go to you unless—"

"Unless I'm the hardship," Mother said, and almost smiled. "Yes. I know how the world counts its mercies."

The stove ticked. Snow came against the window in long slow diagonals, and out on the water, faintly, a ship's engine turned over and over in the cold like a man clearing his throat before speaking.

It was Ivo who broke the silence, because she had done the arithmetic hours ago, in the pantry, while Rafael talked around it.

"There's a fourth option nobody's saying," she said. "Strike my name. Send Rafael." She held up a hand before either of them could speak. "Listen to me. My papers are the weakest thing in this family — one clerk with a good memory undoes me at any bridge between here and the sea. If I'm stopped, I'm stopped alone, and I've been alone before; I'm practically fluent in it. But Rafael gets through, and Rafael carries the letter — Henrik's letter, the one he trusted to us — and somewhere on the other side of that water there's a village where a woman named Henrik's wife is still setting two cups on the table. That's worth one signature. That's worth *your* signature, Lieutenant, because you'd be sending a nurse and a letter and a true name across that water honestly, and heaven knows none of us has managed an honest crossing yet."

"And you?" Mother's voice cracked on the single syllable.

"I stay invisible," Ivo said. "I've had practice. And when the century turns, I intend to still be here turning with it, which is more than the ledger can say if we don't act tonight."

Voss looked at her for a long moment. Then he took a folded paper from inside his coat — the substitution form, already drawn up, blank in the crucial line — and laid it on the kitchen table, and beside it he laid his pen.

"Not tonight," he said. "Tomorrow. Embarkation eve. And I'll tell you why." He straightened, and there was something in his face now that hadn't been there before — not resolve exactly, but its exhausted cousin, the thing that keeps walking after resolve has sat down. "Because your brother hasn't answered yet. And I will not take that book, or alter that manifest, or save a single Halloran, until Rafael tells me to my face that surrendering it openly is a gift and not a theft. One night. Let your brother decide what kind of keeper they are."

He put on his cap, nodded once to Mother, and went out into the snow.

The form lay on the table between them, white as a fresh page, blank as an unwritten name. Rafael stood over it without touching it, and outside the bells of the ruined chapel rang the hour — ten, eleven, twelve — and Ivo watched her sibling's hand rise slowly toward the pen and stop in the air above it, trembling, while the clock counted down the last two days of the century.
