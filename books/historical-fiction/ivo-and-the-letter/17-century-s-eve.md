## 17. Century's Eve

The bells began at dusk, though there was no order for them to ring.

Rafael heard the first one from the hospital steps — a single low note rolling up from the chapel across the square, cracked and half-hearted, as if the bell itself had grown old along with the century. Then a second bell answered from the harbor district, and a third from somewhere beyond the mill, and soon the whole town was ringing out of tune, an untidy chorus of bronze and iron, because no requisition order in the world could forbid a town from marking the end of a hundred years.

Below in the streets, children were dragging crates and broken chairs toward the corners of the square, stacking them into pyres. The soldiers let them. Someone had decided — the colonel, perhaps, or some clerk far away who signed papers about coal rations and thought of everything — that bonfires were permitted tonight. A century should end with light, even here. Even in a town that had been inventoried down to its nails.

Rafael stood on the steps with their breath clouding and counted the fires being built. Seven. One for each corner of the square. The flames would be visible from the water, from the black ship waiting out past the ice line with its single berth still unassigned, its manifest still carrying the clerical accident that had put two names where only one belonged.

Three days had passed since the posting. Tomorrow the error would be corrected for them. Tonight was all that remained.

Rafael went back inside.

* * *

The ward was quiet in the way wards were quiet on evenings when the dying had made their arrangements with themselves. Lamps turned low. The stove ticking. Somewhere near the window, a man named Aldric Voss slept with his mouth open, breathing in the shallow, deliberate rhythm of a body that had learned to economize. His greatcoat lay folded at the foot of his cot — the coat, the coat Ivo had sewn shut with her own hands while Rafael stood watch at the door, the ledger flat against its lining like a second spine.

Rafael did not touch it now. There was no need to check it. Ivo's stitches were small and perfect, and Rafael had learned long ago that checking things too often was how you lost them.

Instead Rafael sat at the little table by the stove, set down the lamp, and opened a plain sheet of paper — one of the last sheets in the building, since paper itself had been confiscated weeks ago and every scrap remaining was contraband of one kind or another.

There was work to do that no book could do for them.

It had begun that morning, this final labor, and it went like this: Rafael closed both eyes and summoned the first name — *Aldric Voss, private, third company, wounded at the mill race, died of fever* — and held it in the dark of their own skull until it felt solid, until it stopped being ink and became a person again, a young man who had once shown Rafael a photograph of a girl standing stiffly beside a dog. Then Rafael opened their eyes and found something small to bind to it. The photograph itself was already gone, given back to no one, but there was a button from Voss's cuff, brass, worn smooth. Rafael wrapped it in a twist of cloth and whispered the name over it once more.

*Aldric Voss.* Carried now in two places: in a button, and in the memory behind Rafael's eyes.

One name. One token. That was the arithmetic of the whole night.

The ledger held ninety-one names. Some of the men they belonged to were dead; some were shipped east on trains months ago; three, miraculously, were still in this ward, sleeping ten feet away. For each name Rafael chose something. A bootlace. A broken pipe stem. A playing card, the six of diamonds, soft as cloth from handling. Each object wrapped, each name spoken twice — once to seal it to the token, once to fix it in memory — and then Rafael said it a third time silently, which was the way one said things to oneself, the way one said things that were meant to survive.

By midnight the tokens filled a tobacco tin, and Rafael's voice had worn down to a rasp, and the ward smelled of lamp oil and pine smoke drifting in under the door from the square.

* * *

Near the middle of the night's work, the lieutenant came.

He came quietly, without escort, letting the door fall shut behind him, and he stood a moment just inside the warmth with his cap in his hands like a visitor rather than an officer. His wound was healing; he moved without favoring the shoulder now, but he still carried it slightly high, the way a man carries a thing he has agreed to hold forever.

"You're working late," he said.

"It's the last night of the century." Rafael did not look up from the tin. "I thought the last night should be spent on the last work."

The lieutenant crossed the ward slowly, pausing at each cot as if paying respects — and perhaps he was; Rafael knew what rode folded in the inner pocket of his own coat, knew it as Ivo knew it: the confiscated letters he had been ordered to burn and had not, months of other people's voices pressed flat against his ribs. Two keepers of names, meeting in a room full of names. Neither of them remarked on it. It had gone past remarking.

He stopped at the table. He looked at the tobacco tin, and at the neat twists of cloth, and at Rafael's face, and something in his expression shifted — not suspicion; it had never been suspicion between them, not since the inspection, not really. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing his own secret kept badly by someone else and loving it for its badness.

"Midnight," he said. "They'll fire a flare from the ship when the year turns. And then the gangway goes down."

"I know."

"Your mother is still saying she'll stay."

"My mother," Rafael said, "has been saying she'll stay since Tuesday. She says it the way she says grace. It isn't negotiable and it isn't true, but it must be said aloud or it curdles."

The lieutenant almost smiled. Then he didn't. He looked at the tin again, and Rafael watched him understand exactly what it was — not a keepsake box, not sentiment. An index. A backup. A soul distributed into small objects so that no single confiscation could take all of it at once.

"Tomorrow," he said quietly, "on the quay. You'll bring it? The book?"

"Tomorrow I bring the book."

"And you ask nothing else."

"I ask that the names be read. Once. Anywhere. By anyone. Out loud, so the air has to carry them." Rafael closed the tin. The lid clicked like a small bone setting. "That's the whole price. Ninety-one names, read aloud once, somewhere in the world. After that the paper can burn for all I care. The paper was only ever the vessel."

The lieutenant stood very straight. Then he did something Rafael had not seen him do before: he looked away first.

"I was ordered to burn forty-three letters," he said. "I copied eleven of them before I couldn't bear to keep copying. Do you know why eleven?"

"No."

"Because after eleven I understood that copying wasn't enough. That reading them into my hand wasn't the same as getting them read into the world. I've been trying to find the difference ever since." He put his cap back on, tugging the brim low. "Tomorrow I think I find it."

He left the way he had come, quietly, and the door held his coldness for a moment before the stove took it back.

* * *

Later — it must have been eleven o'clock, though the hospital clock had been confiscated in October and time was now kept by bells and bonfires — Ivo came.

Rafael heard him before seeing him, because Ivo still moved like an orderly, like a man, boots heavy on purpose, shoulders squared by habit rather than nature. But when he came around the row of cots and into the lamplight, and saw the tobacco tin and the open ledger lying on the table with its pages fanned, he forgot the disguise entirely for a heartbeat, and stood the way Ivo stood, and Rafael saw, not for the first time, the sister underneath the soldier, thin as flame.

"They're lighting the fires," Ivo said. "Mother wants you at the quay by half past eleven. She wants all of us standing together when the flare goes up. She says a family shouldn't be scattered at the turn of a century, even a family like ours."

"Even a family like ours," Rafael repeated softly.

Ivo pulled a stool to the table and sat. He looked at the ledger a long time. When he spoke again his voice had dropped its borrowed register entirely, and it was Ivo's own voice, low and quick, the voice Rafael had known before either of them had secrets.

"You're going to give it up tomorrow. Truly."

"At the gangway. Before the ship sails."

"And me aboard."

"And you aboard."

Ivo reached out and rested two fingers on the ledger's cover — the cloth binding, gray-brown, stained at the corners, the book that had lived under floorboards and inside a dead man's coat and once, for three terrible hours, inside Rafael's shirt against their heart. Ivo touched it the way one touches a sleeping animal.

"Ninety-one," Ivo said. "Do you know I know most of them by heart? From watching you write them. I'd stand behind you pretending to fold linens and I'd read upside down." A pause. "Peter Halas, drowned crossing the ford. Emrys Duval, shot going for water. Tomás Weir, who owed me three cigarettes and paid me in letters home that never got sent—"

"Stop." Rafael's throat was tight. "Why are you reciting them?"

"So you know it's not only yours." Ivo looked up. "You've carried this alone so long you've started to believe remembering is a weight one person holds. It isn't. It's a rope. We're all holding it. Me, the lieutenant, those poor burned letters in his pocket, every nurse who ever wrote a name on a scrap of lint because no officer would. Tomorrow you hand over the rope. But you don't let go of the idea of the rope. Understand? The book is the vessel. You told him so yourself. What's in your head—" he tapped his own temple, then reached across and tapped Rafael's, gently, "—that doesn't surrender."

Rafael could not answer for a moment. In the cot by the window, Voss breathed his economical breaths. Outside, the bells were still going, ragged and stubborn, and through the frost-furred windows the square glowed orange where the seven fires had caught.

"The berth," Rafael said at last. "You're still resolved."

"I resolved it before you did. You just hadn't noticed." Ivo smiled, and it was the old smile, the childhood smile, crooked on one side. "Rafael. Listen to me. I have spent nine years being no one. Nine years of binding and strapping and lowering my voice and walking like a plow horse. If I board that ship, I board her as *I. Halloran, orderly*, and somewhere across the water I will have to decide whether to keep being him forever." He shook his head slowly. "But if I go, I can carry the letter. The unsent one. I can deliver Peter Weir's voice to his mother's kitchen. I can be the name-bearer. That's worth more than staying to guard paper. Paper has you to guard it."

"The paper surrenders tomorrow."

"Yes. And you stay anyway. Because staying is who you are, and leaving is who I am about to become." Ivo stood, and squared the shoulders again, and the orderly returned over him like a coat being shrugged on. "Half past eleven. The quay. Don't make Mother send someone."

At the door he paused. "Read me one before you sleep. Just one. Whoever comes next in your head."

Rafael looked at the ledger, at the page marked with a ribbon of torn linen, and read aloud: "*Miklós Ferro, farrier, second company. Burned at the lime kilns. Survived four days. Talked of horses.*"

"Miklós Ferro," Ivo repeated, sealing it. "Talked of horses." And he was gone into the smoke-orange dark.

* * *

Rafael did not sleep. There was no sense pretending otherwise; there was no bed reserved for sleepers on a night like this. Instead Rafael finished the last of the tokens — twelve names remained, and the picking grew harder, the ward stripped of small things long ago — and then, in the hour before midnight, sat with the ledger open on both knees and read it entire, beginning to end, every entry, every date, every marginal note in their own careful hand.

This was the copying that mattered. Not ink to ink. Ink to flesh.

Some names came easily; they had been read so many times they rose like well-fed animals. Others had to be coaxed — *Sander Lindqvist, cook's mate, died in February of the coughing; he had laughed at his own jokes before anyone else could* — and Rafael held each reluctant one to the lamp of attention until it glowed. Ninety-one souls filed one by one through a single tired mind, and the mind ached with them, and welcomed the ache, because the ache was proof.

Outside, the bells changed character. They stopped ringing raggedly and began to strike together — the chaplain, no doubt, hauling the rope himself, since the sexton had been evacuated in November — counting down the old year with terrible sincerity. Rafael heard footsteps and voices in the square, soldiers and civilians mixed, which no order had permitted since October, all of them drawn to the fires by something older than orders.

On the last stroke, the sky above the harbor went white.

The flare climbed, hung, and bloomed — a slow magnesium flower, shedding light on the frozen quay, on the black hull, on the faces below the hospital window, hundreds of them, upturned. And beneath the fading light, faint but unmistakable, the ship's bell rang out the first minute of the new century.

Rafael closed the ledger. Tied it with the linen ribbon. Set it on the table beside the tobacco tin of names — the vessel, and its lifeboats.

Then Rafael stood, put on the coat, took up the tin in one hand and the book in the other, and went down into the burning, bell-ringing, snow-flecked square, toward the quay, toward the gangway, toward the last hour of the world as it had been.

The lieutenant was already there, waiting at the waterline, his breath rising white, his eyes on the book.

He was not alone.
