## 18. The Last Berth

The quay had turned to glass under a thousand boots. Ice had come up the harbor in the night, sheeting the stones where the fishing fleet used to land its catch, and now the whole town walked on it carefully, as though the ground itself had been asked to hold its breath. Bonfires burned at either end of the waterfront — the soldiers' doing, for light more than warmth — and between them the ship sat black against the black water, her funnels breathing steam into the last air of the century.

Ivo stood at the foot of the gangway with her collar up and her hands in the pockets of a coat that was not hers, and counted the ways she could still refuse.

She was good at counting. She had spent eleven years counting: the number of men who'd watched her bind her chest in the orderlies' dormitory and never once looked twice; the number of times Rafael had said *not yet* when she'd asked if it was safe to be herself again; the number of names in the ledger sewn against her ribs, stitched flat inside the lining of Corporal Engh's greatcoat, two hundred and six of them written in her brother's small, upright hand. She could have recited them all tonight. Some nights she did, silently, like a rosary, like proof that the world had held more people than the reports admitted.

"Ivo." Rafael's voice came through the crowd noise the way a needle comes through cloth — thin, precise, finding exactly the place meant for it. "Look at me."

She looked. Her brother stood one step above her on the gangway planks, backlit by the ship's lanterns, and the years fell off him all at once so that he seemed very young: twenty-six, hollow-cheeked, his nurse's gray coat buttoned wrong at the bottom because his hands were shaking too hard to notice. He had not slept since the lottery list went up. None of them had.

"There's been a mistake," Ivo said. "There are two names on that manifest."

"Yes."

"Two names and one berth. That's arithmetic, Rafael. Even Mother can't argue with arithmetic."

"She already tried." He almost smiled. "Come up here. The clerk is waiting."

The clerk was a boy, really, seventeen perhaps, with chilblains on his ears and a manifest board clamped under one arm like a hymnal. Behind Ivo the crowd pressed forward — families with their allotments, mothers holding tickets like relics, old men arguing with sailors who would not bend — and the pressure of them moved her up the first plank whether she willed it or not. She felt the ship take the gangway's weight and shift, gently, like something waking.

At the top of the plank a sailor checked papers. At the second step a second sailor checked faces against papers. Somewhere past that line, the century ended and another began, and she would be on the water when it happened, watching a country she might never see again burn its bonfires smaller and smaller behind her.

"Rafael." She caught his sleeve. Under the wool his arm was rigid; he had been holding himself together by main force for days, and she could feel the trembling through the fabric like current through wire. "Listen to me. You give them my name instead. Tell them the orderly Halloran shipped out sick, tell them anything — your handwriting is on half the paperwork in this garrison anyway, you could forge us both onto Noah's ark and no one—"

"No one would check, I know. Lieutenant Aune thought of it first."

That stopped her. "Aune? He offered?"

"He offered everything. That's what frightens me." Rafael turned so they faced each other squarely, and the bonfire light caught the side of his face, the scar along his jaw from the shrapnel at the east wall, the eyes that had watched more men die than any man his age should carry. "He'll strike any name we choose, Ivo. Any name but the one that matters, unless I give him the ledger openly. And I'm going to do it."

The words landed in her stomach like stones dropped down a well.

"You can't," she said. "Two hundred names, Rafael. They'll burn them. They'll burn *him*—" she touched the greatcoat, meaning Engh, meaning the letter folded inside it, meaning everything the dead had trusted them with, "—they'll read it and laugh and feed it to the stove, and then those people will have died twice."

"They won't."

"How do you know? How can you possibly—"

"Because I'm not giving him paper." Rafael's voice dropped low enough that only she could hear it over the wind off the water. "I've copied every name. Every date, every wound, every letter home they never sent. It's all up here now." He touched his temple, briefly, almost embarrassed. "Three weeks of work. The book is a copy, Ivo. What he gets is paper. What stays alive is memory, and he can't requisition that."

She stared at him. All these months she had believed her brother was simply stubborn — the family's quiet stone, the one who stayed when others vanished, who wrote names when others signed oaths of silence. She had not understood until this moment that the stubbornness was a kind of architecture, built deliberately, load-bearing, designed for exactly this night. He had been preparing to stay behind since before there was a ship.

"No," she said.

"It's done."

"It is not done, I am standing right here, and I am telling you—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated how easily she broke open around him, hated that after eleven years of being nobody, of being *Orderly*, of passing through ward-rooms like smoke, this one person could still reach into her chest with bare hands. "You don't get to decide this alone."

"I decided three weeks ago." He said it gently, which was worse. "The night you sewed the ledger into Engh's coat, I sat in the dispensary and wrote my own name at the bottom of the last page. Entry two hundred and seven. *Rafael Halloran, garrison hospital, staying.* Someone has to be remembered by somebody, little sister. I'd rather it be you."

The crowd surged behind them. A woman was crying near the clerk's table, high and rhythmic, a sound like a pump handle. Down the quay the bells of the chapel began to ring the hour — eleven o'clock — and the sound rolled across the ice and out over the frozen harbor and came back changed, thinner, as if even the bells knew they were ringing for the last time in this century.

Rafael took her elbow. Not roughly. The way he steered patients who could still walk.

"Wait," Ivo said. "Wait — Mother, then. Mother takes the berth. She's older, she's earned—"

"Mother gave up her claim yesterday afternoon. In front of witnesses. She said she'd lived her whole life in this house and she'd die in it, and that neither of her children would talk her out of it, and Father's ghost could keep her company." His mouth twisted. "She also told me to tell you that you were always the brave one, and that you were never fooling anyone with the name Ivo, and that she's proud of you in both languages."

Ivo's eyes stung. The wind took the moisture and made cold tracks down her face before it could properly become crying. Their mother, who had kept the household standing through the war and the requisition and the confiscations, who had quarreled with Rafael about sacrifice until the neighbors must have heard, who had watched her daughter disappear into a man's clothes and never once said the word *why* aloud — that woman had spent her last currency on a blessing.

"She shouldn't have said it like that. Both languages. She promised—"

"She said you'd know what she meant."

Ivo did know. Eleven years ago, in the kitchen, flour on her hands, her mother looking at the dress laid out on the table — the dress Ivo would never wear again — and saying only, *Well. If you're going to disappear, disappear well.* It had not been permission, exactly. It had been an instruction manual.

The clerk called the next name. "Halloran!" He said it twice, scanning the crowd, and Rafael raised his hand.

"That's both of us," the boy said, squinting at the manifest. "There's an error here. Two entries, berth twelve. I'm supposed to hold berths for correction orders, but the correction hasn't come down, and the tide—" He looked miserable. "Sir. Which of you is it?"

Neither of them spoke.

Then Rafael stepped forward and put his hand flat on the manifest board, over the two names, covering one of them entirely, and said, "This one. Ivo Halloran. Berth twelve."

And Ivo understood, in the space of a heartbeat, that the argument was already lost — that it had been lost before the quay, before the lottery, perhaps eleven years ago in a kitchen — and that the only thing left to fight for was the manner of losing.

* * *

They let her walk up the plank on her own feet, which she would think about later and be grateful for, though at the time it felt like being marched.

The gangway rose and rose. Below her the ice-glazed stones fell away; the bonfires became orange smudges; the requisitioned town with its chalked doorways and numbered windows arranged itself below like a map of everything she had survived. She could see the hospital roof. She could see the chapel where they had once argued about hiding the ledger in the wall. She could see, or believed she could see, the small dark rectangle of the floorboard Rafael had pried up in March, beneath which nothing lay anymore, the space empty as a pulled tooth.

Halfway up, she stopped.

"I can't," she said, to no one, to the plank, to the wind. "Rafael. I can't leave you here."

"You can," he said behind her, close, his hand steady between her shoulder blades. "Because I'm asking you to. Because one of us has to carry it, and you're better at carrying things than I ever was. You carried yourself for eleven years. Carry this."

At the head of the gangway the sailor held out his palm for papers. Rafael pressed them into Ivo's hand first — her forged identity, Orderly I. Halloran, fit for transport — and then, over the papers, without ceremony, without looking away from her face, he pushed something else. Folded paper, soft with handling, gone brown at the creases. She knew what it was before her fingers closed on it. She had known since the autumn, since Private Anselm Reyes had died asking for his mother in a language Rafael didn't speak and Ivo half-did, since the letter that no post would ever deliver because the address had been overrun in October.

"You give this to her," Rafael said. "Find her. Whatever country you land in, whatever it takes, find Anselm Reyes's mother and put this letter in her hands. That's your cargo, Ivo. That and the names. That's why it has to be you."

"Why?" Her voice came out wrecked.

"Because you remember everything," he said. "And I only write things down."

The sailor cleared his throat. Steam billowed from the funnels. Somewhere on deck a bosun was shouting about lines, about the tide, about the century ending whether they cast off or not.

Ivo stepped aboard.

The deck tilted under her feet with the living motion of water, and that motion undid her more than the gangway had. Solid ground lies; it pretends nothing changes. Water tells the truth constantly. She stood on the tilting deck with a dead man's letter burning through her glove against her palm and watched her brother walk back down the plank, unhurried, his gray coat flapping, and stop at the bottom and turn to look up at her, and smile.

It was not a brave smile. It was not a sad one, either. It was the smile he'd worn as a boy, catching her at the pond ice when she fell — *there you are; I told you I'd be here.*

"Come with me," she called down. The crowd noise swallowed half of it. "Rafael! Come with me!"

He shook his head slowly, once, and mouthed something she could not hear over the engines beginning to turn over, the deep gathering thrum that climbed up through the hull and into her bones. She leaned over the rail. She shouted his name, and a sailor touched her arm and said, gently, "Miss — sir — we're casting off," and she shook him off and shouted again, *Rafael, Rafael,* and saw her brother raise one hand, palm out, the same gesture their mother used at the window when they left for school.

Then the bell rang.

One clear stroke, then two, then the long triple sequence of departure, and the sound cut through everything — the crowd, the engines, her own voice — the way a blade goes through cloth. Sailors moved along the deck with practiced urgency. The gangway groaned and swung inward. Between one bell-stroke and the next, the gap of black water opened between the hull and the quay, a meter wide, then two, and Ivo pressed against the rail with her arms over the side as if she could hold the ship back by will alone.

"Let go!" someone shouted on the dock. "Cast off! Cast off!"

She watched the quay begin to slide. She watched the bonfires slide with it. She found Rafael's face in the crowd — found it instantly, the way you find the one lit window in a dark street — and held it as long as physics allowed, and physics allowed longer than she expected, because the ship came out of the harbor slowly, respectfully almost, as though it knew what it was taking and what it was leaving.

The last thing she saw clearly was her brother turning away from the water, shoulders square, walking back toward the hospital with the easy stride of a man who knows exactly what he is for. Then the breakwater intervened, and the town folded itself away behind stone, and there was only the harbor mouth, and the ice, and the first pale suggestion of snow beginning to fall into the sea.

* * *

She did not know how long she stood at the rail. Long enough that frost formed on the metal under her hands. Long enough that a steward came twice and asked if she wanted tea and she heard him the second time only. The ship cleared the headland and the swell took her proper, the long slow lift and fall of open water, and passengers stumbled and laughed nervously and went below to find their berths, and the deck emptied until Ivo was nearly alone in the falling snow, a figure in a soldier's greatcoat gripping a rail at the edge of the century.

Berth twelve waited below: a curtained bunk, a basin, a porthole showing black water. She would not go to it yet. There would be time — years, maybe — to lie in narrow spaces and count what she'd lost. Tonight she had one task, and she took it out with numb fingers and unfolded it under the deck lamp.

Anselm Reyes's letter, unsent. The ink had feathered where damp had found it. *Mamá,* it began, in a careful schoolroom hand, and then a paragraph she could not fully read and would spend the rest of her life reading, and then, at the bottom, in different ink, added later in Rafael's upright script:

*Mrs. Reyes, if this reaches you, your son died on the 14th of October of a wound to the chest, without pain in his last hours, thinking of you. He was brave. He asked for you. — R.H., garrison hospital*

Below that, in a third hand — Ivo's own, from the day she had sewn the ledger into the coat lining, added in secret while Rafael slept — a single line:

*He was not alone when he went.*

She had forgotten writing it. Now, rocking on the black sea with the old century running out behind her, she read it and understood it newly, as prophecy rather than comfort. Nobody went alone who was remembered by someone else. That was the whole of it. That was the ledger's entire theology, squeezed to a sentence. Rafael had taught it to her without ever saying it, and now he was ashore practicing it in reverse: staying, so that she would carry him.

Snow thickened. From the shore, faint with distance, the chapel bells began again — midnight somewhere behind the hills, though the ship's clock said quarter to eleven — the old year dying early, or the town ringing for its own departed. Ivo listened until the last bell faded under the engines.

Then she folded the letter along its worn creases, put it inside her shirt, against her skin where the ledger had lain for weeks, and went below to find the purser's list.

Because a plan had assembled itself while she gripped the rail, quiet and complete, the way plans do when they've been building for years: she would find Anselm Reyes's mother. And she would write letters — hundreds of them, one for every name in the ledger she had helped sew away — until every family on that list heard their dead spoken of honestly. She had a memory that held everything and a brother who had given her the reason to use it.

She only needed to learn one thing first: whether the book had survived its surrender.

She would not know until a reply came, if a reply ever came, across however many miles of water — a letter in a hand she knew, or in a hand she didn't, telling her whether the names had been read aloud somewhere, someday, as Rafael had asked. Until then she would write into the dark.

At the purser's desk, the clerk looked up. "Name?" he said, pencil ready, a fresh page open.

Ivo Halloran stood in the last minutes of the nineteenth century, disguised no longer to herself, and gave him the true one — all of it, every syllable, out loud, for the first time in eleven years.
