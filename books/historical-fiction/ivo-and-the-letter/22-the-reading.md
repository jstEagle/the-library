## 22. The Reading

### IVO

Rafael's hair has gone the color of the winter light through my kitchen window — that thin, silvered gray that the north Atlantic gives to everything it touches twice. They stand at my door with a single canvas bag and a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, and for three full seconds neither of us speaks, because there are some reunions that words would only make smaller.

Then Rafael says, "You've kept the house warm," and I say, "You've kept yourself alive," and we are past it, the way siblings are always past it before strangers have even understood there was anything to be past.

The parcel goes onto my table like something consecrated. I do not unwrap it yet. Neither does Rafael. We drink tea instead, and talk about the crossing, and the caretaker with the bad knee — Mr. Aldous, he is called — who has already chalked the date onto the hall's booking board as though it were a wedding or a harvest supper: *Reading of Names, Saturday, seven o'clock.*

"Are you afraid no one will come?" Rafael asks.

I consider the question honestly, the way I have learned to consider everything since I stepped off that ship with a dead man's letter in my pocket and my brother's ledger living in my memory like a second heartbeat. "I am more afraid everyone will come," I say. "I am afraid of what their faces will do."

Rafael nods slowly. My sibling has spent fifty years learning what faces do. That was always the difference between us: I hid myself from the world, and Rafael refused to let the world hide anyone else. Both of us paid. Only one of us ever thought the price was worth arguing about.

That night, after Rafael has gone to the spare room where our mother's old quilt still lies folded at the foot of the bed — I never could give it away — I sit alone at the table and finally untie the oilcloth.

The ledger comes out into the lamplight looking exactly as I remember it from the floorboards of the garrison hospital: cloth-bound, water-stained along one edge, its spine soft as an old shoe from handling. But when I open it, my breath catches. The violet pages are still there, all of them, the names in Rafael's small upright hand — and beside them, in a different ink entirely, a second hand. Careful. Slightly formal. A young officer's schoolroom copperplate grown old and patient over decades.

He copied them. Before he burned the rest, he copied every name, and he carried them through his whole life the way I carried mine across the water, and when he was old and the war had become history and then became shame, he wrote to me.

*They were never yours alone to keep,* his last letter said. *But they were mine to carry for a while. Forgive me the while.*

I close the book. Through the wall I can hear my sibling turning, turning, the way they have slept badly every night of their life since 1899, and I understand suddenly, sitting there in the lamplight with two hands' worth of witnesses open on my knees, that tomorrow night the hall will not be full of grief.

It will be full of company.

* * *

### RAFAEL

I had prepared myself for an empty room. I had prepared myself for a full one. I had not prepared myself for the queue.

It stretches down the street by half past six, out of the hall's yellow lamplight and into the blue dark of the evening, and it is not made of the important people. There is no mayor, no consul, none of the men whose signatures once decided which lives merited ink. There is Mrs. Okafor from the laundry, whose grandmother appears on page eleven. There is the schoolmaster and three of his older students, notebooks in hand because I asked him to have them write the names down — *not to remember them,* I told him, *they'll do that anyway — but so that the remembering can be handed on.* There is the caretaker, Aldous, who has set out every one of his hundred and forty chairs himself, bad knee and all, and who now stands at the door nodding people through like a verger.

And there are strangers. Dozens of them. People who came because someone wrote to them, years ago or months ago, a letter beginning *Dear Sir or Madam, you do not know me, but I am writing to return a name to your family—*

Ivo stands beside me at the back of the hall while the chairs fill, and we watch them settle, and I feel my sibling's shoulder press against mine. Once. Briefly. It is the whole speech.

"You should read first," Ivo says quietly. "You wrote them."

"No," I say. "We read together. That was always the point." I look out at the filled hall, at faces young and old and in between, and something in my chest that has been clenched for sixty years begins, very slowly, to unclench. "Besides. You know some of them better than I do. You carried them across an ocean."

"I carried your voice saying them," Ivo says. "There's a difference."

But it is not a correction. It is a gift. My sibling has spent a lifetime giving gifts disguised as corrections, and I have spent a lifetime pretending not to notice, and tonight I decide to stop pretending.

Aldous taps his stick against the floor three times. The hall quiets. Somewhere outside, rain has started, soft and unhurried, the kind that means to stay all evening — and it occurs to me that sixty years ago, on the last night of the old century, it rained exactly this way on the frozen quay while a ship's bell argued with my sister and won.

I take the ledger to the front. My legs are old but they remember how to walk toward difficult things.

"The official record of the garrison town where we were born contains four hundred and six names," I say, and my voice carries further than I expected; the room is very kind to it. "Four hundred and six men, listed as wounded, missing, or lost. This book contains nine hundred and thirty-one."

I let the number sit in the air. Someone near the front draws an audible breath.

"Some were soldiers the reports omitted because they died too quickly to be counted. Some were civilians the requisition swept up and never gave back. Some were people who could not appear in any report, because appearing would have destroyed them." I glance back at Ivo, and Ivo gives me the smallest nod, and I go on. "My sibling taught me something I did not understand when I began this book: that invisibility can be a shelter, but it is also a kind of hunger. Every name in here went hungry. Tonight we feed them."

I open to the first page. The lamp gutters once and steadies.

"Halloran, Rafael," I read — my own name, first of all, entered long ago in my own hand because I swore I would count myself among the counted, would be witness even to myself. "Entered the service of remembrance by choice. Still standing."

A ripple of something — laughter almost, grief almost, the place where they meet — moves through the room. And then Ivo steps up beside me, takes the book when my voice tires, and reads, and when *our* voice tires, Aldous reads, and then the schoolmaster, and then a girl of perhaps sixteen who reads slowly and beautifully, stumbling once on a Hungarian name and being gently corrected from the third row by a woman who explains, in accented English, that it was her grandfather.

The names go out into the warm room like birds released. Some land. You can see them land — a woman pressing her hand flat to her sternum; an old man removing his cap; two brothers holding each other's wrists. Some fly onward, unanswered, into the future, waiting for whoever will one day need them.

We read for two hours. We rest, and Aldous brings water, and we read again.

And then, near the end, Ivo turns a page and stops. I see my sibling's throat move. I know that page. Of course I know that page. I have known it since I wrote it, kneeling on cold floorboards with a stolen pencil stub, while the century behind us burned itself down to its last hours.

Ivo looks at me. I shake my head — *no, you.* Because there are debts that only one debtor can pay, and this one belongs to the person who lived hidden so that the rest of us could live seen, and who chose, on the frozen quay, to carry all of us out into the world under whatever name would serve.

Ivo lifts their chin the way they used to when a clerk's eye lingered a moment too long on a letter of a name.

"Halloran, Maren," my sibling reads, and their voice does not break, though everything else in the hall seems to hold very still. "Midwife. Mother. Keeper of a household that stood when nothing else would. Stayed behind so that her children would not both starve. Died in the spring of 1901, in the requisitioned town, in her own bed, attended by her own child, who held her hand and told her —"

Ivo stops. Swallows. Looks straight down the length of the hall at me.

"— told her that she was remembered. And was heard."

Nobody applauds. There is nothing to applaud. But the silence that follows is not empty, and it is not grief either. It is the silence of a hundred and forty people carrying one woman's name out of the hall and into their separate lives, and I stand at the front of the room, gray and old and weeping without shame, and I think: *this is what survival is. Not the berth. Not the ship. This.*

### IVO

Afterward the hall empties slowly, the way churches do, people reluctant to break the spell by moving too fast. A line forms in front of Rafael — it always forms in front of Rafael, it formed in front of Rafael in hospital corridors in another century — of people wanting to say thank you, wanting to say a name, wanting to touch the ledger as if it were a relic, which, I suppose, it is.

I stand aside and let them. That, too, is a familiar posture. But it is different now, and I take a moment to understand why, and then I do understand, and it nearly undoes me:

I am standing in a lit room, in a village where everyone knows me, beside my sibling, wearing my own true name, and I am hiding from no one.

Aldous locks the doors at half past ten. The rain has stopped. The lamps along the street burn in their little glass houses, and the wet cobbles hold the light like a spilled basket of coins.

"He'd have come, you know," I say to Rafael as we walk. "The lieutenant. If he'd lived another year."

"Yes." Rafael walks with the ledger under one arm, refusing my offer to carry it. "Though knowing him, he'd have stood at the back and refused the chair."

"He stood at the back of everything," I say. "Except the one time it mattered."

We walk the rest of the way in the companionable quiet of people who have said everything worth saying and will spend the next fifty years finding new ways to say it anyway. At my gate Rafael pauses, looks up at the sky — clearing now, stars coming through the torn clouds — and says, without any preamble at all:

"There's a boy in the third row. Sixteen, maybe. Dark coat. He wrote down every single name. Didn't miss one. I watched him."

"I saw him."

"When I die," Rafael says, in the tone other people use to discuss the weather, "someone should tell him where the book lives."

"It lives wherever you live," I say. "That's been the arrangement for sixty years."

"Mm." Rafael opens the gate. Then, pausing, half-turned, with the lamplight silvering that gray hair and the whole ordinary street listening around us: "Or — I've been thinking. There's a spare room in this house. And I'm too old for bad landladies and colder climates. And someone ought to teach that boy in the dark coat how to hold a pen properly."

I look at my sibling — the stubborn, impossible, faithful heart of my entire life — and I find that there is only one possible answer, and it has been waiting at the tip of my tongue since 1899.

"The spare room is already yours," I say. "It always was. Come inside. I'll make tea, and you can tell me again about the night at the quay, and I'll pretend I haven't heard it, and tomorrow—" I push open the door, and warmth and lamplight spill out onto the step, and somewhere far off, a church bell begins to mark the hour. "Tomorrow we'll start a second book. There are still names out there nobody has written down."

Rafael smiles — the old smile, the defiant one, the one that started all of it — and steps over the threshold into the light.
