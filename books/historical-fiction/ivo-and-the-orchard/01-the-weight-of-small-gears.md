## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

There is a particular hour in a clockmaker's workshop when the daylight fails and the lamps must be lit, and every man in the trade pretends he does not mind the interruption. It is the hour when the light goes soft and gold and forgiving, and you can see your work whole for the first time all day — not as a hundred small frustrations, but as a thing.

I was at that hour on the last evening of September, in my master's workshop on Chandler's Lane, holding Mr. Pettifer's pocket watch in my palms, and it was finished.

I do not mean repaired. Repairs are bread and butter; repairs are what apprentices are for. I mean finished — cased, regulated, running true through forty-eight hours of observation without gaining or losing so much as a breath. A three-quarter plate movement, eleven jewels where most watches carry five, and an escapement I had re-cut myself from stock brass because the original had been worn into dishonesty by thirty years of a merchant's fat thumb winding it too hard every morning of his life.

It weighed perhaps four ounces. It had taken me nine weeks. And if you had asked me then which of those numbers mattered more, I would not have been able to tell you, which is probably the most honest thing about me.

"Wendell." Master Hobb's voice came from the back room, where he did his own work behind a curtain, in the belief that we learned faster if we could hear him but not see him. "Is that the Pettifer piece?"

"It is."

"Bring it."

I carried it back with both hands, the way you carry a bird. The back room smelled of oil and lamp-black and the boiled ham that was our supper cooling on the shelf, because Master Hobb held the firm conviction that a meal eaten before eight o'clock made a man sleepy and sleepy men ground their pivots crooked. He did not look up when I came in. He never looked up until he wanted to, and then he looked like a magistrate.

He took the watch from me without asking, turned it over, opened the case back, held it to the lamp. For a long moment there was no sound but the watch itself — a small dry heartbeat, quick and even.

"Hm," he said.

From Master Hobb, *hm* was a sonnet. I stood very still and let my heart do its unprofessional hammering against my ribs.

"Escapement?"

"Re-cut. You'll find the impulse faces within a tenth of the theoretical."

"I don't doubt I'll find them somewhere near it." But his thumbnail touched the pallet stones almost tenderly, and I knew. Nine years I had worked for the man, sweeping his floor first and filing his rough stock second and finally, these last two years, being allowed near anything with a spring in it, and I could read his silences better than most men read print. "Pettifer will pay four pounds ten for this. You'll see none of it."

"I know," I said.

"And yet you'd do it again for nothing."

"Yes," I said, because lying to him was pointless. He could detect a lie the way he detected runout — by holding the thing up and listening.

He closed the case with a click that sounded, to my ear, exactly like a door closing, and handed the watch back. "Wrap it. Pettifer collects tomorrow at ten. And then come here, there's a letter came for you this morning, and I've been deciding whether you've earned it or whether reading it would only make you miserable, and I've concluded that misery is educational."

* * *

A letter addressed to me by name is such a rare event in my life that I have kept nearly all of them. This one sat on the bench between us while I wrapped Mr. Pettifer's watch in wadding and string, and I confess my fingers were clumsy with it, twice dropping the knot, because part of my mind was already on that paper like a dog at a gate.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, sealed in red wax with a device I didn't recognize — a circle quartered by lines, like a wheel seen edge-on, or an eye drawn by someone who had never trusted eyes. The hand that had addressed it was copperplate so perfect it looked printed: **Mr. Wendell Dravenmoor, apprentice to A. Hobb & Son, Clock- and Watchmakers, Chandler's Lane.**

*Dravenmoor*. Even written down it looked wrong, a name out of somebody else's story. There were Dravenmoors enough scattered through the county — it wasn't a rare name, merely an unremarkable one, attached to no property and no history that anyone had troubled to keep. My father had been a cooper. His father, I'd been told, had drunk himself out of a farm somewhere east. That was the whole of the family legend, and legends thinner than that have held kingdoms together.

"Open it," said Master Hobb. "You're breathing on my lamp oil."

I broke the seal.

> *To Mr. Wendell Dravenmoor,*
>
> *Sir,*
>
> *The Observatory at Marrowgate finds its great regulator clock in a state of progressive derangement, having resisted the attention of two practitioners from the city. We are given to understand, through Mr. Aldous Pettifer of this town, that your master's establishment produces work of unusual precision, and that among his people there is one whose treatment of escapements is especially recommended.*
>
> *The clock in question governs the observatory's transit observations and, by long custom, the town time of Marrowgate itself. Its repair is a matter of some institutional importance. The position offers remuneration of two guineas per week for the duration of the work, lodging at the observatory, and the gratitude of the Corporation of Marrowgate.*
>
> *Should you accept, present yourself at the observatory on any weekday before noon. Travel may be arranged by the carrier Jupp, who passes through your town Thursday.*
>
> *I remain, sir, your obedient servant,*
> *B. Tull, Caretaker, The Marrowgate Observatory*

I read it twice. The second reading changed nothing except to make the words heavier.

"Marrowgate," I said. "That's the hill town. Past the ridge. They have the big clock."

"They have the biggest clock in three counties, and for some years it has kept time the way a drunk keeps a secret," said Master Hobb. "Two guineas a week." He said the figure again, slowly, the way you'd turn a gear over in your fingers to feel for a bent tooth. "That is a journeyman's rate. That is, if we are being precise, rather above a journeyman's rate, offered to a boy who has never signed his own work."

"Mr. Pettifer recommended—"

"Pettifer talks. Pettifer also has cousins in Marrowgate, which explains how my shop reached the ears of the Corporation." He leaned back, and the lamplight caught the grey stubble on his jaw, and for a moment he looked older than his sixty years, which he generally took pains to disguise. "Wendell. Sit down."

I sat. Nobody in nine years had ever been told to sit in the back room.

"A clockmaker's reputation," he said, "is built on other men's clocks. You fix what they cannot, they tell their friends, their friends have money, and in twenty years you are *Master Hobb*, and your name goes inside the case instead of outside the ledger. That is the whole trade, understood? Everything else is gears." He tapped the table. "Now. An observatory clock is the summit of that road. If you go to Marrowgate and mend that machine, and it keeps time afterward, you will be spoken of in places that have never heard of Chandler's Lane. Men will bring you their dead grandfathers' repeaters and trust you with them. Do you follow me?"

"Yes," I said. Something in my chest had gone tight and bright.

"Do you also follow," he said, quieter, "that when a great institution summons an apprentice instead of engaging a master, it is because an apprentice is cheap, disposable, and can be blamed?"

The bright thing cooled somewhat.

"The city men failed," he went on. "Twice, by the sound of it, and expensively. Whoever fixes this next inherits whatever broke the first two — whether that's the clock, the climate, the management, or something worse. Two guineas a week says they know it's worse. You are being paid hazard money, boy, and at nineteen you are flattered to receive it. So was I, once."

"What happened, once?"

"None of your business," he said comfortably, "and instructive anyway, which is why I mention it. Go to Marrowgate. I'm not a fool; I know what this could make you, and God knows I've spent nine years trying to make you worth making. But you'll go knowing what you are. You're not the man they want. You're the man they can afford to lose."

* * *

I did not sleep much that night, which will surprise nobody who has ever been nineteen and offered a future.

Our lodgings were above the shop — one room for me, one for Master and Mrs. Hobb, and between them the thin wall through which I could hear, if I pressed my ear to the plaster, the tick of the regulator clock that governed the whole establishment. Every clockmaker sleeps surrounded by time. Most people let it pass over them like weather. We lie awake counting it.

I lay there doing sums instead. Two guineas a week was more than I saw in half a year, after what Master called my indenture deductions — the cost of my coal, my lamp oil, my ruined files, my education. With it I could buy tools of my own. Real ones, not the mixed inheritance of dead men's kits that filled my roll. A good graver, a rounding-up tool, a jeweling press. And after Marrowgate — after, if the clock ran true and the Corporation wrote letters saying so — perhaps a place in the city houses. Perhaps, someday, a bench with my name burned into it.

I was ambitious. I want to be honest about that, because everything that happened afterward has made ambition sound like a sin, and it isn't. It's just a hunger, and like all hungers it doesn't ask where the food came from. I wanted my name to mean something hard and precise, the way a good pivot means something. I wanted, more than I wanted almost anything, to stop being replaceable.

Because that is the truth of an apprentice's life, and no master will say it aloud: you are portable. You are, in fact, the most portable thing in the shop. Cheaper than the lathe, lighter than the vise, easier to move. When you are gone, the floor gets swept by someone else, the rough stock gets filed by someone else, and within a season the customers have forgotten which boy it was with the steady hands. I had watched it happen to two apprentices before me. One left for the army and one left for a wife, and the shop absorbed their absence the way a movement absorbs a removed wheel — a brief irregularity, then the train runs on, redistributed, indifferent.

Nine years of my life, and I could be erased in a season.

Remember that, when you judge what I did later. I knew precisely what it felt like to be unwritten. I had simply never imagined that anyone else might be written out too, or that the erasure could be done in marble and municipal record instead of in the small sad ordinary way it was done to boys like me.

* * *

Thursday morning I presented myself to Master Hobb with my roll of tools packed and my good coat brushed, and found him waiting at the bench with something wrapped in chamois.

"For the trip," he said. "Open it."

Inside was a loupe — not the cracked thing I'd used since my first year, but a new one, doublet glass, heavy and cold in its brass mount. I turned it over twice and could not speak.

"You'll sign nothing at Marrowgate," he said, in the same breath, "not receipts, not statements, not any document that says the clock was faulty in any particular way, until you've seen with your own eyes what's wrong with it. City men failed twice. Before you touch a screw, you find out why. Institutions do not lose money twice on a simple problem, Wendell. They lose it twice on a complicated one, and then they send for a boy, and the boy takes the blame home with him in his toolkit. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And write to me. Not about the clock. About the people. Clocks are simple; I've never met one that lied. People—" he waved a hand, dismissing the entire species. "Go on, then. Jupp's cart leaves from the Saracen's Head at eight, and if you miss it you can walk to Marrowgate and arrive humble."

At the door I stopped. I don't know why I said it; it seemed suddenly necessary, the way a watch seems necessary to strike even when no one asked it to.

"Thank you," I said. "For — all of it. Nine years."

He did not look up from his bench. "Come back and thank me properly when you're somebody," he said. "Gratitude from apprentices is like rain on a lead roof. It makes a noise and then it's gone."

But as the door swung shut I heard him add, very quietly, to nobody — to the lamp, maybe, or the ticking dark of the shop —

"*If* you come back."

* * *

The Saracen's Head smelled of straw and previous passengers. I wedged myself and my toolbox into the carrier's cart between a woman with day-old chicks in a basket and a farmer going to market, and by seven we were climbing out of the river valley with the mist still standing in the fields below like milk poured and forgotten.

Marrowgate lay over the ridge, Jupp told us, half a day by road. Hill country, orchard country — "was," he corrected himself, sucking his teeth. "Fine cider once. Not now."

"Why not now?" I asked.

He glanced at me in the way carriers do, measuring how much answer a fare had purchased. "Times change," he said, and flicked his whip at nothing, and would not be drawn further.

The road climbed through hedgerows gone wild and generous, black with late sloes, and I sat with my toolbox on my knees and the letter in my breast pocket and thought about clocks. About the great regulator waiting for me at the top of the county, bigger than anything I had ever touched, its trains the size of wagon wheels, its pendulum swinging with the slow patience of a thing that had counted more seconds than I had lived days. I thought about what it would mean to open a machine like that. To stand inside its case, if you could, with a lamp, among wheels tall as myself.

I was nineteen years old, and I believed that a broken thing mended was the best kind of victory, and that precision was a kind of honesty, and that if I worked well enough and long enough I could file my way out of being replaceable.

All of that was true. None of it was the whole truth. On the hills ahead of me, past the ridge, lay orchards I did not know were mine, terraces I could not see from the road though they saw me, and a clock that had been keeping a secret along with the time.

The chicks cheeped. The wheels turned. Somewhere behind us, in a shop on Chandler's Lane, a regulator ticked on without me, keeping the only kind of time that never lies.

I did not look back. I have wondered since whether, if I had, I would have turned around — and I have decided, in fairness to the boy I was, that I would not have. He was already halfway to Marrowgate in his heart, and the rest of him was merely catching up.
