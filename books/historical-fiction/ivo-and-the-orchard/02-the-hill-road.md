## 2. The Hill Road

The coach to Marrowgate cost eleven shillings and smelled of wet wool and someone else's regrettable cheese. I mention the price because it mattered. Eleven shillings was most of what I'd earned in a season of mending other people's watches, minus the cost of a new pair of tweezers after I dropped the old ones into a works of gears and never found them again. There is a particular grief in losing a tool inside a clock. It becomes part of something larger than you and you can never get it back.

I did not know yet that this was to be the theme of my year.

The letter of summons — heavy paper, a wax seal pressed with an armillary sphere, the kind of stationery that arrives pre-satisfied with itself — had instructed me to present myself at the observatory by the fourteenth of September. It had also enclosed payment for my travel, generous to the point of insult, as though the town of Marrowgate wished me to understand from the outset that I was being *handled*. My master, Aldous Crane, read it twice, handed it back, and said only: "Observatory clocks are temperamental things. Mind you don't let anyone else's hands near your work."

He said it in the tone he used for everything, which was the tone of a man describing weather to a field he had no intention of harvesting himself. But when I left the workshop at dawn on the twelfth, he was standing in the doorway watching me go, and he did not close the door until I had turned the corner. I have thought about that door more times since than I can reasonably account for.

* * *

The road out of the lowlands climbed through country so handsome it felt faintly accusatory, as if the hills were showing off specifically for my benefit and expected gratitude in return. September had gotten into the hedgerows. The morning light came slantwise through a haze that turned every field into an etching of itself, and for the first hour I was entirely content, which should have warned me. Contentment on approach to any great house or institution is, in my experience, the overture to being robbed.

The orchards began around noon.

They began, and then they simply continued — terrace upon terrace cut into the hillside, following the contours in long patient curves, stone retaining walls laid with a precision I could not help but appraise professionally. Whoever had built those walls had understood load and drainage the way I understood escapements. This was not farming done by eye. This was engineering wearing an apron.

And every tree on every terrace was dead, dying, or mad.

I do not use "mad" loosely; there is a specific madness to a neglected fruit tree. Unpruned, it grows itself into a thicket of competing leaders, branches crossing and strangling, the whole crown becoming a fist clenched against the sky. Some of these trees had gone feral decades ago. Others had simply given up mid-effort, standing grey and leafless in rows so straight they still held their formation like soldiers after a rout — no order to fall back, nobody left to tell them.

Here and there a tree still bore fruit, small and scabbed, dropping apples into the grass where wasps attended them like creditors.

What unsettled me most was not the neglect itself. Neglect is common; the whole world is a museum of unfinished maintenance. It was the *scale*. These terraces ran for miles up the flank of the hill. An orchard like this would have wanted dozens of keepers — pruners, grafters, cider-men, families, generations. You do not build stone walls by hand along eight miles of contour and then simply forget to come back. Abandonment on this scale is not an accident. It is a decision, renewed annually, like paying rent on a grudge.

The cottages confirmed it. They came at intervals along the terraces — small, well-made, stone-roofed houses, each with the same proportions, each with a bench beside its door facing the view, as though the people who lived there had been meant to sit in the evenings and look at what they'd made. Doors stood open to the weather or hung by one hinge. In one garden, a rose bush had swallowed an entire gatepost and flowered defiantly atop it, the only thing on the whole hillside behaving as though anything here had ever been loved.

I counted eleven cottages between the valley road and the first ridge. Not one showed smoke.

* * *

I stopped at a farm where the road bent toward a ford — a low stone house with real smoke in its chimney, which after the terraces felt almost indecent, like applause at a funeral. A woman was beating a rug on the fence rail, and a man who was presumably her husband stood in the yard repairing a cart wheel, or holding a cart wheel in the manner of men who intend to repair things presently.

"Good day," I said. "Is this the road to Marrowgate?"

"It's the only road to Marrowgate," the man said, which was true and somehow unkind.

I asked if I might water my boots, meaning my feet, and the woman nodded me toward the pump without pausing her assault on the rug. While I worked the handle, I made conversation, because I am constitutionally incapable of not making conversation with strangers about their own locality, a failing my master has described as the occupational disease of tradesmen.

"The orchards up the hill," I said. "Are they yours?"

The rug-beating stopped. Not slowed. Stopped, mid-stroke, with the rug lifted, the way a clock stops when the mainspring lets go.

"No," the woman said.

"They're nobody's," the man said, at exactly the same moment, and then the two of them looked at each other with the expression of people who have both answered a question wrong and are deciding whose fault it was.

I pumped water. I thanked her. I was three steps back toward the cart when the man called after me.

"You're going up for the observatory, then? The clock work?"

"That's right. Apprentice clockmaker." I touched my hat brim, a gesture I hoped conveyed competence. It has never once conveyed competence. "Wendell Dravenmoor."

For a moment — and I want to be careful here, because I have replayed this moment many hundreds of times since, and memory is a workshop apprentice who files everything under the wrong name — for that moment, the man looked at me the way you look at a door you thought was nailed shut that has just swung open on its own.

"Dravenmoor," he repeated. Quietly. As if testing the weight of it.

Then his face closed like a ledger, and he came around the cart wheel and gripped my forearm in the hearty way of rural greeting, which is designed to communicate friendship while establishing exactly how strong the other man's fingers are.

"Marrowgate's a fine town," he said, too loudly. "Good folk. The Founders' Hall is worth seeing, and the alderman keeps a fine table. You'll want to be getting on. Light goes early this time of year."

"I only wondered about the—"

"Don't." He said it without heat, without anger, in the flat voice of a man telling you the bridge is out. Then, softer, glancing once at his wife, who had resumed beating the rug with tremendous concentration: "Whatever you're here to fix, fix it. Take your pay. Go home. Don't ask questions up the hill. Questions don't keep you warm in winter, and they've a long memory in Marrowgate."

"For what?" I asked.

He let go of my arm.

"That's a question," he said.

* * *

I rode the rest of the way in a silence broken only by the driver's whistling, which was tuneless and ceaseless and eventually achieved the quality of a natural phenomenon, like wind. But I will set down honestly what went through my head, because a man who lies in his own account is building a clock with a warped mainplate — everything afterward runs crooked and blames the pendulum.

I told myself the farmer was simply a frightened provincial. Frightened provincials are ten a penny and usually frightened of tax assessors, Methodists, or turnip blight, and rarely of questions. I told myself the orchards had failed because of some blight — there had been talk in the papers, years back, of a canker that took whole counties' worth of fruit stock — and that the cottages had emptied because labor follows work the way water follows slope. Economical explanations. Tidy ones. I assembled them the way I assemble a theory of a broken movement before I open the case back, and I noticed, even then, that I was doing it: constructing the story that required the fewest new parts.

My master taught me that trick, though he'd hate to hear it called a trick. "Before you open a case," he says, "you'll already believe you know what's wrong. That belief is your enemy. The watch doesn't care what you believe. Neither does anything else, ever, in the history of the world."

So I let myself hold the tidy story in one hand and the hillside in the other, and I noted that they weighed differently. Eight miles of dead terraces weigh rather more than a canker.

There was also, I admit, the matter of the man's face when I gave my name. *Dravenmoor.* It is not a common name. I grew up assuming it wasn't a name at all, properly speaking — more a collection of sounds that clerks had agreed to tolerate. Nobody in my family had been anybody. My father mended harnesses; his father, so far as anyone knew, had also mended harnesses, possibly the same ones. We Dravenmoors were people things happened *near*, not *to*. And yet a stranger on a hill road had heard my surname and looked at me as if a piece of furniture had spoken.

You cannot imagine — or perhaps you can; perhaps everyone carries some sealed room in the house of themselves — what it does to a person to discover, at twenty-two, that somewhere far from home, his name has a history he was never told. It is like finding a compartment in the bottom of a familiar drawer. You may leave it shut. But you will never again open that drawer casually. Every time, your hand will hesitate, and your heart will knock once against your ribs like a loose pallet falling into place.

* * *

Marrowgate announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and river-mud and, underneath, faintly, the ghost of apples, drifting down from all those ruined trees like perfume kept in an empty house. Then the town appeared below the observatory hill: white gables, a market square, a spire, a mill, all gathered around its square like sheep around a feed trough. It was, objectively, beautiful. It was beautiful the way the orchards had been orderly — with an effortfulness that showed.

The coach set me down outside the Rose & Compass, where the landlord took my trunk and my coin and my measure of me in one practiced motion. A good landlord reads a guest like a hallmark: young, alone, decent coat slightly worn at the cuffs, here on business, business likely lucrative, tip accordingly hopeful. He asked what brought me, and I said the observatory clock, and he brightened with what I would later recognize as civic relief.

"Aye, the old girl's been poorly. Half the town sets its watches by her and she's been losing minutes like a spendthrift. Alderman Hale wants her right as rain before the Founders' Feast." He leaned in confidentially. "Big do. History of the town being written, proper history, by a gentleman from London. Vey's his name. They say Mr. Hale means Marrowgate to be famous."

"Famous," I said.

"Well — *settled*," he amended, in a tone suggesting these were the same thing. "Four hundred years, and half the old records lost to a fire and the rest scattered. Mr. Vey's putting it all to rights. There's pride in a town that knows its own story, my dad used to say."

"And the orchards?" I asked. "Up the hillside. Who kept those?"

The landlord's cloth, which had been wiping the same clean spot on the bar, stopped.

"Orchards?" he said, with the mild blankness of a man consulting an empty ledger. "There's no orchards at Marrowgate. Never were. Cider comes up from the vale, always has." He smiled, ready to serve, and the smile was entirely genuine, and that was the worst part. "Room's number four, end of the hall. Supper's at seven."

I carried my bag up to a small clean room with a window facing the hill. The observatory stood at its summit against the evening sky, a pale dome and tower catching the last copper light, and the dead terraces fell away beneath it in long silver-grey curves, eleven roofless cottages strung along the slope like beads off a snapped cord.

No orchards. Never were.

I sat on the bed and looked at that hillside for a long time, and I thought about the farmer's grip on my arm, and the rose bush flowering on its drowned gatepost, and a man's face opening and shutting like a ledger at the sound of two syllables.

A clock that loses minutes, my landlord had said. Losing them *like a spendthrift* — carelessly, wastefully, without meaning to. But I had spent six years learning that machines do nothing without meaning to. A clock cannot lie about time. It can only be made to.

Tomorrow I would climb the hill and find out what the great clock had been made to forget.

From the window, in the blue dusk, the observatory dome had begun to shine faintly — lamplight behind glass, someone awake up there in all that dark. Watching the town, or waiting.

I unpacked my tools and did not sleep much, and the last thing I heard before sleep finally took me was the observatory bell striking eleven, soft across the rooftops — a minute late, by my watch.
