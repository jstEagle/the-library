## 3. The Master Clock

The observatory door was taller than any door had a right to be, and it opened before I could knock.

"You're the boy from Drayhollow," said the man in the doorway. Not a question. He looked me over the way a farrier looks at a horse brought in on a Sunday—checking the legs first.

"Wendell Dravenmoor," I said. "Apprentice to Master Hollis Fenwick. I've come about the clock."

"I know why you've come." He stepped back to let me in. "Bram Tull. Caretaker. Which is a polite word for the man who sweeps up after everyone else has gone home."

He was somewhere past sixty, built like a door hinge—narrow, rusted at the joints, but clearly still load-bearing. His hands were enormous for his frame, knuckles like walnuts, and they hung at his sides with the particular stillness of hands that have learned not to touch things without permission.

I have a theory about hands. A man's face will lie to you all day if you let it, but his hands only know how to tell the truth. Bram Tull's hands told me he'd been careful for a very long time, and that the carefulness had cost him something.

"Master Fenwick sends his regards," I said, producing the letter of introduction. "And his apologies that he couldn't come himself."

"He sent you instead." Bram read the letter without hurry. "Because the town of Marrowgate is beneath his dignity, or because Marrowgate's money isn't?"

"The second," I said, before my manners could catch up with my honesty. "The first would be untrue. He speaks well of this place."

"Mm." He folded the letter and gave it back. "Well. Come and meet her, then."

*Her.* Clockmakers do that. We pretend we don't, and then we do it anyway, and no one ever points out that a profession devoted to cold brass and colder arithmetic keeps talking about its machines like sweethearts.

* * *

The clock room was at the heart of the observatory, behind two doors and a corridor that smelled of lamp oil and cold stone. And there she was.

I want to describe her properly, because she deserved description and because what I found wrong with her only makes sense if you understand what was right.

She stood three stories, case of black walnut gone silver-grey at the edges, glass panels let into the sides so the whole mechanism was visible like a heart behind ribs. The great wheel alone was taller than I am. Above her, through an opening in the ceiling, the drive shafts climbed toward the telescope dome and the dome's rotation gears; below her, the pendulum dropped through a cut in the floor into a stone-lined pit, where its bob swung in darkness like a diver who never surfaces. She drove the observatory's sidereal clock, the dome itself, the chimes that carried down into the town—and, I would learn later, half the lesser clocks in Marrowgate were set by her by proxy, so that a lie in her works became a lie repeated in four hundred parlors.

"She was built by Aldous Merrick," Bram said. "Forty-one years ago. He worked on her eleven years and died the winter after she first ran true. They say he wept when she struck midnight correctly the first time."

"Do you say that?"

"I say he had reason to." Bram handed me a lamp. "She's been failing for eight months. Slowing. Irregular. Sometimes she loses a minute in an hour, sometimes she runs near-true for a day and then drops five minutes between supper and bed. Three men have looked at her. Two gave up. One pronounced her 'tired.'"

"Tired," I repeated.

"That was the word. As though she were an old dog that ought to be put out on a blanket in the sun."

I set down my bag and rolled my sleeves. "May I?"

"Boy, I've been waiting six weeks for someone to ask me that instead of telling me they already knew."

* * *

Diagnosis begins with listening. Any apprentice can look at a clock; looking tells you almost nothing. You listen. Every fault has a voice. A worn pivot whispers. A dry bearing clicks. A cracked tooth knocks—a small wooden knock, once per revolution, patient as a debt collector.

I stood under the great wheel with my head tipped back and listened for ten minutes while Bram pretended to sweep a floor that did not need sweeping.

There it was. Not a knock. Something worse.

A hesitation. Once every sixty seconds, precisely, the whole train seemed to hold its breath—a hitch so slight you'd miss it if your pulse were quick or your mind elsewhere. Then release, and on she went as though nothing had happened.

"There," I said. "Every minute. Like clockwork." I winced at myself. "You know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean. I've heard it every night for eight months." Bram came to stand beside me. "The other men heard it too. One of them called it settling. Said old foundations breathe."

"Foundations don't breathe in sixty-second intervals. Foundations don't keep time." I fetched my loupe and began at the top, working down the train the proper way—escapement last, always last, because the escapement is where every lie upstream comes to confess.

The going train was clean. Oiled recently, oiled *well*—better than most town clocks ever see. The great wheel's teeth were sound. The intermediate wheels ran true within a hair. Whoever maintained this machine had loved her, and recently.

Which made the next thing I found considerably more interesting.

At the fourth wheel—the one that carried the minute hand—the arbor was new. Bright steel against the surrounding brass gone soft with decades of handling. New work, fine work, fitted maybe a year ago. And on that new arbor, the collet holding the wheel had been filed. Not worn. *Filed.* Someone had taken a smooth-cut file to a finished component and removed metal in two places, each no wider than a fingernail, each placed with the precision of a man who knew exactly which fraction of a revolution corresponded to which moment of the hour.

I crouched there a long time with the lamp close and my breath fogging the glass panel.

Understand what I was looking at. Wear is honest. Wear happens where friction happens, along the paths physics chooses, and it tells you a story you can trust: *this rubbed against that, for this long, with this much force.* Wear is a clock's autobiography. But filing is authorship. Filing says: *I wanted the story to go differently, so I changed the page.*

"Mr. Tull," I said carefully, "who maintains this clock?"

"I do. Have done for thirty years." He didn't bristle. He watched me with an odd, flat patience, like a man watching weather come in over hills he knows well.

"Then who else has touched her? Recently. Within the last year or two."

Silence. Long enough that the pendulum ticked perhaps forty times in it.

"Why do you ask?" he said at last.

"Because she hasn't failed. She's been *made* to fail." I stood, knees cracking, and pointed with my tweezers at the filed collet. "This isn't age. This isn't neglect. This is a deliberate alteration, placed to introduce a periodic irregularity that no simple adjustment can correct. It's elegant, actually—it's the kind of fault that makes a repairman chase his own tail for weeks. Whoever did this understood escapements better than most men who claim to."

Another silence. When Bram spoke, his voice had changed—lower, flatter, the voice of a man reading from a document he'd memorized against his will.

"The third man who examined her. The one who called her tired. He was here in the spring. Before him, in the autumn, there was a fellow from Corvus Hale's own household—a Mr. Quill, he was called, who did some cleaning of the works. Hale pays for her upkeep, you understand. The observatory's maintenance is endowed by the founding families. Hale administers the endowment."

"And Mr. Quill cleaned the works."

"Cleaned them beautifully. I'll say that for him. Best oiling she's had since Merrick died." Bram's walnut hands closed slowly, opened again. "And after Quill's cleaning, she began to slow."

I looked at the new arbor. At the filed collet. At the beautiful oil lying over both like snow over a grave.

"Mr. Tull," I said, "you must have seen this. Thirty years of maintaining her—you'd know new work from old at a glance. You must have known something was wrong the day you saw that arbor."

The lamplight moved a little. My hand wasn't steady, or his wasn't, or the building itself breathed.

"I saw it," Bram said.

"And you said nothing?"

"I said nothing." He met my eyes, and there was no excuse in his face, which I respected even as it infuriated me. "I reported that she needed attention beyond my skill. That much was true. What happened after that was not mine to tell. Not yet."

"*Not yet?*"

"Not yet." He took the lamp from my hand with those enormous careful fingers and set it on the bench. "You've told me what I paid six weeks' postage to hear a stranger say, boy, and I'm grateful, and now I'm going to ask you to do the hardest thing a craftsman does."

"File the collet true? Re-cut the wheel? Because I'll need patterns for—"

"Wait." He held up one hand. "Wait before you fix anything. That's the hardest thing. Waiting. Looking. Understanding what it is you've actually been summoned here to repair." He walked to the clock's case and laid his palm flat against the walnut, the way you'd touch a horse's neck. "Because I don't believe you were sent here to mend a clock, Wendell Dravenmoor. I believe you were sent here because you're the first pair of eyes in eight months that nobody in this town owns."

The pendulum swung in its pit below us, patient as arithmetic.

"I don't understand," I said, which was true, and which I was beginning to suspect would be the most-honest sentence I uttered in Marrowgate.

"No," Bram agreed. "Not yet. But you will. There's a letter. Sealed. Odile Fen left it—she was our archivist, forty years in these archives, and she died in March. She left instructions that it go to whoever finally diagnosed the clock honestly." He looked at me, and for a moment the caretaker fell away and I saw the man underneath: tired, frightened, and stubborn as a root. "You've earned it. Though God help me, I wish you hadn't. It was easier when everyone who came up that hill was a fool or a coward."

My professional pride—which had been swelling pleasantly all afternoon, fed by clean wheels and a difficult diagnosis—curdled all at once, like milk in a thunderstorm. Pride in finding the fault curdled into the question underneath the fault: *who files a clock, and why, and what are they afraid she'll say if she runs true?*

"When do I get this letter?" I asked.

Bram Tull picked up his broom.

"When you've slept on it," he said. "Truth keeps. That's the whole point of truth. It's the only thing in this world that improves with waiting."

* * *

They gave me a room in the observatory's east wing, narrow and clean, with a window facing the hill road and the town below. From it, Marrowgate looked like what the guidebooks claimed: slate roofs, church spire, the founders' mansions ranged along the ridge like chess pieces mid-game, and beyond them the terraced orchards stepping down the hillsides in their long grey sleep.

I sat on the bed with my tools spread out on the blanket—not to work, just to look at them. A craftsman's tools are the most honest company there is. They don't flatter. They don't console. They simply wait to be picked up and used well or badly, and they remember which.

Someone had sabotaged the finest clock I'd ever stood beside. Someone had done it skillfully, recently, and with access. Someone had then hired three repairmen, two of whom had failed and one of whom had lied—or been lied to. And the man who paid for all of it, the almighty Alderman whose name was already on the endowment plaque downstairs in letters taller than my thumb, had personally welcomed me to town with wine and warmth and a promise of permanent employment.

I lay back and stared at the ceiling and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the hill road.

If the clock was being kept broken on purpose, then someone wanted it broken. And if someone wanted the master clock of Marrowgate to run false—to lose minutes, to drift, to strike uncertain hours across four hundred parlors—then the question was never really about the clock at all.

It was about what people would believe if it ran true.

Below me, faint through the floor, I could hear Bram Tull moving through the clock room on his nightly rounds. Stopping. Standing a long while. The sound of a man keeping a vigil over something he was not allowed to save.

In my bag, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom where I kept the things I valued, my half-finished pocket watch ticked softly to itself, keeping perfect time for no one, in a town full of clocks that had forgotten how.
