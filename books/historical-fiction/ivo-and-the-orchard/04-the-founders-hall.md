## 4. The Founders' Hall

I dressed for the alderman the way I'd been taught to dress a movement: everything clean, everything in its place, nothing showing that might suggest strain. My second-best coat — the best one was my funeral coat and would have announced ambitions I couldn't yet afford — my collar freshly turned, my hands scrubbed to the pink. In Marrowgate's economy of appearances, an apprentice with clean nails is still an apprentice, but he is an apprentice who has read a book about hands.

Bram Tull walked me down from the observatory himself, which surprised me until I understood it wasn't companionship but escort. There is a difference between *I'll come with you* and *they'll want to know you arrived*, and Bram's silence on the hill road was firmly the second kind.

"Say little," he told me at the foot of the drive, not looking at me but at the white front of Founders' Hall, which sat at the bottom of the town like a wedding cake left out in the weather. "Answer what's asked. Don't mention the mechanism."

"The mechanism is why they sent for me."

"Yes." He adjusted his cap. "Well."

Then he left me standing at the gate like a delivery.

* * *

Founders' Hall had been built by people who wanted history to feel inevitable. The stone was pale and deliberately old-looking, though I'd lay my best file that it had been cut within living memory — there is a freshness to fake age that betrays itself the way fresh varnish does. The windows were tall and evenly spaced, the steps wide enough for processions, and over the door a carved frieze showed figures I took at first for saints or seasons before recognizing them as men: founders, presumably, rendered with the serene confidence of people who knew their faces would be looked at for centuries.

There were no orchards in the frieze. No trees at all. For a town that smelled, even from its center, of apples gone feral on the hillsides, that seemed a curious omission. Like painting a portrait and forgetting the head.

A housekeeper took my name without writing it down — *Dravenmoor*, I said, and watched her face do nothing at all, which is its own kind of performance — and led me through a hallway lined with portraits into a study that smelled of pipe smoke, lemon oil, and money that had stopped apologizing for itself.

Corvus Hale rose from behind a desk large enough to land aircraft on, if aircraft existed, and crossed the room with both hands extended, as if one hand could not possibly convey the whole of his welcome.

"Wendell Dravenmoor." He said my name the way a man tests a coin against his teeth. "At last. Bram wrote that you were coming Tuesday; I've been pacing since Monday. Sit, sit. Do you take tea? Coffee? Something stronger? It's barely eleven, but the observatory has a way of making eleven feel earned."

"Tea, thank you."

"Sensible man." He pulled the bell rope himself rather than summoning anyone, poured with his own hands when it came, and settled across from me rather than behind the desk, which was either genuine warmth or a very good imitation of it. I made a note to find out which, because the difference mattered more than most things.

He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples in the deliberate way of a portrait sitter, with the kind of easy good looks that suggested he'd been handsome young and had simply declined to stop. His hands were soft. But I noticed — because it is my trade to notice — that his signet ring sat loose, worn at the band, and that he kept turning it with his thumb while he talked. A nervous habit surviving inside a confident man, like a staircase bricked over but still creaking underfoot.

"So," he said. "The great clock. Tell me honestly — how bad is it?"

"Honestly?"

"Especially honestly."

"It isn't wear," I said. "Wear has a signature. Gears thin where they rub, pivots oval out, the escapement loses its geometry gradually, predictably, like anything that ages. This clock hasn't aged. It's been *injured*. Someone filed the pallet stones past tolerance and displaced the crutch pin. Deliberately, and recently, and by someone who knew exactly what they were doing."

I had not planned to say all of that. It came out because I am constitutionally incapable of lying about mechanisms, which my master assured me would be either my salvation or my ruin and which so far had managed only to be inconvenient.

Hale's thumb paused on the ring. One full second, no more. Then he smiled — warmly, regretfully — and said:

"Well. I suspected as much, and I'm glad to have it confirmed by someone who knows his trade. You'll forgive the theater of sending for an outsider, Mr. Dravenmoor, but when a thing has been mishandled close to home, one wants eyes that arrive unclouded."

"And who handled it?"

"The previous caretaker, we believe. An honest soul but past his competence, and ashamed of it. He fiddled where he should have fetched help, made it worse, and died before he could confess to anyone but God." He spread his hands, a gesture of gentle, inclusive sorrow. "Old men and clocks, Mr. Dravenmoor. They hate to admit they've slipped. Now — can you repair it?"

"Yes."

"How long?"

"A week. Ten days if parts must be made."

"Ten days." He nodded slowly, and then he leaned forward, and something in the room changed temperature. Not dramatically. Just the way a fire changes a room when someone feeds it.

"Mr. Dravenmoor, let me speak plainly, because I'm told plainness is a novelty in this town and I pride myself on supplying novelties. Marrowgate is a town with a future. A fine future — a railway spur, a proper academy, a printed history worthy of its founding. What it does not need is its past rattling around loose. The observatory clock is the oldest public instrument we have, and people are sentimental about it, and sentiment, unchecked, becomes argument. If word gets about that the clock was sabotaged — that word itself, *sabotaged*, with all its ugly implications — every crank and grudge-holder in three parishes will have a theory, and theories about our clock become theories about our families, and I will spend my remaining years putting out fires instead of building anything." He sipped his tea. "So. Repair it. Say nothing. Let the town believe the old instrument simply wore out, as honest things do, and was restored by a clever young craftsman. Which happens also to be true, in every particular that matters."

"That part is true," I agreed carefully.

"All the comfortable parts are true. That's the art of it." He set down his cup. "And here is what Marrowgate offers a clever young craftsman who helps her keep her dignity. There is a position here. Permanent. Warden of the Observatory Clock — a title we will invent tonight if it doesn't already exist — with a cottage, a salary your master would weep at, and the freedom to take private commissions besides. You'd be your own man at twenty-something. Most men aren't their own men at seventy."

The fire in the room was very warm now. And here is the part I am not proud of: for a moment — a real moment, measurable, maybe four seconds — I lived in that cottage. I saw the bench by the window, the commissions stacking up, the name painted on the door in modest gold letters. *W. DRAVENMOOR, CLOCKWAKER.* I was twenty-two years old and tired of measuring my life in other men's portable things, and Corvus Hale had just offered me a case to live inside.

"Say little," Bram had said. I said the worst possible thing instead. I said nothing at all, and let him watch me think.

Hale saw it too. To his credit — and I mean that sincerely, because his credits were the most dangerous things about him — he didn't push. He leaned back, satisfied, and turned the ring twice around his finger.

"There's no urgency to your answer," he said. "Take the ten days. Fix the clock. Get to know us. Marrowgate grows on people." He smiled again. "Some of her, anyway."

* * *

Lunch was served to me alone in a small dining room, on porcelain with the founders' crest on it, by a boy who didn't meet my eyes. I ate roast fowl in a house where I'd been offered a future, and I tried to work out what was wrong, because something was wrong, and wrongness in a mechanism is easier to locate than wrongness in a kindness.

It was the portraits that did it. I counted them while I chewed. Eleven founders along the hallway, all men, all rendered in oils with the same heavy confidence, each with a plaque giving name and dates and offices held. And beneath the arrangement of them — beneath the whole gilded grammar of the hall — ran a single sentence you weren't meant to hear aloud: *these people built this, and their descendants own it, and it was always thus.*

But towns aren't built by eleven men in coats. Towns are built by dozens, by hundreds — by the people whose names don't get plaques because plaques cost money and portraits cost more, by farmers and masons and the ones who do the unglamorous arithmetic of survival. I had grown up in a market town myself; I knew how it went. Somewhere under every founders' frieze there was a second ledger, written in sweat, kept by families nobody painted.

And a town that erases its workers once will erase them again. That was the thought that finally put its cold finger on the back of my neck. Hale hadn't offered me a post. He'd offered me a place in the frieze's margin — small, painted, permanent, and silent. *Warden of the Observatory Clock,* keeper of the machine, sworn by salary to see nothing.

My half-finished pocket watch ticked in my bag against my knee, keeping perfect time for free, and I found I loved it a little for that.

* * *

I walked back up the hill road in the mid-afternoon light, and I let myself be honest, since honesty about mechanisms was apparently permitted and I was out of practice elsewhere.

I wanted the offer. I want it now, if I'm being exact. There is a version of me — call him the sensible Wendell, the one my master groomed with such patient disappointment — who takes that cottage, fixes that clock, says nothing for forty years, and dies respectable with a full bench and an empty life. He wouldn't even be unhappy, exactly. He'd be *comfortable*, which is happiness's well-mannered cousin, the one who visits more often and never brings anything.

Against him I set the following evidence, entered into the record of myself:

First: Alderman Hale lied to me about the sabotage. Not badly — beautifully — but I know a fitted part when I hold one, and *the old caretaker fumbled it* was machined to fit the socket before I ever arrived. Which meant Hale knew what had actually been done to the clock, and possibly who had done it, and had constructed a story to cover both, and had done so *before* meeting me, which meant the story wasn't for my benefit specifically but for whoever came to look. How many people had been sent up that hill before me?

Second: a man does not pay a journeyman's wage to suppress a small truth. You pay that to suppress a large one.

Third — and this was the item I turned over longest, walking, the gravel talking under my boots — the orchard terraces. All morning, from the observatory windows and the hall grounds alike, I had seen those hillsides: rows of apple trees running in ordered lines up slopes that no one farmed anymore, cottages dark along the ridge road, the whole apparatus of a working landscape sitting abandoned in plain sight, like a clock with its hands removed. Nobody abandons productive orchard. Orchards are patience made edible; they take a generation to establish and they feed a town for a century after. Somebody had planted those trees with the same long faith that builds an observatory.

And somebody had arranged for them to stop mattering.

I stopped at the bend where the hill road looked back on the town. Marrowgate below me in the late light: Founders' Hall white and certain, the rooftops huddled around it, the church spire, the terraced hills rising green and derelict behind everything like a debt nobody would acknowledge.

Two histories, I thought. The one in the portraits and the one in the orchards. And a broken clock sitting between them like a witness who'd been paid to forget.

I had ten days to fix the mechanism. I intended to. I am a tradesman before I am anything else, and a clock that runs false offends me in a place below opinion.

But somewhere between the roast fowl and the ridge, the offer had changed shape in my hands. It had gone in a gift and come out a payment, and I had begun — quietly, without ceremony, the way all the important repairs begin — to wonder what, precisely, I was being paid to hold shut.

Behind me, down the hill, the hall's clock tower rang three — slow, thick, a quarter-tone flat, a sound like a lie told by someone who used to tell the truth.

Ahead of me, up the slope, Bram Tull stood waiting at the observatory door, holding a letter in both hands, watching me come the way a man watches a fuse he lit years ago finally burn into view.
