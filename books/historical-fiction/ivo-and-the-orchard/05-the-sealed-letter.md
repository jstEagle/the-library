## 5. The Sealed Letter

Bram Tull had kept many things in his sixty-one years, most of them badly. He had kept his father's tools until rust ate them. He had kept a promise to a woman named Odile Fen for eleven years, and that one he had kept well, which was the problem, because keeping it meant carrying it.

He carried it now up the observatory stair, one hand flat against the cold stone wall, the letter in his breast pocket pressing against his heart like something with teeth.

The boy was in the clock room. Of course he was. Wendell Dravenmoor had been in the clock room every waking hour since he'd arrived three days past, sleeves rolled, loupe screwed into his eye, murmuring to the great machine in the low coaxing voice of a man talking a frightened horse off a cliff. Bram watched him from the doorway a while. The boy didn't notice. Good clocks and bad apprentices noticed everything; good apprentices and bad clocks noticed nothing, and Wendell was very much the former.

*Dravenmoor.* Odile had written the name on the outside of the envelope, before she wrote anything else. Before the instructions. Before the apology. She had written it the way you'd underline a word in a ledger, twice, hard enough to dent the paper beneath.

He had asked her once, in her last spring, why it had to be a stranger. Why not give the letter to Hale himself, or nail it to the chapel door, or shout it from the market cross and be done with the whole rotten business.

"Because, Bram," she'd said, not looking up from her writing — she never looked up when she was writing; she said her eyes were cowardly and needed the excuse — "a dying woman shouting is a spectacle. A sealed letter is a *mechanism*. It only fires when the right pressure is applied. And I have arranged the pressure." She'd smiled then, thin as wire. "I spent forty years cataloguing other people's papers. Allow me to catalogue my own death properly."

She had died in October, tidy as an invoice, and Bram had put the letter in his lockbox and told himself every day since that tomorrow he would decide what to do with it, and every day *tomorrow* had agreed politely and failed to arrive.

Now the pressure was applied. The boy had said it himself, last night, standing at the base of the great clock with oil to his elbows and despair all over his young face: *"This mechanism resists repair."*

Those were practically her words. Odile Fen, dead eleven years, reaching out of her grave to tap a clockmaker's apprentice on the shoulder.

Bram's knees complained about the stairs but delivered him to the clock room floor regardless. He cleared his throat. The boy looked up.

"Mr. Tull. I nearly have the third wheel free. Someone filed the arbor — filed it, deliberately, and then peened the brass over the marks so it would look like wear. You'd need to know what wear looks like after thirty years to see the difference, and I know, so I see it."

"I know," Bram said.

The boy blinked. "You know it was sabotaged?"

"I know a great deal more than that." His hand had gone, without permission, to his breast pocket. "And I've been trying for eleven years to decide how much of it a stranger ought to carry, and I find I can't decide anymore, because my chest isn't large enough and my spine isn't straight enough, and you—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Odile Fen was our archivist. Forty-one years. She knew every document in Marrowgate's founding chest, including the ones that weren't supposed to exist anymore. Before she died she gave me this, with instructions. Her instructions were plain: if the clock's mechanism resisted repair — those words, near enough — then the letter went to whoever was trying to mend it."

He drew it out. The wax seal was unbroken, stamped with the fen orchid she'd used instead of arms, because archivists, she liked to say, had no family and therefore no right to heraldry. The paper had gone the color of weak tea. On its face, in her cramped upright hand: *For the one who finds what should not be found.*

Wendell set down his screwdriver with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a live coal. "What's in it?"

"If I told you, you'd have the choice of whether to believe me secondhand," Bram said. "That's a kindness to you and a cowardice in me, and I've done my share of cowardice already. Read it or don't. But if you read it, there's no putting it back. Some knowledge is like that. Like a gear tooth, once cut."

The boy took the letter. His hands were steady — clockmaker's hands, they don't shake, it's bred out of them or beaten out — but his face was doing plenty of shaking on their behalf.

"Who was she to you?" Wendell asked.

"Someone who trusted me with the heaviest thing she owned." Bram turned toward the window, because he could not watch the boy break the seal and also watch the boy's face; he lacked the constitution for both. "I'll be downstairs. There's tea, if tea helps. It doesn't, usually. But there is some."

Behind him he heard the small crack of wax giving way, and thought: *there it is, Odile. Fired true. God help us both.*

There are documents you read and documents that read you. I learned the difference at half past four on a Thursday afternoon, standing under the master clock of Marrowgate with a dead woman's letter shaking in my hands.

Her hand was beautiful — the tight upright script of someone trained to make records legible to posterity — and it began without salutation, as though she couldn't afford the politeness.

*If you are reading this, then the clock has refused you, and you are honest enough to say so rather than pretend otherwise. That is already more than can be said for most men in this town. Sit down if you can. If you cannot sit down, read standing; I wrote it standing.*

*I will tell you what the clock is hiding, and then I will tell you who you are, and you will not believe me, and then I will tell you how to prove it, and you will.*

*Marrogate was not founded by six families. It was founded by fourteen. Six held the money, four held the land grants, and four held the orchards — the hillside terraces above the town, planted in apple, quince, and medlar, tended by families whose charters predate the founders' charter by nine years. The orchard-keepers fed Marrowgate through two famine winters while the founders' granaries sat locked awaiting profitable prices. This is not legend. I have read the grain accounts. I have read everything.*

*In 1801, Alderman Josiah Hale — grandfather of the current alderman — conceived an ambition to consolidate the hill lands. The keepers' grants were inconvenient. So he had them forged away. Not stolen: forged. The founding registry in Founders' Hall is a copy, made in 1801 in an imitation of the 1768 clerk's hand, and it names no keepers at all, as though the terraces planted themselves. The original registry — with the grants, the stewardship oaths, and my own deposition, sworn before a magistrate who has since proved conveniently forgetful — I hid where no forger would ever look and no historian would ever think to search.*

*Inside the master clock. In the case, below the movement, behind the escapement mounting plate. Josiah Hale rebuilt that clock's reputation for precision so thoroughly that no one in Marrowgate would dream of disturbing it. Precision makes excellent camouflage. I chose it for that reason.*

*Now. Who you are.*

*Your name is Wendell Dravenmoor. Your grandfather was Elias Dravenmoor, fourth keeper of the High Terrace, whose grant is in that clock along with your family's name and your family's claim. When the forgeries came, Elias fought the loss through the courts for six years and lost on the strength of documents Josiah Hale's own clerk had manufactured. The family scattered. Some went to Ashbourne. Some went farther. One line kept working metal and measuring time, because — Elias wrote in the last letter I possess from him — "a man who keeps time honestly cannot be wholly dispossessed." I did not understand that sentence for forty years. Perhaps you will do better.*

*I cannot give you back what was taken. I am dead; it limits one's options. But I can tell you where the proof lies, and I can tell you this: Alderman Corvus Hale knows the clock contains something. He does not know what. If he knew, the clock would have burned years ago. Watch him. He is kind the way ice is smooth.*

*One caution, and I make it solemnly. Once you open the compartment — and you will, you are a Dravenmoor, obstinacy is in the grant somewhere — the clock becomes evidence. Every gear you touch afterward may be examined by men paid to find your fingerprints and not mine. Work cleanly. Document everything. An archivist's habit: trust nothing to memory, least of all your own innocence.*

*Yours in the record,*
*Odile Fen, Archivist of Marrowgate, 1807–1837*

I read it twice. Then a third time, standing, because my legs had opinions about chairs.

Below the escapement mounting plate. I knew exactly where that was. I had spent the previous evening lying on my back beneath the great movement with a lamp, mapping every flaw some careful vandal had introduced, and the mounting plate had seemed to me the one part of the machine in suspiciously perfect condition. Perfect the way a locked door is perfect.

*Your grandfather was Elias Dravenmoor.*

Here is the trouble with being raised an apprentice: your history is handed to you in pieces, and you're taught not to ask where the rest went. My parents were gone early — fever, I was told, the great explanatory fevers of the world — and my master took me in at ten and gave me a bench and a rule: *the work in front of you, lad, and nothing behind.* I had assumed the nothing behind was poverty. Poverty explains most absences. It had simply never occurred to me that absence could be *manufactured*, filed smooth and peened over like a sabotaged arbor so it would look like natural wear.

But it explained things. Oh, it explained things. It explained why my mother sang a planting song about terraces and quinces that no village we ever lived in grew. It explained why my father turned his face from orchards in blossom like a man passing a house where he'd once been happy. It explained the name. *Dravenmoor.* Moor and drave — drove-from-the-moor. I'd always thought it sounded invented. Families that get erased have to invent themselves somewhere else; apparently mine hadn't even bothered to invent well.

I laughed. I'm not proud of it. It came out of me like a burr from a drill, short and ugly, and it echoed in that stone room among all that patient ticking, and then I sat down on the floor of the greatest clock in the county with my back against its case and felt the ground tilt under me, slow, the way the earth tilts — imperceptibly, continuously, until one day you notice the stars have moved.

Eleven years that letter had waited in Bram Tull's lockbox. Eleven years, and it fell to me — an apprentice with forty shillings saved and a borrowed loupe — to be the pressure Odile Fen had arranged. She'd been dead longer than I'd been at the bench. She had still managed to address me by name across that distance, the way you'd address a letter to a house you'd never seen, trusting the post.

*A man who keeps time honestly cannot be wholly dispossessed.*

"Well," I said to the clock, because talking to machines is a habit I refuse to apologize for, "you and I are going to have a conversation."

The clock ticked. It had been ticking through everything — the forgery, the evictions, the famines, Odile's death, Bram's eleven years of dread, my whole small life of portable things. It struck five, one clean note rolling down the hill toward the town, and I thought: every soul in Marrowgate hears this bell and believes it tells the truth. They set their markets and their marriages and their graves by it. And bolted to its own heart was the one document that proved its town's great bell of honesty had been ringing over a lie for fifty years.

I folded the letter along Odile's creases. Carefully. Archivist's property now; I was only borrowing my own inheritance.

Then I got up, took the lamp, and knelt again below the movement, and put my eye to the mounting plate the way you'd put your ear to a door.

Bram found me there an hour later, cold tea forgotten in his hand. He looked at me a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face made him set the cup down on the floor beside us like a man laying down a weapon.

"You read it," he said.

"I read it."

"And?"

"And," I said, "someone built a false wall inside this clock, Mr. Tull, and I mean to find the door in it. But the plate is seated under the escapement, and the escapement is the one mechanism I am not yet licensed to dismantle." I heard my own voice go quiet and steady, which surprised me, because inside I was nothing but weather. "So I'll need to become licensed. Or desperate. Or both."

Bram lowered himself onto an upturned crate with the groan of a man whose joints had been keeping their own dishonest time, and rubbed his face with both hands.

"Desperate I can help with," he said. "Sit down, boy. Because if you're going to open what Odile hid, there's things you must hear first — and I'd sooner you heard them from me than from the alderman."

That is precisely, I would later reflect, the sentence I should have feared most.
