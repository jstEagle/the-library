## 6. What the Letter Named

I did not open the letter that night.

This will strike you as strange, given everything that followed, but I ask you to remember what I was: an apprentice with one good coat and a reputation still wet enough to smudge. Bram Tull had pressed the thing into my hands like a man handing over a live coal, told me it came from a dead archivist, and instructed me to open it only if the clock's mechanism resisted repair. The mechanism had, so far, resisted repair in the manner of a stubborn mule rather than a haunted one — gears gummed with old grease, a mainspring let down carelessly, nothing that a week of honest work wouldn't set right.

So I put the letter in my coat pocket, hung the coat on the back of the chair in my room above the workshop, and went to sleep as though the paper were not warm against my ribs. I have often wondered since whether sleep came to me honestly that night or whether I simply fled into it. Either way, I dreamed of my master's workshop — of small gears sorted into trays by size, each one in its place, everything measured and known — and woke at dawn with the unreasonable conviction that order was a thing you could keep, if only you kept it small enough.

* * *

The clock gave me two more days of honest lying before it confessed.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I've ever kept. On the third morning after receiving the letter, I found the third anomaly, and anomalies are how a clockmaker reads a confession.

The first anomaly I had attributed to sabotage-by-neglect: the great barrel that drives the time train had been packed with grease so old it had turned to something like amberglue, which slows a mechanism without breaking it. A clock that limps is harder to diagnose than a clock that stops. Whoever had done the work knew that.

The second anomaly was the pallet stones. Someone had replaced two of them with stones of slightly wrong angle — not wrong enough to fail inspection, wrong enough to lose four seconds a day. Four seconds a day is nothing to a farmer and everything to an observatory. Over a season, the town's one true clock would drift into gentle, deniable falsehood, and every almanac and shipping schedule built on its chimes would inherit the lie without ever seeing it enter.

The third anomaly was the one that undid me.

Deep in the going train, behind the second wheel, where no inspector looks unless he is paid to look elsewhere, I found a gear tooth filed to a peculiar profile. Not broken — *shaped*. Filed with intent, with skill, with the particular patience of someone who understood escapement geometry well enough to alter it invisibly. And stamped on the wheel's face, so faintly I had to hold it to the window light and breathe on it twice, was a maker's mark I did not recognize: a small tree with seven branches, struck inside a circle.

I sat back on my stool and looked at that mark for a long time.

Here is what I knew, and all I knew: the mark was not the observatory's, not Hale's founders' seal — I had copied that seal's engraving style for a commemorative fob the year before, and I'd know its fat laurel leaves anywhere. It was not my master's mark. It was not any mark in the pattern books I had studied. But the *work* — the cut of that tooth, the confidence of the file — the work was familiar in a way I could not name, the way a stranger's laugh can be familiar, the way you find yourself listening for the rest of a sentence you're sure you once heard.

My hands were steady. I noticed that with professional detachment, because some part of me had expected them not to be.

I climbed down from the clock's interior, washed the grease from my fingers in the basin Bram kept by the door, and walked back through Marrowgate to my room with the letter burning a hole in my coat the whole way, which is not possible, paper being paper, but is nonetheless exactly how it felt.

* * *

Bram Tull was in the observatory's lower gallery when I returned that evening, sitting in the dark with his pipe unlit, which I had learned was his way of thinking — tobacco present, fire withheld, everything suspended in a state of potential he never quite committed to. He heard my boots on the stair and said, without turning:

"You found the tooth."

"You knew about the tooth."

"I knew there was something wrong with that clock that wasn't age." He turned then, and his face in the dusk-light from the oculus looked older than it had any business looking, folded in on itself like paper left out in the rain. "I didn't know what. Odile knew. Odile knew everything, and she's dead, boy, and she left you a letter, and I'd bet my pension you haven't opened it."

"I was going to," I said. "If the repair failed."

Bram laughed, and there was nothing kind in it. "And who decides when a repair has failed? You? You'll nurse that clock for six months and call every setback instructive. That's what apprentices do. That's what I did." He tapped the unlit pipe against his knee. "The condition's been met, son. You found work in that mechanism that no wear puts there. Open the letter."

"I don't see—"

"Open the letter or I'll open it for you and read it aloud in the courtyard, and then neither of us will have a choice anymore."

There is a species of silence that follows a threat made out of desperation, and we sat in it together for perhaps a minute. Then Bram's shoulders dropped, and he became an old tired man again, and he said, much more gently:

"She made me swear. Dying women extract terrible oaths, Wendell. She said the letter goes to whoever comes to fix the clock, and it opens when they find what shouldn't be there. She said—" he stopped, worked his jaw. "She said the right hands would know. I didn't understand it then. I watched your face when you came down that ladder just now, and I understood it."

* * *

I opened the letter in my room, alone, by candlelight, because some things you do not do in front of witnesses until you know what they are.

The seal was plain wax, unmarked, cracked already with age. The paper inside had gone the soft yellow of things kept dry and dark and important. The hand was small and exact, a clerk's hand disciplined into something almost artistic, and it began without salutation, as though the writer had run out of time for courtesies and knew it:

*To the one repairing the clock —*

*If you are reading this, you have found the altered tooth, and you are therefore either the person I hoped would come or a hired instrument of the men who broke it. If the latter, burn this and save yourself the discomfort of knowledge. If the former, read on, and forgive me for what I am about to take from you, which is the comfortable version of your own life.*

*I was registrar of Marrowgate for thirty-one years. In that time I sealed, witnessed, and archived the town's founding records, and in that same time I watched those records be removed, replaced, and rewritten — quietly, legally, and with the full cooperation of every family whose name now hangs on Founders' Hall. The orchard-keepers made this valley habitable. They terraced the hills, bred the rootstock, drained the low fields, and fed the town through three failed harvests while the founders' ancestors traded in wool and speculation downriver. When the land grew valuable, the founders took it. When the keepers objected, the founders erased them. Not with violence — violence makes martyrs, and martyrs leave graves, and graves make questions. With paper. They rewrote the registry, bought the assessor, married their daughters into the magistrate's line, and within a generation the orchard-keepers had become what inconvenient people always become in official histories: laborers, tenants, ghosts.*

*I copy the original registry before each revision. There are now four versions of Marrowgate's founding, and only one is true. I have hidden the true one where the founders would never think to look, because they believe history lives in archives, and archives can be burned. History also lives in mechanisms. The original registry, the keepers' land grants, and my sworn deposition regarding the forgeries are concealed within the master clock itself, in a chamber of the maker's devising, keyed to a tooth cut to his own secret profile. You have already held that tooth. Consider whose hand cut it, and consider why the clock was given to this town at all.*

*One name more, and then I am finished. Among the families erased from the registry, listed in the grants under the marks of their orchard rows, is this one: DRAVENMOOR. Row seven, east terrace, forty trees, grant dated the founding year. Your family, clockmaker. The keepers of row seven were your people, and the man who designed this clock's hiding place signed his work with a tree of seven branches.*

*You came here to mend a machine. You will leave knowing what was done with it. I am sorry. Truth is a poor inheritance; it pays no dividend and costs everything to keep. But it is yours now, and I am dead, and there is no one else.*

*— O.F.*

* * *

I do not remember standing up. I was sitting on the chair, and then I was standing, and the letter was somehow across the room, though I don't recall throwing it, and the candle had guttered in some draught of my own making.

Dravenmoor.

Row seven, east terrace.

I want to tell you what happens to a person when the ground tilts — not metaphorically, I mean the actual sensation, the vestibular betrayal, the moment your body decides the floor has opinions. I braced my hand on the table and the table was real and solid and utterly indifferent, and I stood there in my rented room in a town I'd been in for less than a fortnight, discovering that I had been *from* it all along.

You have to understand what my history was. Orphaned early — a fire, they told me, in a river town, papers lost, everything convenient about my origins conveniently ash. Raised in my master's household as part wages and part charity, measuring my life in portable things precisely because nothing permanent had ever been offered to me. Dravenmoor was just a name I wore, the way apprentices wear coats handed down from better men. I had never asked where it came from because asking questions of that kind is like counting money you don't have — an exercise that ends only in arithmetic and grief.

And now a dead woman, in a clerk's exact hand, was telling me the name was not a coat. It was a row of forty trees on an east terrace, and it had been taken, and the taking had been signed and sealed and shelved in the very hall where Alderman Corvus Hale poured wine for promising young tradesmen.

I thought of Hale's dinner invitation. I thought of the permanent post, mentioned twice now, each time with the particular casualness of a man laying bait he expects to be taken politely. I thought of the founding mural in Founders' Hall — I'd passed it my first day, all those stern benefactors with their wool bales and their deeds — and I understood, with a lurch worse than the first one, that I had *looked* at it and seen only paint.

Then I thought of the tooth.

Seven branches. Row seven. My master — no. Not my master. The maker of the clock. Someone with my surname's blood in his hands and a file between his fingers, who had built a vault into the heart of a public clock and keyed it to his own secret geometry, and then — what? Left? Died? Been erased in his turn? Was that why the Dravenmoor name had drifted downriver, orphaned, scattered, reduced to a boy in a workshop sorting other people's gears?

I picked the letter up from where it lay. My hands were not steady anymore. I noted that too.

The candle had recovered and thrown my shadow large against the wall, and I confess I stood a while looking at it, this silhouette with its shoulders suddenly carrying weight it hadn't carried at supper. Somewhere below, Marrowgate settled into its evening noises — cart wheels, a dog, the distant clatter of the inn. An ordinary town going about an ordinary night, sitting on top of a story it had agreed, collectively and comfortably, to forget.

And underneath the town, or rather inside the town's one honest clock, was a second Marrowgate, waiting for someone with the patience to open it.

I should have been afraid. I record, honestly, that what I felt instead — for about ten seconds, before the fear arrived — was something I can only call recognition. The way you feel when you pick up a tool that fits your hand as though it were ground for it. The altered tooth, the secret profile, the maker's mark: I had looked at that work and found it *familiar*. Not similar. Familiar. As if some part of me had been apprenticed to that dead man's hand without ever meeting him.

Odile Fen had written, *forgive me for what I am about to take from you.* She needn't have worried. She hadn't taken anything. She'd given me the only thing I'd ever owned outright: a reason.

The fear arrived on schedule, as fear does. It arrived wearing Hale's charming smile and Vey's polished questions — for Vey had begun interviewing townsfolk that week, gathering material for the official history, and I had liked him, God help me, found him funny and quick, and now I wondered whether liking a man and being measured by him could happen in the same handshake. It arrived wearing the memory of Bram's face in the dark gallery, an old man's shame folded into his voice when he said *open the letter.* It arrived as arithmetic: a permanent post on one side, a dead archivist and a ruined terrace and forty trees on the other, and me, one apprentice with one good coat, expected to balance the ledger.

I sat back down at the table. I read the letter twice more, slowly, the way you'd read a difficult drawing before cutting metal. Then I took out my notebook — the small one I keep for job notes, measurements, sketches — and on the first blank page I drew, from memory, the tooth I'd found behind the second wheel. Every angle. Every radius. I marked it with the seven-branched tree.

If the registry was inside the clock, and the clock was keyed to that tooth, then my path ran directly through the hardest problem in horology, the one my master had called the wall that separates men from boys: rebuilding an escapement without destroying it.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight I had a simpler one.

Because I had been in that clock for days, and I knew its geometry better than anyone living except possibly the man who'd built it, and I could picture the space behind the second wheel, and the space was *wrong*. Too deep. Deeper than the train required. There was a chamber in there — I was suddenly certain of it, certain the way you're certain of a note that must follow the last one — and it was locked, and the lock wanted a key I would have to make with my own hands, in a town full of men who would burn the workshop down before they let me finish it.

I blew out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to Marrowgate not-knowing what I knew.

In the morning, I decided, I would walk up past the terraces. East side. Row seven.

I wanted to see what forty trees looked like when nobody was allowed to remember planting them.
