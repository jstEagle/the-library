## 7. The Empty Cottages

I did not sleep well. That is not remarkable — I have never slept well in a bed that isn't mine, and the caretaker's spare room at the observatory smelled of lamp oil and another man's decades. What was remarkable was the direction my sleeplessness took me. A sensible apprentice would have lain awake rehearsing his conversation with Alderman Hale, polishing his gratitude until it shone. Instead I lay awake thinking about sabotage.

Deliberate sabotage. Someone had bent the recoil pallets with a tool, not carelessly but carefully, the way you'd bend something if you wanted it to fail slowly enough that no one could name the hour of its death. I had turned that thought over so many times in the dark that by morning it had worn smooth, and I found I could not stay indoors with it.

So I did what any reasonable man does when a town begins to feel like a mechanism with tampered works: I went for a walk.

Bram Tull saw me off at the observatory door with a look I couldn't read. "The hill path," he said, as if I'd asked. Then, after a pause: "Mind how you go." Which is either a pleasantry or a warning depending on the mouth it comes from, and Bram's mouth did not seem built for pleasantries.

The morning was the kind that makes liars of landscapes. Mist burning off the valley, sun coming up gold over the ridge, and everywhere the orchards climbing the hillsides in long green terraces like stairs built for giants. From the observatory road Marrowgate looked like the sort of town painters come to and never leave. It was only when you got closer that you noticed the trees were untended — branches crossing and crowding each other, suckers shooting from the rootstock, grass grown to the thigh beneath them. An orchard is not a forest. It doesn't survive neglect; it just takes longer to die than a single tree would.

Somebody had planted these. Somebody had pruned them for years, and then stopped.

* * *

The first cottage stood where the path forked, and I nearly mistook it for a heap of stones. The roof had gone soft in the middle, a green shallow bowl of moss and self-seeded rowan. The door hung open on nothing — no hinges left to hold it accountable. Inside, a hearth, a shelf bracket, a square of lighter plaster on the wall where something had hung a long time.

I don't know why I said "hello" out loud. Habit, I suppose. In my master's workshop we announced ourselves before entering a room, because a startled clockmaker is a dangerous clockmaker, all elbows and loupe. The word came out absurdly loud in that empty room and came back to me without an echo, as if the cottage had swallowed it politely.

There was a garden behind it, run entirely to nettles, but the rows were still legible under the weeds. Two beds of herbs, one of roots. A woman's garden, maybe. A careful gardener's, anyway, even now.

I stood there longer than a man should stand in a stranger's ruin, and then I walked on, telling myself I was stretching my legs.

* * *

By noon I had counted seven cottages and stopped counting. They strung along the terraces like beads on a wire — some collapsed outright, others merely shuttered and patient, as though their owners had stepped out to check the weather fifty years ago and were due back any minute. In one, a child's wooden horse sat on the windowsill, its paint faded to the ghost of a dapple. In another, initials were scratched into the plaster by the hearth: *E.M. + J.M.*, inside a heart someone had drawn with more enthusiasm than skill.

Nobody had told me about these cottages. Nobody in Marrowgate had mentioned orchard-keepers at all, except obliquely, the way you mention weather that happened before your time. Vey's commission — the official history, Hale's pride project — would presumably explain them. *Agricultural laborers, long since departed for the cities,* or words to that effect. History is very good at finding tidy words for empty houses.

Here is what troubled me, professionally speaking. Orchards this size don't tend themselves, and they don't tend themselves badly for a generation and still be standing. Every terrace I passed showed evidence of real skill — drainage channels cut along the contours, stone walls laid without mortar and still true after decades of frost heave, graft lines visible on the old trees at exactly the right height. These were not laborers. These were craftsmen of the same order as clockmakers, working in a slower material. And somebody had made them stop, or made them leave, and had then made the leaving quiet.

I ate my bread and cheese sitting on a wall above the fourth terrace, looking down at the roofs of Marrowgate, small and neat below me like gears in a movement. It occurred to me that I was the only living thing on this whole hillside that wasn't a tree or a weed, and even that felt uncertain, standing as I was among all this planted intention.

That was when I saw the lintel.

* * *

You have to understand: I was not looking for it. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever registry keeps such things. I was walking down through the terraces with no plan beyond avoiding the afternoon, and the cottage appeared the way they all appeared — around a bend, half-swallowed by ivy, smaller than memory makes cottages.

But this one was different. This one had been kept. Not inhabited — I could see that immediately — but kept. The ivy had been cut back from the doorframe recently; the stubs of the stems were pale and fresh. Someone had cleared the path to the door within the last week. And above the door, carved deep into the lintel stone, letters filled with dark lichen:

**DRAVENMOOR**

My surname. My father's surname, and his father's before him, back through generations of clockmakers in a city three hundred miles from here — or so I had believed. The Dravenmoors were a workshop family. We measured ourselves in portable things: watches, regulators, tools handed down and worn to fit particular hands. I had never once heard the word *orchard* attached to us. We were people of brass and steel, of interiors. Trees were things that happened outside windows.

And yet there it was, cut into stone by a hand that had taken its time. Deep strokes. The work of someone who intended it to last.

I am not a man who feels things in his body, as a rule. I feel things in my hands — they go cold, they go clumsy — and so it was my hands that told me first. They went cold in the September sun while the rest of me stood there like an unstarted watch.

Dravenmoor. On a keeper's cottage. On a terrace of an orchard nobody tended anymore, in a town whose founding families had hired a historian to write a history that did not contain the word.

* * *

The door opened stiffly — swollen in its frame but not locked, not barred. Whoever kept the place wanted it enterable.

Inside: one room, and above it the shadow of a loft. A table. One chair, set neatly against the wall. A hearth swept clean, which struck me as strange until I understood that swept-clean is what abandonment looks like when it's done with love rather than haste. There was a tin box on the mantel. I opened it with hands that had finally remembered how to move, and found it empty — but the bottom of the tin was scratched, as if papers had lain there a long time and been taken away. Recently, I judged, by the brightness of the scratches.

On the wall beside the hearth hung a tool rack, and here I have to confess I laughed, once, short and ugly, because I didn't know what else to do with it.

Pruning shears. A grafting knife. A little saw with a curved blade. And hanging apart from the rest, worn smooth as an old prayer book, a whetstone in a leather sheath — the kind of thing a working hand reaches for daily until it fits the palm like a third finger.

It was a craftsman's rack. I knew it the way you know your own trade anywhere: the arrangement of most-used nearest the reach, the hooks sized to the tools, the little shelf beneath for oil and stone. My master's workshop was organized the same way, in a different alphabet.

Someone had worked these terraces the way I worked movements — with attention, with pride, with the daily discipline of keeping a complicated system alive. And their name was on the lintel, and their name was mine.

* * *

I sat down in the chair. I don't remember deciding to; I remember the moment my knees gave and the chair was there, which is either luck or providence or the courtesy of dead people who set a chair where a tired person would need one.

Grief is an imprecise word for what I felt, because grief requires a specific loss, a face, a date. What I felt was more like discovering a hole in a movement you've owned all your life — opening the caseback of your own history and finding an entire train of wheels missing, a whole architecture of cause and effect removed, and no record of when it was taken out or why. You can't mourn what you can't see. But you can feel its absence pulling at everything that remains, the way a missing tooth affects the whole gear train.

Who were they? When did they go? Did they go, or were they gone? Were there Dravenmoors in the ground under these terraces? Was there a Wendell before me, some orchard-keeping grandfather or great-uncle who sharpened that grafting knife every morning and never imagined his family's name would survive only because he'd cut it into a doorframe?

My father had taught me my letters on brass plates, my mother had died when I was six, and neither of them, so far as I ever knew, had ever mentioned Marrowgate. But names are stubborn. Names travel in pockets and letters and silences, and they wait.

I sat in that chair for a long time. The light moved across the floor. Somewhere below, Marrowgate rang a bell for two o'clock, and I noted — automatically, professionally, because a clockmaker cannot help himself — that it rang two minutes slow.

* * *

I heard her before I saw her: footsteps on the terrace path, quick and light, and then stopping abruptly, the way a person stops when they realize the house they're approaching has a lit window or an unexpected guest.

I stood up too fast and knocked the chair over, which is not how one wishes to meet anyone.

She was in the doorway — a young woman, roughly my age, with dirt to the elbows and an expression like a drawn bolt. She held a billhook low against her hip in a way that suggested she knew exactly what it was for and hoped I didn't.

"This cottage is closed," she said.

"It opened," I said, gesturing vaguely at myself, at the door, at the general situation. "I'm sorry. The path was clear. I assumed—"

"You assumed wrong." Her eyes went past me into the room, doing a rapid inventory: chair knocked over, tin box on the table where I'd moved it, tool rack undisturbed. Satisfied, apparently, on the last point, because the billhook lowered perhaps half an inch. "Who are you?"

"Wendell Dravenmoor."

I have played that sentence back many times since, and I want to state for the record that I said it plainly, the way one says one's name to a shopkeeper. I did not know yet what it would mean to say it on this hillside. But I watched it land on her like a dropped weight. Her face went through three expressions in the space of a breath — disbelief, calculation, and something underneath both that I couldn't name and that frightened me slightly, because it looked like hope, and I hadn't done anything to earn it.

"Say it again," she said.

"Wendell. Dravenmoor."

She looked up at the lintel, and then at me, and then at the lintel again, as though comparing two documents for signs of forgery.

"How," she said, very quietly, "can you possibly have that name?"

"I might ask the same about the carving," I said. "Though I suspect our answers would rhyme."

For a long moment neither of us moved. The wind came up the valley and moved through the unpruned trees above the cottage with a sound like a crowd murmuring before a verdict. She studied me with the thoroughness of someone deciding whether to let a stranger into a secret that had cost her family everything, and I stood there feeling measured — properly measured, not the way Hale had weighed me over dinner, but weighed against something real.

Then she stepped aside from the doorway, though she did not put the billhook down.

"Come down to the lower terrace," she said. "If you're going to carry that name here, you'd better see what it's worth."

I followed her out into the light, and behind me the door stayed open, and I remember thinking — absurdly,professionally — that whoever had carved those letters had cut them deep enough to last exactly this long, and not one day further. As if someone had known that someday, sooner or later, a Dravenmoor would come back up this hill, and the name needed only to hold until he arrived.

What I could not know — walking down through the terraces behind a girl with a billhook and a grudge — was that the letter in my coat pocket, sealed and waiting, had already begun to burn a hole through everything I believed about why I had been sent to Marrowgate at all.
