## 8. The Historian Arrives

*Lachlan Vey had a theory about small towns, and it was this: they remembered everything except the parts that mattered.*

He arrived in Marrowgate on a Tuesday in late September, in a hired carriage with two trunks of books and a third trunk of blank paper, which he considered the most honest of his luggage. The town spread below the hill road like a spilled purse — slate roofs, a market square, four church spires competing for the same sky, and beyond them all, climbing the terraced slopes in silent ranks, the orchards.

They were dying. He could see it even from the carriage window. Rows of apple trees, some of them clearly old enough to have been planted by hands now eighty years in the ground, leaning into each other like conspirators no one had bothered to hang. Half the terraces were given over to bramble and birch scrub. It was, he thought, exactly what land looked like when the people who loved it had been subtracted from it.

Alderman Hale's letter had described Marrowgate as "a town with a history worthy of the nation's attention." Vey had written six such histories. He knew precisely what that sentence meant. It meant: *write us flattering, pay us in permanence, and do not look too hard at the ledgers.*

He was very good at not looking too hard at ledgers. That was, if he were honest with himself — and he made a practice of honesty in private, as one makes a practice of flossing — the entire basis of his reputation.

The Alderman met him at the Marrowgate Arms personally, which was itself a performance worth the fare. Corvus Hale had the sort of face built for engravings: strong jaw, silver at the temples arranged as though by appointment, eyes that gave you their full attention the way a lender gives you their full attention. He clasped Vey's hand in both of his own.

"Mr. Vey. You honor us."

"History honors you, Alderman," said Vey. "I merely transcribe."

Hale laughed, a warm measured laugh, and Vey filed away the fact that the warmth came half a second after the measurement. Over dinner — lamb, wine older than the town's charter, a pudding shaped like the observatory dome, someone had actually commissioned that — Hale laid out the project. Three hundred years of Marrowgate. Founding families, civic triumphs, the great clock, the orchard industry in its golden age. Publication funded by the Hale Trust. Distribution to every school in the county.

"A town should know what it is," Hale said, refilling Vey's glass himself, an intimacy he dispensed like coin.

"And what it has been," Vey agreed. "Though those are rarely the same thing."

"Are they not?" Hale smiled. "In my experience, Mr. Vey, what a town *has been* is whatever its records say it has been. Which is why records matter so much. Which is why you matter."

*There it is,* Vey thought, and drank the excellent wine, and smiled back. Somewhere beneath his sternum, in a compartment he did not open in public, something shifted half a tooth out of true.

* * *

*They sent for me on Thursday,* Wendell Dravenmoor wrote in his pocket notebook, because writing things down was how he kept from saying them. *The historian wants to interview the clockmaker. I am apparently part of the mechanism now.*

I dressed in my second-best coat, which was my best coat minus three years of workshop grime that no amount of lye would surrender, and presented myself at the Marrowgate Arms at the appointed hour. A clerk showed me into a sitting room that had been temporarily converted into an office: papers in ordered stacks, books propped open with whalebone stays, and in the middle of it all a man who rose to greet me like we were old friends who'd merely misplaced twenty years.

"Wendell Dravenmoor! The young man who is going to make the great clock sing again. Sit down, sit down. Do you take tea? He takes tea," he told the clerk, who withdrew. "Lachlan Vey. I'm writing the town's history, and before you protest that you've only been here a month, let me assure you that clocks have longer memories than people, and you are the only man in Marrowgate currently listening to one."

Up close he was fifty-ish, well-fed without being soft, with quick clever hands that he kept busy while he talked — squaring stacks, aligning pens — the way restless people do when stillness feels like waste. His eyes did the arithmetic of my coat, my boots, my calluses, and arrived at a sum he found acceptable or at least usable.

"You flatter the clock," I said. "Half its train is seized and its escapement is a crime scene."

"A crime scene!" He laughed with genuine delight. "What a way to put it. Tell me — whose crime?"

That was the first question, and it arrived wearing a jester's cap, friendly as anything, and I felt immediately that I was being weighed on a very fine balance. I said, "Wear, mostly. Neglect. Some deliberate interference near the strike train, but that's common enough in neglected movements. Rats. Boys. Boredom."

"Mm," said Vey, and wrote something. Not much. Historians, in my limited experience, write least when they're most interested.

The tea came and he poured it himself, another intimacy, another coin. Then for an hour he asked me questions that seemed to be about nothing. Where had I trained? Who was my master? How long had the Dravenmoors been in trade? Was it a large family? Did we keep records? Were we from anywhere in particular — the west counties, perhaps?

"The west counties," I said slowly, "have a lot of families in them."

"They do indeed." He smiled into his tea. "It's a habit of mine, you understand. Context. A town's history isn't only kings and charters; it's tradesmen and their grandfathers. Where a family comes from tells you where a town went." He set down his cup. "Your master speaks highly of you, by the way. Wrote to say your escapement work was 'beyond his instruction,' which from him I understand is roughly equivalent to a papal blessing."

"He wrote to you?"

"He writes to everyone. Correspondence is the poor man's archive." He leaned back. "Now. The master clock. Walk me through it. I confess I don't understand mechanisms, but I intend to describe one in print regardless, so you may as well save the town from my ignorance."

So I talked about the great clock — the going train, the striking train, the gravity escapement that let it keep time through gales and frost — and he listened with his head tilted, interrupting only to ask questions that were sharper than he pretended. And once, when I mentioned that the strike train had been interfered with by a knowledgeable hand rather than a rodent, he asked, lightly, almost idly:

"Knowledgeable how? What kind of knowledge does it take to spoil a clock politely?"

I looked at him. He looked pleasantly back, pen poised, and I understood that this was the actual interview and everything before it had been calibration.

"It takes the knowledge of a clockmaker," I said. "Someone who understands where a movement is fragile. Sabotage by an ignorant man is loud. This was quiet. It was meant to look like wear."

"How interesting," said Vey. "And how sad, that anyone would want a town's clock to lie about the hour."

"That's not quite what sabotage does," I heard myself say. "A broken clock doesn't lie. It just stops telling the truth. There's a difference. A stopped clock can be repaired. A lie has to be found first."

His pen made a small dry sound against the page. When he glanced up there was something new in his face — not hostility, nothing so useful as hostility. Recognition, maybe. One craftsman noting another's mark on the work.

"You should write the history yourself, Mr. Dravenmoor."

"I fix things. I don't record them."

"Yes," he said softly. "Well. Sometimes fixing and recording turn out to be the same trade." Then he straightened, and the pleasant professional mask settled back into place like a case closing over a movement. "One last thing, purely administrative. The Alderman asks — and I stress this is his concern, not mine — that until publication, any unusual discoveries regarding the clock be reported to him first. For accuracy. Historical material in private hands has a way of becoming inaccurate. He'd hate for the record to be muddied by well-meaning speculation."

"I'm just repairing a clock," I said.

"Of course." He rose, shook my hand warmly, warmly, warmly. "You'll go far, young man. Everyone says so."

Everyone says so. I walked back up the hill road in the long gold light with that sentence turning over like a gear with a chipped tooth. Everyone says so, everyone expects so, the apprenticeship waits, the future is measured and approved. And underneath the approval, faint as a hairline crack across a ruby pallet stone, the sense of having been weighed by a man whose trade was subtraction — who could take a whole family off a page with a well-placed footnote and sleep soundly after, because the footnote was elegant.

At the top of the hill Bram Tull was waiting by the observatory door, twisting his cap in his hands, and his face told me before his mouth opened that something had changed.

"Lad," he said. "We need to talk. There's a letter I've been carrying. I think—" He swallowed. "I think tonight's the night it stops being mine."

Behind us, far below in the town square, the broken clock struck four — thin, wrong, half-swallowed by wind. Vey would be writing his notes by now. *A town should know what it is.* I wondered what was in the letter that made an old man look like that, and I already knew, in the way you know a gear is cracked before you find the crack, that once I read it, everyone-says-so would never fit me again.
