## 9. Gears and Ghosts

There is a rule in the clockmaker's trade, handed down from master to apprentice with all the solemnity of scripture: *never open a mechanism you have not first listened to.* My master said it a hundred times. He said it the way other men say *mind your fingers* or *don't drink the mercury*, which is to say he said it until it stopped sounding like advice and started sounding like the shape of my own thoughts.

For six nights I listened to the master clock of Marrowgate before I touched it.

Not continuously, mind you. I had a cover story to maintain, and cover stories are hungry things. By day I was the diligent young journeyman-to-be hired to repair the great clock's failing train — oiling, cleaning, replacing worn teeth, all the honest drudgery that Hale was paying for and that Bram Tull supervised with the wary attention of a man watching someone else handle his own guilt. By night, when the observatory emptied and the hill went dark and the town below became a handful of candles scattered on black cloth, I came back up through the door Bram had given me the key to, and I listened.

The clock ticked. That is what clocks do; you may quote me. But this one ticked *wrong* in ways I could not yet name, and every wrongness I found seemed less like decay and more like intention. Sabotage leaves a signature. Wear is honest — it happens where friction happens, where gravity pulls, where neglect lives. But what I'd found in the going-barrel had happened nowhere in particular, which meant it had been done by someone who understood exactly where to strike so that no single wound would look like murder. Only time would die, slowly, and everyone would shrug.

Someone had wanted this clock unreliable without wanting it obviously broken. And I had been summoned — by letter, with fees paid in advance — to make it reliable again.

Which raised the question I turned over each night as I climbed the hill: if they broke it on purpose, why hire me to fix it?

Unless the thing they wanted fixed wasn't the timekeeping at all.

* * *

On the seventh night I stopped listening and started taking the casing apart.

I want to be clear about the atmosphere of these nights, because "secretly examining a clock" sounds tidy, almost cozy, and it was neither. The observatory after midnight is a cold cathedral. The dome above the telescope room groans when the wind comes off the western hills, a sound like a giant clearing his throat in disappointment. Rats — or something rat-adjacent — conducted business in the walls with more confidence than any merchant I've ever met. And the master clock itself, four feet of oak case and brass heart, stood in its alcove like a confessor's booth, ticking out the seconds with the smugness of a witness who knows the defense has no case.

I worked by two candle stubs set behind the case so their light wouldn't show through the observatory windows. I laid my tools on a folded blanket in the exact order I removed them, because a man searching for secrets cannot afford to leave evidence of his search, and a disordered tool roll is an autobiography written in steel.

The inner casing came away in panels. I'd mapped them by day, under the innocent pretext of lubrication, so by night I knew which screws were liars — painted-over heads, screw slots filled with wax and painted brass-colored, the oldest trick in concealment and still a good one. Two panels on the rear face of the case were false-fixed. Behind the first: nothing but dust and a spider who regarded me with what I can only describe as professional respect. Behind the second—

Behind the second was a box.

Not a grand thing. A shallow compartment of sheet brass, maybe nine inches by twelve, let into the case's inner wall between the great wheel and the striking train, in a space that any honest clockmaker would swear was solid timber. It had been there a long time — the dust on its face was thick, undisturbed, the kind of dust that takes decades to accumulate and longer to forgive. Its edges were soldered to a brass frame, and the frame was pinned into the case itself, and everything about it said *built with the clock, not hidden in it.*

Which meant Odile Fen — or whoever made the compartment — had done it while the clock was being built, or during some full teardown afterward, and had then arranged matters so that no one doing routine service would ever find it. Clever. Patient. The work of someone who thought in years the way I thought in hours.

And locked. Of course locked.

I should say here that I am not a romantic about locks. A lock is just a gear that refuses to turn, and I have spent my life persuading gears. I got my picks out — yes, a clockmaker carries picks; we are one bad winter away from locksmithing, always — and I examined the lock's aperture with a jeweler's loupe and growing unease.

It wasn't a keyhole. It wasn't even a proper lock. It was a slot, precisely cut, sized for something thin and shaped — and beside it, stamped small into the brass, a number.

**47.**

Forty-seven. Not a year, I didn't think. Not a date. A count. A tooth count.

I sat back on my heels in the cold and stared at that little stamped numeral and felt the shape of a problem assemble itself in my chest with the terrible smoothness of a well-cut gear engaging.

Because I knew where you found tooth counts. You found them in the wheels of a clock train. Forty-seven teeth on a wheel was nothing unusual — except that the standard counts ran sixty-four, seventy-two, ninety-six, the comfortable divisible numbers that watchmakers use so the arithmetic of the train comes out even. Forty-seven was a prime number. Prime-numbered wheels are rare. They're custom work. They're the kind of thing a clockmaker machines once, for a specific reason, and remembers.

Somewhere in this clock — or somewhere in this clock's *design* — was a forty-seven-toothed wheel, and I suspected that if I found it, or made it, it would fit that slot and the compartment would open.

Fine. Good. Findable. Except.

Except that I spent the next hour with the loupe going over the striking train and the going train and the escapement, cataloguing every wheel I could reach, and none of them had forty-seven teeth. The escape wheel was thirty. The great wheel ninety-six. All standard. All honest.

So either the wheel was somewhere I couldn't reach —

— or the wheel didn't exist yet.

* * *

Here is where I must confess to a failure of patience, because the record — Vey's history, or mine, whoever ends up writing it — should not portray me as cooler than I was.

I tried to open the compartment anyway.

I tell myself now it was reconnaissance. It was not reconnaissance. It was three in the morning, I had been sleeping badly for a week, my hands smelled permanently of clock oil, and I had discovered that my own family name might be scratched out of this town's founding like a misspelling, and the proof of it sat eight inches from my knuckles behind a sheet of brass thinner than my conscience. I took a fine chisel to the solder joint at the compartment's lower edge. Just a test. Just to see how the metal would give.

The metal gave beautifully. That was the problem. I had the seam opened perhaps half an inch — enough to see the edge of paper inside, paper and something flat and darker, a book or board — when I heard it.

A click. Small. Above me, inside the mechanism, at the level of the escapement.

Every hair on my arms stood up and took formal notice. Because that click had not come from my chisel. It had come from within the train, and it sounded like a detent releasing — like something designed to *do* something when tampering was detected, and I do not use the word *designed* loosely, because I have since traced what that click was connected to, and I will get to that, because it is the whole reason this chapter does not end with a triumphant young clockmaker and a folder of stolen documents.

I froze. Waited. Counted a hundred ticks of the clock, which is either ten seconds or an eternity depending on your circumstances. Nothing burned, nothing sprang, nothing shattered. The clock went on ticking with the maddening serenity of a machine that knows it has out-thought you.

I eased the chisel out of the seam. Looked up into the works with the loupe.

Between the fourth wheel arbor and the frame, in a position I had examined twice already and dismissed as decorative — because it *was* decorative, a small brass rosette, the kind of flourish apprentices polish and masters sign — I now saw, by angling my candlelight just so, a hairline seam in the rosette itself. And beneath the rosette, when I pressed it very gently with a peg wood and felt with fingertips trained to detect a burr at forty diameters, a lever. A trip lever, sprung-loaded, held by a wire that ran — where? Into the frame. Along channels I couldn't see. Toward the escapement.

Toward the escapement.

Do you understand what that means? Let me explain, because it took me the rest of that night and most of the next day to understand it, and I resented every minute.

The escapement is the soul of a clock. Every other part merely transmits force; the escapement *decides*. It metes out the falling weight in equal sips, tick and tock, and it is the most delicate assembly in the whole instrument — pallets adjusted to tolerances you could lose in a fingerprint. If you opened that compartment with force, if you pried or sheared or melted your way in, the trip lever would release, and whatever it actuated would actuate *at the escapement*. And there are only so many things a trip can do to an escapement. It can jam it. It can snap the pallets. It can drop a wedge between the escape wheel and its arbor and destroy the entire assembly beyond repair — beyond repair, at least, by anyone short of a master, and rebuilding it would mean tearing down half the train.

Odile Fen — I was certain it was Fen by then; the hand-stamped numeral had the same slightly drunken baseline as her signature on the letter Bram had given me, still unopened in my coat pocket, still burning there like a coal — Odile Fen had built a trap. Not against thieves. Against *impatience.* Against anyone who wanted the registry enough to break the clock to reach it.

She had made the truth cost exactly what she chose. And what she'd chosen was this: you may have what's inside, but only through the mechanism, only properly, only by understanding the clock as thoroughly as she did. Break it and you lose both the records and the clock that hid them. She hadn't hidden the documents in the case. She'd hidden them in the *craft.*

I put my chisel down and did not pick it up again that night.

* * *

I reseated the panel. I swept the floor where brass filings had fallen — filed under *things Hale's people might notice*, along with tool marks, disturbed dust, and my own heartbeat. Then I sat on my bed in the caretaker's attic room where Bram lodged me and I thought about patience.

Patience is a workshop virtue. My master drummed it into us: *the file cuts in one direction only, Wendell, and so does regret.* I could wait out a difficult fit. I could spend three days truing a wheel that a lesser man would force. Patience with mechanisms is easy for me, honestly; gears don't lie to you, don't flatter you, don't offer you permanent posts in voices warm as mulled wine. A gear wants only what it wants and tells you plainly whether you've given it.

But patience with *people* — patience while Alderman Corvus Hale smiled at me across his breakfast table and asked, solicitously, how the great work came along; patience while Bram Tull moved through the observatory like a man carrying a full cup of something scalding; patience while I oiled the sabotaged bearings by day knowing they were sabotaged, saying nothing, fixing them quietly as instructed, being *useful* to the men who had broken them — that patience had a shelf life, and I could hear it expiring.

Because here is the arithmetic that kept me awake. The compartment could not be forced. Fine. Could it be opened *properly*? The lock keyed to a forty-seven-tooth wheel — a wheel that did not exist in the current train. Which meant that at some point, either the original design included such a wheel (and it had been removed), or Fen intended the opener to *make* one. To machine a prime-counted wheel to the tolerance that slot demanded, fit it to some arbor I hadn't yet identified, wind the train to the right position, and present the tooth to the slot like a key presented to a lock.

I lay in the dark doing the kind of arithmetic other men do with drink. A forty-seven-tooth wheel, if it once existed, would have been removed by whoever sabotaged the clock — by Hale's order, presumably, by Bram's reluctant hands, though Bram didn't know that yet and wouldn't confess it to me for days. Or it had never existed, and Fen expected a clockmaker of sufficient skill to design and cut one from scratch. Either way, the message was identical:

*You want your family's name back? Build it. Tooth by tooth. No shortcuts. Shortcuts are what got your name erased in the first place.*

I'm paraphrasing. Dead archivists are pushy.

And beneath all of this, quieter, worse: I did not know what opening the compartment would *cost*. Fen had trapped it. What else had she trapped? I kept seeing that half-inch of exposed paper, that flat dark object — a ledger? a register? — and I kept feeling the click travel up the chisel into my bones. Some part of me, the part my master would have called the good part and I call the inconvenient part, whispered that maybe the answer wasn't to beat Fen's puzzle but to understand why a woman would spend her last years engineering a box that punished greed and rewarded craft. Maybe she wasn't testing whether I was clever. Maybe she was testing whether I was *careful.* Whether I loved the clock as much as the secret. Whether I could be trusted with a truth that had already destroyed one family — mine — if I came at it with crowbars and entitlement.

Or maybe, said the other part, the part that had watched Hale's charming mouth promise me a future, maybe you're romanticizing a dead woman's paranoia, Wendell, and meanwhile the historian Vey arrives within the fortnight, and Hale wants his clock running sweetly and his history printed and his orchard empty, and every night you spend communing with a brass box is a night the machinery downstairs grinds another turn toward finishing all of that without you.

Both parts were right. That's the trouble with parts; like a good train, they all mesh, even the ones grinding.

* * *

The next evening Bram found me in the clock room, ostensibly checking my progress. Ostensibly. He looked at the reassembled casing, then at me, and his eyes did the thing I'd begun to recognize — flicking to my tool roll, counting the tools, the way a man counts spoons after a guest leaves.

"Young Dravenmoor," he said. "You work late."

"The clock doesn't," I said. "Seems rude to abandon it."

He almost smiled. Almost. His hands, folded on his stomach, tightened on each other like two men gripping wrists in a flood.

"There's a woman," he said, and stopped. Started again. "Up on the terraces. There's a woman goes up to the old orchard, evenings. Tends it. She shouldn't — there are orders — and you shouldn't be seen speaking with her, and I shouldn't be telling you, and God help me I don't know why I am."

I looked at him. Old man, guilty hands, eyes wet at the rims with something he'd been carrying so long it had worn a groove in him.

"Why *are* you telling me?"

Bram studied the clock as if it might answer for him. Tick, said the clock. Tock.

"Because she's up there alone," he said finally, "and because you keep looking out that window at the terraces like a man reading a letter he hasn't opened yet. And because—" He stopped himself hard, jaw working, and whatever the third reason was, he swallowed it whole. "Mind the frost on the path," he said, and left me alone with the clock and the ghosts and the window.

I looked out at the terraces. The last light was coming off the western hills, and the abandoned orchard stood in blue shadow, rows of leafless trees like a congregation that had outlived its faith — except that in the middle terrace, where three nights ago I had noticed the pruning cuts, clean and recent and defiant, there was a small figure moving between the trees with a lantern.

Someone was keeping the orchard.

Someone, in defiance of eviction orders and the town's whole polished official memory, went up every evening to trees whose keepers had been erased from the founding registry — erased, possibly, along with a family named Dravenmoor — and tended them anyway.

My hand went, without permission, to my coat pocket, where Odile Fen's sealed letter waited. My other hand rested on the cold oak of the clock case, behind which a brass compartment held whatever truth Hale had spent years strangling, locked to a gear that did not exist, guarded by an escapement I could not yet rebuild.

Patience, my master used to say, is just anger wearing respectable clothes.

Mine had never dressed so well as it did that night — nor strained so badly at the seams. I took my coat down off its hook, and I went out into the frost, down the hill road, toward the lantern moving among the bare trees.
