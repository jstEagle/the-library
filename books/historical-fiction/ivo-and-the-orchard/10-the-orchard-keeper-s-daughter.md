## 10. The Orchard Keeper's Daughter

The first thing you learn as an orchard keeper is that most of the work is invisible. Nobody sees you wrapping the grafts in October or dragging brush away from the trunks so the winter mice don't girdle the bark. Nobody sees you at all, if you're doing it right, and I had gotten very good at doing it right. Three years alone on these terraces and the town below had settled into a comfortable agreement with itself: the orchard was dead, the Ashcombe girl was a stubborn ghost haunting a hillside, and ghosts were best left unmentioned over supper.

I was fine with that. Ghosts get the best working hours.

So when I came around the bend of the third terrace that morning — pruning knife in my apron pocket, a sack of ash for the plum roots over my shoulder — and found a stranger standing under the oldest Baldwin apple with his head tipped back like the tree owed him money, I did what any sensible keeper of stolen ground would do.

I put down the ash sack quietly and picked up a limb.

"Excuse me," he said, without turning around. He had a voice like a man reading instructions aloud — careful, a bit dry, faintly surprised to be using it. "Is this tree yours?"

"It's mine to worry about," I said. "That's different from mine."

He turned then. He was young — younger than me by a year, maybe two, though he carried himself like something older that had been wound too tight at the factory. City hands, soft-looking at first glance, but there were calluses on them arranged in a way I recognized: file-wear, lathe-wear, the particular shiny patch on the thumb and forefinger that comes from holding small things very still for a very long time. A clockworker's hands. I'd seen them once on my grandmother's watchmaker, before we could no longer afford watches.

"You're not from the Alderman's office," he said.

"No," I said. "Are you?"

"No. I'm—" He stopped himself, which I found interesting. Most people fill silence with their whole biography. This one rationed it. "Wendell Dravenmoor. I've been engaged to repair the observatory clock."

"Engaged," I said. "By whom?"

"The town. By Alderman Hale."

"Mm." I let the limb rest across my palm, not pointed at him, but present. "Then you work for the man who sent men to nail eviction notices to my door twice last spring."

Something moved behind his face — not guilt exactly, since he had nothing yet to feel guilty about, but the faster cousin of guilt, the one that arrives ahead of it to look around. "I didn't know anyone still lived up here."

"I don't live here," I said. "That's rather the point. According to the town, nobody has lived here for thirty years, and everything you see grew wild out of sheer spite." I gestured with the limb at the terrace around us: the rows kept hoed, the grass scythed short between the trees, the windfall apples collected in a pile by the wall to be culled and carried down to whoever in Marrowgate would take fruit from a ghost without asking where it came from. "Remarkable spite, isn't it."

He looked. That was the thing about him I noticed second, after the hands. He looked properly, the way a person looks at a mechanism — following each part to whatever part drives it. His eyes went from the hoed rows to the scythe leaning against the wall, to the grease-tin of grafting wax on the fencepost, to me, and I watched him do the arithmetic. Kept ground doesn't happen. Someone keeps it.

"You've been tending this alone?" he said.

"For three years. My grandmother before me for forty, though she was officially dead for the last twelve of those, so perhaps I'm misremembering."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being erased: it doesn't feel like nothing. It feels like being made of paper in a town full of matches. Every person who looks through you, every clerk who says *no Ashcombe on the rolls, miss*, every cart driver who won't stop for you on the hill road because stopping for a ghost invites questions — each one is small, and each one teaches you the same lesson, which is that the truth of a thing lives wherever the record says it does, and the record can be carried off and rewritten while you sleep.

My grandmother taught me the other lesson, the better one, on her knees in the dirt of the fourth terrace with her fingers around a graft she was too old to be making. She said: *Nell, they can take the paper. Paper isn't where we live.* And then she showed me the ledger — hers, and her mother's before her, going back past the founding of the town itself, row after row of planting dates and frost records and which rootstock took and which died, written in four different hands because keeping a living record is work and work needs many hands.

*They can burn the orchard,* she said, *but every tree in it is an entry, and entries have a way of getting read.*

She died believing that. I spend most days believing it and some days just carrying it, the way you carry a heavy thing downhill — not because you want it at the bottom but because putting it down would break it.

All of which is to say: I did not trust strangers, and I especially did not trust polite young strangers with clockworker's hands who appeared under my oldest tree within a month of the second eviction notice and looked at my kept ground with a mechanic's eyes, doing arithmetic.

"So," I said, when we had stood there long enough for the silence to acquire texture. "The observatory clock. What's wrong with it?"

"It was broken on purpose," he said.

I'll grant him this: he didn't dress it up. He said it flatly, watching me the way I'd watch a graft for signs of taking — not wanting to hope too early.

"On purpose," I repeated.

"Some of it. Wear explains some, but not a file mark across the great wheel's teeth, and not oil that sets like glue instead of lubricating. Somebody wanted the clock to fail slowly, in a way that looked natural." He paused. "You don't seem surprised."

"I'm not." I picked up the ash sack again, mostly so my hands would have employment. "When your family spends thirty years being subtracted from a town, you develop opinions about how the subtraction got done. It doesn't happen with fire and torches anymore. It happens with clerks and ledgers and quiet arrangements, and apparently with clocks." I started up the row toward the next tree. He fell in beside me, which I permitted because a man following me openly is easier than a man I lose sight of.

"You think the clock matters," he said. "To the records."

"I think everything in Marrowgate that's true got hidden somewhere, and hiding places are usually clever ones, and clever is a clockmaker's whole trade. But that's a girl's guess, and girls' guesses carry no weight at law. You'd need paper. Sworn, sealed, dated paper."

He went quiet at that. Quiet in a specific way — I'd learn later it was the way he went quiet around anything he couldn't take apart yet — but that morning all I knew was that I'd said something that landed somewhere in him and he needed a moment to stand next to it.

We pruned. Or rather, I pruned and he watched, and then he asked if he might try, and I handed him the knife expecting butchery, and he didn't butcher. His cuts were clean, angled right, made with the same economy I imagined he used on gear teeth. He held the branch the way you hold something you respect: firmly enough to work, gently enough not to bruise.

"Who taught you that?" I asked, before I could decide not to.

"My master. He says a cut is a promise to the tree — you're telling it where to spend its strength." He studied the whorl of bark at the joint. "This orchard was planted by someone who understood trees the way I understand clocks. Look at the spacing. Whoever laid out these terraces was calculating sun and drainage decades ahead. That's not farming. That's engineering."

"That was the keepers," I said. The word came out of me with more weight than I intended, the way it always does. "Orchard-keepers. Before the founders' families decided the hill was theirs and the people who made it bloom were weather."

"The keepers." He said it carefully, testing the word. "There's no mention of—"

"There's no mention of anything. That's the entire point." I stepped back from the tree and looked at him straight on. He had a good face, I suppose, if you go in for faces — narrow, serious, with the kind of eyes that make you think he'd be terrible at lying and dangerous at persisting. "Mr. Dravenmoor. Why are you really up here? Not why did you walk. Why did you come looking? Because men like you don't wander. You're summoned or you follow a question, and you told me already you weren't summoned."

He set the knife down on the wall, precisely parallel to its edge, the way a person does when their hands need order. "Because my family name is in the town's founding registry," he said, "and also not in it. There are two versions of everything in Marrowgate, Miss Ashcombe, and I've only recently learned I exist in the wrong one. My grandfather appears in land records as an orchard-keeper. In the official histories, the Dravenmoors were never here at all."

The hillside was very quiet. Late autumn quiet, the leaves mostly down, the wind doing its low work in the grass. I heard my own pulse, which annoyed me.

"Dravenmoor," I said.

"You know the name."

"It's carved on a lintel on the fifth terrace." I watched him absorb that. "Cottage with the collapsed east wall, the one with the pear trees gone feral around it. My grandmother pointed it out to me the day I was tall enough to see over the wall. She said a Dravenmoor built that house, and a Dravenmoor grafted half the pears on this hill, and if I ever met a Dravenmoor I should mind my manners and mind my ledger both, because families that got erased usually got company coming sooner or later."

He looked toward the upper terraces, where the cottage roofline sagged against the sky. When he spoke again his voice had lost its instruction-reading steadiness. "She said that? Your grandmother said to expect—"

"She said history leaks. Paper burns, memories die, but stone holds its carvings longer than powerful men hold their patience." I retrieved my knife and closed it. "And she said most leaks stay plugged. So don't go getting hopeful. Hope is expensive up here."

"I'm beginning to understand that." He hesitated, and then did the thing that unsettled me more than anything else that morning: he told me the truth without being asked for it. "There's a letter. From the archivist — Odile Fen, she was, before she died. Bram Tull at the observatory is holding it for me. It names my family among the erased, and it says—" He glanced up the hill again, toward the observatory's dome catching the pale light. "It says the original registry is inside the master clock. Hidden in the mechanism."

I stood very still. Somewhere below us a cart went along the hill road, wheels complaining, and the sound seemed to belong to another country.

Odile Fen. My grandmother had spoken that name maybe six times in my life, always quietly, the way you speak of someone whose mail gets read. Odile Fen knows, she'd say. Odile Fen remembers what she catalogs. And once, the winter before she died, gripping my wrist harder than her strength should have allowed: *If ever the clock speaks truly again, Nell, the Fen woman will have had a hand in it.*

"And you're telling me this," I said slowly, "why? You've known me for the length of one pruning lesson. For all you know I'm mad, or a trespasser, or exactly the kind of person the Alderman pays to find loose threads and pull them."

"Are you?"

"No. But you didn't know that ten minutes ago."

"No," he agreed. He looked down at his hands, then at the terrace, the hoed rows, the evidence of years of solitary care that he had read correctly in under a minute. "No, I didn't. But you're keeping this orchard alive knowing the town will evict you for it, with no reward except the orchard. I know what that is. I've been doing a smaller version of it my whole life without knowing it — keeping time for a trade that turns out to have been keeping something else entirely." He met my eyes. "I don't trust easily either, Miss Ashcombe. But I recognize the vigil."

There it was, then. Wary recognition between two people keeping the same watch, as my grandmother would have put it, and she'd have added that such recognition is worth more than trust because trust can be misplaced but recognition rarely lies.

I didn't say anything warm. I want that recorded — in whatever ledger counts such things, let it be written that Nell Ashcombe did not melt, did not confide, did not hand her heart to a stranger with clean cuts and honest eyes. What I said was:

"If you open that clock and the papers aren't there, you leave this hill and forget my face. If they are there, you come to me before you go to anyone else — anyone — because the moment the Alderman smells what you've found, every document in this valley will grow legs. Do we understand each other?"

"We understand each other," he said.

"Then finish learning that cut. You'll pinch the branch if you hold it like that, and pinched branches remember."

He finished learning the cut. We worked the rest of the morning in a silence that wasn't companionable yet but wasn't hostile either — a negotiated silence, a border drawn and respected. When the light began to slant he gathered his coat and started down toward the road, and I stood at the wall watching until he was out of sight, telling myself the tight feeling under my ribs was just the cold.

It was not the cold. It was the other thing, the thing my grandmother warned me about even as she taught me to hope for it: the feeling of the story starting to move, the gears beginning to turn after thirty years of a held breath.

I went back to my trees. I worked until dark. And when I finally climbed to my cottage and lit the lamp and opened the ledger to make the day's entry — *pruned third terrace Baldwin; met a Dravenmoor; he cuts clean; God help us both* — I heard, faint across the dark hillside, the observatory bell strike the hour.

It struck eleven times, at eleven o'clock, which was wrong, and had been wrong for years, wrong in exactly the way a lie is wrong: confidently, and on schedule.

I sat with my pen above the page and thought: *somebody filed those teeth, and somebody paid for it, and now a Dravenmoor is climbing toward the truth with nothing but a letter and his grandfather's name.*

I blew out the lamp and lay awake listening to the false chimes fade, and I made myself a quiet promise about padlocks and ledgers and which of my grandmother's hiding places I'd need to check at first light.

Because if the original registry was in that clock, then the Alderman didn't send a repairman up the hill road.

He'd sent an archaeologist into his own buried crime.
