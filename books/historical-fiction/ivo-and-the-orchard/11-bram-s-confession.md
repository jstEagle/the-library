## 11. Bram's Confession

I have kept the observatory for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned that a building talks to you if you listen long enough. The stones settle. The roof ticks in the cold. The great clock breathes through the tower like an old man asleep. Most nights I find the company of the building preferable to the company of my own thoughts, which are poor lodgers — they arrive uninvited, they break things, and they never pay.

The boy Dravenmoor had been at the observatory nine days when I decided to tell him. Or rather, when I stopped deciding not to. There is a difference, though it took me most of those nine days to understand it.

He worked the way his grandfather must have worked — and yes, I will write that plainly now, since I am writing any of it at all: *his grandfather*. Odile Fen told me once that the truth is easiest to say the second time. She was wrong. It is easiest to say the hundredth time, and I had only ever gotten as far as saying it to nobody, in this room, after the lamps were out. Saying it to another living person was a different animal entirely, and it had teeth.

But I am ahead of myself. Old men narrate the way old clocks run — with drift. Let me wind back.

* * *

On the ninth day I brought him tea at the hour I always do, four o'clock, because a man who works gears needs something warm in his hands by four or his fingers betray him by six. Wendell Dravenmoor was lying on the platform beneath the great clock's movement, half inside it the way a surgeon lies inside a chest, with a lamp wedged between two beams and a mirror angled to catch the light off the recoil pallets. He did not come out when I set the tray down. He said, "Thank you," to my boots, which was more courtesy than most people show my boots.

"You've been at the escapement all week," I said.

"It's wrong," came the voice from inside the machine. "Everything else is merely dirty. This is wrong."

My hands went still around the teapot. "Wrong how?"

He emerged then, blinking, with grease on his cheekbone and that expression young craftsmen get when they have found something broken that was broken on purpose. I have seen that expression perhaps five times in my life, and every time it has preceded trouble.

"Someone filed the locking faces," he said. "Not carelessly. Carefully. A worn pallet fails gradually, over years, and it fails in a pattern you can read. These fail all at once, and they were made to fail soon after whoever did the filing left." He looked up at me. "Bram. Who would sabotage a clock?"

"A person who wanted it slow," I said.

"Nobody wants a clock slow."

"Somebody does," I said, "if the clock is the town's authority on time, and somebody else wants to be the authority instead."

He stared at me, and I saw him turning that sentence over, looking for the joke in it, finding none, and becoming appropriately frightened. Then he asked the question I had been dreading for nine days, asked it mildly, the way you'd ask whether I took sugar.

"Did you know?"

And there it was. The moment. I had rehearsed it, God help me, I had rehearsed it in the bath and on the stair and once out loud to the cat, who had regarded me with the flat contempt cats reserve for men confessing to furniture. And now it stood in front of me wearing a boy's greasy face, and I found that all my rehearsal amounted to nothing, because I opened my mouth and what came out was:

"Sit down, lad. It's a long story, and I've only ever told it to the cat."

* * *

He sat. He even drank his tea. Young people have a talent for accepting enormous statements as though they were ordinary ones, which is either their great virtue or the reason civilization keeps lurching into ditches; I have never decided.

"I was forty-one," I began, and then stopped, because that wasn't where it started. Where it started was earlier, so I wound back again, and this time I made myself keep the hand steady.

"When Marrowgate built this observatory — before your time, before mine — the town had three claims to importance. The river trade, the orchards, and the stars. Two of those were taken away from the people who made them, and one was kept, and I have spent thirty-one years keeping the one that was kept, and polishing it, and winding it, and telling myself that tending a thing well makes up for how it was gotten."

"The orchards," Wendell said quietly. Not a question.

"The orchards." I looked at my hands. A caretaker's hands. Clean nails, steady grip, the hands of a man who has done no harm with them in decades, which is precisely the point I want to make about hands: they remember nothing. That is their trick. They let the rest of us off.

"The founders' families — the Hales, the Marchmonts, the Vessons — they didn't build this town. Nobody builds a town. Towns are grown, slowly, by people nobody writes songs about. The terraces on the west hills were planted and grafted and pruned and defended through forty hard winters by maybe thirty families of orchard-keepers. Ashcombes. Fens. Dravenmoors." I said the last name and watched it land on him like a dropped wrench. He didn't speak. I hadn't expected him to. "They held the land under the old grants, written in the founding registry, sealed with the first magistrate's mark. And then the river trade made the founders rich, and rich men look at hillside terraces and see not trees but the absence of profit, and they wanted the land for wool, and then for quarrying, and they could not simply take it, because the registry existed, and the registry was kept here, in this tower, under this clock."

"Under it?" His eyes went to the great movement above us, its brass wheels hanging in the dark like a suspended forest.

"In it," I said. "But I'm coming to that. Patience. If you work on clocks you must have some."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. It was a thin, strained thing, and I deserved thinner.

"Odile Fen was the town archivist. Grandniece to one of the keeper families herself. She was the one who noticed, twenty-odd years ago, that pages were going missing from the archive. Not torn — that would leave a ghost, a stub, evidence. Removed and replaced with facsimiles. Beautifully done. New parchment aged with tea and smoke, matched ink, forged hands. Someone with money and patience was rewriting Marrowgate's founding, page by page, grant by grant, until the orchard families were tenants on land they'd been granted outright, and then squatters, and then evictable. And she could prove none of it, because proving it required the originals, and the originals were the things being eaten."

"So she hid them," Wendell said.

"She was married to a clockmaker," I said, "before he died. A good one. Better than me, and I'll thank you not to repeat that. Between the two of them they worked out that the one place in Marrowgate no one would ever open was the master clock itself. Everyone looks at a clock. No one takes one apart. It runs, therefore it is understood — that is the great lie of mechanisms, and men like Hale believe it absolutely." I paused. "Corvus Hale's grandfather, that is. Our Alderman inherits his appetites along with his warehouses."

"And you," Wendell said. His voice had gone very level, the way voices go when a man is holding something heavy and doesn't dare shift his grip. "Where were you in this story?"

And this was the part. Thirty-one years, and this was the part.

* * *

I was the man who opened the clock.

I was young then — younger than he is now, if you can believe it, though I wore my youth like a borrowed coat even then, ill-fitting and anxious. I had come up from the low country with a journeyman's certificate and a head full of admiration for precision, and the observatory needed a caretaker who understood the great movement, and I was flattered to be asked. Do you see? It begins with flattery. Every rotten thing I have ever done began with someone important telling me I was skilled. Skill is a lever, and powerful men are always looking for long ones.

"Hale's father came to me in the winter of my third year," I told Wendell. "Not Corvus — the old man, God rot him. He said the master clock was drifting. Losing time. He said it was embarrassing the town before the astronomical society, and would I undertake a thorough overhaul. And I did what any proud fool would do: I stripped the movement nearly to the frame to find the fault. And while it lay open on the platform, two of his men came up the tower stairs carrying a case of tools, and one of them knew movements, Wendell. He knew them well. He made adjustments I didn't understand at the time — small alterations to the pallets, a substitution in the count wheel, and a compartment built into the inner casing where the original registry panels had lain flat against the frame since the Fen widow put them there. They emptied it. Took everything. And then they rebuilt it as a false chamber with a lock keyed to a single gear tooth, and they filed the escapement so the whole train would begin failing within the year, so that the clock's decline would be blamed on age, and any future repairman would find exactly what you found last week — wear, honest wear, nothing worth looking past."

Wendell had gone pale under his freckles. "You watched them do this."

"I helped," I said, and the word came out of me like a tooth coming out, roots and all. "That is the whole of my confession, boy, so mark it well: I did not stand aside. I held parts for them. I advised on the escapement alteration, because the other man's plan would have damaged the train permanently and mine degraded it gracefully, and God forgive me, I wanted it graceful. I wanted the lie to run smoothly. I told myself the registry was probably copies anyway, that Odile Fen was a hysterical woman making mountains out of misfiled paper, that Hale's father knew best, that a clock that ran true was worth more to Marrowgate than whatever quarrels dead men had about hillside boundaries. I was paid a year's wages in advance. I bought a coat." My voice cracked on the coat, which was absurd, and I pressed on before it could get worse. "Within eighteen months the evictions began, because the grants could not be produced. Families that had pruned those terraces for three generations were turned out with a paper that cited law at them. Some went to the low towns. Some went overseas. Some just — thinned out, the way small communities do, quietly, like a candle guttering. And the clock slowed, month by month, exactly as designed, exactly as graceful, and everyone in Marrowgate said what a pity it was that such a fine instrument couldn't hold its time anymore, and I said it too. I said it loudest."

The tower was very quiet. Above us the great pendulum swung on, indifferent, keeping the corrupted time I had taught it.

"Why?" Wendell asked at last. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — genuinely asking. "Why tell me this now? Why give me the letter at all? You could have let me fix the pallets and go home."

"Because Odile Fen came back," I said.

* * *

"She came back eleven years ago," I said. "Old, widowed twice over, ill with the lung thing that eventually killed her. And she came to the observatory — walked up that hill on legs that could barely manage it — and she sat where you're sitting, and she did not accuse me. That's what undid me, lad. I had spent twenty years braced for accusation, and she offered none. She said, 'Bram Tull, I know what you did, because I know what was in my husband's design and what isn't, and I have spent twenty years watching that clock run a lie.' And then she asked me for tea."

He laughed despite himself, a short unhappy sound, and I nodded.

"That was Odile entire. She'd have made a fine general; she understood that the enemy's shame does your fighting for you. We talked for four hours. She showed me her copies — she had transcribed the registry before hiding it, every panel, in her own hand, witnessed and sworn. It wasn't the original, but it was testimony, and testimony has a way of gathering weight if it survives long enough. And she made me a bargain. Not forgiveness — she was explicit about that; she said, 'I don't forgive you, Mr. Tull, forgiveness is above my station' — but a task. She said the false compartment was still there, empty, and that emptiness was worse than theft, because theft can be repaid in kind but an empty chamber proves nothing. She believed the originals still existed somewhere — Hale's family would never destroy them, she said; men like that keep trophies and insurance both — but belief wasn't evidence. What she needed was the mechanism itself restored to truth. Rebuilt so honestly that opening the chamber could be done openly, in daylight, by a craftsman with clean hands, so that whatever was found in it — or proved missing from it — would be found by someone blameless."

"And she wrote the letter," Wendell said.

"She wrote the letter that night, at my table, and sealed it, and gave it to me with instructions: the next competent repairman who found the sabotage and seemed likely to ask questions — not the ones Hale sends, mind, she was specific about that, she said I'd know the difference — that man should receive it. And then she went back down the hill and died within the year, and I put the letter in the iron box behind the coal store, and I waited." I spread my hands. "Eleven years, I waited. Two repairmen came in that time. Both sent by Hale, both incurious, both gone in a week. And then a letter arrived from a workshop in the low country saying Master Aldous Dravenmoor's apprentice was being dispatched, and I read that name — Dravenmoor — and I sat down on the stairs like an old sack, because either God has a sense of humor or history does, and I no longer distinguish."

"Does my master know?" Wendell asked. "Aldous — is he—"

"No," I said. "No kin, or distant ones at best; the family scattered too wide for anyone to trace it cleanly, which is rather the point of what was done to it. Your master is an honest tradesman who got an honest commission through channels Hale arranged without quite knowing why the name pleased him. Fate is lazy, Wendell. It uses whatever's lying about." I leaned forward. "But you — you found the filing in four days. You asked questions in nine. You have the hands and the stubbornness, and apparently the blood, and last night you asked me who would sabotage a clock, and I thought: Odile, you patient, unforgiving woman. Here he is."

He was quiet a long while. The pendulum counted out the silence in seconds, sixty to the minute, the wrong sixty, the lie I wound every Sunday.

"What do you want from me?" he finally said.

"Nothing," I said, which was true, and then amended it, because it was also cowardly: "No. That's a lie, and you've had one lie from me today already, delivered as a life story. I want what Odile wanted. I want that compartment opened by hands that haven't signed anything, and I want the truth of it laid before this town while there are still people alive who remember the orchards. I'm sixty-eight. Bram Tull's testimony will weigh an ounce in any court against Corvus Hale's word, and I know it, and I'm giving it to you anyway, because a confession that changes nothing is still heavier than the one you swallow." I looked at my tea, gone cold, and laughed at myself, briefly and without much humor. "Thirty-one years I called that letter penance. It wasn't. Penance would have been walking down to Founders' Hall and putting it in Corvus Hale's face. Giving it to you is hope, and hope is cheaper. But it's what I have, boy, and it's yours, along with everything I know about that movement — which is considerable, since I helped make it dishonest and have spent three decades studying exactly how."

Wendell set down his cup. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way it had been careful all afternoon, but underneath the care there was something moving, something I recognized because I had heard it in my own chest thirty years ago before I strangled it: a young man discovering that the world is worse than he thought and deciding, dangerously, that it ought not to be.

"There's a feast at Founders' Hall tomorrow," he said. "Hale's. For the historian — Vey. I've been invited. Hale intends to present his draft history of the town."

I felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does when a piece you thought was seated turns out to be loose. "Vey," I said. "The one writing the official history."

"He's charming," Wendell said. "And he asks questions like a watchmaker — small, precise, and always aimed at the spring." He stood, wiping his hands on a rag, and looked up at the great dark movement with an expression I did not like at all, because it was the expression of a man about to walk into a room full of wolves wearing mutton as a coat. "If Vey's history is being drafted now, Bram, then the record is being finished right now. Whatever we're going to do, we may not have the leisure of your eleven years."

I looked at the boy, and at the clock, and at my own reflection dim in the case glass — an old man who had polished a lie for three decades and called it stewardship — and I reached for my stick, because it appeared I was going down the hill to Founders' Hall after all, and my knees would have opinions about it.

"Well then," I said. "It seems penance keeps office hours after all. Help me with my coat, lad. And bring the letter."
