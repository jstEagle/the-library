## 12. The Founders' Feast

The invitation arrived on cream paper with a wax seal, which in my experience is how trouble dresses itself when it wants to be liked.

*Mr. Wendell Dravenmoor is requested at the Founders' Feast, Alderman Corvus Hale presiding, in celebration of the forthcoming history of Marrowgate.*

I turned it over twice looking for the part where it said what I owed for attending. There wasn't one. That was the part that worried me. In my master's workshop we had a rule about free things: a watch given away always comes back for repairs, and the repairs are never free. Hale was giving me an evening of candlelight and roast fowl, and I had been in Marrowgate long enough by then to know that everything the alderman gave away came back for repairs.

"You'll go," Bram said, not looking up from the fire he was poking as though it had personally offended him. He'd been poking it for an hour. It's hard work, pretending to be busy.

"Wasn't planning otherwise."

"Good." Poke. "And you'll keep your mouth shut."

"That's two instructions."

"They come as a set." He set the poker down and looked at me with those milky, all-seeing eyes of his. "There'll be food you've never tasted and wine that costs more than your tools, and every man in that room will be watching to see what sort of apprentice Corvus Hale has collected. Smile. Eat. If anyone asks about the clock, tell them it's coming along beautifully and leave out everything true."

"And if someone asks about the orchard?"

The poker went back into his hand. "Then you've learned more than I have about surviving this town."

He said it lightly, but there was a stone under the lightness, and I could hear it grinding.

* * *

Founders' Hall had been dressed for the evening like a woman of certain years dressed for church — powder over the cracks, flowers over the smell. Garlands of late apples and pears hung along the banisters, which struck me as either sincere or obscene, depending on who you asked. Ask the fruit, I suppose. Ask whoever had picked it and wasn't invited to eat any.

I counted the servants first. It's a habit. A room tells you its truths by who's carrying trays through it, and Marrowgate's truths were being carried by faces I'd seen on the hill road, in the doorways of the keeper cottages, in Nell Ashcombe's defiant little market stall. Not those exact faces — those people wouldn't have come — but their cousins, their neighbors. The orchard families had been evicted off their land and into the town's kitchens, and now they were serving their own harvest back to the men who'd taken it, on plates nobody would let them sit down to.

I took a glass of wine from a girl who couldn't have been more than fourteen and didn't meet her eyes, because I was afraid of what mine would say.

"There he is." Hale materialized the way he did, warmth arriving ahead of him like heat off a stove. His hand landed on my shoulder with the weight of ownership. "The young man who's going to make our observatory sing again. Gentlemen — Dravenmoor. Apprentice to old Hollis himself in Kingsbridge. I've promised him a permanent post when the clock's restored. Marrowgate intends to keep its treasures."

*Its treasures.* I felt the phrase turn over in my head like a coin being checked for counterfeit. I was fairly sure I was the treasure in that sentence only until something better came along.

A circle of men gathered — magistrates, mill owners, the sort of soft-handed men whose portraits would hang in this hall for a century. They asked polite questions. Was the mechanism very fine? Were the works very old? I gave Bram's answers: coming along beautifully, nothing true. One of them, a heavy-jowled justice named Prewitt, leaned in with wine-breath and confided, "That clock's older than any of us, lad. Older than the town, some say. You take care you don't wake anything up in there."

Everyone laughed. I laughed too, and the sound came out of me thin and wrong, because three nights earlier my fingers had found a compartment in the great clock's casing that no maintenance ledger had ever mentioned, sealed so cunningly that finding it felt less like discovery than like being let in on a secret by a dead woman.

Prewitt had no idea how well he'd chosen his verb.

Hale steered me by the shoulder toward the head table, and that was where I saw Vey.

* * *

Mr. Lachlan Vey sat slightly apart from the feast the way a surgeon sits slightly apart from a dinner — present, observing, mentally opening everyone up. He wore his polish like I wear my apron: professionally. When he saw Hale steering me over, he rose and extended a hand with exactly calibrated warmth.

"The clockmaker. I've been hoping to finish our conversation." We had spoken once before, briefly, on my second day — he'd asked about my journey here, the state of the roads, whether the hill cottages had seemed occupied. Friendly questions. Each one a small hook, and me too green to notice the line attached. "You find Marrowgate to your liking?"

"Very beautiful country," I said.

"It is. Remarkably unspoiled." Something crossed his face when he said it — quick, gone before I could name it. I'd see that expression again later, though I didn't know it yet. "Gentlemen," he said to the gathering, raising his glass, "if you'll allow me. A preview."

The room hushed with the eagerness of people about to be told good news about themselves.

Vey produced a sheaf of pages tied in ribbon and read without needing them, which told me he'd written it by heart or wanted us to think so. "In the year of the founding," he began, "when the first stones of Marrowgate were laid upon this generous hill, the founders did not labor alone. They were aided — faithfully, skillfully, and with quiet devotion — by the orchard keepers of the upper terraces. These humble stewards of the fruit, whose names time has mercifully spared us the burden of recording individually, tended the slopes that fed the settlement, and their labors were ever held in warm regard by the founding families, whose generosity granted them their cottages and their livelihoods for so long as the orchards prospered."

He lowered the pages. Applause. Genuine applause, warm and rolling, from men who had just heard a theft described as a gift.

I want to be precise about what happened inside me in that moment, because precision is my trade.

*Whose names time has mercifully spared us the burden of recording.* Time hadn't spared anyone anything. Time doesn't erase names; people do, and then they blame the clock. I knew — because a sealed letter was burning a hole in the lining of my coat at that very moment — that the keepers' names were recorded, individually, in ink, by the town's own first archivist. That the land grants existed. That among the erased names were three Dravenmoors: my blood, my surname, carved on a lintel on a terrace where I'd stood last week with my hand flat against the wood like a fool touching a grave he hadn't known he had.

*Generosity granted them their cottages.* Granted. As though the cottages weren't built by keeper hands on keeper ground.

*So long as the orchards prospered.* And when the founders decided the orchards should prosper differently — well. There were eviction orders now, served on the last keeper still standing in her grandmother's house, timed like a escapement spring to release exactly when the published history would make the clearing look settled, tidy, inevitable.

One paragraph. The keepers got one paragraph, flattering and hollow, a painted backdrop behind the founders' portrait. And I realized, standing there with good wine going sour in my mouth, that a paragraph can be a weapon. That erasure doesn't always look like burning. Sometimes it looks like a compliment.

"Dravenmoor." Hale's hand on my shoulder again. "You've been up there among the terraces. Isn't it so? Romantic country. A touch of wildness gives a place its character, don't you find?"

Every eye in the circle had turned to me, mildly, pleasantly. Nobody in that room suspected — why would they? — that they were watching a man decide something. Least of all me; I'm not sure I knew it yet myself.

Here is what I could have said. I could have said: *Mr. Vey, the names weren't lost, they were taken. There is a registry in the archives Odile Fen kept that lists every keeper by name, and I'd wager my best file that page has been cut out rather than misplaced.* I could have said: *My own family is in whatever you removed, and you've just read them their eulogy as a footnote.* I could even have said it gently, wryly, in the register this room understood — a clever apprentice making charming trouble — and half of them would have laughed and the other half would have remembered, and a seed would have been planted somewhere public where I couldn't dig it out later.

Instead I said: "It does. Character. Very picturesque."

Hale beamed. Vey made a small note — I watched his pen move, and couldn't tell afterward whether he was recording my agreement or my cowardice. Perhaps he keeps both kinds of notes. Perhaps they're the same column in his book.

The applause started up again for the roast course, and I ate it. I ate everything. That's the detail I keep returning to, the one I can't shake loose: I ate. The food was extraordinary — the pears in the tart were from the upper terraces, keeper-grafted stock, fruit from trees whose owners' names had just been declared a burden — and I cleaned my plate while a girl of fourteen refilled my glass, and when Hale proposed a toast to *the harmonious story of Marrowgate*, I raised my glass with the rest and drank.

Complicity, I learned that night, tastes like excellent cooking. That's what makes it dangerous. If wrongdoing were bitter, honest men would spit it out. Instead it arrives glazed, and warm, and someone keeps filling your glass, and you tell yourself you're only being polite, and the politeness goes down smooth, and by the time you understand you've swallowed something it's already part of your blood.

Bram had told me to keep my mouth shut. He'd meant it as protection. But protection and imprisonment use the same bars, and I spent the rest of the feast behind them, smiling when smiled at, nodding at the harmonious story, feeling the letter against my ribs like a coal wrapped in oilcloth. Once — only once — I caught Vey looking at me across the hall. Not glaring. Studying. The way I study a movement that runs true right up until the moment it doesn't. Then he raised his glass an inch, courteous as a drawn blade, and returned to his notes.

* * *

I walked home up the hill road in full dark, which was stupid and which I did anyway, because I needed the distance between Founders' Hall and the terraces the way a man needs to step outside after a lie.

The orchard stood black against the stars, untended, patient. Somewhere up on the middle terrace a single lantern moved — Nell, doing her rounds, guarding her ground the way she did every night the weather allowed. She'd have heard about the feast. The whole town had. She'd know by tomorrow that the keepers had received their paragraph, and she'd know exactly what it was worth, and she would not forgive me for sitting silent through it. She shouldn't. I didn't.

Halfway up the second terrace I stopped at the cottage with the lintel. I couldn't see the carving in the dark, but I put my hand on it anyway — DRAVENMOOR, cut deep, cut by someone who expected the name to outlast him. He'd been right, barely. By one apprentice with a stolen inheritance and a pocketful of somebody else's guilt.

I had said *very picturesque*. I had drunk the toast. And the worst of it, the part I can hardly write down even now, is that some small, hungry piece of me had *liked* it — the candlelight, the flattery, Hale's hand warm on my shoulder saying *Marrowgate intends to keep its treasures.* Some part of me had sat at that table wanting to belong to the men who buried my family under a nice sentence.

Apprentices are replaceable. I've known that since I was twelve. But tonight taught me the uglier lesson: that replaceable people are offered a bargain — stay small, stay sweet, stay silent, and be kept — and that I had very nearly signed it in front of a hundred witnesses.

I took my hand off the lintel and climbed the rest of the way to the observatory, where Bram sat waiting with two cups of chicory and no questions, which is its own kind of mercy.

"Well?" he said.

"I kept my mouth shut."

"Good boy." He handed me a cup. Then he looked at me properly, the way old men look when they've spent decades learning to read fires and apprentices, and whatever he saw in my face made him set his own cup down. "Wendell. How bad?"

"She called them a burden to record, Bram. Their names. She read them out of existence in one sentence and the whole room applauded."

Bram was quiet a long moment. The fire cracked. Above us, faint through the floor, the master clock ticked with its damaged, halting patience — the false compartment sealed inside it, the registry sleeping inside that, my family sleeping inside the registry, everything waiting on a gear tooth that didn't exist yet.

"Odile Fen once told me," Bram said finally, "that silence isn't neutral. She said every quiet man is holding a door open for somebody, and you ought to know, before you go quiet, whose door you're holding." He rubbed his hands together, knuckles like tree roots. "I held Hale's door for eleven years. I know exactly how it's done."

"And if I don't want to hold it anymore?"

"Then you'd best hurry." He nodded toward the window, toward the town below where Founders' Hall still glowed with its harmonious story. "Because Vey sends his manuscript to the printer at month's end. After that, lad, the paragraph becomes the truth. Ink dries faster than gears turn."

I looked at the clock through the doorway. Month's end. A rebuilt escapement, a gear machined from scratch, a compartment opened, a registry brought into daylight — all of it before ink.

I picked up my cup and drank my chicory, and made myself a promise with teeth in it: never again would I applaud.
