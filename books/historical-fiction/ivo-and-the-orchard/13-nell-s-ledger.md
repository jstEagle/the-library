## 13. Nell's Ledger

I had decided not to trust him, and I was sticking to it with the same stubbornness I applied to everything else — the orchard, the eviction notices, the question of whether a person could live through a winter on apples and spite. The answer to the last one was yes, by the way. I'd done it twice.

But Wendell Dravenmoor kept coming up the hill.

That was the trouble with him. He came back. Most men from town came once, out of curiosity or official business, took a look at the woman living alone among dead trees, and went away satisfied they'd seen something worth gossiping about. He came every few days for two weeks, always with some excuse — he needed cuttings from the healthy stock to test something, he wanted to know the age of the terraces, he'd brought bread that Bram Tull had baked and slightly burned and didn't want to admit to. And each time he did a little work without being asked. Cleared a drainage channel. Righted a fallen wall stone. Fixed the hinge on my gate, which had squeaked like a dying goose for three years, in about four minutes, without comment, which was either kindness or the most elaborate courtship strategy ever attempted by a man who blushed when I looked at him directly.

"You could just say good morning," I told him on the day it happened, "instead of inventing reasons."

"Good morning," he said. Then, because he was incapable of leaving well enough alone: "The hinge needed a washer."

"The hinge has needed a washer since before you were born. Nobody fixed it."

"I'm nobody, then." He said it lightly enough, but his hand stayed on the gate a moment too long, and I remembered what Bram had told me — that the name carved over my lintel was carved somewhere else too, or nearly so, and that this boy with the ink-stained fingers had come up the hill carrying it like an unexploded shell.

That was the morning I decided to show him the ledger.

Not decided lightly, mind you. I want that understood. My grandmother spent the last thirty years of her life teaching me what was in that book and what would happen to anyone who carried it carelessly. She'd outlived two confiscations, one fire that was called an accident, and a constable who'd read every page of her grandfather's almanac looking for sedition and found only rainfall tables. She taught me the ledger the way other families teach children to swim — early, thoroughly, and with a great deal of shouting about drowning.

* * *

I made him wait until evening. I don't know why, exactly, except that some things want lamplight. A ledger read by daylight is a document; the same pages read by candlelight are a testament, and I suppose I wanted him to receive a testament.

He sat at my kitchen table, which still bore a ring from his own teacup left there days ago because he was too polite to move it and I was too contrary to wipe it up. The fire was small. It was April, but the hill keeps its own opinions about seasons.

"This isn't what you think it is," I said, taking the book down from behind the loose brick by the hearth where it had lived through my grandmother's tenure and my mother's absence and mine.

"I don't think anything about it yet."

"Everyone thinks something about it. You'll think it's a planting diary. Grandmother Ashcombe's little garden notes. That's what it wants you to think. It's very good at it." I set it on the table between us. It was a fat, square book, boards worn round at the corners, rebacked twice with leather from old boot tongues, because in my family we did not waste anything, least of all bindings. "Go on. Open it."

He opened it the way clockmakers open things — carefully, flat-handed, as if it might spring. The first page was what everyone saw: a planting calendar, 1794, rows of beans, a note about grafting stock. Honest, dull, domestic.

"Now turn past the calendar," I said.

He turned the page and stopped.

What he was looking at was what I had been looking at since I was six years old: the true ledger, written in the margins, in the gutters, upside down between the lines of beans and rainfall, in three generations of hands. Names. Dates of tenancy. Yields measured in bushels and assigned to specific keepers. Signatures — real ones, made by people who'd been told all their lives they couldn't write. Little drawings in the corners: a cherry branch, a ladder against a tree, a child's sketch of a dog that had clearly belonged to someone who loved that dog.

"It's a registry," Wendell said quietly.

"It's *the* registry. Or the ghost of one." I sat down across from him. "When they rewrote the founding records — and they did rewrite them, don't let anyone tell you different — they took the originals. What they couldn't take was this. My grandmother's grandmother started it the year after the first erasure. Every keeper who tended these terraces wrote their season into it. Who planted what, where, and how much it gave. Whose hands did the grafting. Which trees came from whose stock." I reached across and turned another page for him, because I wanted to be the one to show him. "It's not land grants. It's not legal paper. But it's forty years of proof that we were here, working, keeping, *feeding this town* — written while the town was telling itself we never existed."

Wendell ran one finger along a margin without touching the ink. Even that, he did gently. I noticed things like that about him. I tried not to, and noticed them anyway.

"Why hide it in a bean calendar?"

"Because nobody burns a bean calendar." I shrugged. "Grandmother said the first keeper tried hiding the record proper — a fair-copied book, proper pages, proper claims. The constables took it. So her daughter wrote the next one inside something too boring to steal. Every generation since has added its layer inside whatever harmless thing was at hand. Rainfall tables. A hymnal, for a while — the margins there were mostly births and deaths. This is the third book. There are parts copied forward from the ones that were lost, from memory, marked so we know which is witness and which is inheritance."

"Marked how?"

"A leaf drawn beside the entry means *this was copied, the original is gone.* A hand holding fruit means *this was seen with my own eyes.*" I tapped a drawing near the middle of the book — crude, but you could see the shape of it, fingers curled around something round. "There are more hands than leaves, but there are leaves. Enough."

He was quiet a long moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, an owl was conducting its usual complaints about the state of the hill.

"My family's name is in here," he said finally. It wasn't quite a question, but it wasn't not one.

I turned to the page. I'd known I would show him that part; I'd known since the gate hinge. "Dravenmoor. Terraces nine through twelve, east aspect, 1801 through 1819. Cherry and perry pear, mostly. Your—" I checked the marginal notation, though I knew it by heart— "great-grandfather, I make it. He grafted the Winter Russet that half the terraces still carry rootstock from. There's a note here about him arguing with the surveyor over a boundary wall and winning." I looked up. "He sounds like he was stubborn."

"He sounds like he was *here,*" Wendell said, and his voice did something complicated on the word *here.*

"That's the whole point of the book."

* * *

Here is what I did not tell him that night, and I have thought about why, and the honest answer is that I am a coward in exactly one direction and it points inward.

The ledger was not just names and yields. In the last gathering of pages, in my grandmother's hand, tight and small because paper was dear, there was a list of what was taken. Not generally — specifically. Dates, officers' names where she knew them, and above all, *where she believed the originals had gone.* Because she had talked, over the years, to old men and old women in Marrowgate who remembered things, and more than one of them had said the same strange thing: that the rewritten history wasn't built on nothing. That the founders had needed the originals close, to copy from, to forge against, and had never trusted them to any archive that might burn.

That they'd put them somewhere safe.

Somewhere with a lock on it.

My grandmother died before she finished that line of thinking, and I had followed it halfway up the hill myself and then stopped, because following it all the way meant believing the originals still existed, and believing that meant hoping, and hoping was the crop I could least afford. You can lose an apple harvest and plant again. You cannot lose hope annually and replant it. It doesn't keep.

So when Wendell sat across my table with his finger hovering over his great-grandfather's stubborn signature, I showed him the names and the yields and the drawings of dogs, and I did not show him the last pages, and I told myself it was prudence.

It was mostly fear. But a girl on an evicted hill learns to call fear by prettier names.

* * *

What I did tell him, later, when the candle had burned down to a thumb's width and he still hadn't left, was the thing I'd never said aloud to anyone, including Bram Tull, who got most of my unsaid things secondhand.

"Do you know why I keep tending? Everyone in town thinks it's obstinacy. Bram half-thinks it's grief. Hale's men think I'm waiting for compensation." I turned the ledger toward the light so the margins caught it. "I tend because of this. Because my grandmother told me: *a record no one tends dies.* Paper lasts, she said, but meaning doesn't — meaning has to be walked out every season like a horse. If nobody plants where these pages say people planted, then in fifty years this is just a curiosity. A folk artifact. Oh, how quaint, the little keepers' diary. Put it in a glass case in Founders' Hall next to the paragraph Vey writes about us." I heard my own voice go sharp and stopped myself. "Sorry. That's not aimed at you."

"It's aimed at someone."

"It's aimed at a whole hall of someones." I closed the book, softly, the way you close a sleeping child's door. "Forty years of stewardship in the margins of a bean calendar. That's what memory can hold, when it's all it's given. And I am proud of it. Truly. It's the bravest book in this county and almost no one will ever read it." I smiled, and the smile hurt. "Fragile pride. Grandmother's phrase. She said pride that survives in small places gets small and strong, like boxwood."

Wendell looked at the closed book for a long time. When he spoke, it was slowly, the way he seemed to do everything except blush.

"In the clock," he said, "there's a compartment. False casing, locked, keyed to a gear tooth I haven't identified yet. Odile Fen's letter says the original registry is inside. The founding records. The grants. Everything they took."

I had known this, in outline, from Bram. Knowing it in outline and hearing it said across my own kitchen table, next to the teacup ring, with his great-grandfather's name three pages away under my hand — those were different animals.

"And if it's true?" I asked. "If it opens?"

"Then your margins stop being the only witness." He met my eyes. He was getting better at that, or braver, or both. "Then the ledger and the registry match, entry against grant, and neither one can be dismissed as sentiment."

I should have felt triumphant. What I actually felt was terrified, and I've learned to read that correctly: terror is what hope feels like before it commits.

"If you're wrong," I said, "if you break your clock chasing this and find an empty box, then I've spent my hope for nothing, and I'll still be here tending terraces the town says aren't mine, and it will have cost me the only currency I had left."

"Yes," he said simply. No reassurance. No pretty promise. Just the acknowledgment that I'd counted the price correctly, which was somehow the most respectful thing anyone had said to me in years.

"Then we do it in order," I said. "You find your tooth. I'll start copying the ledger — a fair copy, hidden separately from the original, because if anything happens to one of them—" I stopped, hearing my grandmother in my own mouth, and finished anyway. "Because memory keeps better when there are two of it."

He nodded and rose to go, and at the door he paused with his hat in his hands like a man at a graveside, and said, "Thank you for showing me. I know what it costs."

"It doesn't cost anything tonight," I said. "Come back in a week and we'll discuss the invoice."

He laughed — startled, honest — and went out into the dark, and I watched him pick his way down the terrace path by lantern light, and I stood there longer than was sensible, holding a bean calendar against my sternum like a shield.

* * *

Three nights later, I came home to find my gate off its hinges.

Not broken — lifted, set aside, laid neatly against the wall. Someone had taken it off to work quietly and hadn't bothered to replace it, which told me they'd expected not to be interrupted and hadn't planned on me walking back up from the lower terraces until full dark.

Inside, the cottage was untouched. The loose brick was in place. The fire had been raked and banked — by my hand, hours ago, and it still held its shape. Nothing was missing.

Nothing was missing, and that frightened me worse than loss, until I understood what it meant. They hadn't come for silver or coin, because a cottage like mine has none. They hadn't come for the ledger, because they didn't know where to look, or possibly didn't know what they were looking for beyond *something of hers.*

They had come to learn the layout. To measure the room. Whoever it was now knew exactly how many steps ran from my door to my hearth, and had left the gate off its hinges as a message: *we came in; we can come again; and next time we won't be tidy.*

I put the gate back up myself, in the dark, my hands steady and my mouth dry, and then I sat inside with the ledger on my knees and a knife I mostly used for grafting, and I thought about Wendell's stolen tools, and the note he'd shown me — *finish the clock and leave* — and I understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like calm, that we were not being warned.

We were being inventoried. And something of theirs was missing from the count, and they intended to keep looking until they found it.

At dawn I was on the observatory road before the mist lifted, ledger wrapped in oilcloth under my coat, going to find the clockmaker.
