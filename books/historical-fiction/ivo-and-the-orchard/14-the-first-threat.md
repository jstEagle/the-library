## 14. The First Threat

A clockmaker's tools are his biography. Open my roll and you could read it like a diary: the burnished pliers my master gave me at fourteen, handles worn to the shape of my grip; the loupe I bought secondhand from a dying man in Ashford who swore it had looked into a prince's watch; the fine files I'd saved three years' wages to own, each one wrapped in its own pocket of oiled cloth. Forty-one pieces, arranged in an order only I understood, because disorder in a tool roll is how fingers lose nails.

I discovered their absence on a Thursday morning, and for a full minute I stood in my narrow room above the cooper's shop performing a kind of arithmetic that made no sense whatsoever.

The room was not disturbed. That was the first wrongness. My spare shirts still sat folded on the chair. The letter from Odile Fen — hidden, since Bram's confession, inside the hollowed spine of a hymnal I had no other use for — lay exactly where I'd left it. Nothing taken but the roll itself, which had lived beneath the bed in the dark like a tame animal that had wandered off.

Except tools do not wander. And whoever had come for them had known precisely what to take, and precisely what to leave, because taking the letter would have been a crime with consequences, whereas taking a tool roll was merely misfortune.

I sat down on the edge of the bed and let that thought walk all the way through me. It is a particular sensation, being robbed by someone careful. A brute who ransacks your room insults you. Someone who lifts one object and leaves the dust undisturbed *addresses* you. It says: we know where you sleep. It says: we chose this deliberately, the way a watchmaker chooses which tooth of a wheel to file.

Then I saw the note.

It had been placed on the workbench — the small table by the window where I'd been oiling the clock's remontoire parts each evening — weighted under the brass screw-gauge I kept there. Folded once. Cream paper, good stock, the kind sold at the stationer's near Founders' Hall. The hand was round and even, a clerk's hand or better, written slowly enough to be legible rather than hurried enough to be anonymous.

*Finish the clock. Leave Marrowgate. Your master's house has a long memory and longer arms.*

No signature. Of course no signature. Men like Alderman Corvus Hale never sign anything; they have other people for signatures.

I read it four times. Somewhere around the third reading, I noticed my hands were shaking, and somewhere around the fourth, I noticed something more interesting than fear: the note said *finish the clock.* Not abandon it. Finish it. Whoever wrote those words wanted the master clock running, its chimes restored to their proper hours, its great face serene over the town — and then wanted me gone before anyone asked what else might be found inside it.

Which meant they believed the clock could be made to tell more than time. Which meant Odile Fen's letter had not exaggerated. Which meant, in the perverse economy of threats, I had just been paid a strange compliment: I was now worth robbing and warning.

Fear, I find, behaves much like a mainspring. Coiled, it does nothing but ache. But let it down slowly, tooth by tooth, and it becomes power.

* * *

I did not go to the constable. I want that recorded plainly, because I considered it, standing in my small room with the cream-colored note in my hand, and I want future readers of whatever honest history eventually gets written to know that I weighed the option and rejected it not out of cowardice alone, though cowardice took its share, but out of arithmetic.

The constable in Marrowgate is appointed by the town council. The town council is chaired by Alderman Hale. Going to the constable about the theft would be like carrying a broken escapement back to the man who'd broken it and asking him to certify the damage. He would be sympathetic. He would take notes. He would observe that Mr. Dravenmoor had perhaps misplaced his belongings, that workshops are cluttered places, that young men away from home are often careless. And Hale would learn, within the hour, exactly how much the threat had frightened me — information I could not afford to sell at any price.

Instead I went to see Bram Tull.

He was in the observatory's undercroft as always, doing his slow rounds among the instruments with a lamp and a rag, tending machines nobody else remembered how to love. When he saw my face he set the rag down at once, which told me my expression required no interpretation.

"They've taken my tools," I said. "And left this."

I put the note on the bench between us. He read it the way he read everything — twice, slowly, lips moving slightly on the second pass — and when he finished, he did something I hadn't expected. He closed his eyes, the way a man does when a wound he's been guarding finally opens, and said, "So it's started."

"You've seen this before."

"Once." He lowered himself onto the stool by the wall, and I was struck again by how old he'd become since my arrival, as though the sabotage he'd confessed to were a weight that grew heavier by the week. "A stonemason, forty years back. Grigor Ashcombe's father-in-law, if you follow the threads. He repaired a lintel up at the Hall and found mason's marks underneath that didn't match the official story of who'd built what. His tools went missing the next week. Then his commission. Then, when he wouldn't stop talking, his health — he fell from a scaffold that everyone agreed was sound." Bram rubbed his eyes with a knuckle. "Everyone agreed very quickly. That's how you know, boy. When everyone agrees too fast, someone's paid for the agreement."

"And the constable—"

"Saw nothing. Heard nothing. Wrote it down as misadventure." He pushed the note back across the bench toward me. "You can't prove whose hand wrote that. You can't prove the tools didn't grow legs. In Marrowgate, proof belongs to whoever owns the paper."

"I know," I said. "That's rather the problem, isn't it? The whole town runs on paper Hale's family wrote."

Bram studied me for a while. The lamp between us threw his shadow enormous against the stone vaulting, so that the stooped old caretaker looked briefly like a giant bent under a doorway.

"What will you do?" he asked.

What will you do. I turned the question over and found, to my surprise, that it had already been answered somewhere below language, in the same place a watchmaker knows a train will run before he's assembled it. "Work," I said. "They've taken my tools, but half the mechanism I need is already fitted, and the rest I can make. There's a lathe downstairs in your care that hasn't turned a spindle in twenty years. I'll need files. Stock brass. A graver, even a poor one. And I'll need them bought quietly, in small lots, from towns far enough away that no stationer connects the purchases."

"That will cost money you don't have."

"Hale is paying me a generous wage to repair the clock," I said. "There's an irony there I intend to enjoy fully."

Bram almost smiled. Then it faded, and he gripped my wrist — hard, for an old man, hard enough that I felt the tremor in his fingers and understood it wasn't age alone. "Listen to me. Whatever they took the tools to stop, they'll try worse if you don't stop first. Grigor Ashcombe got a warning and ignored it, and he fell off a sound scaffold. You're not a fool, so don't act like one. Don't walk the hill road after dark. Don't keep the letter where you sleep. And for God's sake, boy, don't tell anyone what you're building until it's built."

"Anyone," I repeated.

"Anyone." His eyes held mine, and I watched him think of Nell Ashcombe and decide, visibly, painfully, not to say her name aloud. Which was its own message.

* * *

I spent that afternoon in a state I can only describe as angry precision. Anger is a fine lubricant in small quantities; it reduces friction between a man and his purpose. In large quantities, of course, it seizes the whole works, and I have seen what that does to people. So I measured mine out carefully, a drop at a time, and went to the observatory's under-lathe and began to bring it back to life.

Twenty years of neglect comes off a machine slower than it goes on. The lathe was a good one — English iron, hand-scraped beds, the kind of tool built by men who assumed their grandchildren would use it — and beneath the grease and the mouse droppings and one genuinely impressive spiderweb, it was sound. I cleaned the ways. I freed the spindle. I made a drive belt from a strip of harness leather Bram produced from a storeroom that seemed to contain everything the observatory had ever forgotten, which I suspect is most of its true history.

By lamplight, with my borrowed tools and my improvised ones — a graver ground from an old screwdriver, files bought through Bram from a dealer two towns east — I began cutting the first test gear.

And here is what I learned about myself that night, working alone in that cold stone room with the stolen roll of my old life sitting somewhere in some alderman's drawer like a pulled tooth: I had expected, once the fear arrived, to want to go home. That is what fear is supposed to purchase. Pack the bag, finish the clock as instructed, take whatever quiet reference my master could salvage, return to cramped rooms and portable things and a life measured in other people's heirlooms. The note practically drew me a map.

But every pass of the graver through brass felt like testimony. Every tooth I cut was a word in a language the Hales of this world cannot read, filed in a record they cannot burn, because it lives in the metal itself and in my two hands. They could steal my pliers. They could not steal the knowledge of why the escape wheel must be cut to six and a half degrees, or the memory of my master's voice saying *the clock is the only witness that cannot be bribed,* or the image — new, still tender, absurdly precious — of a lintel over a cottage door with DRAVENMOOR carved into it by ancestors I had never known existed until a dying archivist spent her last strength telling me.

You do not get to warn a man away from his own name. That is not how inheritance works.

Somewhere past midnight I set down the graver, blew the filings from the wheel blank, and examined it under the loupe. Crude, by my standards. Serviceable, by necessity's. I wrapped it in cloth and hid it inside the hollow leg of the workbench, along with the letter from the hymnal, because Bram was right — nothing I valued should sleep where I slept.

I walked back down the hill road in the grey hour before dawn, and yes, I flinched at shadows, and yes, I kept to the orchard side where the terraces gave cover, and I am not ashamed of either. Fear sharpening into resolve does not stop being sharp. A blade that has lost its edge is called a bar; a blade that keeps it is called a weapon, and I intended, for the first time in my obedient apprentice life, to be a weapon.

* * *

Nell was waiting for me at the bend where the third terrace overlooks the road. I nearly walked into her before I saw her — she has a way of standing among trees that makes her one more vertical thing among vertical things — and she watched me startle and took obvious satisfaction in it.

"You look like a man who's been robbed," she said.

My hand went to my empty belt before I could stop it, and she nodded as though I'd answered in words.

"Bram told me," she said. "Don't glare at him. Somebody in this conspiracy of ours has to be able to move freely, and an old caretaker talking to the keeper's daughter draws less attention than either of you pretending you don't know each other." She fell into step beside me on the descent, arms crossed against the morning cold. "Tools. What exactly?"

"All of them. The roll, the loupe, the good files. Left the clothes, left the papers, left everything that would make it burglary instead of loss."

"Hale's men?"

"Hale's *something.*" I hesitated. This was the part I had turned over all night, the part that kept me walking instead of running. "Nell — the note told me to finish the clock and leave. Not to stop working. To finish."

She stopped walking. For a moment the only sound was wind moving through the untended apple rows above us, and I watched her do the same arithmetic I had done, arriving at the same ugly sum.

"They want it running," she said slowly. "Before the history's published. Before the eviction. A fixed clock is a *finished story.* The town looks up, hears the chimes, thinks: all is well, all is as it always was, Marrowgate whole and honest. And the man who fixed it is gone, and the compartment stays shut forever, because whoever winds that clock after me won't be looking for secrets."

"Yes."

"So they're afraid of the same thing you are." Her jaw set in that way I was learning meant she'd reached a decision and dared the world to contest it. "Afraid of what's inside it. Good. Fear means the records are real, Wendell. If Odile Fen lied, they'd let you open that casing themselves and laugh at you."

"It also means," I said, "that when the compartment opens, they will do considerably worse than steal a tool roll. We are, both of us, approaching the point where this stops being repair work and becomes something they must treat as a war."

"We were always at war," Nell said. "Some of us just found out recently." She looked up the road toward the town, then back at me, and something in her face shifted — not softening exactly; Nell does not soften; more like a gate unbolting. "Bram says you can rebuild the escapement. Says your master trained you past what apprentices are usually taught."

"My master says many things. Some of them are even encouraging."

"He says you cut a test gear last night that would pass inspection."

"He *told* you that?" I stared at her. "Bram was watching me work?"

"Bram watches everything in that building. It's how he's survived Hale his whole life." She permitted herself the ghost of a smile, then put it away. "Here's what matters. The ledger is still safe, the letter is still safe, and as of last night we know their timetable: they want the clock done before Vey's book goes to print. Which means we have a deadline, and so do they. From today, you don't work alone at night anymore. I keep watch while you machine. Bram carries what you make down the hill in pieces, mixed in with scrap, so no single search finds anything. And when it's ready—" she glanced up at the observatory, black against the paling sky, "—we open it together, in daylight, with witnesses, before Hale's men can put their hands on it. No midnight secrets. Secrets are how they've won for sixty years."

"Witnesses," I said. "In Marrowgate. Where proof belongs to whoever owns the paper."

"Then," said Nell Ashcombe, with a flat calm that frightened me rather more than the note had, "we had best make sure the evidence is the sort that doesn't live on paper."

She turned and walked up toward the cottages, and I stood a moment longer on the road, looking at the master clock's dark face above me, keeping its stubborn, silenced hour over a town built on a lie.

Then I saw the rider.

Far down the valley road, where the morning mist still pooled, a horseman had stopped at the foot of the hill — stopped, and sat motionless, and raised something to his face that caught the early light. A glass. However long he had been watching us, he had been watching deliberately, and he was pointed not toward the town but toward the orchard.

He turned his horse, unhurried, and rode back the way he came.

Nell had seen him too. When I looked at her, she had gone very still, and her hand had moved to the pocket where the ledger's key hung on a cord against her heart.

"Not Hale's usual men," she said quietly. "Hale's men wear livery. That one dresses like money."

And I understood, with a cold settling in my stomach, that the note in my pocket had not been the threat. It had been the courtesy.
