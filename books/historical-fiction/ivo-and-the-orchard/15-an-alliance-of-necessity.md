## 15. An Alliance of Necessity

*Nell*

I had decided not to trust him, and I stuck to that decision for the better part of three days, which for me is a kind of discipline.

The trouble with the clockmaker was that he kept coming back to the orchard. People who want something from you come once, make their offer, and go away when you refuse. People who keep coming back want something they haven't found a name for yet, and those are more dangerous, or more useful, depending on how the wind sits.

He came up the terraces on the third morning carrying a paper-wrapped parcel and an expression like a man walking into a sermon he'd been warned about. I was pruning the Gravensteins on the middle terrace — dead wood mostly, the trees gone wild in all the places my grandmother's ledger said they should have been shaped — and I let him stand there a while before I acknowledged him. A person's patience is worth measuring before you hand him anything true.

"You're Ashcombe," he said, which was not a question, so I didn't answer it.

"That parcel," I said instead, "is either bread or a bribe."

"Bread." He held it out. "My own hands. Well — my hands, my master's recipe. He believed an apprentice should be able to feed himself as well as mend things, on the theory that hunger makes bad solder joints."

I took it. It was still warm. That was the moment, if I'm honest, when the whole enterprise began to slide sideways, because I had spent two years being hard as frostbite and here was a boy who baked bread and climbed a hill at dawn to bring it to a woman who had done nothing but glare at him from a tree.

"I don't want your bread," I said, holding it.

"Noted," he said. "You're holding it anyway."

I put down the shears and looked at him properly. He was younger than his hands — I'd noticed that the first time, watching him turn the clock key Bram had lent him. Hands older than the face attached to them, the way workingmen's hands get. But there was something else. He stood like a man standing in a doorway deciding whether to enter a room he can't leave again.

"The clock," I said. "You've been asking Bram about the clock. And now you're asking about me. Say what you came to say."

So he said it. All of it. The letter, the name Fen, the compartment in the mechanism, and then — quietly, the way you set down something breakable — the word *Dravenmoor*.

The wind moved through the Gravensteins and I heard my grandmother's voice in it, which happens less now than it used to, and I sat down on the pruning stool without meaning to.

"Say that last part again," I said.

"Odile Fen's letter names the Dravenmoors among the erased families. Orchard-keepers. Founding registry, land grants, all of it hidden inside the master clock." He watched me the way you watch a fuse. "I didn't know until six days ago that my family had anything to do with this town except by accident of my apprenticeship."

"Dravenmoor," I repeated. The ledger had the name in it. Of course it did — my grandmother recorded every keeper's planting going back past living memory, names in her cramped slanted hand, and *Dravenmoor* ran through those pages like a warp thread through cloth. "Terrace four," I heard myself say. "The Dravenmoors kept terrace four. Pears, mostly. Your people grew pears while mine grew apples, and we argued every autumn about which made better cider, and my grandmother wrote that the argument was older than the trees."

Wendell Dravenmoor sat down on the ground. Just sat down, right there in the wet grass, like his legs had been borrowed and recalled without notice.

"There's a cottage," he said after a while. "On the upper terrace. The lintel—"

"Carved. Yes. DRAVENMOOR, and beneath it, smaller: *planted 1791, may it outlive us.*" I recited it flat, because if I didn't keep my voice flat it would do something I'd regret. "I've swept that doorstep every month for two years. I don't know why. It seemed rude to let one family's house forget itself entirely."

He looked up at me then, and whatever was in his face, I recognized it, because I'd worn the same one at my grandmother's grave: grief arriving late for someone you never got to meet, which is the loneliest kind there is.

"All right," I said, before he could thank me or apologize or whatever boys do when they've been handed their own history by a stranger. "Here's how it will be. You know the clock. I know the records — what's left of them. Bram knows both, and knows Hale, which means he knows where the knives are kept. If we're going to open that compartment and live afterward, it has to be the three of us, and none of us tells a soul, and none of us flinches. Can you do that?"

"No," he said honestly. "But I'll learn."

That, more than the bread, was the thing that undid me. Everyone else in Marrowgate claimed certainty the way other men claim land titles. This one admitted the gap between what was asked of him and what he had, and offered to close it anyway.

"Then sit down properly," I said, "eat your own half of that bread, and tell me everything about the mechanism. We'll start with what's broken and work toward who broke it."

*Wendell*

I have since learned that Nell Ashcombe negotiates the way her grandmother grafted trees: cleanly, low on the trunk, leaving no wasted wood. At the time I only knew that I had walked up the hill carrying bread and a confession, and walked back down carrying an alliance I hadn't fully understood I was asking for.

We met twice more before we brought Bram in. Once in the orchard, where she showed me the ledger — wrapped in oilcloth inside a hollowed-out beam in her cottage, a hiding place that told me more about her life than any conversation could have. Her grandmother's hand filled page after page: plantings, grafts, frosts, harvests, and threaded through them, the names of the keepers. Reading it felt like listening to a room through a wall. Everything the town's official history omitted was in there, patient as sap.

Once at the observatory, after midnight, where she watched me work on the escapement housing and asked questions until my answers got honest.

"It won't open," I told her, the second night, with my tools resting useless in my lap. "Not without rebuilding the escapement entirely. Whoever installed the compartment keyed it into the mechanism itself. Force it, and you destroy the clock and probably the records with it."

"Then rebuild it," Nell said, as though I'd described a fence needing paint.

"Apprentices don't rebuild escapements. Journeymen barely do. My master says—" I stopped.

"Your master says what?"

"That it separates the men from the boys who clean mainsprings." I turned the key of the great clock's winding drum over in my fingers. "He also says a clock can't lie. About time, anyway. Whatever else Marrowgate has done with its history, this machine has kept honest hours for forty years, even sabotaged. There's something — I don't know. It feels like the only witness in town that never took their money."

Nell was quiet a moment, watching the pendulum swing in the lamplight.

"My grandmother used to say the orchard keeps its own books," she said. "Every spring it writes down exactly what was done to it the year before. Blossom doesn't perjure itself." She glanced at me. "You and the clock are going to get along."

It was such a strange feeling — being *understood*, and by the one person in Marrowgate least inclined to flatter anyone — that I nearly missed the sound of boots on the observatory stair. Nell heard it too. She was behind the pillar with the lamp shuttered before I'd finished standing.

Bram Tull came through the door holding his own lantern, saw my open tool roll spread across the housing, and went the color of tallow.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, lad. You've started."

"We need to talk," I said. "All three of us. Sit down, Bram."

"I don't think—" He looked at the door, then at Nell emerging from the shadows, then at the door again, and I watched the arithmetic of an old man's fear run visibly across his face. "If Hale were to hear—"

"Hale already knows about the letter," Nell said.

Bram stopped moving entirely. Even his lantern stopped swinging.

"How would you know that?"

"Because a man came to my cottage two weeks ago asking whether 'the Fen woman left papers,' and he wasn't curious, he was confirming. Because my eviction notice was drawn up the same week. Because nothing happens in Marrowgate that Alderman Hale doesn't count first." She stepped forward into the light. "You gave Wendell that letter. Odile Fen trusted you with it. So the question isn't whether Hale finds out, Mr. Tull. The question is whether he finds out while we're ready or while we're scattered."

I had never seen Bram Tull small before. He had always seemed tall in the way of caretakers, taller than the building they tend. Now he set the lantern down on the clock case and lowered himself onto the step beside it like a man sitting down under a weight he'd been carrying up many stairs.

"I helped sabotage it," he said. Not to me. To the clock. "Eleven years ago. Hale's man brought the drawings, and I made the cuts in the train myself, and I told myself it was only iron and brass, only a machine, machines break all the time. And then Odile Fen began her own counting, quiet as anything, and I watched her gather what Hale's grandfather had buried, and I knew — I knew — that one day she'd hand it to someone, and that someone would be standing where you're standing, and I'd have to look at him."

He looked up at me then. His eyes were wet, but his voice had steadied, the way old mechanisms steady once the sticking point is passed.

"She made me swear the letter would go to no one unless the clock resisted repair. As if she knew even her own death would need a lock on it." A rough laugh. "She was sharper than any of us. Sharmer than the clock."

"Will you help us?" Nell asked. Plainly. No softening.

Bram was silent long enough that the pendulum counted it for him — seven swings, eight.

"I'm afraid of Hale," he said finally. "I want that written somewhere, since we're keeping records. Write it down: Bram Tull, coward, thirty-one years in the observatory, afraid of Corvus Hale." His jaw set. "But write this too: he came to help anyway. Get me paper. If we're doing this, we do it like Odile would — everything noted, everything dated, everything witnessed. If I'm to be hanged for it, I'll be hanged by an accurate account."

I fetched my journal from my coat, and Bram Tull dictated his confession to me by lantern light inside the belly of the clock he'd helped wound, while Nell Ashcombe checked each date against her grandmother's ledger and found, to nobody's surprise, that the two accounts agreed line for line.

When he finished, something had changed in the room. I couldn't name it precisely. It was as if the three of us had been separate gears lying loose on a bench and someone — Odile Fen, most likely, working eleven years ahead of us — had dropped us into the same train.

"Well," Nell said, closing the ledger. "Now we need a plan."

What we had instead, by the time the sky went grey at the windows, was worse than a plan and better: a division of labor. Nell would reconstruct the full keeper rolls and land claims from the ledger. Bram would use his keys and his thirty-one unremarkable years to map when Hale's men entered the observatory and what they touched, so we'd know what they already knew. And I —

"And you," Nell said, pointing at me with the sort of finger that assigned terraces and pruning schedules, "are going to build an escapement."

* * *

*Bram*

They left before dawn, separately, as I'd insisted — the girl down the hill road, the boy by the upper path past the empty cottages. I stood in the doorway watching until both were gone, then went back inside and put my hand flat against the clock case, the way you'd check a fever.

Thirty-one years I'd wound this machine and called it duty. Eleven of those years I'd known I was winding a lie, keeping false time for false men, and telling myself the gears didn't know the difference.

But gears do know. That's the whole secret of the trade, the one Odile understood and I pretended not to. A gear either meshes or it doesn't. There's no charming a tooth into fitting where it was never cut. All these years Hale thought he owned Marrowgate because he owned its records and its aldermen and, in some fashion that no one named aloud, its magistrate. He never understood that the town runs on finer machinery than that, and that fine machinery does not forgive.

The boy would fail, I thought, banking the lamp. Escapement work of that order takes ten years' hand. He had six days' knowledge and a stolen letter.

And then I remembered the way he'd taken dictation — not wincing at the shameful parts, not hurrying past them, writing *coward* in my own words and dating it. Steady hands. Honest arithmetic. Old Fen had chosen the way she chose everything: late, deliberately, and correctly.

I banked the lamp and went down the stairs to sleep a few hours before morning rounds, and it was on the landing that I saw, through the east window, a carriage on the hill road where no carriage had any business being at that hour. Dark lacquer. Hale's crest on the door, catching the first light.

It did not turn toward the Hall. It came up toward the observatory.

I stood very still on the landing and listened to the wheels on the gravel, and I thought: *so it begins today, then. Not next week. Today.*

I put my hand in my pocket, found the spare key to the clock case, and closed my fist around it until the teeth bit my palm.
