## 18. The Eviction Notice

I knew the man was coming before I saw him, because the crows went up off the north terrace all at once, like someone had pulled a stitch out of the morning.

Crows know things. They knew the keepers were leaving Marrowgate before the keepers did, back when my grandmother was a girl; she wrote it in the ledger — *crows gone from north wall, third of May* — as if even the birds could read a deed. So when they lifted off the hedgerow in one black sheet and wheeled toward town, I set down my grafting knife, wiped my hands on my apron, and decided I would not be surprised by whatever came up the hill road.

I was wrong about that, but only in the particulars.

The particulars wore a grey coat and carried a leather document case, and he did not come alone. Behind him, at the respectful distance of a man who wants to be able to say later that he merely accompanied, walked Alderman Corvus Hale himself, in his good brown wool, hatless, smiling the way he smiled at the harvest festival — the smile of a man arriving with a basket he expects to be thanked for.

"Nell Ashcombe," he called, while he was still far enough away that I couldn't answer without shouting. He liked that, I think. It let him do most of the talking on the approach.

"Alderman." I stayed where I was, on the low stone step of the cottage, arms folded. Grandmother's trick. She'd written that one down too, though not in the ledger: *stand still and let them come to you. Men hate walking uphill twice.*

"You'll forgive the climb," Hale said, reaching the gate. He didn't open it. Small mercies. "Mr. Prewitt here is clerk to the magistrate's office. We've some papers for you."

"Have you."

"Formalities," Hale said, and the word came out warm as bread. That was always his gift, making ruin sound like a courtesy. "Nobody's happy about it. Least of all me."

Prewitt unlatched the case with fingers that didn't quite want to be doing it, and I watched him rather than Hale, because clerks will show you the truth in their hands even when their mouths are owned outright. His hands hesitated over the paper. His hands knew what it was before his mouth got around to saying it.

"Notice of eviction," Prewitt said, "from the terraces and dwelling known as Ashcombe Holding, north orchard, pursuant to — "

"I can read," I said. Not kindly.

He held it out anyway, across the gate, and I took it, because refusing paper doesn't make it less true; it only makes you look afraid of it. The vellum was good stock, better than anything in my cottage. Someone had spent money on this. Someone had wanted the thing that ended my family to at least *look* handsome doing it.

The language was exactly what you'd expect. *Whereas the lands in question revert to the corporate body of the Founders' Trust. Whereas no valid tenancy has been recorded. Whereas.* Whereas, whereas. Forty years my grandmother kept that orchard alive through two killing frosts and a fire blight, and the law's whole memory of her fit inside three whereas-es and a wax seal the size of a saucer.

"You have until the first of the month," Prewitt said quietly. "To vacate."

The first of the month. I did the arithmetic without wanting to. The first of the month was four days before Mr. Vey's history went to the printer. I looked up at Hale and found him watching me do the sums, and there it was, just for a heartbeat, under the harvest-festival smile: the flat satisfaction of a man whose gears are cutting clean.

"Before the book comes out," I said.

Hale spread his hands, a man baffled by coincidence. "Publication schedules are nothing to do with me, Miss Ashcombe. This process began long ago. You may thank your own stubbornness for its lateness." He said it almost tenderly, as if my stubbornness were an illness he'd been patiently treating. "The Trust has been generous beyond its obligation. You'll be paid a settling sum."

"I don't want a settling sum."

"It isn't for you," he said. "It's for the record. So that no one can say afterward the town turned out an old woman's granddaughter without provision." And there — *afterward* — was the whole of him laid open like a watch on a bench. He wasn't buying my silence. He was buying the story of my leaving. In his version, printed in Vey's fine prose or some future one, the Ashcombes would have been *provided for*. Gracious, gentle Marrowgate, easing the last keeper down the hill with coin and kind words, and wasn't that decent of everyone.

"That's what the record's for, then," I said. "Making it so nobody has to remember."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes — not guilt; I'd have needed a stronger glass than I own to find guilt in that face — but recognition, maybe, the flicker of a man hearing an unfamiliar gear tooth catch against his train. "Memory," he said, "is a poor steward of land. Look around you, Miss Ashcombe. Terraces falling to bramble. Trees gone untended. This place needs owners, not mourners."

"This place *has* owners," I said. "You're standing on their gate."

The smile thinned. Just at the corners. Then it reassembled itself, and he tipped his nonexistent hat. "First of the month," he said again. "Mr. Prewitt will call to confirm your receipt."

They went down the hill. Prewitt didn't look back. Hale did, once, at the second switchback, and I made sure he caught me watching him go, arms still folded, feet planted on my grandmother's step.

Then I sat down on the stone, which was cold through my skirts even in the spring sun, and read the notice again, properly this time, every clause, because if there was a seam anywhere in that document I intended to find it. There wasn't. Whoever drew it up had done careful work. That frightened me more than sloppy work would have. Sloppy men can be beaten by their own mistakes. Careful ones you have to beat with something else entirely.

* * *

I did what my grandmother would have done, which was: I worked.

There's a cruelty in eviction notices that people who write them never understand. They think the worst moment is reading the paper. It isn't. The worst moment is an hour later, when your hands have stopped shaking and you realize the trees still need pruning whether or not you'll be here to see them fruit, and you cannot make yourself stop caring for them anyway. I pruned the Ribstons on the third terrace. I cleared the mouse-nesting out of the grafting frame. I mended the hinge on the cold-store door, which is a job I'd been putting off since February, and I stood there holding the hammer and laughing at myself, alone, like a madwoman, because who mends a door on a house she's being turned out of?

Someone who means to come back, that's who. Or someone too stubborn to know the difference. Grandmother never distinguished between the two in her writing, and I won't either.

Midday, Wendell came up the hill road with his collar crooked and that particular walk he has, like a man carrying something breakable in each hand at all times. Clockmaker's gait. I'd noticed it before and thought nothing of it. Lately I noticed everything about him and thought a great deal of it, which was inconvenient, and I filed it away with the other inconvenient things.

He saw my face from twenty yards and stopped dead. "What happened?"

"Hale brought me a present." I handed him the notice. I watched him read it, and I'll grant him this: he read it the way I read a mechanism, top to bottom, twice, hunting the seam. His jaw got tighter with each whereas.

"The first of the month," he said. "That's — "

"Four days before the history prints. Yes."

"He's clearing the ground," Wendell said slowly. "If you're gone, and the cottages stand empty, and the terraces are 'reverted' — then whatever Vey writes, there's nothing living to contradict it. No keeper on the land. Just paper. His paper." He looked up, and I saw the exact moment the arithmetic landed on him the way it had landed on me. "He's not evicting you, Nell. He's editing you."

That was the sentence I hadn't managed to build all morning, and he built it without knowing he was building anything, standing in my gateway with my grandmother's pruning shears still hooked over my belt. I had to turn away and fuss with the grafting frame for a moment, which fooled neither of us.

"There has to be a challenge procedure," Wendell said to my back. "Bram will know. There's always a — some filing, some appeal —"

"To whom?" I asked the frame. "The magistrate whose clerk served the papers? The Trust that holds the deeds? Wendell, this isn't a broken escapement. There's no part I can cut that makes it right. The machine *works*. That's what's unbearable about it. Every gear in it turns smooth."

He came and stood beside me at the frame, close enough that our shoulders nearly touched, and for a while neither of us said anything, and the wind moved through the untended rows the way it has moved through them for forty years, patient, indifferent to paperwork.

"My master used to say," Wendell offered at last, "that when a mechanism works perfectly toward a bad end, you don't fix it. You stop winding it."

"And how does one stop winding an alderman?"

"I don't know yet." He said it very simply, no flourish, and somehow that was worth more than a plan. "But the clock finishes before the month does. Whatever's in that compartment — it finishes first. I'll see to it."

"You can't promise that."

"No," he agreed. "But I'm going to say it anyway. Seems unhealthy, the amount of sense you make alone up here."

Despite everything — despite the vellum sitting on my table like a headstone — I laughed. Short, and rough, but real. He looked absurdly pleased with himself, the way men do when they've caused a laugh they didn't engineer, and I hated how much I liked seeing it, and I got on with the grafting frame.

* * *

Evening came down the valley the way it does in April, long gold shadows striping the terraces so the whole hillside looks like ruled paper waiting for someone to write on it. I sat on the step with the ledger open on my knees, reading backward, as I'd been doing all week — not for the planting tables now but for the names. Ashcombe. Fen. Dravenmoor. Quill. The keepers' rows, entry after entry, season after season, forty years of a family the town's records insisted had never existed.

*Third of October. Grafted eight Ribston, six took. W. helped carry water though his shoulder was bad. He says the north wall wants repointing before frost.*

W. My great-grandfather, probably. A man with a bad shoulder and a good eye, who carried water and worried about walls, and whose name appeared nowhere in Marrowgate except in a planting ledger written by women who weren't supposed to be keeping records at all.

That was when I understood what the ledger actually was. Not a memory. A *registry*. Worse — truer — than anything in the Founders' Hall, because every line in it was proved by trees still standing on the hill. You can forge a deed. You can burn a parish roll. But you cannot un-prune an orchard. The evidence was growing all around me, ring by ring, and the only way left to erase it was to cut the trees down and turn the woman tending them out before anyone official thought to look.

Which was, I realized, precisely and exactly what the first of the month was for.

I closed the ledger and held it against my chest and sat in the blue dark listening to the wind work the rows, and I was honest with myself, because Grandmother's margins demanded honesty: I was afraid. Properly afraid, the bone kind. Not of losing the cottage — I could sleep under a hedge and outlive that. Of losing the *proof*. The ledger was one fire, one thief, one clumsy drop in a stream away from forty years of my family becoming nothing at all, and Hale had four days of advantage and a printing press, and I had a clockmaker, an old man full of shame, and a book of grafting notes.

Against a Trust, a magistrate, and a historian with a patron.

The odds were so bad they were almost funny.

Almost.

A light showed at the observatory, high on its shoulder of the hill — Wendell working late, Bram's lamp below it. I watched it a long time. Somewhere in that tower was whatever Odile Fen had died to hide, and somewhere in this ledger was everything it needed to mean something, and neither half of the truth was any use alone. His half couldn't speak without mine. Mine couldn't be heard without his.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow we'd put the halves together, and plan, and I'd make the clockmaker explain to me every gear of that great machine until I understood it as well as he did, because I was done trusting anyone else to carry my family's name up a dark stairwell for me.

I was blowing out the candle when I heard the hoofbeats on the hill road — one horse, moving fast, uphill, at an hour when nobody rode uphill to Ashcombe unless something had gone badly wrong somewhere. The light at the observatory guttered as whoever held it leaned to look.

The rider rounded the last bend into my yard, dismounted hard, and I had the fire-iron in my hand before I recognized the coat.

Bram Tull, white-faced, out of breath, hat gone, gripping his horse's mane to stay upright.

"Miss Ashcombe," he gasped. "Is the clockmaker here? No — no, of course he's not, it's late, God help me —" He steadied himself against the gatepost, and I saw his hands were shaking. "He has to stop. Tonight. Hale knows. He knows about the letter, he knows about the compartment — he told me so himself an hour ago, and he wasn't angry, that's the worst of it, he *thanked* me —" The old man's voice broke. "Girl, he's already sent for the magistrate's warrant. And the name he gave them for conspiring against the Trust's records —"

He couldn't say it. He didn't have to. I was already pulling my shawl off the peg, already thinking of the dark stairwell and the lamp and the man inside the clock.

"It's Wendell," I said. "Isn't it."

Bram closed his eyes, and nodded.
