## 20. The Watchmaker's Lesson

I did not sleep that night. Or rather, I slept the way a mainspring sleeps — coiled, storing something up, waiting for a hand to let it go.

It was Nell's fault. And Bram's. And Odile Fen's, dead these three years and still handing people trouble through sealed paper like a woman posting letters from beyond the grave. Mostly it was my own fault, which is the honest accounting, and I had resolved — somewhere between the third candle and the fourth — to be an honest man in at least one thing per day.

The thing I kept circling was my master's voice. It came to me unbidden, the way old lessons do, arriving precisely when I least wanted instruction. I was lying on the narrow cot in the observatory's guest room — a room furnished, I now understood, with a generosity meant to soften me like wax — and the memory arrived whole, the way a watch arrives at the bench: complete, running, indifferent to whether you are ready for what it will tell you.

* * *

I was fourteen the winter Ambrose Fennick taught me what a clock is for.

Not how one works — I'd had the how beaten into me by then, or as near to beaten as Ambrose ever came, which was the phrase *again, Wendell, and this time feel it* repeated until my fingers bled and then repeated once more after that. The how was labor. The what-for was something else entirely, and he delivered it on the worst night of my life up to that point, which is, I have since learned, the traditional delivery method for the important things.

My mother had been buried in September. My father — well. My father was a story I will come to, though I didn't know it yet, then; I thought I knew that story, and knowing it wrong is the whole reason I ever climbed the hill road to Marrowgate at all. That winter I was an orphan of convenient fiction, apprenticed by parish wardens to a clockmaker in a town whose name doesn't matter because it has, in every way that counts, been left behind me. I was small for fourteen, all elbows and grievance, and I hated clocks that winter with a purity I can still admire. Clocks ticked whether your mother was alive or not. Clocks struck noon over her funeral procession as if nothing whatever had happened. I had decided, in my fourteen-year-old wisdom, that time itself was a kind of insult.

Ambrose found me at the bench past midnight, not working, just sitting there with a disassembled verge before me and my hands flat on the wood like a man praying badly.

"You've filed that pallet crooked," he said.

"I know."

"Then why are you staring at it instead of fixing it?"

"Because none of it matters." I heard the words come out of me with more courage than I actually possessed, and pressed on before cowardice could catch up. "The clock runs or it doesn't. Time goes anyway. It went today, and yesterday, and it'll go Friday when she's been dead three months, and none of it —" I gestured at the brass, the wheels, the whole glittering uselessness of it — "none of it cares."

Ambrose pulled up his stool. He was sixty then, or seventy, or some age that seemed impossibly ancient to a boy of fourteen and seems heartbreakingly young to me now. He had hands like a map of everywhere work had ever happened. He picked up the crooked pallet, held it to the lamp, and turned it slowly.

"Do you know why I keep shop hours?" he asked. "Why I open at six and close at six and never vary?"

"Because you're dull," I said, and flinched, but he only grunted.

"Because a clock cannot lie," he said. "That's the entire trade, Wendell. Not gears. Not brass. Honesty. A man can lie about how much grain he harvested. Can lie about who his father was, about what he saw, about what he owes you. Men lie so easily you'd think it was breathing. But a clock —" he set the pallet down and tapped it once — "a clock says six o'clock or it says nothing. If it says six and it's five, we don't argue philosophy about it. We don't decide the five o'clock is inconvenient and should be revised. We find out what's broken and we mend it. The clock doesn't get to have opinions. Neither, ideally, does the clockmaker."

"That's not fair," I said. "People need clocks to say what they want them to say sometimes."

"Do they?" He looked genuinely curious, which was worse than anger. "Name me the occasion."

"A funeral," I said, bitterly. "A funeral ought to be able to stop time."

"Ah." He was quiet a moment. "No, lad. A funeral is exactly when you want the clock to be true. Because everyone standing in that churchyard is thinking the same mad thought — that it shouldn't be happening, that the world has made a mistake, that surely it isn't really noon, isn't really September, isn't really final. And then the clock strikes, twelve clean notes, no opinion offered, no mercy and no cruelty either, and every person hears it and knows: this is real. This hour is real. She is gone and the world keeps its accounts honestly, even when we cannot bear the ledger." He put the crooked pallet in my hand and closed my fingers around it. "Time doesn't care about us. Fine. But the *record* of time — that's ours. That's the whole of what civilization is, if you want my view. People agreeing to keep an honest record together. And a clockmaker is a keeper of records, Wendell. Every clock that leaves this shop is a promise that somebody, somewhere, refused to fudge the count."

I filed the pallet again. Straight, that time.

"Now," he said, standing, joints cracking like green firewood. "Feel it. Don't think it. Feel it."

And I felt it — the small truth of the escapement, releasing time one tooth at a time, each tick identical, each tick impartial, the mechanism saying *now, now, now* without preference or fear.

* * *

That was the memory I carried up the hill with me, and it had grown teeth in the climbing.

Because here was the arithmetic I could no longer avoid, lying awake while Marrowgate slept below me under its honest moon: Alderman Corvus Hale had offered me a permanent post, a fine room, wages that would make my master whistle, and the friendship of the most powerful man in the town — contingent upon my fixing the master clock *quietly*. Contingent, in practice, upon my not looking too closely at what had been hidden inside it. Contingent upon my filing the pallet crooked and calling it done.

And I had sat at his table, and drunk his wine, and said nothing.

Worse. At the Founders' Feast I had stood among them in my good coat, listened to Vey's polished paragraph — the orchard-keepers dismissed in thirty flattering words, generations of hands reduced to a decorative flourish about "industrious early stewards" — and I had eaten the food and drunk the toast and kept my mouth shut with everyone watching, and called it prudence.

Prudence. There's a word that works harder than any apprentice in England.

I got up and lit the candle. Bram Tull had given me the letter two days ago — Odile Fen's letter, sealed in black wax, opened only when the mechanism resisted me, and it had resisted, and I had read it, and the ground had tilted and never quite righted. *Dravenmoor*, it said. My name. Orchard-keepers of the second terrace, land granted in the founding registry, erased from every printed history in Marrowgate. My father had not, apparently, been the drifting failure of a man the parish records described. He had been something else — something stolen from him, and from me, so thoroughly that neither of us had known enough to grieve it properly.

And the original registry — the true record, the honest count — was inside the clock I had been hired to mend.

I sat on the edge of the cot in my shirt and thought about Ambrose's voice. *We find out what's broken and we mend it.*

The sabotage in the master clock had not been wear. I had known that within an hour of opening the case — a cut file-mark where no file should have gone, a wheel deliberately mispitched by a craftsman's hand, someone who understood escapements well enough to kill one cleanly. Bram knew. Bram had confessed as much in pieces, the way a man gives up contraband a coin at a time, terrified and relieved in equal measure. Hale wanted the clock fixed quietly because a working master clock draws attention, and attention is the one commodity a forger cannot purchase.

And I — I had a choice dressed as a job. Mend the sabotage, pocket the wage, take the post, and spend my life keeping honest time for men who had falsified every other record in the town. Or the other thing. The thing with no wage attached, and a very clear price list instead: the apprenticeship, the reputation, the comfortable future, possibly my liberty if Hale decided a meddling apprentice was a problem to be solved with magistrates instead of money.

*A clockmaker is a keeper of records.* Ambrose hadn't known what he was handing me that winter night. Or perhaps he had. Perhaps that's the whole trick of teaching — you pass a child a truth wrapped in an ordinary evening, and twenty years later it opens in his hands in a dark room on a hill, exactly when it's needed, and he understands that the lesson was never about pallets at all.

I thought of my father then, properly, for the first time since the letter. Not the parish-record version — the drunkard, the drifter, the cautionary tale the wardens recited when they signed me away — but the version the letter implied: a Dravenmoor, of the second terrace, walking out of Marrowgate with his family's name scraped off the books, carrying whatever he could carry. What do you carry, when everything official says you were never here? You carry the truth. Badly, probably. Bitterly, certainly. But you carry it, because it's the only inheritance nobody can seize.

He had carried it and it had crushed him, or so the story went. I resolved, with the particular grandiosity available only to sleepless men at four in the morning, to carry it better.

* * *

Dawn found me in the clock room, which is where dawn finds me most mornings lately, and found Bram there ahead of me, which is where I most often find Bram — a man who sleeps like a cat that's heard dogs before, in short suspicious intervals close to the thing he's guarding.

He watched me cross to the case without greeting. Old men learn when questions have finished cooking.

"You're going to try it," he said. Not a question after all.

"The escapement," I said. "Properly. From the frame up. Not a repair — a rebuild. The compartment in the inner casing is keyed to the motion work somehow; I felt it when the train ran true last week, a click at the top of the hour that belongs to nothing in the striking train. I can't reach it without rebuilding the escapement around it, and I can't rebuild the escapement without Hale noticing the clock is stopped for days."

"Hale notices everything in this building except the things that matter," Bram said. "But you're right that he'll notice silence. The town sets its days by this clock. A silent noon brings people up the hill faster than bells."

"Then I'll need to choose my silence carefully." I opened the case and looked at the great wheels, sleeping in their oil, hiding a founding registry somewhere in their bones. "And I'll need help keeping watch."

Bram was quiet long enough that I heard the tick of the dead clock's pendulum in my memory, which is a strange thing to hear in a stopped room. When he spoke, his voice had the texture of a man setting down something heavy.

"I helped break this clock, boy. It nearly killed me to lay hands on her wrong, and I did it anyway, and I've been living inside that mistake for nine years. If you're going to open her up and pull the truth out of her —" he straightened, and for a moment the stoop went out of his shoulders entirely — "then you'll not do it alone. Whatever Hale's watching for, he'll not watch for two."

There was a third name in my head, though I didn't say it yet. A name attached to a young woman who tended evicted orchards alone at dusk and looked at me like a question she hadn't decided was worth answering. Nell Ashcombe knew the keepers' side of this story better than anyone living. If I was going to pull her family's record out of a machine, she had a right to be standing there when it came loose.

But first — tonight, midnight, when the observatory was emptiest and Hale's men were furthest —

First I had to do the thing my master swore was beyond apprentices.

I put my hand flat against the cold brass of the great wheel and felt, or imagined I felt, the teeth waiting under my palm like a locked sentence.

*Nobody lies in here,* Ambrose used to say, tapping the case of a finished clock. *Everything else may lie. Not this.*

"Not this," I said aloud, and began laying out my tools.
