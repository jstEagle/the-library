## 22. The Stolen Ledger

The morning I found the cottage emptied, I did what my grandmother always did when the world went wrong: I put the kettle on.

It's a habit that sounds foolish until you've lived it. When a blizzard takes the roof, you put the kettle on. When the bailiff's men come with their papers, you put the kettle on. The fire must be tended, the water must be heated, and somewhere in the small, stubborn ceremony of it, your hands remember that they still belong to you. Grandmother Ashcombe said a woman who can make tea in a ruin is a woman who has not yet lost. I believed her. I'd believed her for eleven years, alone on these terraces, with the wind for company and the evictions piling up in the drawer like kindling.

That morning, the kettle boiled, and I stood in the middle of my own cottage and understood that I had lost.

The ledger was gone.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of her. The ledger was not a book, not really. It was a school exercise book, water-stained, its cover gone soft as chamois from handling, bound with waxed thread in three places because the spine had given up twice. Inside were sixty years of planting records in my grandmother's hand — and before that, transcribed in her mother's hand, and before *that*, fragments in a man's copperplate that must have been my great-grandfather's. Every terrace, every graft, every frost date and blight and harvest going back to the founding. Every name of every keeper who had ever worked the Ashcombe ground. It was the only counterweight in Marrowgate to the fine printed histories that said we had never existed.

And it was gone, along with the loose papers beneath it, the pressed apple blossoms between its pages, and the tin box that had held them. The thief had even taken the shelf bracket the box sat on, which struck me then, as it strikes me now, as the mark of either great thoroughness or great stupidity.

The kettle screamed. I took it off the fire and poured the water over the leaves and stood there while it steeped, because the ceremony does not care whether you believe in it.

Then I sat down at the table where the ledger had lived for eleven years, in its place, weighted with the same flat stone, and I drank my tea in a cottage that had suddenly become a museum of absence, and I did not cry. I want that recorded too. I did not cry. I sat very still and listened to the fire tick and thought, with a clarity that frightened me: *They have won, and they did not even have to be clever about it.*

* * *

Here is what I could not stop doing, all that gray morning: replaying the night before.

Wendell had come up after dark. He'd been at the clock all evening — the escapement rebuild was going badly, he said, though he said it in the way of a man who is secretly delighted by a problem, which I was learning was the only way he ever said anything difficult — and he'd climbed the hill road to my cottage with sawdust in his hair and a look on his face like a boy asking to be let in on a secret. We had spread the ledger on the table between us. That was the thing I kept returning to. *Between us.* I had opened it for him. I had read him the entry from 1791, the one in the copperplate hand, the grafting notes in the margin that matched the gear-tooth pattern Bram Tull had pulled from his old files. My grandfather's design. Wendell had touched the page — asked first, which I noted even then, even in the middle of everything — and traced the little stamped flower my grandmother used to mark every tree that took.

"You know what this is," he'd said, not looking up. "This is a registry. Better than the town's. Theirs is a list of owners. Yours is a list of *work*."

I had felt, for one hour, one candle-burned hour, that the ledger was safe. Not because of locks — I had no locks worth the name — but because it mattered to someone besides me now. I had let myself believe that mattered.

I had walked him to the door at half past ten. I had watched his lantern bob down the terraces toward the observatory road. And I had gone to bed without checking the tin box, because I had never once in eleven years needed to check the tin box.

So the question was simple, and it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: had they followed him? Or had they been watching me all along, and the visit was only the excuse they'd waited for?

Either way, the ledger was gone, and either way, it was mine that was gone, and I had been its keeper the way my mother was a keeper and her mother before her, and a keeper who loses the thing kept is not a keeper at all. She is just a woman alone in a cottage on a hill, waiting for the bailiff.

* * *

Wendell found me there at midday, or near enough. I heard him on the path before I saw him — he has a walk I could pick out of a crowd now, quick and slightly apologetic, as if he's always on the verge of excusing himself from whatever room he's entering — and I did not get up. I was still at the table. The tea had gone cold hours ago. I had moved the flat stone from its place to the middle of the table, where it sat like a paperweight for nothing, like a headstone.

He came in without knocking, which was new, and stopped without speaking, which was not like him at all. He looked at the table. He looked at the empty shelf. I watched him do the arithmetic — I could see it happening behind his eyes, gear by gear — and I watched his face go from confusion to understanding to something that looked almost like fear.

"No," he said.

"You're quick," I said. "They said clockmakers were slow, methodical types."

"Nell." He came to the table and put his hand on the back of the chair opposite mine, and did not sit, as though sitting would make it real and standing kept it at bay. "When? Last night?"

"After you left. Or during. Or before, for all I know. They may have been in the walls for a week, waiting to see who I showed it to." I heard my own voice and was surprised by how level it was. Flat as a frozen pond. "It doesn't matter when. It matters that they knew to look."

"They couldn't have known unless—"

"Unless they followed you. Unless Bram told someone. Unless they've had an eye on this cottage since the day I was born and were only waiting for me to hand them a reason." I turned the flat stone over. It was damp on the bottom. "I've been careful for eleven years, Wendell. Eleven years. I never wrote anything down that wasn't already in her hand. I never took the ledger to town. I never so much as quoted it in front of the bailiff. And the one time — the *one time* — I let another living soul see it—"

"It wasn't your fault."

"Of course it was my fault. It was in my keeping. That's what keeping *means*." I stood up, because sitting had become unbearable, and went to the window where I could see the terraces, my terraces, the bare gray rows going down the hillside like ribs. "You don't know what it was. You're a clockmaker. When you lose a part, you machine a new one. When your master's watch breaks, there's always another watch. But that book was sixty years of— it was *her*. Her handwriting. Her pressed blossoms. The entry from the year I was born — she wrote my name in it, Wendell, the day I was born, *Nell Ashcombe, keeper's line, fourth generation*, with a little flower stamped beside it because I was the tree that took. And now it's in some alderman's drawer, or worse, in his stove, and the last true record that we ever existed is smoke."

The words came out of me in a flood, and once they started they would not stop, and I heard my voice crack on *smoke* and hated it. I am not a woman who cracks. I have stood in front of magistrates. I have stood in front of Hale's men with their eviction papers and their soft smiles. I have buried a grandmother alone in frozen ground because the town gravedigger suddenly couldn't recall the way to my hill.

But the ledger was different. The ledger was the whole war in one tin box, and I had left the door open.

Wendell did the worst possible thing. He tried to comfort me.

"I'll make you tea," he said.

I looked at him. The kettle was cold on the stove. The tea things were exactly where they had been since morning, since before morning, since I had performed the whole ceremony at dawn with hands that didn't feel like mine. "There's tea in the pot," I said. "Cold. From six hours ago."

"Right," he said. He looked at the pot. He looked at the fire, which had burned down to almost nothing. And then — I will give him this forever, I will tell this story at his expense and my own for the rest of my life — he knelt down at my hearth and began feeding splints of kindling into it, one at a time, with the concentration of a man defusing a bomb, and he said, "My master had a saying. When a movement's seized up, you don't force it. You find the point of least damage and you work from there."

"I'm not a clock, Wendell."

"No," he agreed, still fussing with the kindling. "You're considerably more important, and considerably less forgiving of amateur repair work. But the principle holds." He got the fire going, badly, filling the room with smoke before it caught, and waved the smoke away from his face with a paper he pulled from his pocket, and I saw that the paper was covered in his cramped, slanting handwriting — gear calculations, escape-wheel counts — and something in me twisted at the sight of it, at all his careful numbers, all his careful plans, and how little they mattered now.

"The ledger was the evidence," I said, when I could trust my voice again. "You understand that. Your clock might hold the original registry, if you can ever get it open — and that's an *if* the size of a cathedral. But the ledger was the living proof. Sixty years of stewardship in one hand. The thing that showed we didn't just own the ground, we *made* it. Without it, even if the compartment opens, even if the registry's inside — it's old paper, Wendell. Old paper can be called a forgery. Hale's historians will call it a forgery before the ink's dry. The ledger was the thing that proved the old paper was telling the truth."

"I know."

"And now it's gone, and I'm sorry, and I'm so tired, and I don't—" My voice went again. I turned back to the window. "I don't know what I'm keeping anymore."

The fire crackled behind me. He didn't answer right away, and I was grateful for that, because the worst comfort is the quick comfort, the comfort that arrives before the hurt has finished arriving.

When he did speak, it was from close behind me, and he had that tone he gets — the one that's half apology and half stubbornness, like a man arguing with a stuck valve.

"Can I say something that will make you angrier?"

"Almost certainly."

"Good. I'd rather have you angry than — than the other thing. The flat voice." He came around beside me, not touching, just standing at the window too, looking out at the bare terraces as if he could read them. "You said a keeper who loses the thing kept isn't a keeper. That's wrong. It's wrong as a matter of fact, and I can prove it, because I've been thinking about nothing but clocks for three weeks and it turns out everything is clocks."

"Wonderful."

"Every clock ever made has lost time. Every single one. The best regulator in Greenwich drifts. That's not failure, that's — that's what time *does*, it slips through every mechanism we build, and the work of a clockmaker isn't preventing the slip. It's noticing it. Correcting it. Setting the thing true again and again and again, forever, knowing it will drift again tomorrow." He turned and looked at me, and his eyes were doing that thing where they're too earnest for his own comfort, and he plows on anyway. "You lost the ledger. It's the worst thing that's happened, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't. But the keeping isn't the tin box. The keeping is you. It's what's in your head — every entry you ever read, every date, every name. You told me you know it by heart. You told me your grandmother made you recite the frost tables until you could do it in your sleep. That's not nothing, Nell. That's the ledger, in a tin box that can't be stolen, because it's locked behind your ribs."

I wanted to throw him out. I want that understood. I stood at my own window with my grandmother's flat stone cold on the table behind me and I wanted to open the door and put him through it, because his comfort was clumsy and mechanical and built out of clock metaphors, because he had no right to be hopeful when I had earned the right to despair, and because — because he was right, and being right when a person is grieving is an act of aggression.

"They took her handwriting," I said, and my voice finally broke all the way, and I let it. "They took the pressed blossoms. They took the flower she stamped beside my name."

"I know," Wendell said. And then, uselessly, sincerely, in the voice of a man who has absolutely no idea what to do with a crying woman and is going to try anyway: "We'll get it back."

"You can't know that."

"No. But I'm going to say it until it's true. My master says that's half of any repair — you state the intended function, and then you work backward from the intention." He hesitated, and then he did touch me, one hand on my shoulder, light as a man setting a balance wheel, and I was too tired to shrug it off, and it was, I admit it, it was exactly the weight I needed. "We'll get it back. And whoever took it made a mistake, because now they've shown us they're frightened. You don't steal from a woman you're not afraid of."

I wiped my face with the heel of my hand. "Hale isn't afraid of me. Hale has never thought about me at all, except as a line item."

"Then he's afraid of the ledger," Wendell said. "Which means the ledger was worth stealing. Which means it was telling the truth." He said it simply, the way he says true things, as if facts were gears and all you had to do was mesh them, and I felt something shift in my chest — not hope, not yet, hope is a luxury I'd stopped stocking years ago — but something adjacent to hope. Something in hope's neighborhood. "And there's this," he went on, and now he was almost talking to himself, working the problem the way he worked a movement, turning it over, finding the tooth. "They took the ledger the night after we found the gear pattern matched your grandfather's design. The night after Bram gave us his files. That's not a coincidence, Nell. That's a *response*. They're not ahead of us. They're reacting to us. And a mechanism that's only reacting—"

"—is already losing," I finished.

He smiled. It was a terrible, tired, sawdust-covered smile, and it was the first thing in two days that had made me feel anything but cold.

* * *

We spent the afternoon doing what I now understand is the only thing you can do after a loss: inventory.

I went through the cottage like a bailiff, listing what was gone and what remained. Gone: the ledger, the tin box, the loose papers, the pressed blossoms, the shelf bracket. Gone also, I discovered with a fresh lurch, the bundle of my grandmother's letters from the Marrowgate seed merchants — correspondence that proved decades of trade, decades of presence, decades of *us*. They had been thorough. Whoever came had known exactly what to take, which meant they had known exactly what the papers were, which meant someone had described them in detail, which meant—

"Vey," I said.

Wendell looked up from the fire he was still fussing with. "The historian?"

"He interviewed me. Three weeks ago. Sat right where you're sitting and asked me sweet, gentle questions about my family and what we'd grown and whether we kept any records, and I told him — I *told* him — about the ledger, because he seemed honest, because he seemed like a man who loved old paper, and God forgive me, I wanted to show off. I wanted someone from the town to know we existed." I sat down hard on the bench. "He wrote it all down in that little book of his."

"Or he didn't need to," Wendell said slowly. "He's writing the official history. He'd have had access to Hale. To whatever Hale knows about this cottage." He was quiet a moment, turning it. "But Vey also asked me questions no loyal historian would ask. About the sabotage. About what I thought had really happened to the clock." He rubbed his jaw, leaving a fresh smear of soot. "I don't know what he is. That's the honest answer. I don't know."

"Then we assume the worst and hope to be surprised," I said. "That's the Ashcombe method. It's in the ledger." I stopped. The joke landed on both of us like a dropped wrench, and for a moment neither of us spoke, and then Wendell made a sound that was half laugh and half wince, and I found, to my astonishment, that I was laughing too — the ragged, unlovely laughter of the exhausted — and that the cottage felt, for one moment, less like a museum of absence and more like a place where two people were refusing, together, to be finished.

When it passed, Wendell stood and brushed off his knees.

"I have to get back before dark," he said. "Bram's watching the clock, but he's a nervous watch, and the escapement won't rebuild itself." He paused at the door, and I saw him wrestling with something, the way he wrestles with a stubborn screw, working it back and forth before committing. "Nell. The frost tables. The grafting notes. The names. If it comes to it — if it comes to testimony, to the magistrate, to the town — you can recite it. All of it?"

"Every entry," I said. "Grandmother's rule. A keeper's memory is the second copy. The book is the third."

"Then they stole the third copy," Wendell said, "and the second copy is standing in this room, and the first copy—" he pointed out the window, at the terraces, at the bare gray rows going down the hillside, at the old rootstock still living under the weeds, still alive, still *ours*, waiting like everything else in this town for someone to set it true — "the first copy is that hillside, and it's been here the whole time, and no one has managed to steal it yet."

He went. I watched his lantern bob down the terraces, the same as the night before, and I stood in the doorway until the dark took it.

Then I went inside, and I took down the flat stone from the table, and I set it back in its place on the empty shelf — not as a headstone, I decided. As a placeholder. The way a clockmaker sets a temporary part, meaning to come back, meaning to machine the true one, meaning to finish the work.

I was blowing out the candle when I heard it: footsteps on the path below, quick and light, and a knock at my door — three raps, hesitant, unfamiliar. I took down the poker before I opened it, and there on my step, gray-faced and out of breath, lantern shaking in his hand, stood Bram Tull.

"They've arrested no one yet," he said, "but they will. Nell — they know about the clock. They know about the compartment." He swallowed, and I saw that the old man was terrified, and that he had climbed my hill in the dark anyway. "Hale's men were at the observatory an hour ago. They took the old pattern files. Every one. And Lachlan Vey was with them."
