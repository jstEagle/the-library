## 23. Vey's Offer

I have been called many things in my profession, most of them behind my back and all of them deserved at one time or another: pedant, parasite, pen-for-hire. But the accusation that has stung me longest — because it is the truest — is that I am a man who mistakes a finished manuscript for a finished truth. A historian who cannot tell the difference is not a historian. He is a typesetter with pretensions.

I had been in Marrowgate eleven days when I first suspected my sources were lying to me. Not lying the way people lie when they are caught out — no flush, no stumble — but lying the way a well-kept garden lies, everything trimmed and watered and arranged so that you never once think to ask what grew there before.

It began, as these things do, with a footnote.

* * *

Mr. Alderman Hale had given me the run of the town archive on my second morning, unlocking it himself with the air of a man bestowing a knighthood. The archive occupied two rooms behind the Founders' Hall, and it was, I will grant him this, beautiful. Ledgers bound in calfskin. Deeds copied in a clerk's hand so even it looked printed. A founding charter under glass, its ink still confident after ninety years.

"Everything you need," Hale had said, resting his hand briefly on my shoulder — a gesture I have since learned he distributes like communion. "The truth of Marrowgate speaks for itself. It needs only a voice to carry it."

Flattery, of course. But I am a vain man, and flattery works on me the way oil works on a clock: it does not change the mechanism, only lets it run more smoothly.

For nine days I wrote. The history took shape the way such histories always do — brave founders, hard winters, an orchard planted by generous hands, prosperity earned and honestly kept. I had done perhaps a dozen of these by now. Towns are alike in their vanities the way children are alike in their tantrums; only the names change, and sometimes not even those.

The footnote was on page forty-one. I had written: *The terraced orchards above the town were established by the founding families in the spring of 1759, and tended thereafter by hired labor under their direction.*

A small sentence. An innocent sentence. Except that I had seen the terraces myself, walked them the evening of my arrival, and something about them had nagged at me the way a misprinted date nags — not wrong exactly, but *off*, the way a portrait is off when the eyes have been repainted.

Terraces of that construction — dry-stone retaining walls, drainage channels cut at the fall line, the whole system stepped to catch the morning sun off the eastern slope — were not the work of gentlemen directing labor from below. I had seen enough of them in the hill country to know. That was engineering. That was generations of accumulated, transmitted, *specific* knowledge, the kind that lives in families and dies with them when the families go.

And the families, I noticed, were not in the archive.

Oh, there were laborers mentioned — seasonal hands, paid accounts, a strike or two smoothed over in a single dry line. But no names that recurred. No dynasties of keepers. In every other agricultural settlement I had chronicled, the men who actually worked the land acquired names the way rivers acquire towns. Here the orchards simply *were*, as if the apples ripened out of civic virtue alone.

I went back to the ledgers on the tenth day and read them differently. Not for what they said. For what they were.

They were copies. Every deed, every grant, every founding document — fair copies, made in the same clerk's hand, in the same ink batch, within what looked like a span of months some thirty years after the founding itself. Nowhere was there an original. Nowhere was there a document with a hole burned in it, or a water stain, or the ordinary damage of ninety years of mice and damp and careless hands. The archive of Marrowgate was not old. It was *restored* — the way a man restores his reputation, by replacing everything inconvenient.

A forger with real skill, I thought, setting down the ledger with a strange calm. And a forger with real authority, because no clerk alive could have emptied a town archive of its originals without someone powerful holding the door.

That was the moment my conscience woke, and I will be honest about how it woke: not with trumpets. With professional embarrassment. I had been hired to write the truth of Marrowgate, and I had very nearly produced another copy.

* * *

I want to say something in my own defense before I go further, because a man writing his own account ought to extend himself the courtesy he demands of his subjects.

I did not come to Marrowgate corrupt. I came hungry, which is different, though they end in the same place if you're careless. I was the son of a printer who died owing money to better printers, and I had clawed my way up through obituaries and almanacs until I could call myself a historian and charge accordingly. Commissions like Hale's — a handsome fee, a fine house, my name embossed on the title page — were the reward for twenty years of writing other men's memories into respectable prose. If the memories were tidied along the way, well. History, I told myself, is a garden too. Some pruning is a kindness.

But there is a difference between pruning and paving, and I had spent ten days walking on pavement and calling it grass.

So on the eleventh day I did something I had never done in twenty years of commissions. I began asking questions where the answers might cost me.

* * *

The clockmaker's apprentice was the obvious place to start, which is precisely why I had avoided him. Wendell Dravenmoor was young, watchful, and — I had noted this at our first interview with the particular pleasure a craftsman takes in another's craft — unusually precise. When I asked him about the master clock's history he gave me facts the way a bank gives coin: counted, weighed, and no more than requested. Men like that are either hiding nothing or hiding everything, and it takes time to learn which.

Also, Hale had mentioned him. Casually, over dinner, in the tone of a man mentioning weather: *"The apprentice is competent enough. Best not to let him fill your head with mechanical difficulties — the clock is old, it wants simple repairs, nothing more."*

When a powerful man tells you not to listen to someone, that someone becomes, professionally speaking, essential listening.

I found him at the observatory in the late afternoon, sleeves rolled, bent over the great clock's case with a lamp turned low. He heard me on the stairs and did not turn around.

"You walk loudly for a historian," he said.

"I walk loudly for everyone. It's why I'm rarely surprised." I came into the clock room and stood where he could see me, because startling a man holding tiny tools seemed unwise. "May I watch? I promise not to ask questions."

"That would be a first."

"For both of us, then. We'll mark the occasion."

He almost smiled. Almost. He went back to his work, and I watched him work, and for a quarter hour neither of us spoke, which is the fastest way I know of learning whether I can trust a man. Liars cannot bear silence. They fill it the way bad writers fill a page, with anything that comes to hand.

Dravenmoor let the silence sit between us like a third person, and worked inside it.

"The escapement's been rebuilt," he said finally, not looking up. "You didn't ask, so I'll tell you anyway. Someone rebuilt it badly, years ago, and I've been undoing their work all week."

"Badly on purpose?"

His file stopped moving. Just for a breath. Then it resumed.

"Why would a man ask that?"

"Because I've spent ten days reading a town archive in which every document is thirty years younger than it claims to be," I said. "Because I've walked orchard terraces built by master engineers whom your town's official record describes as anonymous hired labor. Because Mr. Hale told me not to listen to you, which makes you the most interesting man in Marrowgate. And because—" I paused, and decided, since I had come this far, to spend the whole purse— "because I believe I have been hired to forge a history, and I would very much like to discover I'm wrong before I finish writing it."

The file stopped again. This time it stayed stopped.

He set down his tools with the exaggerated care of a man buying himself time to think, and turned, and looked at me for a long moment. He had the kind of face that shows its decisions the way a dial shows the hour — you could watch the hands move.

"If you're wrong," he said, "you've just insulted the most powerful man in this town, in his own clock room, to his own employee."

"Yes."

"And if you're right?"

"Then I've insulted him anyway, and it doesn't matter, because the book is worthless either way." I took off my hat, which I do when I mean business, and which also gives my hands something to do. "Mr. Dravenmoor. I am going to ask you a question, and I ask you to consider carefully whether answering it helps you or harms you, because I am not yet certain whose side I am on, and neither, I suspect, are you. Has anyone given you reason to believe the founding records of Marrowgate are false?"

* * *

He did not answer immediately. He looked past me, at the great clock, its pendulum hanging still, its face blank as a held breath. And whatever calculation he performed, he performed it thoroughly, because when he spoke again his voice had changed — quieter, and careful, and underneath the care something that sounded almost like relief.

"There's a letter," he said. "From the archivist who died last winter. Odile Fen. Bram Tull kept it sealed until the clock resisted repair, per her instruction. I opened it three weeks ago." His jaw tightened. "It says the original registry — the true one, the founding grants, the land records — is hidden inside this clock. And it says my family was among those erased from the record. Orchard keepers. Dravenmoors."

I confess I sat down. There was a stool by the wall and I used it without ceremony, because my knees had opinions of their own.

"Your family," I repeated.

"I didn't know until three weeks ago that I had one worth the name." He said it flatly, but flatness is its own kind of wound-dressing, and I recognized the bandage because I wear the same kind. "The letter names Odile Fen as witness to the forgery. Names Hale's grandfather as the hand that did it. She hid the originals where she thought no one would look — inside the thing the town trusts most."

"A clock," I said slowly. "Hidden inside a clock. In a town whose entire civic identity is built on being able to tell the time."

"Hale can rewrite paper," Dravenmoor said. "He can't rewrite noon."

I laughed — I couldn't help it, a short ugly laugh, the laugh of a man hearing his own epitaph drafted by someone funnier than himself. Because here was the joke, and it was on me: I had spent twenty years believing that whoever controlled the printing press controlled the past. And here was a nineteen-year-old apprentice explaining to me that the past keeps its own time, and waits.

"Here is my problem, Mr. Dravenmoor," I said, when I had recovered. "My commission ends in nineteen days. On that day, Mr. Hale's version of Marrowgate goes to press with my name on it, and it will stand for a hundred years, and every Dravenmoor and Ashcombe and Fen who ever lived here will be a paragraph of flattering fog. Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless I have something else to print." I leaned forward. "Understand me. I cannot publish an accusation. Accusations are cheap; Hale owns the magistrate and half the town council, and he would bury me in libel suits until my grandchildren inherited the debt. What I need is evidence. Documents. The registry, the grants, the deposition — the actual paper, verifiable, in my hands, before the press runs." I met his eyes. "Can you open that compartment?"

"Not yet." He said it without flinching, which I respected. "The lock is keyed to a gear tooth — a specific tooth, cut to a pattern I don't have yet. Opening it any other way destroys the escapement and possibly the contents. I have to build the key. It will take every day I have and probably a few I don't."

"Nineteen days."

"Nineteen days."

We looked at each other across the silent clock, two men measuring the same span with different instruments.

"Then here is my offer," I said, standing, putting my hat back on, because a bargain should be struck standing. "You get me that compartment open and those documents into my hands before publication day. In exchange, I hold my manuscript — I will tell Hale I'm polishing, revising, awaiting final verification, whatever fiction buys us time. And when you produce the evidence, I print it. All of it. The registry reproduced in full, Fen's deposition, the keepers' names restored to the record — with my name on the title page taking full responsibility for the correction." I paused. "I will lose this commission. I will likely lose others; Hale has friends, and historians gossip worse than laundresses. My career may not survive it."

"And why would you risk that?" Dravenmoor asked. Not hostile. Genuinely asking. It was the question I'd been asking myself since the footnote, and I owed him a true answer, because he had just handed me the largest truth in Marrowgate and I had offered him nothing but ambition in return.

I thought about my father, the printer, dying owing money. I thought about the first falsified obituary I ever wrote, aged seventeen, softening a magistrate's suicide into an accident because the widow asked and the extra coin bought a week of bread. I thought about twenty years of gardens, pruned and pruned, until I could no longer find the wild stock under all the trimming.

"Because I became a historian to write what happened," I said, "and somewhere along the way I settled for writing what was *permitted*. And a man who does that isn't a historian. He's a very literate accomplice." I straightened my cuffs. "I am tired of being an accomplice, Mr. Dravenmoor. It pays well and it costs everything."

He studied me for a long moment. Then he extended his hand, and I took it, and his grip was a craftsman's grip — dry, exact, unhurried.

"One condition," he said. "There's a woman named Nell Ashcombe. Her grandmother kept a planting ledger — the keepers' own record, written in their own hand. It's counter-evidence, and it's hers, and it gets credited in your book by name. Whatever happens, she is not a footnote."

"She'll be a chapter," I said. "If the ledger survives long enough to be read."

Something crossed his face at that — a flicker, quickly mastered, of a fear he already carried. I noted it and filed it away, because a historian learns to note what a man fears almost before the man knows it himself.

"It will survive," Dravenmoor said. "I'll see to it."

* * *

I walked down from the observatory in the blue hour, and the lamps of Marrowgate were coming on below me one by one, and the town looked exactly as it had looked on my first evening — pretty, orderly, certain of itself. A town that believed its own story because the story was all it had ever been shown.

Nineteen days. Somewhere in that great dark clock above me was a chamber of paper that could unmake everything Alderman Hale had built, and somewhere below me was a young woman guarding a ledger with her life, and somewhere between them was a boy with files and files and files, cutting a key to a lock his enemies didn't know existed.

And somewhere in the Founders' Hall, I knew, a manuscript lay in a drawer with my name on it, waiting to make all of it permanent.

I had shaken hands with the enemy, I thought — and then corrected myself, climbing down through the dusk. No. I had shaken hands with the only honest man in Marrowgate. The enemy was the one who paid me.

Tomorrow I would tell Hale the book needed another fortnight. Tomorrow I would begin the delicate work of lying to a liar, which is a trade I know intimately and have never once practiced in self-defense.

Tonight, though — tonight I allowed myself one small professional satisfaction, the first in eleven days. I took out my notebook, turned to page forty-one, and struck a single line through the sentence about anonymous hired labor.

In the margin, in pencil, I wrote: *To be corrected.*

It was, I reflected, the first true sentence I had written in Marrowgate.
