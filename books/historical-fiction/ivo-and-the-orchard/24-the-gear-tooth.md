## 24. The Gear Tooth

The pattern had been sitting under my nose for three weeks, which is the sort of thing a clockmaker is supposed to notice and the sort of thing clockmakers never do.

It happened on the fourth morning after I told Hale his history needed another fortnight — a lie that cost me nothing in the telling and everything in the keeping, since he smiled at me across his desk with the particular warmth of a man who believes he has already bought you. I came back up the hill road with my hands shaking and went straight to the observatory cellar where Bram kept the old workshop, and I pulled out every drawer of patterns and templates he had, looking for anything that might match the tooth-form of the keying gear.

And there it was. Third drawer from the left, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark.

I want to be precise about what I found, because precision is all I have to offer anyone. It was not a drawing. It was not even a proper template. It was a brass plate, hand-filed, the profile of a single gear tooth cut into its edge like a sample — the kind of thing a maker keeps so he can check his work against it over and over without re-measuring. And stamped into the corner, small enough that you'd miss it if you weren't looking for ownership, was a mark: an ash leaf over a circle divided into four.

The same mark, tooth for tooth and line for line, that appeared on the inside of the master clock's false compartment door.

Whoever had built that compartment had built it to be opened by one specific gear — and had left behind the recipe for making it.

"Bram," I called up the stairs, and my voice cracked on his name like an apprentice's, which was fitting. "Bram, come down here. I need you to tell me where you got this."

* * *

Nell Ashcombe had been awake since before dawn, because the frost season was coming and the young trees on the third terrace needed mulching before their roots learned what winter meant. She was in the cottage she'd been born in, wrapping straw around the base of a sapling no taller than herself, when Wendell Dravenmoor came up the orchard path at a pace that could only be called running for a man who spent his life bent over a bench.

She straightened and watched him come. She had learned by now not to trust him when he moved quickly. Quick men wanted things, and men who wanted things in Marrowgate usually wanted them from her.

"You look like a man who's found something," she said.

"I've found something." He held up a piece of oilcloth like a man presenting evidence at trial. "May I?" He didn't wait; he sat down on her chopping block, unrolled the cloth, and laid the brass plate on the block's scarred surface.

Nell looked at it. Then she looked again.

It was nothing. A scrap of metal, a tooth-shaped notch, a leaf stamped in a corner. She had seen a hundred such marks in her grandmother's ledger margins, doodled beside entries like *plum grafted, south wall, good take* — little signatures the keepers used among themselves because paper could be confiscated but memory couldn't. Her grandmother had drawn the ash-and-quarters beside every entry about the terraces' drainage works. *His mark,* the ledger said once, in faded ink. *Grandfer's mark. He put it on everything he made, vain old man, and thank heaven for it.*

"Where did you get this?" Nell said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, the way it did when something moved too fast underneath it.

"It was in Bram's workshop. In a drawer of patterns he swears he never catalogued." Wendell turned the plate toward the light. "He says it came with a crate of tools he bought at auction twenty-two years ago, after the last evictions. He says he never looked at it closely."

"Twenty-two years." Nell picked the plate up. It was lighter than it should have been, or heavier; her hands had stopped reporting accurately. "You're telling me this has been sitting in a drawer below the clock my whole life."

"That's what I'm telling you."

"And the mark?"

"I don't know the mark. But I know the tooth." He took the plate back gently, reverently, the way he handled other people's watches. "This is the profile of the gear that opens the compartment. Exactly. Not approximately — exactly. The involute curve, the root radius, even this odd flattening at the tip. Nobody cuts a tooth like that unless they mean it to mate with one specific wheel." He paused, and she watched him decide to say the next part carefully. "Your grandmother's ledger. Does it mention a gear? A design? Anything about the clock?"

Nell thought of the ledger sitting under the loose hearthstone where it had lived through two inspections and one robbery attempt that had failed only because the thief hadn't known to look down. She thought about how much of it she'd shown him already, and how each time she showed him more, she felt less like she was losing something and more like she was setting it down somewhere safer than her own two hands.

"There's a page," she said slowly. "Near the end. It's not planting notes. It's drawings — gears, springs, little numbers in the margins. I always thought Grandfer Ashcombe had helped repair the observatory clock once and copied the parts down for interest. Grandmother wrote above it: *his clever fingers, wasted on apples.*"

"Wasted on apples," Wendell repeated softly. He laughed, but it wasn't a laugh that meant humor; it was the laugh she'd begun to recognize in him, the one that came out when grief arrived wearing work clothes. "Miss Ashcombe. Your grandfather designed the lock."

* * *

Here is what I understood, standing in Nell's cottage with her family's dead humming in the plaster: I had been thinking about the keying gear all wrong.

I had been thinking of it as a problem of manufacture. Machine a wheel, cut the teeth, fit the lock, done — a fortnight's careful work, hard but honest, the kind of job my master would have priced at three guineas plus materials. What I had not understood until I saw Nell lay her ledger open on the table beside Bram's brass plate, and watched the two profiles lie together edge to edge like hands joining, was that I was not manufacturing a part.

I was completing one. Twenty-three years late.

"He drew it," Nell said. Her finger moved down the ledger page. The drawings were fine and sure, done in the confident shorthand of a man who knew metal the way she knew soil. "See here — he numbered the teeth. Thirty-one. An odd number. Why would anyone use an odd number?"

"So the same teeth don't meet the same teeth twice running," I said. "It distributes wear. Any decent maker would—" I stopped. "No. That's not why he did it. Look at the note in the margin."

She bent close. The handwriting was cramped, urgent, unlike the tidy planting entries: *Odd count. Only the right wheel opens it. Let them hunt.*

"Let them hunt," I read aloud, and felt the hair rise on my arms. "He built it so it couldn't be forced. He knew they'd come looking. He built the compartment knowing the men who erased him would someday stand in front of it and fail."

Nell was very quiet. When she spoke, her voice had a steadiness in it that made me ashamed of my own shaking hands. "He knew they'd win for a while. That's what nobody understands about keepers. We plant trees we'll never sit under. Grandfer just planted in metal instead of apple stock."

Outside, the wind came up the valley and moved through the bare terraces with a sound like slow applause.

"The plate gives me the profile," I said, "but your grandfather's notes give me the intent. Miss Ashcombe — Nell — I need both. I can't cut this tooth from a template alone. There are tolerances here I can feel but can't name, choices a man makes for reasons only he knows. Unless..." I touched the margin note. "Unless he wrote them down. Did he write anything else? Any working notes?"

Nell looked at the page for a long moment. Then she turned to the back cover of the ledger, worked her thumbnail into the spine's stitching, and pulled — and a folded sheet of thin paper slid out onto the table, yellowed, covered on both sides in the same cramped hand.

"My grandmother," she said, "hid everything twice."

* * *

Bram Tull had not slept well, which was no change; Bram Tull had not slept well in twenty-two years, and had lately given up pretending the two conditions were unrelated.

He stood in the doorway of the old workshop and watched Wendell work, and what he felt was too large and too tangled to be named, so he did what he always did with feelings too large to name: he made tea and said nothing.

The boy had the pattern pinned flat under lamps now, and beside it the dead keeper's working notes, borrowed overnight from Nell with a solemnity neither of them had joked about. Wendell worked the way Bram imagined Odile Fen had worked — total, silent, absorbed, the outside world reduced to a nuisance. He had roughed a blank on the lathe. He had cut the blank true. Now he sat with a graver finer than any Bram had seen, beginning the first tooth, and his hands did not shake, though Bram's own did, watching.

"I knew him," Bram said finally. It came out louder than he intended in the stone room. "Ashcombe. Thomas Ashcombe. I carried his cider press screw up the hill myself, the summer of '25. Big iron devil. He gave me a mug of cider and talked my ear off about gear trains for an hour, and I understood maybe one word in ten, and I liked him anyway. Everyone liked him. That was the trouble, later. You can't erase a man everyone likes all at once. You have to do it a family at a time."

Wendell had stopped cutting. He didn't turn around. Bram was grateful for that; some confessions need a listener's back.

"Hale's grandfather came to me in '26," Bram went on. "Told me the registry needed relocating for safekeeping, told me the compartment in the casing was temporary, told me the keying gear was being sent away to be destroyed. And I believed him, or I told myself I believed him, which is the same sin wearing a cleaner shirt. I filed the false drawer. I cut the seal shelf. My hands made half of that trap." He looked at his hands, spread open on the table, ordinary old hands, guilty as ever. "Thomas Ashcombe's design, holding shut the proof that Thomas Ashcombe existed. If there's a joke in that, lad, I've never found the bottom of it."

"There's no joke," Wendell said quietly. "There's a key. And we're cutting it."

Bram crossed the room and set the tea down by the lamp. Then, because his hands needed occupation and his conscience needed a task it could actually finish, he took up the second graver — the one he'd used for forty years, the one Odile had teased him about, worn to a nub — and pulled a stool to the bench.

"Root angle first," he said. "Thomas always cut the root before the flank. Said a tooth was a tree: roots before branches. Now tell me what his notes say about the tip relief, and speak slowly, because my eyes are seventy years old and your future is in millimeters."

They worked through the morning. Somewhere past noon, Wendell's graver slipped — one ten-thousandth too deep along a flank, invisible to any eye but his own — and he set the tool down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes and swore, once, with feeling.

Bram picked the blank up, turned it in the lamplight, and handed it back.

"Do it again," he said. "Thomas would have. He threw away more good wheels than most men ever made. Perfection isn't pride, boy. It's respect for whoever comes after." He hesitated, then added, because it was true and because saying it cost him something he deserved to pay: "Someone came after us. That was the whole idea."

Wendell picked the graver back up.

* * *

Nell came up at dusk with bread and cold chicken wrapped in a cloth, and stayed.

She had told herself she was bringing food because two men hunched over a bench forgot meals, and this was true, but it was not the whole truth and she had promised herself she would stop lying to herself about Wendell Dravenmoor before the lying became a habit. So she stayed. She sat on the stool by the brazier and mended a saddle strap while they worked, and the sound of it — graver on brass, needle through leather, the brazier ticking — filled the old workshop with a domesticity so ordinary it hurt.

It was near midnight when Wendell finished.

She knew the moment without being told. His shoulders dropped a fraction, and his breath went out of him all at once, like a man surfacing. He lifted the gear from the wax with tweezers and held it to the lamp, and Nell set down her mending and came to stand beside him, because whatever else was between them or wasn't, this moment belonged to her too.

It was beautiful, if a person could say such a thing about a gear and not be laughed at. Small as a shilling. Brass, bright-cut, thirty-one perfect teeth around a rim thinner than a fingernail. In the center hub, Wendell had stamped a mark: the ash leaf and quartered circle, copied faithfully from the template.

"I shouldn't have," he said, almost apologetic. "It's not structural. It's vanity."

"It's attribution," Nell said. "Keep it."

Bram brought the second test blank — the checking piece — and the three of them climbed the tower stairs to the clock in a silence that had weight. The great case stood in the dark like a patient waiting for surgery. Wendell lit the work lamps, opened the access panel, and reached into the movement with both hands, and Nell watched him find the hidden seat by touch alone, the way he must have done it a dozen nights now, alone, afraid, patient past all patience.

He fitted the gear to its arbor. He seated it. He turned it one tooth, two, five — and the mechanism inside the false door woke. Nell heard it: a click like a knuckle cracking, then a whisper of released spring, then the soft, enormous sound of twenty-three years ending.

The compartment door swung open an inch.

Nobody moved. The clock ticked overhead, indifferent and true. Wendell's hand hung in the air beside the gap, not touching it.

"You do it," he said suddenly, and stepped back, and held the door toward her.

Nell looked at him. "It's your work."

"It's your grandfather's lock. It waited for your family. It should open for them."

Her eyes stung, and she let them, and she reached past his hand and pushed the little door wide.

Inside, in the lamplight, lay bundles of paper tied with ribbon gone brittle, a leather-bound register fat as a Bible, and on top of it all, sealed in wax stamped with a signet Nell didn't recognize, an envelope addressed in a strong woman's hand:

*To whomsoever opens this — read me first, and aloud.*

Wendell picked up the envelope and turned it over, and Nell saw his face go pale by lamplight.

"What is it?" she said.

"The seal," he said. "It's addressed by name." He held it out to her with a hand that was not quite steady. "*To Wendell Dravenmoor, clockmaker, if he ever comes.*"

Below them, far down the hill, a dog began to bark in Marrowgate, and then another, and then the night watchman's bell rang once — the signal, Bram said quietly from the doorway, that somebody official was on the road up to the observatory at one o'clock in the morning.
