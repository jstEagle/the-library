## 26. The Refusal

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in rich men's houses. It isn't the silence of empty rooms — I know that one well; my master's workshop after closing hours was full of it, and it never troubled me. No. The silence of a rich man's house is a *manufactured* silence, thick as felt, laid down deliberately underfoot so that no sound ever arrives uninvited. Carpets that swallow footsteps. Doors on hinges so well-oiled they might be ashamed to creak. Walls hung with enough fabric and gilt to smother any noise a poor man's truth might make trying to get out.

I sat in that silence at Alderman Corvus Hale's dinner table and thought: *a clock could never be built like this house.* A clock is honest precisely because it cannot afford this kind of quiet. Every gear must announce itself or the whole mechanism fails.

The dinner had been excellent. I want that on the record, since so little else about the evening would survive honest accounting. Quail, if you're wondering. I had never eaten quail before. It tasted like something that had been raised specifically to die for men who owned observatories.

Hale had spent two hours being magnificent. He told the founding story again — the one from Vey's draft history, the one with the brave settlers and the generous land division and the orchards planted "in common fellowship" — and he told it with such warmth, such *conviction*, that I caught myself twice almost believing it. That is the thing about Corvus Hale that no one warns you about. You expect a villain to lie badly, or to lie coldly. He lies beautifully. He lies the way a cathedral organ plays — you feel the truth of it in your chest before your mind has any say in the matter.

Nell would have hated every minute. Bram would have been sick into the soup tureen. I ate my quail and said nothing, which is its own species of sickness, and I knew it even as I swallowed.

* * *

It was over the port — I had been given perhaps three fingers' worth in a glass that probably cost more than my master's entire stock of jeweled bearings — that Hale finally set down his own untouched glass and looked at me with the expression of a man preparing to do business with someone he has decided to like.

"You've done remarkable work on the clock," he said. "Bram tells me the great train runs smoother than it has in thirty years."

"Bram is generous." And lying, but only by omission, which I suppose made us even.

"Not generous. Accurate. I make it my business to be surrounded by accurate people." He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the cloth, a small precise gesture, and I remember thinking with professional irritation that he handled crystal the way a careless apprentice handles brass. "Which brings me to a proposal I've been considering since the day you arrived."

Here it came. I had known it was coming since the feast, maybe since the first day, when he'd offered me the temporary post with a handshake that lingered half a second past friendly. Men like Hale do not spend money on quail without expecting a return.

"The town requires a keeper of the master clock," he said. "A permanent appointment. Not an apprenticeship, Dravenmoor — a *post*. Your own workshop here in Marrowgate, above the chandlery on Founders' Row. A salary of ninety pounds a year. Tools of your own choosing, billed to the observatory fund. And in time — I see no reason to be coy — the mastership itself, when Bram Tull retires. Which, between us, will not be long. The man is held together mostly by habit."

Ninety pounds. My master paid me eight, plus room over the workshop that leaked in March and smelled of solder year-round. Ninety pounds was not a salary; it was a life. It was a life with a door that locked and a name painted on the glass and Sundays free to walk the hill road whenever I pleased.

"And the work?" I asked. My voice sounded steadier than I felt. "What would the work consist of?"

"Keeping the clock running." He smiled. "That's all anyone asks of a clockmaker, surely. That the clock run."

"That's all anyone asks of a clock," I said. "A clockmaker is asked rather more."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Hale did not do anger at table. It was more the look of a man watching a horse refuse a jump he'd been assured it would take. Recalculating.

"I'm told," he said, pleasantly, "that you've taken an interest in the history of the instrument. Its provenance. Who built it, who maintained it. An unusual enthusiasm in a young tradesman."

"Is it? I'd have thought it the most natural thing in the world. You don't repair a cathedral roof without wondering who cut the stones."

"Some stones are better left where they lie." He refilled my glass himself, which I understood to be either an honor or a trap, possibly both. "Wendell. May I call you Wendell? Good. Let me speak plainly, because I respect plainness in a young man, even when he dresses it up in metaphor."

He leaned back. The candles caught the silver at his collar and threw little coins of light across the ceiling.

"This town has one history. It is a good history. It was written by good men, some of them my own blood, and it has kept Marrowgate prosperous and peaceful for sixty years. Mr. Vey's book will tell that history properly, with footnotes and citations and all the apparatus of scholarship, and it will sit in every schoolroom and every parlor until my grandchildren are old men. Now—" he spread his hands, an open book of a gesture— "you are a clever fellow, and clever fellows sometimes amuse themselves digging in old cellars. Finding odd papers. Hearing odd stories from odd people. I understand that. Curiosity is not a crime. But curiosity becomes something else when it starts to interfere with the running of things. When it makes trouble for tenants"— Nell, he meant; the eviction papers were already drawn, everyone knew it— "or embarrasses old servants"— Bram— "or casts shadows on a book that hasn't yet been printed but soon will be."

He picked up his glass and regarded me over the rim.

"So here is what I am offering, plainly. The post. The workshop. The future. In exchange, you finish the clock — beautifully, as you have been doing — and you keep your considerable talents pointed at gears. Not at paper. Not at the past. The past is my department, Wendell. I have family buried in it."

* * *

I want to tell you that I delivered some ringing speech. That I stood, and denounced him, and felt the clean wind of righteousness at my back. That is how it happens in novels, and I have read enough novels to know how much nicer they are than life.

What actually happened was that I sat there holding a glass of port I no longer wanted, and I counted.

That's what clockmakers do instead of praying. We count. Escapements, seconds, costs. And the cost ledger opened itself in my head with terrible clarity, each line item in my master's voice:

*Apprenticeship: revoked. Seven years, struck through in an afternoon.*

*Journeyman's certificate: withheld. Without it, no guild standing, no workshop of my own anywhere in England that would have me.*

*Reputation: Hale need only write three letters — to my master, to the London guild, to the horological society my master dreams of joining — and I become the boy who slandered a respected alderman. There is no appeal from that. Letters travel faster than truth. Truth has to walk.*

*Ninety pounds a year: forfeit.*

*Quail: presumably also forfeit.*

Against which, on the other side of the ledger, the entries were these: a registry hidden in a clock. Land grants with names on them. A deposition sworn by a dead woman. A cottage lintel with my own surname carved into it by hands I would never shake. A girl reading planting records aloud on courthouse steps because paper was all her family had ever been allowed to keep.

And one more entry, which took me longer to find, because it wasn't a thing but a question, and the question was: *what kind of man keeps time for a clock that lies?*

"Wendell?" Hale said. "You've gone somewhere."

"No," I said. "I've just arrived."

I set down the glass. My hand was steady, which surprised me; I'd expected it to shake, and its steadiness felt almost like a betrayal, as if my body had made the decision without consulting me and was now calmly waiting for the rest of me to catch up.

"Alderman Hale. Thank you for the dinner, and for the offer. I mean that — it's a generous offer, and I don't doubt you believe you're being fair. But I can't take it."

He didn't move. "Because?"

"Because you're asking me to sell the same thing twice. You're offering me the clock's keeping, and asking me to give up the clock's truth. They aren't separable. A clock that runs smooth and lies about why it stopped isn't a clock, it's a decoration. And I didn't come to Marrowgate to decorate anything."

"Dravenmoor—"

"Let me finish, sir, because I'll likely never get another chance." I heard my own voice drop into something low and level, and recognized it — it was my master's voice, the one he used when a customer tried to haggle over work that had already cost him his eyesight. "There are names missing from your town's records. I know some of them. One of them is mine. Whatever else happens — whatever you do to me, and I suspect we both know you can do a great deal — those names are coming back. If not through me, then through Miss Ashcombe's grandmother's ledger. Through Mr. Vey, who is a better historian than whoever prepared his sources. Through the clock itself, which I promise you holds more than anyone in this room wants it to hold. You cannot evict the past, Alderman. You can only postpone paying its rent, and the interest compounds."

Silence. The manufactured kind, pressing down like snowfall.

Then Corvus Hale did something I hadn't expected. He laughed — softly, without malice, almost with admiration — and shook his head.

"Do you know," he said, "you remind me of my grandfather. The one whose name is on the observatory cornerstone. Stubborn as a rusted bolt. He'd have liked you." His face cooled by degrees, like a forge banked for the night. "He'd also have ruined you, and slept soundly after. So shall I. Consider the post withdrawn. Consider, in fact, whether you wish to remain in Marrowgate at all. Towns are unhealthy places for young men who make enemies of their elders."

"I'll take my chances with the climate."

He stood, and I stood, and we shook hands — because that is what civilization is, apparently: two men gripping each other's fingers while deciding how best to destroy one another — and he walked me personally to the door, past the portraits of his ancestors, every one of them gazing down with the serene confidence of people whose version of events had won.

"Goodnight, Wendell," he said pleasantly. "Do give my regards to Tull. And to Miss Ashcombe. Do be careful on the hill road at night. It's dark up there, and the ground is uneven, and accidents happen."

*Accidents happen.* Delivered like a toast.

I walked down into the town with the lamps guttering behind me and the cold coming off the harbor, and I did the arithmetic one last time, walking, counting my losses like a miser counting what the thieves had left. Seven years gone. The certificate gone. The name I'd hoped to make gone before I'd finished making it. I had, by any reasonable measure, just thrown away my entire future on a hillside full of dead trees and a dead archivist's letter.

And here is the strange part, the part I'd have disbelieved a year ago: I felt lighter. Not happy — happy was a country I'd lost the map to somewhere around the second course of quail. But lighter, the way a mechanism feels when you finally remove the wrong part that's been binding the whole train. Something that had been jamming against everything else in me simply came out, and the wheels turned freer for its absence.

I had chosen. Whatever came next, I had chosen it with my eyes open, and paid the price at the counter like an honest customer, and there was a horrible clean comfort in that.

* * *

Bram was waiting in the clock room when I got back, sitting on his stool in the dark with a single candle, looking older than the clock.

"Well?" he said.

"He withdrew the post."

Bram nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a diagnosis. "And?"

"And I refused it. Before he withdrew it. Formally. With metaphors." I hung my coat on its peg. "He knows, Bram. About the letter, about Nell, about all of it. He wished me goodnight and told me to be careful on the hill road."

The candle flame bent in a draft from somewhere. Bram's face went gray around the mouth.

"Then it's begun," he said. "That's how he does it, lad. Never his own hands. Always the road, always the weather, always some hired man with a grudge and an alibi. He'll have sent word already tonight, while he was pouring your wine." He looked up at me, and in the candlelight his eyes were wet and furious and afraid, all three at once. "You should have taken the post. God forgive me, you should have taken it and lived."

"Someone has to keep the clock honest," I said. "Might as well be the fool who already gave up everything else."

I climbed to my cot above the chandlery — my rented cot, my few remaining tools, my stolen reputation — and lay in the dark listening to the master clock strike eleven across the sleeping town, its voice rolling down over Founders' Row and the harbor and the dark terraces of the orchard, steady and true as far as anyone could hear.

Too true, I thought drowsily. For some people, far too true.

I was nearly asleep when I heard, faint and wrong beneath the last stroke, a sound I would spend the next hours unable to forget: metal, somewhere below, moving where no metal should move.

Down in the clock room, someone was already at work.
