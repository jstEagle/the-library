## 27. Sabotage

I have thought often since about the eleven seconds between hearing that wrong sound and getting my boots on. Eleven seconds in which I lay in a warm cot telling myself I had imagined it — that a man who spends his days listening to metal hears metal everywhere, the way a nervous man sees thieves in every shadow. Eleven seconds I paid for with three months of work and nearly with an old man's life.

I was down the stairs two at a time and out into the street in my shirtsleeves, no coat, no lantern, the cold of an October midnight slapping me awake as I ran. Marrowgate was asleep. Founders' Row stood in its own long shadows like a row of headstones for men still living. The hill road was black under the elms, and I took it at a pace that would have scandalized my master, who held that a tradesman should never run because running implies panic and panic ruins more mechanisms than rust ever has.

My master was not there to see me run.

The observatory door stood open.

Not unlocked — *open*, both leaves swung wide into the dark, and the draft coming through them was already turning the great pendulum's air cold and wrong. I stopped on the threshold with my heart going like a broken flywheel and made myself look before I made myself listen. The lamp by Bram's chair was still lit, turned low. His coat hung on its peg. His tea sat half-drunk on the shelf where he always left it, and it would be cold now, and somehow that detail — the abandoned, cooling cup — was what told me this was real and not a nightmare I'd be scolded out of.

The master clock was singing.

That is the only word for it. A healthy mechanism hums; a wounded one sings, all its voices wrong at once, and the great clock was keening down in its vitals in a way I felt in my teeth before I heard it in my ears. I crossed the room and threw open the case doors and put the lamp inside, and then I understood exactly what I had heard from town: not someone working, but something dying.

They had gone through the main train. Not the escapement — I checked first, hands shaking, absurdly, protectively, as though the escapement were a patient whose pulse I could still find — but the great wheels above it, the third and fourth and center trains, the whole slow descent of power from barrel to hands. The center wheel was ruined outright. Someone had bent four teeth back against the direction of rotation, which is a thing that does not happen by accident or wear or any act of God. Wear is honest. Wear tells you a story over years and you can read it and correct for it. This was a sentence written in one night, in one language I knew perfectly well: *Stop.*

And they had done it cleverly, I'll grant them that. Whoever bent those teeth had known enough to choose the wheels whose damage would propagate — to make it look, to any magistrate or assessor called later to squint into the case, like a catastrophic failure of old workmanship. Like the clock had simply, finally, after a hundred years, died of age. A story with no author.

Except for one thing. I found it when I knelt to examine the floor beneath the case: a small bright curl of brass on the stone, freshly sheared, and near it a drift of filings too coarse for clockwork and too fine for horseshoes. And pressed into the dust of the threshold, where whoever came in had paused with his weight on the sill, the clear print of a boot heel — a good boot, a gentleman's boot, square-heeled and city-made, nothing like the cracked country brogans every workingman in Marrowgate wore.

I was still crouched over that print when I heard the sound from the gallery above.

It was very small. A breath, caught and let go badly, the way a man breathes when he is trying not to breathe at all. I took the lamp and went up the spiral stair two steps at a time, calling Bram's name, and the sound did not answer, which frightened me worse than silence would have.

He was at the top of the stairs, crumpled against the railing with his legs folded under him wrong and a dark wet shine on his temple catching the lamplight. His keys were scattered around him like dropped coins. He was conscious — his eyes found me and tried to focus, tried, I will say this for him until I die, he *tried* to speak.

"Bram." I got my arms under him, careful of the leg, careful of everything. "Don't talk. Don't move."

"They were here," he whispered. Which I knew. "Wendell. They were here and I couldn't — I heard them and I came up the stair and there were three—"

"Three," I said, and my voice came out flat and strange, because rage had gotten there before grief and frozen everything solid. "Look at me. Three men?"

"Two I saw. One waited below. Gentlemen's boots." His mouth worked. "I grabbed the rail and one of them — I don't remember falling. Wendell, the letter—"

"Is safe. It's hidden. Hush now."

"It isn't the letter." His hand found my sleeve and gripped it with a strength I wouldn't have credited him. "It's you. They'll say I did it. You understand? That's the shape of it. An old caretaker, half-mad with grief for a dead woman, breaks the clock he was set to keep. Who else had keys? Who else had reason?" He laughed, a horrible small sound. "Hale doesn't need to frame a stranger. Strangers are suspects. Old servants are confessions waiting to be written."

Even wrecked and bleeding, the man had spent forty years reading Hale's mind, and he read it correctly now. I want it recorded that I saw the trap at the same moment he named it, and stayed anyway, and that staying was the only choice I never once regretted.

I had him half-lifted, trying to find a way to carry a grown man down a spiral stair without killing us both, when the light came up the drive.

Torches. Two of them, and behind them lanterns, and voices — the particular carrying voices of men who are not searching for anything because they already know where everything is. I got Bram behind the railing, out of the draft, and went to the window, and looked down at the observatory yard, and watched the machinery of Marrowgate arrive to harvest what it had planted.

There were six of them. I recognized four: the two Halemen from the wharf whose names I never learned, the constable's deputy Mr. Quill, and Alderman Corvus Hale himself, in a heavy coat, unhurried, his face upturned to the observatory he owned in every way but ink. The fifth man carried the tools. I noticed that before anything else — a canvas roll, the kind a machinist carries, and pliers with jaws ground narrow, the exact instrument required to reach into a wheel train and bend teeth. The sixth man stood apart, hatless, pale, looking anywhere but upward at the window where I stood.

Mr. Lachlan Vey.

I want to be fair to Vey, because fairness became the whole business of my life afterward, so let the record show: he stood apart, and he did not hold a torch, and when Hale spoke to him he answered in monosyllables. But he was there. Whatever he suspected by then — and I know now how much he suspected — he was there, at midnight, wearing his doubt like a coat buttoned wrong.

"Wendell Dravenmoor!" Hale's voice rang off the stone. "Come down, lad. There's been trouble."

There had indeed been trouble. I came down slowly, leaving the lamp burning at the top of the stair over Bram, and stood in the doorway with the cold at my back and my shirtsleeves and bare throat telling anyone who cared to read it that I had come running from my bed.

"Someone's been at the clock," Hale said. He said it gravely, sorrowfully, a shepherd counting a lost lamb. "Battered the works, from what we can see through the case glass. Criminal damage to the most precious thing this town owns." He turned his head, unhurried, and looked past me up the dark stairwell. "Where's Tull?"

"Injured," I said. "At the top of the gallery. He needs a surgeon tonight."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes — satisfaction, filed instantly under grief — and I watched Corvus Hale, who could have taught Satan a thing or two about composure, arrange his face into concern. "Injured. Good God. Attacked, was he?"

"He was here when they came."

"Was he." Not a question. "Quill."

The deputy went up with a lantern, taking the stairs at the pace of a man gathering evidence rather than a man hurrying to a hurt elder, and I stood in the yard with the torches throwing my shadow huge against the observatory wall and understood that whatever I said in the next quarter hour would be entered somewhere permanent. So I said very little. I gave them the facts that served Bram: injured, keys taken or scattered, found unconscious. I did not mention the boot print. I did not mention the brass filings or the canvas tool roll that one of Hale's men had already rolled up and tucked under his arm like a borrowed umbrella. Evidence, I had learned, is only evidence until it is in the wrong hands. After that it is decoration.

They brought Bram down wrapped in the horse blanket from the duty cart, and he was grey-faced and shivering and his left leg did not bend where legs are meant to bend, and when his eyes found mine over the blanket's edge I made myself give him one nod. *Hold on. Hold steady.* He was too proud to weep and too old to hide it if he started, so instead he did something braver: he looked directly at Corvus Hale, and smiled.

"Evening, alderman," he said. "You're out late."

Nobody laughed. Quill's hand tightened on Bram's shoulder — gently, I'll note, with the gentleness of a man who disliked his errand — and they loaded the old man into the cart between them, and Hale turned to me with warmth I could have heated a workshop by.

"You'll want to assess the damage in daylight," he said. "Of course. Take your time. The town will bear the cost of repair, naturally — and Wendell." He put his hand on my shoulder, and I let it stay there because shaking it off would have been a declaration and I was not ready to declare. "Tull is old, and old men fall, especially on stairs, especially at night. When the magistrate asks, tell the truth. Just the truth. It protects everyone."

*Just the truth.* Delivered like a kindness. That is the mark of a truly accomplished liar: he hands you the poison and calls it medicine, and part of you wants to thank him.

They went down the hill with their torches swinging, and I stood alone in the yard until the last light dropped below the elms, and then I went back inside and was sick in the drain gutter, violently, twice, and rinsed my mouth at the rain cistern, and went back up to the clock.

* *

I worked until dawn. There was no question of sleeping; sleep belonged to men who had not lain in bed for eleven seconds while the thing they loved was murdered downstairs. I got the lamp into the case and catalogued the damage the way a physician catalogs wounds, writing as I went, in my own hand, dated and signed: center wheel, four teeth bent against rotation, two sheared; fourth wheel pinion sprung; barrel arbor scored deliberately with a file — *deliberately,* I wrote, and underlined it, *scored in patterns inconsistent with wear*; hour pipe forced; motion work disordered. Then I knelt on the cold flags and found the boot print again, and this time, beside it, half-hidden under the lip of the stone sill, a second treasure: a short length of brass wire, bent into a hook at one end. A pick. Somebody had used it on the case lock, and in their hurry to leave had missed it in the dark.

I wrapped it in cloth like a relic and put it in my breast pocket, against my skin, where I could feel it with every heartbeat. It wasn't proof of anything. I knew that even then. Wire proves wire. A boot heel proves a boot. My testimony would prove nothing at all against the word of Corvus Hale, because that is how testimony works in towns like Marrowgate: it weighs exactly what the speaker weighs, and I weighed nothing, and Bram weighed less than nothing, being old and foreign-born and lately famous for loving Odile Fen.

Odile Fen. Dead these eight months. And here was her letter hidden in my mattress in town, naming names, swearing truths — and here was her oldest friend in a cell by morning, charged with smashing the clock she'd trusted to keep her secrets. If Hale had designed a punishment calculated to wound her ghost, he could not have done better.

I sat down on the floor with my back against the dead clock and let myself feel it, because I had decided somewhere in the last hour that refusing to feel it would be its own kind of lie. Guilt first, hot and specific: *you heard them. You lay there.* If I had come at the first sound instead of the eleventh second — well. Perhaps the same, perhaps worse. Perhaps three men and a boy with no weapons and a lamp. But the *perhaps* is what guilt is made of, and I let myself have my portion, all of it, because I wanted it to do its work on me. Guilt that is felt becomes fuel. Guilt that is dodged becomes rot.

And underneath the guilt, rage — clean, cold, load-bearing rage, the kind you can build on.

They had made a mistake, you see. In their elegance, they had made the same mistake every hurried craftsman makes: they had worked to a deadline and cut a corner. A clock that dies of age dies gradually, and its death leaves gradual marks — wear patterns, fatigue fractures, the honest archaeology of decades. What was in that case was a single night's surgery, and I could prove it, given time and quiet and my remaining tools. I could show any competent assessor that the scoring was fresh, the brass bright, the bent teeth bent by a hand and not by heaven. It would not name the hand. But it would destroy the fiction, and the fiction was all Hale had. Men who forge histories need the forgery to be seamless; tear one seam and the whole garment hangs open.

And there was another thing, which I did not fully understand until I sat there in the grey beginning of morning with the wreck humming faintly above me like a choir that had forgotten its notes. They had wrecked the main train and left the escapement untouched. Left *my* work untouched — the rebuilt escapement, the new pallets, the delicate true-running heart of the thing I had spent three months restoring. At first I thought it was ignorance: a hired vandal knows the big wheels, not the fine ones. But sitting there, cold to the bone, I began to see it differently. They had left the escapement because they needed it intact. Because the moment they arrested Bram and closed the case, some future assessor would be brought in to pronounce the clock beyond saving — and it must be *plausibly* beyond saving, damaged in its depths but sound in its principles, so that the verdict would stick. They hadn't overlooked my work.

They had preserved the evidence of their own crime, carefully, the way you preserve evidence when you intend to control the trial.

I got up off the floor. My knees cracked like kindling and I laughed once, a hard ugly syllable, at the state of me — shirtsleeves, no coat, brass wire over my heart, and the sun not yet risen over an orchard of dead trees that had once belonged, apparently, to people with my face.

Because that was the other weight I carried down the hill at dawn, heavier than the guilt: they had done this *because of what I might find.* The sabotage wasn't only about silencing a clock. It was about a boy from out of town who asked questions in archives, and an old man who passed him letters, and a registry sealed in brass that could unmake everything Corvus Hale's grandfather had built on. I had wanted, since the night I opened Odile Fen's letter, to believe I had time. That the enemy was history itself, slow and impersonal, a tide of forgetting. Now I knew better. The enemy kept office hours, hosted feasts, and worked nights when necessary. The enemy had a face and a warm handshake and had twice offered me a future.

I walked down into Marrowgate as the bells of the lesser churches rang six — thin, tinny, inaccurate little voices after months in the company of the master clock — and the town woke around me smelling of bread and coal smoke, ordinary, innocent, unaware that anything had changed overnight. A fishwife nodded to me. A dray horse regarded me with vast indifference. Life in Marrowgate, 1848, proceeding as usual on the assumption that the past was safely dead.

By eight o'clock the whole town knew otherwise. By nine I was standing outside the cells beneath the courthouse, having talked my way past a deputy who owed his brother a repaired watch, looking through iron bars at Bram Tull laid out on a plank bunk with his leg bound in sacking and his hands folded on his chest like a man rehearsing for something, and Bram looked up at me and said, before I could speak:

"Don't waste your anger on the bars, lad. Go home and sharpen your files. The hearing is Thursday — and Hale means to have the clock condemned before the week is out."

Thursday. Three days. And across town, I happened to know, Mr. Lachlan Vey was lodging at the Anchor, with a draft history due to the printer in ten days and a conscience he had not yet decided what to do with.

Three days, then, to turn a wrecked clock and a crippled old man and a bent piece of brass wire into a truth the world could not take back.

I put my hand in my pocket, felt the wire's cold hook against my palm, and went to find Nell Ashcombe.
