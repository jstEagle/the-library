## 28. The Magistrate's Hearing

The courthouse at Marrowgate smelled of beeswax and old lies, and I noticed the second smell first, because a clockmaker learns to detect what has been recently polished over.

I had been inside the building exactly once before — to pay a fine for fishing off the mill weir when I was twelve — and I remembered it as a place of boredom and hard benches. Now it resolved itself into something else: a mechanism. The magistrate's bench was the mainspring. Hale's pew at the front was the escapement, releasing power in measured, deliberate ticks. The gallery where the townspeople sat, murmuring and rustling like loose gear train, turned the whole works. And Bram Tull, standing small and gray in the dock in a clean shirt someone else had pressed for him, was the broken tooth that had brought the entire movement to a stop.

I knew mechanisms. I told myself this one could be understood by the same principles.

I did not entirely believe myself, but it was the only comfort I carried into that room, so I held it anyway.

Nell sat beside me, close enough that her shoulder pressed mine whenever she leaned forward, which was often. She wore her good shawl and an expression like a drawn blade. Somewhere behind us, Mr. Vey sat with his notebook open and his pen uncapped, looking — I thought — less like a historian recording events than a man auditing them. I would think about that later. That morning I had no attention to spare.

The charge was read aloud: *malicious destruction of property belonging to the Observatory Trust, to wit, the great regulator clock.* A constable's testimony followed about finding Bram at half past eleven the night before, lying near the observatory's east door with a gash on his head and the clock's main train wrecked beyond casual repair — barrels sprung, wheels bent, the great winding weight dropped its full height and torn through the lower works like an axe through bread.

It was a thorough piece of destruction. It had been done from inside the case, with the door opened by someone who knew the lock, which is precisely why the story made no sense, because Bram Tull had not been able to climb the observatory stairs without resting twice since midsummer, and everyone in that room who mattered already knew it.

"Prisoner at the bar," Magistrate Fenwick said. He was a dried-out man with spectacles that kept sliding down a nose built like a saddle leather patch, and he pronounced the word *prisoner* the way you'd pronounce *sausage* — as a thing to be processed, not pondered. "You have nothing to say?"

Bram lifted his head. His eyes found me in the gallery, and what passed across his face was not fear. It was apology. He was sorry — I understood this with a lurch — sorry toward *me*, as though being beaten nearly senseless and dragged before a magistrate were a discourtesy he'd committed against my schedule.

"I've nothing to say," Bram said, "that the magistrate wants to hear."

Fenwick's mouth thinned. "Then we'll hear from those who can speak to character and circumstance." He looked out over the room like a man surveying stock. "Alderman Hale. You are the Trust's representative. Perhaps you'll enlighten the court."

* * *

Here is what I learned about mechanisms that morning: they do not need to be rigged if they have been *built* correctly in the first place.

Hale rose from his pew and adjusted his coat with the unhurried ease of a man adjusting a chair at his own dinner table. When he spoke, it was with sorrow. Genuine-seeming sorrow, warm and rounded, the voice of a man forced to say hard things about someone he'd rather have loved.

"Bram Tull served this observatory for thirty-one years," he said. "Thirty-one years. I have known him since I was a boy. He taught me the names of the stars." A pause, weighted just so. "Which is why it pains me more than I can say to acknowledge what age and — and perhaps a bitterness about retirement that none of us should judge too harshly — have done to him."

He described the caretaker who had grown "forgetful." Who had "taken against" the clock's new regulation, resented the changes, been heard more than once to mutter that the clock "would be the death of him." He produced — and I watched Constable Prewitt hand it over with the smoothness of long rehearsal — a statement signed by two observatory laborers swearing they'd seen Bram with a crowbar the previous evening.

A crowbar. Against a clock whose case door required a key, and whose inner trains required hands that knew them. If you meant to wreck that movement, a crowbar is the last tool you'd reach for. You might as well try to tune a pianoforte with a fireplace poker. But the two laborers signed their names, and names in Marrowgate were currency, and the men whose names were worth most all spent them at the same counter.

"The Trust does not seek severity," Hale concluded, bowing slightly toward the bench. "Only certainty. An old man, a broken clock, and a town that must know its institutions are safe. Commit him for trial, sir, and let the assizes sort out mercy from justice."

It was beautiful. I want to be fair, even now — it was genuinely beautiful workmanship, seamless, every wheel cut true, every pivot polished. If I had not known it was false, I would have believed it, and I *did* know, and there were moments in that room when I felt my own certainty waver, simply from the sheer professional quality of the lie.

That is what I mean about mechanisms. Hale's testimony wasn't a cog slipped into a machine that would otherwise run honest. It was the machine, working as designed.

* * *

Then Fenwick's sliding spectacles found me. "The apprentice. Dravenmoor. You've had the clock under your hands these weeks past. You'll tell the court what you found."

I stood. My legs felt borrowed. Nell's hand touched my sleeve once and let go.

I told them. I told it plainly, because I had rehearsed it plainly on the walk up from Bram's cottage, and because I am at my most convincing when I am talking about metal.

"When I examined the great clock upon my arrival," I said, "the damage to the going train was not wear, sir. It was sabotage. Deliberate. Someone had filed the teeth of the third wheel so they would bind under load — not break at once, but fail gradually, weeks after the act, so no one would connect the failure to the injury. That is not something an angry old man does with a crowbar in the dark. That is patient, skilled work, done by someone with knowledge of the mechanism and time alone with it."

Silence. The kind of silence a room makes when it decides whether or not to hear you.

"And how long," Fenwick said, "have you been apprenticed, young man?"

"Four years, sir. Under Master Hollis of Ashcombe, in—"

"Four years." He said it the way you'd weigh a coin you suspected of being clipped. "And you'd have the court believe your four years' judgment outweighs the sworn statements of two grown men of good standing?"

"No, sir. I'd have the court examine the clock itself. The filing marks are visible under glass. They cannot be forged after the fact, because—" I stopped, because the rest of the sentence was *because the clock is currently wrecked again, by persons unknown, in a manner that conveniently destroys the evidence*, and saying it aloud would show the court exactly how the mechanism worked, and I suddenly understood that showing them was the one thing I must not do. "Because the marks distinguish themselves from fresh damage," I finished, lamely.

"Can you swear to who made these older marks?"

"No, sir."

"You suspect anyone?"

The room tilted, very slightly, toward the front pew. Hale was watching me with an expression of mild, courteous interest, the look of a host waiting to see whether a guest would take the last of the pie.

I thought of the stolen tools. The note under my door: *finish the clock and leave.* I thought of Odile Fen dying alone with a sealed letter because she had known exactly what happened to people who spoke names in Marrowgate. I thought of Nell's grandmother's ledger, carried out of a burning-adjacent century by women who never once wrote down the name of the man who took their land, because writing it down would only get it taken too.

"I don't name men I cannot prove things against," I said. "My trade doesn't allow it. If I accused a gear wrongly, the clock wouldn't run, and I'd deserve to be laughed at. If I accuse a man wrongly, worse happens. So no, sir. I suspect, and suspicion isn't evidence, and I know the difference."

Something flickered across Fenwick's face — I want to believe it was respect, though it may only have been indigestion — and then it was gone, smoothed away by procedure.

"Your suspicion," he said, "is noted and disregarded. Anything further?"

* * *

There was one card left. I played it badly, because I had no choice, and because hope makes even careful hands clumsy.

"There is a record," I said. "A deposition, sworn, that bears directly on the ownership and history of the observatory lands and on why this clock has been interfered with twice. If the court will grant time—"

"A deposition. Where is it?"

"It exists, sir. I can produce it."

"When?"

*When the escapement is rebuilt and the compartment opens,* which meant *when I have machined a gear tooth from scratch using patterns I only half understand, while the man in the dock rots in custody awaiting trial.* I stood there in the buzzing quiet of that courtroom and heard my own answer for what it was: *not soon enough.*

Fenwick's gavel came down once, softly, almost kindly. "The court doesn't hold prisoners hostage to promises. Commitment for trial. Bail denied given the — ah — severity of the alleged damage and the prisoner's demonstrated familiarity with the premises." He gathered his papers. "Next matter."

And that was it. That was the whole of it. Thirty-one years of a man's life, weighed against two purchased signatures and a magistrate's convenience, and the scales tipped in the time it takes to say *next matter.*

* * *

They were letting the gallery empty before the constables moved Bram, and so I got to him at the rail. His lip had split again in the night — I hadn't noticed in the courtroom light, or had chosen not to — and he wiped it with the back of his wrist and managed something that was mostly a smile.

"You said your piece well, lad."

"I said my piece to a wall with a wig on."

"Aye." He glanced at Hale, who was receiving condolences across the room like a bereaved relative. "Told you once I'm not brave. Brave men end up in dock. Cowards end up old. I got both somehow, which shows you how bad my aim is."

"Bram—"

"Hush. Listen. You're spending your effort in the wrong place. This room" — he nodded at the benches, the bench, the whole waxed and rotten apparatus of it — "this room is a clock that somebody owns. You don't fix a clock by arguing with it in front of its owner. You fix it by getting at the movement." His eyes held mine, and beneath the bruising and the fear there was the steadiness I'd first seen the day he showed me the master clock, that first afternoon, when his hands had moved over the case with the tenderness of a man touching a sleeping child. "Get at the movement, Wendell."

Then the constables took his elbows, gently, because even they seemed half ashamed, and walked him out through the side door toward the cells, and Nell came to stand beside me in the emptying courtroom.

"He didn't do it," she said. Which wasn't information; it was grief, dressed up as fact.

"No."

"And nobody in that room believes he did it either. Did you see them? Half of them wouldn't meet his eye." Her jaw worked. "They *know*, Wendell. They know and they'll let it happen anyway, because it's easier to let an old man hang for a crime than to admit the town's whole history is a forgery. That's not ignorance. That's arithmetic."

She was right, and I hated how right she was. Hale had not needed to convince anyone. He had only needed everyone's silence to remain slightly cheaper than the truth, and in Marrowgate, that morning, it was.

* * *

We walked down the courthouse steps into thin, bright cold, and I stood a moment on the bottom step doing sums.

Here is what the hearing had taught me, and I set it out in my mind the way I'd lay out tools on a bench, each in its place:

First: the law in Marrowgate was a mechanism, and mechanisms run according to their construction, not according to our wishes. This one had been constructed by the founding families for the founding families. Complaining that it ran unjustly was like complaining a watch runs fast. It was doing exactly what it was made to do.

Second: therefore the truth could not win *in* that mechanism. It had to win somewhere else — in the town square, in print, in the mouths of a dozen people who remembered — and above all in the one place no one had yet been permitted to look. Inside the clock.

Third: the deadline was no longer abstract. Bram would sit in a cell until the assizes unless something changed the arithmetic, and Vey's history went to press within the fortnight, and after publication there would be no reason left for Hale to leave a single stone unturned or a single witness unbruised.

"Nell," I said. "How many days can you hide me if I don't sleep?"

She looked at me sideways, and I saw the same arithmetic running behind her eyes, and then something else besides — the thing I was beginning to recognize as the keeper's expression, the one her grandmother must have worn through every frost and eviction notice: *the trees still need tending, so we tend them.*

"As many as you need," she said. "Bram keeps lamp oil in the cottage. And there's a bellows in the orchard shed that would do for cooling brass."

So we turned up the hill road together, toward the observatory, and the master clock waited for us in the tower with its wrecked train and its sealed heart, and somewhere below in the town a press was being inked with a history that was a lie, and a good old man sat in a dark cell for a crime committed against him rather than by him.

I had four years' apprenticeship, a stolen toolbox, and a promise I couldn't yet keep.

Time to find out what an apprentice was actually worth.
