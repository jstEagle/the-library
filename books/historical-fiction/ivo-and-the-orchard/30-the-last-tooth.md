## 30. The Last Tooth

There is a particular hour—somewhere past the third night of working by candle, when your hands stop belonging to you and start belonging to the work—when a clockmaker begins to hear his own heartbeat as an escapement. Tick. Tock. The heart releases, catches, releases. I know this because at some point on that third night in Bram Tull's little room behind the observatory, I stopped being a man making a gear and became a man being made by one.

The keying gear was small. That was the joke of it, if jokes were still available to me. All this—the sabotage, the sealed letter, my name carved into a lintel above a door I'd never known existed, Nell's stolen ledger, Bram's bruised face and bruised worse conscience, Hale's empire of comfortable lies—all of it came down to a brass wheel no bigger than the palm of a child's hand, with sixty-two teeth, each one cut to a tolerance that would make a jeweler weep, because Odile Fen had designed her hiding place to be opened by exactly one wheel in exactly one clock, and she had trusted the future to produce a craftsman patient enough to make it.

I resented her a little, those first nights. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is supposedly the whole business now. I resented her the way an apprentice resents a master's hardest lesson: *you could have left a hinge, Odile. You could have left a door.* But of course she couldn't have. A door invites. A lock keyed to a single tooth of a single gear, whose pattern existed only in the memory of orchard keepers—that wasn't paranoia. That was a woman who knew precisely what men like Corvus Hale would do to her records, because she had watched them do it already.

So I worked. And here is what working around the clock actually looks like, for those who romanticize it: it is cold tea gone thick in the cup. It is candle wax on your knuckles and the smell of your own unwashed collar. It is measuring the same tooth four times because you cannot trust eyes that have stopped reporting accurately, and then measuring it a fifth time because the fourth measurement disagreed with the third, and then putting down the calipers and walking to the window and looking at the dark bulk of Marrowgate below and saying, out loud, to nobody, "I am going to cry or laugh, and either will ruin the file work."

* * *

Bram came in on the second evening with bread, cheese, and the expression of a man attending his own trial.

"You haven't slept," he said.

"The clock hasn't slept," I said, "and one of us has seniority."

He set the food down next to a heap of brass filings that I had begun to think of, with a tenderness that alarmed me, as my children. He looked over my shoulder at the gear blank turning slowly in the little lathe we'd rigged from the observatory's spare parts, and he was quiet a long moment.

"Odile Fen used to work like that," he said finally. "Like the world was ending and she was its executor."

"Cheerful company, then."

"The best I ever kept." His voice did something complicated on the word *kept*. "She'd have liked you, Wendell. She didn't suffer fools, but she had a weakness for stubborn young people with steady hands."

"I'm not steady," I said. "I've measured this same tooth eleven times."

"That's what steady looks like from the inside." He put a hand briefly on my shoulder—heavy, trembling slightly, the hand of a man who had spent thirty years carrying a guilt the size of a town—and left me to it.

I mention this because it matters: on the second night I still had allies, still had bread and cheese and an old man's blessing. By the third night I had only the gear. And by the fourth—

* * *

By the fourth night, the deadline was a sound in my ears louder than my own pulse. Vey's history went to the printer in six days. Six days, and everything inside that false compartment—Odile's deposition, the original registry, the land grants with the Dravenmoor name inked in a hand that predated every document in Founders' Hall—would matter or wouldn't, depending entirely on whether Vey chose his conscience over his patron, and whether I gave him something real enough to choose *for*.

That was the arithmetic I couldn't escape. My gear was necessary but not sufficient. I could open Odile's chamber and find it emptied, sabotaged decades ago by the same hands that had broken the escapement—Bram had confessed to the sabotage, but Hale's grandfather had a whole lifetime unwatched. Or I could cut one tooth wrong, one tooth out of sixty-two, and destroy the escapement trying to force it, and lose everything anyway.

Sixty-two chances to fail. I counted them the way a drowning man counts waves.

Nell arrived past midnight, cheeks red from the climb, smelling of woodsmoke and the lamp oil she was rationing now that her cottage had been searched once already. Since the ledger was stolen she had changed—not smaller, which is what grief usually does to people, but denser somehow, packed tighter, like metal compressed before it's struck.

"How many?" she said, meaning teeth.

"Fifty-one done. Eleven to go."

"And each one takes how long?"

"Longer than the last one."

She pulled up the stool on the far side of the bench, where the light was bad, and took out a piece of paper covered in her careful, slanted hand. "Then while you cut, I'll write. Vey needs testimony beyond the ledger, and my grandmother told me the planting rounds so many times I could recite them backward. Terrace by terrace, year by year. Names." She looked up. "Names are evidence too, when there are enough of them. Ashcombe. Fen. Dravenmoor."

My file stopped mid-stroke. It still does something to me, seeing that word in the mouth of someone else, offered plainly, without ceremony—as though I hadn't learned it myself only weeks ago, as though I'd always been allowed to have it.

"Dravenmoor," I repeated.

"Third terrace," she said. "Your lot ran from the stone wall to the spring, south-facing, best ground on the hill, and my grandmother said the Dravenmoors grew the apples everyone else only talked about growing." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. "You should know your ground before you finish that wheel, Wendell. You're not just opening a compartment. You're opening a claim."

I sat with that a moment. Then I picked the file back up.

"Well," I said. "In that case I'd better not cut it wrong."

* * *

Tooth fifty-three nearly ended everything.

I want to record it honestly, because later—after, when people wanted the story clean—I refused to let them have it clean. On the fourth night, exhausted past the point where exhaustion means anything anymore, in that strange floating country where the hands work on their own, I misjudged the depth of the cut. Not badly. Badly enough. A hair's breadth too deep, a flaw invisible to anyone but the man who had spent three nights learning that tooth's exact character, and I felt it before I saw it—a change in resistance, the file biting differently, the way a horse goes lame under you before you understand why.

I set down the tools very carefully, because throwing them was what I wanted to do, and brass thrown against a stone floor forgets its shape forever.

Then I sat in Bram's chair and put my face in my hands and understood, with total clarity, that I could not do this.

Not couldn't—wouldn't be permitted to. There is a difference, and every apprentice learns it eventually. My master used to say the work sets its own standard and doesn't negotiate. You arrive at the piece with whatever you have—your talent, your training, your cleverness—and the piece says: *that's nice; now be perfect anyway.* And at four in the morning on the fourth night, sitting in front of a ruined blank with five days left and no more brass of the right grade in Marrowgate, I heard that voice in my head and for the first time in my life I answered it aloud.

"I can't," I told the empty room. "Master Fennick, I genuinely cannot."

And the empty room, being an observatory, full of instruments built by people who had also once sat at four in the morning believing they couldn't, said nothing, which was somehow the correct answer.

Because here is what happened next, and I've thought hard about why it happened: I got up, and I swept the ruined blank into the scrap bin, and I began again. Not out of heroism. I felt no courage in it whatsoever. I began again because there was nothing else the work would accept, and somewhere in the last month the work had become the thing in my life that could not lie to me. The gear didn't care about Hale. The gear didn't care about my reputation, or my master's letter, or what I deserved. It cared only whether tooth fifty-three was right. Everything else in Marrowgate was negotiable, purchasable, erasable, forgeable. The gear alone held its position.

A clock is honest because it cannot lie about time. My master taught me that in another life. On the fifth night I finally believed it applied to me.

* * *

I slept two hours before dawn, woke, ate standing up, and cut teeth fifty-four through fifty-eight in a state I can only describe as terrible calm. Nell came back at dusk with Bram, and neither of them spoke, which was its own kind of mercy. They simply took up positions—Nell at the paper, Bram at the bellows keeping the lantern oil warm and the tea hot and himself useful, because Bram Tull could not sit idle near a mechanism any more than I could walk past a ticking one.

Fifty-nine. Sixty. My hands had stopped shaking sometime around dawn and entered a steadiness that felt borrowed from somewhere outside myself. The lathe turned. The gravers sang their thin note. Outside, Marrowgate lit its lamps one by one, all those households setting their clocks by a master clock I had rebuilt to run true, none of them knowing that in six days the chimes they lived by were going to tell them a different kind of time entirely.

Sixty-one.

Nell read names into the quiet. She'd been at it for hours, low and even, like a woman laying stones. "*Ashcombe, spring graft, north terrace, year of the hard frost. Fen, survey of the water channels, recorded by hand of O. Fen, aged fourteen.*" Fourteen. Odile had been keeping the record since she was fourteen years old, and she had kept it until the men in good coats came for it, and then she had hidden it inside the town's loudest truth-teller and died trusting a stranger's grandson to finish the job.

Sixty-two.

I cut the last tooth slower than I have ever done anything in my life. Slower than breathing. The graver took the brass in one long whispering pass, and I eased off, and cleaned the burr, and set down the tool, and did not look at the wheel. I counted to a hundred instead, because Master Fennick taught me that too: never judge a finished cut in the heat of finishing it. Let the work cool. Let yourself cool. Then look.

When I finally picked it up and turned it in the lamplight, sixty-two teeth caught the flame along their edges like a crown of small fires.

"It's done," I said. My voice came out sanded flat.

Nobody cheered. Nell put down her pen. Bram took off his cap, which for a man of his generation was roughly equivalent to weeping. The three of us stood around a brass wheel the size of a child's palm and regarded it in silence, and I found I was laughing—quietly, helplessly, the laughter of a man coming down from a cliff edge—because it was finished, and it was beautiful, and it might still mean nothing.

"It fits," Bram said. Not a question. He'd seen Odile's patterns once, forty years ago.

"It fits," I said, "or it destroys the escapement, and us along with it."

"Wendell." Nell touched my wrist. Her fingers were ink-stained and cold. "Look at it. You made her lock's key from a dead woman's design and your grandfather's trade. Whatever happens tomorrow—" she stopped, corrected herself, went on—"whatever happens in six days, that wheel exists now. They can steal ledgers and burn papers and buy historians. They can't unmake a thing that's been made. You've put that in the world."

I looked at the gear. Small, exact, honest.

Tomorrow I would carry it up the tower stairs to the master clock, and fit it into the mechanism I had spent a month learning like a language, and turn it, and learn whether Odile Fen had trusted well. Tomorrow the chamber would open or it wouldn't. Tomorrow the documents inside would be intact or ruined, and Vey would print truth or print Hale, and the town would reckon or look away.

But tonight—tonight the wheel lay in my palm, finished, sixty-two for sixty-two, and for the first time since I'd opened that sealed letter I was not afraid. Tired past all reckoning, yes. Terrified of the fitting, certainly; I am a tradesman, not a fool. But underneath both, steady as a train of gears: purpose. The specific, load-bearing kind. The kind you can build on.

I wrapped the gear in flannel, put it in my breast pocket over my heart, where it ticked nothing at all and still felt louder than anything in Marrowgate, and went to sleep the sleep of a man who has done the work the work demanded.

I dreamed of apple blossoms on a south-facing terrace, and woke before dawn to Nell shaking my shoulder, her face white in the grey light.

"Wendell," she said. "Get dressed. There are men at the tower door—and Alderman Hale is one of them."
