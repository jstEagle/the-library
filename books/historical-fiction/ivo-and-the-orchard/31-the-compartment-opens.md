## 31. The Compartment Opens

There is a moment in every repair—I had been taught this before I could name it—when the mechanism stops being a collection of parts and becomes a single living thing again. My master called it *the breath*. You fit the last piece, you ease the tension off the pallets, and for one heartbeat the whole machine seems to inhale. Apprentices are told never to mistake the breath for success. The breath only means nothing is broken yet.

At half past eleven on a night in November, in the belly of the Marrowgate observatory's master clock, I felt the breath go through four hundred pounds of brass and iron, and I nearly wept on my own hands.

"Is that it?" Nell whispered from the lamp. "Is that the tooth?"

"That's the tooth."

The keying gear sat in its cradle where the original had been—her grandfather's pattern, cut by my hands over eleven days of not sleeping properly and dreaming about gear trains when I did. Sixty-two teeth. The sixty-second was cut with a shoulder two tenths of a millimetre wider than its brothers, a flaw disguised as a flaw, the kind of secret a man builds into his work hoping someone patient will find it someday. Odile Fen had kept her patterns. Bram had kept Odile Fen's patterns. And Nell Ashcombe had looked at a pencil rubbing of an old gear blank and said, without any drama at all, *that's Grandfather's shoulder-cut*, as if she were identifying a face in a family photograph.

I have thought since about how much had to survive—across forty years, across evictions and silences and one act of sabotage—for a piece of brass to be cut correctly on a Tuesday night by two people who had no business trusting each other. Memory is supposed to be the soft record and paper the hard one. In Marrowgate it had been the other way round. Paper burned. Memory held its shape like tempered steel.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and the clock would not approve. Clocks are strict about sequence.

* * *

We had entered the case at ten, which meant we had perhaps six hours before Bram's dawn rounds gave us cover to still be there innocently, or before anyone else came looking—which was the phrase I kept hearing in my head in Mr. Vey's polished voice, though he had sworn himself to our side and I mostly believed him. Mostly is doing honest work in that sentence.

Nell had brought bread, cheese, and her grandmother's ledger wrapped in oilcloth, because she said if we were going to open a tomb we ought to bring the family. She sat cross-legged on the floor of the case with the ledger open on her knees like a service book, reading names aloud in a low voice while I worked. Ashcombe. Fen. Dravenmoor.

She read mine without emphasis, the way she read all of them, and I found I could bear it better that way. A name in a list. An ancestor among ancestors. My hands stayed steady.

Here is what the escapement rebuild had cost me, since I have mentioned it: nine days of work that should have taken twenty, performed with borrowed tools after mine were stolen, against an escapement design my own master had once called *a master's piece, Wendell, not an apprentice's*, and which I now understood had been built in the first place by an orchard keeper's son who had never been allowed to call himself a clockmaker at all. Every part I machined, I machined twice—the second time truer—and I had begun to feel, somewhere around day seven, that I was not repairing this clock so much as finishing a conversation someone had started with me across a great distance.

The new escapement ran. The train turned. And at the top of the fourth wheel's bridge, exactly where the rubbing had promised, the keying gear engaged.

Nothing dramatic happened. That is the thing nobody tells you about secrets. There was no click of discovery, no shudder in the iron. The sixty-second tooth simply came around, met a detent inside the false plate I had spent three nights locating and two more learning to open without breaking, and stopped. Held. The rest of the train went on ticking above it like a heart above a locked fist.

"There," I said, and my voice cracked on a word of one syllable, which Nell has never let me forget.

* * *

The false plate was a disc of brass eight inches across, fitted flush into the casing behind the motion work, painted the same dull grey as the surrounding iron so that only a man with a lamp and a grudge against appearances would ever see the seam. Whoever had made it had been very good. Better than good. The seam ran along a casting line, invisible unless you knew to look for the absence of forty years' dust—and even the dust had been managed, a fine deliberate film laid over the joint with a dry brush, the forgery of neglect over the fact of concealment.

"Somebody thought about this," Nell said softly, beside my shoulder. She had come up off the floor without my noticing. "Thought about somebody like you finding it."

"Somebody like Odile Fen finding it," I said. "That's who it was built for. Someone who knew clocks and knew what she was looking at and had thirty years to wait."

I introduced the tip of a lifting knife into the seam—not prying, coaxing; there is a difference, and it is the difference between a repairman and a vandal—and felt along the joint for whatever the detent released. For a moment nothing. Then, deep in the plate, something moved with the small decisive weight of a lead counterweight falling an inch inside a channel, and the disc shifted a quarter-turn on a hidden pivot and swung free into my palm.

Behind it, darkness.

Not emptiness. Darkness with texture to it. I put the lamp close, and my hands were shaking now, badly, and Nell made a sound low in her throat that I did not know how to translate except as prayer or grief.

* * *

The chamber was a tin-lined box maybe ten inches deep, soldered seams, wax-sealed corners, dry as a sermon. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed linen in layers, like relics—or like evidence, which is the same word spoken by a different mouth—lay three things.

The first was a book. Not a grand book. A parish register, roughly bound, its spine gone at the foot, titled on the first page in faded iron-gall ink: *Registry of the Founding of the Town of Marrowgate, Being the True and Original Record.* Beneath it, a list of signatures. I turned pages with hands I tried to make into someone else's steadier hands, and the names came up out of the paper like seedlings: Ashcombe, Fen, Harrow, Bellamy, Osei, and there, on the third page, in a hand that slanted the same wrong way mine does—

*Elias Dravenmoor, keeper of the upper terraces.*

I did not say anything. Nell read it over my shoulder and then put her hand flat between my shoulder blades, and we stood like that for a while, a girl who had lost everything and a boy who had never known he'd had anything, looking at the handwriting of a dead man whose knuckles I might have inherited.

"He signed it the same way you sign your work," she said finally. "You do that thing, where the D drops below the line."

"It's how my master always scolded me for signing."

"It's how your family signs," she said. "It's how you've always signed."

* * *

The second bundle was papers: land grants, seven of them, each under seal, each describing terraces I had walked three weeks ago with my heart in my mouth past a cottage with DRAVENMOOR cut into its lintel. Grants of land to keepers of the orchard, in perpetuity, *for the stewardship of the slopes and the feeding of the town.* Signed by founders whose names the town had put on streets and plaques—Hale's grandfather among them, signing as witness on a grant that later records, I knew already, described as lapsed and reverted.

The third bundle was thinner. One document, folded in thirds, the wax of its seals unbroken. Across the top, in a hand I recognized from the letters Bram kept folded against his heart:

*The deposition of Odile Fen, archivist of Marrowgate, being of sound mind and failing body, sworn before God and set down to be witnessed when I can no longer speak.*

I broke the seals. I want it recorded, even if only in this account, that I hesitated—one breath, maybe two—because breaking another person's seal felt like a trespass, and because I understood even then that some doors, once opened, cannot be closed by any amount of wishing. Then I unfolded it and read.

Odile Fen wrote clearly, an old woman's careful hand, and she wasted nothing.

She wrote that in the year 1811, Alderman Josiah Hale—grandfather to the current alderman—had come to the archive with two hired clerks and had spent nine days alone with the founding records. That when he left, the registry was a copy. That the original grants were declared missing, the keepers' tenancies reclassified as tolerances, the tolerances as encroachments. That she, Odile, then a junior archivist, had been ordered to enter the new registry into the record and had done so, and had hated herself for it every day of the forty-one years since. That she had rescued the originals with the help of the clockmaker Ambrose Dravenmoor—*a keeper's son, trained in the trade, the finest mechanic this town ever failed to deserve*—who had built the hidden chamber and its keyed plate as a condition of his silence, and who had died in 1819, she believed of grief as much as anything, on land he still thought of as his.

And then, near the end, the sentence that took the floor out from under me:

*I name Josiah Hale as the forger of the town's history, and I name his grandson Corvus Hale, who knows or should know what was done and has chosen the lie, as I once chose it, to my everlasting shame. Let the record show that I corrected what I could, too late, and that I leave it to whoever finds this to correct the rest.*

Below it, witnessed and sworn before a justice of the peace in 1846, her mark and the justice's signature and date.

* * *

Nell sat down on the floor of the case. Not gracefully. Her legs simply stopped holding her, and she sat, and pulled the ledger onto her lap, and looked at the deposition lying on my knee.

"She waited," Nell said. "All those years she just—waited. And she couldn't even tell Bram properly. He didn't know what was in here."

"Bram knew enough to give me the letter."

"Bram carried the guilt so she wouldn't have to carry it alone." Nell's jaw worked. "Two old people keeping a secret apart from each other so neither of them could be made to tell." She laughed, an ugly small sound. "That's courage, I suppose. Or cowardice. I can't tell anymore which one looks like that from the inside."

"Neither can I," I said, thinking of a feast where I had stood silent through a falsified paragraph and swallowed my complicity with the wine, and hating the taste all over again.

I copied nothing yet. We agreed on that without discussing it—touching pen to paper up here, tonight, would create a document Hale's men could claim was forged in turn. No. What we had was the thing itself, sealed, sworn, witnessed, and hidden inside a mechanism that had now, for the first time in thirty-three years, run true. The plan—we had a plan, God help us—was that tomorrow, or the day after, when Vey could be present and the magistrate summoned and witnesses gathered deliberately rather than ambushed, I would open the plate again in daylight before the town. Let them watch the clock give up its dead. A secret surrendered is testimony; a secret discovered can be buried again.

So we repacked the chamber. Linen, oilcloth, layers, each back in its place. I seated the false plate, reset the detent with the keying gear disengaged—there was a trick to it, a half-back-turn of the fourth wheel that I suspect Ambrose Dravenmoor intended to be found—and closed the casing, and stood in the dark smelling of oil and sweat with the most important documents in the history of Marrowgate ticking away behind a painted seam, safe as anything is safe.

Which is to say: until morning.

* * *

We came out of the case stiff and giddy, drunk on relief, and Nell actually laughed—a real laugh this time—when I discovered I could not straighten my back fully and had to walk to the window bent like a question mark. She poured the last of the tea Bram had left, cold, and we drank it anyway, leaning against the parapet with the town spread out below us, every window dark, every chimney smoking peacefully, three hundred people asleep under a history that was about to break open like ice in spring.

"I'm going to plant the east terrace in April," she said. "Whatever happens. Even if they hang us, they'll hang us on my grandmother's land."

"Noted," I said. "I'll be hanged alongside you. It seems to be the family trade, getting erased and coming back anyway."

And that was when we saw the light.

Down the hill road, past the first dark orchards, a lantern was climbing toward the observatory. Not moving like Bram moves—Bram walks like an apology. This lantern traveled steady and fast, and behind it, in the windless dark, came the sound of horses. More than one. More than two.

Nell was over the parapet edge and down the stairs before I could say her name. By the time I got the documents—no. The documents stayed in the clock. They were safer there than anywhere on earth. I grabbed my coat and my tools and caught up with her at the observatory door as she peered out into the dark, and we watched the lantern come, and behind it, resolving out of the blackness of the hill road, four mounted men and a carriage, and riding at the head of them with the lantern raised high so that its light fell on his face like a benediction—

Alderman Corvus Hale, come calling at midnight, wearing his kindness like a mask worn backwards, showing the thing beneath.

He knew. However he knew—Vey's nerve failing, a bribed ear in Bram's kitchen, or simply the animal patience of a man who had watched us too long—he knew. And he had not waited for daylight, because daylight belonged to magistrates and witnesses and procedure, and whatever he intended to do next was going to be done in the dark, on his hill, to two young people standing between him and a door.

"Wendell," Nell said quietly, backing away from the door into the shadow of the great clock, which ticked above us like a held breath. "How fast can that thing chime?"
