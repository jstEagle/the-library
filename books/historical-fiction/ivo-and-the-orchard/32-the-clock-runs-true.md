## 32. The Clock Runs True

### Wendell

There is a moment in every repair—I had been taught to expect it, though my master never named it—when the work stops being work and becomes something closer to prayer. The last wheel is seated. The last pivot oiled. Your hands withdraw from the mechanism the way a priest's hands withdraw from an altar, slowly, so as not to disturb what you have just made holy.

I had been awake for thirty-one hours. My fingers smelled of brass filings and lamp oil, and there was a cut on my left thumb I did not remember earning. The new keying gear sat in its place among the old wheels like a child among grandparents—brighter, truer to its pattern, unmistakably of the same blood. Nell's grandfather had designed it forty years ago in a cottage the town pretended was empty. I had machined it last night on Bram Tull's worn lathe, with her reading the measurements aloud, and if that is not a kind of resurrection then I do not know what the word means.

The hidden compartment stood open behind me. I had not been able to leave it alone. Every hour or so I found some excuse to cross the room and look at it again, the way you check your pocket for a letter you have already read three times. Inside it lay the founding registry of Marrowgate, wrapped in oilcloth and wax-sealed against forty years of dust: land grants signed by orchard-keepers' hands, names entered in full, and beneath them all, Odile Fen's deposition, sworn before a magistrate who presumably thought he was filing away an old woman's spite and instead preserved her truth like a fly in amber.

*The registry herein is original. The record now displayed in Founders' Hall was altered by order of Josiah Hale, at whose direction I was instructed to burn this, and refused.*

Refused. One word doing the work of a whole life.

"Wendell." Nell's voice came from the clock tower doorway, where she had been keeping watch since midnight, first out of caution and then, I think, because she could not bear to sit still. "It's past four. The town will be stirring soon."

"That's rather the point."

"You're certain the escapement will hold?"

I looked at it. Three days of work. A rebuild my master had once called the province of journeyman hands, performed by apprentice ones, in a tower, by lamplight, under threat of ruin. The pallets were true. The escape wheel ran even. If it failed, it would not be because I was not good enough. It might fail anyway—that is clocks for you—but it would not fail for want of trying.

"It'll hold," I said, and heard, to my surprise, that I believed it.

Nell came and stood beside me at the base of the great movement, and for a moment neither of us spoke. The observatory at that hour is a strange country: all shadows and brass, the smell of dust and machine oil, the stars going pale through the shutter-slit above us like a sky being slowly closed by careful hands.

"How do we do it?" she asked. "Just—wind it and let go?"

"Just wind it and let go."

"Forty years," she said softly. "My grandmother used to say you could set your whole day by this bell. She said when they stopped it, half the town felt lost and didn't know why. They blamed the fog."

"They blamed the fog," I repeated. Of course they had. People will blame anything rather than suspect the machinery.

I wound the weights. It takes strength—the great driving barrel resists you the way history resists correction—and Nell braced the crank beside me, and together we hauled the cable up groaning to its anchor. Then there was nothing left to do but the oldest thing in horology.

I set the pendulum swinging with two fingers, flat against the bob, gentle as closing a door on a sleeping child.

Tick.

A hesitation that lasted a year.

Tick. Tick-tick. The escapement caught, released, caught—an escape wheel turning over forty years of rust in its soul, pallets biting and letting go, the whole vast train waking joint by joint like a body remembering how to stand. The second hand moved. The minute hand began its slow crawl. And high above us, somewhere in the dark throat of the tower, the strike train gathered itself, counted, and remembered.

At half past four by the town's wrong clocks and dead right one, the master clock of Marrowgate rang the hour it had been robbed of, and then rang the quarters after, and the sound went out over the rooftops like a stone dropped into still water.

I have heard bells all my life. I had never heard one mean something before.

Nell had her hand pressed flat over her mouth. When she took it away her eyes were wet, and she laughed once, sharply, the way people laugh when grief and relief arrive in the same post.

"It's loud," she said.

"Yes."

"Good," she said. "Let it be loud."

Below us, in the greying streets, Marrowgate began to wake up wrong—or wake up right, depending which side of the ledger you slept on—and come out of its doors looking up.

### Nell

You have to understand what the bell was, before. It wasn't decoration. It was the spine of the town. My grandmother told me: bakers timed their loaves by it, the ferryman timed his crossings, mothers timed births and funerals and the soaking of beans. When it stopped—when I was six and didn't know enough to ask questions—the town didn't grieve, because you can't grieve a thing you've been told never mattered. But everyone walked slightly crooked afterward. Everyone arrived everywhere either too early or too late and invented reasons why.

I stood in the tower and listened to it correct itself, quarter by quarter, and I thought: *Grandmother, listen.* I thought: *every loaf in Marrowgate will come out right this morning and nobody will know why, and that's fine, that's exactly the kind of miracle she'd have liked.*

Then I picked up the satchel with the registry in it, because miracles are one thing and evidence is another, and I said, "Now comes the hard part."

Wendell looked like a man who had run a race and only just remembered the race was not the point. He had grease to his elbows and that particular blankness of the very tired, and I thought—not for the first time—that he was nowhere near as clever about people as he was about wheels, and that I had decided to trust him anyway, and that both facts still frightened me.

"The town will come up the hill," he said.

"Some of it."

"All of it. You said yourself nobody sleeps through that bell anymore."

"Then we should decide what you're going to say before someone else decides for you," I told him, and I saw the words land, and I saw him straighten, and I thought: *good. Because I can read a planting ledger and tend a terrace and hold my temper in front of Aldermen, but I cannot argue brass. That part's yours.*

We went down through the tower door into the cold blue hour before sunrise, and already there were lamps moving in the lanes below, coming toward us up the hill road like sparks rolling uphill, which is against nature, and exactly what we needed.

### Mrs. Tabitha Quill, baker

I have kept the bakery on Cooper's Lane for nineteen years, and in all that time I have set my loaves by a mantel clock that runs fast and a husband who runs slow, splitting the difference and calling it providence. My mother, rest her, used to talk about the observatory bell the way other women talk about departed sisters. *Before the bell died,* she'd say, and I'd roll my eyes, because I was young and the bell had always been dead.

This morning I woke at half four with my heart going like a drum and the bell ringing in my ears—ringing, do you understand, actually ringing—and I stood in my kitchen in my nightgown with flour still on my hands from yesterday and I cried like a fool. I don't know why. The bread needed doing regardless. But the sound of it went through the floorboards and into my bones and something in me that had been standing crooked for twenty years stood up straight, and I am not ashamed to write that I put on my shawl and followed my ears up the hill with half the town doing the same, and when little Peter Mott asked his mother what was happening, she said, "I don't know, love. But it sounds like being told the time," and I thought that was as good an answer as any.

### Lachlan Vey

I was awake. I confess it plainly: I had been awake since two o'clock, sitting over the corrected page proofs of a history I no longer believed, trying to determine precisely when I had stopped believing it, and whether stopping had been gradual—which would make me merely careless—or sudden, which would make me complicit. A historian's conscience is a delicate instrument. Mine had been running fast for weeks.

When the bell rang I did not startle. I set down my pen, and I listened the way one listens to a witness who has finally decided to tell the truth, and I counted the strokes, and I thought: *someone has repaired it properly, and someone has repaired it deliberately, and both of those someones know exactly what they are doing.*

Then I dressed, collected my notes—including the pages of inconsistencies I had been compiling like a man collecting evidence against himself—and walked up the hill with every other soul in Marrowgate, and I will admit that my step was lighter than it had any professional right to be.

If Dravenmoor has what I now believe he has, then publication is in nine days, and I can still set the record true while the ink on my false draft is barely dry. There is no version of my profession's dignity that survives printing a lie when the truth sits in your satchel. There is, however, a version of my life I could live with. I had begun, cautiously, to prefer it.

### Corvus Hale

I was not asleep either. Let no one say Corvus Hale sleeps while his town's affairs sort themselves.

The bell woke the streets before my man woke me, hammering at my door with news I had already deduced from my window: the observatory clock was running, and the town was pouring up the hill road in the grey light like water finding its level, and somewhere at the top of that water, in a tower I had kept quiet for forty years by the simple method of keeping everyone in it paid or frightened, something had gone terribly, irreversibly right for the wrong people.

I dressed carefully. One does not meet one's own undoing in a wrinkled coat.

I will say this for myself, since no one else will: I never doubted this day would come. Men who alter records imagine the alteration is the act. It isn't. The act is the forty years of vigilance after—the archivists managed, the historians steered, the keepers evicted, the clock kept broken. I did all of it. I was good at all of it. And the flaw in the design was never the clock. It was the boy. I offered him everything a boy in his position could want, and he smiled and thanked me and went on picking at the mechanism like a burr in a sock.

Well. There are more ways to settle a burr than one.

"Bring the cart," I told my man. "And fetch Willets and the others. Quietly."

He hesitated. "For what purpose, sir?"

"For the purpose," I said, "of retrieving town property from the observatory before a crowd gathers around it and starts believing things."

He went. I put on my good coat, and I looked out at the hill where my grandfather's name stands carved in Founders' Hall marble over a signature that was never his, and I felt—do you know what I felt? Not fear. A kind of professional irritation, as at a clock that has finally, after decades of faithful service, chosen the worst possible morning to strike.

### Wendell

By the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge, the observatory yard looked like a market day. I counted faces I knew and dozens I didn't: bakers and ferrymen and shopkeepers, children hoisted onto shoulders, old men who had come up slower than the rest and arrived angrier than the rest, in the way that old men arrive anywhere. Mrs. Quill from the bakery pressed a hot loaf into Nell's hands and said, "First honest morning in twenty years," as though handing over a tribute.

Vey reached me at the tower door. He looked rumpled and entirely alive.

"Dravenmoor," he said, low. "Tell me you have it, and tell me now, because in nine days I either print the truth or I am finished as anything but a hired pen."

"I have it," I said. "Sworn deposition. Original registry. Land grants."

Something crossed his face that on a lesser man I'd have called joy and on him I'd call the satisfaction of an equation balancing. "Then here is my counsel, freely given and worth what you paid. Do not wait for the inquiry. Do not wait for permission. Read it aloud, today, to these people, before Hale can frame the story for them. Evidence kept in a tower is treasure. Evidence heard by a town is law."

"He'll call it forgery," Nell said.

"He'll call everything forgery," Vey said. "That is what guilty men say, and everyone knows the tune by now. What they cannot abide is the sheet music." He nodded at the satchel. "Read it."

And I would like to tell you that I hesitated out of noble caution, weighed consequences, acted with deliberation. The truth is that I was twenty-two years old, I had not slept, and the whole town of Marrowgate was looking up at me from the yard with its face open like a book waiting for the first line. I climbed onto the plinth of the old telescope mounting because it was the highest thing handy, and I took out Odile Fen's deposition, and my hands shook, and Nell put one hand flat against my back between my shoulder blades, and I read.

I read the names. All of them. Ashcombe, Fen, Harrow, Oduya, Bellweather, Dravenmoor. I read the grants, the acreages, the terraces, the signatures. I read the line about Josiah Hale, and I watched it travel through the crowd like weather—you could see it move, family by family, the way frost moves across a window—and I heard a woman near the front say, "My grandfather *worked those terraces*, I knew it, I always knew it—" and an old man simply sit down on the ground, slowly, like something in him had been unlocked.

I got as far as Odile's final sentence—*let the clock tell what the hall denies*—when the yard gate opened.

Corvus Hale came through it in his good coat with six men behind him and a cart, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who has stopped crowds before, and the crowd, God help us, parted for him out of pure habit, forty years of deference folding aside like a curtain.

"Thank you all," he said warmly, lifting his hands, the picture of a public servant. "I see the clock has been restored—a credit to young Mr. Dravenmoor, and the town will see him properly rewarded. Now. These documents in his hand are town property, removed from the observatory archive, and I'm sure Mr. Dravenmoor meant no harm, but they must be returned to Founders' Hall, where trained custodians—"

"They came," I said, loudly, from my plinth, "from inside the clock. Which your grandfather ordered emptied and your father ordered broken. Ask Mr. Vey whether he believes them. Ask the magistrate. Better—ask yourselves why a man needs a cart and six friends to collect one piece of paper."

Hale smiled at me. It was, I thought, almost a fond smile, the smile of a man watching an apprentice ruin a fine movement and knowing exactly which gear will strip first.

"Mr. Dravenmoor," he said pleasantly, "you are a long way from your master's bench," and he beckoned his men forward, and the crowd, uncertain, began to close again around the plinth like water around a stone—

—and Lachlan Vey stepped out of it, satchel in hand, and planted himself between us with the air of a man opening a newspaper.

"Alderman," he said, "before anyone touches anything—you should know that whatever happens to those papers, I have copies, attested, witnessed, and set in type. Nine days from now Marrowgate reads its own history either way." He smiled, very slightly. "The only question left in your hands, sir, is whether you'd like to be in it."

The yard went absolutely silent, except, above us all, clear across the waking town, the master clock struck five—and for the first time in forty years, it struck true.
