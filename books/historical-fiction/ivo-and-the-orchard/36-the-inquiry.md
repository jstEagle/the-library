## 36. The Inquiry

The magistrate's chambers smelled of lamp oil and old paper and, faintly, of the apples someone had left on the windowsill to ripen — which struck me as either a kindness or an accident, since no one in that room had done anything kind or accidental in forty years.

I had counted on being frightened. I had dressed for it, even: my grandmother's good shawl, my second-best boots, my hands folded in my lap like a woman who had never once wanted to throw anything. But when they brought out the land grants — the actual papers, yellowed and cracked, lifted out of the master clock's hidden belly two weeks before and laid on the magistrate's table like relics — I found I wasn't frightened at all. I was furious. And fury, I have learned, is just grief that has finally found somewhere to stand.

"That's my great-grandmother's hand," I said, before anyone asked me to say anything at all.

The magistrate, a dry stick of a man named Prewitt who had spent the better part of three decades agreeing with Alderman Hale about everything including the weather, looked up over his spectacles. "You can identify the script?"

"I can identify her *m's*," I said. "She made them like little gates. My grandmother copied them into her planting ledger, letter for letter, because she said you should know what your own name looks like in the mouth of someone who loved you."

There was a silence of the particular quality that follows a person saying something true in a room built for lies. Wendell, seated beside me with his ink-stained fingers laced together and utterly still, did not look at me, but the corner of his mouth moved, which from Wendell was a standing ovation.

Prewitt cleared his throat and turned the grant toward the light. There it was, in little-gated m's: *Ashcombe*. And beneath it, in the same careful hand: *Dravenmoor*. And *Fen*. And six other names I had grown up hearing my grandmother say like prayers, names that appeared nowhere in the town registry, nowhere in Vey's draft history, nowhere in any record Hale's family had not personally supervised.

The inquiry took eleven days. I will not pretend it was drama every hour of it. Most of it was clerks, and most of the clerks were men who had built whole careers on not noticing things, now being paid — by necessity, and under considerable embarrassment — to notice everything. They measured the terraces. They matched boundary stones to the grant descriptions. They interviewed farmers old enough to remember their grandmothers buying winter apples from keepers whose names were supposedly invented. Mr. Vey sat through all of it with a notebook and an expression I can only describe as a man methodically swallowing his own pride and finding, to his surprise, that it went down easier than expected.

Hale attended the first two days. He sat where he had always sat, front and center, in a coat worth more than my cottage, and he watched the proceedings with the calm of a man watching weather happen to other people. On the third day his seat was empty. On the fourth day his lawyer attended instead, and by the sixth day even the lawyer had discovered more urgent business elsewhere, which is how power leaves a room when it knows it has lost: not with a slam, but by degrees, like heat.

Bram Tull gave testimony on the ninth day. He walked to the witness chair like a man walking to his own gallows, and confessed, in a voice that started as a whisper and found its feet by the second sentence, everything. The sabotage of the clock on Hale's father's orders. The sealed letter. Odile Fen's deathbed instruction. His own twenty years of silence, itemized like sins in a ledger, because Bram was a clock man and could not help being exact even in ruin.

"Why come forward now?" Prewitt asked him.

Bram looked at the floor, then at Wendell, then at me — which was, I think, the order of his courage assembling itself. "Because I mended clocks for thirty years," he said, "and I let the only honest one in Marrowgate be broken. A man wants to die owing less than he does."

I cried, for the record. Quietly, into my grandmother's shawl, so as not to give the clerk taking minutes the satisfaction. But I cried.

* * *

On the eleventh day, Prewitt read the findings aloud, and I want to set them down here exactly as they sounded, because there are sentences you spend your whole life waiting to hear without knowing it.

The original founding registry, he said, was authentic. The land grants were valid. The recorded transfers of 1809 through 1812 — the ones by which nine families' orchards had become four families' estates — were forgeries, executed or commissioned by Josiah Hale, grandfather to the present alderman, in his capacity as town recorder. The terraces were therefore to be restored to the heirs of the original grantees, per the grants' own terms, which had never been lawfully extinguished.

Then he came to the part about stewardship, and this is where my hands stopped being steady.

"The grant to the Ashcombe line," Prewitt read, "specifies succession through 'the keeper who tends.' The court finds Nell Ashcombe to be the sole resident keeper of the orchard lands, and names her steward of the terraces, with authority to restore, plant, and govern said lands as her forebears governed them."

Tends. Past tense was theirs — evicted, erased, forgotten. Present tense was mine.

I stood up when he finished, because it seemed like the thing to do, and discovered my knees had opinions about standing, and sat back down, and everyone pretended very graciously not to have noticed. Wendell put his hand briefly on my shoulder — brief even by Wendell's standards, a touch you could have missed between ticks of a watch — and that was enough.

Outside the courthouse, the afternoon had gone gold, and people were gathered on the steps, and I had a strange floating moment of recognizing the scene from the wrong side. Three weeks earlier I had stood on those same steps and read my grandmother's ledger aloud to whoever would listen, my voice shaking, half-expecting to be arrested for it. Now the same steps, the same faces, and the difference was that the faces were looking *at* me instead of past me, and several of them were crying, and Mrs. Harkness from the bakery pressed a loaf into my arms and said, "Your grandmother sold my mother bread off these hands' work, girl, and my mother never once let me forget it," and walked off before I could answer, which is how kindness is usually delivered in Marrowgate: at speed, and without receipts.

I found Wendell at the bottom of the steps, holding his hat, looking up at the courthouse the way he looked at the inside of a clock — like something whose honesty he was checking against his own.

"Steward Ashcombe," he said.

"Don't," I said. "I'll get proud, and then I'll have to go lie down."

"You should get proud." He turned the hat in his hands. "You tended alone for six years. Against eviction orders. With nobody watching."

"Somebody was always watching," I said. "That was rather the problem."

"You know what I mean."

I did know what he meant. What I meant was that vindication, it turns out, does not feel like winning. It feels like coming home to a house you were told didn't exist, finding the door unlocked, and sitting down inside it to weep for everyone who knocked and was turned away. Every name Prewitt read out was a person I had invented conversations with my whole life — my great-grandmother with her gated m's, old Fen with her deposition, the Dravenmoor man whose cottage lintel bore a surname that had made Wendell go white as bone meal the day he saw it. We had gotten them back the way you get anything back after theft: late, damaged, and with no one punished proportionally to the crime. Hale would face the inquiry's widening circle, yes. But my grandmother would never hear the findings read. The cottages would stay empty another season no matter what the papers said.

And yet.

I stood there with a loaf of bread in my arms and a title I hadn't asked for, and I thought: *the terraces are ours.* Not tolerated. Not squatted. Ours, on paper, in ink, in the mouths of magistrates. My grandmother had written her ledger in pencil sometimes, in the early pages, as if she couldn't quite believe she'd be allowed to keep anything long enough to commit ink to it. I would write mine in ink. Every planting, every graft, every frost date, every name.

"Walk me home," I said to Wendell. "I want to see it in the evening light while it's still mine and I'm still too tired to feel it properly."

* * *

We took the hill road as the sun dropped, and I will tell you that road looked different to me that evening than it had my entire life. It was the same ruts, the same hedges, the same view of the valley going blue below. But I walked it as its steward now, and stewardship, I discovered, changes your eyes before it changes anything else. I saw the first terrace not as a wound but as a bed waiting. I saw the collapsed well cover and thought: *repairable.* I saw the wilding trees that had seeded themselves in the abandoned rows — gnarled, untended, half-crab — and instead of grieving them I thought, *rootstock.* You can graft onto old wood. Everyone had always told me that was sentiment talking, that thirty years of neglect killed an orchard for good. Everyone had been wrong, which was becoming a habit with everyone.

Wendell walked beside me with his hands in his pockets, and I could tell he was working something over because he was silent in the specific way of a man composing and discarding sentences.

"Say it," I said finally.

He was quiet a moment longer, finishing the argument with himself. "My master sent a letter," he said. "It came this morning, before the reading. I haven't opened it."

"Wendell. It's been a month."

"I know how long it's been." He took it out — a plain envelope, travel-creased, addressed in the cramped upright hand I knew from watching him copy gear calculations. He held it without opening it, the way you hold something that might be a verdict. "Whatever it says, I'm staying. I want that understood first. Between us, before I read it."

"Understood," I said. And then, because he was clearly going to stand there all evening being noble about an envelope: "Open it, you fool. It's cruel to make me wait worse than to make yourself."

He almost smiled. He broke the seal.

I watched his face do several things in quick succession, none of which I would have guessed at from the outside: a flinch, a stillness, and then something loosening around his eyes that I had not seen there since I'd known him — some knot of boyhood dread coming untied at last. He read it twice. Then he handed it to me without a word.

The first line was short and cold: *Your apprenticeship is ended as of this date; do not return to the shop.* The rest of the page was given over to a single document, formally drawn and signed: certification of journeyman status, granted for demonstrated mastery of escapement construction, dated and witnessed — and dated, I noticed, nearly a week *before* the hearing, before the findings, before any of it was certain. His master had signed it in advance. Had decided, before knowing how any of this would end, that the work itself was proof enough.

"He knew," Wendell said quietly. "Somehow he knew what I'd attempted. And he judged the work, not the scandal." His voice caught on the last word, just slightly, and he covered it by folding the letter with watchmaker's precision, each crease squared. "I spent six years terrified of disappointing him. Turns out the disappointment was only ever on my side of the bench."

"What will you do?"

"Stay," he said, simply, like a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. "If I'm wanted."

"The terraces need every pair of hands," I said, with enormous dignity. "Also Bram can't haul posts anymore and you're the next strongest thing to useless, so you'll do."

"That," said Wendell Dravenmoor, journeyman, "is the kindest thing anyone has ever said to me."

* * *

We reached the top terrace as the light failed, and I stopped where the lintel stone still stood above the doorway of the Dravenmoor cottage — *DRAVENMOOR*, cut deep, patient, waiting out the years exactly as stone knows how. Wendell put his hand flat on it. He had done it the first day too, but differently then: like a man touching a grave. This time it was more like a man touching a wall of his own house.

"There's a roof beam cracked inside," I said. "And the chimney draws badly. But the walls are sound, and it's got the best morning sun of any cottage on the hill."

He looked at me.

"It's yours by the same ruling," I said. "Heir to the grant. If you want it. I'd — " and here my voice did something inconvenient, so I steadied it and started again. "I've kept this place alive alone for six years, Wendell, and I could keep doing it. But keeping isn't the same as living. An orchard with one keeper is a vigil. An orchard with many is a *place*." I gestured down the hill, where lamplight was beginning to show in the town below, and — new, this season — in Bram's observatory window, and in the two cottages along the valley road whose owners had come forward at the hearing to admit what they remembered. "There are more of us now. There are going to be more of us in spring. Someone ought to live close enough to the north terraces to mind the frost."

"Frost-minding," Wendell said gravely. "A heavy responsibility."

"The heaviest. Some nights you have to ring a bell at four in the morning."

"I've spent my life around a bell that rings at every hour. Four o'clock holds no terror for me."

We stood there while the dark came up out of the valley, and the first star showed over the ridge, and somewhere below us the master clock began to strike — eight clear, true notes rolling across the hills, unhurried, unlying, the sound I had hated for years because it belonged to them and now loved again because it belonged to nobody and everybody, which is the same thing said properly.

"Spring," Wendell said, when the last note had faded. "First thaw. We replant the top terrace."

"We replant all of them," I said. "It may take years."

"Good," he said. "I'd hate to finish quickly. I'm told the best mechanisms take time."

I laughed — really laughed, out loud, on my own hill, on my own land, in the gathering dark with the clock striking true behind us and the smell of rain coming and apples, somehow, still ripening on the wilding trees my grandmother's grandmother had planted. It was not victory. Victory would have been swifter and sweeter and would have taught me nothing. This was slower and stranger and better: it was ground under my feet, held not by papers alone but by hands — mine, Wendell's, Bram's, and every one of the dozen who had stood on the courthouse steps and said *I remember.*

Below us, a lantern bobbed up the hill road. Bram, by the uneven gait of it, climbing toward us with something bundled under his arm and the unmistakable air of a man carrying news.

"Well," I said. "Here he comes. Shall we bet on whether it's trouble or plans?"

"With Bram?" Wendell said. "It's always both."
