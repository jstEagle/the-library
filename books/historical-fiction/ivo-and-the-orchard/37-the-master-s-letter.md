## 37. The Master's Letter

The letter arrived on a Thursday, which felt appropriate. Thursdays were, in my experience, the day on which bad news preferred to travel — it had all of Friday to settle in before anyone could do anything about it over a weekend, and by Monday the wound had scabbed over just enough that picking at it seemed ungracious.

I knew it was from Master Hollis before I touched it. I knew it from the way the envelope was folded — three creases, knife-sharp, sealed with plain grey wax and no signet. My master had never trusted symbols. "A seal is a promise made in metal," he used to say, pressing his thumb into cooling wax when he bothered to seal anything at all, which was rarely. "A signature is a promise made in ink. Ink can be witnessed. Metal only wants to be admired." He had trained my eye on folds before he'd trained it on gears. Three creases meant business. One crease meant he wanted money. No crease at all meant my mother had written to him about me again, and he wished to share his suffering.

This letter had three creases.

I stood in the doorway of Bram Tull's kitchen holding it, and Bram looked up from his tea, and Nell looked up from the planting ledger she was copying out in her careful hand — the second copy now, since the first had been stolen and recovered through means I will perhaps describe someday when enough brandy is involved — and both of them went still in the particular way of people who have learned, in recent months, that letters arriving at this house are rarely neutral correspondence.

"Well," said Bram. "Open it, lad. It won't bite."

"It might," I said. "Everything else in Marrowgate has."

Nell put down her pen. That small gesture — the pen set precisely parallel to the page's edge, as though order could be imposed on the situation by alignment — did more to steady me than any speech could have. She had a way of treating dread as a problem of tidiness. I have come to love her for it, though I would have bitten off my own tongue before saying so in March, when I still believed love was a thing that happened to other people with better posture.

I broke the seal. Three creases. Business.

*Wendell —*

*Your conduct has been reported to me by persons whose accounts I am obliged to treat as reliable, being that they hold the purse from which your indenture fees are drawn.*

*I write to inform you that your apprenticeship is revoked, effective the date of this letter. Your tools remaining in my workshop will be held for collection until Michaelmas, after which they will be sold against your outstanding debts. You are not to present yourself at the premises. You are not to use my name in connection with your own, professionally or otherwise. These are the terms, and you know me well enough to know I do not restate terms.*

*You also know me well enough, I hope, to read to the end of a letter before you burn it, which I suspect you are considering.*

I stopped. My hands were not entirely steady, and I noticed this with the detached professional interest of a man observing a fault in someone else's mechanism. Nell had risen and come to stand at my shoulder without touching me. She read upside-down faster than anyone I have ever met. A useful quality in an archivist's granddaughter; a disquieting one in a person you occasionally wish to keep secrets from.

"Read to the end," she said quietly.

"I'm getting there."

"You've gone white as the envelope."

"There's a second sheet."

There was a second sheet. Of course there was a second sheet. Master Hollis had taught me that every mechanism contains its counterweight — that you cannot have a train of gears doing honest work without something somewhere pulling the other direction, silently, unglamorously, or the whole machine tears itself apart. I had assumed for four paragraphs that the letter was all mainspring and no counterweight. I should have known better. The man had spent forty years teaching apprentices to look inside the case.

The second sheet was smaller. Heavier stock. Ruled with the double border reserved — as any journeyman in the trade knows — for certificates of completion.

*Certificate of Journeyman Status,* it began, in his upright, unforgiving hand. *Be it known that Wendell Dravenmoor has demonstrated mastery of the escapement arts sufficient to the standing of journeyman clockmaker, this work having been examined by me in absentia by way of drawings, testimony of witnesses of good character, and one (1) report in the public prints regarding the restoration of the Marrowgate observatory master clock, which restoration I have reviewed and found to be the work of a craftsman and not an apprentice.*

*Signed, Aldous Hollis, Master of the Worshipful Company of Clockmakers, this day.*

And beneath the signature, already dated, already witnessed by the one other member of the Company whom Hollis trusted further than he could throw — which, given Hollis's throwing arm and the general character of the Company's membership, was a meaningful endorsement —

*For the escapement work alone. Let no man say I certified more than I saw. But let no man say either that I did not see it.*

*You were the most stubborn apprentice I ever took on, and I took you on because your mother begged me, and I kept you on because you asked questions that annoyed me, and I have discharged you because the answers turned out to matter. This is how it goes, boy. The work grows larger than the workshop. It is supposed to. When it stops growing larger, check whether you have died.*

*A journeyman travels. That is what the word means. It does not mean you must go far. Sometimes the travelling is only from one life to another, across a distance of a few feet, and the road is the hardest kind because nobody can see you walking it.*

*Burn the first sheet if it pleases you. Keep the certificate somewhere dry.*

*— A.H.*

* * *

I sat down. Not gracefully. Bram moved the teapot out of range with the practiced efficiency of a man who had raised children, and Nell pulled out the other chair, and for a while nobody said anything, which is the particular genius of found family: they know when words would only be furniture.

"He revoked the indenture," I said finally, mostly to the table.

"He did," Bram agreed.

"Which means Hale's people got to him. Which means Hale wrote to the Company, which means Hale is not finished, whatever the magistrate said, whatever the inquiry is doing. A man like that doesn't lose the field and go home. He goes around it."

"Likely," said Bram, placid as a millpond. "Drink your tea."

"Bram, my livelihood is — "

"Lad." He set down his cup with a click that carried the finality of a well-seated pallet arbor. "Six weeks ago I helped sabotage the finest clock in this county because I was afraid of one man. I have some small expertise in the subject of livelihoods lost to Corvus Hale. And I am telling you, as an authority, that you are looking at the wrong half of that envelope."

I looked at the right half. The certificate lay where I'd set it, that heavy cream stock with its double-ruled border, and the morning light coming through Bram's kitchen window fell across it the way light falls across things that have been waiting to be seen.

*For the escapement work alone.*

My master had always said the escapement was beyond apprentices — had said it a hundred times, in a hundred moods, sometimes as encouragement and sometimes as a wall. *The escapement is where the clock decides to be honest*, he told me once, when I was fourteen and full of questions about why we couldn't simply make the wheels turn faster and be done with it. *Every other part moves. The escapement is the part that chooses. It lets time through one tooth at a time and it cannot be hurried and it cannot be bribed. Build one true, boy, and you've built a conscience you can hold in your hand.*

I had built one true. In the dark, under threat, with stolen tools and borrowed courage, I had rebuilt the escapement of the Marrowgate master clock so that it could open its own hidden heart, and behind that heart had been thirty years of lies and two hundred years of truth, and the whole county had heard the difference ring out across the valley.

And Aldous Hollis — who could not lie about craftsmanship even when lying would have been kinder, who had signed the revocation with the same hand that signed the certificate — had looked at that work from a hundred miles away and pronounced it journeyman's work. Had signed it *in advance*. Before the inquiry concluded. Before the land grants were verified. Before anyone knew whether I would end this story as a hero or a cautionary tale.

He had bet on the work. Not on the outcome. On the work.

That is the highest compliment my profession contains, and I sat in a shabby kitchen in a hill town that wasn't my home yet and received it, and I am not ashamed to record that my eyes stung, and I am somewhat ashamed to record that I blamed the tea steam.

"The apprenticeship," Nell said slowly, working it through the way she worked through a ledger line. "If it's revoked, Hale thinks he's taken something from you."

"He did take something."

"And the certificate — "

"Is worth more." I picked it up. The paper had the good weight, the honest weight, of a thing made to last longer than the hands that made it. "An indenture binds you to a master. A journeyman's papers mean no one binds you at all. He's cut a rope and called it a loss. Hollis has handed me the only document in England that says Wendell Dravenmoor answers to his own work and nothing else."

Bram smiled into his tea. It was the smile of a man watching a young person arrive, several years late and exactly on time, at a conclusion he had reached long ago himself. "So," he said. "What does a free journeyman do with his freedom?"

It was, I grant, a fair question. And I want it recorded that I considered it honestly. I thought about the city, and the Company halls, and workshops where my name would open doors for a season on the strength of the Marrowgate affair, until the affair grew stale and the doors quietly resumed their habit of staying shut. I thought about a bench of my own somewhere neutral, in some town where nobody had carved my surname into a lintel and then spent three generations pretending the carving wasn't there.

I thought about terraces of old apple trees going up the hillside in the grey light of a March that was thinking hard about becoming April.

"I expect," I said, "he finds out what a certain orchard needs. There's grafting to be done soon, if Nell's grandmother's ledger runs true, and I understand the keeper's cottage on the third terrace has a clock that hasn't run since before the eviction orders. Someone ought to see to it. A journeyman's work, properly speaking. Escapement and all."

"A cottage clock," Nell said, in a voice she probably believed was neutral. "Hardly worthy of the papers."

"All the same. Someone ought."

"Somebody should live closer to the work, you mean."

"That may have been part of the reasoning, yes."

Bram stood, gathered his coat, and announced with tremendous unsubtlety that he had business at the observatory that would take him most of the morning, and that we should mind the stove, and that the third cottage key hung on the nail by the door where it had hung, he happened to know, since November. The door closed behind him with the soft click of a case lid shut by a careful hand.

Nell and I regarded each other across the kitchen table, over the ruins of a revocation letter and the bright fact of a certificate.

"He hung the key back in November," I said. "Before the clock ran true. Before the inquiry. Before any of it."

"He's been hanging keys on nails since before you came, Wendell Dravenmoor. Some of us keep places ready. We simply don't always know for whom." She took the certificate gently out of my hand, studied the signature, and something moved through her face — grief and pride tangled past separating. "My grandmother kept records because paper remembers when people won't. Your master writes paper that remembers what a man is worth. Between the two of us, between the two of them—" she set the certificate down on the table, square with the edges, the way she squared everything "—we might manage to build something that lasts."

Outside, the wind had shifted to the south, carrying the smell of wet earth and blossom-to-be down from the terraces. Planting weather. Grafting weather. The orchard's year turning toward its work the way a train of wheels turns toward the escapement — everything moving, everything gathering, everything waiting on the single tooth that decides when the hour strikes.

I burned the first sheet, as instructed. It made a poor fire but an excellent ceremony.

Then I took down the key from the nail by the door, and Nell fetched her coat, and we went out together onto the hill road — upward, toward the third terrace, toward the cottage with our families' names worn into the same stone — and behind us in the valley below, faint and faithful, the observatory clock struck ten.

True, every stroke.
