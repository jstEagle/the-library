## 38. The Planting

### I.

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that governs a clockmaker's life. Every spring counts. Every tooth of a gear counts. You learn to think in increments so small that a whole year can pass without your noticing, the way a clock's hour hand moves — visibly only in retrospect. My master used to say I had a mind suited to small things, and he meant it as praise, mostly.

But standing on the first terrace of the Ashcombe orchard on an April morning with mud to my ankles and forty apple saplings stacked like soldiers waiting for orders, I found myself thinking in seasons instead of seconds, and I will confess it frightened me a little. A season cannot be regulated. It cannot be adjusted by removing a beat and adding another. It simply arrives, does its work, and leaves you either planted or unplanted.

"You're staring at the trees as if they've insulted you," Nell said, hauling a barrow past me with the ease of someone who has done this work since before she could read. Which, she informed me once, was nearly true; the ledger came first.

"I'm trying to determine," I said, "which end of the tree goes in the ground."

"That joke was stale when Bram made it last week."

"It's a good joke. Good jokes keep."

She almost smiled. With Nell, an almost-smile is a whole conversation, and I have learned not to press my luck beyond it.

The terrace itself had taken three weeks of labor to make ready — Bram and I hauling out bramble and stone, Nell directing the clearing with the ledger open on a flat rock beside her like scripture, consulting her grandmother's notes on which rows had stood where, which soil had been turned and when, what had been grafted onto what. The old woman had recorded it all: not just plantings but weather, losses, the names of neighbors who'd come to help at harvest. Half the names in that ledger belonged to people who now lived comfortable lives in Marrowgate below and had not, until very recently, admitted to remembering any of it.

Some of those people were climbing the hill this morning.

I counted them as they came through the gate — because of course I counted them, I am what I am. Eleven townsfolk besides our own number. Mrs. Hettick, whose bakery had quietly supplied our bread all winter and who had never once said aloud why. The cooper Fennimore and his two sons. A widow named Prue whose grandmother appeared in the ledger three times under "helped with the grafting." Young men and old women and one nervous clerk from the magistrate's office who kept glancing down the hill as though the ground itself might report his presence to Alderman Hale.

Eleven. It is not many. But I have spent my life among mechanisms, and I know that a train of gears does not need to be large to move something enormous. It needs only to be connected.

Bram Tull arrived last, moving slowly, favoring the side that still pained him where the scaffold timber had caught him — no, I will write it plainly, where Hale's man had shoved him, whatever the magistrate's inquiry chose to call it. He carried a sack of grafting knives over his shoulder like a man half his age, and pretended he felt like one, and fooled nobody.

"Master Dravenmoor," he said, settling himself on the flat rock next to the ledger with a groan that was half performance and entirely genuine. "You'll supervise the holes. Straight sides, no stoning the bottom, roots spread like fingers, not folded like fists."

"I know how to dig a hole, Bram."

"You know how to dig a hole for a fence post. A tree is not a fence post. Nothing about this hill has ever been a fence post." He looked out over the terraces, and his voice went quieter than I had ever heard it. "Odile would have liked today."

I did not say anything. There are moments when even I keep my hands off the mechanism.

Nell clapped twice, sharp as an escapement engaging. "Rows follow the old marks. We planted the survey stakes last week. Holes first, all of them, then trees, then backfill, then water. Anyone who plants one crooked digs it up and starts again." She paused. "That includes family."

I felt those two words land in my chest like a dropped weight. Family. She said it the way you'd say *Tuesday* — casually, as if it had always been true, as if she were not handing me something I had spent thirty years of my life assuming I didn't get to have.

"Who counts as family?" asked young Tam Fennimore, eleven years old and fearless.

"Anyone holding a shovel," Nell said.

Then we dug.

* * *

### II.

*Nell*

Grandmother's ledger says the third week of April, after the thaw but before the bud-break, and it says it in her cramped left-slanted hand with a little pressed blossom beside the entry that I used to think was decoration and now understand was her way of signing off on the timing — the orchard itself countersigning the page.

I had read that entry a hundred times since I was a girl. *Planting day. All hands. Even the ones that argue.* I never thought I'd live to see it done again.

Here is what nobody tells you about keeping something alone: you stop expecting help, and then you stop wanting it, because wanting it is the wound and indifference is just the air around it. I kept the terraces through four winters after the eviction notices started coming, alone, and I told myself it was stubbornness, and some days it was, and the rest of the days it was grief wearing stubbornness like a coat.

And now there were twenty-odd people on my grandfather's terraces turning the earth, and I did not know what to do with my face.

Wendell solved it without knowing he'd solved it, which is the most Wendell thing about him. He came and stood beside me at the head row, holding a sapling awkwardly, the way he holds everything that isn't made of brass — competently, precisely, and slightly afraid of it.

"This one's mine?" he asked.

"First tree of the first row goes to whoever rebuilt the clock," I said. "That's the tradition."

"There isn't a tradition. You invented that this morning."

"Traditions have to start somewhere. Usually somebody invents them and doesn't admit it afterward." I handed him the trowel. "Roots spread like fingers."

"Not folded like fists. Bram told me. Twice. He's telling everyone. He's worked his way up to Mrs. Hettick."

"He rehearsed that speech all winter, you know. In the workshop, when he thought I wasn't listening. Practiced saying *spread like fingers* to the fire."

Wendell went down on his knees in the mud, sleeves rolled, and dug. And here is the thing I decided on that morning and have never regretted: watching him kneel on the terrace my family kept for five generations, planting the first tree of the restored orchard, tongue between his teeth the way it gets when he's concentrating — I stopped being afraid that this was temporary. People help with harvests and disappear by winter. Nobody kneels in cold mud for a tree that won't fruit for seven years unless they mean to be there when it does.

Tam Fennimore appeared at my elbow with a question about drainage, and behind him his father, and behind his father Mrs. Hettick, who took my hands in both of hers, flour still in the creases of her knuckles, and said, "My mother's name is on page twelve. Ruth Hettick, helped with the grafting, autumn of '31. I never said so out loud before. I'm sorry for that."

I looked down at the ledger in my memory. Page twelve. *Ruth Hettick, good with the wax, laughs too much.*

"She sounds like she was good company," I said.

"The best." Mrs. Hettick wiped her eyes with her wrist and picked up a shovel, and that was that. That was the whole ceremony. Grief and shovels. It turns out most ceremonies are that, if you strip the ribbons off.

By midday the first row stood in the ground, twelve saplings in a line as straight as anything Wendell ever cut, and I let myself do the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do: seven years to fruit. Seven years means they all expect to still be here. All these people, digging in my family's dirt, had silently promised me seven years.

I had to walk to the far end of the terrace and pretend to check a stake until my face agreed to behave.

* * *

### III.

*Wendell*

We broke for bread and hard cheese at noon, sitting on stones and upturned barrows, and Bram told stories. This was new. Bram has always been a man who rationed his words like a miser rationing coin, guilt being the vault they were locked in. But the confession — chapter and verse, delivered at last to the magistrate and to anyone in Marrowgate who'd listen, and God love him, he's told it in the bakery and the cooperage and once, Mrs. Hettick says, to a captive audience waiting for loaves — having been made, the words seem to flow easier now, as if shame had been a cork.

"Your grandfather," he said to Nell, pointing at her with a heel of bread, "fought with Odile Fen every planting day of their lives. Every single one. About rows, about grafts, about whether the Russets wanted more sun than he allowed them. She'd come up from the archive with her notes and he'd meet her at the gate already arguing. Best friends I ever saw."

"They sound exhausting," Nell said, but she was pleased. You could tell she was pleased the way you can tell when a mainspring is fully wound — everything in her sat a little taller.

"And your grandmother would stand between them eating an apple off whatever tree they were fighting about, until they both forgot what the quarrel was."

"A peacemaker with stolen fruit," I said. "A woman after my own heart."

"Yours?" Bram snorted. "You don't eat fruit, boy. You *examine* it. I've watched you turn a plum over for a full minute looking for flaws in the skin like it was a watch case."

"Flaws tell you the history of a thing. That's not examining. That's introductions."

Nell threw a crust at my head, which is, I have gathered, an affection.

It was in the afternoon, on the fourth row, that the moment happened that I suspect I will remember when I've forgotten everything else, including possibly my own name. Old Widow Prue — who talks to her grafting knife, we have all accepted this — knelt beside young Tam Fennimore to show him how to wax a graft joint, and she said, "My grandmother taught me this on this exact terrace, before they drove us off," and Tam said, without looking up, "Before who drove who off?"

And the whole terrace went quiet. Twenty people, spades in the ground, all listening.

Mrs. Hettick answered him. Steadily, plainly, kneeling in the dirt with her sleeves rolled. "Before the town took their land and wrote them out of the books, love. That's why we're here. To put it back."

Tam considered this with the gravity of an eleven-year-old weighing whether the information was worth interrupting play for. Then he nodded and said, "Good," and went back to his waxing.

Good. As if the matter were simple. As if a wrong identified simply required correcting, the way a slow clock requires regulating. Children have no patience for the machinery adults build around simple wrongs — the committees, the inquiries, the careful language about *historical complexities*. They hear the escapement slip and they want it fixed.

Perhaps that is the whole difference between Tam and Alderman Hale. Both of them look at a mechanism and see what's wrong with it. Only one of them wants it running true.

* * *

### IV.

*Bram*

I am an old man and I have dug my last hole, or near enough, so I sat on the rock with the ledger while the young ones worked, and Odile's ghost sat beside me, and for once we had nothing to accuse each other of.

Forty years I kept that observatory. Forty years I wound the great clock and swept its stairs and called myself its caretaker, and the truth was I was the jailer of it. I helped Corvus Hale's father choke that clock — my own hands set the false beat into the strike train, my own file cut the tooth that would miscount the hours — because I was told it would only be temporary, until tempers cooled, until the town forgot. Towns forget so easily when they're helped along. And every year the clock ran wrong and every year the record stayed crooked and every year I told myself the same lie with better diction.

A clock is honest because it cannot lie about time. Odile wrote that to me, in her last letter, the one I carried like a stone for two years before I gave it to the boy. She knew me too well. She knew that sentence would eventually find the one person in Marrowgate constitutionally incapable of leaving a dishonest mechanism alone.

I watched Wendell now — on his knees in the fourth row, showing Tam Fennimore how to test the soil's give with two fingers, patient as Job, precise as prayer — and I thought: yes. Her. It was her doing, all this. She aimed that letter the way you aim a keying gear, and twenty years later, here's the mechanism, turning.

Nell came and sat beside me and did not speak, which is her finest quality. We watched the lines of saplings take shape across the terrace, row after row, and the afternoon light came slantwise up the valley, and Marrowgate lay below with its chimneys beginning to smoke for supper.

"They'll want to see it finished," Nell said finally. "The others. The ones still too proud or too frightened to climb the hill. When the trees blossom, they'll come."

"Some to look. Some to help."

"All of them to remember." She closed the ledger on her knee. "Memory's like a rootstock, Bram. You can suppress the top growth all you like. Cut it back, burn it, pave it over. But the stock is down there in the dark, alive, and given half a chance it throws up suckers everywhere until the true variety comes through again."

I laughed, which surprised us both. "You've been talking to Wendell too much."

"Wendell talks about gears. I talk about trees. Same sermon, different church."

Below us, the supper smoke rose from the town that had wronged her family, and she did not hate it, and I marveled at that. I had hated on her behalf for forty years, hoarded it for her like an inheritance she never asked for. And here she sat, planning blossoms.

Maybe that's why Odile chose her letter to go to the clockmaker's apprentice and not to the keeper's granddaughter. Nell had grief enough to carry. What she needed was somebody to carry the *proof*.

* * *

### V.

*Wendell*

We finished at dusk, forty-one trees in the ground — forty planned and one extra that Tam Fennimore planted crooked on purpose, he confessed much later, "so it would be mine" — and we stood at the terrace's edge in the last light, twenty-some people filthy to the elbows, looking down a hillside that had been bare scrubland in the morning.

Nothing looks less like victory than a field of sticks. Forty-one whips of grey-brown wood, waist-high, leafless, absurd. And yet Mrs. Hettick was crying again, and Widow Prue was talking to the nearest stick in encouraging tones, and I felt something in my chest I had no gear-train to describe.

I had spent my apprenticeship measuring life in portable things. A watch fits in a pocket. Its whole world is the size of two palms. You can carry it anywhere, and therefore you belong nowhere in particular — that was the trade I hadn't known I was making. Today I had helped put forty-one unportable things into the ground, each of them committed, each of them saying *here, here, here* for the next hundred years, and I understood at last what my master's certificate had actually certified: not that I could build an escapement, but that I could choose what a thing was for.

Nell stood beside me, mud to her knees, and followed my gaze down the rows.

"Seven years," she said. "First fruit in seven years. Full crop by ten. Grandmother's note says the Pippins on the upper row were the best keepers in the county — 'they hold till March,' she wrote, 'like secrets.'"

"Appropriate."

"Don't get sentimental, Dravenmoor. Your tree's the scrawniest of the lot."

"It's not scrawny. It's economical."

"It's *scrawny*, and you'll baby it, and everyone will know."

Bram joined us at the rail, leaning on it heavily, breathing the smell of turned earth like a man reading a letter from home. "Your master ever plant anything?" he asked me.

"I don't know. He kept a window box of herbs. He was rough with them. Said living things didn't keep tolerances."

"Mm." Bram looked down the hill. "And what do you say?"

I thought of the watch in my pocket — my first real piece, the one I'd carried from my master's workshop across all these miles, the portable thing that had measured my whole small portable life. I took it out and set it against the light, out of pure habit, and the second hand ticked steadily across its little enamel sky.

"I say," I told him, "that tolerances are for machines. Everything worth keeping runs a little loose and needs winding daily."

Nell made a noise that might have been agreement or might have been her suppressing a laugh at my expense. With her, the two are often identical.

We walked down together as the dark came up, and it was at the bottom of the hill road, at the gate, that we met the boy running up from Marrowgate with a lantern and a folded paper in his fist — a printer's boy, ink to the wrists, breathless, calling my name before he could even see our faces in the dusk.

He thrust the paper at me. It smelled of fresh type. At the top, in letters I recognized from Vey's drafts spread across the archive table a dozen nights running, stood the title:

*THE TRUE HISTORY OF MARROWGATE: Being a Corrected Account of Its Founding, Together with the Original Registry of the Orchard Keepers, Complete.*

And beneath it, the printer's imprint — and beneath *that*, a note in Vey's narrow, elegant hand:

*Dravenmoor — it goes to the booksellers tomorrow morning. Come tonight. Bring the girl. There is a page you both should see before the ink dries.*

Nell read it over my shoulder by the boy's lantern, and I felt her go very still against my arm.

"What page," she said quietly, "could there possibly be, that we haven't already seen?"

I had no answer. But I noticed — because noticing is the whole of my trade — that her hand had gone to the lintel-carved cottage on the ridge behind us, and to the name cut into its stone, and that she was smiling.

"Come on," I said, and we turned toward the lights of Marrowgate.
