## 40. What Can Be Carried

There is a particular hour in a clockmaker's life—I had never reached it before that spring—when he stops measuring time and starts keeping it. The distinction sounds like nothing. It is everything. A man who measures time is holding a ruler up to a river. A man who keeps time has built the riverbed.

On the last morning of April, I set my pocket watch against the master clock of Marrowgate Observatory for the first time.

I want to be honest about how long this took me, because honesty is the whole trade now. The watch was my master's gift when I was fourteen—a plain silver case, a movement so simple a child could service it, and I had serviced it perhaps four hundred times since. For eleven years it had been the most accurate thing in my possession, and I had loved it the way you love a crutch: gratefully, and without examining why you lean.

The master clock did not need my watch corrected to it. The reverse, obviously. But there is a ritual in the setting, an old clockmaker's courtesy, and besides, I wanted to feel the moment when the small thing acknowledged the great one. I opened the case back, thumbed the crown, and listened—the deep, patient tick of the observatory regulator above me, the thin, quick answer of the little watch in my palm—and brought them into agreement to the second.

"There," I said, to no one. "Now we're both telling the truth."

Bram Tull would have made a joke about that. Bram was not there to make it, which is its own kind of wound, but I have learned this year that a wound and a debt can look identical from the inside, and that some debts you pay by living differently rather than by grieving loudly. More on Bram shortly. He would insist on being mentioned shortly. He hated being the sad part of any story.

* * *

Let me back up three weeks, because endings are like escapements: what looks like a single instant of release is actually a long train of consequences, each wheel turning the next, and if I tell you only the click you will miss the machinery.

Hale's fall was not dramatic. That surprised me at the time; it does not anymore. Power, I have come to believe, is mostly a story people agree to keep telling, and Corvus Hale had been Marrowgate's best storyteller for thirty years. Once Vey read Odile Fen's deposition aloud on the observatory steps—his voice steady, his hands not—half the town stopped agreeing before sundown. By the following week, Hale's allies were resigning from committees with the alacrity of rats who had read the ship's maintenance logs themselves. He came to see me once more, in the workshop below the clock room, and offered me money, position, and a version of events in which everyone behaved reasonably and no one had ever done anything that could not be charitably explained.

"No," I said.

He looked at me with something close to curiosity. "You understand you've cost yourself everything, boy. Your master. Your trade connections. Any town in the county."

"I understand I've kept one thing," I said. "It happens to be the thing I'd have traded all the rest for anyway, if I'd known it existed."

He left without slamming the door, which somehow was worse. A man who slams doors still believes doors respond to him. Hale walked out the way water leaves a cracked basin—all at once, and because it could no longer hold itself where it was.

* * *

The inquiry took six weeks and was, as Nell predicted, "a committee dressed as justice." But committees dress better than nothing. The land grants were verified against the registry I'd pulled from the clock's heart—Odile Fen's careful hand, her grandfather's earlier entries, and beneath them, in faded iron-gall ink, the names of nine orchard-keeper families whose labor had terraced those hillsides and whose reward had been erasure. Dravenmoor was fourth on the list. I touched the letters of my own name the way you touch a scar you didn't know you had: confirming it's real, confirming it doesn't hurt quite as much as feared.

The terraces reverted to the heirs. Most of the heirs were dead or scattered or had married into the very families that had erased them, which is history's favorite irony and costs nothing to manufacture. But two lines survived intact. One was the Ashcombes. The other, through a chain of daughters and changed spellings nobody had bothered to track, was mine.

Nell Ashcombe was named steward of the orchard lands. She accepted the appointment with a speech consisting of the words "It's about time," which the magistrate did not realize was a pun until Bram, wheezing with laughter from the gallery, explained it to him.

Ah—Bram. Yes. Here is the part I keep circling, the way your tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

His arm never healed right after Hale's men worked him over outside the clock room the night of the sabotage. The magistrate cleared him entirely—the hearing collapsed within a day of the compartment opening—but a cleared name mends faster than a shoulder. He could no longer wind the great weights alone. On the first morning after the inquiry closed, he stood at the base of the clock tower, looking up the stairwell he had climbed ten thousand times, and I watched him decide something.

"You'll need someone to haul the weights," I said. "Twice a week. Heavy work. Terrible hours. No pay worth mentioning."

"I'm aware," he said. "I was offering."

So we split the winding between us, the old caretaker and the new one, though neither of us used either word. He taught me the clock's moods—which bearing ran warm in August, how the strike train liked to be oiled on a waning moon, which was superstition, and how the pendulum shortened itself in summer heat, which was physics wearing superstition's coat. I taught him the escapement rebuild I'd done, tooth by tooth, and he wept openly at the quality of the finish work, which embarrassed us both and pleased us both, and is the single finest review my craft has ever received.

He moved out of the caretaker's cottage—gave it to me, over my objection, on the grounds that my objection was "sentimental nonsense from a boy sleeping on a workshop bench"—and took rooms above the chandler's in town, where, I am told, he has become a fixture at the evening card table and tells the story of the clock's hidden chamber with escalating embellishments. Last week the chamber contained gold. I have decided not to correct him. Some mechanisms run better with a little deliberate play in them.

* * *

Vey published in March.

I had expected to hate him longer than I did. Hatred, like a mainspring, holds its charge only if you never let it do work. But the man sat across from me in the observatory that winter, pale and sleepless, surrounded by Odile Fen's papers, and said: "I spent four months writing a beautiful lie, Mr. Dravenmoor, and the worst of it is that it was well-written. Anyone could have believed it. I nearly did." Then he asked me to check every date in his corrected manuscript against the registry, twice, because "if I am to be the historian of the truth, I intend to be accurate at it, which is harder."

The book is called *Marrowgate: A True Account*, which is a dull title and exactly right. He reproduced the founding registry in full, every keeper family named, their grants quoted verbatim. The first page after the preface carries a dedication: *To Odile Fen, who kept the record when no one would read it, and to the orchard-keepers, who planted what others claimed.* He credits Bram by name too, including the sabotage, in a paragraph Bram has read aloud at the card table so many times the chandler's wife can recite it. He includes me in the acknowledgments—"to the clockmaker who understood that a mechanism can testify"—which I pretended to find excessive and have reread perhaps sixty times.

The falsified draft history, the one unveiled at the feast where I swallowed my complicity like a bad oyster? Vey burned it. Actually burned it, in a brazier on the courthouse steps, before witnesses, saying, "A record that must be protected from examination is not a record; it is a hostage." Nell says he rehearsed the line. I say even rehearsed truths outrank spontaneous lies, and Nell conceded the point with the grace she reserves for things she intends to bring up again later.

* * *

Which brings me to spring, and the planting, and the part of this account where I stop narrating events and start confessing happiness, which is a far more difficult genre.

We replanted the first terrace on the last Saturday of April. Not just the four of us—though the four of us were there, Nell directing operations with her grandmother's ledger open on a stake like scripture, Bram sitting on a sack of grafting stock issuing opinions, me doing the actual digging because someone sensible had to—but a dozen townspeople besides. The cooper's widow, who remembered her mother buying apples from the Ashcombes. Two of the Fen cousins, come down from the coast, one of whom had Odile's eyes and all of whom had her stubbornness. A boy of maybe ten who attached himself to Bram within the hour and was, by afternoon, being taught to prune with a patience Bram had never once displayed toward an adult.

We put forty trees in that day. Apple, mostly, with a row of pears along the windward edge where Nell's ledger said her grandfather had always planted pears "because the wind respects a pear." The holes went down through soil that had lain fallow twelve years and found, beneath the weeds, earth still dark and generous, as if the ground had been waiting with the same stubbornness as the people.

Nell knelt beside the third tree with her sleeves rolled and her hands black to the wrist, and she tamped the soil around its roots with a tenderness she shows to nothing else on this earth except, occasionally, me, and she said, "My grandmother planted this exact row. Same spacing. She had it in the ledger to the hand-width."

"Then we're not planting," I said. "We're resuming."

She looked up at me with dirt on her cheekbone and something in her expression I have learned to read correctly about seven times out of ten, and she said, "Wendell Dravenmoor. You are the only man in Marrowgate who could make replanting an orchard sound like winding a clock."

"It's the same act," I said. "You store up force against a future you won't fully see, and you trust the mechanism."

"That's the least romantic sentence ever spoken on this hillside."

"And yet."

"And yet," she agreed, and went back to her tree, and I stood there holding a spade like a fool, happier than I had ever been in a workshop, which is a sentence my fourteen-year-old self would have found genuinely alarming.

* * *

Here is what I did not say at the feast, and did not say to Hale, and have been saying ever since in actions because the words still catch: I refused the apprenticeship's completion. My master's letter arrived in February—revocation on the first page, cold and formal, and folded behind it a journeyman's certificate already signed and dated, certifying the escapement work, "an achievement beyond the ordinary reach of the trade." He wrote one line beneath his signature: *You were never my apprentice, Wendell. You were my colleague who hadn't finished noticing.*

I wrote back. I told him about the clock, the registry, the orchard. I told him I was staying. His reply was two sentences: *The bench in Basel remains yours if you ever tire of hillsides. From the sound of your letter, you will not.* He enclosed a small parcel: a boxwood escapement model, the kind he'd taught me on, worn smooth by forty years of students' fingers. It sits on my windowsill now, facing the orchard.

People ask—politely, curiously, sometimes with the faint horror of those who cannot imagine choosing less over more—what I carry from my old life. The watch. The tools Bram recovered from the thief, minus the files, which were never found and which I have decided to consider a tithe. The certificate. And something less portable: the understanding my master gave me at fifteen, kneeling beside him at the bench, when he said a clock is the only machine that cannot lie, because whatever else you build into it, time itself checks your work. I thought he was teaching me horology. He was teaching me ethics with a hammer in his hand, which is the only way ethics sticks.

* * *

The blossoms came the second week of May.

Not on our new trees—they're whips and switches, years from flowering—but on the few surviving ancients on the upper terraces, the ones old enough to remember the keepers, gnarled and half-wild and suddenly, extravagantly white, as if the hillside had decided to celebrate ahead of schedule. Nell walked me up there at dusk and we stood under the largest of them, a pear gone so wide you could stand inside its shadow like a room.

"My grandmother used to bring me here," she said. "Before the evictions. She'd say, *This tree fed your great-great-grandmother, and it'll feed whoever comes after, if anyone's fool enough to stay.*"

"Are we fools enough?"

"We're the exact amount," she said. "It's a precise measurement. You'd respect it."

Under that tree, with the petals coming down like slow snow and the master clock beginning the eight o'clock strike somewhere below us—eight measured notes rolling across the valley, each one true—I asked her, clumsily, in the manner of a man attempting fine adjustment with a mallet, whether she might object to company. Permanent company. The sort that shares a ledger.

She let me finish the entire fumbling sentence, which was merciful, and then she said, "Wendell. I have kept vigil over this orchard alone for six years. I have exactly one skill from it: knowing what's worth keeping." She took my dirt-stained hand in her dirt-stained one. "Keep you."

The clock finished striking. Neither of us moved for a while. Somewhere downslope, Bram's young apprentice shouted something about a ladder, and the evening went on around us the way evenings should: unremarkable, unremarked-upon, and completely sufficient.

* * *

Tonight I sit in the caretaker's cottage—my cottage, though I still knock on my own doorframe superstitiously sometimes—with the window open to the blossom-scented dark. On the sill, my pocket watch rests against the boxwood model, and beyond them the observatory rises against the stars, and inside it the master clock ticks with the patience of a thing that has outlasted sabotage, silence, and the ambitions of important men.

Tomorrow: the second terrace. Nell has the rows mapped; Bram has opinions; the cooper's widow has promised cider from a recipe "older than any of your histories, official or otherwise." There is grafting stock to heel in, a strike-train bearing that wants attention before summer, and a wedding to plan that Nell insists will be small and Bram insists will involve the entire chandler's card circle, and both of them are probably right, which bodes interestingly.

But tonight, the last thing I do, as it will be every night, is this: I climb the tower steps, I stand before the great clock, and I listen. Tick. Tick. Tick. Nine families erased, and named again. A letter sealed for twenty years, and opened. A lie told beautifully, and a truth told plainly, and the truth still standing. All of it held in brass and steel and ink and memory, in trees and ledgers and the calloused agreement of people who chose each other.

The clock strikes ten. Ten true notes, carried down the hill on the warm air, over the terraces, into the town that finally hears them.

I set my watch by it, and I go home.
