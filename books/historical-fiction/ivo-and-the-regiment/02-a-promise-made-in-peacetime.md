## 2. A Promise Made in Peacetime

Three years later, Ivo Crane would still be able to describe the exact crease in the stranger's trousers.

That was the trouble with promises. They attached themselves to details — the way a smell attaches itself to a season — so that long after the promise should have faded into the general weather of a life, one fold of cloth or one turn of a phrase could bring it back whole, urgent, and unpaid.

In the spring of 1911, Ivo Crane measured his life in what could be carried in a coat. A timetable. Sixpence for emergencies and threepence for ordinary days. A pencil worn to a stub. The rest — the house on Wexford Row, Mrs. Marlowe's letters, the small warm machinery of the Marlowe household — that was carried some other way, a way Ivo had never found the word for and had stopped looking for one.

It was Mrs. Marlowe's habit to send her driver north twice a year to collect pamphlets from a printer in Leeds who owed her discretion more than he owed anyone money. The journey took most of a day. Ivo did not mind it. A train was one of the few places where sitting still was invisible, and Ivo had built an entire architecture out of not being noticed.

* * *

The compartment at King's Cross was empty when Ivo boarded it, which was the first mercy of the morning. Ivo took the corner seat facing backward — you could watch where you'd been without anyone watching where you were going — and opened the newspaper to the shipping news, which required no thought and offered none, and let the platform noise wash over like tide over a breakwater.

The train was already moving when the compartment door banged open and a man came in with too much luggage and not enough apology.

"Ha — still moving. Good. I've chased slower things." He wrestled a leather case onto the rack, missed twice, succeeded on the third attempt as though the rack had simply grown tired of arguing, and dropped into the seat opposite. He was perhaps thirty-five, sunburned across the bridge of the nose in a way that suggested India or somewhere pretending to be it, and he wore his uniform with the particular ease of a man who had stopped being impressed by it years ago. Captain's pips. A regimental crest Ivo half-recognized from the society pages.

"Crane," the man said, extending a hand. "Aurel Brandt." Then, catching something in Ivo's face: "Not *that* Brandt. My brother sells insurance and is proud of it. I'm the disappointing one."

"Ivo," said Ivo, because the hand was still there.

"Ivo." Brandt tried the name the way a man tries a coin between his fingers. "Good Saxon name. Means yew tree, I think. They live a thousand years, yews. Outlive the churches they're planted in."

"That's a lot to put on a man you've known four seconds."

Brandt laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, loud enough that a woman in the next compartment coughed meaningfully — and settled back, and for twenty minutes he asked nothing and volunteered everything. He talked about the monsoon at Poona as if it were an acquaintance who had behaved badly. He talked about his sister Hester, who kept bees in Kent, and about the bees with more tenderness than he spent on anything human. He talked about a horse called Regret who had once refused a fence in front of three hundred people and whom he had forgiven but never forgotten.

Ivo said little. That was usual; people filled silence around Ivo the way water fills a hollow, gratefully, without wondering why the hollow was there. But Ivo listened, which was also usual, and listening had a way of turning into knowing whether one wanted it to or not.

And somewhere outside Peterborough, between one sentence and another, Ivo understood two things at once, arriving together like twins: that this man's brother was the Mr. Brandt whose motor Mrs. Marlowe's client Sir James kept in town — which made this a coincidence, and coincidences were how the world pretended to have no plan — and that Ivo liked him. Which was worse. Liking people was expensive.

"You're very quiet, Ivo-the-yew-tree," Brandt said. "Either you're bored, or you're the only man in England who actually listens. My brother says nobody listens anymore. He says everyone just reloads."

"I'm listening."

"Hm." Brandt looked at him for a moment longer than comfort allowed. "Yes. You are. That's rarer than you'd think." He turned to the window. Fields went by, green and indifferent. "Do you know what I hate about garrison towns, Ivo? Everyone talks. All day, all night — talk, talk, talk, and none of it means a damn thing, because the moment anything real gets said, the walls have ears and the ears have superiors." He was quiet for a while. "I miss being talked *to*. There's a difference."

The train rocked. Somewhere down the corridor a trolley rattled and a guard shouted a station name that wasn't theirs.

"Why are you telling me this?" Ivo asked. It came out blunter than intended, and Ivo braced for the small familiar wound of having spoken too plainly.

But Brandt didn't flinch. He turned back from the window, and his face had changed — the performance of good humor set aside like a coat hung on a hook, and underneath it something plainer and much heavier.

"Because you're a stranger," he said. "That's the whole of it. You're a stranger on a train, and by God, that's restful."

* * *

Now, three years later, standing in the garage on Wexford Row with a rag in one hand and a wrench going cold in the other, Ivo would try to reconstruct the moment the promise was actually made, and fail, because there wasn't one. Promises like that don't get made. They get noticed, afterward, the way you notice you've been bleeding only when someone asks about the stain.

* * *

Somewhere after Grantham, Brandt produced a flask, offered it — Ivo declined, driving or not, it sat badly — drank once himself, and capped it, and then was quiet for a full minute, which felt, in that compartment, enormous.

"I'm being transferred," he said finally. "Belgium. There's a mapping exercise, officially. Unofficially—" He waved a hand, dismissing the unofficial. "The point is, I'll be near the frontier, and the frontier has developed opinions lately. And I've been thinking — I think about this more than is healthy — I've been thinking about what happens if it all goes wrong."

"It won't go wrong," Ivo said, because that was what one said.

"No," Brandt agreed pleasantly. "Almost certainly not. The Germans aren't fools and neither are we, and my brother the insurance man assures me that war is bad for premiums." He smiled. Then the smile went away. "But I've read the staff papers, Ivo. The ones that aren't for reading. And the men writing them don't sound like men who expect premiums to matter."

He reached into his breast pocket. What he brought out was a letter — sealed, addressed, and yet somehow not finished, in the way of things carried for a long time. The envelope was soft at the corners, worn the way a prayer book wears.

"This is for my sister. Hester Rook. There's a farmhouse — Rook Farm, near a village called Denmore, in Kent, apple country — you can't miss the farm, it's the one with the crooked chimney that every generation swears they'll fix. If anything happens to me out there, the army will write to her. The army writes beautifully, Ivo. Condolences are their finest genre. But there are things the army won't say, and this letter says them, and I can't post it, because posting it makes it real, and I'm a coward about exactly one thing." He held it up between two fingers, lightly, as though weighing nothing. "So here's what I want."

"Don't," said Ivo, before thinking. "You don't know me."

"I know you've listened to me for three hours and haven't once looked at your newspaper since Doncaster. I know you declined my flask, which means you have rules. And I know—" Brandt leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his voice dropped out of the railway carriage entirely, into some smaller room where only the two of them existed— "that when I got in this compartment you moved your bag off the seat *before* I asked. Nobody does that. Everyone makes me ask." He straightened. "Strangers are the only honest men left, Ivo. A friend lies to spare you. A colleague lies to spare himself. A stranger has nothing to spare either of us."

"That's mad," Ivo said.

"It's completely mad," Brandt agreed cheerfully. "Will you do it anyway?"

And there it was — the trap, sprung gently, with the door left open. Ivo could refuse. Nothing bound them. In an hour they would separate at some junction and never see each other again, and the letter would stay in a captain's pocket until it became a widow's problem or nobody's, and the life Ivo had built out of not being noticed would go on not being noticed, safe, seamless, small.

Ivo looked at the letter. Soft corners. Worn like a prayer book. Somebody had held this thing often enough to wear it.

"What do I do if—" Ivo began.

"If I die?" Brandt said it the way he might have said *if it rains*. "You post it. Or better, you carry it. Postmen read things. You look like a man who doesn't read other people's letters." He said it without suspicion, without weight, and Ivo — who had read forty-one of Mrs. Marlowe's letters by lamplight in the past year alone, who could recite three addresses in Brussels and two in Amsterdam from memory — kept his face perfectly still and hated himself for how easily it stayed still.

"And if you don't die?"

"Then burn it and tell no one I was ever sentimental." Brandt grinned, and for a moment he was only a sunburned officer on a train, ridiculous and young. "Give me your hand."

"Captain—"

"Aurel. Give me your hand, yew tree. Trees keep their promises. It's the whole point of standing still."

And Ivo — Ivo who had rules, Ivo who declined flasks, Ivo whose entire survival depended on carrying nothing that couldn't be explained — gave the hand. The shake lasted two seconds. The letter changed pockets in the same motion, so smooth that Ivo couldn't afterward remember the moment of transfer, only that one moment the coat was light and the next it weighed six ounces and would weigh six ounces for three years.

"There," said Aurel Brandt, satisfied, settling back as though he'd done nothing stranger than borrow a match. "Now we're bound. Isn't it restful? Everything's simpler once you're bound to somebody. All those decisions — gone. You just do the thing."

The train ran on through the fens. Brandt talked again — about bees, about Regret the horse, about a girl in Folkestone he hadn't married and was glad of it daily except on Sundays — and Ivo answered in the right places and mostly listened, and beneath the seat, against Ivo's ribs, the letter rode like a second heartbeat.

At York, where their routes divided, Brandt gathered his luggage, failed twice with the rack again, and paused at the door with one hand on the frame.

"You know the strange thing?" he said. "I feel lighter. I've been carrying that letter eight months, and I hand it to a complete stranger and I feel *lighter*. Explain that to me sometime, if we live." He touched his cap brim, half-salute, half-joke. "Look after yourself, Ivo Crane. Live to be a thousand."

The door slid shut. The platform pulled away. And that should have been the end of it — a strange hour, a stranger's whim, a story Ivo might have told someday in an old age that now seemed unlikely ever to arrive.

* * *

Except that promises don't stay in peacetime. Peacetime doesn't hold them; peacetime barely holds itself.

Three years later, on an evening in late July 1914, Ivo stood in the garage on Wexford Row polishing a headlamp that didn't need polishing, because Mrs. Marlowe had guests and the wireless in the parlour was saying words like *mobilization* and *ultimatum*, and the household above was rearranging itself around those words the way water rearranges around a dropped stone.

Six ounces. Ivo still knew the weight to the ounce. Some nights, lying in the narrow room above the garage, Ivo could feel it in the coat on its hook, hanging there in the dark like a passenger waiting to be driven.

Rook Farm. Denmore. Kent. The crooked chimney. Every generation swears they'll fix it.

Ivo set down the rag. Down the row of mews houses, a church bell began ringing — not the hour, not any service; just ringing, the way bells ring when people don't know what else to do with their hands. Overhead the evening sky went the color of old paper.

Live to be a thousand, the dead man had said — no. Not dead. Not yet. Only far away, near a frontier that had developed opinions.

Ivo hung the coat on its peg, letter and all, and went up the stairs toward the lighted kitchen, where Mrs. Marlowe was waiting to say that everything, starting tomorrow, was going to change.
