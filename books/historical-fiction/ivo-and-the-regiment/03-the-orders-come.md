## 3. The Orders Come

The news came over the wireless in the servants' hall at ten minutes past eleven on the fourth of August, and Ivo Crane heard it standing very still beside the sideboard with a tray of clean glasses he had no reason to be holding.

*This country is at war with Germany.*

The voice was calm. That was what struck him first, and what he would remember longest — not the words, which everyone had been rehearsing for a week, but the announcer's tone, level as a timetable read aloud. As though war were simply another service to be announced, a delay on the Ashford line.

One of the glasses rang faintly against its neighbour. Ivo set the tray down before his hands could betray him further.

He did not belong in the servants' hall. He belonged in the garage, or at the wheel of the Daimler, or leaning against the kitchen doorframe waiting for Mrs. Petch to decide whether the day required him at nine or at half past. But that morning everyone had drifted toward the wireless the way iron filings drift toward a magnet, cooks and maids and the gardener's boy all standing shoulder to shoulder in the strange democracy of bad news, and nobody thought to remark on the chauffeur among them.

That was the last morning anyone stood shoulder to shoulder without thinking about it.

By noon the flags were out along the High Street. By two o'clock the paperboys had gone hoarse. By four, Edith Marlowe had called the household into her study, and Ivo understood, watching her face as she waited for them to assemble, that she had known before the wireless told them. She had known the way a barometer knows.

She stood behind her desk rather than sitting at it. Behind her the wall held its usual arrangement: the framed photograph of her and Mrs. Pankhurst outside Holloway, the map of London with its coloured pins marking branch meetings, the shelf of blue-bound pamphlets with *Votes for Women* stamped in white. She looked at these things — Ivo watched her look at them — the way a person looks at furniture in a house they are about to leave.

"You'll have noticed," she said, "that the country has lost its mind."

Nobody laughed. She hadn't meant them to.

"Tomorrow the newspapers will tell us this is a war for liberty. The week after, they'll tell us it's a war for civilization. By September they'll have stopped explaining altogether, because by then no one will ask." She put her hand flat on the desk. "I have spent eleven years teaching women that the state does not own their consciences. It would be an interesting kind of cowardice to teach that lesson only until it became expensive."

Miss Hartley, who typed the newsletters and suffered from chilblains, said, "You mean we're stopping."

"I mean we're changing direction. The suffrage work continues — quieter, smaller, underground if it must. But there will be other work now, and more of it. There are Germans married to Englishmen in Camden Town who woke up this morning to find themselves enemy aliens in their own kitchens. There are reservists' wives who won't see the separation allowance the papers promised. There will be conscientious objectors before Christmas, and refugees, and deserters, and men back from the front who can't say what's happened to them." She took a breath, and Ivo saw the cost of it; she was frightened, and was going to be magnificent anyway. "There will be a great deal of paper, is what I'm saying. And paper is what we do."

Mrs. Petch, who had kept house for Edith since before Ivo's time and trusted neither governments nor opinions, asked the practical question. "And the police?"

"The police," said Edith, "will be busy. For a while, at least, being busy cuts both ways." Her eyes crossed the room and rested on Ivo for half a second longer than on anyone else — not suspicion, he would tell himself later, only arithmetic. She counted people the way she counted pamphlets. "Ivo. The car will be wanted more than ever. Can you manage irregular hours?"

Ivo could. Ivo said so. It was, he reflected that night, the entire substance of his biography: Ivo could, Ivo would, Ivo was available. Eleven years of being the answer to other people's logistics.

"Good," said Edith. "Then we begin tomorrow."

They dispersed. The house went on making the small sounds of a house — water in pipes, a door drawn shut, Mrs. Petch scolding the cat — and none of it knew, Ivo thought, none of it had heard the wireless. The world inside the walls still believed in Tuesday.

* * *

The first censored letters arrived on the Wednesday, and they came to Ivo because everything eventually came to Ivo.

A man from the Quaker relief office in Bishopsgate delivered them in a plain leather valise, and Edith signed for them, and then she did something she had never done before: she closed the study door while Ivo was still in the room.

Not out. In.

"I need hands that aren't mine," she said. "These have to be copied, sorted, and dispatched along three different routes, and I can't be seen carrying them — my face is too well known since the hunger strike. You don't have a face yet, Ivo. That's worth more than you know."

*I don't have a face.* Most people would have been wounded. Ivo felt something nearer to professional recognition, the way a burglar might nod at a locksmith.

The letters were from German-born women in England writing to relations across the North Sea, routed through a charitable society whose letterhead made them officially parcels enquiries. Every one had passed through a censor's office first — some bore the censor's violet stamp, some had lines sliced out with a blade so cleanly the letters seemed to have been written with gaps already in them, silences shaped like England.

Ivo's job, as Edith explained it, was mechanical. Copy the address columns into a ledger. Note which route each letter should take — the Dutch boat, the Danish corridor, the Swiss exchange. Repack. Forget.

He copied the addresses. That part was mechanical.

But he was who he was, and by the third evening, alone in the garage flat with the good lamp angled away from the window and the blackout curtain barely invented yet but improvised anyway from an old motor blanket, he found he had done the other thing too. The thing he always did.

He had memorized them.

*Fräulein Hedwig Albrecht, care of Brenner & Sons, tobacco importers, Rotterdam.* Route two. *Fru Ingrid Møller, Jægersborggade 14, Copenhagen.* Route three, via the Norwegian exchange. *Herr Emil Ostermann, currently detained, Knockaloe Camp, Isle of Man — write to him through his sister, Frau Lene Brandt—*

He stopped.

Brandt.

It was nothing. It was a common enough name; there were Brandts all over the German-speaking world the way there were Cranes all over Kent. He read the sentence again, telling himself it was only the coincidence of sounds, and then he read the rest of the entry, and the coincidence grew teeth.

*Frau Lene Brandt, née Ostermann, Wilmersdorf, Berlin. Brother interned in England. Correspondence may pass via the Red Cross exchange.*

Somewhere in a camp on the Isle of Man there was a man named Brandt, and somewhere in Berlin a sister who would wait for his letters, and Ivo sat with the ledger open on his knees and felt the future lean its weight against the present, subtle as a change in wind.

He thought of a train compartment three years ago. He had been trying not to think of it for days — the whole country was thinking of nothing else, and every thought led eventually to the same border crossing between *then* and *now*. Three years ago there had been a compartment, a delayed connection at some junction whose name he'd forgotten, and a man in uniform — German uniform, which had seemed charmingly foreign then, a costume, a thing from operetta — who had shared Ivo's bread and his newspaper and, by the end of four hours, his silence, which was rarer.

Captain Aurel Brandt. A client's brother, over on manoeuvres, stranded by a cancelled ferry, speaking beautiful careful English. Who had said, as the train crawled through the marshes, *You strike me as a man who keeps his word once given. Most men keep it twice, at most. Give me yours and I shall believe it forever* — and had told him about a farmhouse in Kent, and a family, and a letter that might someday need carrying, and Ivo, embarrassed by the solemnity and moved by it in equal measure, had given his word the way you give a coin to a beggar you hope never to see again: sincerely, and expecting to be rid of it.

Three years. The coin had come back.

Ivo closed the ledger. He sat in the dark with the lamp off and listened to the house settle, and he performed the exercise he had taught himself at fourteen, when invisibility had first become a survival and then a craft: he measured the room by what could be carried in a coat. One bag. Two shirts. A ledger's worth of addresses held where no search could reach them, in the one vault no magistrate could open.

The trouble with memorizing things, he thought, is that memory cannot be burned.

* * *

August thickened. The heat came and stayed, and with it the smell of hot dust on the High Street and the new smell of khaki — wool, canvas, boot-black — that hung around every public building like incense. Recruiting posters bloomed on hoardings overnight. Ivo drove Edith to meetings where the vocabulary had changed entirely: *relief, remittance, internee, next of kin.* Words like *votes* had been folded away with the summer whites.

The world, he observed, had begun measuring people in new units. Not in shillings or acres or reputations now, but in classifications. Alien, naturalized, exempt, fit, unfit, Class A, Class B. Men he had driven for years were suddenly the sum of their regiments. The greengrocer's son was a middling shot and a good swimmer. The vicar's nephew was flat-footed and therefore, temporarily, a human being again. Everyone walked around wearing their new measurements like armbands, and everyone checked everyone else's.

Ivo's own measurement remained conveniently blank. Chauffeur. No regiment, no parish ties that anyone had mapped, no sweetheart, no pub where his absence would be noted. He had spent a decade making himself unmeasurable, and now unmeasurability had become a species of wealth. He was surprised to find it gave him no pleasure. A currency is only comfortable when others hold it too.

Edith worked eighteen-hour days. Letters came and went through the house in growing volume — under parcel wrappers, inside hatboxes, once sewn into the lining of Miss Hartley's travelling coat for the Folkestone run. Ivo learned the routes the way other men learned trench maps: the Dutch boat from Harwich, Tuesdays; the Danish corridor through Bergen, fortnightly; the Swiss exchange, slow and sacred, handled by a woman in Geneva whose name appeared in the ledger only as *M. Roux.*

And every route, every address, lodged itself in him. He fought it at first — there was safety in ignorance, and he knew precisely how much, because he had lived on ignorance's rents for years — but memory was his native language. He had left school at twelve and educated himself entirely by retention: timetables, registration numbers, faces, the precise phrasing of instructions given once. His mind kept things the way chalk cliffs keep footprints. So he learned the network despite himself, address by address, until he understood one grey evening in late August, drying the Daimler's bonnet in the dusk, that he now carried in his head enough names to hang a dozen households, and that he was, in the eyes of any court that cared to look, the most dangerous piece of furniture in Edith Marlowe's house.

Major Corven's name began appearing in the margins of that same week — written in Edith's hand on memorandum slips, *R. says interception begins Monday,* *R. confirms postal raids north of the river.* R., Ivo gathered, was a friend from the old campaign days, a man who had chosen the other uniform. A man who sent warnings. Ivo filed the name with the rest, in the vault, without knowing yet why it would matter.

On the last night of August, a letter came through that was different.

It arrived in the ordinary way — parcel wrapper, charitable letterhead, violet stamp — and it was only when Ivo had it home under the lamp that he saw the censor had struck out not a line or a phrase but nearly everything, leaving a message composed almost entirely of holes. What survived the blade was this:

*My dear — the parcels reach us, tell the lady — my brother writes that — remains of the — believe me ever,*

Ivo read it four times. *Remains of the.* Remains of the what? Of the old system? Of the letter? Or — his eye snagged and tore on it — *remains*, as in what is left of a man?

He copied the surviving fragments into the ledger, packed the letter for route two, and lay awake long past midnight listening to the blackout-dark town breathe. Dread, he decided, was not an event. It was weather. It moved in gradually, altered the light, got into the joints of things, and after a while you forgot there had ever been a season without it.

September came. The first lists of casualties appeared in the papers, thin at first, like the leading edge of rain. Mrs. Petch took to listening to the wireless with her arms folded. Edith wrote *R.* less often, and received fewer replies.

And on a Thursday evening at the end of a day of ordinary driving — stations, committees, a wounded reservist's mother delivered weeping to a house in Islington — Ivo came up the drive at dusk with the lamps just lit and saw, standing at the garden gate in the last grey light, a man who did not stand like a visitor.

He stood like a man who had walked a long way and was no longer sure of his welcome anywhere. Cap pulled low. Left sleeve empty and pinned. Young — younger than the ruin of him suggested. As Ivo slowed the car, the man straightened, and looked past the bonnet, past the headlights, directly at the driver, and Ivo had the sudden vertiginous certainty, before a word was spoken, that for the first time in eleven years he was about to be seen.

"You're him," the man at the gate said. "You're the driver who reads."
