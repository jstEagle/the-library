## 6. Major Corven Calls

He came on a Thursday, which meant nothing to anyone but me, because Thursday was the day the letters went out and the day the letters came in, and if a man were going to choose a moment to catch the Marlowe household with its hands full of paper, Thursday between two and four was the hour.

I saw the car first. A Humber, dark grey, government plates, driven by a man who sat too straight. I was polishing the windscreen of Edith's Austin for no reason except that polishing was what I did with my hands when my head was busy, and I watched the Humber come up Marlowe Lane at exactly the speed limit, no faster, which told me the man behind the wheel was being watched by the man beside him and did not like it.

Two of them. The driver stayed with the car. The passenger got out, and I knew him before he reached the gate.

Major Corven. I had seen him twice before, both times in the years before the war, both times at suffrage meetings where he had stood at the back with his hat in his hands looking like a schoolmaster at a recital—attentive, courteous, and taking notes. Edith had called him *the reasonable one* back then, and had meant it as a compliment and, I think, as a warning. *There are reasonable men in every office, Ivo. They are the dangerous ones. The fanatics you can predict.*

He was greyer now. The war had taken the softness out of his face and left the architecture. He wore it well, the way a building wears a shell.

"You'll be Crane," he said, at the gate, before I had said a word. "The driver. Edith speaks of you."

"Does she, sir."

"Constantly. She says you hear everything and say nothing." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and I understood in that moment that everything I had ever believed about my own invisibility was a story I told myself to sleep at night. "I'm here to see Mrs. Marlowe. Is she at home?"

"She's at home, sir. Shall I announce you?"

"Please."

I left him in the hall, under the coat pegs and the framed print of the Pankhursts, and went up to the study where Edith sat with her sleeves rolled, addressing envelopes in her small fierce hand. I said, "Major Corven," and watched her pen stop mid-word, the ink pooling, and I thought: *she is afraid.* Edith Marlowe, who had faced mounted police and hecklers and her own family's contempt, was afraid, and she was afraid in the particular way of a person who has just remembered that she left something out on the table.

"Give me two minutes," she said. "Put him in the parlour. And Ivo—" She looked at me then, and whatever she was going to say, she decided against it, and said instead: "Tea. The good cups. If he's come to arrest me, I'd rather be arrested over decent china."

* * *

The parlour had been arranged by Edith's mother thirty years ago and had not been permitted to change since: low chairs, a low table, a window that faced the lane, a clock that ticked too loudly for the size of the room. I put Corven in the chair nearest the window because I wanted the light behind him, and I don't fully know why, except that it felt like a small advantage and I was beginning to understand that the afternoon would be made entirely of small advantages.

Edith came down in four minutes with a cardigan buttoned to the throat, and they greeted each other like old comrades, which they were, and like strangers, which they had become.

"Ivo," she said, "the tea."

I had made it before he arrived. I don't know how I knew to do that. Some animal instinct in the back of my skull had smelled the government plates coming up the lane and put the kettle on. I poured for three and took my place at the sideboard, where a servant stands, and where a servant sees everything and is seen by no one, and I settled into that old comfortable blindness like a man settling into a bath.

"Edith." Corven took his tea without sugar. "I'll come to the point, because I know you value that."

"You never valued it," Edith said. "You valued the appearance of it. But go on."

"The War Office has begun intercepting correspondence. All correspondence. Letters to and from enemy aliens, letters to and from the front, letters between—" he paused, and the pause was a knife laid flat on the table, "—certain charitable organizations. There are people in my office who believe that pacifist networks are conduits for enemy communication. I don't share that view. But I thought you should know that your mail, Edith, is being read. All of it. Before you read it."

I did not look at the sideboard. I looked at the tea things. But my whole body had gone quiet, the way a house goes quiet when a floorboard creaks upstairs.

"How kind of you," Edith said, "to warn me."

"I was your ally for six years. I marched with you. I was arrested with you—" he touched his own jaw, an old habit, an old scar I had never noticed before, "—and I have not forgotten it. Whatever else has changed, that hasn't."

"The war changed it."

"The war changed everything," Corven agreed. "That is rather my point." He set down his cup and leaned forward, and the light I had placed behind him now did its work, throwing his face into half-shadow, and I realized I had made a mistake with the chairs and that he had let me make it. "I'm going to ask you some questions, Edith. Not as an intelligence officer. As a friend who would rather hear the answers from you than from a file."

"Ask them anyway," Edith said. "As whichever you are."

* * *

He asked them for forty minutes.

He asked about the relief fund for dependents of interned Germans, which was real and which Edith ran openly. He asked about the Quaker shipping lists, which were real and which Edith showed him. He asked, with the delicacy of a man lifting a stone to see what was under it, about *correspondence with the continent*, and Edith said, "I write to pacifists in Holland and Switzerland, Major, as I have since 1907, and every letter says what I say at every public meeting: that this war is a slaughter and that someone will have to make peace with the people we are currently shelling. If that is a crime, arrest me. You know where I live. You've known for years."

"I don't want to arrest you."

"No," Edith said. "You want to be the man who didn't have to."

And here is the thing I learned that afternoon, the thing I have never since been able to unlearn: I watched Corven's face when she said it, and I saw that she had cut him, and I saw that he had come here carrying a wound from her already, from some earlier conversation, some letter, some moment in the years when they had stood on the same side, and that his questions were not only questions. They were also an argument. He was trying to win her back. And a man who is trying to win you back will show you his hand without meaning to, because wanting is a kind of showing.

He wanted her to be clean. That was the whole of it. He had built a theory of the world in which Edith Marlowe was difficult but honest, and every question he asked was a question he hoped she would answer in a way that let him keep the theory.

And she was honest. That was the horror of it. She answered everything, and everything she said was true, and it was true in the way that a floor is true when you are standing on it over a cellar you have not mentioned.

Because there was the second mailbag. Because there were the letters that came inside other letters, the addresses written in lemon juice and heat-developed over the kitchen range, the names I memorized at night with the lamp turned low. Edith had not lied to Corven. She had simply let him stand on the part of the floor she had chosen to show him.

And I stood at the sideboard with the teapot warming my hand and understood that I was now the only person in the room who knew the shape of the whole house.

* * *

Then he turned to me.

It happened the way a change of wind happens—you feel it on your neck before you can say where it came from. Corven had been looking at Edith, and then he was not, and I felt his attention come around the room and settle on me like a hand on my shoulder.

"Crane," he said. "How long have you driven for Mrs. Marlowe?"

"Four years, sir."

"And before that?"

"Before that I drove for a wine merchant in Clapham. Before that a livery stable. I've driven since I was old enough to reach the pedals."

"You like the work?"

"I like the roads, sir."

He smiled at that, and it was the gate smile again, warm at the edges, and I understood that it was a tool and that he was using it and that I was expected to be flattered by it. A man like Corven did not ask the servant questions because the servant mattered. He asked because the servant was a door into the house that the house had never thought to lock.

"You hear a great deal, I expect. Driving. People talk in cars. They forget there's anyone up front."

"People talk in cars, sir."

"Do they talk to you?"

"Mrs. Marlowe dictates letters sometimes. I take dictation when her hands are tired."

"Do you." Corven turned his cup slowly on its saucer, a quarter-turn, another. "And the letters she receives. Do you handle those?"

Here it was. The whole afternoon had been a road and this was the bend in it, and beyond the bend I could see what was coming, and there was no time to decide anything, and so I did not decide. I simply answered.

"Some, sir. She's particular about her correspondence—she's a suffragist, she's had her mail tampered with since before the war, windows smashed, letters stolen. She doesn't let the post go through anyone's hands but her own and mine, and mine only because I'm the one who collects it from the office in town. She says if it's going to be stolen, better I know what was in it."

I heard myself say it and marveled. It was not a lie, exactly. It was better than a lie. It was a lie wearing the clothes of a complaint, and it did the work of a lie while having the texture of a grievance, and Corven nodded at it the way men nod at grievances, because grievances are the one currency everyone accepts.

"Tampered with," he said. "By whom?"

"Suffragettes, sir. Before the war, I mean. By the authorities." I let a small pause sit there, the kind of pause a careful man makes before he criticizes his betters. "With respect, sir, she's been having her letters opened since 1909. She keeps a file on it. She'd show you, only she'd rather show you than tell you, and she's told you everything already, so."

Corven looked at me for a long moment. The clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled.

"Edith," he said, without turning his head, "your driver is wasted on driving."

"He says so himself," Edith said, "constantly."

But she was watching me, and I did not look at her, and I knew that later—tonight, tomorrow—there would be a reckoning in her eyes, not for what I had said but for how easily I had said it. Because she had hired me, in part, I think, because I was a man who could not lie. She had said so once, early, laughing: *You have an honest face, Ivo, it's the only honest thing about you, don't ruin it.* And I had just spent four minutes ruining it, fluently, with a steady hand, and some part of me was standing back from myself in horror and some other part—the part that had memorized forty addresses by lamplight—was saying: *you can do this. You can carry this. You can lie well enough to keep them all alive.*

It was the most frightening thing I had ever felt. Not fear of Corven. Fear of how good it had felt, the moment the lie left me and landed and held. Like the first time you drive a car alone and feel the machine become your body. Power. That's what it was. I had spent four years cultivating the absence of it, and it had been sitting in me the whole time like a passenger.

* * *

Corven stayed another twenty minutes and did not learn anything else, because there was nothing else to learn from the room he was in. At the door he shook Edith's hand and held it a moment longer than a handshake requires, and said, "Burn nothing. Destroying documents is the one thing that turns suspicion into evidence. Whatever you're doing that's legal, keep doing it in daylight. And Edith—" and here his voice dropped into something older, something from the marches and the arrests, "—if it comes to it, send for me before you send for a solicitor. I can still be useful to you. I would prefer to be."

"I remember," Edith said, "when you were useful to everyone."

He took it like a blow and bowed to it, and went down the steps, and the Humber pulled away up the lane at exactly the speed limit, and I stood in the hall with the door in my hand and watched it go, and my heart was going like a sewing machine and my face was doing nothing at all.

* * *

Edith did not speak until the car was out of sight. Then she said, "The tea things, Ivo," and went up to the study, and I cleared the parlour, and washed the good cups, and dried them, and put them away, and only then, with my hands in the water, did I let myself shake.

She came down at dusk. I was in the garage, because the garage was where I went to be a man with an engine and no thoughts, and she stood in the doorway with the last light behind her and said:

"That business about her letters being opened. Where did that come from?"

"It's true," I said. "She keeps the file. Second drawer of the green cabinet."

"I know it's true. I've known you four years, Ivo, and I know when you're handing a man a true thing to keep him from asking for a false one." She came into the garage, into the smell of oil and cold iron, and looked at me the way she looked at a paragraph that wasn't working. "You lied today."

"No, ma'am."

"You lied today," she said again, "beautifully, and I don't know whether to be proud of you or afraid for you, and I find I don't have time for either, so I'll say only this." She put her hand flat on the Austin's wing, as if taking an oath on it. "Whatever you're carrying that I don't know about—whatever it is, and however heavy—you will tell me before it gets us both killed. Not because I have a right to know. Because I'm better at this than you are, and you'll need me."

She went back to the house. I stood in the garage in the dark with my heart hammering, and I thought about the letter folded inside my waistcoat, the dead man's handwriting, the farmhouse in Kent, and I thought: *she doesn't know yet. But she will. And God help me, I think she'll help.*

And I thought, too, with a coldness that did not feel like my own: *Corven will come back. Men like him always come back. And next time he will not ask questions in a parlour over tea.*

Next time, he would already know the answers.
