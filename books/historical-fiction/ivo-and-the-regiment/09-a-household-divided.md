## 9. A Household Divided

Edith Marlowe had built a career on reading people who believed themselves unreadable. Cabinet ministers who thought a woman couldn't follow arithmetic. Police inspectors who thought a woman couldn't follow motive. Young men from the War Office who came to warn her off and left having told her more than they'd learned. She had spent twenty years being underestimated, and she had turned it into an instrument, the way a carpenter turns a flaw in the grain into the joint that holds.

Which was why it troubled her so much that she could not read Ivo Crane.

Not entirely. She could read him the way one reads weather—a change in pressure, a light gone wrong at the edges. For three years he had been the most reliable constant in her life: the car warmed at seven, the petrol measured to the half-gallon, the letters carried without question and returned without comment. He had opinions about tire pressure and almost none about anything else, or if he had them, he kept them folded away like papers in a coat pocket. She had hired him precisely because he was quiet. She had kept him because his quietness had turned out to be a kind of loyalty, and loyalty, in her experience, was rarer than courage and worth considerably more.

But for eleven days now, something in the household's weather had shifted.

It was small things, because with Ivo it would only ever be small things. The car had been ready four minutes late on Tuesday—four minutes, when in three years he had never been thirty seconds. He had taken to walking out after supper, saying the evening air helped him sleep, which was plausible; half of England was walking off its insomnia these days. But he went in one direction always, toward the river, and he came back with his boots dusted differently than the lane would leave them. And twice she had caught him looking at her across a room—not the quick assessing glance of a driver checking whether his employer wanted the car, but a long, weighted look, the look of a man measuring a debt.

She noticed, also, that he no longer read at the kitchen table.

This was the thing that frightened her, though she could not immediately have said why. From her first week in the house, Ivo had sat of an evening with whatever periodical or penny novel the household had going, positioned exactly where she could see the cover of what he held. She had understood it once as a small courtesy—the servant assuring the mistress there was nothing hidden—and later as something more like a signature. *I am here. I am doing nothing. You may look at me.* And now the reading happened upstairs, behind a closed door, and the kitchen table held only the lamp and the bread board and her own correspondence, and she missed him in a way that had no tidy name.

She stood at the scullery window on Thursday morning, watching him check the car's tires with more attention than any tires deserved, and admitted to herself what she had been circling for a week: either Ivo Crane was in trouble, or Ivo Crane was keeping something from her, and she did not know which possibility she dreaded more.

There was a third possibility, the one she handled only with tongs: that he had found something among the letters. That her work—the routes, the names, the careful smuggling of pacifist correspondence through the postal gaps that the war was closing one by one—had become visible to the one person she had never intended to make a confidant. If that were so, then every night he didn't speak was a night he chose not to. And choice deferred, in her experience, curdled.

"You'll wear a groove round that motor," she said, coming out onto the gravel.

He straightened too quickly, then corrected it, then gave her the small apologetic smile that was his entire public personality. "Rear left's been losing pressure. Thought I'd catch it before it caught you."

"Did you?"

"It's holding."

She came down the steps. The morning was grey and moving, the sort of light that made everything look provisional. "Ivo."

"Ma'am."

"I don't pay you to lie about tires, and I don't pay you enough to lie about anything else."

The pause lasted less than a heartbeat. To anyone else it would have been invisible. To her it rang like a dropped pan. "I'm not sure what you mean."

"No," she said. "I imagine not."

She let it sit there between them on the gravel. This was the technique she used on ministers: state the accusation without sharpening it, then wait, because people rushed to fill silence the way water fills a hole. But Ivo didn't rush. He looked at the rear left tire as though it might intervene on his behalf, and when he finally spoke his voice was perfectly level and perfectly empty.

"If there's something wrong with the car, ma'am, you'd be the first to know."

*If.* Such a small hinge for such a large door.

"The post was late again," she said, changing ground deliberately. Watch how a man moves on one terrain and you learn his habits; move him suddenly and you learn his reflexes. "Two days from Harwich. It used to be one."

"There's a new sorting office," he said. "They're opening everything with a German surname first. It backs up the rest."

"How would you know that?"

And there—he flinched. Not visibly. His hands didn't move. But something behind his eyes did, a shutter drawn in haste, and she knew she'd scored. He recovered beautifully, which was itself informative: *beautifully* meant *practiced*. "The postman complains," he said. "To Mrs. Tice. She tells everyone."

Mrs. Tice did tell everyone. It was airtight. It was also the first time in three years that Ivo Crane had ever offered her an explanation she hadn't asked for.

"Well," she said lightly. "Mind the tires." And she went back inside, because pressing further now would only teach him where her instruments were, and because a colder thought had arrived and needed attending:

Someone had told him about the sorting office. Someone in the network, or someone outside it. Either way, her driver was talking to people she didn't know about, and receiving things back.

She climbed the stairs to her study and did not look out the window, because she knew he would be watching the house the way he always watched it, faithfully, invisibly, and she could not bear just now to be looked at by a man she could no longer fully see.

* * *

Corven arrived at half past three, unannounced, which was unlike him, and in uniform, which was unlike everything he had stood for.

She met him in the hall because she refused, on principle, to receive him as though he required a drawing room. He filled the doorway the way he always had—broad, rumpled at the collar even in major's khaki, his moustache greying faster than the rest of him—and for a moment he was simply Gerald, who had chained himself to railings beside her in 1908 and wept openly at the funeral of her friend Constance Lytton's sister. Then he took off his glove finger by finger, and the gesture was all officer, and the man she'd marched with receded behind it like a coast in fog.

"Edith."

"Gerald. You've come to arrest my hatpins."

"I've come," he said, "as a friend."

"Friends write ahead."

"Friends," he said, hanging his own coat on the hall peg because he still remembered where it went, and that small domestic betrayal hurt worse than the uniform, "are becoming a liability in my profession."

They went into the morning room. She poured the tea herself, refusing Mrs. Tice, because she wanted no witnesses to whatever this was and no one else implicated in it. He took the chair opposite hers—the guest chair, never his old usual seat—and she registered that too, the deliberate choosing of distance.

"The Postal Censorship has expanded its remit," he said. "You'll have seen the notices. Correspondence to and from enemy territory, correspondence in foreign languages, correspondence deemed prejudicial—" He waved a hand, weary. "Broad language. Broad language invites broad application."

"You said this in June."

"In June I came to tell you to burn your German addresses. In August I came to tell you the interception had moved inland. Today I've come with less pleasant news." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning the handle with the rim, and she watched the compulsive neatness of it and thought: *he is rehearsing.* "I have a warrant, Edith."

The word landed in the room and stayed there.

"To inspect correspondence held at this address. Yours and—" he hesitated, delicately, "—whatever passes through it. It is framed as routine. Every organization with international contacts is being inspected. The Quakers. The trade unions. The No-Conscription Fellowship when it formally exists, which give it a month."

"And you volunteered to deliver it yourself."

"I requested it," he said, with the flat honesty that had once made him the most infuriating ally she had, "because I wanted to look you in the face when I told you, and because I wanted to conduct it myself rather than send men who would enjoy it."

"Conduct it." She laughed, and heard how ugly the laugh came out. "Gerald. We buried women's cause together. You held my coat at Holloway. Now you sit in my morning room with a piece of paper authorizing you to read my mail."

"Yes," he said simply. "That's the shape of it. I won't insult us both by pretending otherwise. The country has changed units, Edith. Everything is measured differently now." He set the warrant on the table between them, squared to the table's edge. "I can do this tomorrow with two sergeants who think pacifists are a species of vermin, or I can do it today, slowly, badly, and overlook whatever a reasonable man might overlook. Your choice."

The manipulation was so naked it was almost tender. She looked at the paper. Her mind ran the inventory with the speed of long practice: the current bundle in the bureau drawer—twelve letters, three coded by the flour-water trick, one containing names that would put a schoolmistress in Hull in front of a tribunal. The packet due tomorrow from the north-east route. The list she did not keep written anywhere, which existed only in her memory and—

And, apparently, somewhere in Ivo Crane's.

The thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome: *what does he know, and when Corven's men turn this house over, what will they find that points at him?* She had spent years ensuring that everything dangerous in this house pointed only at her. She had designed it that way the way other women designed kitchens. And now there was an unknown quantity in the garage with dust on his boots from a road he shouldn't have walked, and her design had a hole in it shaped like a quiet man.

"Today," she said. "Slowly and badly suits me fine."

He almost smiled. "You haven't asked what happens if I find something."

"I know what happens. I've read the Defence of the Realm Act more carefully than the men who wrote it." She stood. "You'll want the bureau. I'll have my driver bring better light in. He's discreet, my driver—you'll remember you used to value that in a person."

"I remember," Corven said quietly, "valuing a great many things I'm paid to suspect now."

For one moment the major's face slipped and Gerald looked out of it, tired and grieving, and she nearly reached for his hand. Nearly. Then he straightened the warrant again, quarter-turn, edge aligned, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and Edith performed the role of her life.

It was not the searching that tested her—Corven was true to his word, turning pages at the pace of a man reading obituaries, holding the flour-water sheets to the window light for a full theatrical minute before observing that the paper seemed quite blank. What tested her was Ivo.

She had warned him with ten seconds' notice: *"Mr. Crane will assist Mr.—"* a glance at the sergeant—"with the lamp. The study light is poor." And Ivo had come in with a hurricane lamp as though summoned to fix a fuse, and he had stood for three hours holding light for the men going through her drawers, and his face had been a masterpiece of bland helpfulness.

That was what frightened her. Not fear in him—she would have known how to read fear, would almost have preferred it—but *fluency*. When the sergeant asked whether the household received much foreign post, Ivo said, "Only the vicar's sermons from his Swiss holiday, sir, and those are duller than any cipher," and the sergeant laughed, and the laugh bought them all a foot of ease. When Corven asked how often she dictated correspondence, Ivo gave exact figures—Tuesdays and Fridays, roughly forty items a month—that were accurate and therefore disarming, and she realized he had been counting her letters for three years the way she counted hers.

Her driver knew her traffic. Had always known it. Had perhaps memorized it.

She watched him hold the lamp steady while Corver's man shook out the lining of her writing case, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *he is lying better than I am, right now, in my own house, and I cannot tell whether he is lying for me or merely near me.*

Because there was a difference, and the difference was everything. A man lying *for* you could be trusted with your life. A man lying *near* you—one who had simply decided that concealment served him while it served—was a draft through the floorboards, cold air from a room you didn't know existed.

At six o'clock Corven closed the bureau, satisfied or pretending to be, and stood in the hall pulling on his gloves finger by finger. "Nothing irregular," he told the sergeant, loud enough for the household. Then, to her, lower: "The inspection will be marked concluded. Whether it stays concluded depends on what comes through the sorting offices in the next few weeks." He put on his cap. "Burn the German addresses, Edith. Do it tonight. I won't ask again, and next time I may not be the one standing here."

"I'll weigh it," she said, which was what she had said to him in June, and August, and every time, and he nodded as though she'd promised, because he needed to believe it, and went out into the dusk with his sergeant trailing.

The door closed. The house exhaled. Mrs. Tice could be heard in the kitchen putting the kettle on with aggressive normalcy.

Edith remained in the hall, listening to the car carry Corven down the lane, and made herself stand still until the engine sound faded entirely, because her legs wanted to shake and she refused to permit it while anyone might see.

Then Ivo came around the corner with the hurricane lamp, already doused, held in one hand like a thing he wasn't sure he was allowed to put down.

"You did well," she said.

"Thank you, ma'am."

"Too well, Ivo."

Silence. The lamp ticked faintly as it cooled. Somewhere outside the first owl of the evening started up, and beyond it, far off, the strange far thunder that everyone pretended was weather but no one anymore believed was weather.

"Those figures you gave," she said. "Tuesdays and Fridays. Forty items a month. You've been counting."

He looked at the doused lamp. At the floor between them, which was polished oak and reflected nothing useful. When he raised his eyes they were, for once, entirely unguarded, and what was in them was not guilt exactly but something older and heavier, the expression of a man carrying something up a long stair.

"Yes, ma'am," he said. "I count everything. It's how I keep people safe."

*People*, plural. She held his gaze and did not let go, and the whole house seemed to lean in around them—the bureau upstairs with its twelve letters, the garage with its punctured tire she now intended to examine before dark, the lane where a major's tail-lights had just vanished toward town.

"Whose people, Ivo?" she asked quietly. "Count them for me."

He opened his mouth—and the bell at the garden gate rang, sharp and urgent, once, twice, a hand far less patient than Corven's, and whatever confession was balanced on the edge of Ivo Crane's tongue went back into the dark where it had been living, and Edith Marlowe turned toward the door with her heart already sinking, because she knew, before she touched the latch, that the world had stopped waiting for either of them to finish their sentences.
