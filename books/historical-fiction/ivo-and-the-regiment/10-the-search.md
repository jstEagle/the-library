## 10. The Search

They came at half past nine in the morning, while the frost was still on the gravel and the kitchen still smelled of toast. Ivo knew before Edith's housekeeper finished the sentence — *men at the gate, sir, with papers* — because his hands had gone cold two minutes earlier, some animal part of him hearing engines that hadn't yet turned into the drive.

Four men. A black Hupmobile behind them, and behind that a lorry with a canvas back, which was not the vehicle of a warning visit.

Ivo was in the garage by then. He did not remember deciding to go there. His body had learned the geography of fear the way other bodies learn dance steps.

The letter was where it always lived, folded small inside the lining of his driving coat, against the left breast, where a man might carry a photograph of someone he loved. He had told himself for eleven days that this was the safest place for it. Now, with the lorry idling in the drive and boots coming up the path, he understood what he'd actually done: he had strapped the thing over his heart like a target.

*Think.* The garage was cold, smelled of petrol and old leather and the particular sweetness of rubber. Edith's Daimler sat under its dust sheet. Behind it, against the wall, stood the stack of worn tires they kept for the estate cars — three of them bald, one flat, punctured in April and never sent for repair because there had been no hands to spare and then no point, since the estate car had been requisitioned anyway and the tire had simply waited, patient as everything else in this house that war had made useless.

A punctured tire. A hole in it, wide enough —

He took up the awl from the bench first, and then hated himself for the thought, because widening a wound already made was a kind of desecration, even of rubber. The valve stem came out with a twist of the pliers. He rolled Brandt's letter tight — tight, tighter, his thumb pressing until the paper complained — and fed it through the valve hole into the dark of the casing. It vanished the way a coin vanishes into a drain. He worked it deeper with the awl shaft, down along the inside wall of the tire, until he could feel nothing of it but the faintest stiffness in the rubber, the sort of lump any old casing develops.

Then he put the tire on top of the stack, third from the top, unremarkable, and pulled the dust sheet half across the Daimler so the pile looked like what it was: junk waiting for a war to end.

His hands were shaking now. That was fine. Hands could shake. It was the face that mattered. He had learned that much from years of standing behind motor cars while his employers' guests said things about people like him within easy earshot, believing the driver was furniture. A steady face was a tool, and tools were kept sharp whether or not you expected to use them.

He wiped the bench. He closed the valve box. He went out the side door and around to the front just as Major Corven stepped out of the Hupmobile, and Ivo arranged his face into the mild, slightly dull attentiveness of a man who has been interrupted at chores.

* * *

Corven wore civilian clothes again — grey suit, good boots — as if the uniform would have been an admission. He carried a document folder under one arm the way another man might carry a hymn book, and he gave Ivo a nod that was almost warm.

"Crane."

"Sir."

"Miss Marlowe at home?"

"She's in her study. Shall I announce you?"

"That won't be necessary." Two of the men were already moving toward the front door; the other two stood by the lorry with their hands in their pockets, looking at the house the way surveyors look at land. Corven produced a paper. "Warrant to inspect correspondence and related materials. There'll be a search of the premises. Routine."

"Routine," Ivo said, and heard how flat he made it, neither question nor protest, just an echo. Good. Questions invited answers. Answers invited follow-up questions. He had spent the night rehearsing exactly this and now found the rehearsal useless, because terror had rinsed everything out of his head except the physical facts: the letter in the third tire from the top. Vane's whereabouts — the loft above the stable block, sleeping, or pretending to sleep, a discharged soldier with no discharge papers worth the name. And himself, standing here, guilty of nothing the law had a word for.

Corven looked at him a moment longer than courtesy required. "You seem well, Crane."

"Well enough, sir."

"No trouble sleeping?"

The question landed with a precision that told Ivo it had cost Corven something to ask, some old loyalty to Edith spent like a coin he couldn't afford. *No trouble sleeping.* As if sleep were the thing at stake.

"I sleep when the car's maintained," Ivo said. "It's a quiet life, sir."

Something moved behind Corven's eyes — disappointment, maybe, at how easily the surface held. He turned and went inside, and the search began.

* * *

They were thorough in the way Ivo had feared and also hoped for: systematic, unhurried, indifferent. They did not smash things. They read things. That was the horror of it — a sergeant with spectacles going through Edith's desk drawer by drawer, holding each letter to the window light as if steam might rise off the lies. Another man went through the linen cupboard and patted down the flour sacks in the pantry. A third took the books off the shelves one at a time and riffled them spine-up, and Ivo watched him and thought about all the letters that had passed through this house and none of which were here, none, because Ivo had memorized them instead — every address, every route, every name written between lines — and the memorized things were safe, and the memorized things were also a gallows being built plank by plank inside his own skull, and he filed that thought away where he kept the others.

Edith received them like visiting relations. Ivo saw her only in passing, once, on the landing: she wore her grey day dress and her reading glasses pushed up into her hair, and she said, "Mind the fourth step, Sergeant, it complains," and the sergeant with the spectacles actually apologized. She caught Ivo's eye for half a second. Whatever she saw in his face, she turned away from it deliberately, giving him privacy for it, which was somehow worse than if she'd stared.

Then two men came out to the garage.

Ivo followed them, because following them was the only way to keep his body from doing anything else. He offered help. They declined it politely. One climbed into the Daimler and ran his hand along the underside of the seats; the other began on the walls, tapping, sounding out the panels like a doctor percussing a chest.

The tire stack sat in the corner. Third from the top. The letter was six inches of rolled paper inside a dead casing, and it might as well have been ticking.

The tapper finished the walls and turned around. He was young, twenty-five perhaps, with sunburnt knuckles and the careful movements of a man who had recently learned searching as a trade. He crossed to the tires and lifted the top one off and weighed it in his hands, and set it down, and took the second.

Ivo made himself useful. He picked up the tire iron. He said, "Those are mostly done, I'm afraid. Scrap, more or less."

"Mm," said the searcher, which meant *that's for me to say.*

Second tire: he flexed it, checked the tread, put it down. Third tire — the third tire — he hoisted onto the bench, and the sound it made hitting the wood was soft, dead, wrong, and Ivo's whole consciousness narrowed to the size of a valve hole.

The man rotated it slowly. Ran his thumb along the bead. Found the puncture, put his finger in it.

"There's something—" he began, and Ivo said, "Valve stem's gone, she's been flat since spring," and heard his own voice come out level, bored even, and some detached part of him stood to one side and marveled: *listen to yourself. Listen to how easily it comes.* The lie had come out fully dressed, with details, with history, with the weary tone of a man tired of discussing a scrap tire. He had never lied like this before Chapter Six. He was getting better at it every week, and someday, he understood dimly, he would have to account to himself for what that practice was for.

The searcher grunted and pressed his finger deeper into the puncture. The rubber gave. Beneath his fingertip, six inches of folded paper lay in the dark like a swallowed secret.

"Here." The other man straightened up from the Daimler. "Sergeant wants the tools laid out. All of them."

The young man set the tire down — set it down on its side, puncture facing the wall, Ivo noticed, without knowing why he noticed — and went to lay out the tools. Jack, crank, spanners, the awl, the pliers. Each item handled and catalogued. Ivo stood with his hands loose at his sides and breathed the way you breathe when you've been underwater too long and can't let anyone see you gasping.

Close range. That was the phrase he'd carry out of this morning: the search conducted at close range, a stranger's fingers four inches from Aurel Brandt's handwriting, a stranger's breath fogging in the cold air of the garage where Ivo's oath lived in a piece of rubber. Terror in stillness. Nobody tells you that terror is mostly stillness — the body refusing every instinct it has, holding the shape of an ordinary man while everything underneath screams to run.

They finished the garage in eleven minutes. The sergeant signed a paper saying they'd finished it. The young searcher paused at the door, looked back at the tire stack, and Ivo felt the look like weather moving across skin.

"You keep these long?"

"Till the war's over and rubber's cheap again," Ivo said. "Everything waits nowadays."

The man nodded, satisfied by the shape of the answer rather than its truth, and went out.

* * *

It was past noon when they brought Vane down.

Ivo was cleaning the windscreen of the Hupmobile — ordered to, pointedly, so that his hands would be occupied and visible — when two of the men came around the corner of the stable block with Tomas Vane between them. Vane had his coat over one arm and his cap crushed in one fist, and his face had the grey, apologetic look of a man who has been taken somewhere before and expects it always.

He didn't struggle. That was the worst of it. He went between them like a man boarding a train, docile, used to being processed. At the gate he stopped and turned, and found Ivo across the yard, and his mouth opened.

"Don't," Ivo said, very quietly, from behind the windscreen cloth. Not to Vane alone — to everything, to the morning, to the whole machinery now grinding forward. But Vane heard it, and whatever he'd been about to say died in his face, and he turned and was put into the lorry.

Corven came last. He paused by Ivo at the car, adjusting his glove.

"We may want to talk to you as well, Crane. About the household's comings and goings."

"I don't come and go, sir. I'm the coming-and-going."

Corven smiled at that — a real smile, briefly, the ghost of the man who had once marched beside Edith Marlowe and believed the world could be argued right. Then the smile folded itself away.

"Yes," he said. "That's rather what interests me."

The lorry pulled out. The Hupmobile followed. The gravel rang with engines and then held only frost and silence, and Ivo stood in the empty yard with the cleaning cloth still in his hand, listening to his own heart try to slow down and fail.

Vane would be questioned tonight. Tomorrow. However long it took. And Tomas Vane — gentle, broken, grateful Tomas Vane, who had carried a dead man's letter across half of England because he had nowhere else to put his loyalty — would be asked, patiently and repeatedly, what he had seen at this house. Who he had met. Where he had slept. Whom he had spoken to.

And Vane had never seen Brandt. That was the mercy. Vane had dealt only with Ivo — the meetings at the mill, the letters passed in the dark, the arrangement that had grown between them. Every fact Vane possessed pointed not at the traitor in hiding but at the driver who read.

Ivo walked slowly back to the garage. The tire sat where the searcher had left it, puncture to the wall. He knelt, worked the letter out with two fingers, and unrolled it on the bench: *the same hand*, upright and patient, the hand of a man who wrote carefully because he was writing from inside his own death. He read the final line again — *tell Crane the debt stands, and the road is the road we agreed* — and folded it, and put it back into the dark of the casing.

Then he went to find Edith, because tomorrow — or the day after, or whenever Corven's patience ran out — a frightened soldier was going to describe him.
