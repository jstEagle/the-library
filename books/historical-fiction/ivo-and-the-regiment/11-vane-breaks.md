## 11. Vane Breaks

The news came the way bad news always came to our house: folded inside an envelope that looked like nothing at all.

Edith's Tuesday correspondence arrived in three batches—a grocer's circular, a church newsletter from a parish none of us attended, and a plain brown wrapper postmarked Croydon containing a single sheet of thin paper on which Mrs. Ferris, who ran the relief office and had a genius for hiding alarming sentences inside dull ones, had written about coal prices. Halfway down, between two figures for tons delivered to the Leeds depot, was a line in slightly smaller hand:

*V. detained at Marleigh since Friday. Asked same questions twice daily. Has given a description. Come soon.*

I was standing in the hall with the post sorted into its usual piles when I read it, and I am pleased to report that my hands did not shake, my face did not change, and when Edith called from the study asking whether there was anything of interest, I said, "Coal's up another sixpence," in the flat tone of a man who found the entire world as boring as coal. The lying had not worn off yet. It still felt like a talent rather than a wound.

But upstairs, in my narrow room with its narrow bed and its one window facing the mews, I sat down on the floor with my back against the bedframe and read the sentence again until the words stopped meaning anything.

*Has given a description.*

A description of what? Of the man at the mill—of Brandt, half his face ruined, wrapped to the eyes in a muffler, voice like gravel dragged through water. That would be expected. That was survivable; a ruined face was a poor portrait, because no one looking for such a man could believe he might walk past them unnoticed. Ruin was its own disguise.

But Vane had been questioned twice daily since Friday, and Vane knew almost nothing about Brandt. He had carried a letter. He had asked at the gate for *the driver who reads.* What did Vane know of Brandt? A name never spoken aloud. A meeting at a mill arranged through intermediaries. Nothing.

What Vane knew—the only thing Vane knew whole and complete, having stared at it across a stable yard by lantern light—was me.

I got up and stood at the window. Below, Peters the groom was currying the bay with the slow patience of men who have outlived three wars' worth of rumors. The morning went on being ordinary. Somewhere in Marleigh, in a room with a table and probably a lamp and probably a stenographer, a clerk was writing down words that Vane had said, and those words were assembling themselves into a picture, and the picture had my height in it, and my age, and my hands, and the way I spoke, and sooner or later it would have my name.

*Betrayal without malice,* I thought—that phrase came to me then and has never left. Vane had not sold me. Vane had simply broken, the way a man breaks, the way anyone breaks, under the patient grinding of questions asked twice daily by men who could go home to their dinners afterward. He had described the last kind face he'd seen before the cell door closed. It was not treachery. It was arithmetic. The machinery of survival ground on, indifferent to whose bones it used, and Vane's bones were the nearest to hand.

The trouble was that mine were next.

* * *

I drove Edith to her afternoon committee—the Pacifist Relief Bureau had absorbed her suffrage network the way a river absorbs a tributary, all the same water moving faster now—and while she sat behind frosted glass arguing about shipping manifests that were not about shipping at all, I walked to the public library on Fetter Lane and read four days of newspapers.

This was how you learned things now. Not by asking. Asking left fingerprints.

There was nothing in the papers, which told me Corven had kept it quiet. A detained discharged soldier meant nothing to editors; a traitor hunt meant something, and Corven was too careful to show his cards before he had the whole hand. But the library had another resource the papers lacked: Sergeant Dalgleish, formerly of Edith's marches, currently employed cataloguing regimental donations, who heard everything the police heard because his brother-in-law drank with constables.

I did not approach him directly. I shelved myself near his desk and waited, and after ten minutes he said, without looking up from his ledger, "Rotten weather for October," which was our old signal for *someone is asking dangerous questions.*

"How rotten?" I said, also not looking up.

"Marleigh way. Fellow named Vane. They're saying he deserted, but he's got his discharge papers, so that doesn't sit right." Dalgleish turned a page with great ceremony. "They're saying there's someone else. Someone who helped him. They've been round the garages."

"The garages."

"Chauffeurs. Mechanics. Anyone who'd hire out a motor." He turned another page. "Man named Corven's been doing it himself, some of it. Not the sort to send juniors. Likes to look people in the eye, they say."

I thanked him for the weather report and walked out of the library at exactly my ordinary pace, and my ordinary pace nearly failed me at the door, because Corven himself, going garage to garage, looking chauffeurs in the eye—that was not a hunt anymore. That was a net, drawn tight around the shape of a man, and every mile of it I had driven in the last week was a thread.

He had seen me. Twice now he had sat in our kitchen drinking tea I poured him. My face was already in his collection. All he needed was for Vane's words to arrange themselves next to it and say: *this tall. This young. This quiet. Hands like a mechanic's. Eyes down, mostly, but when they come up—*

When they come up. God help me, that was the thing Vane would have remembered. Every servant learns to keep eyes down; it is the first lesson of invisibility and I had taken the degree in it. But at the gate, at dusk, I had looked up at a stranger and asked him if he was hurt. I had let myself be *kind*, and kindness is the one act no disguise survives, because it is done with the whole face.

* * *

That evening I took the Daimler out on the pretext of fuel and ran her along the river road with the windows open, letting the cold air scour the panic down to something usable. Panic is useless; I had learned that in other, smaller crises—wrong turns, flooded carburetors, a magistrate's horse spooked by a lorry. You cannot drive a car while your body believes it is dying. You convert the fear into lists.

List one: what does Corven know tonight?

He knows a soldier named Vane passed through a certain district carrying correspondence of suspicious origin. He knows Vane met someone. He has a description: male, twenties, competent with motors, soft-spoken, working or recently worked in service in this general area. He does not yet, I judged, have the connection to the Marlowe house—Dalgleish would have said so, and more importantly, Corven would not have drunk our tea so amiably if he had it. A man with a name does not probe. A man with a name arrives.

Which gave me list two: how long until he has the name?

Vane knew I served the Marlowe household—he had come to our gate. Even broken, even sorry, he would give that eventually, because it was true and because after enough repetitions a man forgets which truths were safe. Say three days. Say five. Say a week if Vane's conscience fought hard, and I believed it fought; there had been something in the man at the mill, some stubborn decency under all the damage, that would resist naming the one person who had helped him. But resistance was only delay. The machine did not stop for consciences. It merely noted them and applied more pressure.

So: a week, at the outside, before Major Corven connected the driver who reads to the household of Edith Marlowe, and everything I had built my invisibility to protect came apart at once—Brandt, the letters, the addresses in my head, and Edith herself, who had spent thirty years making enemies precisely the lawful way and did not deserve to be destroyed the criminal one.

List three: what do we do?

We move. Not next month. This week.

The word *we* arrived in my head before I noticed it, and I made myself look at it squarely, driving along the black water. We. Brandt, yes—Brandt had to go regardless; the promise required it and the danger demanded it. But *we* had crept wider than Brandt. *We* meant the plan, the route, the timing—and the person who would have to approve a route, who kept maps in her study drawer, who knew every back road from here to the coast better than any Ordnance Survey because she had walked them organizing marches when the police blocked the main roads.

To move Brandt safely, in a week, with Corven's net tightening, I needed help. And the only help that existed was Edith. And to ask Edith for help was to confess—to tell her everything, the letters I had read for years, the promise, the dead man alive in a barn, all of it—and to confess was to place on her the very danger I was trying to carry alone, and possibly to discover that the belonging I had built my whole life around could not survive the truth.

The river kept moving beside me, dark and indifferent, doing what rivers do regardless of who drowns in them. I thought: she would want to know. I thought: knowing will ruin her. I thought both things with equal force and neither won, and I turned the car for home with the decision still unmade and the deadline suddenly visible, the way a reef becomes visible when the tide drops—not closer than you feared, just finally undeniable.

* * *

Brandt I saw once more before the week turned.

It was a risk to go—I knew it was a risk, and I went anyway, because a description loose in the world changes the mathematics of everything. If they were hunting my shape, then every day Brandt stayed within a day's travel of me was a day the two halves of the evidence could collide. I told Edith the car needed a bearing looked at in Ashford and drove the long way round, arriving at the mill near dark.

Vane was not there. That frightened me worse than anything, until I saw the chalk mark on the lintel—two short strokes, their agreed sign for *moved, waiting*—and found Brandt in the second barn of the derelict farm half a mile on, sitting propped against hay bales with his ruined face turned toward the one small window.

"They took him Tuesday," Brandt said, before I had finished closing the door. His altered voice still startled me every time, gravel and wind through a broken flute. "Your soldier boy. Plainclothes men, at the lodging house where he'd been sleeping. I watched from across the lane. Couldn't do a thing."

"I know. I came to tell you—" And then I stopped, because telling him meant telling him everything, and I found I could not feed him the whole weight of it. He was a dead man living on borrowed silence; he did not need mine too. "They've been questioning him. He'll have talked."

"He'll have talked," Brandt agreed, with terrible calm. "Anyone would. I did, once, in Germany—first hour, before they even hurt me, I told them my mother's maiden name and hated myself for a year before I understood it hadn't mattered." He shifted against the bales. "The question isn't whether he talked, Crane. It's what talking cost. Him or you?"

"Me."

"Ah." He was quiet a moment. In the grey light his good eye found me and held. "Then you shouldn't have come here. You know that."

"I know that."

"And yet." Something that was almost a smile moved on the intact half of his face. "You came anyway. That's why I trusted you on that train, you know. Not the promise—any fool makes promises on trains. It was that you looked worried for me. A stranger, worried, before I'd asked for anything." He pulled the muffler higher over the ruin of his jaw. "So. How many days do we have?"

"A week. Less."

"Then we go in three." He said it without hesitation, the way he must have said orders once, in another life, with another face. "Three days, before their picture finishes developing. Whatever you need to do—whoever you need to become, whatever you need to confess—you do it tomorrow. Understood?"

Understood. I said it. I shook his hand, the whole unscarred right one, and I walked out through the dark farmyard to the car, and the whole drive home the word rang in me like a struck pipe: *confess.*

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow I would climb the kitchen-table mountain of it. Tomorrow I would lay the letters, the promise, the dead man, all my years of beautiful theft on the table between us and watch to see whether Edith Marlowe's face showed betrayal or understanding—or something worse than either: pity, for a driver who had thought himself invisible and learned, far too late, that he had been watched all along.

Her lamp was still burning when I brought the car round to the mews. Through the study window I could see her bent over her desk, grey hair coming loose from its pins, utterly unaware that the net had her address in it now, that it was drawing in by inches, that the clock above her own bookshelf had become the enemy's instrument.

She looked up as the car door shut. Through the glass, across the garden, she raised one hand—an ordinary gesture, a household gesture, *you're late, come in*—

and tomorrow I would have to answer it with the truth, and I did not know yet which of us that would destroy first.
