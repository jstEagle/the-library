## 13. The Plan

Edith Marlowe had spent thirty years learning that the most dangerous thing a woman could do was think clearly in front of men who preferred her frightened. So she did her clearest thinking alone, at the kitchen table, at four in the morning, when the house held its breath and even the pipes stopped their complaining.

She sat with a cup of tea gone cold beside her and three sheets of paper laid out flat, and she allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to feel the full weight of what she was contemplating. Then the minute ended, and she began to work.

The boy—Ivo, though she thought of him as *the boy* only in the privacy of her own skull, because he was twenty-six and had driven her through two general elections and one near-riot and deserved better than a diminutive—had told her everything two nights ago. The letters. The promise made on a train before the war, to a captain whose name now appeared in the newspapers between the words *coward* and *traitor*. The farmhouse in Kent. And lastly, the thing he had saved for last, delivered in a voice sanded down to almost nothing: Brandt himself, alive, half-ruined, hiding within a day's drive.

He had expected rage. She had watched him brace for it, shoulders set like a man awaiting a blow, and she understood then how lonely his months of secrecy must have been—how heavy the arithmetic of protecting everyone by deceiving everyone. Instead she had put the kettle on, because shock wants something to do with its hands, and when the tea was poured she said: "You should have told me sooner, but I understand why you didn't, and I'm not going to waste the time we have being angry."

He had wept then. Just once, briefly, the way men of his generation wept—turned toward the window, jaw working, as if grief were a private function best conducted facing outdoors. She had let him. Some dignities were worth preserving.

Now she drew a line down the first sheet and wrote *CORVEN* at the top.

Here was what she knew about Major Corven, and she knew more than most, because she had known him when he was merely Cyril, a lean young lawyer who came to suffrage meetings and took minutes in a hand so precise it looked printed. He had marched with them. He had been arrested once—a calculated arrest, she'd suspected even then, the kind that looks good at committee dinners—and he had spoken at her kitchen table about justice as though it were a building they could all live in if only they kept sweeping the steps.

Then the war came, and Cyril Corven discovered that the state paid better than the movement, and that suspicion was a currency more reliable than hope. He had come to her house twice since, wearing his intelligence officer's face, asking questions shaped like courtesies. She had answered them shaped like ignorance. Both of them had known the other was lying, and both of them had let it stand, because there was an old affection there that neither wished to be the first to kill.

But he would kill it. She had no doubt of that anymore. Vane's detention had seen to that—the boy-soldier taken in for questioning, released hollow-eyed, and now Ivo's report that Vane had described *him*, not Brandt, to Corven's people. The hunt had a shape now, and the shape was her chauffeur.

Corven would come for her papers soon. Perhaps a warrant, perhaps another friendly call followed by men in the drive. Her correspondence—years of it, names and routes and the quiet machinery of relief work that had drifted, war by war, letter by letter, into territory the government called treason—would hang a dozen people if it reached his desk intact.

So: it would reach his desk. That was the beginning of the plan, and the plan's whole terrible elegance, and she had arrived at it the way she arrived at most decisions—not through courage, which she distrusted as a motive, but through bookkeeping.

*Item,* she wrote. *They are looking for me already.*

*Item.* A hunt concentrates on its quarry. While Corven sat across from her, cataloguing her, arresting her, drowning in her papers, his attention would be a fixed point. Men could move in the dark a fixed point leaves behind.

*Item.* Ivo drives Brandt to Kent.

She underlined nothing. Underlining was for people who needed to convince themselves.

* * *

She woke him at seven, which felt almost merciful after her own night, and found him already dressed in the kitchen, boiling water he didn't need, the way he did when his thoughts were running too fast for his hands. There was flour on the counter. He'd started bread and abandoned it.

"Sit down," she said.

"I'll make the tea—"

"Ivo." He sat. She took the chair opposite and folded her hands, and saw him register the gesture, because he knew her gestures the way she knew the sound of his boots on the gravel—he could tell from the second stair whether she'd slept. Three years of shared roads did that. It was why the next part would hurt him, and why she would do it anyway.

"Here is what happens," she said. "On Thursday, you take the Humber—no. Not the Humber, Corven knows the Humber. Mr. Petch's hearse, the one his nephew borrowed for his mother-in-law's funeral in March. Petch owes me; his wife's operation was paid out of funds he believes came from a charity and actually came from me. The hearse goes north on the Ashford road, joins the cortege route out of Marleigh—there are two military funerals Thursday morning, the whole road will be black with mourners and no one stops a hearse. You'll be in Kent by evening."

"Edith—"

"Let me finish. This is the part you will argue with, so hear all of it first." She slid the second sheet toward him. "Thursday at eleven, I telephone Corven myself. I tell him I have documents he wants, and that I am prepared to discuss terms, and I ask him to come to the house. He comes. I give him my files—*all* of them, the real ones, the ones that matter—and while he sits here reading them like a man counting his Christmas, you are forty miles away behind a coffin lid."

"That's not—" His voice cracked and reassembled. "That's you handing yourself to him."

"It's me handing him paper," she said. "Paper is what he wants. Paper is what his superiors want. A woman of my age and reputation, cooperating voluntarily? He'll parade that for weeks. There will be telegrams, interviews, a careful search of every drawer in this house—all of it centered *here*. On me." She tapped the table once. "You cannot rescue anyone from inside a cell, Ivo. But I can empty one. That's the trade, and it's a good one, and I've been making trades with powerful men since before you could drive."

"You don't know he won't just—" He stopped. They both heard the end of the sentence. *He won't just keep you.*

"No," she said quietly. "I don't know that. Corven may decide I'm worth more locked up than free. His superiors may overrule him, or fail to. There is a very real chance that Thursday is the last time I sleep in this house." She watched the boy—watched the man—take that in, his hands flat on the table as if steadying it. "I need you to hear the rest anyway. Because if you turn that car around, everything I do Thursday becomes decoration."

* * *

He argued for an hour. She let him, because the arguing was really grief looking for a door, and she had learned long ago to let grief exhaust itself against facts rather than against people.

He proposed alternatives the way a drowning man proposes swimming routes. Drive her out of the country first—no; Corven would have ports watched within a day of any false step, and flight confirmed guilt and doomed the network. Burn the papers and refuse him everything—no; burned papers told Corven there were addresses worth hunting, and he'd pull every thread from Vane's description outward until the whole web came in. Go together—one car, one run, all of them at once—no; a hearse carrying three fugitives past a checkpoint was a lottery, and Edith Marlowe did not buy lotteries.

"One of us has to be the noise," she said finally, when the arguments had thinned. "And it can't be you. You're the only one who knows the Kent road, the only one Brandt trusts, and—you'll forgive me—but no one at a checkpoint remembers a chauffeur's face. That has been your gift your whole life, Ivo. Being forgettable. Spend it now. Spend it on him."

"And what do I spend on you?"

She hadn't expected the question to land the way it did. She had prepared for his fear and his guilt and even his anger, but the rawness of that—*what do I spend on you*—went through her armor the way only kindness could.

"You spend Thursday on me," she said. "Every hour you're moving, he's sitting in my parlor. That's what you spend. Don't waste it looking over your shoulder for news of me. There'll be time enough after."

He looked at his hands for a long moment. When he raised his head, something had settled in his face—not peace, nothing so kind, but the specific stillness of a decision made. She recognized it. She'd worn it herself at nineteen, telling her father she would attend Girton whether he paid or not.

"The funeral route," he said, pulling the second sheet closer. "Marleigh. Which cortege, and what time does it pass the bridge?"

"There's your soldier," she said, and meant it as praise, and saw him almost smile.

* * *

Wednesday was for logistics, and logistics, Edith had always believed, were simply love that had learned to write things down.

She spent the morning with Petch, confirming the hearse and the paperwork—his nephew's name on the transfer, a funeral in the family, condolences required, the man nearly wept with gratitude for the loan of his own vehicle. She spent the afternoon burning what could not travel: letters without strategic value but with names that deserved protection, feeding them page by page into the kitchen range while the house filled with the smell of her own history turning to smoke. Twenty years of correspondence. Some of it she read again, briefly, before the flame took it. A note from Christabel Pankhurst. A card from Corven himself, 1911, warm as toast: *To Edith, whose stubbornness is the movement's good fortune.* She burned that last, and felt nothing she was willing to name.

In the evening she packed the files she would surrender—the true ones, the dangerous ones—into two leather cases, and then, in an act she had debated all day, she removed three pages from the middle of the third folder. Addresses. Real ones. Routes that were still warm. If Corven studied the files closely enough—and he would, he was constitutionally incapable of not studying things closely—he might notice gaps where pages had been. Or he might not. It was the one gamble she permitted herself, and she permitted it because somewhere beyond his reach those three pages might still save lives, and because a sacrifice offered up complete was not sacrifice but suicide, and she had no intention of dying useful only to Cyril Corven.

At ten she found Ivo in the garage, checking the hearse's tires by lamplight with a patience that fooled neither of them. She stood in the doorway a while before speaking.

"When this is done," she said, "whatever happens Thursday—when you get to Kent, when he's safe behind that farmhouse door—you'll have done something almost no one manages in a lifetime. You'll have kept a promise to a dead man. People will call it treason. History, if it bothers to look at all, will call it the only honest thing done in England that week."

"He's not dead," Ivo said.

"No," she agreed. "Which makes it harder, not easier. Promises to the living renew themselves daily. Terrible design flaw." She came forward and touched the hearse's cold flank, absurdly, as if greeting a horse. "I've written instructions in the glovebox. The cortege leaves Marleigh churchyard at nine-forty. Stay behind it past the bridge whatever happens—mourners don't get separated from mourners."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Ivo." He looked up. Lamplight, grease on his knuckles, that face she had trusted on a hundred dark roads. "Whatever you see or hear after eleven o'clock tomorrow—none of it is yours to fix. Your job ends at the Kent line. Promise me that, too."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he might refuse. Drivers, she had learned, made the best liars and the worst promise-breakers; they understood that some directions, once given, had to be followed all the way to the end of the road.

"I promise I'll get him there," he said.

It wasn't what she'd asked. She decided, in the last hour of her last ordinary night in her own house, that it would have to do.

* * *

Thursday came up grey and windless, a sky like unpolished pewter. At eight-thirty Edith dressed with care—her good wool, the brooch her mother had left her, low heels suitable for paving stones and police steps. She arranged the two leather cases on the hall table where Corven would see them the moment he entered, evidence offered like a bouquet.

At nine o'clock she watched from the study window as Ivo led the hearse down the drive, slow and black and correct, and turned onto the lane toward Marleigh. She did not wave. Waving was for passengers, and she was not a passenger today; today she was freight, and freight traveled light.

At nine-forty, somewhere east of her, a military band would strike up and a cortege would begin to crawl toward the bridge, and a borrowed hearse with a forgettable man at the wheel would fold itself into the mourning like a thread into cloth.

At ten past eleven, she went to the telephone and asked the exchange for the number she had sworn never to dial.

"Cyril," she said, when the familiar voice came down the wire. "This is Edith Marlowe. I have documents you've been looking for, and I've decided to stop making you work for them. Come to the house. Bring your cases. And Cyril—" she smiled at the wall, though there was no one to see it, "—do wear your sympathetic face. I imagine this will be difficult for us both."

She rang off, checked the clock, and sat down to wait, hands folded, spine straight, a woman entirely alone in a house that was suddenly, enormously quiet—and forty miles away, at that same moment, the first mourners were already gathering at the Marleigh churchyard gates.
