## 14. The Funeral Road

The hearse smelled of old lilies and fresh lies.

I'd borrowed it from Hollis & Sons under a name that didn't exist, for a widow who didn't exist, conveying a body that didn't exist to a churchyard that did—St. Aldric's, near Faversham, which sat conveniently on the road to Kent. The undertaker's man had asked no questions. Grief buys discretion the way nothing else can; even in wartime, a hearse gets waved through where a lorry gets searched.

Brandt lay inside the coffin with the lid half-latched, a blanket folded under his head, and the ruined side of his face turned toward the slats of light coming through the boards. I drove. Vane rode up beside me in a mourner's black suit that hung off him like laundry, playing the bereaved nephew. He kept smoothing his lapels with both hands, over and over, as if grief were something you could press flat.

Behind us, at a respectful distance, followed a second motor—a hired Daimler carrying three actual mourners, women paid two pounds each by Edith before she went back to London to do the hardest thing any of us had ever asked of anyone. They wept on cue. One of them had a real handkerchief trimmed in lace. It was astonishing how much verisimilitude money could buy.

"Crane." Brandt's voice came through the coffin wood, muffled and strange, pitched low so it sounded like the car itself was speaking. "Pull over at the next lane. I want to ride properly for a while. A man should see the country he's likely to die in."

"You'll be seen."

"I'll be bandaged. Bandages explain everything." He worked the lid up an inch. "That's the whole trick of it, isn't it? Give people one visible wound and they stop looking for the others."

I pulled into a farm lane past a field of turnips gone to seed, and he climbed out of the coffin like a man rising from a very deep nap—stiff, careful of his balance, one hand braced against the lid. Up close, in daylight, the damage was worse than the letter had prepared me for. The left side of his face was a geography lesson: grafted skin, sunken cheek, an eye permanently half-closed as if perpetually squinting into wind. The scars ran down beneath his collar. His hands, though—his hands were a captain's hands still, clean-knuckled and steady.

He got into the front seat between me and Vane, wrapped his scarf high, tugged the mourning band down over his brow, and became merely a disfigured soldier. England had ten thousand of those now. He had made himself ordinary by force.

"There," he said. "Better. I was starting to feel like a liar in there."

* * *

We crossed into Kent as the morning wore on, and the country did what Kent does—folded itself into hops and orchards, oast houses standing like fat chess pieces against a sky the color of dishwater. Traffic thickened near every crossroads. Troop lorries. Red Cross vans. A column of recruits marching the wrong way, toward the coast, their boots making a sound like slow rain.

The first checkpoint appeared outside Lenham. Two military policemen and a sawhorse barrier, casual as a tollgate, and my heart performed a single enormous contraction and settled.

"Hearse," one of them said, without much interest, and waved us through. The other glanced at Vane's black armband, at the coffin through the hearse's little rear window, and touched his cap. Even the war had manners about funerals.

But there would be more checkpoints. Word had come through Edith's last letter before she surrendered herself: Corven had circulated a description to the Kent constabulary. Not of Brandt—of a driver. *Mid-thirties, competent manner, hired motors under false pretenses.* He had my shape now. Somewhere in a filing cabinet in London, I existed.

"You knew," Brandt said quietly, after the Lenham barrier shrank in the mirror. "On the train. That they'd come looking eventually. You knew what you were agreeing to carry."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway, because we had four hours of road ahead and nothing left to hide behind. "I knew it was a letter. I didn't know it would be you."

"No. Nobody ever knows it will be them." He looked out at the orchards. "Do you remember that day? The 4:12 from Ashford?"

I remembered everything. That was my whole condition—I remembered, because forgetting was the one luxury invisibility couldn't afford.

Three years ago. A client of Edith's had engaged the firm to drive to a house party, and I'd taken the train part of the way to collect papers, and ended up in a first-class compartment opposite a captain on leave, uniform pressed to a knife-edge, reading a German grammar of all things. He'd caught me looking at it.

"You speak it?" he'd asked.

"Enough to read addresses," I'd said, which was true, and which I'd regretted saying the moment I said it, because it told him exactly what kind of household I worked for.

And then, somewhere past Pluckley, with the Weald unrolling green and patient outside the glass, Captain Aurel Brandt had put down his book and said: *If it starts—if it truly starts—I'll need someone who can move unnoticed. You're nobody, aren't you? No offense meant.*

*None taken,* I'd said. *It's a profession.*

And he'd laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him—and told me about a farmhouse in Kent, and a family named Harker who owed his mother their lives from another war, and made me swear, hand on his cigarette case because neither of us owned a Bible, that if he ever sent word, I'd carry whatever he gave me to that farmhouse, no questions, no delays, no matter what the world said about him by then.

*No matter what the world says,* he'd repeated. *Because it will say things. If it comes to it, it will say the worst.*

* * *

"Why me?" I said now, on the funeral road, three years too late. "You had friends. Family. Men from your regiment."

"Friends get questioned. Family gets threatened." He adjusted the scarf at his throat. "A stranger has no leverage on him. You can't arrest a promise. And—" he paused, choosing, and I watched him choose, "—you read. Vane told me how they found me. 'The driver who reads.' Do you know what that means, Crane? It means everyone in your mistress's orbit is known for something loud. She speaks. Her colleagues march and shout and print pamphlets. But you—you were famous for silence. In my trade, silence is the rarest quality there is. I could have trusted a brave man and gotten betrayed by his courage. I trusted an invisible one and here I am, alive, riding to Kent in a coffin."

"That's not trust," Vane said suddenly, from the far seat. His voice cracked on it. "That's arithmetic."

"It's both," Brandt said. "The best trust usually is." He turned the good half of his face toward me. "But I'll tell you the truth of it, since we're telling truths today. There was a moment on that train. You thought I wasn't watching. I dropped my glove, and you picked it up and set it on the seat beside me instead of handing it to me—so I wouldn't have to interrupt my page. You arranged the world around a stranger's comfort and expected nothing, not even thanks. I thought: *there's a man who has decided what he is, and it costs him something daily, and he pays it anyway.* I wanted that quality on my side if the worst came."

I kept my eyes on the road. There was something unbearable in being seen that precisely—it was like being arrested.

"The worst came," I said.

"The worst came," he agreed. "And here you are, paying again."

* * *

Near Charing the traffic stopped entirely. A troop train crossing, then a broken-down lorry, and then—we learned when we crawled abreast of the crossroads—a checkpoint that was not casual at all. Sandbags. A Lewis gun mounted on a private motor, which told me whose operation this was: regular army didn't mount guns on cars for mail checks. This was intelligence work, dressed in khaki.

Corven's reach, stretching all the way into Kent.

"Through the window," Brandt said calmly. "They'll look at faces in cars. They don't look at coffins. Put me back."

There was no time to argue. He folded himself into the box with a practiced grimness that told me he'd done worse things in worse places these two years, and I pulled the lid down to within an inch of closed, and Vane began, softly, to weep.

Not pretend weeping. Vane had been holding it since dawn—the guilt of what he'd given Corven under pressure, the terror of the road, some private ledger of his own—and now it came out of him in small, wrecked sounds, a young man grieving at his own funeral, and it was the most convincing thing any of us had done all day.

The corporal at the window had a list in his hand and youth in his face and the particular exhaustion of a boy given authority he hadn't asked for. He looked at Vane's tears. He looked at me.

"Papers."

I handed them across. Undertaker's certification, transit permit, parish burial receipt—all forged by Edith's forger, a mild retired schoolmaster who did better work than most government departments. The corporal read them the way people read documents they've been told to read: front page only.

"Deceased's name?"

"Brandt," I said, and watched the word land. "Aurel Brandt. Of the Ninth Buffs."

His eyes came up. Something moved behind them—the flicker of a name remembered from a bulletin, a court-martial notice read aloud in a mess tent, *traitor* attached like a label to a jar. His gaze went to the coffin. To the lid, nearly shut. To me.

"That name's—"

"Captain Brandt died at Mons," I said, and used Corven's own voice doing it, the flat administrative certainty Corven had worn in our parlor over tea: *Names are the cheapest thing we have, Mr. Crane. We spend them daily.* "Killed in action, August 1914. The family declined a military burial. Some families do. They prefer their own ground." I let my own grief into my face, and it wasn't hard, because some of it was real. "This is his mother's parish. We're taking him home."

The corporal stood in the road with my papers in his hand and the word *traitor* and the word *mother* colliding somewhere behind his eyes, and I understood in that moment that I had become fluent in a language I used to only read. Every address I'd memorized, every letter I'd studied—they had all been lessons in what people mean underneath what they say.

He stamped the permit. "Sorry for your loss. Move along."

We moved along.

* * *

Ten miles on, at a place where the road ran between hop fields and nothing else moved but crows, Brandt had me stop again and got out of the coffin for good. He'd had enough of dying, he said. He rode upright after that, scarf high, staring at the hedgerows like a man counting them.

"He recognized the name," Vane said, still shaky. "Back there. He almost—"

"He almost," I agreed.

"But he didn't. Because of what you said about the mother." Vane rubbed his face with both hands. "How did you know to say that?"

I didn't answer. The truth was I hadn't known anything; I'd simply spoken faster than my fear, and discovered afterward what I was capable of. That discovery had a taste to it, faintly metallic, like a coin held in the mouth.

"Mr. Crane." Brandt's changed voice, gravel and whistle. "Ahead there. What do you make of that?"

Around the bend, the road dipped toward Marleigh Bridge—and the bridge was blocked. Not sandbags this time. A proper cordon: barriers across both lanes, six men at least, a staff car parked with its bonnet up as though mechanics were at work on it, and standing beside the staff car with his hands clasped behind him, hatless, patient as a heron at a riverbank—

Major Corven, watching the traffic come down toward him one vehicle at a time, and not waving a single one through.
