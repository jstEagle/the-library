## 15. Checkpoint at Marleigh Bridge

The bridge announced itself before we saw it: a smell of wet stone and engine oil, and then the barrier, striped red and white like a barber's pole, rising out of the morning fog off the river. Two lorries. A Lewis gun mounted on sandbags. Six soldiers, maybe seven, one of them already walking out into the road with his hand up, palm flat, the gesture of a man who had done this a hundred times and was bored by it.

Behind me in the box of the borrowed hearse, Brandt stopped breathing.

I had heard him stop breathing twice before on this journey—once at a level crossing outside Ashford, once when a drunk in a village pub had pressed his face to the window and squinted at the coffin windows as if expecting someone inside to squint back. Both times the moment had passed. I told myself this one would too. I told myself a great many things that morning, and lying to myself turned out to be excellent practice for what came next.

"Pull over to the left," the soldier said. "Kill the engine."

I killed the engine. The silence it left was enormous. In a hearse there is nothing between you and your own heartbeat except a coffin lid's worth of oak and brass, and behind that, a living man with half a face who had been holding himself rigid for three hours so that no board would creak.

The fog was thinning. That was the problem. Fog had been our friend all morning, laying a grey gauze over the countryside, making the world soft-edged and unaccountable. Now the sun was coming up through it the way light comes through a dirty window, and every detail of the roadblock was acquiring the cruel precision of things seen clearly for the first time. The corporal's cap badge. The clipboard under his arm. The way he looked at the vehicle first and the driver second, because paperwork was his religion and men were only its congregation.

"Funeral party?" he said.

"Yes, Corporal."

"Where from?"

"St. Aldhelm's, Marlowe parish." I named the church the way you set down a heavy object—carefully, with both hands, showing it was nothing. "We're bound for the family plot at Wethering."

He wrote something down. He had the particular slowness of a man who has been given authority recently and intends to enjoy every minute before someone takes it back. Young. Twenty, twenty-one. Chapped hands. A wedding ring too big for him, worn on a chain because he'd swelled since he enlisted. His name tape read PEMBERTON. I catalogued all of this without deciding to, the way I had learned to catalogue everything—addresses, routes, faces—the habit of a man whose survival had always depended on being nobody in particular and noticing everybody else.

"Coffin?"

"In the back."

"I'll want to see it."

And there it was. The whole morning tilted.

* * *

Here is what I knew about lies by then, three days into the longest drive of my life: a lie is not a sentence. It is an architecture. You cannot build it on the spot; you can only live inside the one you built beforehand, or discover, horribly, that you are standing in open ground.

Edith had taught me the architecture without meaning to. Watch her at a suffrage meeting years ago, some heckler shouting about unnatural women, and Edith never defending—only redirecting, agreeing with part of what the man said, folding his own anger into her argument like an egg into flour until you couldn't tell where his outrage ended and her case began. *Never fight a man on your ground,* she used to say. *Fight him on his, and let him hear his own voice saying yours.*

Corven had taught me the rest. Chapter six of my education, tea in the front room, that lean careful face asking questions shaped like courtesies. Corven lied the way other men breathed, and I had sat there watching him do it, serving tea, terrified—and somewhere in the terror I had studied him the way a apprentice studies a master. What I remembered now, with Pemberton's hand already reaching for the hearse door, was something Corven had said that afternoon, almost idly, peering into his cup:

"You know what gives a man away, Crane? It's never the documents. Documents are forged by clever people. It's the pause. The half-second where a man consults his story instead of his life. Truth is instant. Falsehood rehearses."

So when Pemberton said, "Papers," I did not reach for them. I sighed. I let my shoulders drop with the weary theatrical patience of every driver who has ever been delayed by officialdom, and I dug out the funeral director's packet while saying, "You'll want the burial certificate as well, and the parish letter—it took me an hour at St. Aldhelm's to get the vicar to sign it, and he wasn't pleased about the hour either."

Truth is instant. So I gave him truth, or the shape of it—a dead woman's name, a real vicar's signature, a real village—and wrapped the falsehood inside it like a pill inside meat.

He took the papers. He read them. And I watched his face do exactly what I had known it would do, because the papers *were* wrong—I had bought them from a frightened undertaker in a market town two days earlier for more money than I'd ever held at once, and the undertaker's stamp was from the wrong county, and the date on the transit permit had been altered in ink that didn't quite match.

Pemberton's eyes came up. "This stamp's a Kentish firm."

"Yes."

"This permit was issued at—" he checked, wanting me to sweat— "Whitstable. And you're coming from the west."

"The deceased's family," I said, "lived in Whitstable until March. Then the house was requisitioned. They moved to Marlowe parish to stay with cousins. The firm handled arrangements from their old address because the cousin in Marlowe rang them direct—he'd used them for his mother, apparently. Family loyalty. You know how it is when someone dies." I shrugged. "Everything about a funeral is the wrong county if you look hard enough, Corporal. Half of England's been moved twice since August. My own garage was conscripted, my lodger's called up, and the lady I drove for eleven years was arrested last month for handing out leaflets. If the paperwork of grief doesn't match the map, I'd say the map's at fault."

It was a good speech. Too good, maybe. Pemberton blinked at me, and for a moment I thought I'd overshot—that a boy like him, who had probably never argued with anyone he couldn't salute, would find fluency itself suspicious.

Then he almost smiled. Because I had said the magic thing. I had complained, mildly and plausibly, about the war, and every man at that roadblock had a list of identical grievances, and complaint is a password that opens doors across the entire British Army.

"All right," he said. "Still need to see the coffin. Regulations."

"Regulations," I agreed, and got down from the cab, and opened the back.

* * *

Brandt lay inside the coffin with the lid propped on two blocks of wood, which had been Vane's idea and a good one—the lid could be lifted without fumbling, and fumbling kills faster than suspicion does. Brandt's ruined side faced away from the door. His good eye found mine above the corporal's shoulder, and in it there was no fear at all, only a kind of terrible attention, the look of a man measuring the distance between the present moment and its possible endings.

Bandages showed at his neck and wrists. We had dressed him as we might dress any corpse badly preserved by a long delay—morticians' cotton, sheeting, camphor, the smell of which I had doused him in so thoroughly at dawn that my own coat still reeked of it. Camphor explains much. Camphor keeps questions at a distance the way incense clears a room.

"Deceased died of wounds?" Pemberton asked, peering in with the squeamish reverence of the young.

"Septicaemia," I said. "Three weeks after he was wounded. The family wanted him home." I lowered my voice, as men do around coffins. "He was at a clearing station near Boulogne. There was—some difficulty getting him shipped. Hence the state of the paperwork. Nothing about that journey made sense to anyone, least of all to him."

That was true. Every word of it was true, only of a different man than the one in the box, and I felt the strange vertigo of the accomplished liar: that the best lies are not inventions but transpositions. Take a truth, move it twelve inches sideways, and it will bear your weight anywhere.

Pemberton straightened up. He glanced at the bandages, at the camphor-soaked sheeting, at the waxen pallor that fear and stillness lend better than any mortician's craft. His nose wrinkled against the smell. Sympathy moved across his young face like weather.

"Bad business," he said. "My mate died at Ypres in October. Took nine days to come home. Nine days in August heat." He looked away from the coffin. "His mother never forgave the army for the condition of him. Never."

"No," I said quietly. "They don't."

And there was the bitter taste, arriving right on schedule, right on top of the triumph: because Pemberton's mate had existed, had died, had come home in a box that broke his mother's heart—and I was using that grief, this boy's real grief, as a lever to lift my own falsehood over the barrier. I was becoming fluent in the language of other people's losses. It was, I realized, standing in the fog with my hand on a coffin lid, precisely the fluency Corven had. The major had looked at Edith's cause for years and seen only material—useful outrage, useful networks, useful people. And here was I, three days into crime, already spending a dead soldier's mother like currency.

Survival by becoming a better liar. Nobody warns you that the price is paid in instalments, and that each instalment feels, while you're paying it, like competence.

"Right," Pemberton said, stepping back. "I'll just check the manifest against the vehicle registration—"

"Sergeant!" One of the soldiers by the lorries, holding up a bundle of letters. "Sarge says bring the drivers over, they're checking post again!"

"It'll take two minutes," Pemberton said to me, apologetic now, one working man to another. "New orders. Everything's being read going south. Someone's been writing to the enemy, if you can believe it. Civilians." He shook his head with genuine bewilderment, and I thought of Edith, of the cell they would have put her in, of the word *traitor* doing its work in the mouths of boys like this one who meant no harm by it and could not be argued with because the harm was done upstream of them.

"Take your time," I said.

While he bent over the registration book, I stood beside the hearse and let myself think the unthinkable: that if this went wrong—if Pemberton lifted the lid fully, if the sergeant decided to search the coffin for whatever civilians smuggle southward these days—then Brandt would act. I knew it without asking. There was a revolver under the sheeting, taken from the same frightened undertaker who sold me the papers, and Brandt had made me understand, in his ruined whisper, at the mill: *If it fails, you run and I settle it. You're the one carrying the promise. I'm the one it was carried for. Those aren't equal weights, Crane.* And I had said nothing, because arguing with Aurel Brandt about weights was like arguing with the tide, and because some cowardly arithmetic in me had agreed.

Two minutes. Pemberton licked his thumb, turned a page.

"Marlowe parish," he muttered. "Know a house out that way. Big place, red brick. Used to be meetings there—women in sashes, before the war. My aunt went once. Father called it treason." He looked up, suddenly chatty, the barrier of rank dissolved by boredom and shared grievance. "You know it?"

I felt the road tilt beneath me. "I drove for a household in that parish," I said. Carefully. Instantly. "Years ago."

"Lady there got arrested, I heard. Sedition." He said the word with relish and no understanding, a coin he'd been handed and accepted at face value. "Major came down from London personally to take her in, my sergeant says. Intelligence fellow. Very keen."

"Very keen," I agreed.

Keen enough, I thought, to let a hearse through a roadblock forty miles back and put a motorcycle dispatch rider on its tail. Keen enough to trade a baroness for the bigger fish he was certain swam behind her. The thought arrived fully formed and cold, the way worst thoughts do, and I sent it away, because there was nothing to be done about it on a bridge with a Lewis gun on the sandbags, and a man can carry only one dread at a time.

"There you are." Pemberton shut the book. Handed back the papers. Stood aside. "Sorry for the delay. Give my regards to Wethering—pretty country. Get her home decent, eh? She's earned it."

"She has," I said, which was true of two women at once, and only one of them was in the box.

I climbed into the cab. My hand found the starter. The engine caught, coughed, settled. Behind us the barrier arm was already lifting for the next vehicle, a milk lorry whose driver wore the expression of a man approaching purgatory.

"Crane," Brandt said, very low, as the bridge unrolled behind us. His ruined voice, all gravel and whistle, had a way of making even comfort sound like a verdict. "Your hands are shaking."

"They've been shaking since Ashford."

"Not like this."

I looked in the mirror. Pemberton stood in the road watching us go, hand raised—not flagging us, just shielding his eyes from the sun coming through the fog, an ordinary boy in an ordinary morning. In the far distance, small and black against the brightening east, something moved along the lane that ran parallel to the road. I took it for a crow at first. Crows keep pace with funerals; it seemed fitting.

It kept pace for four miles. Then the fog closed, and I lost it, and told myself crow, crow, crow, all the way to the turning for Wethering, where we would leave the hearse in a spinney and finish the journey on foot with a suitcase, because a coffin draws eyes even when the paperwork is perfect.

Brandt slept, or pretended to. I drove. And the bitter aftertaste of the bridge stayed with me longer than the relief did, because I understood now what Corven had understood years before me, standing in Edith's front room with his tea: that lying well is not a shield you put on. It is a door that, once you have walked through it, will not close behind you. On the far side of it, everyone becomes material. Even the ones you love.

Even the ones waiting at home, alone, keeping the machine occupied so that a hearse could pass a bridge—which is why, when we finally stopped at noon and I asked at a post office for telegraph forms under a false name, the message waiting for collection was one line, unsigned, in handwriting I knew better than my own:

*They have come for the papers. Do not turn back.*

I turned back.
