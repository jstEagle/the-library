## 16. Edith Taken

The hearse smelled of lilies and old varnish, and I could not stop smelling it.

We had passed Marleigh Bridge an hour before, and the triumph of that still sat in my chest like swallowed coal—warm, then not warm at all. The young corporal had waved us through with his own superior's words handed back to him, and for eleven miles I had let myself believe that the road was ours, that Corven's machinery had ground past us in the wrong direction, that Edith was at home this minute pouring tea over the ruins of her afternoon and laughing at how easily men were managed.

I should have known better. I had spent three years learning how easily *I* was managed by hope.

Brandt slept against the coffin rack, or pretended to. His ruined side faced the wall of the hearse, the way he always turned it, and his breathing had the deliberate evenness of a man who had learned sleep as a discipline rather than a surrender. In the dim light through the curtained rear window his good hand lay open on his knee, palm up, as if waiting to receive something. A letter. A verdict. Whatever came next.

The funeral cortege we had joined outside Ashford had peeled away at the crossroads, mourners peeling off toward their churchyard like leaves down a gutter, until we were alone on the coast road with our false paperwork and our borrowed dead. The driver's coat fit me badly. Everything fit me badly today.

At Rye I stopped for petrol and bought a newspaper because buying nothing would have been stranger than buying something.

I did not read it at the pump. I read it leaning against the hearse's flank, in full view, a man killing five minutes, and the headline went through me the way cold water goes through a coat—not all at once, but finding every seam.

*PACIFIST LEADER HELD UNDER DEFENCE OF THE REALM ACT.*

*Mrs. Edith Marlowe, whose anti-war correspondence has long been a subject of official concern, was taken into custody yesterday evening at her residence in ———. A spokesman declined to comment on whether further arrests are expected.*

Yesterday evening. While I had been talking my way past a corporal with a steady voice and a liar's heart, two men had been walking up Edith Marlowe's front path with a warrant folded in a pocket, and she had opened the door to them herself, because she was the sort of woman who opened doors.

The paper trembled. I made it stop trembling, because a man reading a newspaper on a forecourt does not tremble, and I had become, in the last twenty-four hours, very good at being what I was not.

"Petrol's in," the attendant said.

"Thank you." My voice came out level. That frightened me more than the headline had—the ease of it, the fluency. Chapter by chapter I had been discovering what I could do, and every discovery had cost someone something, and here was another entry in the ledger: *the driver can hear terrible news and sound like a man who has read about weather.*

I folded the paper, got back into the hearse, and drove.

* * *

I told Brandt four miles later, when the road straightened enough that my hands could be trusted to be idle.

He listened without moving. When I finished he looked out at the marshes sliding past, grey-green under a sky the colour of dishwater, and said, "She knew."

"She couldn't have known."

"She planned it, Ivo. You told me the plan yourself. She walks into Corven's office with her papers held out like a lamb—" He stopped himself, which was unlike him; Brandt did not usually bother sparing anyone's feelings, least of all mine. "What I mean is, she chose this. Don't build her a martyrdom she didn't order."

"She thought she'd buy us six hours. Maybe twelve. She thought they'd hold her, question her, release her with a warning because there'd be nothing to charge her with." I heard my own voice climbing and brought it down. "There's always something to charge her with now. There has been for months. She gave herself to a man who doesn't trade."

"No," Brandt said quietly. "She gave herself to a man who follows."

And that was the sentence that dropped the floor out from under everything, because I had been carrying the checkpoint at Marleigh Bridge like a trophy, turning it over, admiring it—and now I turned it over again and saw the other face of it.

Corven had let us through.

Not failed. Not fooled. *Let.* A man like Corven did not lose a hearse at a country bridge because a chauffeur quoted his own memoranda back at him. He lost it because losing it served him. Because a hearse bound for the coast with a dead traitor inside was worth more moving than stopped—stopped, it was a charge he'd have to prove; moving, it was a thread he could follow all the way to whatever loom it was woven on. And at the end of the thread, if he pulled hard enough, sat the one person whose testimony could hang the whole network: not Brandt, whom nobody officially believed alive, but Edith Marlowe, whose letters Corven had wanted for two years.

He hadn't needed to catch us at the bridge. He had needed only to know where we were going. And then he had gone and taken the woman who could tell him—or confirm, under pressure, everything he already suspected.

"We turn around," I said. "We go back. We can be in London by midnight—"

"And do what?" Brandt's voice stayed gentle, which was how I knew he understood exactly how much it cost me. "Stand outside her door? You'd drive straight into the same net she stepped into on purpose. She built that net around herself so you wouldn't be in it. Turning back is the one thing guaranteed to make her sacrifice worthless."

"You don't get to lecture me about worth. You're the reason she's in a cell."

"Yes." No flinch. He had a soldier's relationship with guilt—it lived in the house with him, ate at his table, and he did not pretend otherwise. "And you're the reason I'm not in a grave with a traitor's stone on it, and she's the reason you're both those things, and somewhere Major Corven is the reason for all of it, and blaming each other in a borrowed hearse will not move one of us one mile closer to fixing any of it." He shifted, wincing, his bad side against the wood. "Drive. Get me to Kent. Keep the promise. Then we decide what we owe her—and we do owe her, Ivo, we owe her more than either of us can count—but we decide it with our heads, not with your foot on the accelerator pointing the wrong way."

I drove. What else does a driver do.

* * *

We put the hearse up at a livery stable outside Winchelsea, telling the stableman a story about a family plot and a delayed burial that he accepted with the indifference of a man paid in cash. From there we walked the last two miles, two men in dark coats on a bright cold morning, one of them keeping his scarred half-face to the hedgerows. The marshes opened around us, flat and glittering and empty, and I kept thinking: *this is what invisibility looks like from the inside. It looks like landscape. It looks like nowhere.*

The farmhouse stood where Brandt's letter had promised—a low grey building with a tiled roof, elms behind it, a lane running down from the ridge road. Smoke should have been rising from its chimney. It was not.

I noticed that first, and then I noticed the rest: no dog barking, though a dog kennel stood by the gate with its chain run through it; the gate itself sagging open on a broken latch that weather, not traffic, had broken; a cart track grown with weeds to the height of a wheel hub. The farm had been empty since summer at least. Since before the war, perhaps. Since before the promise, maybe even—though I refused to finish that thought—since before the train compartment, the stranger's hand on my sleeve, the oath taken between two stations.

Brandt stood at the gate a long time. He didn't touch it.

"The Harlows," he said finally. "Eli Harlow wrote to me himself, in 'thirteen. Said the room above the kitchen would keep. Said I'd be family if it came to it." His thumb moved along the top rail of the gate, back and forth, a small mechanical motion, a man checking that the world still had texture. "His handwriting was worse than mine."

"There'll be a neighbour," I said. "Someone who knows. Someone who took the letter on when the family left—"

"Ivo." He turned his good eye on me. "Look at the lane. Nobody's taken anything on. This promise died while I was being reported dead, and nobody thought to send word to a prisoner because prisoners don't get post." He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "I escaped a camp in Germany, crossed half a continent, and the door I broke myself open to reach was locked from the other side by time."

I had delivered letters for three years. I knew what an undeliverable letter felt like in the hand—heavier than paper, heavier than lead sometimes. Now I was standing inside one.

But Brandt only stood there another moment, and then he squared himself the way soldiers square themselves, some internal parade-ground command issued and obeyed, and said: "The barn, then. Or the mill you mentioned. Somewhere off the road. We reassess tonight."

"We reassess *after* we talk about Edith."

"After," he agreed, and I understood that he meant to spend the whole day making sure I didn't do anything stupid, and that I would let him, and that neither of us would say so.

* * *

It was Vane who found us, near dusk, which was its own kind of alarm bell—I had left no address, given no route, told no one where the hearse lay up. Yet there he was at the edge of the yard as the light went copper, capless, thinner than when I'd first seen him at the gate in London, with the look of a man who had travelled hard and been handled harder.

"They released me Tuesday," he said, before I could ask. "No charges. They just—finished with me. Like a form filled in twice." He glanced at Brandt, at the ruined side of his face, and had the grace not to stare, though his eyes went there and came back. "I've been looking for you two days. I asked at the mill. There was chalk on the doorframe—your mark, sir, from the meeting. I gambled you'd come back this way eventually."

"How did you find the hearse?" I asked.

A pause. Not a guilty pause exactly. A careful one.

"I followed the cortege out of Ashford," he said. "I was in it. Third car back. I've been in it since before the bridge."

The marsh wind moved through the elms and made a sound like a long exhale.

"In it," I repeated.

"I hired on as a mourner. There's a man at the railway hotel who hires mourners for funerals that come up short—two shillings and a black armband. I thought—if you were moving Brandt, you'd move him that way, it's the only way left that works. So I got myself hired onto the first cortege heading for the coast and rode along and watched." He spread his hands. "I watched you talk your way through Marleigh Bridge, sir. It was—well done. Truly."

"And after the bridge?"

Vane's face did something complicated. "After the bridge, a staff car came up the line and stopped at the checkpoint. An officer got out. Tall, grey at the temples, gloves. He spoke to the corporal for perhaps a minute. Then he got back in the car and the car turned around and went back the way it came." Vane swallowed. "He never looked at the hearse. Not once. He walked past it close enough to touch the paint, and he never looked at it, and I've been a soldier long enough to know what that means. That's a man confirming what he already knows. That's a man letting the fish run so he can see which way the river goes."

Brandt said, softly, "Corven."

"He had a newspaper under his arm," Vane said. "Folded to the front page. I saw the headline as he passed. Something about—" He stopped, and looked at me, and I saw that he knew, that he had carried the knowledge across two counties looking for us, and that delivering it was the hardest thing he'd done since the war broke him. "About Mrs. Marlowe. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I read it and I thought, they've taken her *because* the hearse got through, and I thought, they'll blame the driver, and I had to find you before you found out some other way and did something—"

"Reckless," Brandt supplied.

"Human," Vane said.

I stood in the failing light with the elms ticking overhead and felt the two truths grind against each other like millstones. Gratitude: she had done it for us, had walked into Corven's office with her chin up and her papers arranged, had spent herself like coin so that a hearse full of ghosts could roll unremarked to the sea. Guilt: she had been able to spend herself because I had spent her—my lies at the tea table, my absences, my promise to a dead man, all of it funnelled into that single moment on her front step when the warrant came out of the pocket and she said, I imagine, something perfectly composed, something with a little irony in it, because that was who she was even at the end of things.

Both true. Both mine. The floor was gone and there was no floor coming back.

"Then we know where the river goes," I said, and heard my own voice come out flat and strange, the voice of a man reading a headline on a forecourt. "Corven follows the hearse. The hearse ends here. Which means by tomorrow, maybe tonight, this farmhouse, this lane, this parish fills up with men asking questions about strangers." I looked at Brandt. "Kent is burned. The promise is kept—we're here, we stood at the gate, and there's no one to shelter you. What's left is getting you out from under what's coming, and then getting her out from under Corven."

"That's not a plan, Ivo, that's a wish wearing a plan's clothes."

"Then help me dress it."

For a moment none of us moved. Then Vane reached into his coat and brought out a folded paper, creased and soft with handling, and held it out to me.

"There's one more thing," he said. "The officer at the checkpoint—the tall one. After his car turned round, the corporal threw something in the brazier. It didn't burn. I went back for it after the cortege passed." His hand was not quite steady. "It's a telegram, sir. Routing slip, military police, Rye district. Dated tomorrow."

"Dated—" I took it. The paper was cheap and the type was smudged, but the line was clear enough, and I read it aloud because Brandt's eye was not what it had been and because saying it made it somehow survivable:

*"Subject vehicle observed southbound. Do NOT intercept. Surveillance only. Full authority transferred to this office pending subject's arrival at destination. — C."*

Full authority. Pending arrival. Destination.

Corven wasn't chasing us anymore. He was *escorting* us—patient as weather, letting us carry his quarry to wherever we thought was safe, gathering the whole knot of us—Brandt, the network, the addresses in my head, and Edith rotting in a cell as leverage—all to be closed on at once, in one place, at a time of his choosing.

The marshes had gone violet. Somewhere behind the elms a night bird started up, indifferent, insistent.

"He knows we're here," Brandt said. "Or he will by morning."

"Then we're gone before morning," I said, folding the telegram into my coat, into the place where everything I had ever chosen to carry lived. "All three of us. And Vane—you said there's a man at the railway hotel who hires mourners."

Vane blinked. "Sir?"

"How many funerals pass through Rye in a week?"

Understanding came up his face slowly, like dawn over water. "More than you'd think," he said. "This year, more than anyone would think."
