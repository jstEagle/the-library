## 17. The Farmhouse in Kent

The hearse smelled of lilies and old varnish, and by the second hour of driving both had become unbearable in the same way—thick, sweet, funereal, a smell that insisted on grief whether or not anyone in the vehicle was mourning. Brandt sat behind me with the coffin lid propped against the seat beside him like a third passenger. We had taken nothing out of it. The plan had been to bury it empty at St. Dunstan's and let the parish record a name that belonged to no one, which struck me, somewhere past Tenterden, as the truest funeral either of us would ever attend.

"Left here," Brandt said. His voice was gravel dragged through water—the voice of a man whose throat had been near a fire, or near something worse; he had never explained and I had never asked. "Then follow the lane until the elms stop."

"The elms stop," I repeated.

"You'll know it when you see it. The Dutch took the big ones in '87. They never grew back right."

I turned where he told me. The lane narrowed to two ruts stitched with grass, hedges high enough to scrape the hearse's paint on both sides at once. The morning was grey and rinsed clean from overnight rain, the kind of English light that made everything look like evidence. Behind us the road we'd come by stayed empty. Ahead of us it stayed empty too. After Marleigh Bridge I had stopped trusting empty roads—they were not the same thing as safe roads—but there was nothing to be done about emptiness except drive through it faster.

Brandt had been quiet most of the way. Not the quiet of a man sleeping and not the quiet of a man afraid. It was the quiet of someone rehearsing. I watched him in the mirror whenever the lane straightened: the good half of his face turned to the window, watching hop fields give way to orchards give way to pasture, and the ruined half angled carefully toward the coffin lid, as if even a box of sawdust shouldn't have to see it. He had shaved that morning using my razor, holding the glass so only the left side showed, and I had stood in the doorway of the hotel room realizing I was watching a man say goodbye to a face. He caught me looking once and said, without turning, "It grew back crooked. That's all. Everything grows back crooked."

I hadn't known what to answer, so I'd said nothing, which by then was its own language between us.

"There." His hand came up and touched the glass. "The gate."

* * *

The gate was open. That was the first wrong thing.

I stopped the hearse where the ruts widened into a small yard, engine ticking as it cooled, and looked at what Brandt had carried across three years and one war inside his head: a brick farmhouse, low and long, slate roof furred with moss, a barn beyond it with its door standing wide to weather. An apple tree leaned over the yard wall, heavy with unripe fruit no one had picked. A dog should have been barking. There are farmhouses where the absence of a dog is nothing, but this was not shaped like one of those. The silence here had furniture in it. Someone had arranged it.

"It's early yet," I said. "They might be at church."

"It's Thursday." Brandt opened his door before I could stop him. He got out slowly, one hand braced on the doorframe, and stood in the yard in his borrowed black suit—the mourner's suit, the mourner's posture—and I understood suddenly that he had imagined this moment so many times that the reality of it couldn't find anywhere to set itself down. He looked at the house the way a man looks at an address from a letter he has read a hundred times in a prison camp, in the dark, by feel.

"Aurel," I said quietly.

"I'm all right." He wasn't, but there was no version of this where he would be, so we walked to the door together.

The door was unlocked. The kitchen beyond it was swept, the table wiped, four chairs pushed in neatly under it as if the family had stepped out to the lane and meant to come back directly. On the dresser, plates. On the hearth, cold ash raked flat. And on the table, weighted under the salt cellar because there was no one left to hold it down, a note in a hurried farmer's hand.

Brandt read it. I watched him read it, and I watched the reading do what three years of war had not managed: it emptied him. His hand went out and flattened on the table as though the floor had tilted.

"They went in June," he said. His changed voice made even plain words sound broken open. "The notice—the traitor's notice. It ran in the county papers. *Any person harbouring or aiding.* Mrs. Aldercott read it at market." He looked up, and the good eye found mine. "She read it at market, Crane. My name, on a board, in the village where I bought my boots."

I didn't touch the note myself. Some documents you memorize and some you leave where they lie, and I had learned the difference. But Brandt read it aloud anyway, twice, the way men repeat things to make them true or to make them end, and the substance of it was simple: gone to her sister's near Ashford, took the children, took nothing else, God forgive us, we could not stay.

"He promised me the loft," Brandt said. "Aldercott. Three years ago, on the platform at Ashford, shaking my hand like we'd known each other always. He said, *Whatever happens, there's a loft.*" His thumb moved along the edge of the table, back and forth, back and forth. "He kept the loft. Look—you can see where he left the ladder. He kept his promise too. He just couldn't keep it and his children."

"Then we go to Ashford," I said. "Her sister's. It's twenty miles."

"No."

"Aurel—"

"*No.*" It came out cracked, and he pressed his hand harder on the table, and for a moment I thought the wood would take the shape of his fingers permanently. Then he breathed, and steadied, and said in something closer to his old register: "You don't put a woman who fled her own village in front of that face, Crane. You've driven me two days. You've earned the right to look at it without flinching, and I thank you for the discipline it cost you. But she read my name off a board. If she opens that door and sees me, she won't see the man who shook her husband's hand. She'll see the notice. She'll see the soldiers it brings." He shook his head slowly. "This was the address. This was the whole of it. The promise was *this gate*, and the gate is empty."

I looked around the kitchen again—at the chairs pushed in, the plates waiting, the ash raked flat by hands that expected to return or hoped to be seen returning—and I felt the promise settle through me like silt in still water. Kept. Technically, immaculately kept. I had carried him here. Every mile accounted for. And it was worth precisely nothing, because promises are not kept at gates; they are kept at tables, among people, and the people were in Ashford or nowhere or dead of the influenza for all any of us knew.

"Then you stay somewhere tonight," I said, "and tomorrow—"

The dog barked after all. Not on the property. Down the lane, behind the hedge—a farm dog doing its duty, announcing us, and then a man's voice calling it quiet, and then footsteps on the rutted ground, unhurried, the walk of a man coming to inspect something rather than welcome it.

Brandt moved first. He was through the kitchen's back door and flat against the wall in the shadow of the jamb before I had finished turning, and I had time to register two things: that a half-ruined man moved like that because he had learned to in places where moving slowly meant dying, and that I was alone in a dead man's kitchen wearing a borrowed suit beside a borrowed coffin, about to lie again.

The man came around the hedge. Sixty or older, corduroys, a stick for walking that he did not lean on, and eyes that went from me to the hearse to the open farmhouse door in one flat sweep, cataloguing.

"Morning," he said, in the tone of a man for whom everything that morning had been a warning already.

"Morning." I gave him the funeral. It was the best card I held and I played it immediately, gesturing at the hearse. "We're expected at St. Dunstan's. We took the lane wrong."

"St. Dunstan's is back through Tenterden."

"So we're told now." I smiled the small apologetic smile of a driver whose employer gives bad directions, and nodded toward the house. "We stopped for shade. Door was open."

"That's the Aldercott place." He said it the way you'd identify a corpse. "They're gone since June."

"Gone?"

"Went away sudden." His eyes came back to me and stayed. He was looking at my collar, my hands, my shoes, and I knew exactly what he was doing because I had done it to other men: measuring whether I was worth a walk to the constable. "You're the second lot to come up this lane asking after them since the summer."

Something in my chest went very cold and very still. "The first lot?"

"Two gentlemen. Civil enough. London coats." He shifted the stick to his other hand, unhurried. "Said they were solicitors settling the estate. Only Aldercott isn't dead, is he, so far as anyone knows, so I don't know what estate there'd be to settle." He let that sit in the yard air between us. "And now a hearse comes to their gate with the blinds drawn, and one driver, and nobody gets out but you."

Behind the wall, in the shadow of the jamb, I could feel rather than see Brandt not breathing.

"We'll be on our way," I said. "I'm sorry for the disturbance."

"Mm." He didn't move. The dog had come up the lane behind him and sat at his heel, watching me with the professional interest of an animal that barks at whatever its master is suspicious of. "You'll pardon an old man's mouth, but there's a notice been in the paper all summer. About a captain. Traitor's name, reward mentioned, and instructions for anyone as sees anything." His eyes did not blink. "Man comes to a hidden farmhouse in a black carriage, alone, on a false errand—I'd be failing my neighbour, whoever's paying, if I didn't take the number off that vehicle."

I want to lie down here and say my mind worked quickly. It didn't. It worked simply, the way it had learned to work over two years of Edith's correspondence: find what the man wants and stand in front of it. He wanted to be neither fool nor accomplice. So I gave him neither.

"Take the number," I said. "Take two numbers. And then ask yourself, sir, why the military would send a hearse with military plates—" and I recited, evenly, the registration that had opened every checkpoint from Rye to Tenterden, the one Vane had copied from the quartermaster's book with his careful soldier's hand, "—to a farmyard, if not because they were already looking here? You said yourself gentlemen in London coats came asking. Do you imagine they were solicitors?" I let my voice drop, confiding, regretful. "There's a man they want. We believe he passed through this valley in July. The family was questioned and moved for their own protection. I'm not at liberty to say more, but if you write to the provost office at Ashford and mention today's visit, they'll confirm it—and they will also ask why you delayed a serving driver at a post of observation."

It was magnificent nonsense. Every word of it was nonsense. And I delivered it with the flat boredom of a man who has said the same thing eleven times this week to slow-witted civilians, which is, I have since decided, the entire secret of lying: not invention, but fatigue. The old man looked at me a long moment. The dog looked at me. Somewhere behind me a floorboard in the farmhouse settled with a small dry sound that I prayed corduroy and country ears would file under weather.

"Provost office at Ashford," the old man repeated.

"With the date," I said. "They'll know the file."

He touched his cap—not a salute, nothing so committed—and turned back up the lane, the dog wheeling at his heel, and I stood in the yard until the footsteps faded and the gate hinge creaked and the lane was empty again, genuinely empty this time, and even that felt like a trick.

* * *

Brandt didn't come out until I whistled softly, twice, the signal we'd agreed on somewhere outside Rye and never needed until now. He emerged from the back of the kitchen pale under his good cheek, and he had heard enough—he must have heard all of it—to skip past the old man entirely.

"London coats," he said. "Asking after the family. In July."

"Corven," I said. "Or Corven's people. He's been ahead of us the whole road, Aurel. He didn't follow the hearse out of Rye. He followed the idea of it. He knew where the promise pointed before we did." I heard my own voice saying it and hated how certain it sounded, and how certainly true. "The farmhouse was never a refuge. It was a pin in his map."

Brandt walked past me then, out into the yard, and stood under the apple tree with its hard green fruit, and I let him stand. There are arguments you can only have with a man after he has finished arguing with himself. Above us the sky was deciding between more rain and less. The hearse ticked. Somewhere a church bell rang the quarter hour in a village we could not see.

"She's arrested because of me," he said finally, to the tree, to the apples. "Edith Marlowe. I never met the woman and she's in a cell in London because I wrote a letter to a driver instead of burning it like a sensible ghost." His hand rose halfway toward the ruined side of his face—an old gesture, I think, from before, when it would have been a rub of the jaw, a thought-gesture—and fell again. "You kept your word, Crane. Here I am. Delivered to an empty loft in a denounced valley with a neighbour writing down our number." He turned, and the good eye was wet and furious. "So tell me what your promise was actually for. Tell me what it *buys*, standing here."

"Shelter," I said. "That's all I ever promised. Shelter. And shelter isn't a building, apparently, because I've spent the last two days learning that. It's people who choose to be in the room with you." I heard it come out of me and recognized it as something Edith would say, and let it stand anyway, because she would have said it better and meant it harder. "Your friend Aldercott kept the ladder and lost his nerve, and I don't condemn him for it—there's his children to weigh. But there is a woman in a cell who weighed her own safety last Tuesday and found it lighter than yours. You want to hide in Kent while she pays for you. You can't. I've watched you try to sit still for two days and you can't."

"You're saying go back."

"I'm saying you already decided in the yard before the old man ever came up the lane, and you want me to say it out loud so it's a plan and not a madness." I looked at him steadily. "So. Out loud: we go back. Tonight, before the old man's letter reaches any provost office worth the name. Vane knows which cells they'd hold a woman like Edith in—civil, not military, they'll want her network before her neck. And I know a man who knows a man at the Home Office, which is to say I know a lever, and levers are what she taught me to look for."

Brandt laughed. It was a terrible sound, rusty and half-choked, and possibly the first true laugh I had heard from him, and it broke something loose in my chest that had been set since the mill.

"Three years I carried that address," he said. "Every march, every hut, every month in that camp. *There's a loft in Kent.* It was the whole country to me—one square foot of it. And it's empty." He picked one hard apple off the low branch and turned it in his fingers, weighing a thing that had no weight. "All right, Crane. Turn the hearse around. We'll deliver this coffin back the way it came and bury our empty man another day." He dropped the apple and stepped toward the car, and paused with his hand on the door. "But understand what you're driving toward. If Corven has her, and we go at him, we're not rescuing anyone. We're volunteering."

"I know," I said, and got in, and turned us south toward London and whatever Major Corven had arranged to be standing in the road when we arrived—because as I pulled out onto the main road, past the first milestone, a motorcar that had been parked beneath the elms since before we reached the gate started its engine and fell in behind us at a distance too exact to be accidental, and the man at its wheel wore a London coat.
