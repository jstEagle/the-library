## 19. The Fake Ledger

Vane came back at half past two in the morning, and I knew he'd come because I'd sent for him, and I still opened the door with my heart going like a man answering a knock he'd been dreading for years.

He stood in the doorway of the Ashford rooming house with his hat in his hands and rain coming off his shoulders in a fine mist. He looked worse than when I'd first seen him at Edith's gate — thinner, the shell-shock tremor back in his left hand so that the hat shivered like something alive. He'd given Corven a description of me under questioning. He'd told me so himself, three days ago, standing in this same doorway, and I had wanted to hate him and found I couldn't, because there was nothing in him to hate. Only a man whose nerves had been burned down to the wiring.

"You came alone," I said.

"Like you asked." He stepped inside and stood dripping on the linoleum, not sitting until I told him twice. "I keep thinking about it. What I did."

"I know. You've apologized eleven times."

"It doesn't wear out." He set the hat on his knee and looked around the room: the bed, the basin, the table by the window where I'd laid out my materials like a surgeon. Ink, three pens, a bottle of blotting sand, a ream of thin paper bought from four different stationers so no one purchase would be remembered. "Is that it, then? The ledger?"

"That's paper. The ledger has to exist here first." I tapped my temple. "Four years of addresses. Every route Edith ever ran, every name I memorized against the day the war made every letter a crime."

"And now you're going to give them to Corven."

"Some of them," I said. "The wrong ones."

* * *

Here is what I had learned about Major Corven, from watching him over teacups and then from studying him the way you study a lock before picking it: he was not a liar. That was the strange thing. He was a believer. He believed in the machinery — files, forms, cross-references, the great filing cabinet of England into which every suspicious person should be sorted and stamped. And because he believed in the machinery, he trusted its outputs. Give him paper properly formatted, properly sourced, and part of him would accept it the way a devout man accepts scripture: not because he checked it but because checking it would be a kind of blasphemy.

But only part of him. The rest of Corven was clever and suspicious and had spent months circling Edith's household like a hawk that had scented something and couldn't say what. So we couldn't feed him fiction. Fiction has a smell. Fiction is too tidy, names too plausible, streets too conveniently arranged. No — if we were going to satisfy his superiors, we would have to give them the truth, or a truth, carefully selected the way a butcher selects: all the parts nobody will miss.

"The dead," Vane said slowly, when I explained it. "You're going to give him the dead."

"And the gone. Everyone who emigrated before the war closed the ports. Everyone who died under their own name — of wounds, of fever, of ordinary peaceable mortality. Every address in my head that leads nowhere." I spread the first sheet. "Corven wants the network's arteries. I'm going to hand him a map of veins that have been cut for years. Real names. Real houses. Some of them empty. Some of them occupied by widows who haven't passed a letter since 1915 and never will again. His men can raid every one of them and find tea cosies and grief."

Vane turned a page of my draft notes with his trembling finger. "And the live ones?"

"The live ones aren't on paper. They're here." I tapped my temple again. "And they'll leave here one way or another before the exchange."

"You could burn yourself clean," he said quietly. "Refuse the trade. Let Edith—"

"Don't," I said.

He shut up. He was good at shutting up; the war had taught him that. But I heard the sentence finish itself in the silence anyway, and I let it, because he wasn't wrong, and it was better for both of us if the thing we were doing sat honestly between us. Corven had offered me a mirror, not a bargain. Hand over the network, get Edith back. A clean transaction, mercy-shaped. And every hour since he'd made it I had been living inside the knowledge that the easiest version of me — the invisible version, the driver who reads and says nothing — would have taken the deal, handed over everything, and told himself it was for Edith, only for Edith, as if saving one woman with thirty nooses were arithmetic instead of murder.

So we would cheat him with paperwork. It felt fitting. The machine had eaten the world on forms and requisitions and casualty lists; we would fight it with a forged casualty list of our own.

* * *

We worked four nights.

I wrote; Vane verified. That was the division I hadn't expected to need him for, and it turned out he was the most valuable instrument in the room. He'd been at the front, then in hospital, then in transit camps, and soldiers' talk is a river that runs through everything, and he remembered things. When I gave him the name Aldous Wray, lodgings in Canterbury, he shook his head. "Wray died at Loos. My mate Tom carried the news to his mother in Dover. She's dead too — Tom said so, '16." Good. Wray went on the ledger, marked active until spring 1915, deceased after, address intact. A perfect thread for Corven to pull: real, checkable, and leading into cold ground.

Other names I cut without mercy. Anywhere the trail was warm — anyone moved recently, anyone whose family still answered letters — those stayed in my skull, unspoken, unwritten, already dying there as I recited them once more and then let them go, address by address, the way you close rooms in a house you're leaving.

The forgery itself was the easy part, because it wasn't really a forgery. It was a compilation, which is a subtler crime and a safer one. I copied the format of Edith's own route books — I knew them by heart, having filed them for years — and I wrote in a hand I had been practicing since I was fourteen: not my hand, not Edith's, but the neutral clerk's hand of a network that needed records no individual could be tied to. Dates adjusted. Ciphers rendered into plain form using the key Vane had watched Brandt's letter teach me — simple, substitutional, the kind of system frightened people invent twice a week in every country in Europe. Plausible. Slightly imperfect. I aged pages over steam. I put a coffee ring on one corner, because no document that matters is ever entirely clean.

"She'd hate this," Vane said on the third night, watching me rule columns. "Edith. Using dead people like this."

"She'd do it faster than me," I said. "And she'd write the widow in Canterbury afterward to apologize."

"Do you believe she's all right?"

I kept ruling lines. "Corven needs her alive. She's his proof of concept. If she dies in custody, his superiors have a suffragist martyr and an empty file, and Corven knows it. She's safe the way a hostage is safe, which is to say she's safe until she isn't."

"And Brandt?"

"Brandt is the part I haven't solved yet."

That was true, and saying it aloud made it truer. Because the ledger would satisfy the machine, but it wouldn't satisfy Corven. Corven's obsession was not networks. Networks were his language, but his hunger was Brandt — the traitor who wasn't a traitor, the dead man writing careful letters, the one quarry that had escaped the filing cabinet entirely. A ledger would buy Edith's release on paper. But Corven would want to watch the handover. He would insist on a public exchange, controlled ground, his own men. And somewhere in that arrangement he would be hunting Brandt, whether Brandt appeared or not, and if Brandt did appear—

"He'll come," Vane said, reading me. "You know he will. He'll say it's to draw Corven off you, and it'll even be true, mostly."

"Mostly," I said.

* * *

On the fifth night I burned the drafts.

In the grate of the Ashford rooming house, at four in the morning, with Vane keeping watch at the window, I fed six nights of work into the fire sheet by sheet and watched four years of memory turn to ash and curl. It should have felt like loss. It felt like cleaning a wound. The false ledger lay finished in my coat — the old measure, everything I owned measured by what could be carried in a coat — sewn into the lining over my left ribs, where a man keeps the things he cannot afford to lose and cannot afford to be found holding.

"There's one more thing," Vane said, while the last of the drafts blackened. He spoke to the window, not to me. "When this is done. The exchange. Afterward. I won't be going with you. Wherever you go."

"I didn't assume—"

"No, listen." His reflection in the glass had steadied somehow; the tremor was still in his hand but not in his voice. "I broke. That happened, it's done, I can't unsay what I said to Corven. But I can carry the next thing. Whatever the next thing is. You'll need someone on the docks who looks like nothing — a discharged soldier, half-crippled, invisible. I'm very good at invisible now. The army taught me, same as it taught you, only different lessons."

I looked at him a long time. Tomas Vane, who had walked to Edith's gate at dusk carrying a dead man's letter because some scrap of honor in him demanded letters reach their people even when armies and governments and sanity stood in the way. Broken, and still choosing. We were all choosing, that year. It was the only thing the war had left us in abundance.

"All right," I said. "The docks. Then we'll see."

* * *

I sent the message to Corven through the one channel he couldn't ignore: a note delivered to his office by hand, unsigned, in the neutral clerk's hand, naming a place and an hour. The ledger exists. One copy. Your prisoner for your paper. Bring your superiors' authority in writing or bring nothing.

Then I did what I had promised myself I wouldn't do, which was go and see Brandt.

He was staying in the loft above a farrier's shop outside town, under the farrier's deaf mother's nonexistence on any register anywhere. He was sitting up when I climbed the ladder, awake as always at that hour, the ruined side of his face turned toward the lamp so the good side could read. The book was one of the farrier's — a manual on horseshoeing — and he held it the way he'd held books on the train three years ago, thumb along the spine, entirely absorbed, a man who had decided that whatever time remained would be spent paying attention to something.

"Well?" he said. His voice was still the changed voice, gravel over broken glass, but I had stopped hearing the change. "Your face tells me the ledger's done. Your face also tells me it cost you."

"The ledger's done." I sat on the crate across from him. "It will buy her out. On paper."

"But."

"But Corven will want to see it handed over with his own eyes, and he'll want more than the ledger, and we both know what more means. He wants you, Aurel. He's wanted you since the first report crossed his desk, and a forged list of dead men's addresses is going to taste like ash to him unless—" I stopped.

"Unless I'm standing on the dock," Brandt said, "being the bait."

"It doesn't have to be you."

"It has to be me." He closed the horseshoeing manual and set it down with the care of a man who handled everything gently now, as if the world owed him fragility and he refused to collect. "Ivo. Listen to me, because I've had a lot of time in lofts and cellars to think, and I've thought this through from every angle I own. Corven's superiors are embarrassed by him. You said it yourself — he's spent too much on one man, and bureaucracies forgive anything except expense. If the exchange goes through cleanly — ledger for the woman, papers signed, done — his superiors will take their prize, close the file, and rein him in before he can spit. The ledger makes him respectable enough to stop. But if he senses the ledger is false, if he gets one thread loose, he'll tear the whole coast apart looking for me, and Edith goes back in a cell within the week, and you go into one beside her."

"So he has to believe it completely."

"He has to be looking at exactly what he wants," Brandt said, "at exactly the moment he expects to want it. And what he wants is me." The good side of his face did something that was almost the old smile. "I've been dead since 1916, Ivo. Dead men are very hard to inconvenience. Let me stand on the dock and be worth stopping for. By the time anyone decides what to do with me, the trade will be done and witnessed and his superiors will be watching him do it."

I wanted to refuse. I sat with the wanting for a long moment, and it was a physical thing, a hand at my throat, because I had made one promise to this man on a train in peacetime and I had built my whole crooked life around keeping it, and here he was asking me to spend him like currency.

"You trust me to run it," I said finally.

"I trusted a stranger on a train to carry a letter," Brandt said. "It worked out rather well, as I recall. Now go and sleep. Tomorrow you sell the machine its own paperwork, and I stand in the light, and we find out what England does with a dead man who came back."

I climbed down the ladder into the dark with the ledger riding against my ribs and the whole plan balanced on its final point: a waterfront, a night, a major with a mirror in his hands, and a forged list of the dead, waiting to see if lies written carefully enough could pass, just this once, for the truth.
