## 20. The Exchange at the Docks

The tide was going out when we came down to the water, and the river smelled of tar and dead fish and the cold iron breath of ships that would not sail for years. Vane walked ahead of me with the ledger under his arm like a hymn book. Behind us, half a street back and wrapped in a borrowed greatcoat with the collar up, walked a dead man.

I had not wanted him to come. That argument had lasted two days and ended the way all our arguments ended now: Edith deciding, Brandt agreeing, and me being outvoted by people who were braver than I was.

"He wants Corven," Edith had said in the kitchen, her hands flat on the table as if she could pin the whole plan down through the wood. "Corven has spent eight months chasing a ghost. If the ghost stands in front of him, he'll forget everything else—his orders, his superiors, his sense. A man like that doesn't want a ledger. He wants a trophy."

"And if he takes it?" I asked.

"Then we take Edith," Brandt said quietly, "and we are even."

I hated the plan. I want that written somewhere, in case anyone later mistakes what happened at the docks for cleverness. I hated it, and I did it anyway, because the alternative was letting Edith rot in Holloway while Corven built his career out of her bones, and there are limits to what a man can carry and still call himself a man.

* * *

We had chosen the place the way you choose anything in wartime: by what was broken already. The coal wharf east of Limehouse had been half-derelict since the first autumn of the war—cranes rusting mid-gesture like men frozen reaching for something they'd never get, barges settled low and rotten against the quay, warehouses with their windows gone dark and their doors chained since before anyone thought to chain anything. There was one working lamp at the wharf's mouth, and beyond it the moon did what little it could, laying a grey path across the black water where the tide sucked and gurgled against the pilings.

Vane and I arrived first, at eleven. The ledger sat between us on an upturned crate, tied with string, looking for all the world like a parcel of groceries. Inside were forty-one addresses. Nine of them belonged to people who had died—one of influenza, three at the Front, the rest in the ordinary ways the poor die—and the rest to families who had emigrated or scattered or simply ceased to exist at any address the War Office could reach. Vane had done most of the forgery. His hand, it turned out, was very good at being other hands. He had learned that in the trenches, copying passes, and he said it without pride, the way you'd mention knowing how to dig.

"You'll give it over slow," I told him, for the fourth time. "You'll ask for the woman first. You don't let go of the ledger until she's standing beside Corven and you've seen her face."

"And if he won't play it that way?"

"Then you drop it in the water and we all find out how good a swimmer he is."

Vane almost smiled. It was strange, watching him. Three months ago he had been a trembling thing at our gate, and Corven's questioning had bent him until something cracked and he'd given up my description—a tall driver, careful hands, reads too much—and set the hunt on me. Now here he stood in the cold with his shoulders squared, repentant the way soldiers are repentant: not with words but with willingness to be shot at.

"I owe her," he said. "And I owe you."

"You owe nobody," I said. "You were ground between machines. So was I. So is everyone." But I put my hand on his shoulder anyway, because some debts aren't about arithmetic.

At twenty past eleven, a motorcar came down the far road with its lamps hooded, and stopped a hundred yards off, and Major Corven got out alone.

That was the first wrong thing. I knew it in my stomach before my head caught up. Corven did not go anywhere alone; he went with clerks, with witnesses, with the small bureaucratic armor of men who build cases. A Corven who came alone was a Corven who intended to do something no witness should see.

He walked toward us across the cobbles, and the moon gave me his face in pieces—the long jaw, the eyes that had once burned with suffrage meetings and now burned with nothing else. He had been handsome in the way of church architecture. He had been Edith's ally once. He had held her umbrella at demonstrations and argued constitutional law with her over tea in the very kitchen where I'd later confessed everything, and I tried to hold on to that man as he approached, because hating him cleanly would have been easier than what I actually felt, which was grief with a badge on it.

"Mr. Crane," he said. He knew my name now. Of course he did. "Where is she?"

"Where's your authority?" I asked. "Where are your men?"

"My men," Corven said, "are wherever I say they are." He looked past me, down the dark length of the wharf, and I saw his nostrils move slightly, the way a dog's do. "You brought him, didn't you. You actually brought him."

"I brought a ledger," I said. "Forty-one names. Every address your war office has been bleeding itself dry trying to map. The network entire—or near enough. You can spend a year verifying it, and by then half of it will have moved twice."

Something moved in the shadows behind the second warehouse, and my heart went up into my throat—but it was only Brandt, stepping out into the open exactly as planned, exactly where the moonlight could find him.

He had shaved. Vane had shaved him that afternoon with a shaking hand and a dull razor, and dressed the ruined side of his face toward the dark. In the flat moonlight, from thirty feet, he might have been any gaunt man in a greatcoat. From ten feet, he was a corpse that had learned to walk. Half his face was a landscape of healed burns and missing flesh, the eye on that side gone to a white seam, the ear a curl of scar. The war had signed its work and left the signature showing.

"Hello, Major," Brandt said. His voice was still the changed voice, gravel poured through a grate, but the diction was untouched—officer's English, precise and faintly amused. "You've made rather a project of me."

I will give Corven this: he didn't flinch. What he did was worse. He smiled. It was the smile of a man watching a year of work walk into his hand voluntarily, and behind the smile I watched every scruple he had left pack its bags and leave.

"Captain Brandt. Or whatever you're calling yourself this month. You understand I'm obliged to arrest you."

"I understand you're obliged to try," Brandt said. "There's a difference, and you're about to learn it."

"The ledger first," Corven said, turning to Vane, holding out his hand, and his voice had gone quick and hungry, all the officer stripped away and just the appetite showing. "Give it to me."

"The woman," Vane said. "First. Those were the terms."

"The terms," Corven said softly, "were mine to grant and mine to revoke. Marlowe is in custody. She will remain in custody pending—" and he stopped, because headlights swept the far road.

A large car, unhooded lamps blazing, coming fast. It swung onto the wharf approach and halted in a wash of light, and two men got out of it wearing civilian coats that fit like uniforms, the way clothes do on men who have worn nothing else for twenty years. One carried a leather dispatch case. Neither hurried.

Corven turned toward them and I saw it happen to his face—saw the blood leave it in stages, like a tide going out.

"Major Corven." The older of the two had a voice like a filing cabinet closing. "You were instructed to report to the Admiralty annex at nine o'clock. You did not."

"This is an ongoing operation—"

"It *was* an operation." The man opened the dispatch case and produced papers with the unhurried cruelty of a man who has rehearsed. "It has been reviewed. At length. Do you know what the review concluded, Major? It concluded that for eight months, an intelligence officer of this department has devoted the resources of the Crown to the pursuit of one shell-shocked deserter—excuse me, one *escaped prisoner*, whose actual value to the enemy is precisely nil—while authorizing the interception of civilian mail in quantities that have produced three hundred pages of ladies' knitting patterns and not one actionable fact." He let that sit in the cold air. "It further concluded that the arrest of Mrs. Edith Marlowe—a woman with powerful friends, Major, friends who write letters to newspapers—was effected on evidence your own subordinates described in writing as *circumstantial*. Their words are in this file. Your name is above theirs."

"Sir, if you will permit me to conclude the exchange—"

"There is no exchange. There is no network worth the name—that much your obsession has finally proven, at considerable expense. There is a prisoner to be released with apologies, and there is an officer to be reassigned somewhere the weather is bad and the paperwork is worse." The man's eyes moved, briefly, across Vane, across me, across the crate with its string-tied parcel, and moved on with the magnificent incuriosity of power. "Get in the car, Major."

And that should have been the end of it. That is what I keep returning to, in the nights since. It *should* have ended there, with Corven standing in the lamplight being dismantled by his own machine, with Edith free by morning, with the ledger never even changing hands. The machinery had turned around and bitten its own tooth.

But Corven had spent eight months becoming the thing he hunted, and a man cannot be unmade in a minute.

"No," he said.

"Major—"

"*No.*" His hand went inside his coat, and everything after that happened in the order that disasters happen: instantly, and wrongly, and all at once.

I shouted—I don't know what, some syllable—and threw myself sideways off the crate. Corven's pistol came up not toward me, not toward the officials, but toward Brandt, because Corven's whole self had narrowed years ago to a single point and that point was a dead traitor alive in front of him, and if his career was ending tonight then the ghost would end with it, proof or no proof, witnesses or none.

The shot went wide because Brandt was already moving—not away, I understood that even as I fell against the crates, he wasn't running *away*—because Edith was coming out of the shadow of the third warehouse.

She was supposed to stay hidden until the car left. She was supposed to wait for word. But Edith Marlowe had heard engines and raised voices and a single gunshot, and Edith Marlowe had spent fifty years walking toward the sound of trouble instead of away from it, and so she stepped into the moonlight in her plain grey coat with her chin up like a suffragist facing mounted police, and Corven's second shot was already leaving the gun.

Brandt reached her first.

He crossed the space between them in four strides and took her around the shoulders and turned, putting his back to the pistol, his ruined body between her and the muzzle, and the third shot took him high in the back, below the shoulder blade, and I heard it hit the way you hear an axe go into wet wood.

After that the night came apart properly. The older official's companion drew his own weapon and fired twice and Corven spun and went down against a bollard, and whether he was dead or merely finished I never learned that night and have never quite wanted to learn since. Vane was shouting. The officials were shouting. Somewhere a whistle began shrieking from the direction of the police station, thin and getting thicker, more whistles joining it. And I was running, stumbling over cobbles slick with fish scales and rope ends, to where Brandt lay with Edith kneeling over him and her hands pressed hard against his back, her grey coat going black at the seams.

"Stay down, stay down, help is—" Edith was saying, in the voice she used at rallies, the carrying voice, and it broke halfway through and became just a woman's voice saying *stay with me, stay with me.*

I knelt in the mess of it. Brandt's face was turned toward me, the good side up, and he was looking at me with the eye that still worked, and he was trying to smile.

"The letter," he said. The gravel voice was thinner now, watered down. "Did you ever read it? The one Vane brought."

"I memorized it," I said. "Every word. I couldn't risk keeping it."

"Good." Blood at the corner of his mouth. "Then you know... the farmhouse was never the point. The promise was never about Kent." His hand found my wrist, gripped with a strength that surprised us both. "It was about whether anyone... would keep faith with a stranger. When it cost something. You did. Tell me you know that."

"You're going to tell me yourself," I said, pressing my own hands over Edith's, feeling the terrible heat of him pumping away between our fingers. "You're going to live under some idiot dead man's name in some damp cottage and complain about the tea for forty years. That's the arrangement. I don't break arrangements."

Boots on the cobbles now, many boots, lantern light swinging up the wharf. The officials had withdrawn to their car with the speed of men whose involvement had officially never occurred. Vane appeared at my shoulder, white-faced, and put his coat off and wadded it against the wound without being asked.

"Crane." Edith's face was close to mine, streaked, fierce, terrified. "They'll take him to a prison hospital if they take him at all. He's a dead traitor to every record in this country. We have to move him *ourselves*."

Lanterns. Whistles. The tide sucking at the pilings. A man dying slowly under six hands in the moonlight, and the law arriving to claim him.

"Then we move him," I said.

* * *

What follows, I set down plainly, because it was the least complicated thing I have ever done.

The borrowed hearse was still where we'd left it, two streets back, because funerals keep their own hours and the undertaker had been paid for the full night. Six of us carried Brandt along the dark lanes—me, Vane, and four dockmen whom Edith summoned out of a public house with five sentences and the particular authority of a woman who has been arrested for other people's freedom and does not intend to waste the credential. They asked no questions. The war had cured the poor of questions; questions were a luxury taxed at the highest rate.

We laid him on the bier where a stranger's coffin should have been, and Edith rode beside him with her coat stiffening black, and Vane drove, and I sat in the back with my hand on Brandt's chest counting the beats under my palm like a man counting coins he is about to lose.

He spoke once, near the bridge at Marleigh, where a constable lifted his lamp and looked in and saw exactly what the paperwork promised: mourners, a widow, a burden bound for consecrated ground. The constable touched his hat. The war had taught England to look away from coffins; there were too many to inspect.

"Kent," Brandt murmured. "Or wherever. Doesn't matter." His eye found mine in the dark. "You kept it. That's the whole... that's the whole of it, Crane. Everything else is paperwork."

Then he was quiet, and the quiet was worse than the talking, and I counted the beats under my hand all the way across the city, slower and slower, like a clock winding down in a house where everyone has gone to bed.

We reached the surgeon Vane knew—the one who patched men off the books, who asked nothing and charged little and had seen enough of the war's paperwork to prefer wounds to forms. They carried Brandt in at three in the morning. At dawn, the surgeon came out wiping his hands and said the words *he may live* and *it will be months* and *if you have any sense you'll be gone before he wakes.*

Edith sat down on the surgery steps in her ruined coat and began, very quietly, to laugh—the laugh of a woman who has been in a cell for a month and shot at for an hour and is, at this moment, free, and knows exactly what freedom is going to cost her tomorrow, when the newspapers find out and Corven's friends come asking how a traitor vanished from a police wharf.

I sat down beside her. Down the street, the sky was going the colour of pewter, and the first milk cart of the morning rattled past, and the driver looked straight ahead the way everyone looks straight ahead now, at everything and nothing.

"They'll come for you," I said. "For harboring him. For the dock."

"Yes," Edith said. She said it the way she said *votes for women*, as a fact requiring no debate. "So I shall have to leave England. Isn't that funny. Fifty years fighting to belong to this country, and now I'm to be its guest nowhere." She looked at me sideways, and there was something new in her face, tired and clear at once. "Unless," she said, "you happen to know a driver with a car, a full tank, and no attachments whatsoever."

I started to answer her.

And that was when Vane came running bareheaded out of the alley behind the surgery, shouting my name, saying there was a boy, a messenger boy, asking at the door for *the driver who reads*—and holding out an envelope addressed in a hand I did not know, which meant that someone, somewhere, already knew where we were.
