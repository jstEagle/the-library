## 1. The Hour That Would Not Hold

The summons came on a Tuesday, in the voice of a boy who had run up three flights of stairs without pausing to breathe and now stood in the doorway of Reyes & Son, Clockmakers, wheezing like a bellows with a split seam.

"Master Voss wants you," the boy said. "The observatory clock. It's stopped."

Dominic set down his tweezers. Across the workbench, his father did not look up from the pocket watch he was dissecting, but his shoulders went still in a way Dominic had learned to read the way sailors read clouds.

"The observatory clock has not been opened since before you were born," his father said to the watch.

"Then it's overdue," Dominic said, and heard how young and eager his own voice sounded, and hated it a little.

* * *

Vell sat in its bowl of mountains like water in a cup, and above it all rose the observatory, a pale stone fist raised against the sky, crowned with its copper dome gone green as pond ice. The master clock lived in the tower beneath the dome, and every clock in town — every shop clock, every church clock, every kitchen clock with its chipped porcelain face — took its orders from that one machine, the way all the rivers of the valley took their orders from the snowfields above.

Dominic climbed the spiral stair with his tool roll under his arm and his breath smoking in the cold. The tower smelled of dust and old oil and pigeon, and something else beneath it, faint and chemical, like the memory of ink.

Master Voss was waiting at the top, wrapped in a coat too heavy for the season, his face gray as the stone around him. He was not an old man, but he looked old that morning, the way a house looks older when its windows are dark.

"You came alone," Voss said. Not a question. A relief.

"You sent for me."

"I sent for your father." Voss's eyes flicked toward the stairwell, empty now, then back. "He sent a boy instead."

"He sent his best hands."

Something moved across Voss's face — not quite a smile. "So he did." He stepped aside. "It stopped at four minutes past eleven last night. It will not be restarted until it is repaired properly. Do you understand? Properly. No shortcuts. Whatever you find in there, you repair it or you leave it exactly as it lies, and you tell no one what you find."

Dominic laughed, because the alternative was to ask what a man might find inside a clock that required secrecy. "It's a clock, sir. Not a grave."

Voss did not laugh. He stood by the door with his arms folded while Dominic knelt before the great case, and only after a long moment did he say, quietly, "No. Not a grave," and left it there between them like a coin on a table.

* * *

The case was walnut, tall as a coffin standing on end, and the door gave onto the pendulum, the weights, the great brass wheel-train hung with decades of dust like gray fur. Dominic worked by lamplight, easing the movement forward, checking each wheel. The escapement was sound. The mainspring held tension. Everything was sound, which was itself strange; clocks do not stop for no reason.

He found the reason wedged behind the weight channel, where no weight should ever go.

At first he thought it was a bird's nest, some wintering thing, a bundle of rags. Then his fingers found metal — cold, precise metal, edges machined straight — and he drew it out into the light with both hands.

A typewriter.

Not a large one. A portable, the kind traveling salesmen carried, its black enamel chipped along one corner, its carriage locked mid-line as though the machine had simply been interrupted and never forgiven. Someone had wrapped it in a wool scarf and forced it into the space behind the weights, where the constant shudder of the striking train would have hidden any rattle. Hidden it for years, perhaps. The dust on it was thick enough to write in.

Dominic sat back on his heels. Above him, through the floor, he could hear Master Voss pacing, three steps and turn, three steps and turn.

There was no reason to open the typewriter. There was no reason even to touch it again. His business was the clock; the clock was sound; whatever slept behind its weights belonged to whoever had put it there, and that person, whoever they were, had wanted it sleeping.

He told himself this very clearly, kneeling in the lamplight, and then he pressed the carriage release, because he was nineteen years old and his hands had never once in his life obeyed the wiser part of him.

The platen turned with a dry whisper. In the feed, still gripped where the roller met the guide, was a ribbon — cloth, inked, half-spent. And threaded through the machine, sheet after sheet of it, a document typed in a hand so disciplined it looked printed:

*I, Aldric Fenner, magistrate of the district of Vell, being of sound mind and failing body, do set down the following as truth...*

Dominic read the first line twice. The name meant something. Everyone in Vell knew the name Fenner; the magistrate had died two winters ago, quietly, of the congestion in his chest, and the town had given him a funeral with four horses and the governor himself among the mourners, hat in hand, eyes appropriately damp.

He read the third paragraph. Then the fifth. Then he stopped reading in order and read everywhere at once, the way a drowning man grabs at the water.

*...the deeds of the eastern holdings were never surrendered. They were taken. I witnessed the signatures myself, coerced under threat of audit and ruin, and I watched Governor Halloran's clerk carry the originals out of my own courthouse in a leather case with brass corners, and I said nothing, because I was paid to say nothing, and the payment was my silence and my pension and my life...*

*...four hundred and eleven parcels. Every farm east of the mill race. The governor holds papers that say the land is his. The people hold memories that say otherwise. Memory is not evidence. I am writing the evidence...*

The tower was very cold, and Dominic discovered he was sweating.

He thought: *this is not my business.* He thought it the way you press a hand over a wound, firmly, believing pressure will keep everything inside where it belongs. He was an apprentice clockmaker. His concerns were escapements and mainsprings, the fair price of a cleaned movement, whether Marta Brenner would dance with him at the harvest festival if the festival happened this year. History happened in capitals, in newspapers, to other men. Vell was a town where things were mended — clocks, roofs, fences — not a town where things were decided.

And yet his thumb had left a smear in the dust on the typewriter's lid, and there was no un-smearing it. He had touched it. The machine knew his hand now, in the dumb way machines know everything.

Below him, three steps and turn, three steps and turn. Master Voss knew. Voss had known the clock was sound — of course he had known; a man does not summon a clockmaker across town for a healthy machine. He had summoned someone to find this. Or to confirm it was still here. Or —

Dominic looked at the pages. There were dozens of them, typed close and numbered in a small neat pencil hand in the corner: 1 through 47, and the forty-seventh ended mid-sentence, the ribbon trailing off in letters gone pale, as if the ink itself had died before the man did.

He fed the sheets back through, closed the carriage, wrapped the machine in its scarf, and — after a moment's argument with himself that he lost — slid it back behind the weight channel, exactly where it had lain, exactly as it had lain.

Then he stood, brushed the dust from his knees, and called down the stairwell in a steady voice that cost him more than any repair of his life so far:

"Master Voss! I've found the fault. It's in the strike train. I'll need the morning to true it up."

Silence below. Then Voss's voice, carefully flat: "You found nothing else?"

"The clock is a clock, sir. Nothing else."

Another silence, longer. When Voss answered, Dominic could not tell whether the relief in it was for the lie or against it.

"Good," Voss said. "Come down when you're finished. We'll have tea. My wife will want to meet the boy who fixed the master clock."

Dominic worked through the afternoon with the lamp burning, truing wheels that needed no truing, listening to the wind come down off the peaks with a new edge in it, the first taste of the season's snow. And all the while, behind him, the great case stood shut and silent, keeping its secret the way the mountain keeps its dead, and Dominic kept hearing one sentence turning over in his mind like a coin he could not stop flipping:

*Memory is not evidence. I am writing the evidence.*

He walked home in the blue dusk. From the ridge above the bridge, far off, he could see lanterns moving along the far shore — more lanterns than a toll house needed, gathered like moths at the only road out of the valley.

The first snow would close that road within the week. Everyone in Vell knew it.

Everyone, it seemed, except him, until that moment.
