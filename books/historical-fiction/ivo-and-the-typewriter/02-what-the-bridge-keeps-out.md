## 2. What the Bridge Keeps Out

The snow came at night, the way debt comes — quietly, and then all at once.

Dominic woke to a light he did not recognize, a white pressure against the shutters, and lay still in his narrow bed above the workshop listening to the town change its voice. Vell in snow was a different town. The cartwheels went mute. The church bell seemed to ring through wool. And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of men shouting down by the river — not alarm, exactly, but the brisk, official shouting of men who have been given permission.

He dressed in the dark and climbed to the ridge.

The bridge was gone. Not destroyed — swallowed. Snow had come down the valley in the night like a hand closing, and where the road had crossed the gorge there was now only a long white seam, unbroken, running from shore to shore. On the far bank the lanterns from yesterday had become a camp: three tents, a sledge, a stack of crates stamped with the governor's crest, the double-headed eagle that looked, from this distance, like a bird being pulled apart by its own wings.

A man with a shovel stood at the near end of the bridge, though there was nothing left to shovel. He was not clearing anything. He was guarding the absence of a road.

*Closed for the season,* Dominic thought, and the phrase arrived with a strange doubling, as if he were reading it on a placard and feeling it close around his own ankles at the same time. Every winter of his life the bridge had closed, and every winter Vell had folded into itself like a clock being cased up — self-sufficient, patient, sealed. It had never once felt like a lid before.

He turned back toward town and saw the sledges already moving through the lower streets. Governor's men, six of them, going door to door with the cheerful thoroughness of tax collectors. At the Hradil house they were carrying out books. At the schoolhouse they spoke to the headmaster for a long time without going inside, which was somehow worse.

Dominic went to work.

His master, old Brenner, was deaf in the way of men who have chosen to be, and spent the morning hunched over a regulator for the priest's sacristy, saying nothing. Dominic cleaned wheels. He polished pivots. He let the small holy machinery of the bench absorb him, gear by gear, until the shaking in his hands stopped. This was the trade's first mercy: you could not think about anything larger than a wheel train for more than an hour at a stretch, because the work would not permit it.

It was nearly noon when the knock came, and Brenner answered it, and Elka Marchetti stepped into the shop trailing cold air and the smell of pine smoke, and everything the work had held at bay came back through the door behind her.

She was not dressed as a widow dresses. That was the first thing he noticed — no black, or none that he could see under the grey traveling coat, and her boots had been oiled for walking. She looked at Brenner, then at Dominic, and said, "I've brought you a movement to repair," in a voice pitched to carry no further than the counter, "and I would like the apprentice to do it, since he has worked on our house clocks before."

Brenner grunted assent to whatever version of the sentence his ears supplied and returned to the sacristy regulator.

Elka set a wrapped bundle on the counter. Her hands, unwrapping it, were perfectly steady, and Dominic understood that the steadiness had cost her something to build and was costing her something to maintain.

"You took papers from the observatory," she said quietly. "From the case."

"I don't know what you—"

"Magistrate Marchetti typed his confession on that machine every Tuesday for eleven months." Her eyes did not move from his face. "He told me if anything happened to him, the ribbon would remember what the town would prefer to forget. Then I read your repair ticket in the observatory log. *Case interior fouled; foreign object removed and disposed.*" A pause. "'Disposed' is a word men use when they haven't decided yet."

Dominic's mouth was dry. Outside, a sledge runner shrieked past on the packed snow, and both of them waited until it had gone.

"Madam," he said at last, "I'm an apprentice. I repair what I'm asked to repair. What was in that case has nothing to do with me."

"No," Elka said. "That's almost true, which is why it's almost comfortable. But consider what actually has nothing to do with you." She drew a folded sheet from her coat — one of the typed pages, and he recognized the typeface now, the slight lean of the *e*, the way the machine struck heavier on the left side of the platen. "Page nine. Read just the names, if you won't read the rest."

He should not have. He knew he should not have, even as his eyes went down the column.

The magistrate had been precise. There were dates, parcels, registry numbers — and beside them, the names of those who had witnessed transfers, co-signed deeds, accepted consideration for silence. And third on that list, in the flat grey ink of the typewriter, was *Aldo Reyes, millwright*, and after it, *his brother Tomas Reyes, constable of the north district.*

His uncle. His father's brother. The man who had paid for his apprenticeship. Who came every Sunday to dinner and complained about his feet and slipped coins into Dominic's pocket when he thought no one saw.

"They didn't steal anything," Elka said, watching him. "They signed. They watched. They confirmed that the meadow above Tern was 'unclaimed' when their own father had grazed sheep on it for thirty years, and they confirmed it because the governor's clerk explained to them, very reasonably, what happens to families who obstruct administrative processes. Your uncle kept his mill. Half the valley lost theirs." She refolded the page, gently, as if it were laundry. "You are right that you stole nothing, Dominic. You are only wrong about what you're carrying."

* * *

He did not go home. He walked the long way, past the frozen fountain, past the shuttered assay office, and the whole time the page turned over in his mind like a leaf in wind. Tomas Reyes. Uncle Tomas, who called him *piccolino* and asked about his tools.

By four o'clock the searchers had reached Clockmakers' Lane.

Dominic heard them before he saw them — boots on the boards, two pairs, and the particular rhythm of men who knock on doors expecting to be thanked. When they came to the shop he was alone; Brenner had gone to the priest's. They were polite. One of them had a document with a seal on it, and read aloud something about *recovered municipal property, missing registry folios, cooperation rewarded*. The other one looked around the workshop slowly, at the benches and the drawers and the hundred small drawers within drawers, and Dominic realized with a lurch that a clockmaker's shop was made entirely of hiding places, and that these men knew it.

"Registry folios," the reader repeated. "Typed material. Paper."

"We repair clocks," Dominic said. His voice sounded far away to him, belonging to someone steadier.

They found nothing, because there was nothing to find — he had taken the pages home days ago, folded into the lining of his tool roll. But standing there while a stranger's gloved fingers walked across his master's bench, Dominic understood something with terrible clarity: the papers in his room were not evidence. Evidence required a road out of the valley, and the road was under four feet of snow. What he possessed was, for the next five months, only a way to die.

And yet he could not burn them. That was the thought that frightened him most — not the soldiers, not the governor, but the simple fact that when he imagined holding the pages over the stove, his hands refused in advance. Magistrate Marchetti had typed those words with hands that must have shaken worse than these. To destroy them now would be to finish, free of charge, the job the governor's men had started.

After they left, he sat for a long time in the blue evening light with the pages spread on his bench.

Then he began to take apart the tall case clock that stood in the corner — Brenner's old demonstration piece, never sold, never missed. He lifted out the weights, great brass cylinders as long as his forearm, each with a threaded cap at the base for adjusting the fill. He weighed a page in his hand. He rolled one tight as a wick, and another, and another, and slid them down into the hollow of the weights among the lead shot, and screwed the caps home, and hung them back on their cables where they had hung for forty years, pulling the mechanism honestly downward, asking nothing, hiding nothing — except now everything.

When it was done he wound the clock and set it ticking and listened to it in the empty shop, that steady unbending heartbeat, and felt no relief at all. Only the cold specific knowledge that he had passed some line in the night without noticing, and that invisibility — his oldest comfort, his craft of standing behind counters and letting history happen elsewhere — was no longer a shelter.

It was a side. And men on the other side of that line had already begun, politely, with seals on their documents, to collect names.

He was pulling on his coat to go when the shop bell rang again.

Elka Marchetti stood in the doorway, snow in her hair, breathing hard from the hill. Behind her, far down the lane, lanterns were coming on one by one, and somewhere below the town a sledge turned for Clockmakers' Lane.

"They're at your uncle's house," she said.
