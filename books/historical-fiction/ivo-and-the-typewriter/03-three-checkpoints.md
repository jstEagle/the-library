## 3. Three Checkpoints

**Dominic**

The plan, if it could be called a plan, was made of small things.

That was Elka's genius, Dominic came to understand. Where he saw the confession as one dangerous whole — forty-one typed pages that could hang them all from the bridge's iron spine — she saw only pieces. *A thing carried whole is a thing caught whole,* she said, laying the pages out on her kitchen table like cards dealt for fortune-telling. *Carried in pieces, it is nothing at all. A watch repair. A hymnbook. A man buying oil.*

They divided it over two nights by candlelight, and even in division the pages seemed to breathe with danger. The first twelve went into the case of a hunter's pocket watch Dominic had been paid to repair — thin sheets folded to the size of a coin, wedged beneath the brass movement where only a master's fingers would think to look. The next fifteen Elka slit the hollow spine of her dead husband's hymnal and sewed shut again with black thread, singing under her breath as she worked, some low mountain hymn about the valley of the shadow. The last fourteen pages went into the sack of clock oil Dominic would carry openly, because the boldest hiding place is the one you show the world.

*Three checkpoints,* Elka said, tapping the table. *The tavern, where Vranek's men drink and count who passes. The toll house, where they open every crate but read no books. And the clerk, Havel —* here she paused, and her face did something complicated, *— Havel is the worst of the three, because Havel thinks.*

Dominic asked why she trusted him with any of it. Why him at all.

Elka looked at him for a long moment across the candle flame. *Because you repair clocks,* she said. *You already know how to make something false keep honest time.*

* * *

**Elka**

She watched him go down the white street in the grey of morning, sack over his shoulder, breath steaming, and thought: he walks like his father. She had known Reyeses all her life in this town; they were the family that fixed what others broke and said nothing about the breaking. Good people. Invisible people. The governor had built his whole theft on the shoulders of such people.

She had loved Tomas for twenty-two years, and in all that time he had believed the same thing Dominic believed — that history was weather that happened somewhere else, that a man could keep his head down inside his own small workshop life and be passed over. Then the deeds began to vanish. Then the farmers began to owe land they had owned since before there was a governor. And Tomas, who could not fix an injustice with his hands, had done the only repair available to him: he had typed the truth, page by page, in the dark hours, and hidden the machine where only a clockmaker would ever find it.

He had not lived long enough to finish.

Elka stood in the doorway until the boy disappeared past the frozen fountain, then went inside and took the hymnal from under her shawl. The tavern checkpoint was hers to walk through. That had been argued about and lost — she insisted; Dominic had gone pale and called it madness — and so she put on her blackest wool, the widow's costume that made men's eyes slide off her like rain off slate, and she stepped out into the falling snow carrying God on her hip in a book with a false bottom.

* * *

**Dominic**

The tavern smelled of smoke, wet dog, and the particular sourness of men who have been drinking since the bridge closed and intend to continue. Dominic kept his eyes on the floorboards — worn smooth in the doorway, gouged near the tap — and counted his own footsteps the way he counted the tick of an escapement: even, patient, unremarkable.

*Clock oil, is it?*

The speaker was a big man with a beard full of crumbs, one of Vranek's winter men, hired muscle wrapped in a municipal coat. He hooked the sack off Dominic's shoulder with two fingers and held it up to the light like a jeweler judging a stone.

Oil sloshed. Dark, thick, smelling of animal fat and mineral spirit. Dominic made himself say, *The observatory clock needs servicing. It's been losing four minutes a week.* A lie so close to truth it hardly deserved the name — the clock had been losing time for years; he had simply stopped being the sort of man who let clocks lie.

The big man peered into the sack, found nothing but glass bottles and darkness, and dropped it back with a grunt. *Four minutes a week. Even the clock's crooked in this town.* He laughed at his own joke, and his friends laughed, and Dominic walked out into the snow with his heart hammering so hard he could feel it in his teeth.

One. One checkpoint passed. He told himself the shaking in his hands would stop by the toll house.

It did not stop. But he found, walking, that he did not want it to. The fear was proof he was awake.

* * *

**Elka**

In the tavern doorway she was nobody, exactly as designed. Widows were furniture in Vell; men looked through them the way they looked through curtains. She clutched the hymnal to her chest and murmured that she was going to light candles at the chapel for the souls of the drowned — there had been a drowning on the river two summers ago, still good currency for grief — and the big man by the door actually stepped aside for her, and touched his cap.

Inside the book, fifteen pages naming Governor Vranek as a thief pressed against her heartbeat like a second heart.

She almost pitied the guard. He was perhaps twenty-five, and he held the door for her with the clumsy courtesy of a boy raised by mothers, and she thought: this is how it works. Not monsters. Boys holding doors for widows in the morning, and burning papers in the afternoon, and never once seeing the line between the two. Power corrupted quietly, the way damp rots a beam — no drama, no crack of collapse, just wood becoming something else, slowly, while everyone kept living in the house.

She crossed herself at the chapel for real, because her hands wanted something true to do, and prayed — not for safety. For steadiness.

* * *

**Dominic**

At the toll house the wind came off the gorge and cut sideways, carrying ice grains that stung like thrown sand. The guard there was thorough in the wrong way: he opened crates, shook flour sacks, ran his knife along the lining of a traveling salesman's coat, and waved Dominic through without a glance, because a boy with a sack of oil and a toolbox was scenery.

But the third checkpoint was not a barrier at all. It was a warm room.

Havel, the governor's clerk, received him in the little stone office beside the last house before the bridge, and offered him tea, and asked after his master's health, and spoke in the soft, unhurried voice of a man who reads everything and signs very little. On the desk between them lay a pocket watch, open, its movement exposed — a confiscated thing, or a thing awaiting repair, Dominic couldn't tell. Havel turned it toward him with one finger.

*Your work?* he said. *This escapement. The finish on the pallet stones. I've seen it before. In the magistrate's clock, when I inventoried his effects.* He let that sit in the steam between them. *You serviced Tomas Ishikawa's clock for years. His widow's too. You're a boy who takes care of things other people are afraid to touch.*

Dominic's mouth had gone dry as old shellac. Under the counter, inside the oil sack, fourteen pages weighed nothing and everything. He heard himself say, *I take care of clocks, sir. Whatever's inside them is usually just dust.*

Havel looked at him for a long moment. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the river ground its teeth under the ice. The clerk's hand rested on the open watch, thumb moving once across the balance wheel, and Dominic understood that Havel knew — not the details, perhaps, but the shape of it, the weight in the room, the way a man who reads everything eventually reads the thing in front of him. Havel could end it here with a word. A career made in an afternoon. A promotion, a coat with better buttons, a place at the governor's right hand.

Or he could do the smaller, stranger thing.

*Four minutes slow,* Havel said quietly, closing the watch. *The whole town, running four minutes behind the truth, and calling it the time.* He stood, buttoned his coat, and opened the door onto the white roaring cold of the bridge road. *Go on, then. Mind the ice. It doesn't forgive.*

Dominic walked out. He did not run until the office door was shut behind him, and then he ran, across the frozen ruts and past the last lamppost, the sack banging against his hip, the bridge rising ahead of him out of the snow-fog — iron ribs, hanging frost, the far bank a grey suggestion of post office and escape.

Halfway across, he stopped.

A figure stood at the far end, where the road narrowed onto solid ground. Broad-shouldered, fur-collared, unmistakable even through the fog of snow. The governor had come out himself, into the cold, to stand at the mouth of the only road in or out of Vell — and he was watching Dominic come, and he was smiling.
