## 1. The Weight of Small Names

The tide went out at dawn, and Tamsin Vane went out with it.

They waded into the grey shallows before the sun had fully cleared the headland, breath held against the cold that came up through the water like a hand closing around the ankles. The oyster beds lay beyond the second sandbar, marked by crooked stakes the company had driven into the muck two generations ago. The stakes leaned now. Everything in Greyharbour leaned — the wharves, the drying sheds, the old men who'd spent their youth bent over nets. Only the company's counting house stood straight, its whitewashed walls and green shutters gleaming above the town like a face turned politely away.

Tamsin dove.

The water closed over them, and the world narrowed to the slap of their own pulse. Down among the beds they worked by touch, fingers reading the shells in the dark, prying loose only what the quota demanded. Their mother had taught them to hold their breath so long the horizon seemed a rumor — *breathe with the sea, little fish, not against it* — but their mother was three years drowned, and the sea had stopped answering questions.

They surfaced with a basket heavy against their hip, gasping, and dove again. And again. The cold burned away. The work became a kind of prayer without words: fill the basket, haul it to the skiff, dive again. Useful. That was the whole of a diver's life, and Tamsin had been useful since the age of seven, when the Meridian Trading Company first wrote their name into an indenture ledger with a flourish of green ink — *T. Vane, term of fourteen years, value assessed at eleven shillings.*

Eleven shillings. They could still see the figure. Some nights it was the last thing they saw before sleep.

By midmorning the light had gone from pewter to pale gold, and Tamsin sat on the wharf mending nets with a bone shuttle, fingers moving through patterns they didn't need to think about. Around them the harbor worked itself awake. Gulls screamed over the fish stalls. A cooper's hammer rang somewhere behind the salt houses. Old Brannigan argued with his grandson about the price of eelgrass, as he did every morning, in words that never changed.

Tamsin watched everything and spoke to no one. That was the trick of surviving an indenture: be useful enough to keep, invisible enough not to be noticed keeping. They had learned it young, learned it the way other children learned prayers.

*There's the little Vane,* said no one, ever. But eyes followed them anyway — the foreman's eyes, tallying; the overseer's eyes, assessing. Property moved through a town the way a coin did, passed hand to hand, worn smooth by handling.

* * *

The Atlas kept his office on the second floor of the counting house, in a room that smelled of lamp oil, sealing wax, and the particular dust of old paper — a smell Tamsin loved the way some people loved bread.

He was called the Atlas because he carried the whole company in his head. Thirty-one years of entries: every cargo manifest, every wage docked, every diver contracted, injured, sold, or lost. Other clerks consulted books. The Atlas consulted himself, and he was never wrong. Master Corvel, the factor, liked to demonstrate him to visiting merchants the way a man shows off a trained dog. *Ask him anything. Go on. The Pearl run of '61? Crew losses, spring of '58?* And the Atlas would close his milky eyes for a moment and recite figures in a dry paper voice, exact to the farthing, while the merchants laughed in delighted disbelief.

His real name was Aldous Wren. Almost nobody remembered it.

Tamsin copied for him four afternoons a week — a task assigned years ago and never revoked, because Tamsin's hand was small and neat and cost the company nothing beyond the indenture it already owned. They sat across the scarred oak desk from him now, transcribing entries from loose slips into the great bound ledgers while the Atlas read aloud, quill scratching, afternoon light crawling slowly across the floorboards.

"Diver's wage, Maren Kell, less breakage." He paused, and Tamsin heard the pause — a small hitch, like a stitch dropped. "Less *so-called* breakage," he corrected softly, and waited until Tamsin had written it exactly so.

They glanced up. The Atlas was looking at the window, not at them, his faded eyes fixed on the harbor.

"Your mother's entry is in volume nine," he said. "Page one hundred twelve."

Tamsin's quill stopped.

"I don't—" they began.

"You come every week," the Atlas said mildly, "and you have never once asked to see it. I find that more eloquent than asking." He turned back to the ledger open before him. "Loss of diver, Maren Vane, née Kell. Cause recorded: misadventure. Date, depth, boat assignment — all entered. All witnessed by none."

The gulls cried outside. Tamsin's heart was doing something strange and slow, like a bell struck underwater.

*Misadventure.* One word for a woman who could hold her breath longer than anyone in Greyharbour, who knew the beds better than the company's own charts. One word, and then the company had collected insurance on her loss — Tamsin had found that much out themselves, piecing together whispers: that the Meridian insured its divers like cargo, and that boats went out in weather they shouldn't, and that widows' petitions were refused at the door of this very building.

"The record is false," Tamsin said quietly. It was not a question, and they were surprised at the sound of their own voice saying it aloud, here, in the belly of the company.

The Atlas looked at them for a long moment. Then he opened volume nine without rising, from memory, to page one hundred twelve, and turned it around on the desk.

There she was. Their mother's name in green ink, the date, the single lying word. Below it, in smaller script, the payout figure — forty pounds — and beside that, initialled, the notation Tamsin would carry for the rest of their life: *hull dispatched despite objection of crew.*

*Hull dispatched despite objection of crew.*

"She objected," the Atlas said. "The whole crew objected. I know because I entered it, and then someone entered over me." His voice stayed level, dry as paper. But his hands, folded on the desk, had gone white at the knuckles. "I am an old man, Tamsin. My memory is the only place your mother's truth still lives. Memory is not evidence. It can be dismissed, denied, buried with me. But if the page and the memory stood together, in front of someone with the authority to make them permanent—"

He stopped himself. Shook his head. "Copy your lines, child."

Tamsin copied their lines. But when the Atlas dozed in his chair at dusk, as he always did, they stood before the shelf of bound volumes, and their hands shook so hard they could barely lift down volume nine. Page one hundred twelve. They pressed the page flat and copied it — every line, every figure, every damning initialled notation — in their small neat hand onto a scrap of company paper, folding it small, smaller, smallest.

Then they went home to the loft above the net shed, knelt in the corner where the floorboard had worked loose for years, and hid their mother's death beneath the floor, wrapped in oilcloth, next to the one thing of hers they owned outright: a single imperfect pearl, clouded, worthless, beautiful.

* * *

Autumn came down the coast like a slow tide. The herring ran thin. Men stood in knots at the harbor mouth speaking low, and there was a new quality to their silences. Even the gulls seemed to scream differently — shorter, sharper, as if the air itself had grown stingy.

Tamsin noticed things. Noticing was their trade as much as copying. They noticed the company's ships staying at anchor, unwilling to risk deep water. They noticed Master Corvel burning papers in the counting house yard one evening, and how he posted a watchman afterward, which told Tamsin the burning had not been enough. They noticed that the price of flour doubled in a week and nobody complained about it loudly, because complaining loudly had begun to feel dangerous.

And on the last morning of the herring season, standing knee-deep in the shallows with an empty basket, Tamsin looked up at the horizon and saw the shapes.

Three of them at first. Then six. Then more, sliding out of the sea-haze in a long dark line, square-rigged and gun-black, flying colors Tamsin knew only from the corners of company maps — the colors of the Republic's navy, come up the coast with the war trailing behind it like netting behind a boat.

They stood very still in the cold water and counted ships until counting was pointless. Behind them, across the whole curve of the harbor, Greyharbour had gone silent — every soul on the shore, faces turned seaward, watching the war arrive.

Tamsin thought of the oilcloth bundle under the floorboard. Of page one hundred twelve. Of an old man whose head held thirty-one years of drowned names.

The lead warship turned into the wind, deliberate as a hand reaching for a ledger, and began to come about toward the harbor mouth.
