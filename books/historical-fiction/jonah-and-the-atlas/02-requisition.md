## 2. Requisition

The warships took three days to come close enough to matter, and in that time Greyharbour learned to be quiet.

Tamsin felt the change first in the water. The herring had gone, frightened off by anchors and hull-sound, and where the herring went, so went every diver's morning. Instead of diving, they were set to hauling crates of company paper up from the Meridian's cellar, stacking it in the counting-house under the eyes of soldiers who wore grey instead of blue and who looked at the town the way a man looks at a room he intends to sweep.

Tamsin kept their head down and their hands moving. That was the whole art of surviving an indenture: be useful enough to keep, invisible enough to forget. They had been practicing since they were seven years old, when their mother waded out into cold green water for the last time and came back as an entry in a ledger — *Vane, M., lost at depth, no salvage recovered, wages settled* — and Tamsin had learned that names were dangerous things. Better to be *the Vane girl*, *the diver's brat*, *you there, hold this rope*. A small name weighed nothing. It could be carried anywhere without strain.

They carried crates now. The counting-house smelled of dust and lamp oil, and the Atlas sat amid the mountain of ledgers like a king enthroned on rubble, reading each volume before the soldiers stacked it. He was older than the building, it seemed; his spine had curved into a permanent question mark over a desk, and his hands trembled unless they held a pen. The divers' children called him the Atlas because he was said to carry the whole company in his head — every cargo, every wage, every debt. Tamsin had copied entries for him for four years, and he had never once forgotten anything they told him, including things Tamsin wished he'd forget.

"Tamsin." He didn't look up. "The third crate. There's a ledger with a cracked spine, brown leather. Bring it."

"How do you know what's in the crates?"

"Because I know what I put in them." He opened the book when it came, wet his thumb, turned pages with the tenderness of a man handling relics. "Thirty-one years," he murmured. "Thirty-one years of other people's money. And they'll call it nothing."

"Call what nothing?"

He looked up then. His eyes were milky at the rims but sharp at the center, and they fixed on Tamsin the way a hook fixes on a lip.

"The insurance ledgers," he said quietly. "You've copied from them. You know the columns. *Loss at depth.* Do you know how many times those words appear?"

Tamsin's hands went still on the crate. Below the floorboards of their rented room, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the page they had stolen two winters ago — their mother's death entry, torn out whole, because Tamsin could not bear that the company owned even her dying. They had never told anyone.

"Some," they said carefully.

"Nineteen in eleven years. Nineteen divers drowned in waters too deep for pearl, sent down past safe measure because the insurers paid more per lost life than per lost season." His voice stayed level, clerkly, as if he were dictating an inventory. "I checked the arithmetic myself, when I was young enough to believe checking mattered. The premiums came in. The divers did not."

The door banged open and a lieutenant came in, all polished buttons and boredom, and the Atlas closed his book and became furniture again. But Tamsin stood there with the weight of nineteen names pressing through their ribs, and one of them was *Vane, M.*

*No salvage recovered. Wages settled.*

Settled. Like a debt. Like she had been a column sum.

* * *

The order came down on the fourth day, read aloud in the market square by a captain with a voice like a landslide: the Meridian Trading Company was dissolved by right of conquest, its assets seized, its records confiscated as evidence against its directors. A cheer went up — half the town owed the company money and grudges in equal measure — and then the cheer faltered, because the second part followed the first: the archives would be processed for matters of *commercial and military relevance*. All else disposed of.

Tamsin learned what *disposed of* meant on the fifth day, watching soldiers feed a brazier behind the counting-house. Not ledgers yet. Nets, broken gear, old contracts gone brittle. But the pile waiting beside the brazier included a stack of thin volumes with grey cloth spines, and Tamsin knew those spines. They had spent four years copying from them. Diver rolls. Wage books. Death records.

"You can't burn those," someone said, and Tamsin turned to see Widow Cress — Marta Cress, whose husband Dell had gone down off the Sallow Rocks two springs past, leaving her with three children and a settlement notice she used as a bookmark in her Bible. She stood in front of the brazier with her arms crossed like a woman barring a gate.

A corporal looked at her the way you look at weather. "These are company records, mistress. Not your concern."

"Dell Cress is my concern. He's in one of those books."

"Then he'll be warm tonight." Someone laughed. The corporal didn't; he simply waited, and eventually Widow Cress's arms came down and she stepped aside, because there is only so long a body can bar a gate against armed men, and everyone in Greyharbour knew the mathematics of that.

Tamsin stood at the corner of the counting-house with a coil of rope in their hands, being useful and invisible, and watched. And hated themselves for watching. The smoke rose straight in the windless air. Somewhere in that fire, or soon to be in it, was proof — nineteen deaths bought and paid for, including one named for a saint of lost sailors, including one who had left behind a child with her own dark hair and her habit of counting breaths underwater.

They should have said something. They had a tongue. They had lungs.

They stayed at the corner.

* * *

That night the Atlas found them — or let them find him; with him it was hard to tell which way a thing moved. He was sitting on the harbor steps in the last light, and he patted the stone beside him, and Tamsin sat, because refusing the Atlas was not something their body knew how to do.

"They're burning the diver rolls tomorrow," he said. No preamble. The Atlas considered preambles a waste of ink. "The officers want the cargo fraud and the smuggling evidence. That serves their war. Nineteen drowned divers serve nothing, so into the fire it goes."

"There's nothing anyone can do." Tamsin heard their own voice, flat and careful, a servant's voice. "The army takes what it wants."

"The historians are coming. Victory's historians, writing the official account of the requisition. They have the power to enter testimony into the sealed record — and once it's entered, it stands. Even the army can't burn what's written into the official history." He turned to look at them, and the lamplight caught his spectacles and made two small moons of his eyes. "I can testify. Every page, Tamsin. Every column, every name, every date. Thirty-one years. It's all in here." He tapped his temple with one trembling finger. "It has been for years. I memorized the ledgers as I entered them — started as a young man's game, ended as an old man's religion. I am the ledger now."

Tamsin stared out at the black water. Their heart was doing something painful and slow, like a rope going taut.

"What do you want from me?"

"A witness. Testimony alone is a voice in a room. Testimony corroborated by an original document — that is evidence. And you have a document." He said it gently, which was worse. "I've known about the loose floorboard for two years, child. I also know what's under it. I copy everything, remember. Including faces, including thefts I chose not to report."

Tamsin was on their feet before they knew it, three steps away, fists clenched. "You'd have me walk into a requisitioned archive and hand my name to an army officer? To a historian? People who notice me don't forget me, old man. The company notices a person, the person gets sold, or flogged, or sent down deep with a bad rope and *wages settled* written after. Invisibility has kept me alive my whole life. You're asking me to die of conscience like your divers did."

"I'm asking you to live as something more than un-noticeable," the Atlas said. "There's a difference between surviving and being alive. I had hoped you'd learn it before I got too old to demonstrate."

"Well, I haven't." Tamsin's voice cracked on it. "Find another witness."

"There is no other witness. There's you, and there's me, and there's tomorrow morning." He rose slowly, joints ticking like a ship in a swell, and looked at them a long moment — not angry. Sad. That was unbearable; anger would have been easier to carry. "Sleep on it. But not too long, child. The fire doesn't sleep."

He left. Tamsin stayed on the steps until the cold came up through the stone, doing sums of their own: the odds of survival against the odds of decency, the weight of a small name against the weight of *Vane, M., lost at depth*.

* * *

Near dawn, unable to sleep, Tamsin walked past the counting-house and stopped dead.

Widow Cress was at the officers' door again. She had dressed in her best — a faded blue dress, mended at every seam — and she held a paper in both hands like an offering: Dell's settlement notice, perhaps, or his marriage lines, some scrap to prove he had existed. An adjutant stood in the doorway blocking it entirely, a wall in uniform.

"He died in their water," she was saying, loud enough to reach the street. "In *their* water, working for *their* pearls, and you're going to burn the only place his name is written—"

"Madam, the tribunal's schedule does not include—"

"He has a *name*."

"Noted. Good day."

The door closed. She stood there a moment, and then she pressed the paper to her mouth, and her shoulders shook, and no one in the street did anything at all. Neither did Tamsin. Tamsin stood in the shadow of the fish racks, invisible, useful to no one, and felt the last of their refusal go soft and rotten inside them.

She turned away from the door and saw them. Red-eyed, furious, she crossed the street and stopped an arm's length off.

"You work for the Atlas," she said. "The old clerk. They say he remembers everything."

Tamsin's mouth was dry. "Yes."

"Then tell him—" Her voice broke and rebuilt itself. "Tell him if he's going to speak, to speak *today*. Before they burn it."

Tamsin looked past her, to the counting-house yard, where the brazier waited cold and patient, and beside it the stack of grey-spined volumes, and above them the window where the historians would take their seats when the sun was fully up.

The loose floorboard was a twenty-minute walk away. The oilcloth bundle under it weighed almost nothing at all.

Tamsin began to run.
