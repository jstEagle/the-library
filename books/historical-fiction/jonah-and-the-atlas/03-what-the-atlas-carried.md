## 3. What the Atlas Carried

They took the Atlas at low tide, when the harbour lay flat as hammered tin and the whole town could watch without pretending not to.

Tamsin was mending a requisitioned net on the customs-house steps — the soldiers had decided idle hands were suspicious hands, so now everyone worked for the army's benefit and no one was paid for it — when the squad came marching him down Rope Street. He looked smaller out of his chair. Without his ledgers he seemed to be made mostly of coat, a thin man swimming in grey wool, his wrists like knotted twine above the irons. But he walked slowly, deliberately, the way he did everything, and when he passed Tamsin he turned his head. His eyes, naked without the spectacles, found them among the crowd with terrible accuracy.

He did not call out. He knew better than to spend their name in the open air. He only mouthed something, two words, and Tamsin read them off his lips the way they read the tide tables: *the page*.

Then the soldiers turned him up toward the requisitioned archive — the Meridian Company's own counting house, its brass plaque pried off and replaced by a board stenciled HISTORICAL COMMISSION — and the door closed on him like a lid.

"Obstruction," the sergeant said to no one in particular, the way you'd report weather. "Interfering with commissioned business."

Beside Tamsin, a woman made a sound that wasn't quite a word. Maren Cole. Widow of Diver Jossen Cole, drowned 14th of October, six years past — Tamsin knew the date because they had copied it, once, into the margin of a ledger page, in the Atlas's cramped brown hand, while he recited it from memory: *Jossen Cole, boat's tender's report says rope fouled, but the insurance schedule entered three days prior lists hull loss coverage doubled for crew fatality.* She had come every day since the requisition to the counting house door, and every day they turned her away. *Irrelevant records. Irrelevant grief.*

She was turned away again that morning. Tamsin watched her stand on the steps arguing with a soldier young enough to be her son, watched the soldier's face go through embarrassment and harden into duty, and watched her walk back down into the town that had no more use for its drowned.

Tamsin looked at the net in their hands. Looked at the knots. Useful and invisible, useful and invisible — that had kept them alive for nineteen years, and it would keep them alive today, and next week, and always, right up until the day the historians burned the diver ledgers in a barrel behind the counting house and everything their mother had been went up as smoke. Survival-by-invisibility. The Atlas had spent his whole life being invisible too, and look where it had gotten him. In irons. In a building full of his life's work, with men who meant to make ash of it.

Tamsin put down the net.

Their hands were shaking. They noticed this with a strange detachment, as if the hands belonged to someone braver.

*The page.*

The floorboard under their cot at the indenture barracks came up without protest. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp the way the Atlas had taught them to wrap anything worth keeping, lay the record: *Diver Maren Vane, lost with cargo, 9th of June. No body recovered. Salvage claim settled. Insurance indemnity paid to Meridian Trading Company, sum of £340.* Their mother, priced. Their mother, profitable. Below it, in different ink, added later in the Atlas's hand: *Schedule shows diver-loss coverage purchased eleven days before sailing. Ask why.*

Tamsin held the paper and understood, finally, what the Atlas had been doing all those years — every name he'd memorized, every marginal note, every quiet question pressed between columns like flowers in a book. He hadn't been keeping accounts. He'd been keeping evidence, waiting for someone to ask.

Nobody had asked for thirty years. So now Tamsin would do the asking.

* * *

Maren Cole opened her door before Tamsin finished knocking, which told Tamsin she had been standing behind it all evening.

"You're the Sterling boy." Not quite an accusation. "The clerk's shadow."

"I need your help getting into the counting house."

Maren looked at them for a long moment. Lamplight from inside cut her face into halves. "They'll hang you."

"They're going to burn the ledgers tomorrow. I heard the quartermaster say so — the diver records especially, 'irrelevant to naval matters.'" Tamsin's voice came out steadier than expected. "The Atlas is in there. He has every page in his head, every name, every number, thirty years of it. If we can get him before a historian, in front of witnesses—"

"Why you?" Maren said. "Why would they listen to an indentured child?"

"Because I'm not going to hide." It surprised them, saying it. It felt like setting down a weight they had carried so long they'd mistaken it for their spine. "I've been invisible my whole life, mistress. Invisible didn't save my mother. It won't save anyone. Someone has to stand where they can see us and say it out loud."

Maren Cole studied them a moment longer. Then she reached inside the door and took down her husband's old oilskin coat, still stiff with salt.

"The sentries change at the second bell," she said. "My Jossen tended boats for the company eleven years. I know which window sticks."

* * *

The counting house at night smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the Atlas's ink. Soldiers had stacked the ledgers in barrels by the far wall, ready for burning, and seeing them handled like scrap — the Atlas's life, the divers' lives, spines cracked, pages dog-eared by boots — filled Tamsin with a cold fury that made the lockpicking easier. Maren stood watch at the stuck window, humming softly, an old hauling-song, the kind that carries over water.

Tamsin found the Atlas in a locked records room, chained to a ring in the floor like contraband, spectacles gone, squinting at nothing. When the lantern light touched him his face went through astonishment and grief in equal measure.

"Tamsin." His voice was a ruin. "Child, no. You should not be here."

"You said *the page*." They knelt, working at the chain lock with a file from Maren's kit. "I brought it."

"You brought—" He stopped. Something moved across his face that Tamsin had never seen there: hope, unguarded, terrible in its size. "Thirty-one years I have carried that ledger in my head. Every column. Every name. Do you know what it is to remember four hundred and twelve drowned people because no one else would?" His hands, freed, trembled as Tamsin pressed the oilcloth bundle into them. He unwrapped it and read by touch, by memory, barely needing the light. "Maren Vane. Ninth of June. Coverage purchased eleven days prior. I wrote *ask why* in the margin and waited thirty years for someone to ask."

"I'm asking," Tamsin said. "Come on. We're going to ask very loudly."

They almost made the stairs. Then lanterns bloomed in the hall below, and boots, and a voice like a slammed drawer: "Counting house! Secure it!"

Soldiers filled the doorway. Behind them, wrapped in a dressing gown over his uniform at one in the morning, came Commission Historian Edvard Lune — a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and tired, honest eyes, which was somehow worse than cruel ones. He looked at the Atlas, at Tamsin, at the page in the old man's hands.

"Well," Lune said quietly. "Either thieves, or the most important people in Greyharbour tonight."

* * *

They gave their testimony in the archive hall while dawn came up grey behind the shutters, because Lune — whatever else he was — was a scholar first, and the page had gone into his hands like scripture into a believer's. He cross-checked it against the barrels. He questioned the Atlas for two hours without sitting down, and the Atlas, hoarse and swaying, gave names, dates, sums, hull numbers, tide tables, the whole buried architecture of the drownings, while a commission scribe wrote as fast as his hand could move.

The sergeant argued for arrests. The barrels argued louder — Lune kept glancing at them, at the fire order lying on his desk stamped with the army's seal, at the clock crawling toward the hour he was ordered to destroy it all.

When soldiers came to seal the doors with lead and wax, Lune stopped them long enough for the deposition's final line. The Atlas gripped Tamsin's wrist, his hand ice-cold and iron-firm.

"Witnessed and attested," he said to the scribe, "by Silas Merrick, clerk of accounts, and by—" he looked at Tamsin, waiting, giving them the choice like a gift.

Tamsin stepped forward into the lantern light, where everyone could see.

"Tamsin Vane," they said, loud enough to reach the sealed doors. "Diver's child. Witness."

The wax pooled. The lead pressed shut. And through the closing gap, Tamsin saw the historian slide one thin ledger — not into the barrel, but into his own coat.

Then the doors boomed, and outside, Maren Cole was already shouting the news into the waking street.
