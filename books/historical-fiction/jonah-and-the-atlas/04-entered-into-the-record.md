## 4. Entered into the Record

The Atlas died on a Tuesday, in the third week of October, with rain coming off the sea in grey sheets and the whole of Greyharbour smelling of wet rope and woodsmoke.

I was not there when it happened. I had gone back to the Meridian counting-house — no longer requisitioned, merely abandoned, its ledgers carted away in army waggons like so much contraband grain — to stand one last time in the room where I had spent seven years copying numbers I was never meant to understand. The floorboard where my mother's death record had hidden for six years lay loose under my hand. The space beneath it was empty now, and would stay empty. The page lived in the historians' strongbox, in the archive tent at the head of the quay, filed under a number instead of a name.

That was the thing about records. They could save you and unmake you in the same motion.

Maren found me there. She came in without knocking, because Maren had never knocked on anything in her life, and stood dripping in the doorway with her shawl pulled over her head.

"He's asking for you," she said. "Or he was. Half an hour ago."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, past the empty hiding place, into the earth beneath Greyharbour. "Asking?"

She didn't answer, which was its own answer. We ran.

He was still alive when we reached his rooms above the chandler's shop — a single chamber he had rented for thirty-one years, furnished with a bed, a chair, and four walls of shelving that held not books but memory: bundles of correspondence, old tide tables, a dried seahorse Maren's daughter had given him a decade ago. He lay propped against pillows gone yellow, his great bulk diminished, his hands — those hands that had turned three thousand pages into something no fire could touch — quiet on the blanket.

"Tamsin." His voice was small, but his eyes were exactly as they had always been: patient, amused, seeing more than he let on. "You look terrible."

"You look worse," I said, and sat on the edge of the bed because my legs had stopped consulting me.

"I have been informed by the surgeon that my heart has concluded its business." He said it the way he announced ledger balances. "Which is inconvenient. There is a final entry I wish to make."

"Don't," I said. "Don't make it a joke."

"Everything is a ledger, child. I have simply been keeping mine longer than most." He turned his head toward the window, where the rain ran down the glass in silver threads. "They read the names yesterday. Did you hear them?"

I had. The whole town had. From the steps of the archive tent, the junior historian had read aloud two hundred and forty-three names — the divers lost to the Meridian's careening grounds, the winter beds, the insurance settlements paid out to the company for deaths the company itself had arranged. My mother's name among them. *Sera Vane, of the *Cormorant* crew, entered as drowned by misadventure, 14th of March.* Entered now, in the victors' own hand, as what she was: murdered for profit.

"They got my mother right," I said. "Her birthday. They had her birthday wrong for six years in your ledger. Off by a month."

"The copyist's error," the Atlas said gravely. "A notorious one. Unreliable fellow. In love with the wrong entries." His mouth twitched. Then the twitch faded, and what was left in his face was nothing but truth. "Tamsin. I want you to take the spectacles. The second pair. In the drawer."

"I don't want—"

"It is not a request. It is a bequest, and I will haunt this town if you refuse. I have memorized enough of it to manage it." He waited until I opened the drawer and closed my hand around the case without looking at it. "Good. Now listen. The historians will dissolve the company within the fortnight. When they do, every indenture it holds dissolves with it. Yours included. You will be told you are free, as though freedom were theirs to grant." His breath caught; he rode through it. "It is not theirs. It never was. You were free the day you chose to be seen. Do you understand me? The paper only catches up."

I could not speak. So I nodded, and he watched me nod, and seemed satisfied, the way he always had when my sums balanced.

"Maren," he said then, and she leaned in from where she stood at the foot of the bed, jaw set like a woman refusing to cry in front of witnesses. "The widow's pension funds. Third shelf, the tin marked *candle wicks*. The company never knew I kept it. It's yours now. Distribute it as you see fit. You have been distributing justice already; you may as well do it with coin."

"You stubborn old anchor," Maren said, voice cracking on the last word.

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "That too."

He died two days later, in his sleep, with the tide going out. They buried him on the hill above the harbour, and half of Greyharbour came, which would have mortified him and pleased him in equal measure. Maren put the dried seahorse in the ground with him. I put nothing, because everything he had given me I was carrying: the page, the name, the spectacles heavy in my coat pocket like a second heart.

* * *

The dissolution was signed on the first of November, read out on the quay in a wind that tore at the herald's proclamation. The Meridian Trading Company — its charters, its debts, its claims upon human beings — ceased to exist. A clerk from the historians' retinue moved down the line of indentured workers with a list, striking names through one by one. Freed. Freed. Freed. As if the word were a stamp, as if we had not all been standing there whole and unfree for years.

When he reached me, he frowned at the ledger. "Vane. Tamsin. Indentured, age nine, term of—"

"That's struck through now?" I asked.

"Struck through," he confirmed, and moved on.

Just like that. Seven years of being property, cancelled with a stroke of ink. I stood on the quay and felt precisely nothing, and then felt everything, all of it arriving at once like a wave you don't see until it has already broken over you. Maren's arm came around my shoulders. Her daughter Brinice grabbed my hand and held it, gap-toothed and solemn, the way you hold someone who might otherwise blow away.

But it was not finished. Not quite.

Senior Historian Edvane received petitioners in the archive tent each morning, and on the last morning before the archives were sealed — wax poured, crates nailed, history closed like a door — I stood in the queue with the page in my satchel and my pulse hammering in my ears the way it used to before a dive, before the moment you let the water take you.

When my turn came, I did not wait to be asked.

"My mother's record is wrong," I said. "In your official account. Not the death — you have the death right now. But it says *survived by no one of record*. That's not true. She was survived by me. And I am of record now, or I mean to be."

Edvane looked up. He was a tired man with ink-stained fingers and the particular patience of someone who had listened to five hundred petitioners and could afford to truly hear perhaps one. He heard me. I watched him decide to.

"State your mother's full name," he said. "For the record."

"Sera Vane. Born Sera Kellish, twenty-second of January, in Saltpan Row, Greyharbour. Pearl diver, eleven years in the company's service. Murdered on the fourteenth of March, six years ago, by the order of men who profited from her drowning." My voice held. I made it hold. "And I am her child. Tamsin Sera Vane. Free. Witness. Entered into the record by my own word, and by the testimony of Aldous Wren, clerk of the Meridian for thirty-one years, who carried every page of their crimes in his memory so that no fire and no officer and no convenient forgetting could ever put them out."

Edvane wrote. I watched him write it — my mother's name, then mine, in the fair hand of the victors' official history, permanent as stone, and I thought: *the Atlas would check your figures, sir, and find them finally correct.*

The quill stopped. "Entered," he said, and stamped the page, and it was done.

* * *

We left Greyharbour on a bright cold morning, three days later. Maren had cousins up the coast in Waycross who needed hands for the winter nets, and Brinice had packed everything she owned into one bundle and spent an hour convincing us it was heavier than it looked. The pension tin sat in Maren's pack. The road north followed the cliffs, and the sea below was the same grey-green it had always been, indifferent and enormous, holding whatever it held.

At the top of the cliff road I stopped and looked back once. The harbour lay below with its masts and its smoke, small as something drawn on a map. Somewhere down there was a loose floorboard in an empty room, and a grave on the hill with an anchor-shaped stone Maren had commissioned, and two hundred and forty-three names that would outlive every man who tried to drown them.

I took the spectacles from my pocket, put them on, and found that the world through Aldous Wren's spare lenses was slightly blurred at the edges and perfectly clear at the centre, which seemed exactly right. I did not need them to see. I wore them anyway. Some things you carry because they are useful, and some things you carry because they are true.

Brinice tugged my sleeve. "Are you coming?"

"I'm coming," I said, and turned north, a witness with no master, my mother's name and my own stitched somewhere safe into the world's long account — and the road ahead open, unwritten, waiting for whatever I would enter into it next.
