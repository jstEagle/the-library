## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The satchel weighed nothing and everything, depending on how Silas thought about it. Fourteen hundred grams of leather and paper, if he was honest — he had weighed it on the customs scale at the depot in Vellany, an act of pure superstition, since the manifest already said what it weighed. But a man carrying sealed dispatches through a country eating itself alive wants to know the number. Wants to hold something countable.

Everything else about the job was uncountable.

He boarded the mail coach at the Vellany gate before dawn, in the blue hour when the lamplighter was still working his way down the boulevard and the snow that had been forecast for three days finally decided to arrive. The coachman, a broad man named Grell with a beard like frozen tar, checked his papers without reading them. That was the first wrong thing. Officials always read. Men who didn't read were either too stupid to be dangerous or so dangerous they didn't need to.

Silas's papers said he was Albrecht Fenner, journeyman clockmaker, licensed for repair work in the coastal districts, carrying tools of trade. The false name sat on him the way a coat sits on a man who has lost weight — close enough to pass, loose enough to notice in a mirror. He had worn worse.

His actual tools were real. That mattered. A man who couldn't take apart the cover story he was hiding inside deserved to be caught. In the bottom of his kit, beneath the pin vices and the oilers and the brass tweezers wrapped in flannel, lay the satchel's twin, empty, with a customs stamp from a district he'd never visited. If anyone demanded to see what a clock repairman carried, they'd get a convincing case of nothing. The real satchel rode against his ribs, under his coat, strapped where a man might carry a brace of books or a hidden hunger.

The strap left a line across his chest he could feel through his shirt. He had begun, in the last week, to think of it as a seam. As though he too had a case back and someone, somewhere, held the key.

* * *

The high road out of Vellany climbed through terraced vineyards gone grey and bare, then into pine, then into a country where pine gave up and only rock and weather remained. The republic these dispatches belonged to had maybe a season left — everyone from the bootblack to the archbishop had an opinion on the date — but the mountains didn't know it. The mountains had outlived every republic ever drawn on their slopes. They would outlive this one while the ink on its surrender was still tacky.

Silas knew the cargo by weight and shape, not content. That was doctrine. A courier carried sealed things; a courier who knew what he carried became a target with opinions. He had memorized the handling orders instead: three seals, chancery wax over lead, broken by authorized hands only, penalty named in full. He recited the penalties to himself sometimes the way other men said prayers. It focused the mind.

Four passengers besides himself. An agronomist going home to a farm that probably no longer existed, judging by how he talked about it. Two sisters in matching wool who slept against each other like parentheses. And a young clerk of some kind who asked questions the way children ask — constantly, without strategy. What did Silas repair? Clocks. Did he repair the big ones, tower clocks? Yes. Had he repaired the cathedral clock in Vellany? No. Why not?

"Because," Silas said, "it doesn't want repairing. It wants grieving. I don't do grief."

The clerk laughed uncertainly and wrote something down. Everyone wrote something down now. The whole country had become clerks recording the death of their own employer.

Grell the coachman watched all of it from his box, and watched more than the road.

* * *

Silas catalogued people the way he catalogued movements in a watch — by function, fit, and wear. It wasn't cynicism; it was method. Trust was a thing you extended to routines, because routines had been tested. A train that departed at six had carried a thousand strangers safely; a stranger's smile had carried no one anywhere. So he trusted the schedule, the relay stations, the standard lies in his papers, and he trusted no face at all.

By the second day he had Grell filed under *problem*.

It began small. A glance in the coachman's small square mirror, angled not at the road behind but at Silas's chest, where the coat hung heavy on one side. Then a change in routine: Grell took the midday rest at a waystation forty minutes early, which meant arriving before the kitchen did, which meant standing around in the yard with time on his hands and eyes free to wander. On the third morning, outside the last relay before the pass, Silas came out of the stable and found Grell with a hand resting on the coach door — on the bench where Silas's tool case sat — and the hand withdrew a half-second too slowly when their eyes met.

"You've a heavy kit for clocks," Grell said. "Most of your trade travels light. Small failures, small fees."

"Big clocks break too."

"Up here?" Grell spat into the frost. "Last tower within fifty miles is at the coast. Man comes over the pass in winter to fix a clock that isn't there." He shrugged, enormous, the gesture of a man moving furniture. "Not my business. I drive. That's all I am anymore, a driver."

That was exactly the sentence a man used when it was very much his business.

Silas smiled the smile he kept for this — modest, slightly dull, a craftsman's pride with nothing under it. "There's an inn past the pass. Halfway House. They have an old regulator in their hall, stopped these two years. The owner wrote to my master in the autumn. Winter rates are good rates, for a man without family." He let the last word sit there, plain and sad, until Grell had to look away first.

It was even true. There was an inn, there was a clock, there had been a letter. Silas had read that letter twice, once as a courier assessing a route and once as a man who remembered the inn from years ago and needed no excuse to go back. Both readings had unsettled him, for different reasons he declined to examine.

* * *

The snow thickened through the afternoon. Not the pretty kind — the industrial kind, the kind that came off the glacier fields sideways and erased the road ten meters at a time. Grell drove well; Silas granted him that much. Whatever else the man was, his hands knew the horses and the horses knew the mountain. The agronomist prayed quietly. The sisters held hands across the gap between them. The clerk asked no questions at all, which was the most alarming development of the day.

At the last rise before the bridge country, Grell reined in at a chapel-shelter and called a halt. Steam rose off the horses like breath off a wound. Inside the shelter, a stove burned low and someone had left a chalk scrawl on the wall: *THE PASS CLOSES WHEN IT CLOSES.*

Grell poured brandy from a flask and offered none around. He drank looking at Silas over the rim.

"I had a cousin drove courier run," he said. "Before the war. Good money, short life. You know what killed them? Not bandits. Not the enemy." He tapped his own temple. "Knowing. A courier who knows what he carries starts making decisions. Decisions are heavy. Heavy men drive slow."

"I'm a clockmaker, friend."

"Mm." Grell screwed the cap back on the flask. "Then tomorrow, when we make the bridge, you'll stand where I put you and touch nothing, and we'll have no trouble. And if the bridge is out—" he looked at the snow hammering the little window, and for one moment something almost human moved behind the calculation in his face, something that might have been pity or might have been arithmetic— "then we'll all be guests of the mountain together, won't we. And guests talk. Guests share burdens. Guests are just people, stuck in one house till spring."

Silas felt the strap against his ribs and thought, with a clarity that frightened him more than the threat had: *he knows there will be an inn. He has known the route ends there since the snows were forecast. This was never a question of whether.*

"Spring's a long way off," Silas said.

"It always is," said Grell, "the first time."

They drove on into the white. Behind them the chapel-shelter shrank to a smudge, then a memory, then nothing. Ahead, unseen but certain, the bridge waited — the season's last crossing, the iron spine of the pass — and beyond it the Halfway House with its stopped clock and its letter and whatever else it held that Silas had spent seven years not thinking about.

He reviewed his routines the way a priest reviews his sins. Papers consistent. Cover story boring. Tools genuine. Satchel warm against his body, three seals intact, fourteen hundred grams plus the weight of whatever was written inside that he had sworn not to know.

The coach tipped around a shoulder of rock and the wind hit them broadside, and far below, through a tear in the cloud, Silas saw it: the gorge, black and deep as a dropped thought, and spanning it, thin as a hair, the bridge.

Grell turned in his seat and looked back at it, and then at Silas, and smiled like a man greeting a solution.

The bridge, when they reached it twenty minutes later, was closed.
