## 2. Bridge Out

The snow began an hour before the bridge, which Silas Almeida would think about later, the way a man thinks about a door he might have shut.

It came down fine and dry at first, more grain than flake, hissing against the coach windows like sand thrown by a child. The mountains on either side of the road went from grey to white in the space of a conversation. The coachman, Hollar, stopped talking mid-sentence about a dog he'd once owned and didn't pick the thread up again. He drove with his shoulders up around his ears, and every few minutes his eyes left the road and found the mirror, and the mirror found Silas, and behind Silas sat the satchel, strapped flat against the luggage rack where a clock repairman's tools ought to be.

Silas kept his hands loose on his knees and let the man look. Curiosity was weather; you endured it.

"Bad year for an early fall," Hollar said finally.

"Every year's an early fall until it isn't."

Hollar laughed at that, or made the shape of a laugh. "They tell me you fix clocks."

"When there are clocks to fix."

"And when there aren't?"

Silas smiled the smile he'd practiced in front of hotel mirrors in three capitals — modest, slightly tired, the smile of a tradesman who earned his bread and didn't ask questions about other men's mantelpieces. "Then I wait for spring," he said, "like everyone else on this road."

That shut the man up, but not, Silas judged, for long. Hollar had been watching the satchel since the relay station at Kessel, where two soldiers had boarded, ridden twenty minutes in silence, and gotten off at a crossroads with no crossroad visible from the road. Someone had paid Hollar to notice things. The question was who, and how much, and whether the answer would still matter by the coast.

The dispatches were sealed. That was the whole architecture of his life: sealed, signed across the flap, countersigned, chained to protocol like a dog to a kennel run. Nine documents, wax the color of dried blood, and Silas did not know what any of them said. He had told himself for eleven months that this was a kind of cleanliness. You carry the thing; you do not become the thing. But the road to the coast was long, and a man alone with a secret he cannot read begins to feel that the secret is reading him.

The bridge announced itself as a sound before it was a sight: the river underneath, running black and fast through its gorge, loud as a train that never arrived. Then the coach came around the shoulder of the mountain and there it was — a span maybe sixty meters end to end, iron and timber, older than the republic Silas served, its far side already whitening under the snowfall.

A lantern swung at the near abutment. A man in a railway company coat stood under it, waving both arms, and the gesture had none of welcome in it.

Hollar reined in. Stood up on the box to look. Sat back down slowly, the way old men sit down when they've received bad news and want to finish receiving it privately.

"No," he said.

"What?" Silas put his head out the window. Snow came into his collar, shockingly cold, precise as a needle.

"Span's sagging." Hollar's voice had gone flat, professional, emptied of everything except the road. "See the dip? Mid-span. That's not snow load doing that, not yet. That's a truss gone. Could've gone this morning. Could go now." He turned and looked at Silas directly, no mirror this time, and for one unguarded moment Silas saw the calculation in the man's face laid open like a shop ledger: *one passenger, one satchel, one winter.* "We're done, friend. Season's closed."

* * *

The argument at the abutment lasted forty minutes and settled nothing, which Silas understood to be the argument's true function.

The railway man, whose name was Proch and whose misery was total, explained it four times. The span could take a man on foot, perhaps, walking the centerline, light and quick, if the centerline held. It could take nothing with wheels. There was no ferry — the river ate ferries in October. There was no road around below November. The telegraph line had come down in the first squall two days ago, somewhere up-valley, and no one had gone to look because no one was fool enough to be up here to look.

"So we turn back," Silas said.

"To where?" Proch spread his hands, and the snow filled them. "Kessel's a day and a half, if the pass at Dren holds, and Dren closes at the first serious accumulation, and this—" he tilted his head back, let flakes land on his face, "—this is becoming serious accumulation. The mail goes nowhere till thaw. Nobody goes anywhere till thaw. You want to ride six days of switchbacks to watch a snow gate shut in your face, that's your romance."

Silas did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since the dip in the bridge. The capital expected the courier in nine days. After that, silence. After silence, inquiry. After inquiry — well. He knew what ministries did with couriers who arrived empty-handed in spring. They asked what had happened to the intervening months, and they did not stop asking, and eventually the asking acquired a room and a lamp and men who were good at it.

And the dispatches crossed with him or not at all. That was doctrine. That was written down somewhere in a hand that had never seen this gorge.

"There's an inn," Hollar offered, not looking at either of them, addressing the snow. "Halfway House. Voss runs it. Two hours back along the lake road. I winter there myself, most years." A pause with weight in it. "Rooms are honest. Food is honest. Man can keep himself busy."

*Man can keep himself busy.* Silas heard the second sentence inside the sentence, the one Hollar thought he was too clever to say aloud: *and a man with a satchel full of paper has all winter to wonder what's in it.*

"Fine," Silas said, and hated how easily it came. Dread dressed as inconvenience; he could feel the costume on himself, hear it in his own reasonable voice. *Annoying. Regrettable. Merely logistics.* Underneath, something colder lay down and made itself comfortable. He was going to be stranded, with the papers, among strangers, for a season. Every plan he had was built on movement, and the world had just voted for stillness.

* * *

The Halfway House came out of the snowfall the way things do in dreams: first a yellow smear of lamplight, then gables, then the whole dark bulk of it with smoke standing straight up from the chimney in the windless cold. A sign creaked. Somewhere behind the inn, dogs took an interest and were called off.

Inside was heat and tallow-smell and the particular hush of a building holding more quiet than it needed. A big grey-bearded man — Voss, by the resemblance to the portrait over the bar, which meant the portrait was thirty years old and flattered nobody — took his coat without being asked. A boy of maybe fifteen fed the stove with the economical motions of someone who had done it five thousand times.

Three guests already sat in the common room, arranged at distances from each other that suggested diplomacy rather than chance.

Nearest the fire, a surveyor — Silas placed the profession from the calipers on the table and the mud on the boots, mud that had no business existing in fresh snow, meaning the man had been poking around the gorge — a compact, restless figure drinking brandy at eleven in the morning with the air of a man conducting research. His name, once produced, was Brandt, and his handshake was a test you were being graded on.

"Stranded?" Brandt said, with relish. "Welcome to the tomb. We're very cozy. The food is excellent and the conversation is limited to three subjects, all of them dead."

At the far table, a priest raised his eyes from a breviary, held them on Silas exactly one heartbeat longer than courtesy required, and returned to the page. He hadn't spoken, Voss confided later while showing Silas upstairs; had been there four days; paid in advance; walked to the chapel in every weather. *Quiet as furniture,* Voss said, approvingly, and Silas thought: *furniture is exactly what you don't see.*

The third guest was in the corridor.

She came out of the linen room with a stack of sheets against her hip and stopped when she saw him, and the stack shifted, and her arm tightened around it — small adjustments, a whole language conducted in forearms — and Silas felt the floor of the last eleven months tilt under him and hold.

He knew her. He had known her as Liesl, in another city, another autumn, when he had been another name himself and the work had still seemed like it might mean something. She had sold flowers near the archive office, then worked the cloakroom at the Winter Hall, and he had spent one entire season finding reasons to walk past the cloakroom. Then the arrests started, the lists, the reshuffling of names that the city performed so efficiently that autumn, and he had told her he was leaving on the Thursday boat, and she had said she would come, and he had gone alone, because the boat manifest of a state courier is a document too, and documents get read, and he had chosen the work. He had told himself many elegant things about that choice. None of them survived contact with her face.

Her eyes went through recognition, past it, through anger, all the way to something calm on the far side that was worse than either.

"Room three's ready," she said to Voss, not to Silas. "Fresh sheets." Then, handing her father the linens and turning away, in a voice pitched only high enough to reach one person in the hallway: "Hello, Anton."

Anton. The name he'd worn the autumn he loved her. It landed between his shoulder blades and stayed there, warm as a coal, all the way up the stairs.

* * *

The room was honest, as advertised. Narrow bed, washstand, a chair, a window giving onto the lake road and, beyond it, the grey water taking snow without comment. Silas set the satchel on the bed, unlocked it with the key on the chain under his shirt, checked the seals — intact, nine of them, blood-dark, patient — locked it again, and put it in the bottom of the trunk under the false tray of clock parts that justified his existence.

Then he sat on the chair and listened to the inn.

Old buildings talk. This one talked in pipes and settling joists and, beneath it all, a rhythm he took a full minute to place: ticking. A tall case clock somewhere below stairs, keeping time for the house, steady as a pulse. He found the sound strangely steadying, and then found it strange that he should, and then understood that everything about this winter was going to arrive that way — disguised, muffled, wearing the clothes of something harmless.

Downstairs, voices assembled themselves through the floorboards. Brandt's bark, quick and amused. Voss's rumble. The boy — Tomas, someone called him, Tomas, bring in the lamps — answering. And once, crossing the hall, a woman's step he would have known blindfolded on a crowded street, a step he had listened for outside a cloakroom in another life.

He had nine documents he could not read and a season he could not leave and a name he had buried coming back up through the floorboards like water.

Silas took out his repairman's ledger and wrote, in the neat column he kept for appearances: *One regulator, longcase, in need of cleaning.* It was even true; he'd heard the clock dragging slightly on the half-hour, a hesitation, a hitch in the escapement. Things that keep time honestly always show their wear.

Below, the priest's door opened and closed, opened and closed — twice, softly, within ten minutes, though the chapel lay in the other wing.

Silas capped his pen and sat in the ticking dark and admitted it to himself at last, the way you admit the bridge is down: he had not come here by accident, the season had not caught him, and no part of what was coming would be inconvenience.

It would be cost. It was only waiting to learn whose.
