## 3. What the Inn Remembers

Silas knew the rules of inns: pay ahead, eat what's offered, learn the exits before you learn the names. He had worked them for six years without a slip. So it was a failure of discipline, nothing more — he looked up when the kitchen door swung open, and the girl carrying the bread tray stopped mid-stride as though she'd walked into glass.

The tray tilted. A loaf slid, caught itself at the edge. She didn't notice.

"Careful," Silas said, because there was nothing else safe to say.

"Silas." Not his name. Not quite. She said it the way you test ice with one foot.

"Miss Voss," he said, and hated how easily the formality came, how many years of practice it had behind it.

She set the bread down with exaggerated care, as if her hands needed instructions. Up close she was thinner than the girl he remembered, and harder in the jaw, but her eyes hadn't changed at all — dark, quick, always half a question ahead of whoever was talking to her.

"You told the coachman you were a clock repairman."

"I am."

"You told me once you were a shipping clerk from Marren." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, uninvited, which she had never once done in the old days. "You told my father that too. Which of them is true?"

"The clockwork," he said. "The rest was weather."

"Weather." She laughed once, without warmth. "You vanished for four years and now you're weather."

He kept his hands flat on the table where she could see them. That was a habit from another life too — showing unarmed hands to frightened people. "I meant to write. I did write. Twice."

"Burned twice," she said. "Once by me, once by the stove, I forget the order."

* * *

Mara had rehearsed this moment for years without knowing it. In the bad nights, the ones after her mother died and before the estate was taken, she'd composed whole speeches: *You left. You lied. You let me believe—* All the good verbs. She'd imagined his face while she used them.

Now his face was here, older around the eyes, and every verb had fallen out of her.

He looked like a man doing arithmetic behind a calm expression. She knew that look. She'd loved that look, God help her, back when she'd believed it meant depth instead of concealment.

"There's soup," she heard herself say. "It's been on since noon. The road's closed; nobody's going anywhere until thaw, so you may as well eat properly."

"That would be — thank you."

Polite. They were being polite to each other across a table in the inn where they had once stolen plums from this same kitchen and kissed behind the woodshed with snow coming down exactly like this, fat and unhurried, sealing the passes, making the world small enough for two people. She stood up too fast and carried the bread into the kitchen and gripped the edge of the sink until her knuckles went white.

Her father glanced over. "Who's the repairman?"

"Nobody," she said. "Someone I used to know."

"Tomas will want to see inside his toolbag," Tomas said, from the doorway, seventeen and all elbows. "A real courier's bag, Mara. Did you see the seals on those dispatches?"

"He's not a courier," Mara said. "He's a clock repairman."

Tomas grinned the way he did when he'd caught an adult in something. "Then why does he sit facing the door?"

* * *

Silas ate the soup and pretended not to catalog the room. He cataloged it anyway. The surveyor, Brandt, drinking steadily and watching everyone. The priest in the corner with a book he wasn't reading, whose questions earlier by the fire had been a little too precise — *official couriers, you say, still running this road? The ministry trusts the winter post? Remarkable.* Silas had given the clock repairman's answer: *I wouldn't know anything about ministries.* The priest had smiled like a man filing something.

And Tomas Voss, fifteen now, who had been five when Silas last saw him and had apparently spent the interval learning to watch satchels.

"You're staring at my sister," Tomas said, dropping into Brandt's abandoned seat with the confidence of someone who owned the inn and knew it.

"I'm eating soup."

"You knew her before. She went red when she saw you. She never goes red."

"Tomas—" Mara, returning with more bread, sharp.

"What? It's true." He wasn't embarrassed; he was delighted. Then, leaning in, dropping his voice to a stage whisper: "Is it true the government pays couriers in gold?"

"Tomas." Mara again, colder, and the boy retreated toward the stairs, grinning backward the whole way. Silas watched him go and felt the first cold thread of the week go through him: the boy had seen the satchel. Everyone had seen the satchel. It sat upstairs under his spare shirts like a loaded gun in a nursery.

"He's a child," Mara said, sitting down again despite herself.

"He's a witness," Silas said, before he could stop himself.

She frowned. "A witness to what?"

"To nothing. Bad joke." He pushed the bowl away. "The soup is excellent. Thank you."

"You're welcome." She studied him the way she used to study locked drawers. "Four years, Silas. You owe me more than soup commentary."

"I owe you nothing," he said, gently, "and that's the only mercy I can afford you right now."

Something moved through her face — hurt, then anger, then the terrible third thing, which was understanding. She stood. "Rooms are ready by eight. Hot water till nine. We lock the yard gate at ten because of wolves." Reciting. Safe ground. "Your lamp is the second on the left."

She turned away, and he watched her go, and thought: *the only mercy I can afford you.* What kind of sentence was that to hand the woman you'd crossed two hundred miles of mountains thinking about? It sounded like a line from a report. It sounded like the ministry had gotten into his mouth.

* * *

Mara washed dishes that didn't need washing. Behind her, the inn did what the inn always did at night: settled, ticked, murmured. Her grandfather's clock in the hall kept its slow count — she'd wound it that morning out of habit, though the mechanism had been failing for a month and the hour hand dragged. Everything in this house dragged now. The debts. Her father's cough. The winters.

She had imagined him dead, in the worst weeks. It was easier than imagining him alive and choosing not to come back. Dead men couldn't be blamed.

Now here he was at her own table, lying about his trade with the same clean face he'd used to lie about everything else, and her body — stupid, loyal body — had gone warm the moment it saw him, before her mind could get a word in.

"You knew him," her father said, appearing with the ledger he carried like a weapon. "The repairman."

"A long time ago."

"Can he fix the clock?"

"No," she said. "Probably not."

Her father grunted and wrote something down. Debts, probably. Names of people who owed them. She thought, not for the first time and not without bitterness, that if the family had ever held title to anything anymore, maybe a man like Silas Almeida would have been beneath their notice entirely.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. She heard footsteps pause outside the room where the stranger slept — light steps, deliberate. Through the gap under her door she saw lamplight move in the corridor, and then Father Anselm's shadow, still, for a long moment, outside the second door on the left.

Listening. Or praying. With priests it was hard to say, and Father Anselm did not strike her as a man who confused the two.

* * *

Silas lay awake under the second lamp on the left and listened to the inn talk to itself. Old buildings narrated. This one spoke of settling beams and cooling stoves and, once, distinctly, of footsteps stopping outside his door and staying there long enough that he moved the satchel from the bed to the floor beside it, then thought better of that, then put it under the loose board he'd found within an hour of arriving because routines were all he had left.

The clock in the hall struck eleven, dragging on the hour like a tired man climbing stairs. He'd looked at its works that afternoon — Mara had watched him do it, arms folded, saying nothing, and he'd known she was really watching him, and he'd let himself be watched, which was the closest thing to honesty either of them had managed all day.

The escapement was worn past repair by anyone honest. Someone clever could hide a great deal inside a broken clock case. He filed that thought away and disliked himself for it.

Snow hit the window in soft handfuls. Somewhere below, Mara was still awake — he could tell by the candle glow under her door, the way he'd once been able to tell her mood by the set of her shoulders. Four years, and he still read her like a familiar page. That was the danger of it. You could bury a thing deep, but the body kept its own index.

He thought about the dispatches. He had not opened them. Six years, three wars' worth of borders, and he had never once broken a seal — not from patriotism, which had curdled early, but because sealed things were simpler. A sealed thing was just weight. Opened, it became a decision.

Through the wall, very faintly, he heard Mara's chair scrape as she finally blew out her candle.

*Tomorrow*, he told himself, the way he'd been telling himself for six years. *Tomorrow I'll be only a clock repairman.*

In the hall, the clock ticked on, dragging its hour hand toward a time that wasn't quite true, and downstairs, unheard by anyone, Father Anselm sat in the dark chapel with the government's seal wax warming over a flame, practicing the shape of a signature that was not his own.
