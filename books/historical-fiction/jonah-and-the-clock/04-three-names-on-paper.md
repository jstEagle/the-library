## 4. Three Names on Paper

The inn went quiet in layers. First the surveyor, Hollis, took his brandy upstairs with a thump of boots and a door that didn't quite latch. Then the priest, Father Ivo, extinguished the chapel lamp and withdrew, his footsteps soft as ash falling. Old Voss banked the fire and shuffled to bed, and the kitchen girl climbed the back stair humming something tuneless.

By midnight only Silas and the dark remained, and the satchel lay on the table between them like a guest no one would introduce.

He had carried it for eleven days. He knew its weight the way a man knows his own heartbeat — four pounds and change, leather gone soft at the strap where his palm rode it, the brass seals cold even through wool. The regulations were simple enough that a child could recite them: the courier does not open the case. The courier does not permit the case to be opened. The courier delivers the case intact or answers with his life. Silas had recited those lines to himself on the coach, on the frozen road, on the bridge the snow had eaten, and they had held him up like railings.

But railings are for stairs, and he was no longer going anywhere.

He told himself he was only checking. The bridge was out; the dispatches might have to winter here whether he liked it or not; if the pass sealed and the government needed the contents known — there were procedures for that too, weren't there? A courier stranded beyond recovery might authorize disclosure under duress of circumstance. He invented the clause while he turned the satchel over. Somewhere behind his eyes a colder voice observed that he had never been much good at lying to other people either, so why pretend he could manage it in his own head.

The clock over the mantel ticked with the patience of something that had outlived three governments. He wondered what Mara Voss heard when she listened to it. He wondered why he was wondering about Mara Voss instead of doing the thing he'd come downstairs to do or leaving it undone.

He lit the candle stub the kitchen girl had left. He unstrapped the satchel.

* * *

Inside: oilcloth, folded twice and tied with cord gone waxy from the cold. Beneath that, a canvas envelope stamped twice with the eagle of the Provisional Ministry — wings half-spread, as if the bird were thinking about flying but hadn't committed. The seal broke with a sound like a knuckle cracking, small and final, and Silas felt the sound travel down his arm and lodge somewhere under his ribs.

Four documents. He read the first standing up, because sitting down felt like surrender.

It was an order of designation, dated six weeks past, bearing the signature of a minister whose name Silas had seen on proclamations nailed to courthouse doors. The language was the usual beautiful rot — *whereas the estate known as Aldermoor, seat of the Voss family, having been seized under the Reclamation Act of 1917 and held in trust pending adjudication, and whereas the exigencies of the state require liquid disposition of all assets—*

Silas stopped reading standing and sat down after all.

*Aldermoor.* He knew the name the way you know a word in a foreign song — it had appeared once, years ago, in a conversation by a river, a girl with ink-stained fingers saying *we had a house once, before the courts took it, my father talks about it like a person who died.* She had laughed when she said it. He had thought the laugh was armor. Now, under this candle, with the paper trembling in a way he refused to blame on the draft, he understood the laugh had been grief wearing its good coat.

He read on.

Three names. The document designated three heirs to whatever the adjudication might restore: one Elias Marchetti, of the city, described as cousin to the late Madame Voss. One Tomas Voss, minor son of the present holder. And one — the ink seemed to sharpen as he read it, though ink cannot do that, ink is just ink —

*Mara Voss, daughter of the holder, of the Halfway House, high road to the coast.*

He set the page down. The clock ticked. Snow ticked against the window in fine grains, the mountain sifting itself finer, sealing the world shut above them.

She was heir to a house she'd spoken of like a grave. And this paper, which he had carried against his heart through eleven days of frozen road, did not restore the house to her. That was the second document's business. He made himself read it, because a man who has broken one rule may as well learn how far the floor is below.

The liquidation order was brief. The estate, should any portion be restored by adjudication, would be sold entire — land, timber, the mills on the river bend — proceeds to be remitted to the Ministry's contingency fund at the coastal treasury. Silas knew what *contingency fund* meant in the winter of 1919. It meant steamship tickets. It meant ministers' wives' jewels in diplomatic luggage. It meant a government packing its bags while it signed papers obligating other people's houses to pay for the cab fare.

The third document was a schedule of valuation. Numbers, acreage, the cold arithmetic of a family's life rendered in columns. Four hundred hectares. Two hundred and sixty head of timber marked for felling. A dwelling house, appraised. He thought of Mara's hands, quick and ink-stained, and tried not to imagine them signing anything.

The fourth document was shortest, and worst. It was addressed not to a court but to the couriers of the route — a circular instruction that in the event of interception, compromise, or capture, the carrier was to understand that the documents were of secondary importance to the security of their delivery, and that no negotiation, ransom, or exchange involving the carrier's person should be entertained at the expense of the contents. In plain speech: the man carrying these papers was worth less than the papers. In plainer speech still: expendable. He had always suspected it. Suspicion is comfortable. Reading it under your own name — *the carrier* — by your own candle, is another matter entirely.

Silas sat for a long time with the pages fanned before him like a losing hand.

* * *

He tried to reconstruct his ignorance and found it insulting. Eleven days he had guarded these names without knowing they had faces. That had been the mercy of protocol — it let you serve strangers abstractly. A sealed case has no eyes. But now the case had Mara's eyes, gray-green and unimpressed across a supper table; it had the boy Tomas's crooked grin; it had, God help him, his own reflection in the window glass, a man who had loved someone once and lied to her about the smallest thing first, which is how all of it starts.

Because that was the rot under everything, wasn't it. Not the ministry's theft. His. Five years ago by the river he had told her his name was Corin. A small lie, a traveler's lie, the kind you tell so a story can stay a story — and then the war came and the roads closed and the lie calcified into a fact, and now here he sat in her father's inn under a false name inside a true coat, carrying the paper that would sell her childhood out from under her a second time.

If he delivered the dispatches as sworn, the estate would be liquidated before any court could blink, the money fled seaward with the men who signed this, and Mara would learn that the stranger she'd let back across her threshold had carried the knife in his satchel the whole time, warm against his body, keeping it safe.

If he destroyed them—

He didn't finish the thought. He made himself look at it sideways instead, the way you look at a drop. Destroying official dispatches was treason by any statute still breathing in the capital. But statutes need a state to enforce them, and the state was leaving like a debtor at dawn. The priest's questions at supper — *official couriers, on this road, in this season?* — came back to him with new weight. Who else knew what he carried? Hollis the surveyor had watched the satchel too long on the coach, and Hollis had offered, too casually, to guide him over the pass come thaw, *for a consideration.* The world outside this inn had begun to smell around the edges.

And underneath all of it, patient as groundwater, the truth he kept circling: the paper named her. Whatever else it did, whatever ministry stole whatever house, some clerk in some office had written *Mara Voss* in the column marked *heir*, and the law of the dying republic had acknowledged, in its own bloodless way, that she had a claim. These pages were the only proof of it anywhere on earth until copies reached the coast. If he delivered them, the proof would vanish into a contingency fund. If he burned them—

If he burned them, she would never know what she'd almost had. Or what had almost been taken.

Unless he told her.

The thought arrived whole, the way dangerous thoughts do, fully dressed and holding out its hand. Tell her. Sit across a table and say *my name isn't Corin, it never was, and here is what your government wrote about your family while it packed to leave.* Trade the safe lie for the costly truth and let her do with him what she would.

His training rose up against it — *never reveal contents, never involve civilians* — and Silas examined the training with the detachment of a man auditing a bankrupt firm. The regulations assumed a functioning state at the far end of the delivery. The regulations assumed the courier mattered. Both assumptions were dead letters now, and he was sitting in an inn full of living people, one of whom had a right to the words in his lap.

Guilt, he discovered, is only useful once it hardens into something you can build with. He let it set.

He refolded the documents along their original creases — a courier's habit, hands moving while the mind stands elsewhere — and paused over the fourth page, the expendability notice. He read it once more, slowly. *No negotiation, ransom, or exchange involving the carrier's person.* They had written it plainly enough. Perhaps they would appreciate plainness returned.

He did not burn anything tonight. Burning was easy and left no one wiser. Instead he rewrapped the oilcloth, restrapped the satchel, and set it back on the table where any eye watching would expect it — because he was nearly certain now that some eye was watching, and a man about to move openly should first learn who moves against him.

Then he took the one liberty he couldn't take back: he copied the three names onto the flyleaf of his clock-repair ledger, in the tiny cipher of his apprenticeship years, beneath an entry reading *escapement, worn pallets — return spring needed.* An insurance against fire and theft both. The secret had faces now, and faces deserved more than one copy.

He pinched out the candle. The clock ticked on, marking time toward something.

As he climbed toward his room, he saw light under the chapel door where no light had been — thin gold, flickering, and the faint scratch of a nib moving fast across paper.

Someone else was awake. Someone else was writing.

Silas stood in the cold hallway, a man who had just decided to stop lying, and understood that the night had only begun asking its questions.
