## 5. The Surveyor's Offer

The surveyor's name was Havel, and he sharpened his pencils the way other men sharpened knives.

Silas had watched him do it three times now — once at breakfast, twice by the fire — the long blade drawn along a whetstone in slow, even strokes, graphite dust gathering on his knee like black snow. He kept six pencils in a leather roll and never used more than two before resharpening them all. A man who trusted nothing to stay sharp.

On the fourth morning of their captivity at the Halfway House, Havel sat down across from Silas with two cups of coffee and said, "You measure things too."

"I repair clocks," Silas said.

"Sure." Havel pushed one cup across the table. "And I survey boundaries that haven't existed since the old duke died. We're both lying through our teeth, so let's skip to the part where we don't."

Outside, the snow had stopped insulting them and settled into a siege. Two meters on the level, drifts to the eaves on the windward side. The bridge at the gorge was a rumor. The coachman had gone back down-country with the horses and hadn't been heard from since. They were eleven people in a wooden building at the top of the world, burning wood faster than the woodpile liked, and the thaw was four months away if it was a kind year.

Silas drank the coffee. It was terrible, which meant Mara hadn't made it, which meant he could drink it without thinking about her hands around the cup. He'd been rationing his thoughts about her like hardtack. It wasn't working.

"Say what you came to say," he said.

Havel smiled. He had a surveyor's face, sun-scoured even in winter, lines around the eyes from squinting through instruments at distances most men couldn't imagine crossing. "There's a track over the Kessel Pass. Old shepherd route. Bad in summer, suicidal in winter — but not impossible. Not for a man like me, who's walked worse for men who paid better." He turned a pencil in his fingers, point outward, casual as breathing. "Room for one. Maybe two, if the second is light and doesn't complain about losing toes."

"And you're offering this passage to me because—"

"Because of what's in the satchel you sleep with under your mattress."

The room did not tilt, though Silas felt it try. He kept his face where it was, the way he'd been trained to, the way that had gotten him called *the coldest man in the courier corps*, a compliment he'd once been proud of. Under the table, his hand found his knee and gripped it.

"I sleep with my tools there," he said. "Clockwork. Heavy, awkward, worthless to anyone but me."

"Mm." Havel produced his notebook — the real one, not the survey ledger he showed around the dinner table — and opened it without reading from it. He didn't need to read from it. "Four days ago you asked the innkeeper's boy whether mail still moved up-valley. Three days ago, when the priest mentioned the Ministry's courier line through the mountains, you corrected him on the relay schedule — the *winter* schedule, which hasn't been posted anywhere south of the capital. Two nights ago you checked your satchel after the lamp went out in the common room, in the dark, by feel, in under ten seconds. That's not how a clockmaker checks his tools. That's how a man checks whether his seals are intact." He closed the notebook. "I map things, Almeida. Terrain. Patterns. People are just country I haven't walked yet."

*Almeida.* Not Brandl, the name on the false papers in Silas's pack. The sound of his own name in this stranger's mouth went through him like a nail through wet wood.

"Who sent you?" Silas asked.

"Now that," Havel said, "is the interesting question. Because the answer keeps changing depending on who's asking me, and I've decided the smartest thing I can do this winter is be for sale." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, voice dropping to the register men use when they want to be overheard only by the person paying them. "Here's the shape of it. Your republic is dying — you know it, I know it, the priest knows it, God help us, probably the girl knows it. Everything that government owns is being loaded onto wagons and pointed somewhere else while the wheels still turn. And a courier carrying sealed dispatches through the mountains in the last week of autumn isn't carrying letters. He's carrying the last inventory of a going-out-of-business sale. Names. Claims. Titles. Whatever it is, someone will pay for it, and everyone will pay for it, and the only question I can't answer sitting here is *which* someone gets there first."

"You don't know what's in it," Silas said. "Or you'd have named a price already."

Havel's grin showed teeth. "I know what it's *worth*. Contents are a detail. I'm offering you the pass, Almeida. One crossing, my route, my rope, my knowledge. In exchange, the satchel comes with us, unopened, and goes to whoever meets us on the other side. Could be the Ministry's people at the coast garrison. Could be the faction that pays better. You get your escort; I get my fee; the paper does whatever paper does. You might even tell yourself you delivered it. Men have told themselves worse."

"And if I say no?"

The pencil stopped turning.

"Then I wait," Havel said mildly. "I'm good at waiting. I'll wait out the winter here, drinking this coffee, sharpening these pencils, and watching who else comes sniffing after you. Because they will come, Almeida. The priest has been asking questions since the day we arrived — you've noticed, surely. And when the thaw opens the road, or when somebody skis in over the pass before then, I'll sell what I know. I know your face, your name, your route, and your hiding place. That's worth something too. Less than the satchel, but I'm a practical man. I take smaller fees when the market's thin." He stood, pocketed his notebook, and collected his own cup. "Think of it less as a threat than as a price list. Everything's negotiable except the season."

He walked off toward the window where his instruments were laid out on a card table, calibrating something against nothing, and left Silas alone with the worst coffee in the world and the arithmetic of his own loyalty.

* *

Silas took the satchel out that night and finally looked at what he was guarding.

Not opened — he wasn't ready for whatever breaking those seals would cost him, not yet, not with Havel's pencil turning in the back of his mind like a countdown. But he weighed it. He held it in his lap in the dark of his narrow room and tried to remember why.

He had joined the courier corps at nineteen because his father had served it and died serving it, and because a boy with no estate and no patron could rise there on punctuality alone. Punctuality. That was all it had ever required of him, really: be where the seal needed to be, on time, every time, and ask nothing about the contents. For nine years the arrangement had suited him perfectly. Sealed things asked nothing of you. Sealed things couldn't look at you across a kitchen table with flour on their wrists.

Mara's face came to him unbidden, the way it had come every night since she'd said his name — his real name — in the doorway on the second evening, quiet enough that only he heard it, and then turned away as if she hadn't.

He thought about Havel's offer honestly, because dishonest arithmetic got couriers killed. The truth was that the temptation was real. The pass, the escort, the clean story — deliver the satchel, keep the oath, survive. It was everything he had trained for wrapped in one transaction. And the fact that Havel would shop the contents to whichever faction paid didn't have to touch Silas's conscience at all, if he was careful about the wording. *Delivered into unknown hands* could be written up later as *delivered under duress.* The corps had forms for it. There were always forms.

That was the thing about loyalty, he was beginning to understand. Everyone talked about it like bedrock, like something you either had or lacked. But Havel had priced it in front of him, itemized it, and the numbers came out ugly. His duty to the state: one crossing over the Kessel Pass. His honor: whatever a dying ministry's seal was worth to whoever bought it. His life: apparently included free of charge.

Loyalty wasn't stone. It was currency. It changed hands. It inflated and collapsed, and right now the government that issued it was defaulting, and Silas was holding a purse full of coins from a country that might not exist by spring.

He put the satchel back under the mattress and lay staring at the ceiling beams, listening to the inn settle and crack around him like a ship.

Somewhere below, a floorboard announced someone moving through the hall — light steps, pausing outside his door, moving on. Mara, or the priest, or Tomas the brother, who watched Silas the way boys watch locked boxes. Eleven people in this inn, and every one of them now knew or suspected that the quiet clockmaker was carrying the end of the world in a leather case. Havel had made sure of that much tonight, even if he'd done it quietly. Knowledge spread through an inn like heat through bad insulation.

Silas closed his eyes and did the last piece of arithmetic, the one he'd been avoiding.

If the satchel was worth a fortune to strangers, it was worth something else entirely to the people in this house. Dispatches naming heirs. He didn't know that — not yet, not for certain — but the thought had been circling him since the second night, when Mara had mentioned, offhand, over bread, that her family had once owned everything this valley stood on, before the seizures, before the courts, before her father had bought back the inn alone out of all of it. *They took the rest,* she'd said. *Paper does that, if you let it.*

Paper does that.

He was carrying paper.

In the morning, the storm returned with a violence that rattled the shutters, and Silas came down to find the common room in an uproar — Havel standing at the foot of the stairs with his notebook open, wearing the expression of a man whose patience had just found its market, and Father Anton at the chapel door, pale, watching Silas across the room with eyes that asked a question Havel had only offered to buy.

"Courier," the priest said quietly, into the hush. "We should talk."
