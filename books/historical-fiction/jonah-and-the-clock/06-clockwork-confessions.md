## 6. Clockwork Confessions

The clock in the Halfway House had not run true since before the war, and Mara had decided, somewhere between the second pot of coffee and the third lie Silas told at breakfast, that she was done waiting for it to be honest.

"It loses a quarter hour a day," she said, setting the key and the winding crank on the table between them like a challenge. "My grandfather's clock. My father winds it every night out of habit and it repays him by lying about when dawn comes. You said you repair clocks."

"I did say that." Silas turned the crank over in his long fingers without looking at it. He'd been doing that all morning — looking at everything but her, and then looking at her too quickly, as if catching himself at a door he shouldn't open.

"You also said you'd been through the pass twice before," she said. "You also said your name was Almeida."

"Those are both true things."

"Are they, though?"

He almost smiled. That was the cruelty of him, that he could make her want to laugh while she was furious, that six days of snowed-in strangers had somehow reassembled themselves into this — the two of them alone in the parlor with a broken clock and eleven years of silence between them.

"Show me the movement," he said.

She led him to the hall where the case stood taller than either of them, dark walnut carved with grapevines by a Voss hand three generations gone. Her grandfather had bought the clock in better years, back when the family still owned land instead of an inn and a grudge. She opened the case with the brass key at her throat and stood back.

Silas crouched, peered up into the works with a lamp, and made a sound she recognized — a small, involuntary hum of sympathy, the sound of a man meeting something complicated and wanting to understand it.

"Worn pallets," he said. "And someone's let the oil gum. When was it last serviced?"

"My father says 1911. My father is also an optimist about many things."

That got the smile out of him fully, brief as a match strike. Then he rolled his sleeves and asked for her help, and for the next four hours they took the clock apart together.

It should have been awkward. It was not. That was the part that frightened her — how easily their hands fell into cooperation, how she held weights while he unhooked them, how they passed tools without asking. They had been nineteen the last time they worked side by side like this, in her uncle's boathouse on the coast, repairing a skiff neither of them knew how to sail, and her body remembered the rhythm of him before her mind consented to it.

"You went by another name then," she said, watching him lift the great wheel free. "When I knew you."

Silas's hands did not stop moving. That was its own answer. Men with clean consciences flinched; men with dirty ones worked.

"Mara."

"Jonah," she said. "You were Jonah Kestrel. You wrote me letters signed Jonah for a year, from the capital, and then nothing. And now there's a man in my father's inn calling himself Almeida with a satchel he sleeps beside and a toolbox full of clocks he doesn't know how to—" She stopped. "Except you do know how. That's real. The hands are real. So tell me which parts are real, Jonah."

He set the wheel down on the cloth she'd spread and looked at her, finally, straight on. His face did what faces do when they choose honesty and arrive too late to be believed completely: it opened, and then it guarded.

"The hands are real," he said quietly. "The name isn't. The letters were real. Every word of them."

"And then?"

"And then the people I worked for learned who I'd been writing to, and told me it stopped, or I stopped working. And I chose wrong." He picked up a pivot polisher and began working it against the arbor, slow circles, eyes down. "I chose the work. I told myself it was temporary. Eleven years, apparently, is how long temporary lasts."

The fire ticked in the stove. Above them the empty clock case gaped like a well.

"Why come back now?" she asked. "On this road, in this season, with that satchel. Why here, Jonah?"

"I didn't choose the road. The road chose me because it was the last one open." A pause. The polishing stopped. "I've wanted to tell you something since the bridge closed. Six days. I've started the sentence forty times."

Her heart did something foolish and old. "Then finish it."

He looked up at her and she watched him decide — she saw it happen, saw the confession gather itself behind his eyes like weather, saw him open his mouth —

— and close it.

"There's a tooth missing in the third wheel train," he said. "That's why the strike runs fast. If we cut a new one it'll keep true until spring."

Mara stood very still. She had watched her father bargain with suppliers for fifteen years and knew exactly what it looked like when a man traded one thing for another and hoped you wouldn't count the change.

"All right," she said softly. "Keep it. But know that I can hear the sentence you didn't say, Silas or Jonah or whatever your name is by suppertime. It's sitting in this room with us. It's louder than the clock ever was."

* * *

They cut the tooth by lamplight. It was delicate work, filing brass to a shape fine as a fingernail, and it required four hands and no talking, which suited them both. Mara held the wheel against the jig while Silas drew the file in strokes so even she could have set a pendulum by them.

Somewhere in the middle of it she stopped being angry. She couldn't point to when. Perhaps when he steadied her wrist without thinking, correcting her grip the way you correct only someone you trust. Perhaps when sweat gathered at his temple in the lamplight and he looked, briefly, like the boy in the boathouse again — concentrated, kind, entirely elsewhere.

"You're good at this," she said.

"Lying?"

"Clocks."

"They're related trades," he said. "Both are about making people believe time is going where they think it is."

They fitted the new tooth near midnight. The movement went back together under his fingers piece by piece, wheels meshing, chains threaded, weights hung, and when he finally wound it and set the pendulum swinging, the tick filled the hall like a pulse returning to a body. Steady. True. Mara found her eyes stinging and blamed the lamp smoke.

"It's beautiful," she said.

"So was the year I spent being Jonah," he said, and the words came out raw, unplanned, and she understood that the file had loosened something in him the way it had loosened the gummed oil. "Mara. There are things in that satchel—"

The hall door banged.

Tomas came in first, snow in his hair, seventeen and all elbows, followed by Father Anselm shaking cold off his coat. Her brother was flushed and strange-eyed and would not look at the clock case, which she noticed, and would not look at Silas's room upstairs, which she noticed more.

"Father Anselm wants the chapel," Tomas said. "For reading. Late," he added, oddly precise, and vanished up the stairs two steps at a time.

The priest apologized with courtly weariness, asked nothing, blessed nothing, and went out through the kitchen toward the little chapel her grandmother had kept. Through the wall, faintly, Mara heard a door close.

Silas stood with his hand still resting on the clock case as if the machine were a patient he wasn't sure would survive the night.

"Come upstairs," Mara said. Not a question. She was tired of sentences that finished themselves into silence.

* * *

Her room was small and hot and the window showed nothing but falling snow, the world reduced to flakes appearing and vanishing in the lamplight, which felt accurate. Silas sat on the edge of her bed with his elbows on his knees like a man at a dock watching a ship leave, and she knelt in front of him and untied his boots, one and then the other, because someone had to start and she refused to be the one who waited.

"I have spent eleven years being careful," he said to the ceiling. "Careful is a way of never being anywhere."

"Where are you now?"

"Here." His voice cracked on it, just barely. "Terrifyingly here."

She rose and he caught her hands, and for a long moment they simply stood, foreheads touching, two people listening to a new clock tick through the wall — a sound that meant tomorrow would arrive when it said, whether or not they were ready for it.

What happened after that happened slowly and then all at once, the way winter comes. She would remember afterward not the large things but the small ones: that he folded his coat over the chair with courier's neatness, satchel and all; that he whispered her name — *Mara*, not Jonah's-era endearments, just her name, twice, like checking a knot; that when she slept it was against his heartbeat, which ran faster than hers, and that she thought drowsily, *I could set a life by this,* and believed it, and slept.

She woke once in the grey hour before dawn. Silas was awake, propped on one elbow, watching the window. His face in that light was stripped of everything he performed during the day, and what remained underneath was grief.

"What is it?" she murmured.

"Nothing. Sleep."

"Jonah."

He smoothed her hair back down with a tenderness that was itself an answer, an apology issued in advance, and said, "I'm here," which she already knew was the next thing he would take back.

* * *

Morning came white and windless. Mara was in the kitchen starting bread when Tomas came down pale as the view, pulling on his gloves, saying something about checking the woodpile, escaping — and it was only when she went to the chapel with fresh candles that she found what the morning had been hiding.

Father Anselm had not slept. He sat at the little table beneath the saint's niche, surrounded by papers, and he was writing — not a homily, not a journal. He had a sheet of the inn's own stationery before him and he was copying onto it, line for line in a small clerk's hand, a document whose heading Mara could not read at that distance but whose format she knew in her bones, because she had grown up around official paper: seals, ruled columns, numbered clauses.

Copying. From memory? From what?

The priest looked up. He did not startle, did not cover the pages — worse, he covered them calmly, deliberately, letting her see that he was choosing to hide them.

"Miss Voss," he said gently. "Early for candles."

"What are you copying, Father?"

"A prayer," he said, "for a man who will need it soon," and smiled like winter sunshine, and went on writing.

Behind her, through the wall, the new clock struck eight — true, for the first time in years, each stroke clean as cut brass.
