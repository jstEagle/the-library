## 7. The Priest's Truth

Silas found the priest in the chapel at dawn, or what passed for dawn that week — a gray seeping through the frost-ferned windows, the kind of light that made no promises. Father Anselm was on his knees before the altar, but he wasn't praying. Silas had watched the man pray, two nights ago, and it had looked different: shoulders loose, hands open, the whole body surrendered to something. This was not surrender. This was a man kneeling because kneeling gave him a convenient angle on the loose floorboard near the altar steps.

"Father."

Anselm didn't startle. That was its own confession. He rose with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once been surprised by anything he'd chosen to do, and brushed ash from his cassock. The chapel smelled of tallow and old pine and, faintly, of the ink Silas had noticed missing from the inn's writing desk three days ago.

"You're up early," Anselm said. "Courier."

The word dropped between them like a coin on stone. Not *repairman*. Not *the clock man*, as the surveyor called him with a sneer he probably thought was subtle. Silas kept his face still and his hand where it was, nowhere near his coat, because reaching for the satchel would confirm everything and the satchel wasn't even on him anymore — it was wrapped in oilcloth under the third floorboard of his room, and only he and God knew that, and he was beginning to suspect God had been talking to other people.

"I don't know what you mean," Silas said.

"Of course you don't." Anselm sat down on the front pew, an invitation, and after a moment Silas took it. Outside, the wind worked at the shutters with patient fingers. "You have very good hands, you know. A repairman's hands are scarred differently than yours. You hold tools like you're afraid of them. And when the mail coach came in November, you watched the driver unload the bags the way a mother watches a stranger carry her child down stairs." He folded his hands. "I've been on roads a long time, Mr. Almeida. Men who lie for a living have a posture. Yours is quite good. But it is still a posture."

*Mr. Almeida.* Silas felt the floor tilt a degree, the way it did on bridges just before they sway. His real name — or the name that had been real until a ministry clerk stamped a leather case and made everything else provisional. "Then there's no point pretending," he said. "What do you want?"

"What I've wanted since Innsbruck. To talk to you honestly. Once. Before the others got to you." Anselm's eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they held no warmth but no cruelty either, which was somehow worse. "The surveyor offered you passage for paper. I'm offering you something rarer. I'm offering to let everyone in this house live."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above them, footsteps crossed the landing — Mara, going down to start the bread, her step so familiar now that Silas could pick it out of a hundred. He thought about her hands in the clockwork, dusted with brass filings, and made himself keep looking at the priest.

"Say it plainly," Silas said.

"The dispatches you carry. They name heirs. Voss heirs." Anselm said the name gently, the way one sets down something breakable. "You didn't know when you took the commission. Perhaps you suspected. But you know now — you've known since the night you sat in this chapel with the case open on your knees, reading by candle stub, while the rest of us slept. Yes," he added, mild, "the door has a gap at its hinge. I close doors slowly."

Silas's mouth was dry. "Go on."

"There is a family — you'd know them as the estate's creditors, if you knew them at all — who have spent eleven years and a great deal of money positioning themselves for exactly the moment a bankrupt republic starts selling its ghosts. If those dispatches reach the coast, the estate is liquidated and the money vanishes into the pockets of men already running. If the dispatches burn, the estate falls into litigation that outlives everyone in this valley, and the Voss children see nothing but lawyers. But if the dispatches come to me—" he spread his hands, palms up, a parody of absolution— "they can be *arranged*. The heirs confirmed quietly. Protected. Moved somewhere the creditors' men will never find them. Mara. Tomas. Even the cousin in Graz none of them remembers. All of it secured, all of it safe, and you walk away clean. A courier whose bag was lost to the snow. No one courts-martials a man for weather."

"And your family gets the seals," Silas said. "The authority. Unopened, so nobody can prove what was changed inside them."

Anselm smiled without pleasure. "Every mercy has a mechanism, Mr. Almeida. Mine happens to be legible."

* * *

Silas stood and walked to the window, though there was nothing to see but white. The valley had filled like a bowl, the pass a rumor, the road down to the bridge a pale suggestion under drifts. Four months, the coachman had said. Four months of bread and firewood and Mara's step overhead, and every day of it a countdown.

"You're wrong about one thing," Silas said. "I did take an oath."

"To a state with no treasury, no army worth the name, and a government that left the capital eleven days ago." Anselm remained seated. His voice stayed level, pastoral, infuriating. "May I tell you what I learned before I took orders? I was in the ministry's courier service too. Twenty years ago. Do you know what the manual called us?"

Silas didn't answer.

"*Consumables*," Anselm said. "Stationery. Ink and paper and couriers. There's a ledger in every ministry office with a column for expected losses on mountain routes, and men like you go into it in the winter column, and no one writes letters home about it. When your dispatches fail to arrive, they will not send anyone to ask what happened. They'll simply reissue copies — they always keep copies — and stamp another case, and hand it to another young person with good hands, and that one will also freeze or starve or be robbed on this same road, and the republic will call it duty both times." He paused. "You are not defending anything, Mr. Almeida. You are being *spent*."

"That's a fine speech from a man selling protection."

"It is," Anselm agreed, without shame. "But test it against what you know. Have you received any instruction for this contingency? Any sealed orders for the case the road closes? Any cipher key, any fallback route, any name you could wire?" He let the silence do its work. "They gave you a bag and a story about clocks. Consumables don't get contingencies."

Silas thought of the ministry hall where he'd taken the commission — the warm room, the clerk's bored stamp, the older officer who'd shaken his hand and said *Godspeed* without meeting his eyes. He had read the gesture then as hurry. He read it correctly now.

"They consider me expendable," Silas said slowly. It came out steadier than he expected. "Fine. Then my life is mine to spend, isn't it? And I choose to spend it delivering what I was given."

"To whom?" Anselm rose, and for the first time something urgent moved behind the pale eyes. "There is no *whom*, man. There is a coast that may be in someone else's hands by spring, a government in exile that may disavow these papers within the month to buy creditors of its own. Your oath is to a signature on a seal, and signatures can be forged faster than couriers can ski." He stepped closer. He smelled of candle wax and cold. "I am offering you the only version of your duty that ends with the people in this house alive. Take it."

"No."

The word was out before Silas had decided on it, and having left, it stood there in the cold chapel air, small and absolute, refusing recall.

Anselm studied him for a long moment. Whatever he saw seemed to settle something; the urgency drained out of his face and left the pastor back in place, patient as erosion. "Because of the daughter," he said. Not a question.

"Because of all of them."

"Sentiment is expensive," Anselm said softly. "You'll find the exchange rate worsens daily." He gathered his breviary from the pew, straightened his cassock, and moved toward the door, and it was only with his hand on the latch that he turned back, courteous as a man remembering an errand.

"One thing more, since we're being honest. The surveyor — Herr Brandt. You should know his arrangement isn't with me. I approached him weeks ago and he refused terms. Which means someone else holds his contract." He opened the door onto the white blast of morning and the wind snatched at his words. "Someone paying better than the Church. Whoever they are, they'll want their delivery before the thaw — and men like Brandt don't wait for spring to collect."

He went out. The door swung shut. The stove ticked once more and went silent, and Silas stood alone in the chapel with his duty lying around him like the snow outside — vast, beautiful, and no longer holding anything up.

Behind him, at the top of the stair, a floorboard creaked.

Not Mara's step. Lighter. Quicker. Gone almost before he heard it.

Tomas.
