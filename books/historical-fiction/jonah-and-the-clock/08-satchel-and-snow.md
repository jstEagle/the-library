## 8. Satchel and Snow

Mara knew the satchel was gone before anyone said a word.

She knew it the way you know a clock has stopped — not by looking at it, but by the shape of the silence it leaves behind. The Halfway House had developed a rhythm in the three weeks since the bridge went out, and that rhythm had Silas Almeida's leather satchel folded into it like a beat. He carried it down to breakfast. He carried it to the window seat after supper, where he sat with it on his knees and his thumbs hooked under the strap, not reading it, just holding it, the way a man holds a rifle he has no intention of firing but no intention of putting down either.

This morning there was no satchel at the window seat. No satchel at breakfast. Mara set down the coffee pot and counted the room the way her father had taught her to count a room — guests first, exits second, trouble third.

The surveyor, Brandt, was eating eggs with the deliberate slowness of a man performing innocence. The priest, Father Ivo, sat by the cold hearth with his hands folded, watching the stairs. Her father was in the cellar. And Silas —

Silas came down the stairs two at a time, pale as the snowlight through the windows, and stopped in the middle of the common room as if the floor had ended.

"Has anyone been in my room?"

Brandt didn't look up from his eggs. "Was something taken?"

"Was anything *taken*," Silas repeated, and Mara heard the careful weight he put on each word, the way a man walks across ice he knows is thin.

"I asked first," said Brandt.

Silas ignored him. His eyes found Mara's, and what she saw in them frightened her more than the question itself. Not anger. Not panic. Something worse: a kind of terrible arithmetic, as if he were already calculating what the loss of that satchel would cost, and finding the sum ran past money, past duty, into blood.

"It was there last night," he said. "Under my coat. The door doesn't latch properly — I know it doesn't, I've meant to fix it—"

"The whole inn doesn't latch properly," Brandt said mildly. "It's an inn. Doors are decorative." He set down his fork at last and looked up, and his face had the pleasant blankness of a man who has already decided how the next hour will go and is simply waiting for everyone else to arrive there. "Though I wonder, Almeida. A courier loses his cargo on my road, in my sight, and wants sympathy? What was in it that's worth this shade of white?"

"Nothing that concerns you."

"Everything on this road concerns me until the thaw. It's a small country up here. We're all its government."

Father Ivo spoke for the first time, softly, without moving. "Perhaps it was never stolen. Perhaps it walked."

Nobody asked him what he meant. Everybody understood him anyway.

* * *

By noon the inn had turned on itself.

Her father came up from the cellar and took down the shotgun over the bar, which he had not done in Mara's living memory, and stood it beside the tap handles like a statement. Brandt volunteered to search the outbuildings and did so thoroughly and alone, and returned empty-handed but somehow satisfied, the way a man returns from pricing something. Silas tore his own room apart and then, shamefaced and grim, let Mara search him — coat, boots, lining, the false bottom of his clock-repairer's kit — because whatever he carried, he wanted it back badly enough to be searched by the daughter of the house.

"You'd tell me," Mara said quietly, standing close enough to feel the cold still coming off him, "if there were something I should know."

He looked at her and she watched him decide to lie, watched the decision happen behind his eyes like weather crossing a valley, and then watched the lie fail before it reached his mouth. "There are things I should have told you years ago," he said. "I'll tell them all soon. I swear it."

"Soon," Mara said. "Everyone's secrets keep promising me soon."

She left him there and went through the guest rooms herself, because someone had to, and because searching rooms gave her hands work while her mind did its own colder searching. Tomas was missing. She realized it only gradually, the way you notice a sound has stopped. Her brother had been underfoot all winter, sixteen and restless, chopping wood nobody needed and asking Silas questions about clocks. Now the woodpile stood neatly full and Tomas was nowhere, and when she asked her father, he shrugged and said the boy was probably up at the summer pasture shed, sulking, and Mara put the two facts together — the missing boy, the missing satchel — and felt the floor tilt under everything she knew about her family.

Tomas, who idolized Silas. Tomas, who followed him around the inn like an apprentice. Tomas, who could not have known what was in that satchel unless he'd read it, and could not have read it unless—

Unless he'd learned there was something worth reading.

* * *

She found the clue where she least wanted it: in the clock.

The heirloom regulator had come down through four generations of Vosses, and it had been dying all winter, its strike going hoarse, its hands drifting apart like a couple who've stopped speaking. Silas had been coaxing it back to health for weeks, and yesterday evening he and Mara had worked on it together, shoulder to shoulder in the lamplight, his fingers guiding hers on the crutch wire, and it had felt — she let herself remember it honestly, because she might not get to again — it had felt like being married to someone in some older, quieter sense of the word. Two people keeping one time.

But the case stood open now, the pendulum hanging stilled, and the weights should have been resting on the board below, and they were not. Mara fetched the lamp and looked into the dark of the case, past the chimes, into the cabinet space beneath where the long drop of the weights lived, and saw that the false floor her great-grandfather had built — the hiding place every Voss child was shown at eight years old, the place where letters and coins and, once, a runaway cousin had waited out their seasons — stood slightly proud of its frame.

Not shut. Recently opened. From inside or outside, impossible to say.

Mara knelt on the cold flagstones and lifted the panel with fingers that had gone numb without her noticing.

The satchel was there. And so was her brother, curled into the shadow behind it, asleep or pretending to be, his eyes opening to meet the lamplight with an expression she had never seen on him before and hoped never to see again: guilt with its chin up.

"Mara." His voice cracked on it. "Don't shout."

"Don't shout?" She kept her voice low anyway; the common room was twelve feet away through plaster and pine. "You stole a man's dispatches off the table of a republic and hid in a clock."

"I didn't steal it. I moved it." He crawled out stiffly, sixteen years old and trying to fill a doorway with his shoulders the way their father did. In his other hand, half-crumpled, was a single sheet of paper, and even upside-down she caught the official seal, the printed headings, and — her own name. Not hers alone. Three names in a column. *Voss.* "I read it," Tomas said, and now the crack in his voice widened into something rawer. "I know you won't believe me, but I wasn't going to. I just wanted to know why a clock repairman sleeps holding his bag like a widow. And then I read it, and Mara—" He swallowed. "There's a farm in it. Our farm. Grandfather's place, the one the government seized when Papa wouldn't sign. It says it comes back. To us. To *me*, it says me by name, and to you, and to Aunt Liesl's boy in the city. It says we're heirs."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still between them, and Mara understood that her life had just divided into before and after, and that the seam ran straight through a piece of paper.

"Tomas." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used with drunks and debtors. "Who else has read this?"

"Nobody. I only got it open last night, and the writing's hard, all the officialese—"

"Then why does the surveyor look like a man who's already been paid?"

Tomas's face answered her before his mouth could. He looked toward the window, toward the outbuildings, and said, too late, "I went out to the shed this morning. Brandt was there. He asked me if I'd found what I was looking for. He said it like — like he already knew the answer. He said things change hands up here all the time, and the road to the coast isn't the only road, if a person had goods and wanted a buyer." Tomas's jaw set, and for one second he looked so exactly like their father refusing to sign that Mara's heart broke a little. "I told him nothing. But Mara, he *knew*. He knew before he asked."

Which meant the paper had been whispering about itself since it entered the house. Which meant every hour it sat hidden in a clock case, it grew more expensive and more dangerous. Which meant her brother, her stupid brave baby brother, had made himself the owner of a secret that men killed for, and had hidden it in the family clock like a squirrel hiding a nut.

Mara took the satchel. Tomas grabbed her wrist.

"It's mine," he said. "Part of it. It says so. First thing in my whole life that ever said so."

"And it will be yours," she said, "if you're alive in the spring. That's the whole of it, Tomas. Papers don't make people safe. People make papers safe, and right now you are the least safe thing in this valley." She pried his fingers loose, gently, the way you unclench a hand from a fence rail in deep cold. "Go milk the cow. Be ordinary. Let me think."

He went, shoulders rigid, and she watched him through the window cross the yard with his head high, and she loved him so much it felt like injury.

* * *

She should have given the satchel back to Silas immediately. She knew that even as she climbed the stairs with it under her shawl instead, weighing it in her hands like a stone she was deciding whether to throw or keep.

Because she had read one line, upside-down, by lamplight, and it would not leave her.

*Estate of Voss: designate heirs per enclosed schedule; liquidate remainder to treasury.*

Liquidate. As though the farm were a stock of salt pork. As though the republic that took it from her father were coming back not to give it but to sell it out from under them one final time, to fund its own flight from its own ruin — and her name, and Tomas's, written on the schedule like items in an inventory.

Silas had carried this all winter. Had sat at her window seat with it on his knees. Had kissed her with these papers under the same roof, and said nothing.

She found him at the top of the stairs, drawn up tight against the wall, listening — to her, to Tomas, to the whole house breathing around its wound. His eyes went to the satchel under her arm and then to her face, and whatever he read there made him very still.

"You found it," he said.

"My brother found it. My brother read it. My brother is an heir, Silas — did you know that? Did you sit at my father's table every night knowing his children's names were in your bag?"

"I read them four days ago." His voice was low and wrecked and honest, and it was the honesty that undid her, because it meant the lying was finally over and had bought nothing. "Yes. I knew."

Below them, the common room door opened and closed. Boot heels crossed the floor — unhurried, deliberate — and stopped at the foot of the stairs. Brandt's voice rose pleasantly, addressing the whole house at once.

"Now that we're all awake," the surveyor called, "I think it's time we talked about what's in that clock."
