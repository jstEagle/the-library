## 9. The Last Berth

The bells woke them — not the inn's clock, which had kept honest time since Silas repaired it, but actual bells, three of them, rung from the tree line where the pass road dropped into the valley. Rescue.

Silas was downstairs before his boots were laced. Through the frost-furred window he saw a sleigh laboring up the last rise, its runners throwing white spray, two gray horses steaming like kettles. A driver in a government greatcoat, and behind him an empty bench meant for one passenger. One.

"Room for a single rider," the driver called, stamping into the yard. "Weather turns tonight. After that the pass is shut till April. Who's it to be?"

The inn erupted. The surveyor, Brandt, was already at the driver's elbow, talking low and fast, money changing the shape of the conversation. Silas watched the driver's face go from official to uncertain in the space of ten seconds.

He turned back toward the common room and found Mara standing at the foot of the stairs, Tomas behind her, both of them still in nightclothes, her hand resting on her brother's shoulder like an anchor chain.

* * *

Brandt made his move at noon.

He'd been drinking since the sleigh arrived, but his eyes were clear as glass, and he had positioned himself between Silas and the stairs with the unhurried confidence of a man who had done this arithmetic already.

"One berth," he said. "One satchel. I've done the sums, Almeida. You carry papers worth more than this whole valley, and there are people on the other side who'll pay for them — or pay me for telling them where they froze. Ride out with me. Hand them over at the coast. We split whatever's offered, or you keep your silence and I keep my cut."

"And if I refuse?"

Brandt smiled. It was almost friendly. "Then when the thaw comes, I ride down alone, and I describe you. The coat you wear. The clockman who wasn't a clockman. There's a reward for couriers from that ministry — did you know? Dead or delivered. They don't much care which."

Behind him, Silas heard the floorboard creak — Mara, at the kitchen door, flour to her wrists, listening to everything.

* * *

Father Anselm was dying in the little chapel room the inn had given him, and he knew it, and he was peaceful about it in a way that frightened Silas more than any threat.

Silas found him propped against pillows, lips gray, hands folded. He had gone out into the storm two nights ago chasing Silas through the wood, and the cold had gotten into his chest and would not leave.

"Courier," Anselm said. His voice was a ruin. "Come to gloat?"

"No."

"Good. Gloating is undignified." He coughed, wet and long. "I never lied to you about the essentials. There are men who would have protected those heirs. Protected them better than a burning government ever will."

"You'd have taken the papers unopened. You'd have decided their lives without asking them."

"Yes." Anselm closed his eyes. "That is what power is. I only ever wanted you to admit it." A pause. "You should know — the ministry wires its field reports through relay stations. Yours went missing six weeks ago. They believe the courier dead. You have been carrying ghosts, Silas Almeida. No one is coming for you but the snow."

Silas stood very still.

"They consider you spent," the priest said softly. "Consider what that frees you to do."

* * *

The standoff came at dusk, because of course it did. Everything in that inn happened at the hour the light failed.

Brandt had the driver's ear, a pistol he produced with theatrical reluctance, and the theory that possession of the sled bench was nine-tenths of the law. The innkeeper Voss stood in his own doorway with a fire iron. Mara stood between them all, and Silas realized she had been standing between things her whole life.

"The boy rides," Brandt was saying, magnanimous as an emperor. "I'll even allow it. The heir travels under my protection, for a reasonable fee to be negotiated with whoever holds his estate—"

"He rides alone," Silas said.

Every head turned.

He hadn't planned the sentence. It had simply arrived, fully formed, the way the truth does when you finally stop negotiating with it. He looked at Tomas — sixteen, sharp-eyed, the boy who had read stolen papers by candlelight and learned he mattered — and he understood that the choice had never really been between the ministry and the rivals. It was between his life and someone else's, laid out on a sled bench, and there was only ever going to be one honest answer.

"Tomas goes," Silas said. "As my escort. Official courier's business — witness transfer of documents. That's the protocol. The driver can't refuse a documented escort."

"There's no documentation," Brandt said.

Silas took the satchel from inside his coat and held it up. "There will be. I'll write it now and seal it. And if anyone questions the boy at the coast, the seal speaks for me." He met Brandt's eyes. "And you'll be here when the thaw comes, describing a courier who officially left in December. Who does that story get sold to, exactly? The men who think I'm already dead?"

Brandt's pistol hand wavered. Behind him, the driver said quietly, "Pass seals at moonrise. Decide."

* * *

They dressed Tomas in the yard, in falling dark, breath hanging in clouds. Silas gave the boy his coat — heavy wool, ministry-lined, warm as a held breath — and Tomas protested until he didn't.

"The satchel's empty," Silas told him, low. "Seals and blank paper. The real pages are somewhere no one will find them, and some of them belong to you. Your sister knows where. When spring opens, follow what she tells you and trust no surveyor."

"I don't understand why it's me."

"That's why it's you." Silas gripped his shoulders. "Listen. At the coast, ask for the harbormaster's office and give my name. Silas Almeida, courier, seventh dispatch column. They'll log you and forget you, which is the safest thing a government can do for anyone. You're my escort. Make them write it down. Paper remembers what people won't."

Tomas's eyes were wet. "Will they come after you?"

"They already stopped looking." He smiled, and found he meant it. "Go."

Mara embraced her brother for a long moment, saying something into his hair that Silas didn't try to hear. Then she stepped back and looked at Silas — and everything he wanted to say was in her face already, so he said none of it aloud, because some things break if you handle them.

The sleigh pulled away. Bells fading. Snow filling the tracks.

Mara stood beside him in the yard until the cold drove them both inside, and her hand found his in the dark of the doorway, and held on.

* * *

In the chapel, Father Anselm asked for him one last time.

"Burned?" the priest whispered.

"Not yet."

"Do it tonight. Not for the ministry. For yourself. Some things you carry so long you mistake them for a spine." Anselm's breath rattled. "You chose the boy over the bench. Whatever else you are, courier, you're not their instrument anymore. Act like it."

Silas sat with him while the candle burned down, and outside, the wind rose and began sealing the pass, stone by stone, flake by flake, over the road he could no longer take.

When he climbed to his room, the satchel felt lighter than it ever had — and heavier too, because tomorrow he would open it by the stove and feed the naming pages to the fire, page by page, and then walk down to the kitchen and tell Mara Voss every truth he owned.

Through the window, far below, the valley lights were going out one by one.

Except hers.
