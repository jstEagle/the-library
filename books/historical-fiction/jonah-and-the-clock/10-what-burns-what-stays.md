## 10. What Burns, What Stays

The stove in the back kitchen still held last night's heat when Silas fed it the first page.

He had chosen the hour carefully. Four in the morning, the inn asleep, Tomas three days gone down the pass with his coat on the boy's shoulders and his name in the boy's mouth — *escort to a government courier*, a fiction that would keep him fed and questioned in equal measure until he reached the coast. The fire needed tending anyway. That was the story Silas would tell himself if anyone asked why he was awake: the fire needed tending.

No one asked. No one would.

The dispatches lay on the table in their leather satchel, lighter now than they had ever been, because half of what made them heavy he had already decided to destroy. He unrolled them one last time under the lamp and read the names again, the way a man reads a sentence he already knows by heart, hoping it will surprise him.

*Voss, Mara — recognized as lawful heir of the second portion.*

*Voss, Tomas — recognized as lawful heir of the third portion.*

And beneath them, in the dry clerk's hand that had signed away everything the republic still owned: *the said estates to be liquidated without delay, proceeds remitted to the provisional treasury at Vellmar, all prior claims extinguished.*

All prior claims extinguished. As if Mara could be extinguished by a phrase. As if a boy of sixteen could carry a third portion across a frozen pass in place of a coat.

Silas held the first page over the stove door. His hand did not shake. He had expected it to shake, and its steadiness frightened him more than trembling would have — proof of how easily the body learns to burn things.

The paper caught. He watched the ink go brown, then bright, then nothing, the name *Voss* flaring and folding into itself like a hand closing. He fed the second page after it, then the third, then the schedule of liquidation with its columns of valuations — so many hectares of timber, so many head, so much for a house and the land under it, priced the way governments price the places people sleep.

What remained he squared into a neat packet: the outer seals, intact, blank where the orders had been, the wax unbroken because he had lifted the pages through the fold with a heated knife and a patience he was not proud of. To any inspecting officer the satchel would look exactly as it had looked on the coach, on the bridge, in every checkpoint between here and the capital. Sealed. Weightless. A promise with nothing inside it.

He thought about lying to Mara anyway. It would have been simple. She had loved him once under a name that wasn't his; she could go on loving a courier who had faithfully delivered nothing. But he had stood at Tomas's sleigh three days ago and put his own coat around a boy's shoulders, and somewhere in that gesture he had used up the last of his capacity for the other thing. There is only so long a man can carry two names before one of them starves.

So when she came down just past dawn — she always came down first, he'd learned that much in a winter — he had the satchel closed on the table and the smell of smoke in the room, and he said:

"Sit down, Mara. I'm going to tell you everything, and then you're going to be angry for a very long time, and I'd rather you were sitting."

She sat. She poured herself coffee from the pot that never went cold. Her hands were steady, and he understood suddenly that she had been waiting all winter for this conversation, the way the inn waited all winter for the thaw.

*

She had known since the clock.

That was the first thing he learned, in the strange arithmetic of confession: how little of it was news. She had known since February, when they'd rebuilt the escapement together and his fingers had moved through the gear train without hesitation — not the fingers of a clock repairman learning a mechanism, but a man remembering hands he'd used before, in another life, on another kind of delicate work. Courier's hands. Locksmith's hands. Hands that opened things that were sealed.

"I didn't know what you carried," she said. "Only that you carried it like it was alive. Like it might bite through the leather if you slept too deep."

"It should have bitten me months ago."

"Tell me the rest."

So he told her the rest. The capital in its death spiral, ministries burning their own files. The colonel who had handed him the satchel with instructions to open it only if he reached the coast — instructions designed, he understood now, to make sure the contents died with him if the mountain died with him first. Couriers were not meant to arrive. Couriers were meant to be *plausible*. A sealed satchel arriving empty was a tragedy; a satchel that never arrived was an expense.

"The priest told you," Mara said. "In March."

"He told me the government had listed me among its losses already. He was right. I checked the passenger manifest of the mail sled that came through — my name was struck through in the casualty column." He almost smiled. "I've been dead since January. It's been very restful."

But she didn't laugh, so he kept going, all the way down. The surveyor's offer. The nights he'd spent listening to footsteps in the hall. The page he'd read first, out of all of them — hers — because some part of him had known before he broke the seal what the paper would say, and had needed to see her name written in a stranger's hand, claimed by a state that had never done a single thing for her family except take from it.

"You burned it," she said. Not a question.

"I burned it. Yours and Tomas's both. The estate order too. What's left couldn't hang a laundry line."

"And the court-martial. When you walk back into Vellmar with nothing."

"They'll want to know what happened to the dispatches. I'll tell them the truth. Snow took the bridge, winter took the pages, and a dying republic's paper was worth less than a living boy in a borrowed coat." He turned the satchel over in his hands. "They won't believe me. It doesn't matter. A government that liquidates orphans to fund its own escape has no moral standing to judge a courier who chose wrong on purpose."

"Chose wrong," Mara repeated, and there it was — not anger yet, but the ground anger would grow from. "You're calling it wrong."

"I'm calling it what the tribunal will call it. What I call it—" He stopped. Looked at her straight, which was the hardest thing he'd done all winter, harder than the stove, harder than the knife under the wax. "What I call it is the first honest work I've ever done."

*

Mara walked out to the yard while the road opened.

It opened slowly, the way everything in the high country did — first a brown seam down the middle of the white, then water talking in the ditches, then, on the ninth morning of April, the sound of wheels that no one in the inn had heard since November. The mail sled going down. Then, a day later, a cart. Then footprints.

Silas packed almost nothing. He had arrived in autumn with a satchel and a false trade, and he was leaving with the same satchel and no trade at all, unless you counted the repair of one heirloom clock, which now kept better time than it had in forty years. He left the tools. He left them deliberately, arranged in the clock case beside the weights, so that whenever the mechanism needed him again there would be a reason that wasn't him.

At the door he stopped and looked back at her, and whatever he had prepared to say — she could see him assembling it, sentence by sentence, the way he assembled alibis — fell apart under her look.

"Don't," she said. "Don't make it a speech. You'll only edit it."

So instead he said, "Tomas will write when he reaches the coast. If they give him trouble, send word to me at the Vellmar post — I'll be easy to find. Prisoners usually are."

"That's not funny."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

Then he did the small human things: touched the doorframe the way travelers do, nodded to the kitchen where the stove was warm, let her see him hesitate on the threshold for exactly one breath longer than pride allowed. And then he went down through the mud to the road, and she watched him go the way you watch anything that is walking away from you on purpose.

*

From the bridge, Mara could see the whole valley of the road: the black thread of meltwater below, the pines letting go of their snow in slow white sighs, the figure getting smaller without ever quite becoming nothing. He walked like a courier even now, she thought. Even going to his own trial. Even carrying a satchel full of blank seals and a confession he'd already given to the only person whose verdict mattered.

She had told him, that last night, that he could stay. She had said it plainly, without begging, the way you state a fact of geography: the inn has rooms, the road is long, the capital is a rumor. And he had taken her hand and held it against the face of the clock, so she could feel the tick coming up through the case and into her bones, and he'd said, "If I stay, I stay as a liar who got tired. If I go, at least I go as something true." She had hated him for it with a cleanliness that surprised her, the way you hate weather, or spring itself, for being exactly on schedule.

Behind her, through the open inn door, the heirloom clock struck eight. It had kept perfect time since February. It would keep perfect time tomorrow, and the day after, through whatever came — the estate clerks who might still come sniffing, the tribunal's men if they followed the road up, the ordinary seasons of an ordinary house that had briefly held a war inside it.

She stayed on the bridge until the figure disappeared where the road bent toward the low country. Then she went back inside, took up the poker, and opened the stove — and stopped.

There, laid flat on the coals' pale ash, untouched by any flame, was a single sheet she had not seen him leave: the liquidation order's cover leaf, blank of every name but one, written small at the bottom in a hand she knew better than her own.

*For Mara, who deserves a claim on something. This page is legally nothing. Keep it anyway. — S.*

She read it twice. Then she folded it small, put it inside the clock case behind the pendulum, and wound the mechanism, and set her hand flat on the warm wood, feeling the answer come back — steady, stubborn, keeping time.

Down the valley, faint as a heartbeat, the road went on toward Vellmar, and whatever was waiting there for a dead man walking.
