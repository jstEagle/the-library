## 1. The Weight of Water

The sea did not care that Paloma Vane was nobody, and this was precisely why she loved it.

Down here, five fathoms above the pearl beds of Saltmere Bay, there were no ledgers, no overseers, no quotas chalked on slate boards before dawn. There was only the blue-green hush and the weight of the water pressing kindly against her ears, and the oysters below, patient as stones, waiting for her hands to know them.

She counted by feel. That was the trick no one had taught her—her mother had, but her mother had taught her so many things that didn't appear in any company manual. The right hand swept the seabed in a slow arc while the left gathered, and Paloma kept tally in her head the way other people kept grudges: faithfully, and without letting it show.

*Four. Seven. Eleven.*

An oyster's shell told you everything if you listened with your fingers. A young one sat light in the palm, edges still thin as crockery. An old one had heft, a settled gravity, like a coin worn smooth from too many pockets. And once—only once, three years ago—her thumb had found the particular deep ridging of a shell that held something round and secret inside, and she had made the mistake of smiling about it underwater, where nobody could see her, and then come up and made the further mistake of not hiding it fast enough.

She'd been docked two weeks' wages for "concealment of company property." The pearl had gone into the company strongbox. The smile she'd learned to drown.

*Nineteen. Twenty-three.*

Above her, distorted into a wobbling silver coin by the surface, the skiff bobbed with Overseer Graddick in it, counting her the way she counted oysters. She could imagine his lips moving. He counted everyone. Divers down, divers up, minutes elapsed, breaths held—he tracked it all in a greasy little notebook he believed nobody knew about, though everyone knew, because Graddick believed himself invisible the way only truly visible men can.

Paloma had spent five years learning the opposite discipline: how to be seen exactly as much as required and not one whit more. It was an economy, silence. You budgeted it. You spent a word here, a nod there, and never more than the situation demanded, because every unnecessary syllable was a coin out of your purse and an entry in somebody's notebook. *Vane, P. Unremarkable. Slow but adequate. No family to speak of.* The last part was even true now, which made it easier to say without flinching.

*Thirty-one. Thirty-five.*

Her mother would have laughed at the arithmetic of it. Sera Vane had been the finest diver on the Saltmere beds before the cough took her, and she had never once in her life been quiet when quiet wasn't strictly necessary. She sang underwater—everyone said it was impossible, and everyone who worked the east beds swore they'd heard it, a low thrumming through the water like a hymn played on a half-remembered instrument. When Paloma dove now, in the cold blue mornings, she sometimes thought she could almost make out the tune. She never let herself try too hard. Trying too hard led to wanting, and wanting led to being someone, and being someone, in Saltmere, in the shadow of the Meridian Trading Company, was how people disappeared.

*Forty. Forty-two.*

Enough. Graddick's arithmetic demanded forty per descent for a third-year diver, and Paloma delivered forty-three or forty-four, never more, never fewer. Surplus invited attention. Attention was the one currency this town devalued daily.

She kicked for the surface, breaking through into sunlight and the smell of salt and tar, and spat out her mouthpiece with the practiced economy of a woman who had learned not to waste even her own breath.

"Forty-two," Graddick said, not looking up from his slate. He was a narrow man, Graddick, like something the tide had left standing, with a waxed mustache he tended the way other men tended grudges.

"Forty-four," Paloma said. "You lost two while I was under."

His eyes came up. For a heartbeat something flickered there—the old wariness, the question of whether she was being clever—and then it passed, because Paloma Vane being clever was about as likely as Paloma Vane being rich, and everyone knew both impossibilities by heart. He grunted and scratched a number.

"Two minutes fifty," he said. "Slower than last week."

"The bed's picked over," she said, treading water, keeping her voice flat as a calm day. "East shallows are giving shells, not pearls. You want speed, put me on the deep line and lose a diver a season."

"I'll put you where the company says to put you."

"Then put me where I am and mark forty-four."

A pause. Somewhere behind them a gull screamed its opinion of both of them. Graddick scratched again—she watched him do the small dishonest arithmetic of a man who'd rather have accurate numbers than admit he'd miscounted—and looked back at his slate, and Paloma swam for the skiff's ladder, and neither of them had won, which was the outcome she aimed for in most contests. Not losing was a kind of wealth. She'd learned to live on it.

* * *

The workshop smelled of brine and sawdust and the particular sour sweetness of ten thousand opened oysters, a smell that had stopped registering to Paloma years ago the way your own name stops sounding like a word if you say it enough. Inside the long shed, thirty-odd women and a scattering of men bent over shucking tables, knives flashing in the gray morning light that came through windows fogged with salt-spray. The sound was constant and soothing, a rain of small clicks: shells prised, shells dropped, shells sorted.

Bess Halloway presided over the sorting table like a general who had long ago concluded the war was unwinnable but intended to conduct it with dignity anyway. She was sixty if she was a day, broad as a barge, with forearms that could crack a walnut between wrist and palm and frequently did, for entertainment, during the slow afternoon hours. She'd been at the workshop since before Paloma was born. She was possibly at the workshop before the workshop was born. Nobody asked; Bess rewarded questions with work.

"Forty-four," Paloma reported, emptying her net onto the sorting tray.

"Heard you the first time through the floor." Bess's fingers moved over the pile, quick and blind-seeming, separating keepers from culls by some instinct older than the company itself. "Graddick marked you forty-three."

"He marked what keeps him honest-ish. Forty-three suits us both."

"Mm." Bess pried a shell, squinted, dropped the meat in the scrap pail and a small blister-pearl into the chipped cup at her elbow. "That's the company's cup," she said, to nobody, in the tone of a woman filing a fact for later use.

Paloma took her place at the shucking table beside Teo, who was seventeen and all elbows and enthusiasm, and who greeted her with the conspiratorial nod of a boy who considered them comrades in arms merely because they shared a table. Teo had been at the workshop two years and had not yet learned the local religion of keeping your head down. Paloma privately gave him another six months before the place ground the shine off him, and privately dreaded it.

"You went past the marker buoy again," Teo said, low, knife working. "I saw you. Past it, out toward the old channel. Graddick'll—"

"Graddick marked forty-three. Graddick is satisfied." Paloma split an oyster with a clean twist of the blade. "And I didn't go past the buoy."

"I *saw*—"

"You saw a shadow in bad light." She glanced at him, just long enough. Teo subsided, wounded, and attacked his next shell with the vigor of the misunderstood.

It was true, is what bothered her. She *had* gone past the buoy. Not far—a dozen strokes along the old channel where the beds hadn't been worked since before the war, where the water ran darker and colder and the oysters grew fat and undisturbed. She hadn't taken anything from there. She hadn't even really looked. Her body had simply carried her that way, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth, because her mother had worked the old channel, and because some mornings the pull of that got past Paloma's discipline before her discipline woke up.

This was the trouble with having a dead mother who'd been magnificent. You kept swimming toward her ghost.

"Bess," called the floor manager from the far end, a reedy man named Ostler whose entire personality fit comfortably in a ledger book, "company clerk wants the weekly tallies by noon, and he wants them *legible*, which rules you out—"

"Bess writes a fairer hand than the clerk's own mother," said Bess, not looking up, "and the clerk's mother taught letters, which I know because I paid her to teach me, which is why I'm doing the tallies and he's doing whatever it is clerks do, which is nothing measurable."

A ripple of laughter went down the tables, quickly suppressed—laughter at the company adjacent was tolerated, laughter at the company proper was not—and Ostler retreated with the dignity of a man who had learned to pick his battles by losing most of them.

Paloma smiled into her shells. This was the other thing they never wrote in the notebooks: that the workshop was not merely a place where the company extracted wealth from the sea through their backs. It was a commons, a country, a loose federation of salt-cracked hands and sharp tongues. Bess had run it longer than any overseer. The divers covered for each other's slow days, shared cough remedies, buried each other's dead, and lied to inspectors as one body, seamlessly, without rehearsal. Five years ago this had frightened Paloma—belonging anywhere was dangerous. Now it was simply the water she moved through. You didn't notice water until someone threatened to drain it.

Which was, she reminded herself, shelling steadily, not happening. Nothing was happening. Nothing ever happened. That was the arrangement, and she upheld her side of it admirably.

"Company business today," Ostler announced from the doorway, reading from a paper with visible labor. "Census update. Crown requires current rolls for all company properties. A census officer arrives within the week. All persons are to give full and truthful answers, per statute. Full and *truthful*," he repeated, with the emphasis of a man who found truth exotic. "Names, ages, parentage, place of birth, term of indenture, and next of kin."

The room's rhythm didn't change, but Paloma felt it change anyway, the way you feel a storm still miles offshore—not in the air, but in the water, a wrongness in the pull of the tide. Around her, knives clicked. Shells dropped. But a dozen pairs of eyes had gone briefly, carefully elsewhere, and a dozen minds had done the same swift sum she had: *parentage. Next of kin.*

For the indentured, the census was routine indignity—they were counted like cargo twice a year regardless. But parentage. Next of kin. Those were questions with teeth in them, if you had anything in your history worth biting.

Paloma had exactly one item in her history worth biting, and she had spent twenty-two years—first her mother's lifetime, then her own—keeping it wrapped and buried.

"Full and truthful," Bess murmured beside her, sorting, not looking at her. It might have been mockery of Ostler. It might have been, in Bess's particular dialect of ordinary sentences, a message. With Bess you could never tell, which was the point of Bess.

"Mm," said Paloma, which meant, in her dialect, *I heard nothing, I know nothing, I am nobody,* and Bess's shoulders settled a fraction, which meant *good girl,* and the clicking went on.

* * *

Evening came down on Saltmere the way it always did: the harbor turning to hammered copper, the company warehouse doors closing with the finality of a verdict, the taverns exhaling their first smoke and song. The barracks above the workshop filled with the smell of barley soup and wet wool. Paloma ate hers on the bench by the window, alone by choice, watching the gulls fight over the day's scraps on the tideline.

Five years. Her indenture had five years left of its original seven—no. Fourteen months. Fourteen months and some days; she kept the count the way she kept the oyster count, faithfully, invisibly. Fourteen months and then her contract with the Meridian Trading Company would expire, and she would walk out of these gates owing nothing to anyone, and she would be free to be unremarkable somewhere else entirely. A fishing village down the coast. Inland, even—she'd heard there were places with no sea at all, which she couldn't quite imagine and didn't trust, but the idea of a town where no one knew what a Vane was had a certain perfume.

She finished the soup, washed her bowl, and climbed the narrow stair to the loft she shared with two weavers' widows who slept like the dead and a girl named Marta who talked in her sleep about cheese.

Her sea chest sat at the foot of her cot. It had been her mother's before it was hers—sea chest, cot, and daughter all passed along the same grim chain of company custody when Sera Vane died—and Paloma had not opened it past the top layer in five years. The top layer held practical things: a spare shucking knife, wool stockings, a hair comb missing four teeth. Below that lay the rest, and the rest was her mother, and Paloma had arranged her life around the distinction between grief and drowning.

Tonight, for no reason she could name—or for a reason shaped exactly like the phrase *full and truthful answers regarding parentage*—she knelt and lifted the lid all the way.

Clothes, folded by a dead woman's hands, smelling of nothing now but cedar and time. A diving cap. A coil of line. And beneath the linen, flat and hard-edged, the things she remembered and had hoped she'd forgotten: her mother's papers. A marriage writ for a marriage that had never quite happened, the groom lost to a boat and a winter. Wage slips. And tucked inside a prayer book, because her mother had hidden important things in the one volume no overseer would ever ask a diver to produce—

Paloma sat back on her heels.

There was a letter in the prayer book she had no memory of. Folded small, sealed with plain wax, and burned along one edge—as if someone had tried to feed it to a fire and changed their mind, or been interrupted. Someone had been interrupted. Her mother did not change her mind about fires.

She didn't open it. Her hands were steady on her knees and her heart was going like she was at depth already, and some animal part of her, the part that had kept her invisible for five years, said clearly: *not tonight, not yet, burn it, bury it, you know what paper does in this town, paper is the sharpest thing on the coast.*

But underneath that voice, older and deeper, in a register she only ever heard underwater—half-song, half-hymn—her mother said what she had always said, hoisting Paloma as a child onto the skiff's gunwale, laughing at the gray morning:

*"The sea gives back everything it takes, little bird. Your job is to recognize it when it comes."*

Paloma picked up the half-burned letter and broke the seal.

Outside, far down the lane toward the company house, a lantern was moving against the flow of the evening traffic—steady, official, unhurried—toward the workshop gates. She didn't see it. She was reading.
