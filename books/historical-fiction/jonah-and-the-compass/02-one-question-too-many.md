## 2. One Question Too Many

The census taker arrived on a Tuesday, which Paloma would later think was fitting. Tuesdays were the days nothing happened. The oysters didn't open on Tuesdays. The pay clerk didn't come until Wednesday. Tuesday was the day the workshop breathed out, and she had built her whole life inside that breath.

She was mending a net on the drying rack when the gate creaked, and she knew before she looked up that the day had stopped being a Tuesday in any way that mattered.

He was not what she expected. She had expected someone fat and bored, the way company men usually were, a man who sweated through his collar and wanted only to be finished. This one was young—perhaps twenty-six—and walked through the yard like a man counting things. His coat was good but not too good. His boots were salt-stained. He carried a leather folio under one arm the way a surgeon carries knives: respectfully, but with intent.

"Morning," he said to the yard at large. "Elias Corvane, company census. Here to update the rolls."

Nobody looked up except Paloma, which was her first mistake.

Five years at the Meridian pearl workshop had taught Paloma Vane the fine art of being furniture. Divers were a dime a dozen along the Saltmere flats; a girl with sun-browned skin and no opinions was worth even less. She had made herself a study in the unremarkable. She mended nets competently but not brilliantly. She spoke when spoken to, and then briefly. When the overseer counted them onto the boats each morning, his eyes slid over her the way they slid over the coiled rope. That invisibility had kept her fed, housed, and—more importantly—unnoticed for eighteen hundred and twenty-five days, give or take the ones she'd spent sick.

Now the census taker's gaze crossed the yard, snagged on her face, and stayed a half-second longer than it should have.

"Vane," said Overseer Pratt, shuffling over with the master roll under his arm. "You'll want the workhouse ledger too. Forty-one souls on contract, plus the matron and kitchen staff."

"Forty-two," Corvane said mildly. "You're standing next to forty-two."

Pratt blinked. "What? No. Forty-one divers, I said."

"I count forty-one divers." Corvane's voice stayed pleasant. "Plus yourself makes forty-two persons present, though of course you're salaried and won't appear on the diver rolls." He smiled, and it was a perfectly ordinary smile, but Paloma felt the hairs on her arms lift. It was the smile of a man who counted things for pleasure.

*Don't be clever,* she willed him, going back to her net. *Clever men get counted.*

They set up at the long trestle table under the awning, and the divers filed past one by one while Corvane asked his questions. Name. Age. Port of origin. Term of indenture. Next of kin. The answers came dull as ballast stone. Jorin Hale, thirty-four, Saltmere, six years remaining, mother deceased. Teo Marsh, nineteen, Little Saltings, four years, none. On and on. Paloma sat at the end of the line, last in every queue by careful design, and watched the man work.

He wasn't fast. That surprised her. A man wanting to be done would rattle through; this one took his time, asked follow-ups, occasionally made small jokes that startled laughs out of people who hadn't laughed in weeks. To Teo he said, "Nineteen and already diving the deep beds? You must have lungs like a church organ," and Teo went pink as a boiled shrimp and grinned all afternoon afterward.

*Charm,* Paloma thought. *Worse than cruelty, charm.* Cruelty you could brace against.

By mid-afternoon the line had thinned to her. She set down her net, wiped her hands on her apron, and sat in the chair across from him with the expression of a woman asked to wait for a ferry.

"Name?"

"Paloma Vane."

"Age?"

"Twenty-three."

"Port of origin?"

"Saltmere."

"Term of indenture?"

"Five years served, one year and eleven months remaining." She did not have to consult anything for that number. It lived behind her teeth.

Corvane wrote, then looked up, and his pen hovered. "Next of kin?"

"None."

"Your roll says otherwise. It says mother: Sera Vane, listed here as deceased—" he turned the old page toward her, and there it was, her mother's name in faded copperplate, five years dead and still lying on company paper like a coin at the bottom of a pool "—but it doesn't say deceased when. Or where. And it lists no father at all."

"The sea keeps its own records," Paloma said. "Divers' mothers don't generally leave forwarding addresses."

That was supposed to end it. It was the kind of answer that made clerks tired, that closed conversations like a door. But Corvane tilted his head, and she watched—with the peculiar slow horror of a person watching their own boat take on water—as something in his face sharpened rather than dulled.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But the company does. Which brings me to my last question." He dipped his pen. "Her maiden name. Your mother's family name, before Vane."

The yard went very quiet, or perhaps it only went quiet inside Paloma's ears, the way it does ten feet underwater when your own pulse becomes the loudest thing in the world.

No one had asked that question aloud in years. Not since she was a girl, when a drunk harbor clerk had once shouted it after her mother down a fish-market street and Sera Vane had laughed and thrown an orange at him. Nobody asked because nobody cared, and nobody caring had been the entire architecture of Paloma's survival.

"I wouldn't know," she said.

It was, objectively, a terrible lie. She heard it come out of her own mouth and winced internally. Her voice had gone up half a tone. Her hands, which could count oysters blind in nine fathoms of dark water and never miss, had found her apron hem and begun pleating it like a concertina.

Corvane noticed. Of course he noticed. He was a man whose entire profession was noticing.

"You wouldn't know," he repeated, without mockery, writing nothing. "Your mother raised you here at the workshop from—let's see—the age of four. You lived in the same two rooms for nineteen years. And you never learned her maiden name."

"Families don't always talk," Paloma said, and this part at least came out true enough to hurt.

Something shifted in his expression—not suspicion exactly, or not only suspicion. A kind of care. It was worse. "Mine didn't either," he said quietly, and for one absurd moment Paloma felt a stab of kinship with the man currently dismantling her life. Then he added, "I'll put 'unknown.' It happens more often than you'd think." And he wrote it, and blotted it, and closed the folio.

But she saw how he filed the moment away. Not in the folio. In himself. Behind the eyes, in the place where a man keeps the things that don't fit on forms.

"Thank you for your time, Miss Vane."

"Miss Vane" got up and walked back to her net on legs that felt borrowed, and did not let her hands shake until she was holding twine again, where shaking could pass for the wind.

* * *

Elias Corvane noticed three things about the girl Paloma Vane, and the trouble was that the third one shouldn't have mattered.

The first thing: she lied badly, which in his experience meant she was honest most of the time. Liars who practiced were smooth as river stones. This one had floundered like a landed fish, and had clearly known she was floundering, and had floundered anyway—which suggested either courage or desperation, possibly both.

The second thing: she watched hands. Not faces—hands. While he questioned the other divers, he'd caught her tracking his pen, the overseer's keys, the cook's cleaver. People who watch hands are people who've learned that hands do the damage. Faces just announce it.

The third thing was simpler and stranger: when he'd mentioned her mother, grief had moved through her face like a shadow under the surface of water—there and gone, but real, and deep, and old.

He thought about all three things that evening in the rented room above the chandlery, with the census folio open on the desk and the lamplight pooling yellow around his inkwell.

There were forty-one entries for the Meridian workshop now complete. Thirty-nine of them were exactly what entries always were: names, ages, ports, terms, kin. One entry—Jorin Hale—had a brother in the northern fisheries worth a marginal note for the labor board. And one entry had a gap in it shaped like a woman named Sera.

Elias turned to the back of the folio, where he kept the papers that did not belong to the census at all.

His commission had seemed harmless enough when it arrived from the district office, tucked between a fuel requisition and a circular about cholera. *In the course of updating the company rolls,* the covering letter read, *attention is drawn to irregular bloodlines arising within certain contracted populations. Where lineage cannot be established to standard, flag the relevant entries for administrative review. This is a routine tidying measure.*

Routine tidying. He had been a census man for three years and he had learned what those phrases meant the way you learn the weather in a new port: slowly, then all at once. *Irregular bloodlines* meant somebody upstairs believed that somewhere in the company's workforce there was blood that ought not to exist, and that paper was the cheapest shovel available for burying it.

And now, on his second day at the Meridian workshop, a diver with sea-grey eyes and quick hands had nearly knocked over her chair when asked for her mother's maiden name.

Elias rubbed his eyes. The lamp guttered. Downstairs, the chandlery dog barked at gulls, which was a stupid habit for a dog but an honest one.

Here was the arithmetic of his position, laid out as neatly as he laid out everything: If Paloma Vane's lineage was irregular—if her mother had been something other than what the rolls said—then his duty was to flag the entry. Flagging took one word in a margin. *Review.* After that, the machinery took over, and Elias Corvane would never learn what the machinery did, because the machinery was designed so that men like him never had to learn.

Against that: one word. One margin. One girl who mended nets with the fierce concentration of someone guarding a border, and who had answered *I wouldn't know* in a voice that cracked like thin ice.

He was not a romantic man. He counted things. Numbers didn't lie, though people used them to; forms didn't weep, though they caused plenty of weeping. He had chosen the census service precisely because it was clean—a man, a list, a truth. Write down what is. Nothing more was asked of him, and nothing more should be.

But his instructor, old Master Quennel, had drilled one other sentence into every census apprentice, back when Elias was young enough to believe it was procedure and not philosophy: *A record is a promise to the future.* Whatever you write, you are swearing that it is so. Write carelessly and you lie to everyone who comes after you.

*I wouldn't know,* she had said, in a voice that knew everything.

Elias pulled the folio toward him, found the Vane entry, and read it again. Mother: Sera Vane. Deceased. Father: not recorded. Maiden name: unknown.

Unknown. He left it. It was, after all, technically accurate—the girl didn't know, or claimed not to, and he had no evidence otherwise. He was not obliged to chase shadows. He was not obliged to notice that the overseer had tensed, just slightly, when Corvane had asked the question—that Pratt had been polishing his spectacles with sudden great interest, and had put them back on a shade too quickly, like a man rearranging his face.

Hm.

He had noticed that, hadn't he. He noticed everything; it was his curse and his trade. Pratt hadn't tensed at the lying. Pratt had tensed at the question itself. Which meant the overseer knew something about Sera Vane's name worth tensing over, which meant the question was not routine anywhere except on paper.

Elias sat back. Outside, the harbor slapped against pilings, patient as a debt collector.

He could flag the entry tomorrow. It would be easy. It would be *duty*, in the small cold sense of the word, and no one would ever blame him, because no one would ever tell him what came after.

Or he could do the other thing, the thing that was also duty in the larger, older sense—the promise-to-the-future sense—and simply write what was true: that a record had a hole in it, and holes in records deserved looking into, and looking into things was, in fact, his actual job.

He blew out the lamp and lay down in the narrow bed and listened to the harbor work at the pilings.

He decided nothing, which was itself a decision, and he knew it.

In the morning he would go back to the workshop. There were supplementary schedules to verify—the company house records, the lease documents, the old payment ledgers, all of which the district office wanted summarized. Perfectly routine. And if, in the course of that perfectly routine work, a certain name happened to appear in a certain old ledger—

Well. He was a man who counted things. Sometimes the count told you where to look.

Across the dark harbor, in the women's barracks of the Meridian workshop, Paloma Vane lay awake doing her own arithmetic, and arriving—several hours before he did—at the same conclusion:

Sooner or later, that man was going to open the ledgers. And her mother's name was written in them.
