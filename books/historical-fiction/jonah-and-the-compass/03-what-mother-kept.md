## 3. What Mother Kept

The sea chest sat at the foot of Paloma's bunk the way it had sat for five years: as furniture, as footrest, as a place to stack the mended nets that belonged to no one in particular. It had not been opened since the day she'd dragged it from the dormitory loft after her mother's funeral, and in five years she had developed an impressive array of justifications for leaving it shut.

It was heavy. It was bad luck. The hinge was rusted. She was tired. She would do it on her birthday. She had done it on her birthday, once, and had gotten as far as lifting the lid before Bess called her up to help with a delivery of salt pork, and that had been that.

Tonight there was no salt pork, no birthday, and no pretending anymore. There was only Elias Corvane's voice, polite as a fish knife: *And your mother's maiden name, miss? For the rolls?*

She had said *Vane*, because Sera Vane was all her mother had ever been, publicly. But she'd hesitated first—a half-second too long, a hair's width of silence—and he'd seen it. Census takers probably got trained to see it, the way divers got trained to see the shimmer of a pearl inside an open shell. You couldn't unsee someone noticing you.

So here she was, on her knees on the cold floorboards with a candle stub guttering beside her, working a rusted hinge with the flat of a butter knife and feeling like a burglar in her own room.

"Come *on*," she muttered. "You've survived storms. Survive this."

The hinge gave with a shriek that made her certain half the barracks had heard it. She froze, counted to ten, heard nothing but the usual symphony of the workshop at night—snoring, the creak of beams settling, somewhere below the endless lap of water against pilings—and lifted the lid.

The smell rose up out of it like a held breath finally released: brine and cedar and something floral gone to dust. Lavender, maybe. Her mother had rubbed lavender oil into her hands every night against the salt cracks. Paloma hadn't remembered that until this exact moment, and it hit her with the force of a wave she hadn't braced for.

She sat back on her heels and let it pass through her.

*You're being foolish,* she told herself. *It's a chest. It has clothes in it. Maybe a comb.*

It did have clothes—two dresses folded with a precision her mother had never once applied to anything else in life, which meant they were for occasions, which meant they had probably been worn twice. Beneath them lay a diver's knife with a bone handle; a coil of good cord; three shells arranged largest to smallest, which was exactly the kind of thing her mother would do and never explain; a small purse containing eleven coppers, which explained itself sadly enough; and, wrapped in oilcloth tied with waxed thread, a packet about the size of two hands cupped together.

Paloma held it for a long moment without unwrapping it.

The trouble with secrets, she had learned over five years of cultivating invisibility, was that most of them weren't worth their weight. People whispered things at her all the time—overheard arguments, drunken confessions, a supervisor skimming the weigh-house scales—and she had built a whole architecture of deafness around herself. What people didn't think you knew couldn't be used against you. Her mother had taught her that without ever saying it aloud. Sera Vane had been the least curious woman in Saltmere, ostentatiously incurious, and Paloma had always assumed it was temperament.

Now she wondered if incuriousness could be inherited the way eye color was.

"Right," she said to the chest, to the room, to nobody. "Curiosity. Just this once."

* * *

The oilcloth came away to reveal a locket and a letter.

The locket was heavier than it looked—good brass, the kind that didn't tarnish so much as darken, with a hairline pattern etched around the edge like ripples in sand. The clasp was stubborn. She worked it open with her thumbnail, holding it toward the candle, and the light caught the miniature inside.

She knew the face immediately.

Not because she'd met him—Augustin Meridian had been dead eleven years by the time Paloma drew her first breath—but because his face hung everywhere it could legally hang and several places it couldn't. Behind the weigh-house counter. In the company offices, where she delivered ledgers twice a month. On the shipping manifests, embossed into the wax seal like a small smug god. A broad man with a broad jaw, white hair swept back, side-whiskers like ship figureheads, and eyes that looked at you with the particular patience of someone who owned the dock you were standing on.

Old Augustin Meridian. Founder of the Meridian Trading Company. Builder of the workshop. Owner, in some sense that the lawyers could explain and Paloma could feel in her bones, of every oyster bed from here to the horizon.

Her mother had kept his portrait in a locket.

Paloma turned the locket over. Inside the far lid, folded small as a pressed flower, was a curl of paper. She eased it out. A strip of writing, faded brown ink:

*...and to my daughter, of whom I am not permitted to speak, I leave—*

That was all. The rest had been torn away, raggedly, long ago.

She read it four times. The words didn't change. *My daughter.* Not my dear friend. Not my esteemed colleague. Daughter.

"That's not—" she started, and stopped, because out loud it sounded even more absurd than in her head. Her mother, the pearl diver who ate standing up and swore like a stevedore, daughter of Augustin Meridian?

Except.

Except the company had paid her mother a pension when her lungs began to fail, though Sera had been off the active rolls for years. Except her mother had read—fluently, quickly, in a way that none of the other divers' children could account for, in a port city where reading was a merchant skill. Except the way the senior clerks went quiet and careful around her mother, respectful in the wary way of men who'd been told to be respectful without being told why.

Except the hesitation this afternoon, half a second wide, when Elias Corvane asked a question nobody had asked in fifteen years.

Paloma set the locket down carefully, as if it might go off, and picked up the second item from the oilcloth. The letter. Unlike the scrap in the locket, this one was whole in shape and ruined in substance—one entire edge charred black, brittle as autumn leaf, words eaten away mid-sentence. Someone had tried to burn it and failed, or repented halfway, or been interrupted. Folded once, burned once, hidden forever.

The surviving portion was written in a different hand than the scrap—a rounder hand, hurried:

*Sera—you must understand what you carry. My father acknowledged you to me and to no one else living, and what he acknowledged in blood he provided for in paper. There is a document—*

Char. Nothing. Char again.

*—before he changed his mind, or before those who would change it for him found the means. Do not trust the house. Do not trust the lawyers. If you ever need to prove who you are, the proof is where he—*

The rest was gone. Paloma stared at the burned edge until her eyes stung, as if staring harder might make ink reappear out of charcoal.

*What you carry.*

Carry. Not *what you are*. Carry, like cargo. Like something that could be handed down.

Paloma thought of her mother, in the last winter, pressing her dry cracked hands together and saying, out of nowhere, *Polly, if anything happens to me, don't let them count you.* She'd assumed it was the laudanum talking, or one of her mother's sayings—the woman had dozens, most of them about fish. *Don't let them count you.*

The census taker had come to count her.

"Oh," Paloma said quietly, to the empty room. "Oh, Mother."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat cross-legged on her bunk with the locket in one fist and the letter in the other and watched the candle burn down, and somewhere around the third bell she became aware that she was no longer alone in her own head. Something had moved in. Something with teeth.

Curiosity, she decided, was badly named. It sounded like a kitten. It behaved like a shark.

At dawn she did what she always did at dawn—she went to work, because indentured women did not get mornings off for family revelations. She dove the beds, counting oysters by feel in the grey-green cold, and for the first time in five years the counting failed her. Her hands knew the work; the rest of her was in her bunk with a burned letter. She surfaced with a respectable haul and no memory of gathering it, which frightened her more than the letter had. The water forgave nothing. Divers who thought about other things underwater eventually stopped thinking about things at all.

Bess caught her at the sorting tables.

Bess Harrow ran the workshop's domestic side with the serene tyranny of a woman who had buried two husbands and outlived every rule she'd ever been given. She was seventy, built like a mooring post, and missed nothing that happened between the gates. She was also the closest thing to family Paloma had left, in the way that people who feed you for a decade become family whether anyone signs papers or not.

"You're diving like a woman with her head in a bucket," Bess said, picking pearls out of Paloma's basket with quick red fingers. "Bad ones, too. Your mind wasn't down there."

"I'm fine."

"Mm." Bess held a pearl up to the light. "This one's got a flaw right through the heart. Fine, you say." She set it aside in the flawed pile instead of the worthless pile—a small mercy, done without comment—and then, without looking up: "You went through the chest."

Paloma's hands went still on the table. "How could you possibly—"

"Butter knife on the hinge. I heard it through two floors, girl, I'm not deaf, I'm old. There's a difference." Bess sorted three more pearls, unhurried. "Come walk with me to the drying sheds."

It wasn't a request. Paloma followed her out into the yard, where the morning fog was burning off the harbor and the racks of shell and rope stood in rows like patient ghosts. Bess waited until they were between the drying sheds, out of sightline of the overseer's window, before she turned.

"What did you find?"

"A locket," Paloma said. There was no point lying to Bess; Bess had a nose for lies the way sharks had a nose for blood, and besides, Paloma had never once managed it. "With a portrait in it."

"Of whom?"

"...Augustin Meridian."

Bess closed her eyes. She stood very still in the fog-damp air, and for a moment she looked every one of her seventy years, and older. When she opened them again, they were wet, which frightened Paloma more than anything the letter had said. In ten years she had seen Bess angry, amused, exhausted, and once—during the hurricane of '89—genuinely afraid. She had never seen her cry.

"And the letter," Bess said. It wasn't a question.

"How did you know about a letter?"

"Because I helped your mother hide it, you great fool, and I've spent fifteen years praying she'd thrown it in the tide like I told her to." Bess took Paloma's wrist—not gently, not roughly, the grip of a woman hauling her out of deep water. "Burned letter, torn paper, keepsake of a dead rich man. That's what you're carrying around under your bunk."

"You knew. All this time, you knew what she was?"

"I knew she was Sera, who pulled me out of a squall in '74 and never once let me forget it." Bess's jaw tightened. "What else she was, I knew the shape of, not the name of, and I slept better for it. Listen to me." She stepped closer. Around them the workshop was waking—footsteps, buckets, someone whistling badly near the gate—and Bess dropped her voice to almost nothing. "Whatever's in that chest, whatever name is written on whatever paper, it isn't yours. It's a hook, and the line attached to it goes all the way up to the big house on the hill, and the people holding that line have spent three generations making sure it never gets tugged. Your mother understood that. She stayed poor and quiet and alive for thirty years by understanding it. And now you've gone and opened the box."

"She's dead, Bess. Who's going to know I looked?"

"The census man." Bess said it flatly, like naming a storm. "Corvane. He asked about her maiden name yesterday and you flinched—I saw it from the matron's bench. Men like that don't ask questions for their health. They ask because somebody upstairs wants answers, and when the answers get interesting, the questions stop being routine. You think Aldous Meridian tours a pearl workshop because he loves nacre?"

Paloma said nothing. She had asked herself the same question, lying awake at third bell, and had not enjoyed any of her conclusions.

"So we burn it," Bess said. "Both pieces. The locket too—we melt it down or drop it off the end of the pier. Tonight. Before that clerk files whatever he files and someone upstairs starts wondering why a diver's daughter went pale when asked a simple question." She squeezed Paloma's wrist once, hard, and released it. "Throw it in the tide, Polly. The tide keeps everything and tells nobody. It's the only honest institution in this town."

"Bess." Paloma's voice came out smaller than she intended. "The letter said there's a document. Proof. That her father—that *Augustin*—" She couldn't say the word yet. It was too large for her mouth. "If it's true, then everything the company pays us, everything they take from us, the beds, the lease—"

"If a frog had wings it wouldn't bump its arse hopping. Get back to the tables."

But Bess walked away slower than she'd come, and Paloma noticed that her hands, hanging at her sides, had curled into fists—the fists of a woman who had known a secret for thirty years and carried it like a stone, hoping it would stay a stone and not turn out to be an egg.

* * *

The day crawled. Paloma sorted, hauled, mended, kept her eyes down, and felt the locket's weight in her thoughts the way you feel a splinter—small, wrong, impossible to ignore. Twice she nearly went back to her bunk. Twice she talked herself out of it. By evening bell she had constructed an elaborate compromise: she would not destroy anything tonight, and she would not decide anything tonight, and tomorrow she would definitely, absolutely decide.

Then the decision was taken out of her hands.

She came around the corner of the barracks after supper and stopped dead. There, at the gate, lantern in hand, coat still carrying the damp of the harbor fog, stood Census Taker Corvane.

He wasn't coming for her. Of course he wasn't—he was speaking to the gatekeeper, showing his company badge, gesturing with a sheaf of forms. A follow-up visit, routine, the rolls required verification within three days of initial inquiry, she could hear the phrases drifting across the yard, bland as porridge. But then his gaze traveled past the gatekeeper, along the row of bunks visible through the open dormitory windows, and found hers, and stopped.

He didn't smile. He didn't wave. He simply looked at her for a moment longer than courtesy required—the look of a man confirming an entry—and then returned to his paperwork.

Paloma walked to her bunk on legs that felt borrowed, sat down, and pulled the sea chest toward her.

Tomorrow, she had told herself. Decide tomorrow.

She reached inside and closed her hand around the locket, and the brass was warm, as if it had been waiting all day too.
