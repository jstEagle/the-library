## 5. Silvers and Salt

The harbor market at Saltmere opened before dawn and lied until noon.

That was Paloma's private theory of it, anyway. The fishmongers swore their catch had come in that morning when half of it had been packed in ice since Tuesday. The fruit sellers arranged their bruised peaches bruise-side down. And the spice merchant at the corner of the long shed sold what he called genuine Vashelle saffron, which Paloma happened to know was safflower dyed in turmeric water, because her mother had bought some once, sniffed it, laughed until she cried, and then paid for it anyway just to keep the old man's dignity intact.

Sera Vane had been generous that way. It was one of the few things about her that Paloma could still hold without flinching.

On Thursday mornings, the workshop sent Paloma to the market with a list and a canvas sack: salt pork, dried lentils, lamp oil, thread for Bess's endless mending. It was errand duty, technically a privilege, because it got you off the beds and out from under the overseer's counting eye for three hours. But it also put you among people, and people asked questions, and so Paloma had spent five years perfecting the market walk—head down, pace steady, eyes on the list, a woman so thoroughly unremarkable that even the gulls didn't bother her.

She was halfway through the fish shed, comparing prices for pork with the particular intensity of someone who has no intention of buying pork at either price, when she saw him.

Elias Corvane stood at the far end of the shed near the oyster barrows, wearing the same gray coat he'd worn to the census interviews, looking exactly like what he was—a company man out of his element, holding a notebook as though it were a shield. He was being lectured by Old Maretta, who ran the oyster stalls and who had once, famously, thrown a whole boiled crab at a tax assessor. Elias appeared to be taking notes on the lecture. Whether he was noting Maretta's opinions on the price of shellfish or her opinions on government clerks generally, Paloma couldn't tell, but Maretta's arms were windmilling in a way that suggested passion.

He should have been the last person Paloma wanted to see. He was the crack in her wall, the question she hadn't answered well, the *maiden name* hanging over everything like weather coming in off the water. She should have turned down the next aisle, taken the long way around the tannery stalls, and let the market swallow her the way it swallowed everyone else.

Instead she heard herself say, "You'll want to write this part down too."

Maretta stopped mid-windmill. Elias looked up, and for a moment his face did something unguarded—surprise, then something almost like relief—before settling back into official neutrality. "Miss Vane."

"Madame Maretta." Paloma nodded to the oyster seller, who narrowed her eyes at both of them with equal suspicion. "He's from the Meridian census office. He counts people."

"I count *records*," Elias said.

"That's worse," said Maretta, and went back to her stall.

Paloma hadn't planned any of it. That was the trouble, she realized later. Five years of careful invisibility, undone by an impulse she hadn't even examined. "Do you always take your leisure where people hate you most?" she asked, falling into step beside him without quite deciding to.

"I'm not taking leisure." He held up the notebook. "The office wants current household counts for the harbor district. Unregistered residents are a fineable offense. I'm doing my duty."

"And how's that going?"

"Maretta claims there are four people living in her stall." He glanced sideways at Paloma. "All of them named Maretta."

Paloma laughed before she could stop it—a real laugh, short and surprised out of her, the kind she hadn't permitted herself in public since her second year of indenture. She felt it leave her body like something stolen.

Elias noticed. Of course he noticed; noticing was his entire profession. But he had the grace to look away, out at the water, while she rearranged her face into its usual nothing.

"The pork is cheaper at Tullio's," she said, "if you're actually shopping and not just collecting fines."

"I'm not shopping."

"You're standing in a fish shed with no basket. That's suspicious behavior. I could report you to the census office."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile considering citizenship. "They'd want my maiden name first."

Paloma's stomach dropped, and she knew—he must have known, because his face went carefully blank in that clerkly way—that the joke had landed somewhere he hadn't entirely intended. Or perhaps entirely intended. She couldn't read him, and not being able to read a man was, in her experience, a form of danger.

"Coffee," she said.

"What?"

"There's a stall by the ferry steps. Two coppers a cup, and it tastes like they brewed it through an old sock. I'm going to buy one. You can stand there counting households if you like."

She walked. She didn't look back. That was the test, though she told herself it wasn't a test, it was just coffee, it was just that her feet had decided things without consulting her again, which seemed to be happening a great deal lately whenever this man was within twenty yards.

His footsteps caught up with hers before she reached the end of the shed.

* * *

The coffee stall at the ferry steps belonged to a widow named Ilse, who had exactly two benches, one pot, and a policy of asking no questions, which was why half of Saltmere's unofficial business was conducted on those benches between dawn and dusk. Paloma had chosen it for exactly that reason. If you sat at Ilse's, you became part of the furniture. Nobody remembered furniture.

Two cups came across the counter thick as tar. Elias wrapped both hands around his like a man warming himself at a fire in summer.

"You don't drink coffee much," Paloma observed.

"How could you possibly know that?"

"The way you're holding the cup. Like it might make conversation."

He looked down at his own hands and, with visible effort, loosened his grip. "My father drank tea. Real tea, from the northbound ships. He thought coffee was for dockworkers and revolutionaries."

"Was he right?"

"He was usually wrong about everything else." Elias sipped, and his whole face conducted a brief internal struggle. "This tastes like—" He stopped.

"Say it and I'll pour it in the harbor."

"—like a very honest cup," he finished diplomatically, and this time the smile did arrive, small and crooked, and Paloma discovered that it changed his whole face, which until now she had filed under *unremarkable*, the same category she kept for herself.

That was inconvenient.

"So," she said, and made her voice light, careless, a woman making small talk on a bench. "I've been meaning to ask. When does the census close? For the workshop, I mean. Are we counted and done, or will you be haunting us till spring?"

*Careful,* said the voice of five years' survival in the back of her skull. *Too eager.*

But she wanted to see what he'd do with it. That was the truth she wasn't admitting: she wanted to know whether he was dangerous the way a knife is dangerous or dangerous the way deep water is—which is to say, only if you went in.

"The workshop entries are complete," he said. "Certification comes from the district office. It's a formality."

"A formality." She turned the cup slowly on her knee. "Funny word. My mother used to say formalities were how the rich steal from the poor without breaking a sweat. A signature here, a seal there, and suddenly the thing that was yours isn't."

Elias was quiet for a moment. The tide lapped at the ferry steps behind them. Somewhere across the harbor, a bell buoy rang in the chop.

"That's true often enough," he said at last. "Most of the paperwork I process exists to make someone's life smaller. Evictions. Tax seizures. Debt rolls." He paused. "Some of it doesn't."

"Some of it?"

"The pension lists. Widow's allowances. Orphan registrations." He shrugged, self-conscious about it, as if admitting a weakness. "A record in the right book can be the only proof a person ever existed. Sera Vane is in my rolls now. Birth, trade, residence, death date. Whatever else happens, the record says she lived. Somebody wrote it down."

Paloma stared at him. Her heart was doing something complicated and painful beneath her ribs, because he had said her mother's name into the morning air as though it were an ordinary name, a safe name, a name that belonged on a page, and no one—not Bess, not the divers, not the priest at the burial—had spoken it aloud to her in five years. At the workshop she was Vane, singular, rootless, an entry with no lineage. Hearing *Sera* in a stranger's mouth was like surfacing too fast: everything went bright and ringing.

"You shouldn't say that name," she said quietly.

"I know." He didn't apologize. He looked out at the water instead, jaw tight. "I'm told I have a talent for saying precisely the wrong thing. My supervisor mentions it in every assessment."

"Your supervisor sounds perceptive."

"He is. He's also—" Elias stopped. Paloma watched him weigh something, watched the scales tip and resettle. "He's the one who assigned me to the company rolls in the first place. The Meridian accounts get special handling. Irregular entries get flagged."

The cold walked up Paloma's spine one vertebra at a time. *Irregular.* She set her cup down on the bench with great care, as though her hands might otherwise do something revealing.

"And what gets flagged, Master Corvane? Besides Maretta's four identical tenants?"

"Names that don't match their paper. Households that appear and vanish. Bloodlines with gaps in them." He said it flatly, like a man reading a tariff schedule, but his eyes came around to her and stayed there. "It's dull work. Mostly it's smugglers' wives and bigamists."

"Mostly."

"Mostly," he agreed.

The silence stretched. A gull screamed somewhere above the ferry steps. Paloma understood, with sudden clarity, that she was sitting at a crossroads marked out in coffee steam, and that whatever she said next would be carried in this man's notebook for the rest of both their lives.

So she decided to lie to him. A big lie, a bold lie, the kind that walks right up to danger and shakes its hand—the kind her mother would never have told, and therefore the kind Paloma owed her.

She leaned back against the bench and let her shoulders drop, casual as Sunday. "Well, since we're trading confidences over terrible coffee: you should know that my grandmother's name was Liora, and she kept a boarding house in Casserine before the fever took her. Mother spoke of her often. There's no gap in our bloodline worth anyone's flag. You can write that in your little book and sleep soundly."

Every word of it invented. Liora was the name of Bess's sister. The boarding house was lifted from a story Bess told about her girlhood. Paloma delivered it with the smooth confidence of a woman whose history had never once needed hiding, and she watched Elias receive it, and she watched him *not believe a single syllable*—

—and she watched him nod slowly and look back out at the harbor and say, "Liora. Casserine. I'll note it."

Nothing in his voice accused her. Nothing in his face promised mercy either. He simply took the lie and held it, gently, like something breakable she'd handed him, and gave no sign whether he would keep it or shatter it.

Her chest ached in a way she couldn't account for. It was the loneliest feeling she'd had in years, and the least lonely at the same time, and she couldn't have explained the arithmetic of it to save her life.

"Why?" she asked. It came out rougher than she intended.

"Why what?"

"You know I'm lying. Don't insult me by pretending otherwise—I've watched you catch Maretta in four contradictions in ten minutes. Why let it stand?"

Elias turned his cup in his hands, once around, twice. When he spoke, he spoke to the coffee rather than to her.

"When I was nineteen, the census office sent me to a village upcoast called Harrow's End. Routine update. There was a family there—the Ostlers—who'd taken in a niece after her parents died at sea. No papers for the girl. No arrival record. By the regulations she was an irregular entry, and irregular entries meant a fine the family couldn't pay, and unpaid fines meant the constables, and the constables at Harrow's End were not gentle men." He paused. "I reported it. Because it was my duty, and because I was nineteen and believed the regulations were a kind of fairness—rules applied equally to everyone, which is the closest thing to justice a ledger can offer."

"What happened?"

"They fined them. The family sold their boat to pay it. The father drowned the following winter, fishing from a borrowed skiff, because he no longer had his own." Elias's thumb pressed hard against the side of the cup, whitening at the knuckle. "The girl came to me afterward. She didn't shout. That was the worst of it. She just asked me whether I'd written down her parents' deaths properly, because she wanted them to exist somewhere official, and she thought—if it was me, it would at least be accurate." His mouth twisted. "I have thought about that sentence every working day for eleven years."

Paloma found she had stopped breathing somewhere in the middle of it. She made herself breathe.

"And so now you sit on things," she said.

"Now I check things," he corrected softly. "Twice. Three times. Before anything leaves my desk, I ask myself what boat it costs them." He finally looked at her, and his eyes held none of the clerk's flatness—they were tired and honest and a little afraid. "I don't know what I'm holding yet, Miss Vane. I won't pretend I do. But I know what it costs to be wrong in one direction, and I've decided I'd rather find out what it costs in the other."

The bell buoy rang across the water. Ilse refilled their cups unasked and uncharged, which she did for no one, and Paloma noted it the way you note a change in the tide.

"Elias," she said.

He blinked. "Yes?"

"Your name. You've heard mine plenty of times; it hardly seems fair." She picked up her fresh cup. "Elias Corvane, who checks things three times. What happens when you check something and find out it's worse than you feared?"

He considered the question seriously, the way he seemed to consider everything, as though flippancy were a foreign currency he'd never learned to spend.

"Then," he said, "I suppose I find out what sort of man I am. I've been putting that off for years. It's remarkably easy. The forms do most of the thinking."

Paloma laughed again—her second theft of the morning—and this time she didn't try to hide it, and he smiled back, crooked and startled, and for a moment there on the bench above the ferry steps, two people who had built their whole lives on being unreadable sat reading each other like open ledgers, and neither of them ran.

* * *

They parted at the mouth of the fish shed, formally, with nods, as befitted a census officer and an indentured diver, and Paloma carried her canvas sack back along the harbor road feeling dangerously, recklessly visible.

She was already composing the argument she'd have with herself that night—*it was reconnaissance, it was intelligence gathering, you learned that certification comes from the district office, that's all it was*—when she rounded the warehouse corner by the workshop gate and stopped dead.

A company carriage stood before the gates, lacquered black with the Meridian compass rose gleaming on its door, and a footman in house colors was helping down a gentleman in a dove-gray coat—young, broad-shouldered, with the kind of handsome, unhurried confidence that money breeds into the bone. The workshop overseer was already bowing. Even the gate guards had straightened.

Bess stood in the courtyard doorway, and her eyes found Paloma's over the gentleman's shoulder with an expression Paloma had never seen on the old woman's face before.

Fear.

"Who is that?" Paloma asked the guard at the postern, keeping her voice idle, a woman asking about weather.

The guard glanced at the carriage and lowered his voice. "Aldous Meridian himself. Come to see the beds." He shook his head, half impressed, half uneasy. "Founder's grandson. Never known one of them to set foot in the workshop before."

The gentleman in gray turned, scanning the yard—and for one instant his gaze passed over Paloma the way a hand passes over goods at auction, weighing, pricing, assessing.

Then he smiled at her, pleasantly, and walked in her direction.
