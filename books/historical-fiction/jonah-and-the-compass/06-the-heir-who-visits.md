## 6. The Heir Who Visits

The announcement came down from the company house on a Tuesday, which Paloma would later think was fitting. Tuesdays were when Saltmere's fishmongers hid their best catch under the counter for favored customers; when the harbor master's clerks took long lunches; when nothing that happened in the workshop was ever quite what it appeared to be. Of course Aldous Meridian would come on a Tuesday.

"He wants to see the operation," Overseer Grail announced to the sorting room at large, in the tone of a man reading someone else's bad news. "He arrives at ten. Everything will be clean. Everyone will be working. No one will speak unless spoken to."

"And if we're spoken to?" asked Teo, who had never once in his life been able to leave a question hanging.

"Answer briefly. Answer respectfully." Grail paused at the door. "And answer as though your mother never taught you anything interesting."

Paloma kept her eyes on the oysters in front of her and did not let her hands stop moving. Around her, forty divers bent over their trays and pretended this was an ordinary instruction, but she could feel the room change temperature. Nobody important visited the workshop. Important people sent men with ledgers, men with keys, men whose faces you learned to read like weather. Important people themselves came only when something was about to be taken away.

She spent the hour before ten doing arithmetic with her heartbeat. *He doesn't know. He can't know. The census taker sat across a table from me three weeks ago and I lied so badly he must have tasted it, and still—* But Elias Corvane hadn't reported her. She was almost sure of it. Almost sure was a currency she'd been living on for five years, and it bought less every season.

At five minutes to ten, the gates opened for a carriage too fine for the yard, and Aldous Meridian stepped down into the world his family owned.

* * *

Paloma had imagined him wrong, which unsettled her more than anything else about the day. She had pictured Augustin's grandson as a version of the portrait in the locket—the heavy jaw, the sea-battered confidence of a man who'd built an empire out of drowned things. Instead the man crossing her courtyard was narrow and neat, dressed in gray that cost more than the diving boat, with soft hands he kept folded behind his back like a scholar touring a museum of his own inheritance.

He was accompanied by two secretaries, a lawyer, and Grail, who walked half a step behind him wearing the expression of a dog that has just realized its master might also bite.

"The drying racks are original," Grail was saying. "Built under the founder himself."

"Original," Aldous repeated, touching the nearest rack with one finger and then examining the finger, as if salt might have done something to him. "Charming. One does wonder what we'd replace them with. Something modern. Something that doesn't smell quite so—" He gestured at the air, which smelled of brine and shell and sweat, which smelled, Paloma thought, like money being made by other people.

The tour wound through the sorting room, where Paloma sat among thirty-nine colleagues doing her very best impression of furniture. It failed. She felt his attention find her the way you feel a gull shadowing your boat—not yet diving, but interested.

"You there," Aldous said pleasantly. "You've quick hands. How long have you been with us?"

She rose, wiped her palms on her apron, and gave him the answer she'd given every inspector for five years. "Five years next spring, sir."

"Five years." He smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, like everything else about him. "And before that? Where did you come from?"

"The beds, sir. My mother dove before me."

"Sera Vane." He said the name lightly, conversationally, and Paloma's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth. "Yes, I've been reading the old rolls. Fascinating documents, rolls. You learn such curious things." He tilted his head. "Your mother must have been remarkable. They say she could hold her breath longer than any diver on the coast."

"She was remarkable, sir."

"And she raised you alone, didn't she? No father in the picture?"

"No father, sir."

"How modern of her." He moved along the row of trays, trailing his finger, not looking at the oysters at all. "And no siblings? No cousins? A woman like that, a woman of such—let's call it independent spirit—one imagines she'd have family somewhere. Family tends to turn up, in my experience, usually around the time it's inconvenient."

"I wouldn't know, sir. She didn't talk much."

"No?" Aldous turned back to her, and for a moment the pleasantness thinned and Paloma saw through it to something patient and cold, a hook waiting in clear water. "What *did* she talk about?"

*The sea. The tide. A story about a man swallowed by a whale who found his way out by compass and stubbornness. Your grandfather's laugh, once, when she was small enough to sit on his knee—though she only told me that when she was dying, and I have spent five years pretending I didn't hear it.*

"Diving, sir," Paloma said. "Mostly diving."

"Mostly diving," he echoed, and smiled again, and let her go.

But at the door of the sorting room he paused, as if remembering something trivial. "My condolences, by the way. Late, I know. Four years late, in fact." He said it kindly, terribly kindly. "I've recently developed an interest in my grandfather's personal correspondence. Family history is so easily lost, don't you find? Papers burn, memories fail, people move away and die quietly in distant towns." He adjusted one gray cuff. "One likes to keep track."

Then he was gone, into the courtyard, saying something light to Grail about the state of the roof, and Paloma sat down again and discovered her hands were shaking too hard to hold a knife.

Bess appeared at her elbow within the minute, the way Bess always did, carrying a tray she didn't need.

"Breathe," the matron murmured.

"He knows the name."

"He asked for the name. That's different." Bess set the tray down with unnecessary force. "Though I'll grant you it's a short distance between the two."

"Why now? Why after four years?"

Bess looked out the window at the gray coat moving through her workshop, and her mouth went thin. "Because leases expire, girl. Because wills get reread. Because men like that sleep fine until someone mentions blood in their hearing." She picked up the tray again. "Tonight, after the lamps are out, you'll come to my room. There are things I told you I'd tell you when it was safe. I've decided safe has left the harbor."

* * *

It did not rain that night, which everyone agreed afterward made the intrusion easier. A storm would have covered footsteps; instead there was only the ordinary dark of the barracks, the ordinary snoring, the ordinary creak of a building that had stood through sixty years of hurricanes and company audits alike.

Paloma lay awake anyway. She had spent the evening in Bess's small room above the kitchen learning things that rearranged her spine: that Bess had known her mother since before Paloma was born; that Sera had kept papers Bess considered dangerous enough to hide twice—once inside a false bottom of the records cabinet, and later, after the night someone searched the matron's quarters in 1849, sewn into the lining of a coat Sera wore diving; that her mother had died knowing exactly what her daughter would inherit someday, and had chosen, deliberately, not to tell her while there was still time to be talked out of it.

"She said you'd either drown chasing it or swim faster than you ever had," Bess had said. "She couldn't decide which, so she decided to let the tide choose. Well." The old woman had folded her arms. "Tide's come in."

So Paloma lay in her bunk composing arguments for magistrates who didn't exist yet, and it was somewhere between the fourth imaginary speech and actual sleep that she heard the gate chain.

Not the gate opening. The chain itself—a soft metallic whisper, the sound of a padlock being worked by someone who knew locks but didn't own this one. Then footsteps on gravel, two sets, unhurried. Men who expected to be uninterrupted.

She slid from her bunk without a sound, an old habit from a childhood of listening for her mother coming up the beach path, and crossed to the window.

Lamplight moved behind the shutters of the records room across the courtyard. Not lantern-light, which wandered and guttered; lamp-light, steady, the kind you got from the office lamps with brass bases. Someone had brought furniture-grade illumination to burgle a shed full of paper.

For a long moment Paloma simply watched, cataloguing the way her mother had taught her to catalogue water before a dive—current first, then depth, then what lives in between. Two men, then. One at the door keeping watch, one inside. The watcher stood with his weight on his heels, arms folded, and even at this distance there was something familiar in the silhouette. Not Grail. Not any of the workshop men. Someone from the town, from the company house, from the Tuesday carriage perhaps.

They were inside for most of an hour. When they finally emerged, the taller one carried nothing—that was the detail that made her stomach drop. Nothing visible. Either they'd found what they wanted and it fit in a pocket, or they'd found nothing and wanted the room to know it had been measured.

Or—and this thought arrived cold and stayed—they hadn't come for papers at all. They'd come to see what was *not* there. To learn the shape of the empty space, the way a thief cases a house by studying the walls.

The gate whispered shut behind them. Somewhere in the barracks, Teo rolled over and muttered something about herring.

Paloma stood at the window until the lamplight was gone and the stars came back, and then she did the thing five years of indenture had trained her to do, the thing that had kept her invisible through inspections and storms and the entire slow machinery of the Meridian Trading Company: she went back to bed, arranged the blanket, and slept like a woman without a care in the world.

It was Bess who found her at dawn, already dressed, already standing in the doorway of the records room with her arms crossed.

"Well?" the matron asked.

"They went through the census files. Every drawer, careful as surgeons. And the lease documents." Paloma nodded at the cabinet, whose false bottom she had checked with one finger before Bess even arrived. Still sealed. Still holding whatever Sera's coat-lining papers had left behind—or rather, holding the memory of them, because those pages now lived wrapped in oilcloth under the third floorboard of Bess's room, and had since yesterday evening, because Paloma had decided that safe leaving the harbor meant it was time to stop keeping treasure in obvious places. "Nothing else touched. Not the pay ledgers, not the dive logs. Just the papers that say who people are."

Bess sucked her teeth. "That's not a search. That's an audit of souls."

"Yes." Paloma looked out at the yard, where the morning shift was shuffling toward the boats, where the water beyond the seawall lay flat and glittering and indifferent, holding its pearls the way it always had, for whoever was ruthless or desperate or lucky enough to go down after them. "And sometime soon, Bess, I'm going to need to know exactly what my mother sewed into that coat. Because last night a man with soft hands sent two others to count the empty drawers in my life."

She turned back to the records room, to the neat rows of folders that named every diver, every birth, every death the company permitted itself to record.

"And I don't intend to be standing here empty-handed when they come back to fill them."
