## 7. Indenture Arithmetic

The arithmetic of an indenture was best done at night, when the counting house lamp had gone out and the overseer's boots had stopped pacing the corridor above.

Paloma had learned this in her first year, when the numbers were all she had. Five years minus one day. She'd scratched the figures into the underside of her bunk with a bent nail until Bess caught her at it and made her stop — not because it wasn't allowed, but because "a girl who counts her days out loud invites someone to shorten them." So now Paloma did her sums in her head, the way divers held their breath: privately, and with a fixed sense of how long they could keep it up.

Eleven months. Eleven months and nine days, if one counted from tonight, which she did, lying flat on her bunk with her hands folded over her sternum like a woman at a funeral — preferably her own, so she could supervise.

Eleven months, and then her contract with the Meridian Trading Company would expire like a lamp running dry, and she would walk out of the workshop gates with her wages and her freedom papers and nothing else, unless —

Unless.

She turned the word over. It had a pearl's weight to it.

*If she could prove descent before the lease expired.* She didn't even know yet whether there was anything to prove. A locket with a painted miniature of old Augustin Meridian's face. Half a letter, burned as though someone had tried to feed it to a stove and thought better of it mid-way. *My daughter's child will have her due,* the surviving fragment read, in a hand that trembled but did not waver in its intent. That was all. Against that: eleven months, a company that owned the magistrate, and a family that had apparently spent thirty years making sure no Vane ever asked a question twice.

Paloma did what she always did with unbearable thoughts. She filed them under water, where they couldn't breathe, and went to sleep.

*

Morning brought the beds, and the beds brought their own kind of arithmetic.

Forty oysters to the basket, six baskets before the noon bell, and the whole time the water pressing on your chest like a hand reminding you whose permission you breathed by. Paloma moved through the grey-green morning light with her fingers reading shells the way a clerk read ledgers — smooth, smooth, cracked, smooth — and let her mind run sums beneath the surface thoughts.

Eleven months. If a claim required proof, proof required documents. Documents, in Saltmere, lived in two places: the company archive, which might as well have been the moon, and wherever her mother had put things thirty years ago, which was nowhere Paloma had looked yet.

Sera Vane had been careful the way drowned things are careful about water. She had kept her past folded up small — the locket against her breastbone, the letter somewhere it took fire to find. What else might she have hidden? Paloma dredged her childhood for details the way she dredged the beds. The loose plank under the sea chest, which had contained only fishing line and dried kelp when she checked it last night. Her mother's habit of humming when she lied. The way she'd taught Paloma to write her letters using charcoal on slate and then scrubbed the slate clean each evening, every evening, without fail, as though literacy were something that evaporated if you let it sit overnight.

*She was hiding me in plain sight,* Paloma thought, surfacing for air, the harbor master's bell clanking somewhere above. *And teaching me to do the same.*

It was Teo who broke the morning's rhythm, hauling himself onto the barge beside her, dripping, his grin as wide as ever despite the yellowed bruise still fading along his ribs from the enforcers' attention two weeks back.

"You're doing sums," he said. "I can tell. You get this line right here—" he tapped his own forehead "—like you're arguing with somebody nobody else can see."

"I'm counting oysters."

"You're counting oysters *and* arguing. Different faces." He spat overside, companionably. "Bess wants you in the mending shed after bell. Said to say it's about net weights." He lowered his voice. "There's no such thing as net weights, Romy."

There was no such thing as Romy either, not really — it was the name she used among the divers, half hers and half invented, a coat that fit loosely enough to move in. But Bess sending for her through Teo meant Bess wanted no entry in any book about it. Paloma felt the pearl-weight word turn over again in her chest.

"All right," she said. "Net weights."

*

The mending shed smelled of tar and hemp and forty years of Bess's pipe smoke soaked into the beams. The matron sat on her stool amid a halo of hung nets, mending nothing, which was itself a message; Bess mended constantly the way other people fidgeted. An unmended net in Bess's hands meant her hands were needed for talking.

"Sit," she said, nodding at a coil of rope.

Paloma sat. Bess looked at her for a long moment with those pale, water-colored eyes that had watched three generations of divers come up gasping and go down quiet.

"Corvane came back yesterday," Bess said. "Census man. Asking after the annex records. Old Tull said he spent near an hour with the intake rolls from thirty years back."

Paloma's stomach performed a small, controlled submersion. "He's thorough. It's his job."

"Mm." Bess drew on her unlit pipe, purely for the shape of the gesture. "Your mother worked for me, girl. Did you know that? Before you were born. Best diver I ever signed on, and I've signed on plenty. Could hold her breath long enough to make a priest nervous." A pause. "And she came to me one autumn with an armful of papers and eyes like yours have now, and she asked me to help her make some things disappear."

The tar-smell seemed to sharpen. Paloma discovered she was holding her breath, which was absurd on dry land, and let it out slowly.

"What things?"

"Letters. A christening record — not yours, hers, written up proper on company paper with a seal, which no foundling diver ought to possess. And a copy of a page out of a book I never saw the inside of, but she told me what it was, and I believed her, because Sera Vane never lied about anything worth lying about." Bess tapped the pipe stem against her palm. "I hid them, girl. In plain sight. Where does a company never look?"

Paloma ran the inventory of the workshop through her mind — the counting house, the barracks, the drying racks, the — 

"The chapel pews," she said slowly. "Nobody audits God."

"Nobody audits God," Bess agreed, with deep satisfaction. "Under the seat boards, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with pitch. I put them there myself, and I've resealed them twice since when the damp got in. They'll be there now, or the Lord Himself has moved them, in which case we've bigger problems than Aldous Meridian."

"Why?" The question came out of Paloma rougher than she intended. "Why hide them instead of burning them? Why keep that risk sitting in your chapel for thirty years?"

Bess looked at her, and for once the old woman's face held nothing guarded at all.

"Because your mother stood in this shed and said, 'One day my daughter will need these, and she'll need a friend who remembers where they are.' And I said, 'You haven't got a daughter.' And she said—" Bess's voice did something complicated around the words "—she said, 'I haven't got one *yet*. But I've decided on her name already, and I don't decide things twice.'"

The shed was very quiet. Somewhere outside, gulls were conducting their eternal dispute over nothing.

"She named me before I existed," Paloma managed.

"Women like your mother plan ahead," Bess said. "It's how we survive men like theirs." She set the pipe down and leaned forward, and now her voice dropped into the register she used for matters of actual nets and actual knives. "Now listen, because here's the arithmetic you can't do alone. The founder's will — the true one — had a clause. Old Augustin was sentimental under all that ledger-skin, and he wrote his by-blows into it. Any acknowledged descendant of his blood, proven before the company lease renewal, holds claim to a share of the estate and the workshop charter both. His son knew it. That's why your mother's acknowledgment papers vanished, why her name came off the rolls, why she spent her life diving other people's oysters while her father's fortune paid her hush-money marked 'family obligation.'" Bess's mouth twisted. "The lease comes up for renewal in fourteen months. If no claim is proven by then, everything passes to the son's line clean as a gutted fish. Your eleven months runs out *before* the law's door closes — do you follow me? You've got a margin of three months, no more, and the paperwork alone could eat two of them."

Paloma followed. The numbers assembled themselves in her head with terrible clarity, the way a wreck resolves out of murky water just before you realize it's also a trap. Eleven months of indenture, during which she was legally the company's property and could be confined, transferred, or sold to a mainland concern if anyone decided she was inconvenient. Then a window measured in weeks, in which an orphaned diver with a locket and half a letter must prove herself the granddaughter of the richest dead man in Saltmere, against a family that had already demonstrated its preferred methods for handling problems.

"It's hopeless," she said. Not despairingly — she was checking the depth before diving. "It's arithmetically hopeless."

"Bah." Bess waved the word away like smoke. "Hopeless is a number, and I've never met a number that a determined woman couldn't recount. You've got the locket. You've got the chapel papers. What you haven't got is the original will, or proof of what's been forged, or a soul in authority who isn't bought." She counted these deficiencies off on her tar-stained fingers. "The first two are findable. The third—" her pale eyes settled on Paloma, uncomfortably perceptive "—the third is where you'd need somebody inside. Somebody with access to the archives, and a conscience that still ticks."

Paloma thought of Elias Corvane, with his ink-stained fingers and his terrible honest face, the way he'd watched her lie about her mother's maiden name and visibly declined to write down the lie. The memory arrived with a sensation she refused to name — something between dread and appetite.

"I might know somebody," she said.

"Then pray he's braver than he looks," said Bess, taking up her needle at last, "because from what I hear of census men, that wouldn't take much."

*

That night the barracks lamp went out on schedule, and the boots overhead paced their rounds and faded, and Paloma lay in the dark doing sums.

Eleven months of silence, if she chose silence. Walk out free and poor and safe, and let the Meridians keep their stolen century. It remained on the table. It was, in fact, the only sensible item on the table.

But underneath the sensible sum, another total kept insisting on itself: a mother who had named her daughter before the daughter existed, and planned for her the way one plans a course across open water — not knowing the weather, only the bearing. A matron who had kept faith in a chapel pew for thirty years. A half-letter that ended mid-sentence, *my daughter's child will have her due*, with the *due* trailing off into char, waiting all this time for someone to finish it.

Her mother hadn't chosen safety. Her mother had chosen *her*, and built the choice to last.

"Eleven months," Paloma whispered to the dark underside of the bunk, where no nail marks remained. "Fourteen before the door shuts. Very well."

She would need the chapel papers. She would need the original will, wherever the tide or treachery had put it. And she would need a census taker whose conscience ticked — which meant, tomorrow being market day, that she would need to find Elias Corvane among the silvers-and-salt stalls and discover precisely what kind of honesty he was made of, and whether it bent.

She was nearly asleep when she heard it: soft footsteps in the corridor that were not the overseer's, pausing at the barracks door, lingering there a moment too long, and moving on.

Someone was counting her too.

Paloma lay still in the darkness, heart steady as a diver's, and revised her arithmetic one final time: eleven months, minus whatever the Meridians intended to subtract, plus whatever she dared.

The sum, she decided, was still worth doing.
