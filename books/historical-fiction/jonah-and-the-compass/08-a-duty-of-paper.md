## 8. A Duty of Paper

The Bureau of Rolls and Registries occupied the second floor of the Customs House, a building that had been designed by an architect who clearly believed natural light was a form of extravagance. Elias Corvane had worked there for six years, and in that time he had developed a private taxonomy of paperwork. There was paper that recorded things—births, deaths, cargoes, quarrels over fence lines. There was paper that intended to record things but mostly recorded the intentions of the people writing it. And then there was a third kind, rarer and more dangerous, that looked exactly like the first kind while doing something else entirely.

He learned which category Form 44-B belonged to on a Tuesday morning, when his supervisor, Deputy Registrar Hobb, set it down on his desk with two fingers, the way one might present a dead mouse.

"Certification of completeness," Hobb said. "Meridian workshop, Saltmere harbor. Census entries for the current quarter."

Elias picked up the form. It was ordinary enough: a single sheet with a wax seal already affixed at the bottom, waiting only for his signature and the date. The seal was the problem. Certification seals were supposed to be applied *after* certification. Someone upstairs—or several someones, in a series of carpeted rooms Elias had never been invited into—had decided in advance that the Meridian workshop's census entries were complete.

"I haven't finished the verification," Elias said carefully. "There are irregularities. Bloodline questions on three entries, at least one unresolved name discrepancy—"

"The company has resolved them," Hobb said. He was a small man built like a filing cabinet, all drawers and no windows, and he had the particular talent of bureaucrats everywhere: the ability to make an outrageous statement sound like a scheduling inconvenience. "The Meridian Trading Company is a charter partner of this bureau. They've reviewed their own rolls and found them accurate. Our function is ministerial."

"Our function," Elias said, "is to be accurate about what's ministerial."

Hobb sighed, a sound like a ledger closing. "Corvane. You're a good clerk. You were a good clerk when you arrived and you've remained one, which frankly I find admirable. But look at the form again. Look at the annotation."

Elias looked. Beneath the printed heading, in a hand he did not recognize—someone important, then; unimportant people wrote legibly—was a single line: *All Vane-lineage claims to be treated as lapsed per succession review.*

Succession review. There had been no succession review. Elias had spent three evenings in the archive cross-referencing the census against company payment records, and if there had been any formal proceeding touching the estate of Augustin Meridian, he would have found its paper trail. Paper trails were his whole life. He knew the way documents bred in dark rooms, the way they left tracks like mice in flour.

"This dissolves legal claims," he said. "Any claim. Permanently. If someone's descent is genuine, certifying this makes their own existence a clerical fiction."

"Yes," said Hobb, with the mild satisfaction of a man confirming that two plus two remained four. "That's rather the point. Sign it, date it, initial the margin. And Corvane—" he paused at the door, not turning around, "—the girl. The diver. Paloma Vane. Your flag report on her bloodline was noted upstairs. Favorably. I'd stop digging in the archives after hours; the night porter mentions you in his logs, and his logs go upstairs too. Nothing grows in the dark except trouble, and trouble has better lawyers than we do."

He left. Elias sat with the form for a long moment, listening to the rain begin against the Customs House's mean little windows.

Then he did what he always did when the world behaved unreasonably. He made tea, took out a fresh sheet of paper, and began taking notes.

* * *

The machinery, he had to admit, was beautiful. That was the horror of it, and he felt the horror the way you feel a tooth turning bad—slowly, then all at once, and always from the inside.

Form 44-B did not say *erase the Vanes.* No form ever said anything so honest. It said *certify completeness,* and completeness was defined—in a circular regulation, 12-C, amended two years ago by a committee whose minutes had never been published—as *conformity between census entries and company-verified records.* The company verified its own records. The company's records now contained a succession review that had never happened, annotated by a hand that belonged to somebody's solicitor. His signature would sit at the bottom like a keystone, and pull out that one stone and the whole arch came down: every entry he had gathered, every question he had asked, every careful line of ink about divers and mothers and maiden names—all of it would become legally meaningless, retroactively, tidily, without a single person ever being accused of anything.

No one had lied anywhere in the chain. That was the genius. Hobb believed what he'd been told. The solicitor had cited a regulation. The regulation had been amended properly. Each link was innocent; only the chain was a noose.

Elias drank his tea and thought about Paloma Vane.

He thought about the way she had lied to him on the harbor steps—badly, gloriously, with the transparent desperation of a woman who had never practiced deceit because she'd never needed it. *My mother? Ordinary woman. Ordinary family. Very forgettable.* She had watched him with those level grey eyes, waiting to see if the lie would hold, and he had understood in that moment that her whole life was built out of such lies—not wicked ones, just small gray stones of nothing-in-particular, mortared together into a wall she could live behind.

And he had put his fist through it. With a question. One question, asked in the flat tone the bureau trained into its clerks, and now here he sat, holding the instrument meant to finish what that question had started. First we notice you, the paperwork said. Then we unmake you.

He thought about the locket. He shouldn't know about the locket—he had learned of it through channels he wasn't proud of, a word dropped by the archive porter, a rumor with a price tag—but he knew, and knowing it changed the arithmetic. A miniature portrait of Augustin Meridian, kept by a pearl diver, hidden in a sea chest for years. People did not hide portraits of strangers.

His pen hovered over Form 44-B.

Here is what duty required of him: signature, date, initials. Here is what conscience required: essentially, treason, prosecuted not with swords but with forms, punished not with exile but with dismissal, poverty, and the quiet lifelong shame of a man who had failed to be a good clerk.

He thought, briefly, longingly, of signing. Of going home to his narrow room above the chandlery, eating his supper of bread and sardines, and sleeping the sleep of a man whose world still had walls. He could almost taste it. Six years of believing that the bureau was dull and therefore decent—that corruption, if it existed, lived in grand houses, not in forms 44-B—and the belief had been a kind of warmth. Signing the form would take the warmth back. It would cost him nothing today. That was the trick of it; that was how institutions ate men. Not in one meal but in small servings, each one easy to swallow, each one followed by another.

Elias put down the pen.

He took Form 44-B, folded it once, twice, a third time, until it was a hard little square, and slid it into the inner pocket of his coat—the pocket where he kept things he hadn't decided about yet. It was, he reflected, a coward's drawer. But it was also, for the moment, a safe one. Unsigned, the certification was incomplete, and incomplete paperwork, in the great slow digestive system of the bureau, bought time. Days, perhaps. A week if he filed a query about the seal placement, which he would do first thing, in triplicate, with maximum tediousness. Nobody fought tedium harder than bureaucracy did.

It wasn't courage. He knew that. It was a delay dressed in courage's clothing. But he had watched Paloma count oysters by feel in the cold morning water, hour after hour, breath by breath, endurance mistaken for obedience by everyone watching. Perhaps delay was its own kind of holding your breath.

He filed the query. He went home. He slept badly.

* * *

Three days later, the bureau's answer to his query arrived, and it was a masterpiece: *Re: seal placement, see Regulation 12-C, subsection iv, which permits pre-affixation of certification seals where partner-entity review has concluded. No action required.* They had even underlined *no action required*, which Elias suspected was somebody's idea of mercy.

He was still staring at it when the second document landed on his desk—a routing slip, sending him back to Saltmere harbor. *Final field verification, Meridian workshop, in advance of quarterly certification.* They weren't merely waiting for his signature. They were sending him to stand among the divers, in the workshop yard, beside the drying racks, and certify the erasure in person. Somebody upstairs had a sense of theater. Or a sense of cruelty. With committees, Elias had learned, the two were indistinguishable.

So on Thursday he walked out along the harbor road in a thin drizzle, past the fish stalls and the rope walks, to the gates of the Meridian workshop, and presented his papers to the gatekeeper, and was waved through into the wet stone yard where oysters lay drying in long ranks like rows of sleeping animals.

They were all there. Bess the matron, arms folded, watching him from the doorway with the expression of a woman who had buried three husbands and expected to bury him too, eventually, without hurry. The young diver Teo, who flinched when their eyes met and looked away too quickly. And at the edge of the yard, coiling a dive line with quick economical hands, Paloma Vane herself.

She saw the bureau coat and her face closed like a shutter. But she didn't leave. She finished her coil, hung the line neatly on its peg—an act of deliberate, almost insulting calm—and came toward him across the wet cobbles.

"Census man," she said. "Come to ask more questions?"

"Come to certify," he said. "The quarter's entries."

Something moved behind her eyes—wariness sharpening into something sharper still. She knew. Of course she knew; the workshop was a village and villages heard everything, and Aldous Meridian's people would have made sure the news traveled: the rolls would be certified complete, and whatever the Vanes thought they were would become nothing at all, legally speaking, before the season turned.

"You should know," she said quietly, "that my mother's maiden name was Meridian. Since you're certifying completeness. It seems relevant."

It was the first time she had said it aloud to him, plainly, without hedging or lies, and the plainness of it struck him like weather. She was handing him a weapon, in front of witnesses, in the open yard of the place that wanted her gone. Either she trusted him—which was foolish—or she was testing him, which was worse, because he wasn't sure yet which answer he'd give.

"It is relevant," he said. "And it changes nothing I've written, because what I've written is true." He lowered his voice. "The certification hasn't been signed. I've raised objections. Procedural ones. They won't hold long—a week, maybe two—but while they hold, nothing is erased. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

She studied him the way she must have studied water before a dive: reading currents, checking for undertow. Rain dripped off the eaves between them. Behind her, Bess had drifted closer without appearing to move at all, the way large women can.

"I understand," Paloma said at last, "that you're either the most dangerous man in Saltmere or the only useful one, and I don't yet know which." A pause; then, with the ghost of the market-stall sparring in her voice: "You get one week, census man. Try to be worth it."

She turned back to her lines. Bess gave Elias a single nod, which he suspected was the highest honor the woman bestowed on anyone short of a third husband.

He walked back along the harbor road with the rain easing and Form 44-B sitting in his pocket like a coal that hadn't decided whether to burn him. A week. Two, if he was clever with regulations, and he was very clever with regulations. After that, the bureau would send someone else to sign, and the someone else would sign without losing a night's sleep, and everything Elias had delayed would come due anyway—unless something arrived first. Some proof. Some paper older and truer than theirs.

Behind him, in the yard, the divers were returning from the beds, and their voices carried over the water, ordinary voices arguing about wages and weather. He found himself counting them as he walked, the way he counted everything now. Eleven divers. Twelve.

Thirteen, if you counted the one the rolls were about to erase.

He needed to tell her about the deadline properly. Tonight, he decided, at the harbor market, over coffee bad enough to be honest in front of. And he needed to tell her something else besides—that the company house held the original will, that succession reviews required archives, and that archives, unlike men, could be searched.

The rain stopped. In the east, over the open sea, the light broke thin and gold, and Elias Corvane, enemy of nothing and believer in forms, began—for the first time in his orderly life—to plan a burglary.
