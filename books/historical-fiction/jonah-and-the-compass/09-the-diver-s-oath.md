## 9. The Diver's Oath

There was a particular way Paloma had of asking for things without asking, honed over five years of indenture like a good knife edge. She'd used it to get extra lamp oil from Bess, to trade a night watch for a hot meal, to learn which guards at the counting house could be smiled past and which had hearts like barnacles. It went something like: *I don't suppose*, followed by a shrug that cost nothing and promised less.

She tried it on Teo first, because Teo was the easiest.

They were mending nets behind the drying racks, the two of them cross-legged in a patch of sun like a pair of lazy cats, though neither of them had been lazy a day in their lives. Teo's fingers worked the twine with the absent speed of a man who could tie a bowline in his sleep. His hands were ruined in the way all divers' hands were ruined—split at the knuckles, salt-cured, nails permanently rimmed with gray—and he gestured with them extravagantly while he talked, as if to say, *look what the sea has done, and look how I laugh about it.*

"I don't suppose," Paloma said, "you remember much about old Augustin Meridian's will."

Teo's twine stopped mid-loop. Then it kept going, because Teo was too proud to let anything stop him twice.

"The will," he said. "The founder's will. That you're asking me about. Casually. While holding a net."

"Mm." She pulled a knot tight. "Idle curiosity."

"Paloma." He leaned in, dropping his voice to the register he used for gossip, which meant everyone within thirty feet would now be listening with their whole souls. "You've never been idle in your life. You once organized the salt barrels by grain size out of boredom during a storm."

"They were chaotic, Teo."

"You alphabetized the fish names on the market board. In a language you invented."

"It needed doing."

He set down the net and looked at her properly then, and she saw the thing she'd counted on seeing: not fear, not yet, but the quick bright hunger of a man who had spent nine years hauling oysters for other people's pockets and wanted, more than air sometimes, to be part of something larger than his own exhaustion.

"Tell me," he said.

So she did—not everything, not the locket or the letter, not yet—but enough. Enough that when she finished, Teo sat very still, which for Teo was roughly equivalent to another man weeping.

"Your mother," he said finally. "Sera Vane. The one who—"

"Who dove the deep beds alone when no one else would. Yes."

"I heard stories about her when I first came. They said she could hold her breath through two verses of a shanty." He grinned suddenly. "I assumed they were lying. Divers always lie about each other."

"She could do three," Paloma said. "But only if the shanty was boring."

* * *

She told Bess next, which was less a decision than a surrender, because Bess found things out the way gulls find fish—by circling until something broke the surface and panicking.

"Sit down," Bess said, before Paloma had said a word. This was in the matron's narrow room off the kitchen, where the walls were papered with old shipping notices and the kettle was always, mysteriously, exactly at boiling. "You've got your telling-me-something face on. Your mother had the same face. She wore it right up until the day she told me she was dying, and I'll thank you to spare me the suspense this time."

So Paloma sat, and told her. All of it—the locket, the miniature portrait, the half-burned letter naming Sera Vane the daughter of Augustin Meridian. Bess listened with her hands wrapped around her cup, going grayer around the mouth with every sentence.

When it was done, she said nothing for a long moment. The kettle ticked. Somewhere outside, the third-shift divers were coming in, their wet boots slapping the stones.

"I told you to throw it into the tide," Bess said at last.

"You did."

"And instead you've decided to declare war on the richest family in Saltmere with eleven months left on your contract and a proof-of-claim that wouldn't buy you a herring." Bess set down her cup. Her eyes were fierce and wet at the same time, an achievement Paloma had never managed. "Good. I've been waiting five years for you to stop being careful. But if you're going to do it, girl, you'll do it properly, and you'll not do it alone."

"There are things I need," Paloma began carefully. "From the company house. The original will, if it still exists—it would be in the founder's archive, sealed under the registrar's—"

"I know where the archive is." Bess sniffed. "I carried tea to the clerks who catalogued it, twenty-six years ago, back when my knees allowed for stairs and my opinions were still welcome in polite rooms. What you need isn't the will itself. Not yet. What you need is to know it exists, and where, and how it's kept. And for that—" she rose, groaning theatrically, and pried up a loose floorboard beside her bed— "you need someone who can walk into the company house without anyone looking twice."

She drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and unwrapped it on the table: a woman's diving knife, a coil of silver wire, and a folded page dense with a neat, cramped hand.

"Bess," Paloma said slowly, "what is this?"

"That is the inventory your mother made. Of the archive. Twenty years ago, when she still believed papers could protect people." Bess pushed it across the table. "She asked me to keep it in case it ever mattered. I thought she was being dramatic." A pause. "Turns out she was just early."

* * *

The third one was Marta, and Marta was the hard one.

Not because Marta couldn't be trusted—Marta was the most trustworthy person in the workshop, in the sense that a sea wall is trustworthy; you could lean your whole life against her and she would hold, without comment, without thanks. No, Marta was hard because she was careful in the way that Paloma herself had been careful, and she recognized in Paloma's new recklessness the same thing a weathered skipper recognizes in a green sail: trouble arriving at speed.

They stood on the pier at dusk, after the day's haul was tallied. The water lay flat and pewter-colored, holding the last light like a secret.

"You want us to break into the company house," Marta said.

"Not break in," Paloma said. "Confirm. Look. Learn the shape of the thing. Teo has a cousin among the night porters. There's a records wing that closes at sundown and opens again at dawn, and between those hours one man walks the corridor twice. We only need to see the archive door, take the measure of its lock, and leave no more trace than breath on glass."

"And after we've seen it?"

"Then we plan properly. With Bess's inventory. With time."

Marta looked out at the water for a long while. She was a broad, slow-moving woman who swam like an eel and spoke like a judge delivering a verdict she didn't enjoy.

"My brother drowned in these beds," she said at last. "Company bed, company rules, company boat with a patched hull they wouldn't replace. When he died, they docked his final wages for the lost gear." She turned to Paloma. "I'm not telling you this so you'll feel sorry for me. I'm telling you so you understand I have been angry for eleven years, and anger kept in a jar goes strange. If we do this thing—we do it all the way. I don't want half measures. Half measures are how my brother's hull stayed patched."

"We do it all the way," Paloma said.

"Then say it properly. On the water, like divers do."

So there on the pier, with the gulls complaining overhead and the tide sliding black beneath the pilings, three divers put their hands together over the rail—one ruined hand, one steady hand, one hand still young enough to believe—and Paloma spoke the diver's oath, the oldest form of it, the kind her mother would have known:

"What the sea holds, we hold for each other. What one sinks, all rise for. Breath shared is breath doubled; breath withheld is breath ended."

Marta repeated it. Teo repeated it, and ruined the solemnity entirely by adding, "Also, if either of you dies, I get the better bunk," and Marta cuffed him, and for one moment on that darkening pier Paloma felt something she hadn't felt since her mother died—that she was not alone in the water.

She should have known better. Nothing the sea gives is ever free.

* * *

It took Teo nine days to get inside.

Nine days of his cousin Vetch working the porter's rotation, nine days of Teo memorizing the watchman's rounds from a rooftop across the square with the patient stillness of a heron, nine days during which Paloma woke every morning at the bell and thought, *today it happens, today we are fools, today we are caught*—and every evening the workshop gates closed on another ordinary day, and her heart unclenched by degrees.

On the ninth night, Teo went in. He came out again an hour later, unhurried, whistling, and met them in the net loft, and told them everything in a low triumphant rush: the corridor, the iron-banded door, the lock—a Brammel pattern, old, pre-dating the current registrar—and the archive room beyond it, glimpsed for ten seconds through the door's grille: shelves, ledgers, and a strongbox on the top shelf with the founder's cipher stamped in wax on its face.

"He saw it," Teo breathed. "The seal wasn't broken, Paloma. Twenty years and nobody's opened it. It's sitting there like a—" he groped for the image— "like an egg nobody's noticed is about to hatch."

"It's sitting there," Marta said flatly, "behind a locked door, in a guarded building, and now we know that, and knowing is not having."

"Knowing is the beginning of having."

"Knowing," Marta said, "is also the beginning of hanging."

They argued long into the night, gently, the way old friends argue, and settled nothing except that they would wait, and plan, and tell no one—which lasted exactly four days, because four days later Teo went back.

He didn't tell them he was going. Later, when Paloma could think about it without her chest closing, she understood why: he'd wanted to bring her the prize itself. He'd seen her face when he described that unbroken seal, and he'd wanted, with the whole reckless generous engine of his heart, to be the one who handed her her mother's name back.

He made a wax impression of the Brammel key's bite using a candle stub and a strip of tin he'd palmed weeks earlier. He was good at it—he'd practiced on the workshop's storeroom locks until his fingers could read a keyhole like braille. He was halfway through, kneeling at the iron door with the tin pressed to the wax, when the watchman came around the corner early, because the second watchman had called in sick and the rounds had collapsed into one man walking fast.

Teo ran. Teo was fast. But the company house had corridors like the inside of a shell, and a boy who knew them for two nights cannot outrun a man who has walked them for twenty years.

They brought him back to the workshop yard two days later, and they made sure everyone saw.

Paloma was at the drying racks when the gate opened. She heard it before she understood it—the wrongness in the sound of the crowd, the hush with edges—and then they came through: four of Aldous Meridian's enforcers in company gray, and between them Teo, walking on feet that would not quite carry him, held up under the arms by two of the men as though helping a drunk friend home, which was somehow worse than dragging.

His right hand was wrong. That was the detail her mind fixed on, afterward, the way a drowning eye fixes on light: the hand, wrapped in a filthy rag, bent where hands do not bend, and Teo's face above it wearing a smile.

That was the unbearable part. He was smiling. He'd arranged his face into a smile before they'd even come through the gate, because he knew she'd be watching, and he would not—not for anything, not with his teeth kicked loose and his ribs strapped—give Aldous Meridian's men the satisfaction of her fear.

An enforcer with a ledger-book voice read the charge to the assembled workshop: *vagrancy with intent, trespass upon company premises.* Not the truth, of course. Never the truth. The truth—that Teo had been copying the key to the founder's archive—would raise questions the company preferred not to answer. So they'd charged him with nothing that mattered, and punished him for something that did, and the message in it was addressed to her personally, and everyone standing in that yard could read the address.

"Fine's assessed against the accused," the ledger voice concluded. "Payable in labor. The accused's contract is extended eighteen months."

Someone in the crowd made a small sound. Eighteen months. Nine years of Teo's life, become twenty-seven, for a candle stub and a strip of tin.

They dumped him at the infirmary door like a sack of wet rope and left.

Paloma reached him first. She knelt in the straw and took his good hand and could not speak, and Teo, through split lips, whispered:

"Don't look like that. I got the whole bite impression. It's in my boot."

"Teo—"

"Whole thing. Every notch." His grin cost him visibly, and he paid it anyway. "They can keep the eighteen months. You get the key made. Promise me you'll get the key made."

And because she owed him that, because refusing would have broken something in both of them beyond any enforcer's reach, Paloma bowed her head over his ruined hand and promised.

That night she sat alone in the loft with Bess's inventory spread before her, and by the light of a shuttered lamp she read her mother's cramped handwriting by pages: *Archive, east wing, third door. Strongbox, founder's cipher, top shelf, unbroken.* And at the bottom of the final page, in fresher ink, a line her mother must have added in her last year, a line Paloma had read four times already and still could not pass without touching it:

*If you are reading this, then the tide turned at last. Be brave, little bird, but be clever first.*

Below the loft, in the barracks, Marta sharpened knives with slow deliberate strokes. Across the yard, the infirmary lamp burned late over Teo's cot. And somewhere across the sleeping city, in the company house, a wax impression was already in Paloma's keeping, waiting for a locksmith who owed Bess a favor, waiting to become a key—

—and waiting, though none of them knew it yet, for the season's first storm, already gathering itself beyond the horizon like a debt come due.
