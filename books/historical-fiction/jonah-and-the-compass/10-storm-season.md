## 10. Storm Season

The storm announced itself three days early, the way important things did in Saltmere—first as a rumor among the gulls, then as a color in the water, then as Bess standing in the doorway of the drying shed with her arms crossed and her mouth set in the particular line that meant *argue with me and lose.*

"Beds are closed," she said. "Company's order. Nobody dives until the barometer climbs back above the devil's armpit."

"The beds don't close themselves," Paloma said, wringing out her dive cap. "Somebody has to—"

"Somebody has to stay alive to finish an indenture, is what somebody has to do." Bess's eyes flicked, briefly and meaningfully, toward the counting house where the company ledgers lived. Eleven months. They had both done that arithmetic often enough now that neither needed to say it aloud. "You've been diving like a woman trying to outrun something, Paloma Vane. The sea noticed. It always notices."

Paloma wanted to protest that she was diving exactly as much as any diver with a deadline would dive, which was to say: constantly, hungrily, with a small prayer at the bottom of every breath that the next oyster would be the one that tipped the scales. But Bess had already turned away to shout at Teo about shutters, and the sky had gone the greenish pewter color that meant the horizon was about to stand up and walk inland.

By noon, Saltmere had pulled its shutters and its citizens indoors. By two, the harbor had climbed into the town's lap, waves slapping against warehouse doors that had never met salt water before. And by three, the wind had found every loose tile, every unfastened sign, every secret a town keeps between its teeth, and was shaking them all loose at once.

Which was how Paloma came to be spending the worst night of the year in the workshop barracks with forty divers, three oil lamps, one terrified cat belonging to nobody, and Elias Corvane, census taker, who had arrived at their door soaked to the collar and holding his ledger bag over his head like a man defending himself from accusations.

"They've closed the company house," he said, dripping on the threshold. "The whole street's under a foot of water. Bess said—" he paused, visibly deciding whether to lie, and deciding against it with the air of a man who found lying physically uncomfortable, "—Bess said I could shelter here or I could drown with the paperwork, and that drowning was the more honest option."

"She's not wrong," Paloma said.

"No," he agreed. "She rarely is. It's very inconvenient."

* * *

The barracks during a storm was less a room than an argument between forty people about whose turn it was to fetch more lamp oil. Paloma installed Elias in the corner farthest from the door, where the roof leaked least, and pretended this was an ordinary act of hospitality rather than a strategic seating arrangement. He sat with his ledger bag hugged to his chest like a child, and she caught herself wondering—not for the first time—what was in it that he guarded so carefully. Paper shouldn't have weighed that much.

"You're staring at my bag," he observed.

"I'm staring at the wall behind your bag."

"Ah. My apologies for standing in front of your wall."

The wind chose that moment to find a gap in the eaves and whistle through it—a long, descending note, like a kettle deciding whether to scream. Around them, the divers had settled into storm rituals: dice, songs, the retelling of famous wrecks with escalating inaccuracy. Old Maren was holding forth on the hurricane of thirty years past, which by her account had lifted an entire church and set it down facing the wrong direction, whereupon the congregation had simply turned their pews and carried on, which everyone agreed was very Saltmere.

Elias watched them with an expression Paloma couldn't place at first. Then she could: it was the look of a man watching a family through a window.

"You weren't born here," she said.

"Corvane. No. Inland. My father kept records for a wool concern." He said it without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. "I'm told I showed aptitude for columns at age six. There was no stopping me after that. My mother wept with pride, or possibly despair; the accounts differ."

"And they sent you to count people instead of sheep."

"People are more difficult. They move. They lie about their ages." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "They lie about other things too."

There it was. The conversation they'd been circling since the harbor market, since the coffee, since the afternoon he'd asked her mother's maiden name and she'd answered with a lie so clumsy a child could have unwound it—and he had let it stand, which was worse than catching her, because it meant he knew and hadn't decided yet what knowing was worth.

"Elias." She kept her voice low. Around them the dice rattled and Maren's church sailed majestically backward. "Why are you here tonight? Really. The company house has cellars."

"The company house has company in it," he said. "And I have recently become uncertain how much of the company I want to be inside of."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest shape of one I can manage in public." He looked at her then, directly, and she saw that beneath the clerk's composure there was something worn thin and careful, like paper folded too many times. "You tested me, at the market. With the story about the aunt in the northern islands."

Paloma felt her stomach drop with the particular lurch of a boat going over a swell she hadn't seen coming. "I don't know what you—"

"You invented a relative who doesn't exist, gave her your grandmother's maiden name, and watched to see if I'd write it down." He said it gently, which somehow made it unbearable. "It was well done. Except that I'd already checked the island registries, and there is no such aunt, and no such name, anywhere north of here. You handed me a false confidence to see if I'd file it." He turned his hat slowly in his hands. "I didn't file it. I want you to know that I noticed, and I didn't file it, and I've been unable to explain to myself why."

The wind slammed the shutters. Someone shrieked, then laughed, then the dice resumed. Paloma sat very still, doing what she did best, which was calculating. Five years of invisibility, and this dry, honest man had just told her—in front of forty witnesses who weren't listening—that he was holding her secret in his hands and choosing, nightly, not to crush it.

"If you report me," she said quietly, "they won't just take the claim. They'll close the workshop. Forty divers on the street. Bess is sixty. Teo has a mother."

"I know exactly what happens," Elias said. "I can recite the procedure. Chapter nine, subsection four: irregular bloodlines flagged for administrative resolution." His mouth twisted around the words *resolution* as if they tasted of copper. "Do you know why I became a census taker, Paloma? Because records seemed like the opposite of violence. Every person counted, every name written down honestly, and the paper protects them. That was the idea." He laughed, short and unhappy. "I was six when I fell in love with columns. Nobody warned me who owned the columns."

The lamp nearest them guttered. Paloma reached out and shielded the flame with her palm out of pure habit—the same motion she used underwater, guarding light in dark places—and when she took her hand away, Elias was looking at it as though she'd shown him something.

"My mother," she said, and stopped. Started again. "Her name was Sera. Not Sera Meridian. Sera Vane, because that's all anyone let her be. But Augustin Meridian signed payments to her every month for twenty years and called it *family obligation*, and if you found that entry—and I think you did, because otherwise you wouldn't be sitting in a leaky barracks instead of a dry cellar—then you know that his legitimate heirs have spent two generations making sure nobody says the word *daughter* anywhere near that signature."

Elias didn't flinch. That was the thing about him; he received hard truths the way pilings receive waves. "I found it," he said. "Three weeks ago. I sat with the ledger open until the lamp burned out, and then I went home, and I didn't sleep, and in the morning I certified nothing and reported nothing and invented a delay involving a clerical error that I, myself, committed, on purpose, which is the first dishonest act of my career." He paused. "I've been committing smaller ones weekly since. It's astonishing how easy it gets. I understand now how empires work."

Despite everything—despite the storm, despite the danger pooling under her ribs—Paloma laughed. It escaped before she could catch it, and Elias looked faintly offended, then faintly pleased, which was such an exact portrait of him that she laughed again.

"You're laughing at my moral collapse."

"I'm laughing because you describe treason like a man auditing it." She wiped her eyes. "Elias. If Aldous Meridian learns what you know, you're not a delayed certification. You're a loose thread. Ask anyone here what the company does with loose threads. Ask the annex records that went missing last month. Ask Teo's ribs."

"I have seen Teo's ribs. I helped splint them." His jaw tightened. "I know."

Outside, the storm reached some private crescendo; the whole building leaned, groaned, and stood, like an old woman refusing a hand. In the sudden ringing quiet after, Elias spoke again, very evenly.

"Here is what I can offer. Certification of the workshop rolls requires my signature. I can delay—misfile, misdate, discover errors, commit errors—for perhaps two months. Three if I'm reckless, and I find I'm increasingly reckless. After that, the machinery moves without me, and it will erase your claim as neatly as erasing a column." He held her gaze. "In exchange, you find me proof. Real proof. The original will, or testimony that would survive a magistrate's scrutiny, or whatever your mother left you besides that locket you think nobody knows about."

Paloma's heart did something complicated. "You knew about the locket."

"I read the inventory from the search of your quarters. Which I also delayed filing." He rubbed his forehead. "You see the problem. I am becoming a dossier of crimes, most of them clerical."

"And if I fail? If I can't find proof in three months?"

"Then I go down certifying nothing," Elias said simply, "and you go down having tried, and the difference between us and the honest dead is that we'll have been honest first."

It wasn't comfort. He didn't pretend it was. And that, more than anything—the refusal to dress the bargain in anything softer than arithmetic—was what made her trust him. Not fully. Trust, for Paloma, was a pearl: formed around an irritation, layered slowly, valuable precisely because it was rare. But she could feel a layer forming.

"Then we have a bargain," she said. "Delayed papers for found proof."

They shook on it, formally, absurdly, while a hurricane tried to remove the roof. His hand was dry and warm and held on exactly one beat longer than commerce required.

* * *

Later, when the lamps were trimmed low and the divers slept in heaps like hounds, the two of them sat awake in the corner where the roof leaked least. The storm had blown itself down to a steady fury, rain drumming the shutters like impatient fingers.

"Do you ever miss it?" Paloma asked. "Inland. Wool. Columns that behave."

"Every autumn," he admitted. "There were mornings when the mist came off the hills and the whole valley smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke, and I thought: this is a place where nothing is hidden." A rueful tilt of the head. "I was wrong, of course. Everywhere I've worked, the ledgers lie somewhere. Saltmere just lies wetter."

"And the sea?" he asked. "Do you miss anything about it? You could have left, when your indenture ends. Most do."

She considered the question honestly, because he had earned honesty the way divers earn pearls: painfully, and one layer at a time.

"I miss the quiet under the surface," she said at last. "Down past ten fathoms the world stops talking. No company, no overseers, no names. Just weight and light and your own heart telling you to hurry, and you telling it no." She smiled at the dark. "My mother gave me her name and her lungs and her temper, and I spent five years pretending none of it was mine. Down there, for a minute at a time, I got to be nobody. It turns out being nobody is restful. Being somebody again is going to be terrifying."

"Being somebody named Meridian?"

"No." The answer came faster than she expected, and surer. "Meridian is theirs. Let them keep it, with the fortune and the fraud and the shame. If I win—if—we'll see what I decide to be called when nobody's holding the pen but me."

Elias was quiet a moment. Then: "For what it's worth, the registrar's office allows amended entries. Names, parentage—all of it, with proper documentation." A pause. "I know the forms."

"Of course you do."

"There's one for everything. It's why I became—" He stopped, and she heard him smile in the darkness. "There's a form for everything, Paloma Vane. Even beginnings."

She might have answered—might have told him that he was becoming the first person in five years she was afraid to lose—but the storm saved her the trouble by tearing a shutter off its hinges two rooms away, and the barracks erupted into chaos, buckets and cursing and the cat achieving a height previously thought impossible for cats.

By dawn the wind had died to a sulky breeze, and Saltmere emerged blinking into a world rearranged: boats in streets, sand in bread, and—so the harbor boys came shouting before the sun was fully up—an old wreck sitting high on the outer bar, black ribs gleaming, thrown up by the sea after fifty years of keeping secrets.

Old Maren squinted at it, then crossed herself, then squinted again.

"Well I'll be damned," she said. "That's the founder's yacht. That's the *Meridian Star*. We all thought she went down with everything aboard."

Paloma felt the words land somewhere below her ribs, in the place where bargains live.

Everything aboard.

Beside her, Elias had gone very still, and very pale, and was looking not at the wreck but at Paloma—as if he had just done the arithmetic, and the sum had come out dangerous.
