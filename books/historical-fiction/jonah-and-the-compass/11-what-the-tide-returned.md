## 11. What the Tide Returned

The storm left Saltmere the way a drunk leaves a party—apologizing for nothing, breaking things on the way out.

Paloma stood on the workshop dock at first light and took inventory of what the hurricane had done to her world. The drying racks had survived, mostly. The north pier had not; it lay in splinters like kindling someone had gotten angry with. The oyster beds would be silted over for weeks, which meant no diving, which meant no wages, which meant eleven months of indenture suddenly felt longer than eleven months had any right to feel.

But it was the harbor itself that made her stop counting losses.

The sea had given something back.

She saw it before she understood it—a dark shape beneath the green water of the anchorage, visible now because the storm had shifted the sandbar that had hidden it for forty years. A hull. A large one, lying on its side in eight feet of water, its masts long since gone, its timbers furred with weed but unmistakably, impossibly whole.

"Jonah's teeth," Bess said at her shoulder. The old matron had come out in her shawl despite the wind still worrying the harbor, and her hand found Paloma's arm and gripped it. "Do you see what I see?"

"A ship."

"Not a ship." Bess's voice had gone strange—hushed and reverent and a little afraid. "That's the *Mermaid's Purse*. That's Augustin Meridian's yacht. I'd know that stern anywhere; I used to watch her sail out when I was a girl no older than you. She went down with all hands the winter the old man died. They never found her."

Paloma looked at the dark shape in the water and felt something turn over in her chest—not dread, exactly, though dread had become a familiar lodger. Something more like attention. Like the moment on the beds when your fingers closed on an oyster that felt different from the others, heavier, and you learned not to let your face change.

*Fate offering a hand,* said a voice in her head that sounded uncomfortably like Elias Corvane being precise about something.

She kept her face very still.

* * *

By noon, half of Saltmere stood along the seawall to look at it. Wrecks were treasure, and treasure was gossip, and gossip was free. The harbormaster was already talking about salvage rights. Two of Aldous Meridian's clerks had appeared at the company house windows like crows on a fence, watching, taking notes.

Inside the workshop, the mood was different. Storm season meant no work, and no work meant the divers gathered in the long room with mugs of chicory coffee, trading stories about hurricanes they had survived, and today every story bent toward the wreck.

"My grandfather swore the *Purse* was scuttled," Teo said. He sat with his hands wrapped—healing now, three weeks after the punishment for copying that key impression, the knuckles still stiff—and his voice carried the careful flatness he'd had since. "Not sunk. Scuttled. That she went down too neatly, in too sheltered a spot, the same week the founder died."

"Scuttled by who?" asked Marta, the eldest of the divers, who had opinions about everything and shared roughly half of them.

"That's the question, isn't it." Teo turned his mug slowly on the table. "Old man dies, his fancy boat sinks the same week, and the son inherits everything clean as a whistled note. My grandfather said there were men in this town who drank free for years off that story and never told anyone why."

Paloma sat near the window, mending a dive glove she did not need mended, and listened the way she had taught herself to listen for five years: appearing not to.

Her mind was doing arithmetic it had begun without her permission.

Augustin Meridian's yacht. Sunk the week of his death. Her mother, Sera Vane, eighteen years old that winter, already acknowledged—if the ledgers Elias had described were true—as *family obligation*, already carrying secrets the way divers carried air in their lungs, carefully and under pressure.

And her mother had known the sea. Had trusted it the way other people trusted churches.

*If I needed to hide something where no man would ever look,* Paloma thought, turning the glove in her hands, *I would hide it where only a diver could go.*

She thought of the locket in her sea chest. The half-burned letter naming her grandmother as the founder's daughter. *Throw it in the tide,* Bess had begged her. But what if the tide wasn't where things ended? What if the tide was where her mother had begun?

"You've gone somewhere," Bess said quietly, settling beside her. The matron's eyes were sharp above her cup.

"I'm mending a glove."

"Mm. And I'm the harbormaster's wife." Bess sipped her coffee. "Child, when you look like that, either you've found trouble or trouble has found you, and lately I can't tell the difference."

Paloma tied off the last stitch. "Bess. When the *Mermaid's Purse* went down—did my mother dive it?"

The silence stretched just a beat too long, and that beat told Paloma everything before Bess sighed and set down her cup.

"She wanted to," Bess admitted. "The week after the funeral. She came to me white as sailcloth and said the captain of the *Purse* had been paid to sink her slow, in the shallows off the sandbar, not deep water—that the old man had ordered it himself before he died, that he'd loaded her cabin with his own papers rather than let his son have them. She said—" Bess's voice dropped, remembering a girl fifty years gone. "She said Sera told her, 'He put everything that mattered in a strongbox and everything the box matters to on the bottom, and neither one can be forged.' Then Sera dove it twice, and the third time she came up coughing blood, and I forbid her to go back, and she laughed and said it didn't matter. Said the sandbar would take care of it. Said the sea would keep it until it was wanted."

Paloma's hands had gone cold around the mug. "Until it was *wanted*."

"That's what she said. Girl-talk, I thought. Grief-talk." Bess looked out the window toward the dark shape in the harbor, and her lips pressed thin. "I'm an old woman, Paloma Vane, and I have learned to stop thinking anything is girl-talk."

* * *

They dove the wreck three days later, when the harbor settled and the harbormaster's salvage survey—conducted at a glacial pace by men who billed by the hour—had officially pronounced the hull "of negligible value, cargo long deteriorated." The company's interest cooled accordingly. Aldous's clerks stopped coming to the windows.

Which was, Paloma suspected, exactly how her mother had planned it fifty years ago. Patience was a diver's virtue. Everything worth having waited at the bottom of something.

She went down first, on a slack tide in the gray morning, with Marta above as lookout and Teo on the rope and Bess on the dock pretending to mend nets with the fierce concentration of a woman guarding a secret with her whole body.

The water was cold and green and full of stirred-up ghost-light. Paloma descended the line hand over hand until her fins touched the wreck's upturned deck, and the *Mermaid's Purse* rose around her like a drowned cathedral—beams furred white with plumose anemones, a school of pollock threading through what had been a ballroom, the whole vast hull breathing with the slow tidal surge the way a sleeping thing breathes.

It should have frightened her. Five years ago it would have. Now she felt only the focused calm the sea had taught her, the counting-oysters calm, the *unremarkable* calm—and beneath it, rising like warmth through cold water, a feeling she could not immediately name.

Then she understood. It was the feeling of being somewhere her mother had been.

*Sera Vane dove this deck when she was younger than I am now.* Paloma hung in the green water with her hand flat on the weed-slick planking and let that truth move through her. Her mother had swum this ballroom. Had run her fingers along these beams. Had maybe, in the dark and the pressure and the loneliness of it, talked to the dead man whose daughter she was, or to the child she might someday have.

*Mama. You hid it well. It took me five years and a census taker to even think to look.*

She got to work.

The captain's cabin was aft, below the collapsed quarterdeck, and the storm had done her a kindness there: the sand that had sealed it for decades had scoured a doorway-wide gap. Paloma wedged herself through with her lamp held high.

The cabin was a small green universe. A desk, its drawers long since rotted to lace. A chair, or the bones of one. Cupboards sagging open, their contents dissolved into anonymous drift. Forty years of small creatures coming and going through the gaps in the world.

Nothing. Nothing. Nothing—

Then, behind the fallen paneling where the desk met the bulkhead, in the angle of shadow no casual survey would ever reach: iron.

She knew the shape of it before her fingers found it. Every diver knew a strongbox the way a farmer knew a stone wall—the bands, the rivets, the hasp. This one was smaller than most, built to be carried by one person, and it was buried to its shoulders in the accumulated sand of half a century, as if the wreck itself had drawn it close and gone to sleep on top of it.

Paloma cleared the sand with quick, careful sweeps, her heart hammering in a way she refused to let travel down the rope to Teo. The box came free with a soft grating sigh, and she hugged it to her chest—it was heavier than it looked, dense with whatever papers her mother had sworn lay inside—and kicked for the light.

* * *

They opened it that night in Bess's rooms, doors barred, shutters drawn, a single candle burning, because four women and Teo huddled around contraband by candlelight was dramatic and a magistrate's search party interrupting daylight larceny was merely convenient.

The lock had surrendered to time; Teo eased the hasp open with two flat knives and a diver's patience, and the lid came up with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed linen, layered like the pearls her mother had once harvested:

Papers. Dozens of them. Sealed letters. A marriage record Paloma didn't recognize. And on top, in a hand Paloma *did* recognize—she knew it from the locket's inscription, from the letter fragments, from fifty years of dreaming it—the firm, sloped writing of Augustin Meridian himself.

Bess lifted the top document with hands that trembled and read the heading aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle of it:

"*Last Will and Testament of Augustin Meridian, being of sound mind and memory...*" She stopped. Started again. "*...to my granddaughter Sera Vane, daughter of my daughter Liana, I leave the pearl workshop and its waters, its charter and its people, in trust until her heirs come of age...*"

"Heirs," Paloma whispered.

The word hung in the candlelight.

Because her mother had been pregnant that winter. Barely. Had she even known? Paloma did the arithmetic and her breath caught—she had. Or guessed. Or hoped. The timing was impossible and exact, the way the best secrets always were.

Sera Vane had been eighteen, pregnant, unacknowledged, disinherited by a forged will, standing over her grandfather's sunken ship—and she had known, or believed, that the child inside her was the heir Augustin named. Not her. *Her heirs.* Paloma. Paloma had been written into a will before she was born, hidden in a strongbox at the bottom of the harbor before she drew her first breath.

Her mother hadn't just hidden a document. She had hidden a *daughter*, folded small, tucked into iron, left with the sea to keep.

Paloma sat down slowly on Bess's stool. Nobody said anything for a while. The candle fluttered. Outside, far off, the tide turned with its ancient patience.

"Well," said Marta at last, into the silence, "your mother always did have a flair for the theatrical."

Someone laughed—it might have been Paloma—and once started, they all did, the shaky helpless laughter of people who had been afraid for weeks and had just, for one shining moment, forgotten to be.

But Paloma stopped laughing quickly, because the next thought arrived, cold and punctual as a ledger entry:

A will that named an heir was only as good as the proof it was genuine. And the man who had forged the original—who had spent fifty years building a fortune on his father's erased signature—was the same man whose son now toured workshops with cold courtesy and dismissed divers onto the street. If the *Mermaid's Purse* could surface after half a century, so could news of it. Salvage men talked. Harbormasters filed reports. Clerks watched windows.

The box was in her hands. What was in it could save everyone she loved.

What happened next depended entirely on whether she could prove it before Aldous Meridian learned it existed—and whether anyone in Saltmere would believe a diver's word, a matron's memory, and a handful of papers that contradicted fifty years of recorded history.

She looked up and found Teo watching her, his wrapped hands resting carefully on the table, his eyes steady.

"So," he said quietly. "When do we open the rest of it?"

Paloma looked down at the oilcloth packets still waiting in the strongbox, at the sealed letters with her grandfather's crest stamped fat and red upon them—and beneath them all, one envelope addressed in her mother's hand, in ink gone brown as old kelp:

*For my child, if the sea is kind.*

She reached for it just as a fist hammered on the door downstairs—three blows, hard, official—and Bess blew out the candle.
