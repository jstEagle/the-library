## 12. The Strongbox

The strongbox sat in the center of Bess's mending room like a guest no one had invited and everyone was afraid to look at directly.

It had come up from the wreck two days earlier, hauled aboard Teo's cousin's salvage sloop wrapped in netting and crusted white with forty years of the sea's opinion about private property. They had smuggled it into the workshop after dark, Paloma walking ahead with a lantern held at a casual angle, as though she were merely a woman taking an evening stroll through her own workplace while carrying fire. The trick, she had learned over five years of indenture, was to look bored. Nobody stops a bored person. Boredom is the most convincing uniform in Saltmere.

Now it was morning, low tide, and the box occupied the mending table between a basket of split oyster shells and Bess's good shears.

"It's smaller than I imagined," said Maren, one of the three divers Paloma had sworn in. She was the eldest of them, hands like knotted rope, and she said everything as though grading it against reality and finding reality mildly disappointing.

"Fortunes usually are," said Bess.

The box itself was iron, or had been once. Now it was more rust than metal, the lock furred orange, the hinges weeping orange tears down its sides. The seal of the Meridian Company—a ship's wheel inside a circle—was still legible on the lid, pressed into lead, and unbroken. That was what mattered. Unbroken, dated by the corrosion itself, older than any living memory of the workshop. Whatever was inside had gone into that box before the sea took the yacht *Larkspur* down, and nothing had touched it since.

Paloma laid her palm flat on the cold lid and felt her own pulse answer.

"Well," she said. "Someone fetch me something with an edge on it."

"You're not opening it with my good shears," said Bess.

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were absolutely going to."

* * *

In the end they opened it with a cold chisel, a hammer, and an argument.

The argument was about whether the seal should be preserved entire. Elias Corvane—present because he had become, without anyone ever formally announcing it, one of them—insisted that an unbroken seal, witnessed and dated, might matter later. "If there's a dispute," he said, "about when this was sealed, or whether anyone has tampered with the contents—"

"If there's a dispute," said Maren, "it'll be because someone is trying to take this away from her, and I don't think they'll be swayed by the integrity of the wax."

"Documents are how those people fight," Elias said quietly. "We should fight the same way."

That settled it, mostly because Paloma had gone quiet in the way Bess recognized from her mother, thirty years ago—the silence of a diver deciding where to put her hands.

They did it carefully. A thin blade around the lead collar, easing the seal off whole like lifting the cap from an egg. Bess wrapped it in flannel and put it in the tin where she kept the good buttons, which everyone agreed was the safest place in Saltmere, because nobody had ever once wanted the good buttons.

Then the chisel, then the hammer, then a sound like an old man clearing his throat after a lifetime of smoking, and the lock gave up.

Nobody moved for a moment.

"Go on, then," said Maren. "Before I die of old age."

Inside: oilcloth, wrapped tight and tarred, and beneath the oilcloth, dry. Dry after forty years under the sea, dry as a sermon. Someone had loved these papers enough to protect them from the ocean itself.

Paloma unwrapped them with hands that would not stop shaking, so Bess put her own hands over them until they steadied.

There were letters first. A bundle of them, tied with ribbon gone stiff as wire. And beneath the letters, folded in thirds, four pages in a hand Paloma did not know, stamped with the magistrate's seal, signed by two witnesses whose names meant nothing and everything.

The top page read: *Last Will and Testament of Augustin Meridian, of the city of Saltmere.*

"Oh," said Paloma. It was not a word so much as air leaving a room.

* * *

She read it aloud, because Bess said her eyes weren't what they were, and because Maren said if she had to sit through this she'd at least have it in her ears, and because—though nobody said this part—words spoken aloud in company become true in a way words read alone do not.

The will was short. Founders' wills often are; men who write long wills are the ones with something to hide. Augustin Meridian left modest sums to his son Corwin and his grandchildren, "their provision being already ample." He endowed a school for the children of divers. He freed his household servants outright.

And then:

*"To my daughter Sera Vane, born of my love and kept from my name by the cruelty of convention, I leave the pearl workshop at Graywater Quay, its beds, boats, and charter, together with all shares I hold in the Meridian Trading Company, in perpetuity, to her and to her heirs lawfully begotten. My son knows why. Let him read this and remember that I knew."*

The mending room was silent except for the harbor breathing below the window.

"To her heirs lawfully begotten," Maren repeated slowly. "That's you. That means you."

"It means me," Paloma said, and heard her voice crack on the second word, and decided not to mind.

But it was the letters that turned triumph into something colder. There were eleven of them, in two hands. The larger portion were from Augustin to Sera—warm, wry, guilt threaded through affection like wire through wax. He wrote about tides. He wrote about pearls, which he clearly understood better than people. He wrote, in the last one: *You are more mine than the name I gave you, and worth more than everything I am leaving to those who share it.*

The others were business correspondence, and these were the poison. Three letters between Corwin Meridian and a scrivener named Halvard Pell, arranging—first delicately, then not delicately at all—the production of a replacement will. One of them contained the original draft of the forgery itself, crossed out and corrected in Pell's cramped hand: *the clause re: the Vane woman must go entirely; substitute a bequest of fifty pounds 'in consideration of past services.'* Another instructed Pell to backdate the ink. A third, the last, was Corwin at his most himself: *Burn your drafts. Burn this. The old man lingers, and I will not have loose paper floating when he finally sinks.*

"He forged it," Elias said. He had read the letters twice each and held them as though they might bite. "Corwin forged the will before Augustin was even dead. Everything since—the company succession, Aldous's whole inheritance—it rests on paper made in a scrivener's back room."

"Fifty pounds," said Bess, "for consideration of past services." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "My mother scrubbed floors fifty years for less consideration than that."

Paloma set the will down on the table very gently, the way you set down a sleeping child.

"We need a magistrate," she said.

* * *

They spent the afternoon the way treasure ought properly to be spent: making copies. Bess had a nephew in the printing row who owed her for a bad winter she'd gotten him through, and by evening there existed three fair copies of the will and two of the key letters, distributed to locations Paloma refused to name aloud even among friends. The originals went back into the strongbox, and the strongbox went into the one hiding place Paloma trusted above all others: beneath the flagstone under the drying racks, where her mother had hidden things before her, in a cavity worn smooth by two generations of secrets.

"The racks smell like ten years of oysters," Maren observed, helping slide the stone.

"Exactly," said Paloma. "No Meridian has ever voluntarily smelled like this in their life. We could leave the crown jewels here."

It was almost festive, that evening in the barracks kitchen. Someone found a bottle of plum wine that had been aging, in the sense of being forgotten, behind a sack of barley since before the hurricane. Teo's arm was still splinted and he held his cup awkwardly in his left hand, but he drank with his usual commitment. Even Elias relaxed by degrees, sitting slightly apart on an overturned crate, watching Paloma laugh at something Maren said with an expression he probably believed was neutral and was not.

He should have enjoyed it more. Later he would think that. He should have let himself have the whole hour, because it was the last easy hour any of them would get for some time.

The knock came at the gate near midnight.

* * *

It was Joss, the boy who ran messages along the quay for coppers, soaked to the knees and out of breath. He looked, Paloma thought, like a boy carrying news he hoped would not stick to him.

"There's a man at the Meridian house asking after you," he said. "Aldous Meridian himself. He came down to the salvage office this evening. Wanted to know who bought the wreck rights on the *Larkspur*, wanted to know what came up, wanted—" Joss swallowed. "He asked for you by name. Paloma Vane."

The kitchen got very quiet. The plum wine bottle sat on the table with its neck open, forgotten mid-pour.

"How would he know the name?" Maren demanded. "How does a Meridian know a workshop diver's name?"

Elias stood up from the crate. He had gone pale in the lamplight, and Paloma watched him arrive at the same conclusion she was arriving at, and watched him hate the journey.

"The census rolls," he said. "When I flagged questions about her mother's line, the queries went upstairs. I filed them through proper channels." His jaw worked. "I did it properly. That's the trouble with me. I keep doing things properly."

"So he's known," Paloma said. "For weeks."

"He's suspected. Now the salvage confirms it. A Meridian yacht comes up out of the harbor and two days later a Vane is asking questions about it? He doesn't need proof anymore. He needs speed." Elias was already moving, gathering his coat, his clerk's mind visibly running ahead of his feet. "He'll move against the lease. The workshop's charter renewal comes before the company board—and Aldous sits on the board. He can call closure for restructuring. He can have the gates sealed inside a month."

"A month," repeated Bess.

"Less. If he's frightened, less. Frightened rich men don't deliberate, they act, and then they hire other men to explain afterward why it was necessary." Elias paused at the door and looked back at Paloma, and for once the census taker's careful composure had cracked clean through, and underneath it was just a tired honest man in a wet city. "Paloma. Whatever you were planning to do—do it now. Do it faster than you think is possible. He will not wait for you to be ready."

Joss, who had been edging toward the door with the instincts of a boy who delivered bad news professionally and liked to be elsewhere when it detonated, cleared his throat.

"There was one more thing," he said. "The man at the Meridian house—he asked about you too, sir. The census gentleman. Wanted to know where you lodged, and whether you'd been seen at the workshop lately."

The lamplight guttered in the draught from the door. Somewhere out in the dark the tide was turning, patient and indifferent, the way it always was.

"He suspects us both," Paloma said quietly.

"No," said Elias, pulling the door open onto the rain. "Suspecting is what he does in drawing rooms. This is different." He stepped out into the weather, and his voice came back to them flat and certain over his shoulder:

"This is a countdown."
