## 13. Bargains in the Dark

The invitation arrived the way invitations to one's own funeral generally arrive: on good paper, carried by a man who didn't meet your eyes.

Paloma found it folded inside her ration chit at supper—a small card of heavy cream stock, smelling faintly of cedar and money. The handwriting was elegant without being fussy, the kind learned from tutors who charged by the hour.

*Mr. Aldous Meridian requests the pleasure of Miss Vane's company. Tomorrow, four o'clock, the Harbor Rooms. Transportation will be provided.*

"He doesn't request pleasures," Teo said over her shoulder, reading upside down as divers did everything—by reflex and out of the corner of an eye. "He summons them."

"It says pleasure."

"It says *trap*. The words just got dressed up first." Teo's face was still yellowed along the jaw where the enforcers had worked him over, and he held his left hand curled slightly, protectively, against his chest. But his eyes were sharp as ever. "Don't go."

"I have to eat somewhere tomorrow anyway."

"Paloma." He lowered his voice until it was barely louder than the tide. "Men like that don't invite you to dinner unless they've already decided what you're worth. He's not asking questions. He's making an offer on something he thinks he owns."

She turned the card over in her fingers. It was thicker than three months of her wages' paper equivalent. She wondered idly what one sheet of it cost, and then stopped wondering, because wondering about things like that was exactly how Aldous Meridian caught people.

"Then I'll find out what price he's put on me," she said. "It's useful to know these things. Like knowing the depth before you drop."

Teo looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw—the set of her mouth, maybe, or the calm she wore like a diving weight—he nodded once, slowly, and went back to his soup.

Bess said nothing at all that night, which from Bess was a speech.

* * *

The Harbor Rooms occupied the top floor of the Meridian Trading Company's headquarters, and Paloma hated immediately how much she liked them. Floor-to-ceiling windows gave onto the whole sweep of Saltmere's harbor: the drying racks of the workshop small and gray in the distance, the beds beyond them invisible beneath their stretch of green water. From here the company's empire looked orderly. Beautiful, even. Rooms like this existed to make you feel that the view already belonged to someone else, and that you were being permitted, briefly, to borrow the looking.

Aldous Meridian stood at the window with his back to her when she entered, hands clasped behind him like a man posing for a portrait of himself deciding something.

"Miss Vane." He turned. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, with the founder's same broad brow and none of the warmth the old portrait suggested. His smile arrived precisely when expected and departed the same way. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. Do you take tea?"

"Only if it comes without conditions."

His smile deepened a fraction, as if she'd pleased him. "Then we understand each other already. Tea anyway. And sit. I promise my chairs have no conditions either. They are simply chairs."

They were very fine chairs. Paloma sat in hers and kept her spine away from the backrest, the way you keep your body off a rock underwater—you never knew what was growing there.

"You've worked the beds for five years," Aldous said, pouring. "Productivity records show you in the upper quartile every season since your second year. Quiet. Punctual. No infractions." He handed her the cup. "You'll forgive a businessman his admiration. In my experience, quiet people are either very content or very busy. You don't strike me as content."

"Perhaps I'm just well-rested."

"Mm." He sat across from her, unhurried, and studied her the way an assayer studies ore—not hostilely, but with a complete absence of doubt that whatever was in front of him would eventually be valued, priced, and smelted. "Your mother dove these waters too. Sera Vane. Nineteen years on the company rolls. Did you know she drew a monthly payment from the house for most of that time? Quite generous, for a diver. 'Family obligation,' the ledgers call it. Curious phrase, don't you find? Family obligation. As if the family owed *her*."

The tea was excellent. Paloma drank it slowly and let the silence do her lying for her. She had learned long ago that a badly prepared lie sank fast; silence floated forever.

"You don't seem surprised," Aldous observed.

"You've invited me to a room with a harbor view and better tea than I've tasted in five years," she said. "I'm surprised by all of it. I'm economizing."

That startled a real laugh out of him—one short bark, quickly reeled back in, but real. He resettled himself, and something in his posture shifted from interrogation to negotiation, which was, she suspected, the entire purpose of the laugh. A man who could make you laugh believed he could make you sell.

"Let us not waste the afternoon circling," he said. "There is a story going around—old men's gossip mostly—that Augustin Meridian had a daughter no one speaks of. That he settled money on her. That when he died, certain papers were signed in haste by my father, and certain inheritances flowed in a direction that—" he waved two fingers, a small dismissive tide, "—wasn't tidy. Gossip. But gossip has a way of attaching itself to names, and lately your name has begun to attract gossip like a lamp attracts moths. I dislike moths, Miss Vane. They get into the accounts."

"And what would you do about a moth?"

"Nothing dramatic." He opened a leather folio on the table between them and turned it so she could see. Figures, neat as fish scales. "Passage to any port on any company vessel, first cabin, for you and anyone you care to name. Letters of introduction to respectable houses in Corvane, Ilse, or the northern trade cities—houses that will receive you kindly because I will ask them to. A settlement of eight hundred silvers, paid half on departure and half on arrival, with references sufficient for you to open a shop, buy a boat, marry a doctor, whatever occupies you. In exchange—" he closed the folio gently, "—you sign a simple statement. That your mother was a diver, that her parents were unknown to her, that the gossip is noise. Silence, essentially. Not even silence, really. Just accuracy. My grandfather's will is a matter of public record. There is nothing to contest. All I ask is that you decline to become a fiction."

Eight hundred silvers. Paloma did the arithmetic without meaning to—it was a diver's reflex, counting everything. Eight hundred silvers was forty years of her indenture wages. It was a workshop bought outright. It was freedom, gift-wrapped, delivered by the man who'd had her friend beaten for touching a key.

And it meant he was afraid. Men who weren't afraid didn't pay eight hundred silvers for a signature.

"The terms are generous," she said carefully.

"They are fair."

"They're not the same thing." She set down her cup and turned it slowly on its saucer, watching him watch it. "May I consider it? A decision like this shouldn't be made in an afternoon."

Something flickered behind his courtesy—the first unguarded thing she'd seen in him. Impatience, yes, but under it, calculation running fast, recalculating. She understood then that he had expected the money to work the way money always worked on people who had none. Her hesitation was data he hadn't planned for.

"Of course," he said smoothly. Too smoothly. "Take three days."

"Five."

"Three." His smile returned, thinner. "The tides wait for no one, Miss Vane, and neither does restructuring. The workshop's lease comes up for renewal soon, you know. Old arrangements, old leases—they do have a way of expiring when a family tidies its affairs. If your answer is yes, you'll want to be gone before the paperwork gets restless."

There it was: the deadline, delivered like a courtesy. *Three days, and then we burn it all down and everyone standing too close.*

"One question," Paloma said, rising. "Why me? You could settle this with the magistrate, with the company lawyers. Why pay a pearl diver eight hundred silvers?"

Aldous walked her to the door himself, an honor she trusted approximately as far as she could throw the building.

"Because," he said mildly, holding the door, "a rumor contested in court becomes a legend. A rumor paid off becomes nothing at all." He paused, and his eyes—his grandfather's eyes, she thought, and hated the thought—moved past her shoulder toward the window, toward the harbor, toward the company offices below where the census rolls lived in their locked cabinets.

"Oh—one more thing," he added, conversationally, as though remembering a trifling errand. "Your name came up in some irregular census correspondence recently. Annotations. Queries. A clerk with more conscience than sense, asking questions above his station. If you happen to speak with him—and I imagine you do, a young woman alone can't help needing help now and then—perhaps mention that clerks who amend official records find themselves reassigned. To places without harbors." He smiled his portrait smile. "Inland postings. Terrible for the health, inland. People dry out."

*He suspects Elias.* The knowledge dropped through her like a stone through dark water, down and down, and she made her face into still water over it.

"I barely know any clerks," she said. "We don't move in the same circles."

"No," Aldous agreed pleasantly. "Not yet. Three days, Miss Vane. Do give my regards to the workshop."

* * *

She didn't go straight back. She paid a dock boy two coppers for a bucket of whelks she didn't want and used the next hour shelling them on the seawall, alone, where anyone following her could see exactly what a foolish woman did after receiving eight hundred silvers: nothing. Sat. Shelled whelks. Watched the gulls rob each other.

Inside, she was moving fast.

*He knows about the ledgers—the payment records, the annotation. He knows someone queried them. Which means Elias's questions have already been read by the wrong eyes, and Aldous knows a census clerk has been pulling threads. Not proof yet—proof would have brought soldiers, not tea—but suspicion, and suspicion in a man like Aldous moves on a schedule.*

*Three days. He gave me three days because he needs three days—to close whatever gap remains between suspecting and proving. And whatever he plans for the workshop, it happens on the far side of those three days whether I sign or not.*

She looked at the whelk in her palm, small and armored and utterly spent, and tossed it to a gull.

* * *

Elias found her at the drying racks at dusk, ostensibly delivering a corrected census form, actually delivering himself, pale and tight-jawed.

"You met with him." It wasn't a question. "Half the harbor watched you walk into the Harbor Rooms."

"Then half the harbor knows I'm worth eight hundred silvers, which improves my marriage prospects considerably." She kept her voice light, but took his sleeve and drew him behind the racks, into the smell of salt-rot and rope. When she told him—in a low stream, watching his face as she laid out the offer, the deadline, and finally the warning—she watched the last color leave it at the end.

"Inland postings," he repeated hollowly. "He said that. About clerks who amend records."

"Those were the words."

Elias stood very still. She recognized the stillness; she'd worn it herself for five years. It was the stillness of a person rapidly inventorying their life, deciding what could be carried and what had to be cut loose if they were to swim.

"I should report it," he said slowly. "A bribe attempt against a party of interest. It's procedure. There's a form—"

"Elias." She said his name quietly, and he stopped. "Think about who processes those forms. Think about whose company owns the cabinet your report would sleep in tonight. You'd be handing him the proof he doesn't yet have—that you matter enough to warn."

He closed his mouth. Above them the gulls wheeled and screamed their small eternal grievances.

"Three days," he said at last. "What do we do with three days?"

"We pretend," Paloma said. "I spend them considering his offer—publicly, visibly, greedily. Let him believe I'm counting his silvers. Greedy people are predictable; they're thinking about the price, not the game. And while he watches me count—" she glanced toward the workshop, where lamplight was blooming window by window, where Bess was even now drawing the shutters, where Teo's silhouette crossed the yard with his careful, guarded limp, "—we make sure that whatever he burns, something survives the fire. Copies. Testimony. Names. Somewhere he isn't looking."

"That's a large assignment for three days."

"Yes," said Paloma. "Fortunately, we work cheap."

It earned her half a laugh from him—strained, frightened, but real, and she found she'd been waiting for it the way divers waited for the sun through morning fog. Then his expression sobered, and he reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper, holding it between them without quite offering it.

"There's one more thing," he said. "When I got back to the office tonight, my desk had been searched. Nothing taken. Nothing moved, even—except the drawer with the strongbox salvage manifest. It had been relocked a half-turn proud. Someone wanted me to think it hadn't been touched."

Paloma felt the water rise around her, cold and patient.

"He's not waiting three days," she said. "He's already started."

Out past the harbor bar, where the evening sea lay flat and innocent, the tide was turning—quietly, completely, the way it always did, whether anyone was ready or not.
