## 14. The Turned Key

Paloma knew something was wrong before she knew what it was. The drying racks told her.

She had spent five years learning the workshop's small languages—the creak of timber in changing weather, the pitch of Bess's humming when money was short, the particular way Teo whistled when he was pretending not to worry. And she knew the drying racks, the long salt-bleached tables where oyster shells were laid out like linen on a washing line. Every morning the gulls held their parliament there, loud and shameless, and every morning old Marta shooed them off with a broom and a stream of curses that would have embarrassed a dockworker.

This morning the racks were empty of gulls. Marta stood at the end of them with her broom held wrong—not raised to swing, but gripped like a walking stick, like something to lean on while the world rearranged itself.

Paloma's stomach dropped through the pier pilings and kept going.

"The hiding place," she said. Not a question.

Marta's face answered anyway. "Under the third rack from the seaward end. The loose board." The old woman's voice was flat as a becalmed sea. "It's been turned over, look you. Everything lifted out neat as a surgeon. Whoever did it knew exactly where to put his hands."

The will. Her mother's letter. The proof of everything—gone between one tide and the next.

Paloma did not remember walking to the rack. She did remember kneeling, prying up the board with her fingernails, and finding the shallow cavity swept clean except for a single thing left behind: a button, brass, stamped with the Meridian crest. Not dropped. *Left.* A signature. A calling card from a man who wanted her to know he'd been there, wanted her to know he could reach anywhere, that walls and floors and five years of careful silence meant nothing at all.

She sat back on her heels in the shell dust and laughed—one short, ugly sound that startled even her.

"They're taunting us," Marta said grimly. "That's a taunt, that is."

"Yes," Paloma said. She closed her fist around the button until its teeth bit her palm. "It is."

* * *

Teo had not come to work.

Everyone noticed, because Teo was the sort of person who was always there—first through the gate, last out of it, whistling his tuneless whistle, complaining about his breakfast with the devotion of a man who loved complaining the way other men loved gambling. His absence had a shape. It filled the workshop like smoke.

Paloma found his cousin Nesta in the net-mending shed and asked, quietly, where he was.

Nesta's hands stopped on the net. "His sister came for him two nights past. Men took him from the boarding house—Aldous's men, the ones with the gray coats. Questioning, they said." Her voice shook on the word. "He came back yesterday morning. Paloma—" She looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and terrified. "He could barely walk. They worked him over proper. His hand—" She made a fist of her own, unconsciously, protectively. "Two fingers broken. He keeps saying he didn't tell them anything. He keeps saying it."

"Where is he now?"

"Gone. This morning, before light. Left this." Nesta pressed a folded scrap of paper into Paloma's hand as though it were hot. "For you. He wouldn't say more."

Paloma waited until she was alone in the ropewalk, shadow deep and nobody near, before she unfolded it. The handwriting was Teo's but wrecked by it—letters lurching, the pen pressed so hard it had torn through in places.

*They knew about the key impression. They knew I copied it. They asked where we put things and I swore I didn't know and they broke my fingers one at a time and Paloma I'm sorry I'm so sorry after the fourth one I couldn't—I didn't want to but my mouth just said it. The racks. I gave them the racks. Find me when it's done or don't find me, either way I'm sorry.*

Paloma read it three times. Then she folded it small and held the little square of paper in her cupped hands like a moth she might warm back to life, and she thought: *of course.* Of course it went this way. She had known it in her bones the night she'd sworn Teo and the others to secrecy—that she was asking ordinary people to carry something heavy and dangerous, and that ordinary people break. That was what made them ordinary. That was what made them worth protecting, which she had failed to do.

Guilt is a strange cargo. It doesn't weigh what you expect. She had imagined that if the worst happened, guilt would crush her flat. Instead it made her strangely light, hollowed out, and in the hollowness there was room for one clear thought:

*Aldous has the will. Aldous does not yet have everything.*

Because Elias had memorized the ledgers. Because Bess carried forty years of Sera Vane in her head. Because a stolen document proves only that someone stole—and the magistrate's sitting was still coming, still nine days off, and Aldous would need to do more than hold paper to hold off the truth.

Unless he burned it all first.

She straightened. Down the row, the divers were drifting toward the racks, drawn by the wrongness the way fish drift toward a change in current. Their faces turned to her. Waiting. Five years of silence, and now they were waiting for her to speak.

"Listen to me," Paloma said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "What was under the racks is gone. Some of you know what it was; the rest of you don't need to. What matters now is this: from this hour, trust no one outside this circle. Not the clerks, not the harbor watch, not your own cousins if their eyes are wrong. If anyone asks you questions you don't like, agree with everything they say and forget it by supper. We have spent years being invisible. For nine more days, we are going to be ghosts."

Someone at the back—Dara, the youngest, all elbows and stubbornness—raised her hand as if this were a lesson and not a war council. "And then? After nine days?"

"And then," Paloma said, "we stop haunting and start speaking."

* * *

Elias Corvane had a system for noticing things, honed by eleven years of census work, and the system was screaming.

He noticed, first, that the copy clerk who usually sneered at his boots was suddenly very friendly. He noticed that the annex fire brigade had inspected the records rooms twice in one week, which was once more than they had inspected them in the previous six years. He noticed that his supervisor had begun locking the archive at odd hours and had developed a new cough whenever Elias approached the shelf marked *W — Workshops, Chartered.*

Most of all, he noticed the messenger.

The boy appeared at his lodging house that evening with a note requesting Elias's presence at the Meridian townhouse regarding "an irregularity in the Saltmere rolls." The note was signed with Aldous Meridian's own hand, which was itself an irregularity—such matters went through three layers of bureaucracy precisely so they never touched hands like that.

Elias sat on the edge of his narrow bed with the note in his lap and thought about Paloma Vane drinking bad harbor coffee and testing him with a false confidence, and about the night of the storm when she had traded him her deepest secret for his delay of a single form. He had chosen, in that dim barracks with the rain hammering like applause, to stand on her side of the line. He had told himself the choice was made and finished.

But choices, he was learning, were not doors you walked through once. They were stairs. You had to keep choosing, step after step, especially when a townhouse summons arrived smelling of cologne and consequence.

He went. Refusal would have flagged him faster than any ledger entry.

Aldous received him in a study designed to make visitors feel like overdue accounts. Bookshelves of uncut volumes. A desk older than the colony. Aldous himself stood at the window with a glass of wine, silver-haired and immaculate, wearing the expression of a man watching his own gardens grow.

"Counsel Corvane." He gestured to a chair without turning around. "You've handled the Saltmere workshop rolls for some years."

"Since the re-survey. Yes, sir."

"Mm." Aldous turned. He had a way of looking at a person that suggested he had already read their file, their letters, and possibly their diary. "There was a fire in the records annex last month. Small. Quickly extinguished. Since then I've taken an interest in the integrity of our archives." He let the word *integrity* sit in the air like a coin tossed on a bar. "I understand you've been consulting certain ledgers. Payment records. Older materials."

"I certify census completeness," Elias said carefully. "Cross-reference is part of certification."

"Of course, of course." Aldous smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, and it reached none of the way up to his eyes. "Let me speak plainly, since I respect a plain man. There may come a claim against the estate—a frivolous thing, the fantasy of a dead diver's daughter. When it fails, it will fail cleanly, because the record will show there was never anything to find. Do you see? History is a document, Counsel. Documents have custodians." He set his glass down. "Your certification is due within the fortnight. I trust it will reflect the thoroughness of your work. Nothing omitted. Nothing... added."

The threat was wrapped so neatly Elias almost admired it. *Certify it away, or become an irregularity yourself.*

"I complete my certifications accurately," Elias said. "Always."

"I'm glad to hear it." Aldous returned to his window. "Good evening, Counsel. Mind the fog on your way home. It's been treacherous lately. Men have been known to lose their way entirely."

Outside, the fog was indeed rolling in off the harbor, thick and white and full of muffled sounds. Elias walked into it gladly. Fog, he reflected, was excellent cover—for a man whose pockets contained, folded very small, copies of four pages of payment ledgers that officially no longer existed.

* * *

The network scattered like beads off a snapped string.

Dara stopped sleeping at the workshop and moved to her aunt's, two streets over, and wouldn't say who had frightened her. Old Hollen took extra shifts on the beds and answered questions with questions. Even Bess grew guarded, her warmth banked down to coals, though she still found ways to press food on Paloma—biscuits wrapped in cloth, hard-boiled eggs appearing in her coat pocket like small miracles—with the fierce tenderness of a woman who could not fix the world but could damn well feed it.

"He's picked us apart," Dara whispered to Paloma on the fifth evening, in the shadow of the cooperage. "Teo gone, the papers gone, everyone jumping at shadows. What's left?"

Paloma considered the question honestly, because Dara deserved honesty.

"What's left," she said, "is what he can't pick up and carry away. Bess's memory. The ledgers in Elias's head. Forty witnesses who watched his enforcers break a boy's fingers over a *key impression*, Dara—he's not hiding his cruelty anymore, and men who stop hiding it start making enemies. Every gray coat he sends out buys him fear and spends him loyalty." She managed half a smile. "Also, what's left is nine days and a magistrate with a reputation for disliking rich men before breakfast."

Dara sniffed a laugh. "Nobody dislikes rich men before breakfast. It's too early to be ambitious."

"That's why we're going at ten."

* * *

On the sixth day, Teo came back.

Paloma found him at low tide, sitting on the breakwater at the far end of the harbor where nobody went except gulls and people avoiding other people. His splinted hand rested in his lap like something borrowed. He didn't look up when she sat beside him, but his shoulders, which had been up around his ears, came down an inch.

"I wrote you a coward's letter," he said.

"You wrote me an honest one. Rarer thing."

"Don't." His voice cracked. "Don't be kind, Paloma, I can't afford it. I gave them everything for the price of two fingers. Two. That's what we were worth. All your plans, all our oaths—two fingers' worth."

Paloma watched the water slap the stones below. She thought about what she wanted to say, and discarded several versions—the comforting lie, the rousing speech, the easy absolution—and settled on the true one.

"Do you know what I felt when I saw the empty hiding place?" she said. "Not anger at you. Dread, yes. But under the dread—" She turned her palm up, remembering. "Relief. Horrible relief. Because it wasn't me they found, and it wasn't Bess, and it wasn't the divers. It was paper. Paper, Teo, and I have learned something about paper in the last month: it only matters because of what people carry in their heads and hearts. Aldous stole our proof, but he had to torture a boy to find it. Think about what that says about how scared he is. Think about what it says about how little he understands us. He thinks we were guarding a document." She looked at Teo then, at his bruised face and splinted hand. "We were guarding each other. And that part he cannot steal, because you can't break into a person and take it out. You tried it on yourself already. It didn't take."

Teo stared at the horizon for a long moment. Then, in a voice like gravel underwater: "I want to help. The real way, this time. Not copying keys." His jaw set. "Aldous's coachman owes my sister money and my sister owes him patience. There's talk in that house. Where and when Aldous moves things. I could hear it."

"No," Paloma began—and stopped. Because refusing to let people help had gotten her exactly here: alone at the racks, holding a brass button, watching her network scatter. She had spent five years protecting herself by being invisible, and the habit was killing her allies softly, one excluded soul at a time. "No spying," she said instead. "If his people so much as glance at you twice, you walk away and you tell me and that's the whole of your duty. Swear it."

"Sworn," Teo said, and for the first time in six days, he whistled.

It was terrible, as ever. Paloma found she didn't mind at all.

* * *

That night, a note came to her via Bess, delivered inside a loaf of bread because apparently that was what her life had become: intrigue by bakery. The hand was Elias's, precise even in haste.

*Moved up. Certification demanded in three days, not nine. Workshop closure order drafted—dated for the day BEFORE the magistrate's sitting. He means to dissolve the claims legally before you can speak them, and burn the annex after. I can delay the form one day, no more. One day, Paloma. Make it count.—E.C.*

Paloma read it twice by candlelight while the loaf, betrayed and steaming, cooled beside her. One day. Their careful nine-day arithmetic, shredded. Aldous wasn't just moving pieces; he was flipping the board.

Bess appeared in the doorway, arms crossed, reading Paloma's face the way she read weather.

"How much time?" the old woman asked.

"One day less than we needed."

"Hmph." Bess came in and sat, uninvited, which was her privilege and her nature. "When your mother was your age, the beds flooded out overnight and the company boats were all leased elsewhere, and Sera swam the whole length of the shallows in the dark to raise the alarm before anyone drowned. Everyone said afterward, *how did she know to go that night?*" Bess's eyes glinted. "She didn't know. She went out every night it looked wrong. That's the family secret, girl. You don't wait to be sure. You go out when it looks wrong."

Paloma looked at the note. At the candle. At the woman who had hidden her mother's papers through thirty years of quiet.

"It looks wrong," she said.

"It looks wrong," Bess agreed, and pushed the bread toward her. "Eat first. You'll want your strength. Tomorrow we go see a magistrate's clerk about moving a hearing—and you're going to need to decide, before sunrise, whether you trust that census man with the last card we're holding."

Outside, far across the black harbor, the lights of the Meridian townhouse burned steady and patient, like eyes that had already counted every move she had left.

Paloma tore the bread in half.

One day.
