## 15. Ash and Ledger

Elias Corvane had always believed that fire was the most honest of the elements. Water lied about depth. Wind lied about direction. But fire told you exactly what it wanted, which was everything.

He was three streets from the company house when the bells began.

At first he thought it might be a chimney, some clerk's careless brazier, the ordinary small catastrophes of a port city in winter. Then he rounded the corner by the cooper's yard and saw the glow above the rooftops—not chimney-orange but annex-orange, the particular color of government paper burning, which even at a distance looked somehow official.

The records annex stood behind the main company house, a long low building of stone and slate that everyone in Saltmere referred to as "the fireproof one" with the comfortable certainty of people who had never watched something burn.

Elias stood in the crowd at the cordon and did the arithmetic he was trained to do. The annex held: shipping manifests for the last forty years, tax assessments, harbor fees, and—the third shelf of the eastern racks, wrapped in oilcloth against damp—eleven years of census rolls. Every name, every birth, every parentage recorded in Saltmere since he'd been a junior clerk with ink-stained cuffs and ambitions.

Every entry that mentioned Sera Vane.

He watched the roof timbers catch and thought, with a clarity that frightened him more than the flames did: *Aldous isn't destroying evidence. He's destroying the possibility of evidence.*

And then, because his mind was an orderly place and order demanded completion: *Documents first. Witnesses second.*

* * *

By dawn the annex was a black ribcage filled with steam. The company fire brigade—remarkably prompt, someone in the crowd observed, for men who usually took an hour to find their boots—had saved the main house without difficulty. The annex itself was declared a total loss. The harbormaster's clerk said "total loss" the way other men said "unfortunate weather," and Elias understood that the phrase had been decided upon before the fire was out.

He stood in the wreckage at first light, wearing his census office coat and his most bureaucratic expression, which had the advantage of making people explain things to him slowly and completely.

"The eastern racks are gone," said the fire warden, a broad woman named Ketch with eyebrows singed to stubble. "Slate fell on that side early. Hot burn. Paper doesn't leave much."

"And the western?"

"Smoke and water, mostly." She shrugged. "You want to poke through? Company says salvage what you need for the reassessment. They're being very cooperative."

"I'm sure they are," Elias said.

The western racks were charred at the edges but standing. He worked through them for four hours while laborers hauled debris and nobody questioned why a census man cared so much about soggy paper. Most of it was ruined beyond reading. But the census rolls—his own careful columns, his own cramped hand from years past—had been wrapped, and wrapping, as it turned out, was a kind of argument between the archivist who'd done it and the fire, and the archivist had won narrowly.

The Vane entries were legible. Barely.

Elias sat on an overturned bucket with a scrap of tarpaulin across his knees and made a decision that felt less like a decision than like a door finally swinging open after years of leaning against it.

He could not carry the documents out. The company had posted a man at the annex door—a polite man, a helpful man, whose job this morning was to note what salvaged papers left the site. Any roll in Elias's hands would be logged, and any log would find its way to Aldous Meridian's desk by evening.

So he would carry them somewhere no one could seize them.

* * *

His father had taught him the trick when Elias was nine, back in the inland town where clerks were respected and nothing ever burned. "Paper is for other people," his father had said, tapping his own temple. "Memory is for you. A man can confiscate your satchel. No magistrate alive has jurisdiction over what's in here." His father had been a fussy, honest, largely unsuccessful man, and Elias had spent thirty years trying not to become him.

He sat now among the wet ash and became him entirely.

He copied the pages into his memory the way he'd once copied them into ledgers: methodically, line by line, checking each entry twice before moving to the next. *Vane, Sera, diver, quarter three, year eleven. Dependent listed: none.* Year twelve: *Dependent listed: daughter, infant.* Year fifteen, the last year of her life: *Payment annotated family obligation, per instruction of A. Meridian senior.*

The annotations were the heart of it. Someone at the company—and the hand was old Augustin's own, Elias recognized the heavy downstrokes from the founding documents—had ordered monthly payments to a diver with no explanation except those two words. Family obligation. Written in the founder's hand, for sixteen years, to a woman the current family insisted had no connection to them.

It wasn't proof of inheritance. It was proof of a lie, which in a courtroom was often the same thing wearing better boots.

By noon Elias could recite every surviving Vane entry forward and backward. By one o'clock he had done something additional, something he made himself do slowly so he couldn't pretend later it was accident: he memorized the certification schedule. The quarterly sitting of the magistrate. Nine days off. And the deadline Aldous had set for the workshop closure—one day before.

One day. As if the timing were coincidence. As if Aldous Meridian believed in coincidence, which no corrupt man ever does; corruption requires a horror of chance.

The polite man at the door noted that Census Officer Corvane departed with no documents whatsoever. This was true. Everything Elias carried, he carried between his ears, in the vault his father had built for him one tedious lesson at a time.

He walked out through the gate and turned, instead of toward his lodgings, toward the harbor.

* * *

Paloma found him rather than the reverse, which he should have expected. He was loitering near the drying racks with the aimless air of a man waiting for someone he had no legitimate reason to meet, and she materialized beside him carrying two baskets of oysters, one of which she thrust at him without ceremony.

"You look like a man who needs to look busy," she said.

"I feel like one."

"Carry the basket. Walk. Don't look at the end of the pier."

They walked. The market noise covered them, gulls and fishwives and a boy announcing mackerel prices as though announcing a war.

"The annex," she said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Fire started in the eastern stacks. Where the old assessment rolls were kept. Also where the census archive lived."

"Convenient."

"Aldous's men were seen near the cooper's yard two nights ago buying lamp oil. Not for lamps, according to the cooper, who was paid to forget it and who told me anyway because I buy my barrels from him and he considers me harmless."

"Do you consider yourself harmless?"

"Frequently. It's one of my few comforts." He shifted the basket. "Paloma. Listen. The fire didn't get everything. The Vane entries survived, and I have them." He tapped his temple, feeling faintly ridiculous and entirely serious. "All of them. The payments, the annotations, your mother's name in the founder's own ledger hand. In here, where no one can burn it."

She stopped walking. Just for a step, just long enough that a fishwife jostled her and cursed cheerfully, and then they moved again, two people discussing shellfish.

"You memorized it," she said.

"My father used to say paper belongs to whoever holds it and memory belongs to whoever earned it."

"That's either very noble or very sad."

"In my father's case it was both. He died owning nothing anyone could confiscate, including a coat." He heard her breath catch—not quite a laugh, but adjacent to one—and found, absurdly, that making her almost-laugh steadied him more than the memorization had. "There's more. The certification order came through yesterday. My superior wants the workshop rolls certified 'complete' by month's end. Complete meaning final. Complete meaning legally, no claim by any Vane can attach to anything, ever, amen."

"Month's end."

"One day before the workshop closes. One day before the magistrate's sitting."

"So he closes the door, then locks it, then burns down the building the key fits." Her voice was flat and steady, the voice he'd begun to recognize as the one she used when she was most afraid. "Then what's left for him to do, Elias?"

He didn't answer. The basket of oysters suddenly weighed a great deal.

"Elias."

"He'll tidy loose ends. That's what fires are for. Documents first." He made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plainness from at least one person in Saltmere. "Witnesses second. Bess. Teo. The divers who helped you. You."

The harbor sounds went on around them, indifferent and bright. Paloma walked six more paces, set her basket down on a bollard, and looked out at the water the way he imagined she looked down at it from a dive: measuring, calculating, refusing panic as a professional discipline.

"All right," she said at last. "Then we stop protecting each other from the truth and start using it. Can you write it down? What's in your head?"

"Not safely. Anything in my handwriting that leaves my hands is evidence—for Aldous. But—" He hesitated, then committed. "I can copy it. Slowly. A little at a time. Delivered where no one logs deliveries."

"The chapel of the Tide-Blessed on Gull Street. There's a loose stone under the alms box. Mother put things there once, before I understood why." A ghost of a smile. "Bess thinks the Tide-Blessed don't exist. I think if they do, they owe me."

"Then starting tomorrow, the Tide-Blessed will receive regular donations of census fraud." He kept his voice dry, but something in his chest had gone warm and unsteady. He understood, standing there among the fishwives with a basket of oysters going heavy in his arms, that he had crossed a line sometime in the last minute or possibly the last five years, and that he did not intend to cross back.

"You understand what you're doing," Paloma said. It wasn't gratitude in her voice—it was warning, which was somehow more moving. "Once you write it down and pass it to me, you're not a census officer who noticed things. You're a conspirator. They'll come for your position, your pension, probably your liberty."

"I've been doing the arithmetic," Elias said. "I'm quite good at arithmetic."

"And?"

"And I've spent my whole career certifying other people's lives as accurate. Names, births, deaths—all of it filed and stamped and made official, whether the truth agreed with it or not." He looked out at the water, gray-green and patient. "This is the first time the paperwork and the truth are on the same side. I find I'd rather like to keep it that way."

Paloma picked up her basket. For a moment her free hand rested on his sleeve—two seconds, perhaps three, brief as a diver's breath—and then she was walking again, briskly, toward the market stalls.

"Gull Street chapel," she said over her shoulder. "Sunset. And Elias? Bring the oysters. I'm not paying for those."

He followed her, carrying stolen pearls in a basket and eleven years of suppressed truth in his skull, and reflected that his father would have been proud, appalled, and deeply confused, in roughly that order.

* * *

The copying took three evenings.

He wrote in the census office after hours, by the light of the single lamp he was permitted, on scraps of blotting paper cut small enough to hide in a boot heel. He wrote in a shorthand of his own devising—which was to say, the abbreviated clerk's script every examiner learned and almost no one outside the trade could read—and he destroyed each draft's mistakes by eating them, which his father had also recommended and which tasted the way bureaucracy deserved to taste.

On the fourth evening, the notes went under the loose stone in the Gull Street chapel, wrapped in oilcloth out of habit and respect for the dead archivist who had fought the fire with wrapping paper and nearly won.

Paloma retrieved them an hour later. He knew because he waited across the street in the doorway of a chandler's shop, feeling like the villain in a morality play, until she emerged with her shawl drawn close and gave him a single nod before vanishing into the warren of lanes toward the workshop.

Two days after that, a note appeared inside his own lunch tin, which he had left, per instructions, on the windowsill of the harbormaster's outer office between the hours of eleven and noon.

*Received. All of it. Bess wept and then laughed and then made me eat something. Teo sends word the guards on the north gate changed rotation—new men, Aldous's men, not company men. Whatever he plans next, it will come through that gate. — P.*

Below that, in a different hand, older and rounder:

*You're a good lad. Mind yourself. — B.*

Elias read the note twice, folded it small, and burned it in his lamp flame, watching the words curl and go. Good lad. Mind yourself. Nobody had said either to him in twenty years of service.

That night he drafted his resignation letter and did not send it. Too soon. A resignation now was a flare; it told Aldous precisely which official had gone crooked. Instead he did something quieter and, he suspected, more damaging: he requested, through proper channels, in proper form, a full audit of the certification schedule for the Saltmere district, citing irregularities in the annex inventory following the fire.

The request would take days to process. It would land on desks. Clerks would read it, and clerks talked, and some of those clerks owed favors to people who disliked the Meridian family for reasons predating all of this. A slow, procedural, magnificently boring stone dropped into a very deep pond.

His father would have approved. Elias allowed himself, for exactly one moment, to imagine the old man's face, and then he blew out the lamp and went to bed.

* * *

The reply from Paloma came sooner than he expected, and it did not come by lunch tin.

It came at dusk, two days later, when he opened his lodging-room door to find her standing in the corridor with a basket of mending she clearly did not do, wearing the expression of a woman who had climbed three flights of stairs on an excuse and intended to spend it honestly.

"There's a hole in the north wall of the workshop yard," she said, without preamble. "Teo found it. Old drain, sealed decades ago, big enough for a slim man with good lungs." She paused. "Or a woman who's been diving since she was seven."

Elias looked at her for a long moment, understanding arriving in pieces. "You're going into the company house."

"The original certification register is in the strongroom there. If Aldous certifies the rolls before the sitting, everything we've saved is worthless—your memory included, because memory needs corroboration and corroborating documents burn beautifully." She lifted her chin. "But if the register disappears—or better, if it's found already in the magistrate's hands, dated and witnessed—"

"You'll be caught."

"I've been slipping through doors I shouldn't for five years. This one I'm choosing." Something softened at the corner of her mouth. "Besides. I brought you the basket."

He looked down. Beneath the mending lay a folded scrap: a map of the yard's north wall in her quick sure hand, the drain marked with an X, and beside it, in smaller letters, a line that made his chest ache in a way he decided to examine later:

*If I don't come back, recite it all at the sitting anyway. Every word. Promise me the paper outlives us both.*

"Paloma." He said it more gently than he'd said anything in years. "Come in. Sit down. Because if you're going to break into the Meridian strongroom, you're not doing it alone, and you're certainly not doing it before we've had tea and I've finished being terrified at you."

She laughed—actually laughed, short and surprised, as if the sound had escaped custody—and stepped past him into the room.

Outside, far off toward the company quarter, the north gate changed guard for the night, and the sea kept its counsel, holding whatever it held, patient as paper.
