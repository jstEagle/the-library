## 16. Witnesses

Bess arrived at the drying racks before dawn, the way she always did, as if the workshop were a patient she'd nursed for forty years and could not quite abandon even now. Paloma was already there, sitting on an overturned crate with a slate on her knees and a stub of chalk that had been worn down to something resembling a fingernail.

"You look," Bess said, "like a woman about to do sums."

"I am doing sums." Paloma tapped the slate. "Eight days until the gates close. Nine until the magistrate sits."

"And you've written *eight* and crossed it out twice already."

"Because every time I cross it out, it gets smaller and I haven't gotten bigger yet."

Bess lowered herself onto the neighboring crate with the groan of a woman whose joints had opinions about everything. Up close, in the grey light, her face looked like driftwood—weathered, grain-showing, but solid where it mattered. She had been the workshop matron since before Paloma was born. She had held Sera Vane's hand through storms and fevers and, in the end, through the thing that took her.

"I've been thinking," Bess said, "about what you'll need from me."

Paloma set down the chalk. "You don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." Bess folded her hands, and Paloma saw that they trembled slightly, and saw also that Bess was not going to let them stop her mouth. "I hid your mother's papers under the floorboards of the mending room for eleven years while Aldous's father sent men sniffing around like dogs after a rabbit. I'm too old to start being brave now. So I'll be brave early, while I still remember why."

Paloma said nothing. Some things you didn't thank out loud, because thanking made them smaller.

"Sera came to me in the spring of the year you were weaned," Bess began. "She had the letter half-burned by then—the one naming old Augustin's daughter. She told me her grandmother's name, which was Odile, and that Odile Meridian had been disowned for loving a diver, and that the family had let her drown in poverty while they counted pearls in the hills. She said—" Bess paused, and her eyes went out toward the water, where the morning tide was turning. "She said, *'Bess, if anything happens to me, my girl must know she comes from money she never got to spend.'* Those words exactly. I've kept them like coins in a pocket."

"Can you say all that before the magistrate?" Paloma asked. "Word for word?"

"I can say it word for word, child, I can say it backwards, and I can say it louder than any man in that room. Forty years of shouting over gulls gives a woman lungs."

For the first time in days, Paloma laughed—a short, surprised sound, like a wave slapping stone.

"There," Bess said, satisfied. "Now write me down as a witness before I get modest."

* * *

Elias Corvane arrived at midmorning looking like a man who had slept in his coat, which, Paloma suspected, he had.

He carried no papers. That was the first thing she noticed, and the first thing he wanted her to notice: no ledger, no satchel, nothing that could be confiscated at the workshop gate or found in a search of his rooms. He sat down across from her in the shadow of the net-lofts and spread his empty hands on the table between them.

"It's all up here," he said, tapping his temple. "Every entry. Every date."

"All of it?"

"The payments to Sera Vane ran from the month after Augustin's death to two months before hers. Thirty-one years of monthly disbursements, annotated *family obligation* in the founder's own hand. The annotations stop abruptly in the same quarter Aldous's father took control of the company accounts—which, incidentally, is the same quarter the original will disappears from the registry." He recited it flatly, the way he recited everything, but his eyes had a light in them that flatness couldn't quite smother. "I've been walking the seawall since four in the morning, repeating it. A fisherman asked me if I was well."

"You memorized thirty-one years of ledgers."

"I have a talent for numbers. It's the only reason the company hired me, and possibly the only reason they'll regret it." He pulled a chair closer. "The problem isn't whether I can reproduce the figures. The problem is standing. A former census taker testifying against the company that employed him—I'll be dismissed as disgruntled before I finish my first sentence. Unless."

"Unless?"

"Unless the figures come from more than one mouth. Ledgers can be forged; memories can't be coordinated if there are enough of them. How many divers will speak?"

Paloma consulted her slate. "Seven so far. Teo's brother Renni. The Ferro sisters. Old Marta, who says she's too old to frighten, which is either true or the bravest lie I've ever heard."

"Teo himself?"

The question landed softly, but it landed. Paloma turned the slate over in her hands.

"Not yet," she said. "They're keeping him in the infirmary wing. His hands are bandaged. When I sit with him he looks at the wall." She hesitated. "Elias, what I did to him—"

"What Aldous's men did to him."

"For my cause. Because I asked him to copy a key." She had said this to herself every night since, in the dark of the barracks, and every night the sentence had refused to rearrange itself into something bearable. "If I build this case on top of what happened to him and he never stands up to point at it—"

"Then you build it anyway," Elias said, not unkindly, "and you leave room for him to stand when he's able. You can't carry him, Paloma. You can only make sure that when he can walk, there's somewhere worth walking to."

She looked at him for a long moment. There it was again, the thing she kept forgetting about him and kept rediscovering: underneath the bureaucratic stiffness, a spine made of something quieter and stronger than duty.

"Room worth walking to," she repeated. "Write that down."

"On what? You've taken my slate."

* * *

The divers came to the mending room in ones and twos, as casually as they could manage, which was not very. Renni brought a coil of rope to re-hang, which fooled no one, since the rope had not needed re-hanging since the last hurricane. The Ferro sisters came ostensibly to argue about a net. Old Marta came openly, because Old Marta had decided some years ago that pretending was for people with more future than she had.

Paloma stood at the head of the long table where sails were usually patched, and felt the weight of seven faces, and understood suddenly why magistrates sat behind big desks.

"Aldous's men started coming to the beds three months ago," Renni said, when it was his turn. "Before the census, before any of this. Two men in company coats, taking names. Asking who talked to whom. Dario saw them searching the records annex at night—he thought it was clerks working late until he noticed the lanterns moving room to room in a workshop that keeps no night hours."

"Dario will testify?"

"Dario will testify from a boat in the harbor if he has to. He says he's done diving for men who fear them; he won't dive for men who fear them silently." Renni shrugged, a big slow shrug. "There's a difference, he says, between being afraid and being quiet about it. One's the sea's business. The other's yours."

The Ferro sisters spoke together, as they did everything—the elder supplying dates, the younger supplying grievances, occasionally correcting each other mid-sentence with the ease of women who had shared a bunk and a boat and every breath of air above it for fifty years. The enforcers had broken Tolla Ferro's fingers over a disputed catch-weight two winters back and called it an accounting error. There had been no one to appeal to then. There would be someone now.

Old Marta saved her account for last, and delivered it standing, because she said sitting witnesses looked guilty.

"I dove beside Sera Vane for nineteen years," she said. "I held the line for her the day your grandmother's grave marker went up without a name on it, because the family wouldn't pay for letters. Nineteen years, and I watched that woman hold her breath longer and come up smiling more than anyone has a right to. And I watched the company dock her wages the week she buried her mother, and call it *attendance adjustment*." Marta's chin lifted. "I'm seventy-one years old. Aldous Meridian can take my contract, my bunk, and my oyster knife. He cannot take nineteen years off the bottom of the sea. I'll tell that magistrate everything, and I'll enjoy it more than is strictly dignified."

Nobody laughed, quite—but everybody breathed easier, which amounted to the same thing.

When they had gone, Paloma copied the last notes onto her slate, and then, because slates get wiped and wiped and wiped again, she did something she hadn't dared do until now: she wrote it all out on paper. Real paper, bought from Bess's private hoard, folded small and sewn into the lining of her coat. Names. Dates. The shape of a case, stitched against her ribs.

It was the first document she had ever owned that nobody else knew existed. It felt less like evidence and more like a heartbeat.

* * *

That evening Elias returned with news that was half good and half terrible, in roughly equal measure, which seemed lately to be the only recipe the world offered.

"The magistrate's quarterly sitting is confirmed," he said. "Public gallery, sworn testimony, the whole apparatus. And—" he paused, choosing his words the way he chose them when the choosing mattered, "—Aldous has filed a petition to have the sitting moved up."

"Moved up? To when?"

"That's just it. The petition doesn't specify. Which means he can request it any day, and the clerk—who dines at Meridian's table twice a month, I checked—can grant it any day." He sat down heavily. "We planned around nine days. We may have nine hours. The closing of the workshop, the dissolution of the contracts, the erasure of every Vane entry in the rolls—it could all happen legally, tidily, before you ever open your mouth in that courtroom. And then your testimony becomes the grievance of a dismissed indentured worker instead of the claim of a recognized heir. Procedurally speaking, it's elegant. I'd admire it if it weren't aimed at us."

Paloma stood very still. Outside, the tide was going out, pulling the light with it, and somewhere beyond the breakwater the sea was doing what the sea always did: keeping things, and giving them back on its own schedule, not anyone else's.

"Then we don't wait to be ready," she said slowly. "We're ready now, or near enough. Seven divers, Bess, your ledgers in your skull, my papers in my coat. What we lack is the will itself—and the proof the strongbox seal was never broken." Her mind was moving fast now, the way it moved underwater, where panic was useless and only counting survived. "Which means our real problem isn't the nine days. It's that Aldous knows we have the will, and Teo—"

She stopped.

Elias looked at her. "Teo knows where it was hidden."

"He knew. Before they broke his hands. Before—" She was already reaching for her coat, already feeling for the stitched paper against her ribs, though there was no reason to check it, no reason at all, except that her body had understood the danger several seconds before her mind caught up. "Elias. If they got anything out of him—if he gave up the hiding place beneath the racks—then the will is gone, and everything we built today is testimony without its heart."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"Tomorrow," Paloma said quietly, "I go to the drying racks first. Before anyone. Before dawn."

And far across the darkened compound, beneath racks that should have held a folded will and did not, a lantern was already burning.
