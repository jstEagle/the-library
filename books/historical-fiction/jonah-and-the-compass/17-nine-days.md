## 17. Nine Days

On the first morning of the last nine days of her life as the company knew her, Paloma Vane woke before the gulls and lay still in the dark barracks, counting.

Not sheep. Not breaths. Days.

Nine days until the magistrate's quarterly sitting. Ten days until the workshop closed its gates forever—assuming Aldous Meridian kept to his own schedule, which he would, because men like Aldous believed schedules were a kind of weapon. She had watched them work for five years. A ledger could cut deeper than a knife if you held it right.

Eight days, then, to gather witnesses who were frightened. Seven to finish her indenture's final obligations without giving anyone a reason to tear up her contract early and throw her out before she was legally ready to walk. Six days of being exactly what she had always been: unremarkable Paloma Vane, diver, second bed from the window, no family of note.

She dressed in the dark and went down to the water.

* * *

The beds off Gullspit Shoal had reopened after the storm, though the sea was still sorting through its temper. The water ran green-brown with churned silt, visibility poor even by Saltmere standards, and Overseer Craddock stood on the platform with his counting book under his arm like a man holding court over an empire of mud.

"Vane," he said, as she came up the ladder. "You're first again."

"I'm always first."

"You're always first," he agreed, and made his mark. He was not a cruel man, only a narrow one—a man who had spent thirty years counting oysters and had come, somewhere along the way, to believe that anything uncountable didn't exist. Some mornings Paloma envied him.

She checked her weights, spat in her mask, rinsed it. Below the platform, three of her divers waited in the water—Sefa and Marisol on either side of Teo's cousin Renna, who was sixteen and still dove like she was apologizing to the sea for intruding. They were all of them careful this morning. Careful in the way of people who knew they were being watched, which they were, because two of the gate guards had been replaced overnight by men nobody recognized, men with soft hands and hard eyes who did not laugh at the dock boys' jokes.

Aldous's men. Everyone knew whose they were. Nobody said so.

"Two baskets minimum," Craddock said. "Storm scattered the beds. Company wants the shoals stripped before month's end." Before we close it down and sell the lease, he did not say. He probably didn't know. That was the thing about machinery—it worked best when the cogs didn't ask where the wheels were going.

Paloma went over the side.

The cold closed over her like a hand, familiar and impersonal. Down here there was no census, no ledger, no bloodline—only the weight of the water and the shape of the bottom under her palms, oyster by oyster, her fingers reading the reef the way Bess read letters: quickly, surely, by long practice. Left hand finds shell, tests the gape, twists. Into the basket. Right hand already moving. She had once thought she would do this forever, that this was simply what her hands were for. Her mother had thought so too, presumably, right up until her mother turned out to be something else entirely.

Halfway through her second dive, working the deep edge where the storm had tumbled the beds into new arrangements, Renna appeared beside her, tapping her own wrist twice—their signal. *Something wrong.*

Paloma followed her gaze. Out past the marker buoy, a boat sat idle. Not a company boat. A hired skiff with a canopy, the kind wealthy men used for fishing they never actually did, and a man in it who was not fishing. He was watching the platforms. Watching, specifically, whenever a diver surfaced near the ladder where Paloma tied off her basket.

He had been there yesterday too. She'd told herself it was coincidence.

She finished her basket. She surfaced slowly, unhurried, and handed it up with steady hands, and Craddock counted her oysters while the man in the skiff counted *her*, and she kept her face arranged into the mild blankness of a tired woman thinking about lunch. Endurance, she had learned, was mostly performance. You endured by appearing to be someone who could not possibly need to.

At midday she sat on the seawall with Sefa, sharing bread and sardines out of the same tin.

"He'll report to the house tonight," Sefa said quietly, around a mouthful. "That kind always does."

"I know."

"The sitting is nine days off."

"Eight days, nineteen hours," Paloma said. "But who's counting."

Sefa snorted, which turned into a cough, which turned into laughing, and Paloma found herself laughing too, helplessly, the way you laugh when the alternative is screaming—and felt, absurdly, better. This was the other thing she had learned. Fear shared among divers weighed less. Everything weighed less underwater anyway; that was the whole principle of the trade.

* * *

That evening, Bess summoned her to the mending room with a look that meant *business*, and found it already occupied by Elias Corvane, sitting on a barrel of tarred twine with his census case on his knees like a man attending his own trial.

"You got my note," Paloma said.

"I memorized it and burned it," Elias said. "Which I'm learning is standard practice around here. Do you know, in Corvane we filed things? We had *files*. It was very restful."

Bess shut the door and wedged a chair under the handle—not because anyone would barge in, but because rituals mattered. Then she lit the small lamp and turned it low, and the three of them leaned together over the mending table like conspirators in a bad play, which Paloma supposed was roughly what they were.

"The guards change again tomorrow," Bess said. "Night watch. The two on the gate now, the soft-handed ones—they're paid through the closing. But the night men are locals. Underpaid, overworked, and known to me since they were boys stealing apples off my sill."

"How much?" Paloma asked.

"Forgetfulness is cheaper than courage," Bess said. "Forgetfulness costs four silvers a head per night. Courage, if we needed it, would cost more than we have." She counted coins onto the table, and Paloma watched the sum go and thought about ledgers, and how her whole life had become one—entries and debits, everything priced, everyone bought or buyable, and the only question left was whether she could afford eight more days of it.

"And the near thing?" Elias asked. "You said there'd been a near thing."

Paloma told them about the skiff, the man with the watching habit. "If he's identified me, why haven't I been taken?"

"Because taking you proves you," Elias said slowly. He had the particular frowning intensity he got when the paperwork in his head didn't balance. "Right now you're a diver with a story. If Aldous seizes you, he confirms the bloodline exists, publicly, days before a magistrate's sitting. He can't risk it. What he wants is for you to run—or to find whatever proof you have and destroy it for him." He looked up. "He doesn't know how little proof you have left. He thinks the will is still hidden."

"Thanks to Teo," Paloma said, and the name came out flat and heavy. Teo, who had copied a key impression for her and been caught, and beaten for it, and then—broken—told Aldous's men where the strongbox had been hidden. That the box had already been moved was the only mercy in it. Teo hadn't known that. He'd given away an empty hiding place and hated himself for it anyway; she'd seen the hate in his face when she'd brought him broth two nights ago and he wouldn't meet her eyes.

"Guilt isn't a debt you pay to yourself," Bess said, reading her expression. "It's a debt you pay forward. Sit down, girl, and let's work."

They worked. Elias recited from memory—pages of it, the payment records, the annotations in old Augustin's hand, *family obligation*, month upon month, and the clause of the true will recited word-perfect in his dry clerk's voice, which he pretended was nothing and which made Bess blink with something like respect. Paloma committed names to paper in a cipher of her mother's devising, then fed each page to the lamp. Witnesses: Bess, Sefa, Marisol, the old cook Hesta who remembered Sera Vane diving pearls at thirteen with Augustin's own compass on a cord round her neck. Evidence: the seal from the strongbox, unbroken, dated eleven years before the "original" will's signature. Testimony: everything.

"It comes down to the seal," Elias said. "Everything else he can smear. A broken seal dated and witnessed—that's harder."

"Then we keep it in three places," Bess said, "and tell no one but ourselves which three." And that was settled, and the chair came out from under the door, and they dispersed into the ordinary night like ordinary people, which was its own kind of armor.

* * *

Day six, the sabotage began—or rather, Paloma discovered it had begun without her, which frightened her more.

Marisol came to her at dawn, gray-faced. Someone had slashed two of the cooperative's petition signatures out of the divers' register—the list of names Bess had been quietly gathering, divers willing to testify about Aldous's enforcers. Slashed cleanly, with a blade, and left in place so no one would notice until it mattered.

"They've been in the records room," Marisol whispered. "Again."

Paloma stood very still. Then she went and found Bess, and Bess went and found Hesta, and between them they reconstructed the register from memory—every signature, every mark, the illiterate divers' crosses witnessed and dated. It took most of the day. Paloma wrote until her hand cramped, copying out names that were, each one of them, a person agreeing to be brave in public, and she understood with a clarity that hurt that this was what the fortune actually was. Not the pearls. Not the house on the hill. This: a list of people willing to stand up.

She hid the copy inside the false bottom of her mother's sea chest, where her mother had hidden other things once, and she allowed herself one minute—exactly one, timed against her pulse—to press her palm flat against the wood and miss her.

* * *

Day four, the near-miss.

She should have seen it coming. In retrospect it was obvious: the magistrate's clerks made their rounds of the district rolls in the week before every sitting, verifying residency, and Aldous needed only to whisper a word in the right ear to have a warrant drawn on any pretext at all—unpaid tolls, falsified census answers, *irregular bloodlines*. The pretext didn't matter. Pretexts never did.

She was at the harbor market buying oil when she saw the two constables come through the fish gate, walking with purpose, and saw their purpose was the row of stalls where she stood. There was nowhere to go. The market backed onto the water, and the water was the one direction open to her that wasn't.

So she did the thing her body knew how to do better than anything else on earth. She set down her oil jug, walked to the quay steps as if stretching after a long dive, and slipped into the harbor with barely a splash.

The cold hit like a slap. She dove deep, swam the length of two hulls underwater, surfaced in the shadow of a moored barge where a fisherman she'd bought eels from for five years glanced down at her, sighed, and ostentatiously returned to gutting his catch, providing cover with the magnificent indifference of a man who had lived in Saltmere his whole life. She hung there in the green dark under the pier, treading water, listening to her own heartbeat slow, while above her boots passed and paused and passed on.

When she finally climbed out at the far stairs, wringing salt from her hair, Elias was waiting. He looked terrible—pale, jaw tight, hat clutched in both hands.

"That was me," he said. "I heard the warrant being drawn. I couldn't stop it, so I ran here."

"You ran."

"I *ran*," he confirmed, with the wounded dignity of a man who had recently discovered he possessed legs and resented it. "I am a census official. My last sustained physical exertion was a vigorous bout of filing."

Paloma laughed—a wild, gasping, half-drowned laugh—and then, because the day seemed to demand honesty, she crossed the wet stones and hugged him. He froze, then cautiously returned the embrace, patting her shoulder twice as though defusing it.

"The warrant will fail," he said into her hair. "No magistrate signs off on 'irregular bloodlines' in writing anymore—it's not lawful, only customary, and Clerk Fenwick is a coward about custom. By tomorrow it'll be withdrawn. But Paloma—" He pulled back, and his face was serious. "They're out of patience. Whatever he does next, it won't be paperwork."

"Then we're out of time," she said. "Which means it's time to stop hiding and start standing."

* * *

Day two, the sea gave her her ending—or the end of her beginning, which amounted to the same arithmetic.

Her final required dive. The last obligation on a contract signed by a frightened girl of seventeen with her dead mother's name and nothing else. Craddock read the terms aloud on the platform, because the company loved ceremony when ceremony cost nothing: *full completion of quota, beds of record, season of record.* Two baskets. Same as ever. The last pearls she would ever owe them.

The morning was glass-calm, the kind divers called *lying-water*, and Paloma went down into it feeling strangely light. Nine days of fear behind her, compressed now into this single clean task. She worked the shoals methodically, left hand finding, testing, twisting, right hand following, and somewhere in the middle of the second basket her fingers closed on a shell heavier than the rest, and she knew before she opened it, the way you know a knock at your own door.

Inside, in the nacre's dull morning light: a cluster of seven pearls, sea-touched, slightly uneven, worth more than a year of her wages and worth almost nothing at all next to what the day actually meant.

She surfaced. She handed up the basket. Craddock counted, made his mark, and then—because even narrow men have moments—he looked at her over the counting book.

"Five years," he said. "Never short, never late, never a complaint. Company could have done worse than you, Vane."

"Yes," Paloma said. "It could have done a great deal worse than any of us. That's rather the point."

He frowned, not understanding, and made another mark, and that was that. Her contract complete. Five years of silence, bought and finished, and she owed the Meridian Trading Company nothing—nothing except, in eight days' time, everything.

That night she took the seven pearls from her basket and strung them on a cord, and hung them beside the compass in her mother's sea chest, and slept, for the first time in weeks, without counting anything at all.

In the morning there were eight days left, and a knock at the barracks door—early, urgent, Bess's fist—and Bess's voice through the wood, pitched low and fast:

"Girl, get dressed. Teo's gone missing, and there's smoke coming out of the records annex."
