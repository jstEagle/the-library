## 18. The Closing of the Workshop

The notices went up before dawn, pasted to the workshop gates with a paste that would hold through rain, which told Paloma everything about how long Aldous Meridian expected them to stay there.

*By order of the Meridian Trading Company*, they read, *the Saltmere Diving Workshop is closed effective this morning. All indentured persons are discharged from service effective immediately. All company property must be surrendered at the gate. This action is taken pursuant to restructuring.*

"Restructuring," Teo read over her shoulder, his arm still bound in the sling Bess had made him from an old sailcloth. His voice had not been right since the punishment room—flatter, like a shell with the animal gone out of it—but he managed something close to a laugh. "They restructured my ribs. I didn't care for it."

Paloma did not laugh. She had thought she might, because Teo's jokes had always been the hinge on which bad mornings swung open into bearable ones, but the word on the notice sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Eight days. That was all she had asked the calendar for—eight days between the closing of the workshop and the magistrate's quarterly sitting—and Aldous had given her seven. Whether he knew about the sitting and had chosen his date deliberately, or whether it was only the ordinary cruelty of efficiency, she could not tell. It hardly mattered. A knife did not become kinder for having been sharpened by accident.

Around her, the barracks were waking to the news in stages. First the readers, who carried the notice's words to the rest the way gulls carry scraps, dropping pieces everywhere and starting fights over them. Then the sound—the particular sound of forty divers learning at once that the thing they had counted down to, dreamed about, cursed, and feared was happening today, ahead of schedule, without their consent.

Somebody began weeping in the women's quarters. Somebody else began swearing with real artistry, the kind you only hear from people who have been cheated out of a number they'd been keeping in their head for years. Old Maren, who had eleven months left on her own indenture and had spoken of nothing else since spring, could be heard demanding to know what happened to eleven months, where did they go, could they be fished up again like dropped pearls?

"No," said Bess, passing through the corridor with an armful of linens she was, in defiance of all orders, folding. "They cannot be fished up. They've been spent by men who never earned them. Now get your things, love, and don't give them the satisfaction of watching you shake."

* * *

By midmorning the courtyard looked the way a stage looks after the final performance, when the audience has gone and someone must strike the set.

Aldous had sent enforcers instead of coming himself, which surprised no one. There were six of them, big men in company grey who stood at the opened gates and received the surrender of property with the enthusiasm of customs clerks: diving knives, weights, the good brass gauges, even—over Maren's furious objection—a shawl that everyone could see was not company property until one of the enforcers decided it probably was.

"You'll each receive discharge papers," the senior man announced, in the tone of a man announcing weather to fish. "Signed at the gate. You'll leave Saltmere's harbor quarter within three days, per the vagrancy statutes."

"Three days," muttered Teo beside Paloma. "Generous. Last time they cleared a workshop they gave them two and a dog."

"There wasn't a dog," said Paloma.

"That's what makes it funny."

But most of the divers were not making jokes. They filed through the gate in ones and twos, blinking in the flat white light off the harbor, holding their papers like the papers might explain themselves if stared at long enough. Paloma stood near the drying racks—her racks, or as near to hers as five years of indenture had ever allowed anything to be—and watched them go, and made herself look at every single face.

This was the arithmetic she had refused to do until now, and so she did it now, standing in the smell of salt and old rope: forty-one names. Forty-one people whose livelihoods Aldous Meridian had just folded shut like a ledger he was tired of reading. She had known, in the abstract way one knows a storm is coming, that closing the workshop would cost these people something regardless of how the magistrate's sitting ended. Knowing it in the abstract and counting it face by face were different economies entirely.

Juno passed her, carrying everything she owned in a net bag. The old diver paused, and for a moment her hand came up—hesitated—then pressed briefly against Paloma's shoulder, the way you'd steady yourself against a post.

"Bed four has the best shelf," Juno said, which meant nothing, which meant everything, and was gone through the gate.

Paloma watched her go and understood: they were not scattering like leaves. They were going to ground. Juno's sister kept a room behind the ropewalk; Maren had cousins in the Fisher's Rows; Teo had a hundred hiding places he'd never told anyone about, because a boy who copies keys learns early which doors to know. The network she had spent weeks weaving, carefully, guiltily, one oath at a time, was dissolving itself into the city exactly as a net dissolves into water—invisible, but still there, and still holding whatever shape it had been given.

Eight days. Seven, now.

She turned back toward the workshop for the last time.

* * *

Bess found her in the matron's office, which was not really an office but a closet with ambitions, and was putting the last of it to rights—or pretending to, since she was mostly just touching things. The ledgers were already confiscated. The keys hung empty on their board like shed skins.

"They'll want you out by noon," Bess said. "I told them the matron's quarters needed a final inventory. That buys us the hour."

"Us?"

Bess gave her the look—the one that had been straightening young divers' spines and shortening their swagger for thirty years. "You think I'm staying? That they'd keep a woman my age on after they've burned the records? Child, my discharge paper is already signed. I watched the man sign it. He had ink on his thumb like a schoolboy." She turned a cup on the shelf a quarter-inch, correcting nothing. "I'm going to my sister's in the Rows. She's been asking me to visit for six years. I intend to be an enormous burden."

The gentleness of it nearly undid her. Paloma had prepared for anger, for grief, even for fear—she had packed all of those in advance, the way you pack for rough weather—but she had not prepared for Bess making jokes about becoming a burden, and she had to stand very still for a moment and pretend to examine a crack in the plaster.

"I keep thinking," she said, when her voice was safe again, "that I should feel more. About the building, I mean. Five years. And all I can think is that it's just walls, and the walls were never kind to anybody anyway, and the beds—" She stopped. Started again. "The beds are the workshop. Not this."

"The beds," Bess agreed, "and the people who worked them. Both of which," she added, with terrible mildness, "are still available to you."

Paloma laughed despite herself, a wet, ungraceful sound. "You've been talking to Elias."

"I've been talking to everyone. It's the privilege of age. People assume I'm harmless and tell me things." Bess crossed the little room, and then, without any ceremony at all, took Paloma's hand and turned it palm-up, the way you'd check a diver's grip, and laid something in it.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, small and stiff. Paloma's fingers knew the shape before her mind caught up—the torn edge, the scorch line across the corner, the particular weight of paper that has survived fire by being only half consumed. Her breath went somewhere below her ribs and stayed there.

"Bess—"

"The annex fire," Bess said, conversationally, as though discussing tide tables. "Very convenient fire. Very thorough. But thoroughness requires knowing where everything is, and Aldous Meridian's men did not know that the matron of the workshop keeps certain papers in the lining of her winter coat, because it never once occurred to them that a coat might matter." She folded Paloma's fingers closed over the packet, firmly, as though closing a fist around a pearl too valuable to display. "Your mother's letter. The half that survived. I lifted it from the wreckage two nights ago while two very serious men in grey were looking very seriously at ash."

"You could have been—"

"I could have been many things. I chose to be quick." Bess held her eyes, and beneath the dryness there was something older and rawer, something Paloma had seen only once before, the night Bess first confessed to hiding Sera's papers all those years ago. "Listen to me now, girl, because I'll only say this once and then I'm going to go be an enormous burden to my sister. Your mother wrote that letter with her whole heart, and men tried to burn her heart out of the world, and they missed. Do you understand? They missed. It's in your hand. Whatever you do with it—whatever happens at that magistrate's sitting—see that they miss *thoroughly*."

Paloma's throat worked. "I will."

"Yes," said Bess. "I know. But I've waited thirty years to say it properly, so let me have it." Then she patted Paloma's cheek twice, briskly, the way she'd done when Paloma was seventeen and new and terrified, and stepped back, and was once more entirely a practical woman surrounded by boxes. "Noon," she said. "Don't be late leaving. Nothing irritates powerful men so much as being unable to hurry anyone."

* * *

At half past eleven, the courtyard was empty except for the enforcers, the senior of whom was consulting a list with the air of a man who intended to find somebody missing whether or not anybody was.

Paloma walked the workshop one last time, because she wanted to and because no one had forbidden it yet and because she had learned that most freedoms exist for exactly as long as nobody thinks to revoke them.

She walked the long dormitory with its iron cots stripped bare, the frames casting their grid of shadow on the floor like the memory of sleep schedules. She walked the mess hall, where the benches still bore the ghost-rings of ten thousand tin cups. She put her hand flat on the doorframe of the drying sheds, where the wood was worn smooth and pale at shoulder height from forty years of divers leaning there, waiting on the tide.

In the records annex, burned to its black ribs, she stood in the doorway and did not go in. The ash had already begun to soften under yesterday's dew. Small green things, absurdly, were considering it.

Then she collected her bag—one bag, five years, and it did not fill it—and went out into the courtyard, and the senior enforcer checked her name against his list with visible disappointment at finding it present, and handed her the paper that declared her free.

Free. Discharged. Effective immediately.

She signed where he indicated. Her hand did not shake, which surprised her; she would have expected it to, signing away the only version of her life she had known how to live. But the signature came out steady, and small, and hers.

The gate stood open. Beyond it, the harbor quarter ran loud and ordinary—gulls, fish-carts, a man arguing about the price of mackerel with the passion of a philosopher—as though the world contained no workshops at all, much less ones that closed. Paloma walked toward it slowly, deliberately last, and felt the strangeness settle over her like a garment cut for someone else: for five years she had entered and left this gate by permission, counted out and counted back, and now she was walking out with nothing owed and nothing permitted, and the difference was invisible, and it weighed everything.

Behind her, boots on stone. She did not turn.

"At the corner," Bess murmured, materializing beside her with the unhurried menace of an old woman carrying a bundle of laundry, "there's a fish-cart. The driver owes me for thirty years of overlooking his scale. He'll take your bag to the Rows, and no one searching a departing diver's belongings will find what's in it, because what's in it won't be on you." A pause of exactly one step. "Though I'd sooner trust the lining of your coat than any cart in Saltmere, if you follow me."

"I follow you."

"Then walk, child. Walk like a woman with nowhere particular to be. It's the hardest role in the world and you'll play it for eight days."

"Seven," Paloma said.

"Seven," Bess agreed. "God help us all."

At the corner, the fish-cart waited. Paloma surrendered her bag to a driver who looked through her with professional blankness, and Bess squeezed her hand once—hard, brief, a knot tied rather than a goodbye—and turned toward the Rows, and Paloma Vane walked out into the city alone, carrying nothing visible, with her mother's burned heart sewn against her ribs.

Seven days. The magistrate's sitting. Everything they had salvaged from fire and sea and silence, and everything Aldous Meridian still believed he had destroyed.

She touched her collar where the oilcloth lay hidden, feeling the torn edge of the letter through the fabric like a pulse, and turned toward the harbor—to find Elias Corvane already waiting at the second lamppost down, hatless, pale, and moving toward her at a pace that said the seven days had just gotten shorter.
