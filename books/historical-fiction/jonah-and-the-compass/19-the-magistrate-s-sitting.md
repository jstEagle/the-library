## 19. The Magistrate's Sitting

The magistrate's hall in Saltmere had been built to impress people who were already impressed by themselves. Its ceiling rose into a dome painted with the four winds, each one rendered as a chubby cherub with puffed cheeks, and its floor was marble polished so diligently that Paloma's borrowed boots skated on it with every second step. She had never been inside a building that smelled of anything but salt, tar, and fish before. This one smelled of lamp oil, old paper, and the particular pomade that wealthy men used when they wanted other men to think they hadn't tried.

Her hands were steady. She noticed this with a kind of distant pride, the way a diver notices clear water. Five years of counting oysters by feel in green dark water had trained her hands to be steady even when her heart was doing arithmetic at speed.

"You're skating," Bess murmured beside her. "Walk like your feet are heavy. Like you own the floor."

"I might, by evening."

"That's the spirit. Now stop saying it out loud."

The hall filled slowly. Word had traveled—word always traveled in Saltmere, faster than any ship—and divers stood three deep along the back wall in their work clothes because nobody had told them they couldn't come and nobody dared tell them now. Teo was there, his ribs still strapped, standing upright through what Paloma knew was considerable pain. Old Marta. The twins from the morning beds. Even the cook, who had closed the harbor kitchen for the day, which for her was roughly equivalent to renouncing a religious order.

At the front, behind a desk carved with more cherubs, sat Magistrate Orrey: a small, dry man with spectacles he cleaned when he wished to make people wait. He had the reputation of being honest in the way a breakwater is honest—it doesn't care which ship it wrecks.

And across the aisle, dressed in dove gray worth more than the workshop's yearly lease, sat Aldous Meridian.

He looked well. He looked rested. He looked like a man who had slept soundly on a mattress stuffed with other people's futures. When he caught Paloma looking, he inclined his head with perfect courtesy, as one does to a passing acquaintance at church.

Paloma inclined hers back.

*Let him wonder,* she thought. *Let him spend this whole sitting wondering how much I know.*

Elias arrived last, in his census uniform, which suddenly looked less like an office and more like armor worn thin at the elbows. He did not look at Aldous. He looked at Paloma, briefly, and in that look passed everything he had: *I remember all of it. Every number. Every line.*

Magistrate Orrey cleaned his spectacles, put them on, and surveyed the hall.

"This is the quarterly sitting," he said, in a voice like a ledger closing. "Petitions first. Then claims." A pause. "We have one claim today that promises to keep us past supper. Vane against Meridian. Let's have it."

* *

Paloma had rehearsed her opening eleven times. She got through exactly none of it.

She began, "Magistrate, I am Paloma Vane, indentured diver of the Meridian workshop—" and her voice came out steadier than she'd planned, which unsettled her enough that she abandoned the speech entirely and simply told the truth, plainly, the way she'd learned to speak underwater where there are no words to hide behind.

"Five years ago I entered indenture at the pearl workshop. My mother was Sera Vane, a diver there. The company has always recorded her as a diver. Nothing more. But my mother was also the granddaughter of Augustin Meridian, founder of the Meridian Trading Company. And Augustin Meridian's will—the true will—left the workshop, the beds, and a portion of the estate not to the family that now sits in fine gray wool, but to her."

A rustle went around the hall like wind over water.

"On what evidence?" Orrey asked. "Wills are paper, Miss Vane. Paper burns. Paper walks off. I've seen a hundred wills in this hall and ninety of them were lies wearing wax seals."

"Then you'll want to hear the terms," said Elias Corvane, rising.

* *

He had memorized the will over four nights, hunched over salvaged pages by storm-lamp, reading until the words lived behind his eyes like scripture. Now he stood in the well of the hall with his hands clasped behind his back—a census taker's posture, the posture of a man whose entire profession was getting details right—and recited.

"'I, Augustin Meridian, of unsound body but sound mind, do set down this my final instrument.'" His voice was flat and precise, each clause laid down like paving stones. "'To my daughter, Liana Meridian-Vane, born of my daughter Sera, whom I acknowledge before God and this company, I leave the pearl workshop of Saltmere, its lease, its beds, and its charter, held in trust for her heirs in perpetuity, that the sea may belong to those who work it.'"

He paused. In the gallery, old Marta had begun quietly to cry.

"To my grandson Aldous, the house on Cordage Street and the sum of four thousand marks, which I judge sufficient, having observed his character."

Somebody in the divers' section made a noise that was very nearly a laugh and converted it into a cough at the last moment.

"The document bears three witnesses," Elias continued, "and is dated the ninth of Stormmonth, thirty-one years past. It names its custodian as Captain Ivo Renner of the yacht *Pelican*. I can recite the full text, Magistrate—all forty-one clauses—if the court requires."

"Forty-one clauses," Orrey repeated dryly. "From memory."

"Memory is the only archive some of us are permitted," said Elias, "and therefore the one I keep most carefully."

Paloma watched Aldous. He had not moved. That frightened her more than any motion would have. A man who fidgets is a man surprised; a man who sits still has already decided what happens next.

* *

The witnesses came forward one by one, and here the gentle machinery of five years of quiet competence finally paid its dividend, because it turned out that a woman who makes herself unremarkable sees everything, and a woman who sees everything remembers.

Bess testified to Sera Vane's presence at the workshop, to the visits of a well-dressed gentleman she had known as "the founder's man," to the night she helped carry a sea chest out the back gate ahead of enforcers. Her memory was sixty years deep and sharp as a shucking knife; under cross-examination by Aldous's lawyer—a smooth young man named Pell who smelled of the same pomade as his client—she did not bend so much as a hair.

"Madam," Pell said, "you'd have the court believe you remember a conversation from thirty years ago verbatim."

"No," said Bess comfortably. "I remember it the way you remember a fire. Not word for word. But I know exactly how hot it was."

Old Marta confirmed the payments. Teo, standing with difficulty, described the men who had searched the records room, their faces, their boots, the smell of the lamp they carried. One by one the divers gave their small squares of mosaic, and Paloma felt the picture assembling itself above the court like the ceiling's painted winds.

Then came the signature.

Pell produced, with a flourish, documents signed by Augustin Meridian across twenty years—shipping contracts, charter amendments—and Elias produced, from the recovered annex fragments, two signatures dated within months of the alleged will. He laid them side by side on the magistrate's desk.

"Note the terminal flourish on the M," he said. "In every authentic signature across two decades, the final stroke descends below the line. In the purported original"—he indicated the copy Paloma had transcribed from the strongbox contents before the theft—"it ascends. Note also the crossbar of the T, the doubled dotting of the i. Forgers copy shapes. They rarely copy habits."

Orrey leaned so close to the papers his spectacles fogged.

"A persuasive exercise," Pell said smoothly. "But the original will, Magistrate, is not available to the court. It was lost—or stolen. What Miss Vane offers is a transcription. Anyone can forge a transcription of a forgery." He smiled at Paloma with genuine, unctuous warmth. "The Meridian family regrets deeply that no authentic instrument exists. We can hardly be blamed for what the sea has taken."

"Can't you," said Paloma.

It was not a question. It came out quiet, almost conversational, and it stopped Pell mid-flourish, because the best fishermen know when something has taken the hook.

"Magistrate," Paloma said, "the sea gives things back. Saltmere knows this better than any city alive. Three weeks ago, after the hurricane, the tide returned a wreck to the shallows off Gull Point. The *Pelican*, Captain Ivo Renner, master. Augustin Meridian's private yacht, sunk thirty-one years ago—two weeks, by the manifest, after that will was signed." She let the silence stretch. "My mother was on that boat when it went down. She survived. The will did not go down with it. She took it aboard the *Pelican* deliberately—because she already knew someone in her own family meant to bury it. And then, when the sea came for them anyway, she hid it where only a diver could ever reach it."

"And you reached it," Orrey said.

"We reached it." She glanced at the gallery, at the divers. "Divers reach things. It's the whole profession."

Pell stepped forward. "Magistrate, this is theater. A wreck appears conveniently after a storm, containing conveniently uncorroborated—"

"The seal," Paloma said, "is unbroken."

She nodded to the doors.

* *

They had argued about this the night before, in Bess's cramped room above the drying racks, and the argument had been about more than procedure. Bringing the strongbox itself into open court meant showing Aldous exactly what remained, exactly how much he'd failed to destroy. It meant betting everything on one object, in front of the man who had already burned a records annex to get at paper.

"He'll try to take it," Teo had said. "Here. Now. If he's cornered."

"Let him try," Bess had said, and patted the rolling pin under her shawl, and Paloma had decided that between the magistrate's constables and Bess's rolling pin, the odds were acceptable.

Now four divers carried the strongbox up the aisle on a litter, and the hall went silent the way the sea goes silent before weather. It was not an impressive-looking object—corroded iron, crusted barnacle-gray, streaked with the green of three decades underwater—but it bore, on its face, the lead seal of the Saltmere Salvage Office, applied the morning after the wreck surfaced, witnessed by the harbormaster himself, who sat in the third row looking deeply uncomfortable and deeply certain.

"The seal," Paloma said, "was broken once, in the workshop, before six witnesses, on the eleventh of this month. Inside were the letters of Captain Renner, the yacht's manifest, and the will of Augustin Meridian—which I read aloud to those six witnesses before it was stolen from beneath the drying racks two nights later, by persons I can name." Teo flinched; she kept her eyes on Orrey. "The transcription I offer the court is not my invention, Magistrate. It is my memory of a document that existed, sealed and dated by officers of this port, in the custody of this city, before the Meridian household could touch it. Mr. Pell says anyone can forge a transcription of a forgery. But who forged the forgery the thieves stole? They stole a fake, Magistrate. Because the real one was never where they thought to look."

"Objection," Pell began. "There is no proof the stolen document—"

"There is proof," said Elias, "of what was stolen. I filed an incident report. As a census officer, I am required to file reports. It is the single duty of my profession that has never yet been corrupted." A ripple of something—not quite laughter, but adjacent—moved through the divers. "The report describes the stolen document: three pages, iron-gall ink, wax seal of the Meridian household, signature with ascending flourish. If Mr. Meridian produces the 'original' will, we may compare it to what his men actually took."

For the first time, Aldous Meridian moved. He leaned toward Pell and whispered something brief.

Pell's face performed a small, private catastrophe.

* *

Orrey cleaned his spectacles a fourth time, which by now had become practically liturgical.

"Mr. Meridian," he said. "Your response to the claim."

Aldous rose. He smoothed the front of his gray coat. And Paloma understood, watching him, that he was going to produce the stolen will—that he had walked into this hall intending to all along, believing it airtight, believing the theft had left her with nothing but words.

He believed he still held the real thing.

"Magistrate," Aldous said, and his voice was rich with wounded dignity, "this court has been treated to a performance. Memory recited. Seals from a box anyone could have prepared. I hold in my hand the true will of my grandfather, lawfully in my family's possession these thirty years—" he drew out a folded document, three pages, wax seal gleaming, "—which I submit to the court without fear of comparison."

Orrey took it. Unfolded it. Held it beside Elias's fragments and the salvage record.

The hall held its breath.

"The date," Orrey said at last, slowly, "on this document is the ninth of Stormmonth, thirty-one years past." He looked up. "The *Pelican* sank on the twenty-fourth of the same month. Captain Renner's letters—recovered under port seal—state that he carried the will aboard and feared precisely such a substitution." He set the pages down with terrible gentleness. "Furthermore. This document's wax shows the Meridian household seal. The seal applied by this court's own salvage office—unbroken, dated, witnessed—covers the container from which the claimed true will was read. Both cannot be original. One of these documents spent thirty-one years under salt water, and one of them spent thirty-one years in a drawer waiting for today." He removed his spectacles entirely. "The ink, Mr. Meridian, on yours is barely a decade faded. I've been a magistrate in a port town for twenty-six years. I know what drowned paper looks like. This paper has never been swimming."

Somewhere in the gallery, somebody dropped something metal, and nobody laughed, because the moment was far too large for laughter, but the silence afterward had the shape of laughter held in both hands.

Aldous did not look at the magistrate. He looked at Paloma.

And Paloma looked back at him and thought of her mother, who had hidden a will in a sinking ship because it was the safest place she knew, and of herself, who had counted oysters in the dark for five years because invisibility was the safest life she knew, and understood suddenly that they had always been making the same wager: that the powerful never bother to look closely at the water.

They always forget, eventually, that divers live there.

"Magistrate," Paloma said, "the court has heard the terms of the true will. It has seen the forgery exposed by its own ink. I ask the sitting to recognize the claim of Sera Vane's heir: the workshop, the beds, and the charter."

Orrey put his spectacles back on. He looked, for a long moment, at the divers packed along the walls—at Teo standing straight despite his ribs, at Marta, at Bess with her shawl and her hidden rolling pin—and then at the fine gray man standing alone in the aisle with nothing left to smooth.

"The court will deliberate," he said. "Sit down, all of you. Except you, Mr. Meridian. You'll remain. My clerk has questions about ink."

* *

The deliberation lasted the length of one pot of tea, which the bailiff shared with the front row, and which Bess declared "weak, but then, English tea usually is."

When Orrey returned to the bench, he did not perform. He simply said, "The forged instrument is void. The claim of Sera Vane's line is recognized in principle. Settlement of the estate—" and here he paused, and looked directly at Paloma over his spectacles with an expression she would think about for years afterward, an expression that was almost, underneath everything, kind, "—settlement of the estate is a matter for the parties. Courts divide property, Miss Vane. They are famously bad at knowing what it's worth. You strike me as someone who knows exactly what it's worth."

He struck his small gavel. The sound was tiny in the huge hall, and enormous anyway.

The gallery erupted—not cheering, exactly; Saltmere divers didn't cheer, it wasn't the profession—but a low, sustained roar like surf coming in, wave after wave of it, and Teo was laughing and crying at once, and Bess was gripping Paloma's arm hard enough to bruise, and through all of it Aldous Meridian stood alone in the middle of the marble floor, gray coat and gray face, watching his name dissolve.

Then he crossed the aisle to her.

The hall quieted fast. Constables drifted closer. But Aldous only stopped an arm's length away, and his voice, when it came, was pitched for her alone.

"You don't want this," he said. "Believe me. I've lived inside it. It eats everything it's given." A pause; something moved behind his eyes that was almost human and almost pity. "Name it. Whatever you want for it. Everyone has a price, even divers."

Paloma considered him. She found, genuinely, that she was not angry anymore—anger had burned off somewhere in the last nine days, the way fog burns off the beds by midmorning—and what remained was curiosity, and beneath the curiosity, a strange calm, like the still patch in the center of a turning tide.

"You're right," she said pleasantly. "Everyone has a price. Mine just isn't money. Ask me again tomorrow, Mr. Meridian—I'll be naming it publicly, at the settlement hearing, and I promise you'll find it cheaper than you fear and worth more than you can imagine."

Something flickered across his face—the first honest expression she'd ever seen him wear, and it looked, remarkably, like hope.

Behind him, Elias was already moving toward her through the crowd, and Bess was already whispering, "Whatever you're planning, girl, I can see you're planning it," and outside the tall windows the tide was turning, going out gold in the late light, pulling gently, patiently, at everything Saltmere had built upon its shore.
