## 20. Verdict

The magistrate's hall in Saltmere had been built by men who wanted the room to say *money lives here*, and it said it in every polished balustrade and coffered ceiling. Paloma Vane walked beneath all of it in her one good dress—a diver's practicality disguised as fashion, Bess had assured her—and felt the way she always felt on land: too light, as if someone had forgotten to weigh her down properly.

The gallery was full. That surprised her less than who filled it: divers from the workshop, some with faces she'd known for five years, others from rival beds up the coast who'd come because news of a Vane traveling at company expense was better than any festival. Teo sat near the back, his healing ribs keeping him upright only through stubbornness, flanked by two of his cousins. Bess wore her funeral bonnet, which she reserved for occasions demanding gravity, and had told Paloma that morning, "If we lose, at least I'm dressed for it."

At the front, behind a desk carved with mermaids who looked embarrassed to be there, sat Magistrate Ondrey—a small man with a large reputation for being impossible to bribe, largely because he'd been caught refusing three separate bribes and never one acceptance.

Aldous Meridian sat across the aisle from Paloma with his lawyers arranged around him like silverware. He looked rested. He looked confident. He looked, Paloma thought, like a man who had rehearsed losing gracefully and found the fitting went poorly.

"Call the claimant," Ondrey said, and Paloma's knees remembered every ladder she'd ever climbed out of cold water.

* * *

They had agreed on the order days ago, huddled in Bess's kitchen while rain argued with the shutters. Elias first—because a census taker's oath still counted for something in this city, or at least everyone assumed it did, which amounted to nearly the same thing.

He took the stand looking like a man who had ironed his conscience into his collar. He gave his name, his office, his eleven years of service to the Company's rolls. Then he recited.

"The last will and testament of Augustin Meridian, executed the ninth of Stormmonth, forty years past, as recorded in my memory and transcribed this morning before witnesses." He closed his eyes—which he swore afterward was not theater but habit, the way he read ledgers—and began.

Paloma had heard him practice it perhaps thirty times, first stumbling, then fluent, until the will lived in him like a song. But hearing it now, in this room, under seal and oath, it sounded different. It sounded like a door opening.

"*To my daughter Sera, born of Marisol the diver, whom I could not acknowledge in life and will not fail in death, I leave the workshop at Gull Row entire, its beds, its boats, and its leases, together with the sum of four thousand crowns, held in trust until such time as she or her heirs present themselves.*"

Aldous's lead lawyer rose before the last word settled. "Objection. The magistrate is asked to accept a document that no longer exists, recalled by an official whose own archive burned—conveniently—three weeks ago."

"Conveniently," Ondrey repeated, dry as old rope. "Yes. We'll return to that fire, Master Quill. Continue, Corvane."

Elias continued. He gave the clauses whole: the recognition of Sera as lawful daughter; the provision that any heir of her blood might claim after completing service to the Company; the signature, the seals, the witnesses named—two of them dead, one of them, remarkably, still alive in a fishing village two days north and willing to travel.

Then came Bess, who testified about a young mother with salt-burned hands who paid for her bread in pearls and never spoke her father's name above a whisper. Then the divers, one after another, describing enforcers at the gates, questions asked in the night, Teo's ribs entered into evidence via a physician's diagram that made several gallery members inhale.

Through all of it, Aldous watched Paloma rather than the witnesses. She felt his attention like weather. She kept her eyes forward and her hands still and thought about water pressure, how at forty feet the weight of the sea stops feeling like an enemy and starts feeling like a hand holding you to what's true.

Then Master Quill produced their document—the stolen original, recovered from beneath the floor of Aldous's own townhouse, presented with a straight face as having been "in the family's protective custody."

And Paloma's stomach dropped, because it looked perfect. The parchment was right, the ink aged convincingly, the signature flowing and confident. For a long moment in that hall, she understood exactly how her grandmother had been cheated forty years ago: power doesn't need good lies when it can afford excellent ones.

Quill made his argument—the will a fabrication, the claimant a clever indentured girl coached by a disgraced census taker, the whole affair a conspiracy of the resentful. He was good. He was very good. Paloma watched jurors' faces soften toward doubt and gripped the rail of her seat until Bess's hand covered hers.

"Master Quill," Ondrey said mildly, when the speech wound down. "Your document. It has a provenance?"

"It has been in Meridian family custody since execution, your honor."

"Custody. And the seal?"

"Unbroken, of course."

Ondry nodded slowly, the way he probably nodded before sentencing smugglers. "Then let us examine custody."

* * *

It was Elias who had thought of it, weeks ago, staring at the salvage manifest by lamplight. The strongbox had come up from the wreck with its seal intact—wax pressed with the founder's private mark, dated by two independent witnesses at the recovery site, logged and countersigned because Paloma had insisted, having learned from her mother's letters exactly how easily paper lies when nobody watches it being born.

The box itself sat now on the magistrate's floor, ugly and barnacled, guarded by two bailiffs who kept glancing at it as if it might open on its own.

"The court notes," Ondrey said, "that the document offered by the defense claims to have resided in Meridian custody for forty years. The claimant offers a sealed strongbox recovered from Augustin Meridian's yacht, sunk—per harbor records, insurance claims, and three surviving salvagers—thirty-eight years ago. The box's seal bears the founder's private mark and a date of closure consistent with his final voyage." He paused. "Master Quill, if your document spent those decades in your client's townhouse, kindly explain its presence inside a box that sank before your client's father ever held the estate."

The silence had texture. Quill's mouth opened, closed, opened again. Somewhere in the gallery, Teo laughed once, sharply, then bit it off.

"We would suggest," Quill said at last, with the dignity of a man stepping off a cliff in formal dress, "that the box was tampered with during salvage."

"By the claimant, no doubt. Who then left the seal unbroken and invited the court to break it publicly." Ondrey turned to the bailiffs. "Open it. Carefully. Everything witnessed and written down as it comes out."

The hinge screamed. Salt had done its slow work, but the interior was dry—her mother had packed it with oiled wool, Paloma knew now, had planned this the way divers plan everything, against the day the sea would be asked to give something back.

The documents emerged one by one, each read aloud by the court clerk. The true will, matching Elias's recitation word for word. Letters in Augustin's own hand—tender, guilt-ridden, addressed to a daughter he called *my pearl*. And then the letter Paloma had not expected, the one that made even Ondrey lean forward: a draft of the succession instrument in Aldous's father's handwriting, with corrections, with the original clause struck through and replaced, with a note to a scribe reading *make it look older, spare nothing*.

Fraud, documented in the forger's own impatient pen.

Aldous stood. Sat. Stood again. His lawyer whispered furiously; Aldous waved him off with a gesture that had probably ended careers.

"Your honor." His voice was steady. Paloma granted him that much; he'd been raised to perform composure the way she'd been raised to hold her breath. "I move for a recess."

"Denied. Do you contest the documents?"

"I—" A muscle moved in his jaw. "I contest nothing at this time, your honor. I move instead to address the matter of remedy. Privately. I am prepared to discuss terms with the claimant that would make all of this—" he gestured at the wreckage of his case, at the gallery full of divers, at the strongbox dripping history onto the magistrate's floor— "*unnecessary.*"

"There is nothing unnecessary about fraud," Ondrey said. "But the court will hear the claimant."

Every face turned to her. Five years of making herself invisible, and now every eye in Saltmere's grandest room was a lantern pointed at Paloma Vane.

She rose. Her legs worked, which she considered a personal triumph.

* * *

Here is what she had learned in the water, and what she said:

That a fortune was like an anchor—it didn't matter how much it weighed if you were chained to the wrong thing. That Aldous Meridian was offering her money the way men offer money when they mean *go away and let me keep being what I am*. That she had watched her mother choose silence for safety and die anyway, poor and unnamed, and silence had turned out to be a bad bargain on every count.

"I don't want your money," she told Aldous, and heard her voice carry further than she'd intended, because halls built to announce wealth also announce its refusals. "I want three things, and then you can keep every coin your grandfather mined out of these beds."

Quill made a strangled sound. In the gallery, Bess removed her funeral bonnet, which among workshop women meant either deep respect or the beginning of a fight.

"First: the workshop charter at Gull Row, transferred whole—to the divers. Not to me. To them, as a cooperative, under whatever legal instrument makes it stick. They built every crown your family ever spent. Let them own the walls they bled in."

"Second: full pardons, public, for Teo Sarn and every diver questioned, detained, or punished during this matter. Their records cleaned. Their names said plainly, in writing, where employers can read them."

"Third." She paused, and found she was looking at Aldous directly, and found she could. "You confess the forgery. Openly, on record. Not for me—for your grandfather's daughter, who deserved a name for forty years and got a grave without a stone worth reading."

The hall was so quiet Paloma could hear the harbor outside, the gulls, the ordinary world continuing.

"And what," Aldous said, very softly, "do I receive?"

"Avoiding the magistrate's opinion on fraud charges," Ondrey supplied, "which, given today's evidence, I can promise in advance will be memorable."

Aldous looked at his lawyers. At his ruined case. At the letter in his father's hand that proved rot ran a generation deeper than anyone had proven before breakfast. Paloma watched him do the arithmetic—he was, whatever else, his family's son—and saw the moment the numbers came up short.

"The charter," he said hoarsely. "The pardons. The confession." He swallowed. "Agreed. All of it. God keep it far from my door."

"Then the court so orders," Ondrey said, and his gavel fell like a wave breaking, and the sound that rose from the gallery was not dignified at all.

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor, amid the noise of divers celebrating and lawyers departing at speed, Teo found her. He moved carefully, one arm around his ribs, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"You gave it away," he said finally. Not accusing. Astonished.

"I didn't give anything away. I spent it. There's a difference—Bess taught me."

"On what?"

Paloma looked past him, through the tall windows, to where the harbor lay bright and busy, boats going out on the morning tide. "On everyone," she said. "It spends better that way."

Teo nodded slowly, and then, wincing, pulled her into a careful hug that smelled of tar and salt. "The beds open next week," he said into her hair. "Cooperative beds. First dive's yours by rights."

"Second," said a voice behind them.

Elias stood there, hat in hand, wearing the expression of a man who had just resigned eleven years of employment by testifying honestly and couldn't quite locate the regret. In his fingers he held a folded paper.

"My certification of the workshop rolls," he said. "The one I refused to falsify last month. The Company accepted my resignation this morning. Apparently honest clerks are a redundancy." He hesitated. "I'm told cooperatives have need of people who can keep records that don't lie. Though if the position's filled—"

"The position," Paloma said, "has been open since the day you asked my mother's maiden name."

Above the harbor, the gulls wheeled, and somewhere out past the point, the tide was already turning—going out, Paloma knew, and certain to come back, bringing whatever it brought, ready or not.
