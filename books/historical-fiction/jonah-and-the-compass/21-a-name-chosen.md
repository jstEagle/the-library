## 21. A Name Chosen

The magistrate's clerk had a pen for every occasion. Paloma had watched him select them over the course of the sitting the way a diver selects knives: the broad-nibbed one for headings, the fine steel one for sums, and now—the ceremony of it unmistakable—a fat black quill with a silver ferrule, reserved, apparently, for signatures that mattered.

"Whenever you're ready," the clerk said, not unkindly. He was a small man with ink-stained cuffs and the patient air of someone who had witnessed a great many endings of great many affairs, most of them less satisfying than this one.

Paloma stood at the long table beneath the magistrate's empty bench. The room had emptied slowly after the verdict—first the spectators, reluctant, craning for last looks at Aldous Meridian being escorted out under guard with his fine coat dusted in the shoulders by a constable's hand; then the witnesses, Bess among them, squeezing her arm so hard on the way past that she'd have a bruise shaped like gratitude for a week. Now only the clerk remained, plus Elias, standing by the door as though he hadn't decided yet whether he was allowed to be there.

The charter lay before her on good rag paper, its seal still warm from the wax. *The Saltmere Divers' Cooperative*, it read, in the clerk's best hand. *Charter members: those named below.*

There were thirty-one names on the list already. Teo's was first—he had insisted, hoarsely, that his go above hers, on account of what it had cost him, and no one had argued. Then Bess, who had signed with a flourish suggesting she'd been practicing in secret for days. Then the rest of the divers, the sorters, the boat hands, even old Maren who hadn't dived in nine years but refused to be left off on principle.

At the bottom, a space waited for the word *Founder*.

Paloma picked up the ceremonial quill. It felt absurd in her hand, like diving in a ball gown.

"You can use the ordinary pen," the clerk offered. "Most people do."

"No," she said. "If I'm going to do a foolish, grand thing, I'll do it properly."

She thought about what to write. That was the strange part—that there was anything to think about at all. She had been *Paloma* to everyone in the workshop for five years, and before that she had been *girl* to the company rolls and *Vane's daughter* to anyone who bothered. The name had never once belonged to her. It had been assigned, like a berth, like a quota, like an indenture number stamped beside it in every ledger in Saltmere.

Her mother had given her the name in the first place, though. Sera Vane, who could hold her breath for four minutes and lie for none, who had looked down into a cradle and chosen *Paloma*—dove, the bird that finds its way home over any distance of open water.

Well. If it was good enough for her mother's choosing, it was good enough for hers.

She wrote *Paloma Vane* in careful letters, pressing hard enough that the quill's nib splayed slightly, and found—to her considerable surprise—that her eyes had gone hot and her throat had closed around something about the size and shape of five years.

The clerk blotted the signature with a little sand and said, "There. Witnessed and sealed." And then, because clerks are human too: "My mother was a sorter at the Cod Line works, you know. Forty years. She'd have liked to see this."

"Thank you," Paloma said, and meant it more than any of the eloquent speeches anyone had made at her all morning.

Elias crossed the room as the clerk gathered his pens. He moved differently now—she'd noticed it over the past week, since he'd handed in his resignation from the census office with such brisk finality that his supervisor reportedly dropped a stack of household returns. Lighter. Or perhaps just unarmored. It was hard to tell the difference sometimes.

"Well," he said.

"Well," she agreed.

"That's a very dramatic signature."

"It's a very expensive quill. I wasn't going to waste it."

They walked out together into the square, where the afternoon light lay thick and golden on the cobblestones, and the whole city smelled of tar and bread and the particular green salt smell of the tide coming in—which it had been doing, Paloma reflected, with remarkable indifference to the affairs of men, for rather longer than the Meridian family had existed.

* * *

Word traveled fast in Saltmere, faster than scandal, which was saying something. By the time they reached the harbor market, half the fishwives had heard some version of events, each version wrong in a different and increasingly florid way. In the most popular telling, Paloma had fought a duel with Aldous Meridian. In another, she had raised the founder's yacht from the deep through sheer force of will, which she privately considered the most flattering and also the most exhausting thing anyone had ever credited her with.

"Miss Vane!" called the oyster wife whose stall sat nearest the pier—a woman named Dossa who had sold Paloma chowder every Friday for five years and never once used her name. She was using it now, loudly, and holding out a bowl unbidden. "On the house. On ALL the houses, if you ask me. You've done more for this harbor than the lighthouse."

"I've done considerably less for this harbor than the lighthouse," Paloma said, accepting the bowl, "and I'd thank you not to spread slander."

Dossa cackled. Down the row of stalls, other faces turned—some grinning, some simply curious, a few guarded in the way of people who had lived through enough regimes to hedge their bets. A boy of maybe eight stared up at her with his mouth open until his mother elbowed him, and he said, awed, "Is it true you're rich?"

Paloma crouched down to his level. This was the question, then. The one everyone was too polite to ask except children and the fishwives of Saltmere.

"No," she said. "I'm not."

"But—you WON."

"I won the workshop. Well—I won the right to give the workshop away, which is a bit like winning a boat and being handed the bilge pump." She handed him a coin anyway, because generosity was easier when it came with a joke attached, and because his face did something wonderful when she pressed the copper into his palm. "Buy yourself something. Not an eel. You'll only lose it down your shirt again."

They got their coffee at the same crooked stall where everything between them had started, back when Elias Corvane had been the most dangerous man in Saltmere to her—not because he was cruel, but because he was honest, and honesty turns out to be far harder to dodge. The coffee was still terrible. It had the consistency of harbor water and a flavor that suggested the beans had been roasted near a fire, possibly during one.

"To freedom," Elias said, raising his cup.

"To terrible coffee," Paloma countered, and they drank, and both made identical faces, and laughed in the slightly unhinged way of two people coming down at last from a long terror.

He looked older than he had at the census table. Or younger—it was impossible to say. The stiffness of the institution had gone out of him the way salt goes out of a line left in the sun. Underneath, she supposed, was whatever he had actually been all along, before the forms and the orders and the flagging of irregular bloodlines: a serious young man with ink on his fingers who took other people's lives down in careful handwriting because somebody told him that was his duty, and who had spent a year learning that duty and obedience were different animals wearing similar coats.

"They'll want you back eventually," she said. "The census office. You know how institutions are. They forgive anything except embarrassment."

"I didn't resign to be forgiven." He turned his cup in his hands. "I resigned because I sat down to copy my final reports and realized I couldn't remember a single person's face. Three thousand names, and all I had was columns." He glanced at her. "Except yours. Yours I remembered."

"That," Paloma said, "is either very romantic or a confession of professional negligence."

"I've been told I blur the line."

* * *

They walked out along the breakwater because neither of them wanted to go anywhere in particular and the breakwater was the only place in Saltmere where you could be alone in public. The evening tide came in long and slow, folding itself over the rocks, and the gulls wheeled overhead conducting their eternal, vicious business.

Bess had embraced her twice at the magistrate's hall and then said, in the matron's flat way that meant the opposite of flat, "Your mother would have broken something hugging you that hard. Good. Break things." And then she had gone home to the workshop—which was theirs now, all thirty-one of theirs—to argue about drying racks with Teo, who had taken to management the way some men take to drink: immediately, completely, and with strong opinions about everyone else's technique.

Tomorrow Paloma would go back too. There were ledgers to reopen, contracts to redraw, a lease to transfer, and a hundred small negotiations ahead with guilds and harbormasters and the sort of people who only respected you once you'd bored them thoroughly. Tomorrow she would be the founder of something, which was a word with weight, and she'd have to learn to carry it without letting it press her back underwater.

But tonight—

Tonight there was just the water, and the light going amber and then rose and then that particular bruised violet that Saltmere did better than any city she could imagine, and Elias Corvane standing at the end of the breakwater with his hands in his pockets looking like a man rehearsing a sentence.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"Doing what?"

"The thing where you compose a document aloud before you say it. I watched you do it for a year. Your jaw moves."

He exhaled. "It's a hard habit to break. Everything I've said for six years has been admissible."

"And now?"

"Now—" He stopped. Started again, plainly abandoning the draft. "I don't actually know what comes next. I have savings enough for three months if I'm careful. I have no trade except reading and writing, which pays poorly unless you're willing to serve somebody's paperwork again, and I find I'm not. And I have—" he gestured vaguely at the sea, the sky, her, "—no compass whatsoever. Which strikes me as ironic, given recent events, and also as the first honest situation I've been in since I was nineteen."

Paloma looked out at the water. The first stars were arriving, shyly, the way they always did, as though checking whether the sky was safe.

"My mother had a compass," she said. "Did I ever tell you? Brass case, cracked glass. It came up in her sea chest with everything else. The needle doesn't point north—it points east, stubbornly, forever, out toward open water. Bess says it was always broken. My mother wore it anyway." She smiled. "I used to think that was sad. A tool that doesn't work. Now I think she kept it because it pointed where *she* wanted to go, and she liked having something in the world agree with her."

Elias was quiet a moment. Then he said, "Come work with me."

She turned. "Work with you doing what?"

"I don't know yet. Something that needs doing. There are divers all along this coast under contracts like yours was—like yours *would have been*, if—" He shook his head, declining the detour. "The cooperative will need someone who can read the companies' ledgers and find the places where the numbers lie. Someone they can't intimidate with procedure, because I spent six years being the procedure and I know all its tricks. And it will need—" he faltered, and she watched, with something expanding gently in her chest, as the census taker failed entirely to compose himself, "—I work better when there's coffee. Terrible coffee. At a particular stall. On Fridays."

"Fridays," Paloma said. "That's your offer. Ledgers and chowder."

"And you," he said quietly. "If you want. I'm told I'm honest to a fault. It's my only qualification, but it seems to matter to you."

The tide breathed against the rocks. Somewhere behind them, across the darkening harbor, the workshop lamps were being lit one by one—her lamps, their lamps—and the glow lay along the water in a broken gold road leading out to sea.

"Yes," she said. "All of it. But I'm negotiating the coffee. We're getting real coffee and pretending it's terrible, for tradition's sake."

"That's corruption," Elias said gravely. "I approve entirely."

They stood there while the dark came up out of the east, the direction her mother's compass had always pointed, and did not speak much, because there was no longer anything that needed saying urgently, and that itself was the strangest, newest luxury of all.

When they finally turned back toward the lights, Paloma noticed the boy from the market had left a piece of chalk drawn on the breakwater stone, low where the tide wouldn't take it: a wobbly dove, wings spread, flying east.

She looked at it a long moment.

"We'll need a seal for the charter documents," Elias said, following her gaze. "Something official."

"Do we?" Paloma said, and smiled, and did not tell him he'd just answered his own question. "Come on. Bess will have held dinner, and she holds grudges about cold stew longer than magistrates hold them about fraud."

Behind them the tide rose and covered the rocks, but the dove was drawn high enough, and in the morning it would still be there—flying, as it would fly for years afterward, over the door of a certain workshop, pointing not north but out, always out, toward the open sea.
