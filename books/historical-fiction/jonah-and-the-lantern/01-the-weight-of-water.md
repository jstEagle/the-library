## 1. The Weight of Water

The pearls came up from the water cold, and they left Freya's hands warm. That was the first thing she had learned, at nine years old, kneeling on the sorting bench with a lamp pulled close: that the sea gave up its dead men's wages still holding the chill of the deep, and that by the time they passed through her fingers they had been warmed into something the ledgers could love.

She did not look at the ledgers. That was the second thing she had learned.

The lamp hissed softly in the humid dark of the counting room. Beyond its circle of light, the compound slept — the long barracks where the divers were locked in after sundown, the curing sheds stinking of salt and lime, the great house where Augustus Halloran kept his brandy and his silence. Monsoon season sat on the coast like a hand pressing down. Rain came off the roof in ropes.

Freya sorted. Shell to the left pile, flesh to the pail for the coolers, pearls into the graded trays: seed, button, drop, the rare round ones that went into a locked tin Augustus himself carried to the bank. Her fingers moved without her. Seventeen years old and eight of those years spent at this bench; her hands knew the work better than her mind did, which was, she suspected, exactly how Augustus Halloran wanted it.

*Never count aloud. Never ask what a pearl is worth. Never wonder whose neck brought it up.*

Her father had taught her none of this. Her father had taught her letters, on the backs of waybills, his big blunt hand looping across the paper like rope thrown to a drowning girl. *Tomas Halloran*, who had gone down off the Mannar banks one grey morning five years ago and not come up. Who had been named drowned before the boats even returned to harbor, so quickly that Freya, at twelve, had thought: *they didn't even wait.*

She had never said that thought out loud. It had gone into the same place as everything else — the deep, sealed room inside her where she kept the things that would get her beaten if she let them reach her face.

The door opened on a gust of wet wind. Mr. Quill stood there, the compound's head clerk, a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that never rested anywhere longer than a debt collector's.

"Still at it," he said. Not a question. "Mr. Halloran wants the drop tray weighed before he rides out."

"It'll be weighed."

Quill's gaze slid over her the way it always did — cataloguing, assessing, finding nothing worth recording. She was, officially, nobody. The drowned man's daughter, taken in as a ward of the company out of what the town called charity and what the compound's books, she had once glimpsed for half a heartbeat before snatching her eyes away, called *indenture*. Four more years. Four more years of pearls and lamplight and keeping her face smooth as shell.

"The widow Kandiah came to the gate today," Quill said, and now his voice had that flatness it got when he delivered news designed to wound. "For her husband's final wage."

Freya's hands did not stop moving. They had learned that too.

"And?"

"And there's nothing owing. He died in debt. The company's claim against his account exceeds his earnings." Quill adjusted his cuffs. "She was told."

Died in debt. Every diver died in debt. The gear was rented, the boat space was rented, the food from the company store was priced high enough that a man dove all season and surfaced owing. Freya had understood the arithmetic since she was small enough to hide under this very bench. A diver's death wasn't a loss to the Halloran Trading Company. It was a settlement.

"She has children," Freya said.

"That's not a line on any ledger I keep." Quill paused at the door. "You'd do well to remember, miss, that your own account isn't so full you can afford softness. Mr. Halloran values order in his household."

He left. The rain filled the silence behind him.

Freya looked at the pearls in the drop tray. Round, lustrous, perfect. Somewhere under the grey water off the banks, men had died bringing them up, and their widows were turned away at the gate with empty hands, and the company's books recorded it all in a fair round hand that no one was permitted to examine.

*Never name the drowned.* Not because naming them was forbidden by any written rule. There was no written rule. There didn't need to be. You learned it the way you learned the sea's moods — by watching what happened to the ones who forgot.

She finished the weighing. She locked the tin. And then, instead of going straight to her cot in the corner of the clerks' annexe, she went by way of the kitchen.

* * *

The kitchen was empty at this hour, the cook long asleep, the bread from the morning ration sitting under its cloth on the scarred table. The compound ate well while the barracks ate thin; another arithmetic Freya was not supposed to notice. She took a loaf — a whole loaf, her heart hammering at the size of the theft — and wrapped it in a square of cloth, and slipped out through the door whose latch she had been oiling quietly for two years so it wouldn't announce her.

The rain met her like a wall. She ducked her head and moved along the compound wall, past the curing sheds, to the gap in the palisade where the mangrove roots had rotted through the timber. Outside, the fishing quarter hunched under the weather — palm thatch and driftwood shacks, dogs sleeping in doorways, the smell of woodsmoke and fish guts and rain.

She knew which hut was the widow Kandiah's. Everyone knew. The whole town knew where grief lived; only the compound pretended otherwise.

The widow opened the door before Freya knocked. She was a small woman, dry-eyed in the way of women who had wept everything out years ago or else had decided the company would never see a tear of hers. Behind her, two children slept on a mat, and a third watched Freya with wide, unblinking eyes.

"I've nothing for you," the widow said in Tamil, her voice flat. "If you've come from the gate, tell them they've said it already."

"I haven't come from the gate." Freya held out the bundle. "I've come from nowhere. Take it."

The widow looked at the bread. Then at Freya. In the lamplight spilling from the hut, her face was seamed and hard and suddenly, terribly tired.

"You're Halloran's girl," she said. "The drowned one's daughter."

"Yes."

"You eat at his table."

"I eat at his table," Freya said, "because he owns my four remaining years. It isn't the same as eating at his side." She pushed the bundle forward. "Please."

For a long moment the widow didn't move. Then she took the bread, and something crossed her face — not gratitude, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman seeing that the company's walls did not hold everyone inside them equally.

"They say your father was a good diver," the widow said. "One of the last who'd dive without the new gear. They say the sea took him quick." Her eyes held Freya's. "They say a lot of things."

Something cold walked up Freya's spine. "What do you mean?"

But the widow only shook her head, drawing the bread to her chest like a child. "Nothing. Nothing. Go home, girl, before someone sees you. Kindness is expensive here." At the threshold she paused. "My husband, before the water took him — he used to talk with the other men in words the clerks couldn't follow. If you ever want to know what the sea really says, learn to listen where the company doesn't."

Then the door closed, and the rain took Freya back.

* * *

She was ten steps inside the compound wall when the lantern swung up and caught her.

Quill stood beneath the eave of the counting room, and behind him, wrapped in an oilskin coat with the rain streaming off its brim, stood Augustus Halloran himself. Freya had not seen him out of the great house after dark in two years. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a face built for benevolence and eyes built for nothing of the kind. He looked at her dripping clothes. At her empty hands.

"Where have you been?"

"Nowhere, sir. I couldn't sleep. I walked the wall."

"In this?" His voice was mild. Mildness from Augustus Halloran was worse than shouting; shouting meant a man felt something, and feeling could be worked with. Mildness meant the ledger was already open in his mind and the entry already made. "Quill found the kitchen cloth missing. One loaf. The cook swears she counted them at supper."

The rain ran down Freya's neck. She could lie. She had lied before, small lies, the lies of a girl keeping her inner room sealed. But something in her — the same thing that had carried the bread through the gap in the wall — refused to come up now.

"I took it," she said. "There was a widow at the gate today with nothing."

Silence, except the rain. Quill's face did something complicated and settled into contempt.

Augustus Halloran sighed, a rich man's sigh, heavy with disappointment he had rehearsed. "Your father was the finest diver this company ever employed," he said. "And when the sea took him, I took his daughter in. Fed her. Schooled her. Gave her work fit for gentle hands." He stepped closer, and the lantern threw his shadow over her entire body. "And this is what grows in the soil I planted. Thieving sentiment. Do you know what happens to a company when the people in it start giving away what isn't theirs? It drowns, Freya. Everything drowns, eventually. The trick is deciding what floats."

He nodded to Quill. "Six strokes. The cane. In the yard, so the barracks hears it. Let the divers know that even my own ward answers to the rules."

"Sir—"

"You'll thank me when you're older," Augustus said, turning away. "Discipline is the only inheritance I have to give you."

* * *

The cane was bamboo, and Quill applied it with the thoroughness he brought to everything. Freya did not cry out. She had decided that in advance, standing in the yard with her palms against the wet post, rain still falling, the barred windows of the barracks facing her — and behind those windows, she knew, men watching. Divers. Widows' husbands. The drowned-not-yet-drowned.

She fixed her eyes on the nearest window and did not let her face break, because somewhere behind that glass was a man whose wife had been turned away unpaid, and if he saw the founder's ward weep for six strokes, what hope was there for any of them?

But it was not the cane that stayed with her, lying face-down on her cot afterward with the pain singing along her back.

It was the widow's words. *Words the clerks couldn't follow.* A tongue the company had never bothered to learn, spoken among men who went down into the water and sometimes did not come up, and whose debts were collected anyway.

*They say a lot of things,* the widow had said. *They say the sea took him quick.*

Five years ago, they had named her father drowned before the boats reached harbor.

Freya lay in the dark, listening to the rain, and for the first time in eight years she let the sealed room inside her open one inch — just enough to admit a single question, small and sharp as a seed pearl:

*What if the sea didn't take him?*

Out beyond the compound walls, in the fishing quarter, a dog began to bark — and then, faintly, through the rain, came the sound of hooves on the coast road, a single rider coming into port at an hour when no honest man traveled.
