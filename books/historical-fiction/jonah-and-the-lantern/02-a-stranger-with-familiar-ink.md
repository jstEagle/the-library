## 2. A Stranger with Familiar Ink

The rider did not come to the compound gate. Freya learned this the next morning not from any announcement but from the shape of the silence in the counting room — a silence with a hole in it, like a net that had caught something too large and torn.

Mr. Prasad was absent from his stool. The ledgers lay locked. Two of Augustus Halloran's trusted men, Kroll and the quiet one called only Whit, rode out before breakfast and came back at noon with mud to their knees and nothing in their saddlebags but anger.

Freya sorted pearls through all of it, head down, hands steady, the way she had been taught. *Never look at ledgers. Never name the drowned.* She added a third rule without speaking it aloud: *When men ride out angry and return angrier, become furniture.*

She was very good at being furniture. Eight years of practice.

It failed her at supper.

"You." Kroll stood in the doorway of the servants' hall, rain still dripping off his oilskin. His eyes found her the way a gull finds a fish — from height, without hurry. "The founder wants you in his study. Now."

Her spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. Around her, the other indentured workers developed an intense interest in their rice.

*What have I done?* was the old question, the one that rose automatically, and she let it rise and pass through her, because she had done nothing. Unless wanting to know something counted. Unless a single question, held overnight, could leak out through the skin.

The study smelled as it always did: lamp oil, salt rot, and Augustus Halloran's pipe tobacco, sweet enough to disguise what lay underneath — brine, money, and old fear. He sat behind the desk that had been her father's once, or so the story went, though Freya had never seen proof that Tomas Halloran had owned anything at all, not even his own debts.

"Sit."

She sat.

"A man came into port last night," Augustus said. "A clerk, by his look. Discharged from a trading house on the eastern coast, if his papers are honest, which they are not. He's been asking questions in the fishing quarter. Questions about—" and here the founder paused, and Freya watched him choose the word the way a surgeon chooses a blade— "family matters."

"I don't understand, sir."

"No," he said pleasantly. "You wouldn't."

He studied her for a long moment, and she performed the thing she did best: she emptied her face of everything and let it fill with mild, obedient stupidity. It was a face he had spent eight years shaping. He should have known its every seam. Perhaps he did. But power is lazy; it believes what it has paid to see.

"He asked after a girl," Augustus continued. "The daughter of a diver named Tomas Halloran. A dead man." He said *dead* with a small satisfaction, pressing on the bruise to check it still hurt. "I told him no such girl works my compound. I am now telling you that if such a girl were approached by such a man, she would report it to me. Instantly. There are laws about strangers stirring unrest among the divers, and there are terms in your indenture older than you are. Do you follow me?"

"Yes, sir."

"Good girl." He returned to his papers, dismissing her the way one dismisses weather. At the door, his voice caught her back like a hook in the hem. "Freya. Your father drowned a fool and a debtor. Whatever kindness you spend remembering him, spend it quietly."

*Yes, sir,* she said again, and walked back through the rain with her heart going like a hammer underwater.

They say a lot of things. They said he drowned quick. They said it before the boats reached harbor.

And now a stranger was asking after her — asking after her by name, by father, in a port where she had been raised to be nobody's daughter at all, only the compound's ward, its little ghost with clean hands.

She did not sleep. She listened instead, and past midnight she heard it: footsteps in the wet lane behind the drying sheds, unhurried, stopping once beneath her window as if a man were consulting his own memory of a map.

* * *

She went to him at dawn, because dawn belonged to the workers and not to the watchers.

The fishing quarter smelled of charcoal and low tide. Freya wore her plainest shawl and carried a basket she did not need, and she told herself the whole way that she was simply walking, that women walked, that there was no crime in it — while knowing, all the while, that she had already committed the only crime that mattered. She had decided to know.

The stranger sat under the awning of a tea stall that had no other customers, a thin man in a coat too good for the quarter and ruined anyway by travel, thirty perhaps, with ink stains on his fingers that no amount of sea air would explain. He saw her coming and did not smile, which she later understood was the first wise thing about him. Men who smiled at frightened girls wanted something from them quickly. This man only turned his teacup handle toward her, an offering, and waited until she sat.

"You shouldn't be here," she said.

"Neither should you, probably." His voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man who had learned to read documents aloud without reading them into trouble. "Miss Halloran."

"That isn't my name. Not legally. Not anywhere."

"It's the name your father gave you," he said. "Which is the only use I have for names."

The rain began again, soft and businesslike, drumming the awning. Freya looked at his stained fingers and felt the vertigo open under her feet like a hatch.

"Who are you?"

"Elias Voss. I kept books for the company. Eastern circuit, seven years." He said it without shame, which meant either that he felt none or that he had spent all of his somewhere she couldn't see. "Until I kept one book too many."

"I don't know anything about books."

"No," he agreed mildly. "You know about pearls. And bread." Something crossed her face before she could stop it, and he noted it the way he must have noted everything for seven years — precisely, without mercy. "Word travels oddly in this port, Miss Halloran. The widow Mallini talks. Her husband dove eleven seasons. She says the founder's ward slips bread to unpaid widows and pretends afterward she doesn't know why."

"She's mistaken."

"Probably." He reached inside his coat, slowly, watching her the whole time, and drew out an oilcloth packet tied with string. He set it on the table between them and did not touch it again. "Then you'll want to tell me I'm mistaken about this, too, and we can both go home dry."

She should have stood. She should have walked away and reported the stranger to Augustus Halloran exactly as instructed, instantly, and collected whatever small warmth obedience buys. That was the arithmetic she had lived by for eight years, and it had kept her fed and unbeaten more often than not.

Her hand untied the string.

There were six letters inside, worn soft at the folds, and even before she opened the first one she knew — the way you know a face across a crowded harbor — because the handwriting leapt off the page and took hold of her throat.

She had learned her letters from that handwriting. Every child in the compound had, in those years, though the company never admitted it: Tomas Halloran couldn't keep from teaching, chalk on slate, then charcoal on the backs of packing lists when slate ran short. Big patient capitals for the alphabet. Loops like fishhooks for the rest. He'd made a game of the letter J — *Jonah*, he'd say, drawing it fat and grinning, *Jonah gets swallowed and lives anyway, remember that, Freya-girl* — and here it was, that same fat Jonah, that same leftward lean, swimming across lines dated—

Dated.

Freya turned the first letter over so fast she nearly tore it.

*March 1893.*

Her father had drowned in October 1888. She knew the date the way she knew her own heartbeat; the whole compound had worn black for a week, and Augustus had wept at the memorial service with what everyone agreed was remarkable feeling, considering.

Four years, five months after the sea took him, Tomas Halloran had written — in his own unmistakable hand, in a fist gone slightly weaker, the fishhooks drooping now like tired things — *My Pearl. If this reaches you, do not grieve forward, grieve backward and be done with it. I am not where they say I am. I am not nowhere. Keep your eyes down and count everything. — Your Father, who is swallowed and living.*

The second letter was dated June 1894. The third, November of the same year. The last was two years old. After that, nothing — and the nothing had its own terrible weight, like a door closing slowly.

The tea stall noise receded. The rain receded. Freya existed for some seconds in a place with no floor at all.

Every certainty she owned was built on one foundation stone: *the sea took him*. Grief had been the one true thing in her life. She had hated the sea and needed it and arranged herself around its appetite like a plant growing toward a broken light. And grief, it turned out, was furniture placed in a room she had never been allowed to enter.

"You've seen them before?" Voss asked quietly. "His writing?"

"He taught me my letters." Her voice came out strange, level, very far away. "Where did you get these?"

"From him. Through intermediaries. A curing station up the coast — I won't say where, and you mustn't ask, not yet." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for the first time something urgent broke through the clerk's flat calm. "Miss Halloran. Listen to me. Forty-one men are recorded lost at sea by your father's company since 1888. Their insurance was claimed. Their debts were kept alive and collected anyway. And at least some of them — I believe your father among them — are alive, held where they can't contradict a ledger. I carried these letters three hundred miles. I'm a disgraced clerk with no standing and less money, and you're the only person in this world with a reason to act. So I need you to tell me, honestly, right now, whether you are the daughter of Tomas Halloran."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere down the lane a woman sang to a child, a diving song, the kind with a counting rhythm in it, one for the depth and two for the dark.

Freya looked at the letters. At the fat Jonah loops. At four years of *my father is dead* dissolving into *my father is counting days*.

If she said yes, she became someone. A daughter. A target. A thread Augustus Halloran would pull until she unraveled entirely — and Voss had already been seen asking after her; Kroll had already ridden out angry; the founder had already summoned her to his study and pressed on the bruise to see if it hurt.

If she said no, she stayed furniture. Safe. Silent. Fed.

"I don't know who you are," Freya Halloran said, loud enough for the empty stalls around them, clear enough to be repeated if anyone asked. "I don't know any Tomas Halloran. My father died before I could walk. Don't come near me again."

She picked up her basket, left the letters on the table — left them, God help her, with her fingers stiff as driftwood — and walked out into the rain.

Voss did not follow. He sat where he was, watching her go with an expression she refused to turn around and read.

She made it three streets, walking fast, blind, breath coming wrong. Then the lane bent toward the harbor, and the wind shifted, and she smelled the sea — the same sea that had supposedly taken him, lying to her for five years, lying in the voice of every wave — and her body simply decided without consulting her.

Freya turned. She ran, shawl flying, basket banging against her hip, back through the rain to the tea stall where the stranger still sat over his cold tea and his impossible letters, and she stood dripping in front of him with the ground of her whole life shifting under her feet, and said:

"Tell me where my father is."
