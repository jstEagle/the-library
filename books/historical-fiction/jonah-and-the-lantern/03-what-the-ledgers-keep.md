## 3. What the Ledgers Keep

Elias Voss had spent eleven years learning that numbers did not lie, but men made them lie beautifully.

The fisherman's hut smelled of tar, dried sprat, and rain coming through the thatch in slow gray threads. He had chosen it for its poverty — Augustus Halloran's hired men did not waste bribes on huts like this. The single oil lamp sat between him and the girl on an upturned crate, and he kept his hands flat on the table the way a man does when he is trying not to reach for something and fail.

Freya Halloran had come through the rain behind him without a word, and she had not spoken since. She sat across from him with her spine very straight and her hands folded like a woman at a funeral, which, Elias thought, was close enough to what this was.

"You said letters," she said finally. "In your note. You wrote *letters*. Plural."

"I did."

"And you said my father wrote them."

"He wrote them. I watched him write one of them." Elias let that sit a moment. "The night before the *Merrow* went out. March of '89."

"The *Merrow* sank in March of '89. Everyone on her was lost. It's carved somewhere, I expect, if you carve things for drowned men who owe money."

Her voice stayed level. That frightened him more than weeping would have. He had seen what happened to people who learned to keep their voices level inside the Halloran compound; they were the ones who lasted, and lasting was its own kind of wound.

"Before I show you anything," Elias said, "I want you to understand what I am, so you can weigh what I say accordingly. I was a clerk. Eleven years. I kept the company's books — the real ones, not the ones shown to insurers or magistrates. I falsified entries because Augustus Halloran told me to, and I took wages for it, and I told myself it was arithmetic, that I only moved numbers and never touched a man. Then I checked the arithmetic against names, and found out the numbers had been touching men all along. So weigh me how you like. But weigh me."

Freya's eyes went to his hands, then back to his face. "Show me."

He drew the packet from inside his coat — oilcloth wrapped twice, tied with cord, warm from his body. Inside were three notebooks, cheap ruled paper gone soft at the edges, and beneath them a folded sheet covered in columns. He laid the sheet flat first.

"Forty-one," he said. "Forty-one men recorded as lost at sea between 1888 and this year. Drowned, swept overboard, taken by weather. Every loss certified by the company, every insurance claim paid out to Halloran Trading as holder of the policies. And here—" his finger moved down the margin, where smaller figures ran in pencil, "—here are their debts. Boat levies, advance payments, gear bonds. Collected anyway. From their families. After death."

"Collected from—"

"From widows. From sons. A dead diver's debt dies with him under the law, Miss Halloran, unless someone keeps collecting quietly and never enters it. Which is what the ledger does. Forty-one men died twice. Once in the water, once on paper, and the second death was worth more than the first."

The lamp guttered in a draft. Neither of them moved to shield it.

"Say the figure," Freya said. "You've been waiting to say it since the door."

"Eleven thousand, six hundred rupees in claims. Better than two thousand more in collections that never existed officially. Enough to build the new warehouse. Which you have seen built."

She looked at the column of names. He watched her finger stop moving down it and realized she had stopped at a name she knew. She didn't say whose. Some silences, he was learning, were not empty.

* * *

"Now the part you won't believe," Elias said, "and shouldn't, until you see it."

He opened the first notebook. Pages of entries in his own cramped hand — dates, vessels, disbursements. But threaded through them, in a different ink entirely, ran other lines: short notes, initials, small private shorthand.

"Tomas Halloran wasn't lost on the *Merrow*," Elias said. "He wasn't on her. Three days before she sailed, Augustus summoned him to the office after hours. Tomas had been asking questions about the ledgers — he could read figures better than anyone gave a pearl diver credit for, and he'd noticed the same name appearing as both creditor and claimant. He came to me that night, furious, wanting copies. I gave him none. I was too much of a coward to give him more than a drink and a warning. Two weeks later the company announced he was drowned off Mannar with four others. There was no storm that fortnight. I checked the weather logs myself, later, when I began checking everything."

"Then he is dead," Freya said. Her voice was still level. Her knuckles were not. "Murdered, perhaps. But dead."

"Or held. A man who can read your books is worth more alive than dead, if he can be kept somewhere the books can't hear him." Elias turned to a page near the middle of the notebook and rotated it toward her. "There is a curing station north of Mullaitivu. Company property, nominally for fish, actually for storage — and for men. Augustus has used it before for divers who knew too much and couldn't be drowned without questions. They work, they're fed, they don't leave. On the company's books the station draws a monthly allowance for *provisions, sundry labor*. Sundry labor, singular, ongoing since June of 1889."

"That proves a prisoner, not my father."

"It proves nothing. I agree. Which is why there is this."

He turned the page fully toward the light. In the margin beside each entry for the station, small marks recurred — a short stroke crossed by two shorter ones, slanted, like a bird's footprint in snow.

"Do you know it?"

Freya had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice came from somewhere far below its previous floor.

"It's his mark. He wouldn't sign his name to anything — he said a signature was something for other people to forge, but a mark you make crooked on purpose stays yours. He taught me my letters and made me practice writing my name, and then he showed me his mark and said *this is the only word I trust*." She looked up. "Why would his mark be in your notebook?"

"Because Tomas kept his own count," Elias said. "After I refused him copies, he started making marks wherever he could — on crates he packed, on chits, on anything that passed through his hands. I found them later and understood: he was leaving a trail in the only language the company never read, because the company never imagined a diver would write. The mark appears on chits signed at the curing station. Provisions drawn for sundry labor. Signed with a mark that means *I am alive and I know it counts*."

Freya stood up so fast the crate scraped. She walked to the hut's one window, where rain silvered the dark, and stood with her back to him.

"If you are lying," she said, "you have chosen the one lie that would work on me, and I will never forgive it. Not hate it. Forgive it — no, I take that back. I'll do worse than hate it."

"I know. I've thought about that every day since I decided to come."

"Why?" Still facing the window. "Why throw away eleven years for a dead man's daughter? Why not just run?"

Elias turned the ledger sheet over and considered the blank back, all that clean nothing.

"Because I balanced the books," he said. "Every entry, every transfer, I entered it clean and true into my head, and my head has been running the totals for years and they do not balance. There is a deficit. It has faces. Forty-one of them, plus a man in a hut who asked me for help and got a drink instead." He paused. "And because when I checked the collection records against the parish rolls, I found a widow named Priyanka who was owed her husband's last wage and never paid, and a girl sorting pearls by lamplight who slipped that widow bread at the gate and took a beating for it. I was watching from the office window. I thought: *there's someone in that compound keeping honest books after all.*"

Freya turned around. Her eyes were wet, furious, alive — grief and hope fighting for the same ground, neither conceding.

"My father is alive," she said. It was not a question and not quite a belief. It was a blade being tested against a thumb.

"He may be. I believe he is. Belief isn't proof, Miss Halloran. Proof is what we need, and proof is scattered — in these books, in the memories of forty-one families, in marks on chits in a storehouse that Augustus will burn the moment he smells inquiry. We have weeks. Perhaps less."

"We." She came back to the table and put both hands flat on the notebooks, claiming them, or claiming him. "You said *we*."

"I did. Presumptuously."

"Yes," Freya Halloran said, and for the first time all night something moved through her voice that was almost — almost — the opposite of grief. "Presumptuously. Then listen, clerk. If my father signs his mark on those chits, he signs it knowing someone someday would read it. He left a trail. I'm going to walk it backward to the door of that station, and anyone who stands on it — founder or hired man — is going to wish he hadn't. What do we need first?"

Elias closed his hand around the cold lump of fear in his chest and found, underneath it, something that felt disgracefully like hope.

"Witnesses," he said. "The living kind. And there's an old diver named Sefu the compound taught you never to speak to."

Freya was already pulling her wet shawl back around her shoulders.

"Then it's time," she said, "I learned the language they kept from me."
