## 4. The Divers' Tongue

The widows lived on the seaward side of the compound wall, in a strip of lane the company had never paved because the company had never needed anything from them except grief. Freya told herself she was going to the fish market. She took the long way, which happened to pass the lane, which happened to pass the door of Mariamma, whose husband's boat had come back empty three years ago with the nets stowed and the man not in them.

Freya had been taught not to see these doors. She had been taught so well that seeing them now felt like walking with her eyes open underwater — everything too bright, too near, the pressure of it all against her chest.

She knocked.

Mariamma opened the door halfway, the chain still on it, and looked at Freya the way you'd look at weather about to turn bad. "Halloran girl."

"I want to ask you something."

"Company asks nothing. Company tells." But the chain came off, and Freya understood that the door had not opened for her. It had opened for whatever the company wanted, and the wanting of it was enough.

Inside there was one room, one lamp, one photograph of a man squinting into sun he no longer walked under. Freya stood because sitting felt like presumption.

"Your husband," she said. "Kesavan. When they declared him drowned, did anyone come to count him? Did anyone from the company come to look?"

Mariamma's face did something complicated and then went very still, the way water goes still over something deep. "Why does the Halloran girl ask what the Halloran books already know?"

"Because I think the books lie."

The lamp hissed. Outside, a dog turned over some rubbish. Freya counted four breaths before Mariamma spoke again, and when she did, it was in the diving pidgin — that clipped, salt-heavy tongue the crews used on the luggers, half Tamil, half Arabic, half things Freya had never been taught, a language the company called *kitchen talk* and never bothered to learn beyond *faster* and *enough* and *hold your breath*.

She said six words in it. Freya caught maybe two.

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Mariamma said, switching back to English with a bitterness that sharpened every consonant, "that we have been saying it to each other for years. That the counting is wrong. That men go down whole and come up as paper. You want to know if we suspected?" She laughed, short and terrible. "Girl, we have suspected since before you could read. We have a word for it. A whole word, just for a man the company says is drowned but nobody saw drown. Do you understand? We needed a *word* for it. That is how often it happens."

Freya stood very straight, because if she didn't she would sit down on the floor of this stranger's house and put her head in her hands. "Then why has no one said anything? To a magistrate, to anyone—"

"Said it to who? In what language would we say it, that they would believe us? In their courts they speak English. In their books they speak numbers. And we—" she gestured at the room, the lamp, the photograph, "—we speak kitchen talk. Who writes down kitchen talk?"

"I will," Freya said. "I'm learning to write it down."

Mariamma looked at her for a long moment, and Freya watched her weigh the girl in front of her against the compound behind her, and could not tell which side of the scale came up heavier. In the end she said only: "Come back when you bring more than words. Come back when you bring someone they can't ignore."

It was not an alliance. But it was not a closed door either.

* * *

Sefu kept a hut at the end of the divers' jetty, close enough to the water that at high tide you could feel the boards breathe. He had been diving since before Augustus Halloran owned his first lugger, and his chest was a map of the trade: the scar where a shell knife had opened him, the set shoulder that never sat right again after the year the pumps failed. He was mending a net when Freya found him, and he let her stand there a full five minutes before acknowledging her existence.

"You're the ghost's daughter," he said, not looking up.

The word went through her like cold water through cloth. "You knew my father."

"Everyone knew Tomas. Man talked to fish. Fish probably talked back." His fingers worked the twine without hurrying. "He dove deeper than sense allowed and came up laughing. Your father thought the sea owed him a welcome. The sea doesn't owe welcomes. That's the first thing diving teaches. The second thing—" now he looked up, and his eyes were pale as old pearl, "—is that the dangerous animal isn't in the water."

"The company," Freya said.

"The company," Sefu agreed, and went back to his net.

She should have left then. Everything she had been raised inside told her to leave — the ledgers, the silences, the beating she still felt in her ribs on cold mornings. Instead she sat down on the salt-bleached planks across from him and said, "Elias Voss kept true books. Forty-one men marked lost who weren't lost. My father might be one of them."

The netting stopped.

For a long time Sefu said nothing, and Freya learned that his silence was not like hers. Hers had been trained into her, worn smooth like a stone carried in a pocket until it fit the palm exactly. His was chosen, load-bearing, the silence of a man deciding how much weight a thing could hold.

"Voss," he said finally. "The clerk they broke. I remember him. Soft hands, honest eyes — worst combination on this coast." He tied off the net and folded his hands over it. "Say it again. The number."

"Forty-one."

"And the girl comes to the old diver with a dead man's arithmetic and expects—" he stopped himself. Freya realized with a small shock that it was pity that had stopped him, or something adjacent to it; he had seen her flinch when she said the number, had watched her hold herself together by main force, and he was not going to be the one to shake her.

"They need testimony," she said. "Statements. Sworn ones. Names, dates, what was seen. I've started asking. Mariamma will talk, eventually. There are others. But they won't talk to me unless—" she faltered, hating the falter, "unless someone vouches. Unless someone they trust stands next to me and says the Halloran girl isn't a trap."

"And are you a trap?"

"Yes," Freya said. "That's exactly what I am. I'm a Halloran ward with compound manners and a ledger education, and any sensible person would assume Augustus sent me to find out who's still angry enough to testify. I can't prove I'm not. All I can tell you is that my ribs know what his discipline costs, and that I haven't slept since a stranger showed me my father's handwriting on letters dated after his death, and that I am so tired of being silent that silence has started to taste like his side of the wall."

The tide breathed under the jetty. Somewhere down the beach children were arguing about a crab.

Sefu began, slowly, to laugh. It was not a warm laugh, but it was not a cruel one either; it was the laugh of a man recognizing something.

"You rehearsed that," he said.

"On the walk here. Twice."

"Good. A diver checks his gear before going down. You checked yours." He pushed the finished net aside and looked at her properly for the first time, and Freya had the strange sensation of being measured not as a Halloran but as a hull — assessed for soundness, for whether she'd hold under pressure. "Here is what your lessons never taught you, ghost's daughter, so listen once. Silence protected no one. Twenty years I kept quiet, and do you know what my silence bought? It bought the choosing. It decided *who* got hurt. Every year I stayed quiet, the company chose which family ate and which family buried an empty box, and my silence was their permission. Silence isn't safety. Silence is just pain with better manners."

"Then why did you stay silent?"

"Because alone, a voice is just a noise they can drown." He tapped the net between them. "Together, it's a record. So. If Voss wants statements, he'll get statements. Not because of you. Because forty-one is a number I've been carrying in my chest for twenty years and I am tired, girl. I am tired the way the sea is tired of holding up boats."

He held out his hand across the net, palm open, the old diver's callus like a coin embedded in it.

Freya took it. His grip was dry and strong and utterly without ceremony, and something in her chest — some clenched and guarded thing she had not known she was carrying — loosened by a degree. It was such a small transaction. A hand. A name given freely instead of extracted. She had crossed the compound wall that morning expecting to be the one offering something, and instead she was being offered the first belonging of her life by an old man mending nets, and it cost her nothing except the truth.

Which, she was beginning to understand, was the only currency out here that held its value.

"Tomorrow," Sefu said, releasing her hand. "You come back tomorrow, and you come with paper. Real paper, and ink, and we write down the first name together — mine. Let them come for me first. At my age, drowning would be redundant."

He grinned when he said it, and Freya found, absurdly, that she was grinning back.

* * *

They worked for three evenings in the jetty hut with the lamp trimmed low and the shutters angled against watchers. Word traveled the way it always traveled in the diving community — not spoken outright but folded into other conversations, tucked inside market talk and tide talk, so that by the fourth evening there were two retired divers waiting on the bench outside and by the fifth there were five, and Sefu vetted each one in pidgin while Freya wrote, learning the language the way you learn swimming: by being thrown in.

She learned that *kadhalu-kar* meant a man taken by the deep but unproven. She learned that the widows had passed suspicions between houses for years in phrases that sounded like recipes, like complaints about weather, like nothing. She learned that Rajendran, the harbormaster's boatman, had ferried a crate of personal effects off a lugger the night before its crew was officially lost, and had never understood until now why the timing had gnawed at him. She learned that everyone had a piece, and no one had ever been asked for the whole.

On the fifth evening, writing Sefu's account in her careful ledger-trained hand while he spoke it slow and deliberate, she felt something she had no pidgin word for either: eleven voices in a room that had been built for silence, and hers among them, and none of them breaking.

It was near midnight when the knock came — soft, spaced, deliberate. Sefu's hand went to the knife under his mat. Then came the second knock, and Freya was already moving, because she knew that rhythm; Elias had used it at the fisherman's hut. She opened the door on rain and lamplight and Elias Voss, soaked through, hat gone, one arm clamped against his ribs in a way that made her stomach drop before he said a single word.

"I rode to the curing station," he said. "Freya — they burned it ahead of me. The records, the quarters, all of it. And there's worse." He looked past her at Sefu, at the papers spread across the floor, at the little congregation of witnesses' names drying in ink, and his honest soft-handed face went white. "Augustus knows. Someone told him we were asking."
