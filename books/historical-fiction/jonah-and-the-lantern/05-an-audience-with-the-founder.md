## 5. An Audience with the Founder

The summons came at dawn, delivered by Marius, the compound's head houseman, who had never once in eleven years spoken to Freya as if she were more than furniture that could walk.

"The master will see you in his study. After the nine o'clock bell. Wear the grey dress."

*The master.* Not *your uncle*, not even *Mr. Halloran*. The word choice was a message, and Freya read it twice before Marius finished turning away. She owned two dresses worth the name; the grey was the one without a future, the one she wore when she was meant to disappear into walls. He wanted her small today. That told her something too.

She spent the hours until the bell doing what she always did — sorting pearls at the grading table, holding each one to the lamp and reading its skin for flaws the way her father had taught her before he went into the water for the last time. But her hands worked while her mind ran ahead of her, down every road this summons might take. Had someone seen her slip bread to Nalini Widow-Rajah? No — that had been weeks ago, and Augustus Halloran did not summon people for charity. Had a letter come? Had Elias Voss said something, given up her name under questioning? She had covered for his absence at the dock, forged the entry in the receiving book with a hand she'd practiced from her father's old lesson pages, and no one had questioned it. No one had questioned anything.

Which was, she understood now, exactly what should frighten her.

The nine o'clock bell rang across the compound, brass and flat. Freya put down her tweezers, folded her hands, and walked to the study door knowing that whatever happened on the other side of it, she would need to be two people at once: the obedient ward who had learned silence like an alphabet, and the girl who had followed a stranger into the rain.

She knocked.

"Come."

Augustus Halloran's study smelled of camphor and ink and, beneath both, the sweet brine of pearl meat that no amount of scrubbing ever lifted from the wood. He sat behind a desk built from a ship's keel — he liked to tell visitors it had carried his father out from Cork — with the morning light behind him so that whoever entered stood half-blinded while he watched from shadow. She had known the trick since childhood. It did not stop working on her anyway.

"Freya." Her name in his mouth sounded like an item being checked off an inventory. "Sit."

She sat. The chair across from the desk was lower than his by design; she'd only realized that years later, after sitting in a hundred other chairs and wondering why hers had always made her feel like a petitioner. Today she kept her spine straight and let the chair do its worst.

"You've been well?" he asked.

"Well enough, sir."

"The ledgers agree." He smiled faintly, as though the ledgers were witnesses he trusted more than people, which they were. "You're punctual. You don't gossip. You don't ask questions. In eleven years you have cost me nothing but your keep." He opened a drawer and laid something on the desk, face down, his palm flat over it. "I find I value that. More than you know."

Freya looked at his hand and not the object beneath it. A lifetime in this house had taught her that Augustus offered things in the order he wanted them refused, so that by the time he reached the thing he truly wished to give, refusal felt impossible.

"Do you know why the divers trust no one, child?"

The question caught her sideways. She had prepared for accusation, for the trap springing shut. Not for philosophy.

"Because they're superstitious, sir," she said, giving him the company catechism. "They believe the sea keeps accounts."

"And does it?" His eyes were pale grey, her own eyes, everyone remarked on it, the orphan and the founder, such a family resemblance, God's little joke. "I'll tell you what the sea keeps. It keeps what we give it. A man goes down with a debt on his back and doesn't come up, the sea has done my arithmetic for me. Clean. Final. No widow can argue with the sea." He turned his palm over. Beneath it lay a folded paper, heavy stock, sealed. "But lately the arithmetic is being checked. Someone is asking the widows questions. Someone with a clerk's handwriting and a grudge and, I'm told, a talent for making frightened people feel brave."

Freya kept her face still the way she'd once kept her body still through a beating — by going somewhere else, some small interior room where the pain hadn't followed. *He knows about Elias. He doesn't know about me.*

"That sounds troublesome, sir."

"It sounds like theft." He leaned forward into the light, and now she could see him plainly: seventy years old and hard as shell, the pearl-grey eyes flat as nacre, beautiful and dead. "Forty years I built this coast, Freya. Before me it was chaos — men diving on credit, dying on credit, widows burning their huts for insurance money, no law but the monsoon. I brought order. Order requires that certain sums arrive when they are expected and certain men stay where they are put. This clerk — Voss, his name is Voss — thinks that because he copied the numbers, he owns them." He tapped the sealed paper. "Do you know what this is?"

"No, sir."

"Your freedom."

The word moved through her like cold water through a hull. She let nothing show. *Not yet. Let him spend it.*

"An indenture is a debt," Augustus said, "and I am in a position to cancel debts. Full papers. Your passage wherever you like — Colombo, London, the moon. A settlement besides. Enough that no Halloran would ever need to sort another man's pearls." He slid the paper an inch toward her, then stopped, keeping two fingers on it. "All of it, for one small service. You have ears, child, and the divers' quarter forgets you're in the room. I want to know who speaks to Voss. Who gathers. Which of the old divers — Sefu, I'd wager, Sefu always had notions above his station — which of them are agitating. Names, Freya. That's all. Names, and the rest of your life begins."

There it was. She made herself look at the seal — dark red wax, unbroken, the Halloran anchor stamped fat in its center — and she made herself feel the pull of it honestly, because lying to yourself first was the only reliable way to lie to Augustus Halloran. Freedom. Papers with her name spelled right, witnessed, legal, portable. Eleven years of being furniture that could walk, ended for the price of other people's names.

And underneath the temptation, fouler and stronger, was the seduction he'd hidden inside the offer: *he needed her*. Augustus Halloran, who owned the boats and the banks and the magistrate's Christmas dinner, needed a seventeen-year-old ward to sit in rooms and remember things. Power, she discovered, tasted sweetest exactly there, in the moment of being needed by the man who had never needed anyone. And it rotted sweetest there too. She could feel the rot already, like meat gone off under ice — because the moment she took his papers, every kindness she'd ever done became currency he thought he'd bought, and every friend she made became merchandise.

He was watching her weigh it. He thought he knew what she would choose. Everyone always thought that.

"I can try, sir," Freya said. "The divers won't talk to me directly. But they talk around me."

"They will." He pushed the paper all the way across the desk, and his fingers left it, and something in the room changed — some current completing itself. "You have a fortnight. Bring me names and this is yours, signed and witnessed. Bring me nothing—" he smiled, and it was almost fond, which was the most frightening thing yet, "—and we'll discuss what becomes of girls whose fathers drowned owing the company money. Debts pass down, Freya. Yours died quiet. Keep it that way."

She took the paper. Her hand did not shake, and she noted that with a kind of clinical surprise, the way she noted the flaw in a pearl.

"Thank you, sir."

"One more thing." He had turned back toward the window, dismissing her, but paused with his glass of water halfway raised. "Voss disappeared from the docks ten days ago. If you hear anything — anything at all — about where a man might go when he wants not to be found, I'd consider it part of the same service."

"Yes, sir."

She rose, curtsied the precise depth the house required, and walked to the door with steps that measured themselves so they would neither hurry nor drag. Behind her she could feel his attention like sun through a magnifying glass, and she let it find nothing but the obedient ward, the girl who sorted pearls and asked no questions, walking away with her freedom in her hand and her soul already sold — or so he believed.

The door closed behind her. The corridor stretched long and dim toward the courtyard, and halfway along it Freya stopped, pressed the sealed paper against her breastbone, and breathed.

She had it. His own signature, his own seal, his own terms in writing — *report on agitators among the divers* — proof of exactly the kind of bargain Ruth Adeyemi would want entered into a deposition. And he had told her the rest with his silences: he knew of Elias, he feared gathering testimony, he counted forty years of clean arithmetic. Every hour she stayed inside these walls now was an hour spent inside the enemy's ledger, learning what it kept.

She was no longer the company's ward. She was its double agent, and its home was her cover.

Then the sweetness curdled, fast and complete, as she understood what the role demanded: she would sit at Sefu's fire and eat his fish and listen to his stories, and all the while Augustus would believe she was collecting him like shells off a beach. One misplaced word in the wrong ear — one suspicion in Sefu's sharp old eyes — and she would lose the only family that had ever chosen her, or destroy them with a smile.

From the courtyard came the sound of the diving crews returning, bare feet on coral stone, the pidgin calls bouncing off the walls. Among them, unmistakably, Sefu's laugh, deep as an anchor chain running out.

Freya folded the papers into the lining of the grey dress and went to meet them — smiling, obedient, armed — and found, standing apart from the laughing crews near the compound gate, mud-spattered, one arm bound in a blood-soaked sling, leaning against the wall as though the wall were the only thing holding him upright:

Elias Voss.
