## 6. Fire on the Cure Station Road

Elias had been a clerk for eleven years, and in eleven years he had never once done anything that required a horse ridden hard. The animal beneath him now — a borrowed bay with salt-crusted nostrils and an opinion about it — was teaching him quickly that courage and competence were different animals entirely. His thighs ached. His hands were raw from the reins. The road south from the port unspooled ahead of him through scrub and salt pan, flat as a ledger line, and he rode it the way he imagined a man rode toward his own audit: dreading every figure he might find.

The curing station at Vettukadu sat forty miles down the coast, a place Elias knew only as a column of entries. *Freight, dried fish, company stores — Halloran Trading Co.* For five years those entries had crossed his desk without meaning anything, the way most entries did, and for five years he had copied them faithfully into books that lied by omission. Somewhere past that flat horizon was a station where the company sent things it did not want counted. He had come to believe — had *needed* to believe — that one of those things answered to the name Tomas Halloran.

The fisherman who'd told them had been paid in tobacco and trust, which was to say barely paid at all. *There is a man there,* the old diver had said, in the divers' tongue that Sefu was slowly teaching Freya, word by stubborn word. *Old. Half his wits gone, they say. But when the supply boats come, he stands at the rail and counts the men aboard in English. A clerk's habit. Nobody teaches a dying man to count cargo.*

A clerk counts cargo. Elias had spent a decade among men who thought numbers were neutral. He should have known better. Numbers were never neutral; they were just quiet about whose side they took.

He reached the turnoff at midmorning, left the road, and walked the last two miles through the palmyra scrub with the reins wrapped around his fist, leading the bay. The station resolved out of the heat shimmer gradually: a long low shed roofed with palm thatch, drying racks in crooked rows, a jetty of blackened pilings reaching into water the color of old glass. Smoke rose thin and domestic from a cookhouse chimney. It looked like nothing. That was the genius of places like this — Augustus Halloran had always understood that the best hiding place for a crime was a landscape too boring to look at twice.

Elias crouched in the scrub and watched for an hour, because caution was the one professional habit he could not shake. Four men visible. Two on the jetty mending nets, badly. One at the racks. One walking the perimeter in the unhurried pattern of a man who had been told to watch rather than told what he was watching for. No cages. No barred windows. But also no boat at the jetty, and a station you could not leave except by water was its own kind of cell.

He waited until the perimeter man's circuit took him behind the shed, then crossed the open ground fast and low, heart hammering in his ears like a fist on a door. The drying shed's near end stood open, stacked with crates of coarse salt, and Elias slipped into the shadow between them and let his breathing settle. Through the gap between two crates he could see the length of the shed: racks of split fish, the sweet-rot reek of cure, and at the far end —

At the far end, a partition. Rough planks, recently nailed, with a door of the same timber and a hasp meant for livestock. And sitting against the partition wall, close enough to touch it through the wood, an old wooden stool. On the stool, a slate. On the slate, chalk columns in a cramped, upright hand.

Elias knew that hand.

He had spent five years forging summaries of it, rounding off its figures, feeding its demands upward to a man who read everything and trusted nothing. He had mocked it, resented it, memorized it. Those careful vertical strokes. The way the fives were made like sevens with a crossbar added after, a habit from some schoolroom decades dead.

Tomas Halloran was keeping accounts on a slate, in captivity, thirty feet away.

Elias's legs nearly gave. Not fear — though fear was there, patient as tide — but the sheer vertigo of it. Five years of ledgers, and here was the sum of all of them sitting ten steps off, alive, counting something through a plank wall because a counting man's mind will make order anywhere, even inside a cage.

Then he smelled the kerosene.

It came on the wind in a slow wave, and with it the sound of hooves — many more than four horses' worth. Elias pressed to the crack in the boards and saw riders coming down the coastal track, six of them, moving without urgency, which was worse than speed. Urgency could be outrun. Deliberateness could not. At their head rode Merren Caul, the compound's overseer, a man Elias had watched sign receipts for goods no ship ever carried, and under Caul's arm was a tin that gleamed where it shouldn't.

"Clear the racks," Caul said. His voice carried in the flat morning air the way voices carry when everyone present already knows the shape of the day. "Jetty end first. Then the shed."

One of the net-menders straightened, confused. "The cure's good stock, sir—"

"The stock goes to the second boat. The *paper* stays." Caul dismounted, unscrewed the tin with the ease of long practice, and began walking the shed's length, pouring. Kerosene sheeted off the rack supports and soaked into twenty years of dry palm thatch. "Company's reorganizing," he added, to no one in particular. "Vettukadu's being closed."

Elias understood then, with the cold clarity that comes only to men who have finally stopped lying to themselves, what he was watching. Not concealment. Erasure. Augustus wasn't trying to hide the truth anymore; he was deleting it, page by page, station by station, faster than a disgraced clerk and a pearl-sorting girl could gather it up. Every record at Vettkadu — manifests, ration tallies, the daily logs that would show a prisoner fed year after year, proof written in the plainest ink on earth that a drowned man had been kept alive — was about to become smoke.

And the man behind the partition.

Elias moved before he decided to move. Later he would not be able to explain it any better than that: his body simply refused the arithmetic of self-preservation. He came out of the crate shadows at a crouch, crossed the aisle, and put his mouth to the seam of the plank door.

"Tomas," he whispered. "Halloran. It's Voss. From the office."

Silence. Then, from within, a voice worn down to gravel: "Voss. They've come to burn me with my figures, have they?"

"They've come to burn your figures. There's a horse in the scrub, north side. Can you run?"

A dry sound that might have been laughter. "I haven't run since the year you started shaving."

The kerosene smell thickened. Outside, someone struck a match, and the whole world narrowed to the small scratch of it.

"I'll get you out," Elias said, and heard how young he sounded, how absurd. "I swear it on the true books."

"Boy." The gravel softened by a fraction. "Get the *books* out. I'm an old man who's already drowned once. You're evidence with legs. Go be evidence."

The far end of the shed caught with a sound like a held breath released. Flame ran up the thatch in a bright sheet, and the light through the cracks turned gold, then orange, then the color of everything ending. Elias pressed his forehead once against the plank — the closest thing to a promise he could make with a wall between them — and ran.

He was forty yards into the scrub when the shouting started. He did not look back, which saved his life, because looking back costs speed, and speed was suddenly the only currency he had. A shot cracked the air somewhere behind him and went high, or wide — panic shooting, shooting at noise. The second shot did not go high.

It took him across the outside of his left thigh, a white-hot punch that spun him half around and put him down into the palmyra brush with the taste of copper and dust filling his mouth. He heard hoofbeats fanning out along the scrub's edge, methodical, and understood with terrible clarity that they were not chasing him the way angry men chase. They were beating a field. He was a bird with nowhere to rise.

So he did the only thing eleven years of clerking had prepared him for. He went still, went small, and started keeping track.

*Two riders north. One holding at the treeline. Caul still at the shed — I can hear him giving orders about the boats. Wind from the southeast, pushing fire inland, away from the partition. The partition is stone-based. Stone does not burn.*

That last figure he held onto the way a drowning man holds a spar. The partition wall sat on a stone footing. Fire would take the roof and racks and leave the little room standing, or mostly standing. Tomas Halloran had survived five years of Augustus's mercy. He might survive an afternoon of Augustus's housekeeping. It was not hope, exactly. It was a probability entered against long odds, and Elias had built his whole ruined career on probabilities exactly like it.

He crawled. North by inches, dragging the leg that wanted very much to be screamed about, biting his sleeve against the noise of it. The riders passed twice, close enough that he could hear the horses blowing, and both times he lay in the brush shadow and matched his breathing to the wind. When the sun stood highest he reached the bay, hauled himself into the saddle with a sound he was not proud of, and pointed the animal at the sea wind and home.

Behind him, Vettukadu burned — clean, thorough, deniable. A storage fire. Tragic loss of stock. He could write the report himself; he had written a hundred like it. That was the thing about Augustus Halloran that Elias had never fully understood until this moment, riding with blood filling his boot: the man did not destroy evidence out of fear. He destroyed it out of *bookkeeping*. Every lie needed balancing, and balance, in Augustus's world, was maintained by fire as readily as by ink.

They were not racing to find proof anymore. They were racing a man who was burning proof faster than testimony could be sworn. Which meant the next move was not Elias's leg, or his contacts, or his careful lists.

It was the girl's. It had to happen fast, and it had to happen in writing, in sworn words, in a form fire could not eat.

* * *

Freya felt the absence before anyone reported it.

She had learned the compound's rhythms the way she had learned lamplight — by living inside them — and on Thursday evening the rhythms were wrong. Cookhouse talk too loud, covering a hole. The stable boy walking the bay mare twice her usual distance, cooling a horse that had been ridden to ruin. And Merren Caul back from the south a full day early, standing in the yard with kerosene ghosting off his coat, saying to no one in particular that there'd been a fire at Vettukadu, terrible thing, all them records gone, and smiling while he said it like a man reciting lines he'd rehearsed on horseback.

Elias was supposed to meet her at the fish racks at dusk. Dusk came. So did full dark. So did Sefu, materializing beside her in the way old divers could, silent as displaced water.

"He is hurt," Sefu said. "A fisher family on the north spit has him. Leg. He walks crooked but he lives. He sends a message." Sefu's eyes caught the lantern light. "He says: *the fire took the paper. Whatever we keep from now on, we keep where fire can't reach it. And until he can walk, no one must know you ever had a reason to ride south.*"

Freya stood very still. Around her the compound went on with its ordinary noises — the winch groaning, a diver singing low in the Tamil quarter, the sea doing what the sea always did. In three days she would sit across from Augustus Halloran at his own table, wearing the face he paid for, and report on agitators he suspected she was becoming.

"Elias says also," Sefu added, quieter, "that you should decide tonight what kind of person you are. Because tomorrow requires knowing."

Freya thought of the ledgers she had been taught never to read. She thought of her father's fives, drawn like sevens with a crossbar after — she had not seen them in five years and had recognized them in an instant, the way you recognize your own name spoken in a crowd. She thought about the difference between slipping bread to a widow and what was being asked of her now.

Bread was kindness. This would be a crime. The distinction mattered to her, and she noticed herself noticing it, and understood that noticing was the last luxury she could afford.

At midnight she let herself into the records room with the ward's key Augustus had given her himself — his first mistake dressed as his first kindness — and found the shipping log for Thursday. Caul's party had returned early, yes, but the log showed nothing, because Caul filed his own movements and Caul answered to no one. There was a blank where a trip south should have been.

Blanks, she had learned from watching Elias work, were simply entries waiting to be made.

In her best imitation of the duty clerk's cramped slant — she had been sorting pearls by lamplight since childhood, and a girl who could grade a pearl's flaw by eye could certainly counterfeit a drunkard's handwriting — she wrote: *Thurs. — E.V., courier run south re: cured stock inventory, per Mr. C.'s instruction, ret. Fri. a.m. No irregularity noted.* Then she signed it with initials that belonged to a man who owed her father's ghost a debt he didn't yet know the size of, blotted it, closed the book, and stood there in the lamplight listening to her own heartbeat.

Her hands were steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have.

She had committed her first forgery, and it had taken less than a minute, and the book had accepted it without complaint, the way all the company's books accepted everything — quietly, permanently, without caring whose life balanced against the entry. *So this is what it costs*, she thought. *This is the price of names. You pay in ink instead of bread, and the bread was only practice.*

Sefu was waiting in the alley when she slipped out. He read her face in one glance, the way he read weather.

"Well?"

"It's done," Freya said. "He was never south. As far as these walls are concerned, Elias Voss doesn't exist."

Sefu nodded slowly, and something moved through his expression that she had never seen there before — not approval exactly. Recognition. One survivor marking another.

"Good," he said. "Now sleep, girl. Because Sunday you dine with the founder of all this—" he gestured at the compound, the harbor, the sleeping town where men drowned on paper and lived in cages "—and Sunday night, you will have to tell us what his face did when he spoke your father's name."

Freya looked back once at the dark window of the records room, where her false entry lay drying in the true books' company, honest lies among dishonest truths.

Sunday, she would sit across from the man who had signed her father's death. And for the first time in her life, she would bring something to the table besides obedience.

She would bring a witness.
