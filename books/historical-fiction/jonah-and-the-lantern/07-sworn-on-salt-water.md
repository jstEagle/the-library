## 7. Sworn on Salt Water

The boat had a name once, but the sea had taken it along with everything else — paint, cargo, the stern where it had been bolted. Now she lay on her side above the tideline like a beached whale, ribs showing through rotten planking, and the fishermen called her only *the old lady*. It was there, in the blue hour after midnight, that Freya watched them come.

They came in ones and twos, as Elias had insisted. A widow with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light. Two divers, walking apart on the sand but arriving together, the way men do who have shared an air hose. Old Sefu came last, moving slowly through the soft sand with his bad leg, and when he reached the hull he put his hand flat against it the way you'd greet an old friend.

"Eleven," Sefu said, counting with his eyes. "You said eleven."

Freya counted too. Ten, not counting herself and Elias and the woman sitting in the bow with an oilcloth satchel on her knees.

"Eleven is coming," she said, and did not know until she said it whether it was true.

Ruth Adeyemi climbed down into the hold where Elias had spread a sail canvas and set three candles in jars weighted with sand. Freya had expected the magistrate's clerk to look like the company men — like Augustus, like the overseers — some particular cut of authority you could recognize across a compound yard. Ruth Adeyemi was a small, precise woman with salt-and-pepper hair cropped close and ink stains on her fingers that no amount of scrubbing would ever fully lift. She looked at the ten faces around the hold and Freya saw her take them in one by one, the way she must have taken in documents, filing each.

"I am not a magistrate," Ruth said, without preamble. "I cannot bind any court. What I can do is take your words down properly, witness them before God and each other, and keep them where no one can buy them out of my keeping." She paused. "I will tell you what that costs. If this goes wrong, I lose my post, likely my position in the colony entirely. I have considered it. I came anyway."

Nobody answered her. Freya understood why. In the world they had all been raised in, speeches like that preceded a trap.

It was Mariam who broke the stillness — the widow from chapter one of all their griefs, the woman Freya had once fed bread through a fence. She stepped forward into the candlelight and sat down on the ballast stone as if it were a chair made for her.

"Write this," Mariam said. "My husband Arun dove eleven seasons for Halloran's fleet. On the fourteenth of March, five years ago, he went down off Vandal Point and did not come up. The company paid me nothing. They told me he died in debt." Her voice did not shake. "But Arun came to me in a dream the week after, and in the dream he was not drowned. He was standing on a shore I did not know, and he said, *They wrote it wrong, wife. Make them write it right.*"

Silence held the hull. Then Ruth uncapped her ink bottle, and the small sound of it — glass against glass, the scratch of nib on paper — seemed to break something open in the room.

The others came forward.

A diver named Kiri told how he had been ordered down to a wreck the company knew was fouled, how his partner had been lost and logged as *drunk*, though Kiri had held the rope himself and felt the pull of the snag. A deckhand described ledgers being carried aboard at night, pages added in a hand that wasn't the paymaster's. An old beachcomber named Pillai produced a scrap of canvas with marks on it — a tally, he said, kept by a man named Ossian, dead two winters now, who had counted the boats that went out full and came back light.

Elias spoke fourth from the end. He gave his name, his former post, and the whole architecture of the fraud in a clerk's flat, careful voice: forty-one men recorded drowned whose debts were collected anyway, whose insurance payouts had flowed into private accounts. He named the banks. He named the dates. When he finished, Ruth looked up.

"You swear this is true."

"On salt water and whatever's left of my soul."

One by one they pressed their thumb or signed the page. Freya watched Sefu make his mark — a slow spiral, deliberate as a signature — and thought: *this is what it looks like when silence ends.* Not shouting. Ink drying.

* * *

Her own turn came near the end, and she had prepared nothing.

Ruth looked at her over the paper. "Name?"

"Freya Halloran."

The name moved through the hold like a current. Halloran. The founder's own ward, the daughter of the drowned man, raised inside the walls everyone here had learned to hate. Kiri shifted. Pillai spat carefully between the boards.

"I know what my name buys me in this company," she said. "I'm asking what it buys me here."

Sefu answered, from his place against the hull. "It buys you the same thing it buys the rest of us, girl. A chance to be believed or not believed on what you say." He nodded at Ruth. "Say it, then."

So Freya said it. The bread through the fence. The ledgers she'd been trained never to look at. The letters in her father's hand dated after the day the company said the sea took him. Her voice stayed level until the last part, and then it didn't quite, and she let it not, because these people had earned the sound of it.

"He taught me my letters," she said. "That hand on those letters is the first handwriting I ever read. I would know it if I saw it on my own gravestone."

She signed. Ruth blotted the page, wrapped the depositions in the oilcloth, and tied it with cord, and the knot she tied was complicated and quick, a clerk's knot, and Freya understood that this woman had done this kind of work before, somewhere, for someone, and been changed by it.

* * *

It was nearly three when the wind rose. They blew out two of the candles and talked low in the dark, and that was when Freya noticed it — the way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue.

Pillai was gone.

She scanned the hold. His ballast stone stood empty, his canvas scrap still lying on the sail where he'd left it. No one had seen him go. The beachcomber had come late, sat far back in the shadow of the transom, asked questions about the trunk, about where the papers would be kept, about the magistrate's court and its hours — questions she had taken, at the time, for an old man's caution.

"Maybe he went ahead," Kiri offered. "Maybe he was afraid."

"An afraid man leaves before the writing starts," Sefu said quietly. "Not after."

Freya climbed to the gunwale and looked down the beach. The wind was coming up off the water in long shoves, flattening the grass on the dunes, and the surf had turned the color of dishwater. Far down the strand, at the very edge of seeing, she caught movement — a figure crossing above the tideline, hurrying, bent into the wind, heading not toward the village but inland, toward the cure station road, toward town.

Toward the compound.

"Elias." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "How long, do you think, has Augustus known something was happening? Not what. Just *something*."

Elias came to stand beside her. He followed her pointing finger, and she watched him arrive at the same arithmetic she had: the ambush on the station road, the burning records, and now this — a company always exactly one step behind them, unless it was one step ahead.

"Since the beginning," Elias said. "He's known since the beginning."

Below them in the dark hold, ten depositions lay dried and folded in an oilcloth satchel — the truth, sworn on salt water, complete. And somewhere down that black and blowing beach, an old man with a liar's errand was carrying its whereabouts to the one person in Ceylon who wanted it destroyed.

Freya gripped the rail. "Then we move tonight."
