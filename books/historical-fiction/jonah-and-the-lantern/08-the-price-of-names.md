## 8. The Price of Names

The summons came at dawn, carried by a boy who wouldn't meet her eyes.

Freya dressed slowly, folding each garment as if for a journey. She had known this morning would come since the moment the traitor slipped off the beached boat — she had watched him go in Ruth's lantern light, had felt the cold enter her chest and stay there like swallowed seawater. Three days of waiting. Now the boy stood at her door with wet hair and a message written in Augustus Halloran's own hand: *Come to my study. Come alone.*

Her hands were steady as she braided her hair. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake.

The compound was quiet in the way it always was before the diving boats launched — that held breath, that pause between one tide and the next. She walked through it noticing everything, the way you notice things when you suspect you're seeing them for the last time. The smell of pitch and salt. The pearl-sorting room where she'd spent ten years bent over lamplight. The ledgers behind their locked door, keeping their forty-one lies.

Augustus was standing at the window when she entered.

He didn't turn. For a long moment he simply looked out at his harbor, his boats, his men filing down to the water with their knives and their prayers, and Freya understood that he was letting her watch him watch it all — reminding her what was at stake, what an empire looked like from the inside.

"You attended a gathering," he said. "Three nights ago. A beached boat past the eastern jetty. Eleven witnesses. A magistrate's clerk taking depositions." He turned then. His face was mild, almost kind, which was how she knew how angry he was. "Do you want to hear what I was told, Freya? Or do you already know?"

"I was there," she said. "I know."

"Sit down."

"No, thank you."

Something flickered across his face — irritation, yes, but under it something else. Something almost like respect. He had raised her to be obedient and she had learned obedience so well she could now weaponize it: she stood where she would have stood if summoned, hands folded, spine straight, every inch the perfect ward. She was performing the girl he thought he owned even as she dismantled her.

"You've cost me two days of work," he said, sitting behind his desk. "Two days, because I refused to believe the report until I'd verified it myself. I told myself: not Freya. Not the girl I fed and clothed and taught to read." He opened a drawer and laid a paper on the desk, face up. It was the traitor's account, signed. She recognized the handwriting of the man named Pillai, a retired diver's son who had wept during his own testimony and fled before Ruth could take his oath. "But here we are."

Freya said nothing. Silence had been her first language; she would not surrender it now just because he wanted her to fill it.

"Let me tell you what happens next," Augustus continued, "so you understand the shape of it. This clerk — Adeyemi — has eleven statements. Incomplete ones. Nine, now, since young Pillai came to me. Statements without sworn oaths filed with a magistrate are paper. Paper burns. Paper drowns. Paper is worth exactly what I decide it's worth." He laced his fingers together. "But you. You are not paper. You are Tomas Halloran's daughter, and everyone on this coast knows it, and if you stand in a courtroom and say these men are liars, grieving people confusing grief with fact — then all of this ends. Quietly. Tonight."

"And if I don't?"

"Ah." He leaned back. "Then arrests begin."

* * *

He laid it out the way he must once have laid out cargo contracts, with the same unhurried precision, the same instinct for what a man — or a girl — could not afford to lose.

Freedom first. He drew a document from the same drawer as the betrayal and slid it across the desk. Her indenture papers. He produced a pen and cut the page in half with a single motion, clean as a gull cutting wind.

"You're free," he said. "Not freed — free. No debt, no obligation, no claim. You can walk out of this compound today and no one follows."

Then money. An account in Colombo, in her name, drawn on the company. Enough to live anywhere. Enough to be someone other than the drowned man's daughter.

And last — and she saw this coming from the moment he began speaking, saw it the way divers see the shadow of a shark before their minds will admit what it is — last came her father.

"I have reason to believe," Augustus said softly, "that Tomas is alive."

The words landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. She had prepared for this. Elias had warned her: *he'll offer you your father, it's the only coin he has left.* But knowing a blow is coming does nothing for the impact of it.

"He was taken to a curing station up the coast," Augustus went on. "After the *Vesuvius*. There was a question of debts. Of things he'd seen." A small shrug, elegant, obscene. "I can have him found. I can have him brought home within the month. You'd have him back, Freya. Alive. Whole. Five years returned to you that the sea was supposed to keep."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a boat horn sounded the start of the day.

"All of it," he said, "for nine names."

She looked at him then — really looked, the way she had never permitted herself as a child, when looking too long at the master of the house was one of the silences she'd been taught. She looked at the fine hands that had signed forty-one men into the sea. The well-fed face above ledgers full of widows' unpaid bread. The man who had beaten her for giving away a loaf while he grew rich on the dead.

"You never intended to search for him," she said. "If he comes back, he testifies."

Something moved behind his eyes — the briefest flicker, confirmation passing across it like a fish's shadow. She had guessed right. Tomas alive was not a gift Augustus Halloran was holding; he was a problem Augustus Halloran was still deciding how to solve.

"You mistake me," he began.

"No," Freya said. "I don't think I ever have."

She rose. Half the indenture papers still lay on his desk, cut clean through. She left them there.

"The names you want," she said, "belong to people who trusted me. Sefu, who taught me that silence only decides who gets hurt. Mariamma, whose husband is in your ledgers twice — sold once, drowned once. Ruth Adeyemi, who came three hundred miles because the law is supposed to mean something. And Elias Voss, whom you ruined for keeping honest books." Her voice did not rise. It didn't need to. "You offered me my father's life for theirs, and you want to know the worst part? I think you genuinely don't understand why I'm refusing."

For the first time in her memory, Augustus Halloran seemed to have nothing to say.

At the door she paused, without turning.

"You should know something," she said. "Pillai's statement isn't worth the ink. He signed it before Ruth swore him. Whatever else we did wrong that night, we did that right." It was mostly true. It was enough true. "You bought a coward, and cowards drown first."

She walked out of the study into the white morning, and behind her she heard the chair scrape, and the bell for the overseer ring hard and long.

* * *

They moved faster than she'd hoped.

By noon the compound gates were closed — closed, which hadn't happened since the cholera year. By mid-afternoon she heard boots in the lanes of the town, company men moving in pairs with the particular unhurried authority of people carrying warrants purchased in advance.

Freya spent the hours doing the only useful thing left: warning everyone she could reach. She went first to the widows' lane and found Mariamma already gone, her hut door open, cooking fire still smoking — fled or taken, no way to know. She sent a fisher-boy to Ruth's lodging with a message folded small inside a bread roll: *Burn nothing. Trust the trunk. He knows about Pillai.* She walked the tideline looking for Elias and found only his footprints going north toward the cure station road, and she stood at the edge of the water feeling the whole plan tilt beneath her like deck plating in a swell.

Come nightfall, they took Sefu.

She heard it from the sorting room, where she had gone to be near the ledgers — near the evidence, in case there was any last use for a girl who could forge a fair hand. Shouting, but not much of it. Sefu was seventy years old and had decided, she realized later, not to give them the satisfaction of a struggle.

She reached the courtyard in time to see him between two company men, wrists bound in front of him, walking with the slow dignity of a man crossing his own threshold. His head was bare. Someone had knocked his cap off and left it in the dirt.

He saw her at the gate and stopped, ignoring the hand on his arm.

"Girl," he said. "You listen to an old diver."

"I'm listening."

"The sea doesn't take the ones who fight," he said. "It takes whoever's there. Fighting changes nothing about drowning — only about what you are while the water comes in." He let the company man pull him forward a step, then looked back once more. "I taught you the tongue so the words would survive us. See that they do."

Then they put him in the locked cart, and the wheels ground away over the coral road, and Freya stood alone in the courtyard holding his cap, which she had picked up from the dirt without remembering doing it.

Above the harbor the storm that had been building all day finally broke, rain coming down in gray sheets, and through it she could hear the town — doors slamming, voices calling, the sound of a community learning in real time what its names had cost. Somewhere in that rain was a cart with Sefu in it. Somewhere north was Elias, riding into the country of burned records. Somewhere east was a trunk full of testimony that was now the only thing left standing between forty-one drowned men and a second, quieter death.

Freya put Sefu's cap on her own head. It did not fit. She wore it anyway.

She had one night, one storm, and one ally unaccounted for, and tomorrow the company would move the prisoners somewhere even Ruth Adeyemi's writs couldn't follow.

She turned toward the lockup and began to run.
