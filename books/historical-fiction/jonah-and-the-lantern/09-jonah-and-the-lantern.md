## 9. Jonah and the Lantern

**Freya**

The storm came in off the sea like something owed.

She had watched squalls her whole life from behind glass, but this one arrived with intent, bending the palms flat, driving rain sideways through the lanes of the port until the whole town ran brown. Perfect weather for what they meant to do. No company man would be out in it unless paid double, and even then he'd grumble and lag.

Freya stood under the awning of the chandlery with the trunk at her feet — Ruth Adeyemi's depositions, eleven sworn accounts wrapped in oilcloth — and watched the lockup across the yard. Two lanterns burned inside it. One was a mistake. The guard on duty had lit two instead of one, and Elias had told her that meant there were two men, because no one ever doubled the light without doubling the reason.

"You're certain about the door," she said.

"Sefu's cousin oiled the hinges last month when they put a drunk in there," Elias said. "It doesn't creak. It groans once, loud, if you let it swing. So we don't let it swing."

"And the back window?"

"Big enough for Sefu. Not for me." He looked at her, rain sheeting off the awning's edge between them. "You could wait with the trunk."

"No."

"I thought you'd say that." He almost smiled. In the lamplight his face was still bruised yellow-green from the road, from the ambush, from everything the company had already done to him, and he smiled anyway. "Then remember: we're not brave tonight. We're quick. Brave is what they call you afterward if you live."

They crossed the yard through the worst of the rain, which was its own mercy — no watcher would keep his post dry-eyed and honest when he could shelter under the eaves of the assay office. The door gave without a sound. Inside, the air smelled of wet stone and tobacco, and the single guard at the desk had his chair tipped back, his hat over his face, his pistol hanging on a nail well out of his own reach.

Elias lifted the hat. The man's eyes opened, found Elias's face, and went wide.

"Don't," Elias said quietly. "You work for a company that drowns men on paper. Tonight you get to decide if you drown too."

The guard did arithmetic Freya could see happening behind his eyes — wages against weather against the kind of man who kept true ledgers — and rolled his chair toward the wall with his hands up. She took the keys off the desk herself. Her fingers didn't shake. Five years of sorting pearls without dropping one had taught her hands things her heart never learned.

The cell at the end held Sefu, sitting upright on the plank bed as though he'd been expecting them since noon.

"Took your time," he said.

"There was a storm," Freya said.

"There's always a storm." He rose, joints cracking like rigging, and took her by both shoulders. Up close she could see what the arrest had cost him — a split lip going scabbed, wrists ringed raw — and also what it hadn't touched, which was everything else. "The others?"

"Safe. Scattered like Ruth taught them. Eleven statements in the trunk."

"Then let's go make them worth the paper."

* * *

**Elias**

They went out the back window in order — Sefu first, then the girl, who dropped into Elias's waiting arms lighter than the ledgers she'd been raised on — and the cart was waiting where the fisher boys had promised, one mule, one tarpaulin, and a boy of maybe twelve holding the reins who looked at Sefu and said something in the divers' pidgin too fast for Elias to follow.

Sefu laughed, an actual laugh, soaked and hunted and laughing. "He says the sea owes us a favor."

"The sea owes nobody anything," Elias said, climbing up beside the trunk. "That's rather the point of all this."

They took the low road along the harbor, because the high road had sightlines to the compound and Augustus Halloran would have men on it by now — Elias had counted on hours and gotten, he suspected, minutes. The rain came down so hard the lanterns of the town smeared into long wounds of light, and the sea, somewhere to their left, was making a sound like a crowd deciding something.

"Magistrate's court is on stilts above the tide line," Sefu called over the wind. "Old man Adeyemi built it there so the fishermen couldn't avoid him. Now the water comes to visit instead."

"How high?"

"High enough that a smart clerk would have moved the records upstairs by now. Is Ruth smart?"

"She's smarter than all of us," Freya said. "She moved them yesterday."

Elias kept his eyes forward and did not think about how naturally the girl said *Ruth*, or how three months ago she had stood in the trading post doorway denying her own father's handwriting. People changed in storms. That was what storms were for.

They were halfway along the seawall when the shot came.

It went high — a warning, or bad aim in that murk, and Elias didn't wait to learn which. "The tarpaulin!" he shouted, and the boy hauled it, and Freya threw herself onto the trunk, and Sefu took the mule's bridle and turned them off the road entirely, down a lane Elias wouldn't have found in daylight, between walls that funneled the rain into a single roaring gutter. Hooves behind them now, more than one horse, riders cursing at the flooding street.

"They'll cut us off at the customs house!" Elias shouted.

"Then we don't go past the customs house." Sefu pointed left, toward a black gap between buildings where the water was running deepest. "Jonah's Cut. It floods every monsoon. Nobody rides it."

"Nobody walks it either—"

"Divers walk worse things." And Sefu stepped down into water waist-high and moving, and held up his freed hands for the trunk, and Elias understood there was no vote to be taken.

They went through the Cut in a chain — the boy first, then Sefu with the trunk held overhead like a man carrying a child across a river, then Freya, then Elias last with the mule pushing sullenly behind him — and the water tore at their legs and something struck Elias across the shin hard enough that he bit through his own lip, and the whole time the horses wheeled and screamed at the mouth of the lane, their riders unwilling to follow, their voices thin with fury under the rain.

Then the far side. Then stone steps. Then the court itself, a pale two-story box on stilts, its lower floor already shining with a hand's depth of tidewater, and a lamp burning in the upper window like a promise.

* * *

**Freya**

Ruth Adeyemi met them at the top of the stairs with a candle and a revolver she held as though she'd only read about them, which somehow made it more convincing.

"Eleven?" she said.

"Eleven," Freya said. "Plus whatever I am."

"You," Ruth said, "are the twelfth statement, and the only one the founder will answer for personally. Dry off. The record's open."

The courtroom upstairs was small and smelled of salt and old varnish. Rain hammered the shutters. Below, through gaps in the floorboards, Freya could see the floodwater sliding black and patient beneath the building, carrying leaves and a broken crate and once, absurdly, a single rubber boot.

Ruth opened the trunk. She unwrapped each deposition, checked each signature and mark — Sefu's cross, the widow Mariam's careful letters learned late in life, the old diver Anselm's shaky lines — and entered each into the record aloud while thunder tried and failed to talk over her.

Then it was Freya's turn.

She had rehearsed nothing. There had been no time, and perhaps it was better so, because the words came out the way they'd lived in her for years, packed down under pearl-sorts and beatings and the rule that you never named the drowned:

"My name is Freya Halloran. I was indentured to Augustus Halloran of the Halloran Trading Company from the age of twelve. I swear that the ledgers of that company record forty-one deaths by drowning between the years 1889 and 1894, and that I have seen with my own eyes, in the hand of my father Tomas Halloran, letters written after the date of his recorded death. I swear that the widow Mariam was refused her husband's payout while his debt was collected to the last cent. I swear—" and here her voice wanted to break and she let it want, and it didn't, "—I swear that silence was the law of that house, and that I kept it, and that keeping it protected no one but the man it was written for."

Ruth's pen scratched. Outside, the storm leaned on the building hard enough that the lamp flame lay flat.

"Entered," Ruth said. "Sworn and dated. And now—"

The door burst open.

Augustus Halloran filled the frame with rain running off him, three of his men behind him with pistols, and for one heartbeat Freya saw him as she had at seven years old: the largest thing in the world. Then she saw him as he was — a wet old man whose empire was leaking out through a clerk's pen — and the heartbeat passed.

"This proceeding is unlawful," he said. "This court has no jurisdiction—"

"The court has jurisdiction over sworn testimony taken within it," Ruth said mildly, sanding the page, "and over trespassers standing in it. Also, Mr. Halloran, your boots are in four inches of my courthouse. You may wish to move before the record notes that too."

His men shifted. Sefu and Elias moved just enough that moving mattered. And Augustus looked at Freya across the flooded room — at the ward he'd offered freedom to, the informant he'd bought and never suspected he hadn't purchased — and she looked back, and said nothing at all, because for five years silence had been his language and tonight she declined to speak it.

He knew it, too. She watched him know it. Watched the understanding arrive that the girl had been in his house all those years the way the sea is under a boat.

Ruth sealed the trunk with wax and wire and wrote across the lid in her precise clerk's hand: *Received and recorded. This 14th day of the southwest monsoon.* Then she set it on the high shelf beside the land records, where colonial paperwork goes to become permanent.

"Too late, Mr. Halloran," she said. "For all of it."

Augustus's face did something complicated and small. When he spoke, his voice was almost gentle, which was the worst thing in it.

"You should have asked me about your father, child," he said. "Before you spent all these lives on paper. Ask me now, and I'll tell you where he is — and why he never came back for you."

The rain came down. Under the floorboards, the sea kept rising.
