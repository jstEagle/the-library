## 10. What the Tide Returns

The monsoon ended in April, and with it the season of waiting.

For three months after the courthouse, Freya had lived in a fisherman's hut near the breakwater with Ruth Adeyemi's depositions locked in a trunk that no longer belonged to her — it belonged to the record now, to the Crown, to whatever slow machinery turned in Colombo. She had learned to live beside that machinery the way divers learned to live beside sharks: without sudden movement, without expectation.

But the machinery did turn. First came the investigators, pale men from the colonial office who asked for everything twice and wrote down half of it. Then the company's clerks began leaving, one by one, carrying their ledgers under their arms like men abandoning a sinking hull. The pearl auctions stopped. The warehouse on the north quay stood empty, its doors chained, and children dared each other to press their faces to the gaps and report what they saw: nothing. Just dust where the fortune had been.

Augustus Halloran was taken off a ship at Galle in June, wearing a borrowed coat and claiming, witnesses said, that his name wasn't his name. Freya heard this from Ruth over tea and found she could not summon even anger. There was something almost pitiable in a man who had spent thirty years building an empire out of other people's deaths and then tried to walk away from his own name at the end. Almost. Not quite.

* * *

Sefu came up the beach path that morning with his stride unchanged, though the company lockup had taken four weeks of his life and left him thinner through the shoulders. He carried a letter.

"From Colombo," he said, and sat down heavily on the sea-wall beside her, the way he always did, as if the wall had been built for exactly one purpose.

Freya looked at the envelope and did not take it. Her hands went still in her lap. In five years she had taught herself every kind of stillness — stillness against beatings, stillness before ledgers, stillness when widows wept at the gate. But this was a different stillness, the kind that comes before a wave breaks.

"He is alive," Sefu said gently. "That much I know already. I would not bring you a letter that said otherwise."

She took it. The handwriting inside was not her father's. It belonged to some station clerk or missionary, a careful, educated hand reporting that when the curing station at Kankesanthurai fell to the investigation, eleven men walked out of the drying sheds who had never been entered in any register. One of them gave his name as Tomas Halloran. He was thin. He had lost most of his hearing in one ear. He had asked, first of all, whether anyone had come looking for him, and when told about the magistrate and the storm and the girl whose testimony broke the company, he had wept into his hands for a long time.

He is making his way south, the letter said. He asks for you at the harbor lantern.

Freya read it three times. The words kept rearranging themselves into sentences she could not believe. Alive. Coming home. He asks for you.

"He read my name," she said finally. "In the newspaper account?"

"He read your name," Sefu agreed, "and mine, and Ruth's, and Voss's. He has been reading a great deal these past months, I think. He says—" Sefu paused, choosing. "He says he taught you letters so you would have somewhere to go if he drowned. And then he drowned, and you used them."

Freya pressed the letter flat against her chest and looked out at the water until her eyes stopped burning.

* * *

They lit the harbor lantern at dusk, as they had done every evening since the company's watchmen scattered — except now there were no watchmen, and the lamp had gone dark for want of anyone paid to tend it. So the town had simply begun doing it themselves, by rotation, and tonight it was Freya's turn, which was a lie she had arranged with herself because she wanted the doing of it to be hers alone.

Elias met her at the foot of the tower. He walked better now; the ambush on the cure station road had left him with a limp the rains made worse, but he refused a cane out of what Ruth called pride and Elias called practicality. He carried the oil can and the flint, because he always insisted on carrying things, as if usefulness could pay down a debt he did not owe.

"You don't have to do this part alone," he said.

"I know." She climbed the stairs ahead of him anyway.

From the gallery at the top, the whole harbor lay below them going violet and gold, the boats drawn up for the night, the water flat as poured metal. Somewhere beyond the horizon, a thin deaf man was walking toward this exact point of light. Freya thought about that — that all those years of lessons at the kitchen table, the letters formed under her father's rough fingers, had been aimed across five years and forty-one false graves at this small iron lamp. That love could work like that. Quietly. At distance. Finding its mark.

She trimmed the wick. Elias struck the flint. The flame took, steadied, threw their two shadows long across the gallery floor.

"I keep thinking it will feel different," Elias said. "Finished. It doesn't, yet."

"No," Freya said. "I think it feels like the hour before a tide turns. Everything moving, nothing settled."

"And when it settles? What will you do?" He asked it carefully, the way he asked everything now, having learned that answers given freely were worth more than answers pried loose. "Your indenture is voided. The courts confirmed it last week. You're a free woman with a famous name and no obligations. You could go anywhere. Colombo. England. You could write all this down and be admired in drawing rooms for it."

Freya watched the light swing out over the water.

"That's the strange thing," she said. "For five years I wanted only to leave. I thought freedom meant elsewhere. And then I spent one midnight on a beached boat listening to eleven people tell the truth, and I understood I'd had it backwards. I was never bound by the indenture. I was bound by the silence. The paper was just where they kept the receipt."

She turned to look at him. In the lamplight his ruined face — the clerk's pallor, the new scar along his jaw — looked almost young.

"I'm staying," she said. "Not because I must. Because these are my people. Sefu. Ruth. You. Even the ones who testified and went back to their nets and won't ever speak of it again. I spent seventeen years learning every way a person can belong nowhere. I'd like to spend the rest learning the other thing."

Elias nodded slowly, and something in his shoulders loosened that she hadn't known was tight.

"There's still Tomas," he said. "That's a different kind of belonging. Harder. You'll have grief and joy arriving together, and they will not wait politely for each other."

"I know." She thought of the man she remembered — big hands, salt-cracked voice, the smell of tar and tobacco — and the stranger the letter described, thin and half-deaf, and wondered how you began again with someone who was both. "I suppose we'll learn each other the way I learned the divers' tongue. Slowly. Badly at first. Until it's ours."

Below them, figures were gathering on the sea-wall. Sefu, unmistakable in his rolling gait. Ruth in her clerk's grey, come straight from the temporary magistrate's office, still carrying her satchel of sealed truths. Other shapes too — widows, divers, faces from the boat that night, drawn to the light the way they had once been taught to avoid it.

"They're waiting for you," Elias said.

"For all of us," Freya said, and took his arm, and went down.

* * *

At the foot of the tower, Sefu put a hand on her shoulder and said nothing, which between them had long since become the fullest sentence either needed. Ruth shook her hand formally, then abandoned formality and hugged her hard enough to creak ribs.

"Well," Ruth said, stepping back. "The Halloran estate is in receivership. The compound, the warehouses, the auction house — all of it, tied up in litigation for years. But the little house on the point, the one your father built before any of it — that was never company property. It's yours outright. Has been all along."

Freya stared at her. "I never knew that."

"Augustus knew," Ruth said dryly. "It's why he kept you in the compound instead. Easier to guard a thing when it doesn't know it's free."

Freya laughed — actually laughed, the sound surprising her, rising out of her like a fish breaking water. Five months ago she had not believed she would laugh again, had not been sure she deserved to, and here it was anyway, unearned and welcome, echoing off the tower stones while the lantern above them turned its steady circle over the harbor.

"So," Sefu said, settling onto the sea-wall and patting the stone beside him until all four of them were sitting in a row like the world's strangest family portrait. "The letter says how long?"

"A month," Freya said. "Perhaps less. He walks slowly, the letter said. He stops to look at everything, as if seeing it for the first time." She folded the letter and tucked it inside her shirt, against her heart, where she had carried harder things. "I'll be at the harbor every evening. Lighting the lamp. He knows to look for the light."

The tide was coming in below them, unhurried, certain, filling in everything the ebb had bared. Freya sat among her people and watched the water rise and thought: this is what returning looks like. Not a dramatic arrival. Just the sea, doing patiently what it always does. Coming back over ground it never truly surrendered.

Far down the coast road, small in the last light, a figure appeared — too far to see a face, walking with a slight lean, stopping every few steps to look at the land as if memorizing it.

Nobody spoke. Nobody moved.

Then Sefu said quietly, "Well. Someone should put more oil in the lamp."

And Freya was already running.
