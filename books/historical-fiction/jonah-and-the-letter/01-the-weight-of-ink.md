## 1. The Weight of Ink

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width by the time Gideon Montrose dipped her pen for the final stroke of the final plate.

She had been saving this coastline for three nights, working everything else first — the river deltas, the soundings, the little hatched hills that marched inland like sleeping cattle — because the coast was the soul of any chart, and a soul should be laid down in one unbroken act of attention. Master Corvus had taught her that in her ninth year, standing behind her with his hand hovering over her shoulder but never touching, never guiding, only *waiting*, as though the line might be frightened off by interference. *The coast is where the land meets its truth*, he used to say. *Everything inland is negotiation. The edge is confession.*

So she bent now over the copperplate impression of the Harborwick shore, twenty years of her life arranged beneath the candle's small yellow kingdom, and let the nib find its way from the harbor mouth eastward along the cliffs she could walk blindfolded.

The ink went down black and certain. The pen sang its thin dry song against the paper. Outside, the town slept the way harbor towns sleep — fitfully, with dogs and tide-sounds stitched through it — and inside the workshop the only motion was her wrist, the only light the candle and the low red banked coals in the stove, and the only sound besides the sea was the tick of the longcase clock Corvus had bought in Rye and never once set to the correct hour. It ran eleven minutes fast. She had never corrected it. Some part of her believed the workshop would stop keeping time altogether if she did.

At the harbor mouth she paused to add the last sounding — nine fathoms, dropping to fourteen past the spit — figures she had taken herself, in a borrowed boat, in weather that had frightened her more than she would ever admit aloud. Her hand was steady. Her hand was always steady; that was the first thing anyone ever said about the work of Gideon Montrose, master mapmaker, and it was true in a way that nothing else about him was true.

*Him.* She signed the plate the way she had signed every plate for twenty years: *E. Corvus, Hydrographer, Harborwick*. The E stood for Edmund, though no living soul but Gideon knew that, and she had learned it from a funeral sermon. The flourish of the C, the deliberate severity of the final s — she had practiced that signature for a month before she dared put it on honest work, copying it from ledger headers and receipt stubs until her wrist dreamed in its shape. Now it came out of her hand without thought, smooth as water finding a channel, and watching it appear on the finished plate gave her the same complicated pleasure it always did: pride, yes, real pride, work worth signing — and beneath the pride, cold and patient as groundwater, the knowledge of what the signature was.

A forgery. Twenty years of forgery, practiced so faithfully that the lie had calluses.

She set down the pen and blew across the wet letters, very gently, the way one blows on something alive.

* * *

The atlas lay open on the great table, all eleven finished plates fanned around the twelfth like disciples around a teacher. It was to be Corvus's masterpiece — *The Coasts of England, Surveyed and Drawn by E. Corvus*, a title page she had engraved two winters ago while he coughed his lungs loose in the room above. He had lived to see six plates completed. He had lived to see her finish the seventh badly and tear it out and begin again, and he had said, in the voice of a man rationing his remaining breaths, *Better a late truth than an early lie,* which was the sort of thing he said instead of comfort.

Then he had died in the February of the year before last, in the bed directly over this table, and the atlas had become hers in every way but the one written on its title page.

Three years of finishing it alone. Soundings taken in all seasons. Archives consulted by letter under his name, his handwriting imitated in every request so that no archivist in England would know Edmund Corvus was dead. And tonight, at last, the twelfth plate done, the whole great body of the work complete and ready for the engraver in London who had been promised it these four years and had stopped writing back.

Gideon stood at the head of the table and looked at it the way a mother looks at a grown child leaving for a war: with love, and with the sudden animal certainty that something terrible was coming for it.

Because the atlas was not only coastlines.

She had told herself for twenty years that she didn't see them, the same way a person tells themselves they don't hear the wind. But she had drawn those margins. She knew every fleck of ornament she'd cut into the copper — the compass roses, the sea-monsters, the tiny ships riding decorative swells off every major port. And she knew which ships were wrong.

There was a brig off Flamborough Head whose pennant flew a pattern that matched no navy and no merchant house in England. There was a cluster of initials tucked into the foliage of the cartouche on the Yorkshire plate — *R.H.*, *M.B.*, *T.H.* — rendered in vine-tendrils so cunningly that only someone who already knew to look would ever find letters there at all. There was a lighthouse on the Suffolk coast that existed on no other chart, drawn with loving precision on a stretch of coast famous for having none, and beside it, disguised as a surveyor's bench-mark, the numeral *xii*.

Corvus had made these marks in the last five years of his life, after the first fugitives began moving through Harborwick in the dark, and he had made them for the people who moved them — a private geography, a language of safe water and safe houses and safe names, hidden in plain sight on the most respectable document in the county. Anyone who bought the atlas bought beauty. Anyone who needed it bought rescue. And when it reached London, forty families' worth of secrets would travel in its margins into the hands of whoever paid the engraver's bills.

Gideon had asked him about it once. Only once.

* * *

She had been seventeen, still new enough to the truth of her own disguise that she flinched whenever a customer said *young man*, still listening for her dead name in every sentence. It was summer. Corvus was inking a proof of the Suffolk plate at the window, and she had come upon him quite by accident from behind, and seen him pause at the margin and touch the false lighthouse with one finger, lightly, the way a man touches a scar.

*Master,* she had said — she called him Master then, though he had begged her within a year to stop — *why is there a lighthouse where no lighthouse stands?*

He hadn't turned. She remembered that clearly: he hadn't turned, and his shoulders had gone very straight, and for a long moment the only sound was the clock running eleven minutes fast.

*Because a girl once told me,* he said at last, *that the truest maps are the ones that lie in the margins and tell the truth everywhere else.* Then he had turned, and looked at her with those pale eyes that missed nothing and reported less, and said: *And why does an apprentice ask questions about things she has wisely pretended not to see for two years?*

*I want to know if I'm in danger.*

*You are in danger every morning you lace your breeches,* he said, not unkindly. *That is the price of the life I taught you to steal. But no — the margins are not your danger. They are mine. I made them, and I will answer for them, and you will keep your hands clean of them so long as God grants me breath.* He had held her gaze then, longer than he ever liked to hold anything. *Should my breath run short before the work is done, Gideon—*

He had almost never used her name. It was too dangerous spoken aloud, even indoors, even alone; they had agreed on that in her first month, when her voice was still breaking itself into shape by force of will. Hearing it now stopped her heart.

*—you will burn the proofs,* he continued. *Every scrap. And the plates you will deface with the graver, one line through each margin, and you will sell the copper for old metal, and you will finish my atlas honest and clean, and you will sign my name to it without grief, because my name has kept you fed and it asks very little in return.*

*And if I don't want it honest?*

Something had moved in his face then — she had spent twenty years since trying to name it, and had settled finally on *fear*. Not for himself.

*Then you are your father's daughter,* he said, *and I have failed us both.*

Her father. Captain Jonas Montrose of the revenue service, drowned off Duncow Point the winter she was seven, the man who had delivered Corvus his first smuggled cargo and his first fugitive in the same night and never told which was which. Corvus had taken her in not as charity but as debt — she understood that now, though she hadn't then — and had raised her inside a fiction so complete that the fiction had eaten the girl and left only the mapmaker. Gideon Montrose, son of Jonas, apprentice, journeyman, master. A man who had never existed, except on paper. Paper was patient. Paper never blinked.

That had been twelve years ago. Corvus was six years in the churchyard. The proofs had never been burned, because there had never been proofs — he had inked the marginalia directly onto the finished drawings in the last months of his life, working at night, telling her nothing, and by the time she found them all it was too late to unmake them without unmaking the atlas entire. So she had finished it. She had finished it exactly as he left it, monsters and lighthouses and tendril-letters and all, and she told herself every night that she finished it for him, for his masterpiece, for his name's sake.

And every night the telling got a little thinner.

* * *

She counted the names now, because she counted them every time she let herself, which was less often than she let herself remember counting.

Forty-one, if you included the initials in the Yorkshire foliage as two. Forty-one names of people who had sheltered the fleeing, rowed the dark water, forged the bills of lading, looked away at the right moments. Tamsin Hale's husband among them — *T.H.*, curled in the vines, the man who owned the boat Gideon had borrowed for her soundings and who had never once asked what she sounded for. Forty-one gallows, if the wrong reader ever bent close enough to see the language in the leaves.

She knew them all. That was the terrible arithmetic of it: she had not merely copied the margins, she had *learned* them, because a mapmaker cannot reproduce what she does not understand, and so over three years of finishing the atlas she had assembled in her memory a complete census of the smuggling coast — who hid, who carried, whose cellar had the false floor, whose lighthouse cast its beam only for certain ships. If she died tonight, the knowledge would die with her, and forty families would sleep easier forever without knowing why.

If the atlas died instead, the knowledge would live on in one head, unwritten, deniable, useless to prosecutors and priceless to the people it protected.

She had thought about fire more than once. She thought about it now, standing over the completed work with the candle trembling, and her hands — her steady, famous hands — stayed flat and calm on the table, because she was not going to burn it, and she knew precisely the coward's reason why: twenty years of her life lived inside another man's name, and this atlas was the only object in the world where the shape of that borrowed life had been made permanent and beautiful. Burn it, and there was no proof she had ever been anything at all.

*Vanity,* Corvus would have said, in the voice he saved for bad geometry. *Dressed up as loyalty.*

*Perhaps,* she answered him aloud, to the empty workshop, and her own voice startled her — lower than a woman's, higher than a man's, a voice belonging to no country, pitched twenty years ago on a boy's horizon and frozen there.

The clock struck two, eleven minutes early, or late, or however one reckoned a lie kept faithfully for decades.

She cleaned the pen properly, as he had drilled her — nib washed, barrel wiped, the ink pot stoppered against the damp that came off the harbor at night. She laid blotting paper over the finished coastline and weighted it with the brass meridian bar. One by one she gathered the eleven sister plates and stacked them, and bound the stack in the oilcloth, and tied the cord in the double bowline he had insisted on, left over right, the knot sitting proud like a seal. The parcel was heavy as a paving stone and quiet as a grave.

Tomorrow — today, rather; the clock insisted otherwise but the sky outside the shutters had begun its slow bruising toward grey — the carrier would take it down to the London coach. Four days on the road. Then the engraver, then the printing, then five hundred copies scattered through drawing rooms and admiralty offices and customs houses along the eastern seaboard, each one carrying its cargo of tendril-letters and impossible lighthouses into the wide world beyond anyone's recall.

She had three days' work left on the title page illumination, she decided suddenly. Yes. The gold leaf had not arrived. There was no hurry. The atlas could rest one week more, safely bound, safely hidden under the floorboards where Corvus had kept his army letters, and she would think again about fire, and again not do it, and hate herself a little more efficiently each time.

It was as she knelt at the loose board, lowering the parcel into the dark, that she heard the sound.

Boots. Not one pair — many, and not the shuffling drag of drunkards coming up from the quay, but the spring-heeled cadence of men marching in step, dozens of them, coming along Fore Street from the direction of the customs house. With the boots came fifes. She stood very still in the dark of the workshop, one hand resting on the oilcloth parcel in its hiding place, and listened to the music of her own ruin arriving in Harborwick before dawn.

Through the gap in the shutters she watched them pass: red coats catching the first light, bayonets slung, a sergeant with a hammer and a canvas bag of nailed papers striding from doorpost to doorpost as methodically as a surveyor setting out a baseline. At the workshop's own post he stopped, hammered, moved on.

By the time the column had gone by, the light was strong enough to read by, and Gideon Montrose — hydrographer, master of her trade, keeper of forty-one names and one dangerous silence — crossed the floor and opened her door and took down the paper that had been nailed there like a bill of sale.

**BY ORDER OF HIS MAJESTY'S ARMY**, it began, and below, in smaller type, among the articles of requisition — boats, stores, premises, ledgers, books of account —

*all maps, charts, surveys, and papers of a geographical nature.*
