## 2. Red Coats at the Door

The light came first, before the sound. Gideon Montrose had risen in the dark to work by habit — twenty years of finishing plates by candlelight had bent her hours toward the night — and she was standing at the window with a cup of chicory gone cold in her hand when the harbor turned grey and then gold and then showed her the thing she would spend the rest of the day trying not to think of as an omen.

A ship. No. Three ships, war-rigged, standing into Harborwick roads against the ebb, their topsails catching the first sun so that they seemed lit from within, burning quietly on the water. She counted gun ports out of old habit, the way other people counted sheep. Twenty-two on the nearest. Eighteen on the second. The third sat deeper in the water than a ship of war had any business sitting, and it took her a moment, watching it come up the channel with the patience of something that knew it could not be hurried, to understand what that meant.

It had come to stay.

She set down the cup. Her hands were steady — they were always steady; Master Corvus had beaten steadiness into them with a ruler when she was nine years old, telling her that a trembling hand made a lying map, and a lying map killed sailors — but there was a sensation beneath her breastbone like a key turning in a lock she had hoped was rusted shut.

Below the window, Harborwick was waking to its fishing boats, and the boats were already late.

* * *

She heard the drums before she saw the column. Not the bright parade kind — a flat, dogged beating, two strokes and a pause, two strokes and a pause, the rhythm men march to for hours without dying of boredom because the alternative is thinking. The sound came up Fore Street from the toll road, and then the head of the column came around the corner by the salt house, and Gideon pressed her shoulder to the window frame and looked down through the leaded glass at forty red coats moving through her town like dye dropped into water.

They were not young, most of them. That frightened her more than their number would have. Boys are sent to hold streets; these were weathered men in good boots, and they spread through Harborwick's morning with the unhurried competence of men who had done this before, in towns whose names no one had bothered to teach Gideon, and who would do it again after.

A sergeant with a canvas satchel went ahead of them, stopping at every post. At the corner post outside the cooper's yard he stopped, drew nails from his mouth, and hammered up a printed notice with six blows of a mallet that sounded absurdly small against the drum. Then the next post. Then the next. Gideon watched him work his way along Fore Street the way she herself worked along a coastline, filling in a chart, leaving nothing blank.

By full light there were notices nailed to the church door, the customs house, the fish market, the weighbeam, and both posts of the workshop's own sign, which read CORVUS & MONTROSE, MAP- AND CHART-MAKERS in letters she had repainted herself every spring for fifteen years.

She could not read the notices from the window. She did not need to. She had drawn requisition orders before, in fair copy, for the port authority — harbor dues, billeting schedules, the dry arithmetic by which the Crown ate a town. But she went down anyway, out into the street in her waistcoat and master's coat, into the crowd gathering before the church door, and stood among the fishermen and the widows and read what the army wanted with everyone else, because a man who reads alone is marked, and a man who reads with the crowd is only curious.

BY ORDER OF HIS MAJESTY'S COMMISSIONERS, it began, and Gideon's eye went down the clauses the way a surgeon's goes down a body.

All vessels in the harbor of Harborwick, of whatever size or rig, were seized for the use of the army, their papers to be surrendered to the commanding officer by noon. The countinghouse of Pell & Sons was commandeered as provincial headquarters. All ledgers, manifests, bills of lading, correspondence, and *records of whatsoever nature* pertaining to trade, shipping, or the movement of persons were to be delivered for inventory upon demand.

*Records of whatsoever nature.*

She read it twice. Around her, the town read it once and began, in the wordless way of harborside people, to grieve its boats. Old Marek the pilot was weeping without any noise, tears running into his white beard, staring at the water where the *Gannet* rode at mooring with army men already climbing her rails. A boy of maybe twelve was shouting at a soldier and being laughed at, gently, horribly, the way one laughs at a dog that has not yet understood the fence.

But Gideon stood in the middle of them all with the words *records of whatsoever nature* ringing in her skull like a struck bell, and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost like relief because at least it was definite, that the boats were the smallest part of it.

They had not come for the harbor.

They had come for the paper.

* * *

The workshop smelled of linseed oil and lampblack and the particular cold sweetness of old vellum, and that morning it smelled like safety to her, and she locked the door behind her anyway.

She stood a moment in the middle of the front room, among the flat presses and the drying racks and the great table where the atlas had lain open for three years like a patient on the slab, and she listened to the drums die away down by the quay and asked herself, in Corvus's voice, because it was always his voice that did this part: *What do they know? What do they want? And what will they do to get it?*

What they knew: smuggling had moved through Harborwick for thirty years, and someone in the government had finally decided to stop profiting from it and start punishing it. What they wanted — beyond the harbor itself, beyond the customs revenue — was names. Names of owners, names of skippers, names of the men on the beaches at night with lanterns shuttered northward. An army does not march twenty-two guns up a coast to catch fishermen. Somewhere in London a minister had been given a list, incomplete, and wanted it completed, and had been promised that Harborwick's paper would complete it.

And Harborwick's paper — the true harborwick paper, the record that mattered, the manifest of everything the town had done in the dark for three decades — was folded into the margins of an atlas lying on her worktable upstairs, finished not twelve hours before, its final coastline still faintly tacky under its dusting of pounce.

Forty-one names. She had never counted them aloud, even alone, even to herself in the dark, but she knew the number the way she knew the depth of the channel at low water. Forty-one names written in Corvus's hand over nineteen years, tucked into the decorative borders of charts of Norway and the Baltic and the Carolinas, disguised as flourishes, as surveyor's marks, as the little private shorthand mapmakers use and no one else can read. Initials and dates and tide-times, and each one a family, and each family a rope thrown into the dark to some fugitive going out over the sandbars toward a ship that would carry them beyond the reach of anyone's warrant.

If the atlas reached the colonial office, the clerks there would not read it as art. They would read it as evidence, because that is what it was. And evidence, in matters of smuggling and sedition, did not hang ships. It hanged people.

Gideon took off her master's coat, hung it on its peg with care, rolled her sleeves, and got to work.

Not on hiding it. It was too late to hide it; the atlas was known in the town, known to the customs men, listed in the workshop's own registration papers as the masterwork of Josiah Corvus, deceased, in preparation for presentation to — she had signed the dedication page herself, two years ago, with a flourish she now regretted — *his Majesty's Commissioners of the Admiralty*. You could not hide a thing you had dedicated to the Crown. It would have been funny if it were not likely to kill forty families.

No. There was one thing in the world that could make the atlas harmless, and that was another atlas. A copy. Every margin, every name, every date, rendered in her own hand onto fresh plates and carried somewhere the army could not follow — out to sea, across the water, into the keeping of someone who owed the town a debt or feared it. If the original vanished or burned or sat sealed in an evidence chest for twenty years, the copy would remain. Proof for the defense, when defense became necessary. Leverage, if it came to that. Memory, at the very least, because a name that exists nowhere can be erased, but a name written twice is stubborn.

Copying the marginalia would take days. She had, perhaps, fewer than she thought.

She pulled the atlas toward her, opened it to the first plate — the Norwegian coast, snow-light and black water, the most beautiful thing she had ever helped make — and reached for her burnisher, and that was when the knock came at the door.

Three raps. Unhurried. The knock of a man with authority and time.

Gideon looked down at the open plate, at the tiny ciphered names crowding its border like swallows on a wire, and felt the dread in her chest change temperature — go hard, go dense, the way fog goes to ice on a rigging line. Fear was a luxury. Fear was for afterward, if there was an afterward. She smoothed her face into the mild, slightly bored expression of a tradesman interrupted at his work, and opened the door.

The officer on the step was not wearing red. He wore a clerk's drab coat over a soldier's frame, ink stains on his right cuff, and he held a leather folio under one arm and a printed order in his gloved hand. He was perhaps thirty, thin, with the anxious eyes of a man who counts things for a living and has begun to suspect that counting is a sin.

"Master Montrose." He said it correctly, the way the whole town had learned to say it correctly for twenty years, and as always there passed over Gideon the small vertigo of the lie working, the trapdoor holding. "Elias Finch, provisioner's clerk attached to the commission. I'm instructed to take inventory of the premises. Charts, plates, instruments, papers." His eyes went past her shoulder, into the workshop, and stopped somewhere near the great table. "All papers."

"The shop is registered," Gideon said. Her voice came out level. She was grateful to it. "You'll find our holdings listed with the port authority. We make maps, Mr. Finch. We don't make trouble."

"No," Finch said, and his eyes were still on the table, on the corner of the atlas visible beneath its cloth. "I don't imagine you do."

He stepped inside without quite waiting to be asked, and Gideon closed the door behind him softly, and stood in her own workshop with her back against it, and understood that the three days she needed had just become whatever time this tired, careful man decided to give her.

* * *

He was thorough. That was the worst of it. A lazy clerk she could have steered; a fool she could have buried in detail. Elias Finch went down the inventory line by line, ticking entries, weighing copper plates in his hands as though honesty had weight, and asking questions that seemed idle and were not. When did the master die. Who apprenticed whom. Where were the working drawings kept. Whether the firm received commissions from abroad.

Gideon answered everything with the truth or very near it, because lies multiply, and she could afford none. Yes, the atlas was complete. Yes, it awaited collection for delivery to the Admiralty, per the dedication. No, she kept no duplicate — *no duplicate yet*, said the voice in her skull, cool as tidewater — and yes, Mr. Corvus had been a private man about his margins. Decorative habits. Mapmakers' whimsies.

Finch wrote *decorative habits* in his folio, and underlined nothing, and looked at nothing, and Gideon thought: he has seen them. He has seen the names and he does not know what they are, but he knows they are something.

At noon a sergeant called him out to the quay, and Gideon stood alone in her workshop listening to the boots and voices below, and did the only sane thing left: she memorized. She stood over the first plate and read the margins until the names burned into her — Hale, T., 1741, ebb tide, north light — then covered the atlas with its cloth and went to the window.

Down in the harbor the seizure was nearly done. Boats were being warped to the army hulks, their crews put ashore with paper receipts for vessels worth more than the soldiers would earn in a lifetime. The countinghouse windows on Quay Lane already had lanterns burning behind them at midday, and clerks' silhouettes moved inside like insects in amber. On the *Gannet*'s deck, soldiers were hauling up her nets for no reason except that they could.

Harborwick had gone quiet in the specific way Gideon dreaded — not silent, but quieter than a working town should be at noon. Hammers still swung, but slower. Voices still called, but lower. It was the quiet of a place deciding, all at once and without discussion, how much of itself it could afford to show.

She thought of Tamsin Hale across the lane in the chandlery, whose husband's initials led the border of the Baltic plate. She thought of the boy Tam who gutted fish on the steps of the market and answered questions with his eyes because his tongue would not. She thought of forty-one names and tried not to count gallows, and counted them anyway.

Then, far up Fore Street, past the salt house, she saw a rider come through the toll arch at an easy canter — easy, because nothing in this town could threaten him — in a scarlet coat cut better than any sergeant's, with three men trailing. He reined in at the church door, read the notice there as though checking another man's work, and then lifted his head and looked down Fore Street, slowly, taking in the shops, the roofs, the masts, the way a man surveys ground he intends to own.

His gaze traveled down the street and stopped at the sign reading CORVUS & MONTROSE.

Even at that distance, Gideon felt it land.

The rider said something to one of his men, smiling — she could not hear it, but she saw the smile, and it was the smile of a man remembering an old joke — and turned his horse toward the harbor, and Gideon stepped back from the window into the shadow of her own workshop with her heart going like a drumbeat, two strokes and a pause, two strokes and a pause.

She did not know his name. But she knew the look of him, because Corvus had worn it sometimes, reading letters from his army years: the look of a man who has survived everything, and enjoyed it.

Somewhere in the town, in a saddlebag or a strongbox or a memory, that man carried Corvus's past. And Corvus's past, folded into hers, was standing at her window with the afternoon light coming in and forty-one names drying slowly on the table behind her.

She picked up her pen. Whatever was coming up Fore Street, the copying would begin tonight.
