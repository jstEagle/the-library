## 3. What the Ledger Knows

The clerk arrived on the second morning, when the smell of the soldiers' cook-fires still hung over Harborwick like a fog that would not lift. Gideon heard him before she saw him — the particular hesitation of his boots on the cobbles outside the workshop, stopping, starting, stopping again, as though the man were rehearsing an entrance he had not been given lines for.

She had spent the hour after dawn preparing herself the way she prepared a plate for printing: cleaning the surfaces, ordering the tools, arranging everything in the workshop to say precisely what she wished it to say. Corvus's chair sat square behind the workbench. His coat hung on its peg, too large for her shoulders but carrying his shape still, and she wore it buttoned to the throat. On the bench lay the atlas, open — but open to the wrong page. To an innocent page. The coast of Norway, all skerries and soundings, nothing in the margins but a few tidy figures of latitude.

When Elias Finch came through the door, he came apologizing with his whole body. He was a narrow man, younger than she had expected, with ink-stained fingers and the stooped carriage of someone who had spent years making himself smaller around larger men. His uniform sat on him the way ill-fitting maps sit on a coastline — technically covering the territory, describing nothing true.

"Master Montrose." He said the name carefully, as if it might bite him. "I'm — that is, the requisition office requires an inventory of all — of everything, sir. Charts, plates, ledgers. I'm to make a list." He held up a ledger of his own, blank, its cover stamped with the army's griffin. "It's only procedure."

"Procedure," Gideon said, and kept her voice where she always kept it, in the lower registers, gravelled with a fictitious age. Twenty years of practice. "Then proceed."

He proceeded. She watched him work and understood within a quarter hour that she had nothing to fear from his competence and everything to fear from his conscience. A lazy clerk she could have managed — laziness has rhythms, and rhythms can be exploited. A corrupt one likewise; corruption can be bought, and she had coin enough hidden in the flour bin. But Finch was neither lazy nor corrupt. He was *conscientious*, which was the worst of all possible fortunes, and he counted everything: the burnishers, the quills, the jars of ground gum arabic, each sheet of Dutch paper in the flat press drawer. He wrote the numbers down in a small, careful hand, and his lips moved as he counted, and Gideon stood at the window grinding ink with a stone against glass, *scritch, scritch, scritch*, letting the rhythm of honest labor fill the room so that her own silence would have something to hide inside.

It was going well until he reached the atlas.

She saw the moment it caught him. He had been turning pages with the brisk disinterest of a man cataloguing rope, and then he stopped, and his breath changed, and Gideon felt the change in the room's air the way sailors feel a shift in the wind before the canvas tells them. He leaned closer over the great folio. His finger hovered above the margin of a chart of the Frisian coast — hovered, did not touch; the man had some instinct for what paper could bear witness to.

"These annotations, sir." He squinted. "They're unusual. Tide-tables, I take you, but the hand changes. Here" — his finger drifted along the margin like a fly testing a window — "and here, and here again. Three different hands, or four. In an atlas made by one man?"

Gideon crossed the room slowly, wiping her hands, giving herself the four steps needed to arrange her face. When she reached the bench she looked down at the page with the mild irritation of a craftsman interrupted over trivia.

"Apprentices," she said. "Corvus made them copy tide-tables into the margins for practice. Bad habit. Ruined more than one good chart." She turned the page with authority, under his hovering finger, and the next margin showed only compass variations. "You'll find my own hand in the later volumes. Cleaner. He despaired of me for years."

Finch looked up at her then, and for one long moment his eyes stayed on hers — pale eyes, water-colored, with a kind of unhappy attention in them, the look of a man who notices things because noticing is his nature and wishes it were not.

"You worked with Master Corvus long, sir?"

"Since I was a boy." True. Every word must be true at its root if it was to hold weight. "He took me in when my father died. There was no one else."

"That was kind of him," Finch said quietly, and there was something in the way he said it — not a question, not quite — that made Gideon's spine prickle beneath the borrowed coat. He bent back to his ledger and wrote: *One (1) atlas, folio, incomplete, coastlines of the North Sea and Baltic, marginalia of no account.*

*Of no account.* She could have kissed the phrase. She watched him write it, watched the nib scratch out those three words that might yet stand between forty families and the rope, and thought: *this man has just saved forty lives without knowing it, and God help us both if he ever comes to know it.*

He finished by noon. At the door he paused, half-turned, his ledger clutched to his chest like a breviary.

"They're saying there'll be arrests," he said, to the doorframe rather than to her. "In the next parish first. They're saying it's — smugglers, and worse than smugglers. People who moved people." His ears had gone red. "I only mention it, sir, because — a man in your trade keeps records. Records can be read in ways their keepers never intended."

The workshop was very quiet. Out on the harbor, gulls screamed over boats that were no longer the town's boats.

"I'll remember that," Gideon said, "if I ever keep any."

Finch nodded once, quickly, as though glad to have the exchange over, and fled.

* * *

That night she did not sleep.

She waited until the soldiers' watch changed at the hour past midnight, until the last lantern in the requisitioned countinghouse across the square guttered out, and then she lit a single candle — shielded, hooded, its light cut down to a coin-sized circle on the bench — and brought the atlas to her.

There is a particular terror in opening a book you already know by heart, when knowing it has become a crime. Her hands were steady — her hands were always steady; Corvus had trained that into her before he'd trained her to draw a line, saying *a trembling hand lies even when the mouth tells truth* — but her heart beat against her ribs like something caged and frantic, and she made herself breathe in the slow four-count he had taught her for fine hatching.

The margins.

For twenty years she had seen them every working day and not seen them, the way a man who lives beside a river stops hearing it. They were practice exercises. They were apprentices' scrawls. They were tide-tables copied in different hands because different fugitives had copied them, in this workshop, on nights when the forge was banked and the shutters drawn, learning letters so they could pass as free men in the ports beyond the sea. That was the surface story, the story even the ink half-believed about itself.

But underneath the tide-tables, folded into them the way a watermark lives inside paper — visible only when you hold the page up to the right light, or to the right fear — were the names.

She turned to the Frisian coast, the same page Finch had lingered over, and looked at it properly for the first time in years.

*T. Hale.* Tamsin's husband, dead now two winters, drowned off the point with a cargo of human hope in his hold. His name was written there in a crabbed, hurried fist, disguised as a column of tidal heights: *T. H., the 4th day, height 9 feet,* and anyone reading it casually would see arithmetic. But Gideon knew. Gideon had been in the room — fourteen years old, dressed as a boy, sent to fetch lamp-oil that did not exist so the real business could happen while she was out of the way — and had come back too soon, and seen Thomas Hale writing his own name into the master's atlas like a man signing a confession, laughing softly because he was too frightened to do anything else.

*J. Oostende.* The chandler. Dead of a fever, thank God, past the army's reach.

*M. Brandt* and *M. Brandt,* twice, the sisters who had run the dyer's yard, whose indigo vats had hidden more than cloth over the years. One sister alive, one transported already, before all this began, on an older charge.

She turned the pages and the past turned with them. Here the night the Vane family went out through the marshes in a fish-cart, all seven of them, and the youngest child had cried and a soldier on the bridge had come to look, and Corvus had stood in the road being loudly drunk and helpful until the cart was clear. Here the season of the false-bottomed herring barrels. Here the name of the innkeeper at the sign of the Anchor, who was not brave, who had done it entirely for money and complained every time, and whose name was in the margin anyway, because the atlas did not distinguish between courage and greed. It recorded only what was done.

Forty names.

She counted them the way a debtor counts what he owes, touching each one lightly with the tip of her dry finger, never the wet, never the inked. Thirty-one on the coastlines proper. Nine more scattered through the harbor charts, in the soundings, in the little rhumb-line figures that pointed, if you knew how to read them, at specific cellars, specific lofts, specific nights. Forty names, and for every name a family, and for every family the gallows was no abstraction. She had seen the army's justice before, in another town, another lifetime. It did not weigh evidence; it weighed example. One hanging per offense, publicly, and sometimes two, and children made to watch because it was thought instructive.

Forty names. Forty gallows. The arithmetic would not stay in the ledger of the mind; it spilled out into the dark workshop, into the smell of ink and tallow, and she sat with her hands flat on the bench on either side of the open book, breathing, while somewhere out in the harbor the seized boats knocked against each other in the swell like restless animals.

And beneath the fear, surfacing slowly the way drowned things surface, an older grief. Because she could not look at these names without looking at the hands that had written them, and she could not think of the hands without thinking of the man who had gathered them here, page by page, year by year, in the most dangerous book in England.

Why keep the record at all? She had asked him once. She had been young, and furious — someone had been taken, someone she had known, a woman named Ruth who used to give her honeyed bread — and she had demanded to know what good the names were doing anyone, written down like that, sitting in a book that could hang them all.

Corvus had been mending a pen by candlelight, and he had not looked up.

"Because forgetting is what they want," he said. "Burn the records, and it never happened, and if it never happened it can happen again tomorrow. These names are proof, girl. Proof that it was done, and done often, and done by ordinary people — bakers and chandlers and a miserly innkeeper — which means it can be done again by ordinary people. An atlas shows where the land is. This one shows where the decent land is. It shows the coast is longer than they think." Then he had looked up, and smiled his terrible gentle smile. "And one day it may need to be moved. And the moving of it will be someone's whole life's work, and I am training that someone whether she consents or not."

She had thought he meant the atlas.

Sitting now in the coin of candlelight, Gideon understood — fully, coldly, twenty years late — that he had meant her. That every lesson had been double-inked: the craft on the surface, survival underneath. How to stand so a stranger reads you wrong. How to speak low. How to let a lie be mostly true. How to count exits in every room, how to know which neighbors talk, how to carry dangerous knowledge in the skull where no soldier could requisition it. He had known the army would come someday. Perhaps not for the atlas specifically — perhaps just the general coming that empire makes routine — but he had built her like a ship built for weather, and now the weather had arrived, and the builder was two years in the churchyard, and the ship was alone at sea with forty souls in her hold.

She closed the atlas gently, as one closes a coffin or a cradle.

Then she did what she had been avoiding since Finch's finger hovered over the Frisian margin. She tested her memory against the pages, name by name, line by line, the way she would proof a plate before printing. *T. Hale, height 9 feet. J. Oostende, the 11th, ebb at dawn.* She recited them silently into the dark of her own skull, fixing each disguise, each tide-figure, each hand. Some she knew already. Some she reconstructed from context. Two, near the end — recent, written in the last year of Corvus's life — she found she could not place at all, strangers whose stories had ended before her part in the story began, and she sat with those two names for a long time, learning them like unfamiliar coastline, sounding them syllable by syllable until they held fast in memory alongside the rest.

Forty names, and by the time the candle had burned down to its collar, she carried every one of them in the one place the army's writ did not run.

But memory was not enough. Memory could not be handed to Tamsin Hale. Memory could not testify in a court, could not be shown to a ship's captain, could not survive her own drowning. Paper was the danger and paper was the answer, and the thought that formed as she pinched out the candle was simple and enormous and terrible:

*The atlas must be copied, and then it must cease to exist.*

She sat in the dark and listened to the knocking of the seized boats, and understood also — with the clarity that comes only at the bottom of a sleepless night — that copying would take days she did not have, and that a man with water-colored eyes had written *of no account* in an army ledger today, and that such mercies were candles too, small and hooded and burning down.

Across the square, unseen in the blackness, the countinghouse lantern flickered alight again. Someone was working late over the requisition lists.

Someone was counting.

* * *

Morning brought fog off the water, thick as wool, and with the fog came Captain Aldous Wren.
