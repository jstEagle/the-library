## 5. A Visitor Who Knew Her Father

The knock came at midmorning, and it was wrong.

Gideon knew the sounds of her own door the way she knew the grain of her drafting table — the light rap of Tamsin Hale's knuckle, the flat slap of the fish-boy's palm when he had a delivery, the patient scratch of old Hobbe Marsh who never could manage a handle with his nets still on. This was none of those. This was three measured strikes, spaced like drumbeats, delivered by a man who had never in his life needed to ask entry anywhere.

She set down her pen mid-stroke. The coastline of some foreign shore — she would not remember later which — trailed off unfinished into blank vellum, and for one absurd moment she thought of it as a road running out, and herself standing at its end.

Through the window she saw them first: two soldiers on the cobbles below, muskets shouldered with the studied idleness of men ordered to look relaxed. And between them, dismounting from a grey gelding with the unhurried economy of someone who had been doing hard things all his life, an officer in the red coat of the regulars — older than the others by twenty years, broad through the shoulder gone slightly to seed, silver at the temples and a scar bisecting one eyebrow like a surveyor's line drawn carelessly across a good map.

He looked up at the workshop window before he touched the door. He looked at it the way she looked at coastlines: measuring, memorizing, noting the approaches.

Gideon took one breath, then another, and made her face into something a tradesman would wear — polite, faintly bored, a man interrupted at honest work. Twenty years of practice. She had built Gideon Montrose plank by plank the way her master had built his charts, and she had only ever once doubted that it would hold her weight.

*He cannot know,* she told herself, descending the stairs. *No one living knows.*

She opened the door before the third knock could fall.

"Captain Montrose." The officer removed his hat. Up close he was handsomer than the scar suggested and less kind than either. His voice was warm the way a hearth is warm in a house where something has been burning. "Aldous Wren. I have the honor of overseeing His Majesty's requisition of this fair and inconveniently situated town."

"Captain Wren." She inclined her head the correct degree — not so deep as a civilian groveling, not so shallow as an equal. "You'll find my workshop in order. My ledgers are current. If your clerks require anything, they need only ask."

"Oh, I'm sure they will ask." He smiled, and stepped inside without being asked, which was itself a message. His boots were polished to a black shine; they left damp prints on her clean floor and he did not glance down at them. "I confess I requested this particular duty myself. Harborwick. It's been — how long? — fifteen years since I last stood in this street. Sixteen. There was a tavern on the corner, then. The Drowned Bell."

"The Anchor and Rope," Gideon said. "It burned."

"So I heard. A shame. Good ale." He turned in the narrow entryway, taking in the stairwell, the hanging charts, the smell of ink and lamp-black and the salt that lived permanently in the plaster. "And above us, I suppose, the famous workshop. Corvus Montrose's shop. There can't be another like it in England."

"You knew my master."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, submerged, gone. A fish under ice. "Knew him?" Wren laughed, and it was a good laugh, a soldier's laugh, the kind that makes friends in taverns. "Captain — may I call you Captain? It saves such tedious fencing — I served under the man for six years. Flanders, then the coastal surveys. He taught me more about reading ground than any instructor His Majesty ever paid for. When word came that he'd died, I drank to him. Alone. He'd have hated company for it."

"He wasn't fond of funerals," Gideon said carefully. "Or of talk."

"No. He wasn't." Wren was looking past her now, up the stairs, at the landing where the atlas lay wrapped in oilcloth in its drawer because she could not bear to leave it unwrapped and could not risk leaving it bare. "May I?"

It was not truly a request. She stood aside and let him climb, and followed two steps behind him, and hated every stair.

* *

The workshop was her sanctum, and having Aldous Wren in it felt like watching a stranger's hands move over her own face. He did not touch anything — that was the unsettling part. Most visitors touched everything: lifted a pen, thumbed the edge of a copper plate, dragged a finger along a coastline and asked *did you draw this?* Wren touched nothing. He walked the perimeter slowly, hands clasped behind his back, reading the room the way an engineer reads a fortress.

His gaze snagged on the drafting table. On the half-finished plate. On the pens laid out in their row, cut nibs of varying widths, each ground to Corvus's specifications, each maintained by Gideon's hand these twenty years.

"You've kept his tools," Wren said. "Exactly as he kept them. Nibs in order of width, left to right. Ink pots labeled in his hand." He leaned close over the row of pots without touching. "That's his writing. You've preserved even the labels."

"He liked things kept his way. I saw no reason to change."

"No," Wren said softly. "I imagine you didn't." He straightened. "You must forgive me, Captain Montrose, but you are something of a curiosity. A mapmaker's apprentice who inherits a trade. In my experience apprentices inherit debts and bad backs. They don't inherit workshops."

"My master had no family."

"No family," Wren agreed. "None he'd acknowledge." He drifted toward the window, toward the view of the harbor where the seized boats rode at anchor with soldiers' laundry strung between their masts, and Gideon watched the back of his neck and calculated distances — to the door, to the stairs, to the iron poker beside the cold stove — and understood that she was doing it, and understood what it meant that she was doing it.

"I confess I came partly for the requisition," Wren said to the window. "And partly out of an old man's sentiment. And partly—" he turned, and his face was pleasant, and open, and utterly unreadable, "—out of curiosity about a story I heard once, long ago, and never confirmed."

"Stories are rarely worth confirming."

"This one had a name in it." He crossed back toward the table, unhurried. "When I knew Corvus, he wore a locket under his shirt. Army issue, the cheap sort, but he'd have fought you for touching it. We used to joke — the young ones joked — that it held a woman's portrait. Corvus let us think what we liked. But once, in Flanders, he took fever. Bad fever. For three days he didn't know where he was, and I sat with him because no one else would, and he talked." Wren paused at the far side of the drafting table, and set one fingertip, very lightly, at the exact center of the blank vellum there. "He talked about a daughter."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a soldier laughed at nothing.

"He had no daughter," Gideon said.

"He believed he did. Or he feared he did. Fever makes strange confessions." Wren's fingertip rested on the vellum, motionless, and Gideon found she could not look away from it. "He spoke of a child sent away. A child kept secret. He said — I have remembered the words for thirty years, Captain, words do for me what maps do for you — he said, *'If anyone asks after her, she drowned. Let her be drowned. Drowned girls are safe girls.'*" Wren looked up. His eyes were pale grey, the color of the sea before weather. "Now. That is a curious thing for a man without a daughter to say, wouldn't you agree?"

"A fever dream. Men in fever see children everywhere."

"They do." He smiled again, and withdrew his finger, and the vellum showed no mark at all, though Gideon thought she would see the place forever. "And yet. When the army records listed Corvus Montrose's household, they listed an apprentice taken in the year of his retirement. No name given — he paid the parish clerk to leave it blank, which cost him more than the apprentice's keep, I'd wager. And the apprentice became the master. Inherited the shop, the trade, the name itself, if the harbor registry is accurate." He tilted his head. "Corvus Montrose. It's an unusual name to take, isn't it? An apprentice might call himself Smith or Wright or anything common. But to take the master's whole name — that's not inheritance. That's becoming."

"You flatter me with philosophy, Captain. My master wished his trade to continue. Names carry trade."

"They do indeed." Wren walked to the shelf where the ledgers stood — the requisitioned-in-advance ledgers, the ones Finch would inventory tomorrow — and ran his eye along their spines without pulling one out. "Here is what I know about names, Captain Montrose. I have spent thirty years in the army learning that a name is the most dangerous thing a man carries. More dangerous than powder. Names get men hanged. Names start wars. And a man — or a person — who has chosen a name, rather than received one, has something to hide that is worth more to the right buyer than gold." He turned from the shelf. "I say *a person*, you notice. I choose my words with care. I am careful by nature."

"And I say I am the master's apprentice, and his heir, and that this workshop has work waiting." Gideon heard her own voice and was proud of it: level, dry, faintly impatient. Terror wearing a tradesman's coat. "If the requisition requires my ledgers, take them. If it requires my testimony, ask your questions. But if it requires me to gossip about my dead master's fevers, I'll thank you to conduct that business with someone who has time for it."

For a long moment Wren said nothing at all. Then he laughed — the same warm laugh, but thinner now, stretched over something with edges.

"There he is," he said. "There's Corvus. I thought I saw him in you the moment I came up those stairs. The same stubborn set of the shoulders. The same way of answering a question with a bill of sale." He put his hat back on, settling it with two fingers. "I'll tell you plainly, Captain, because I respect plainness and I suspect you're starved of it. The army wants this town's paper — every ledger, every chart, every scrap with a name written on it. Someone in London believes smugglers have operated out of Harborwick for years, moving people, not just brandy, and believes the proof is in the maps. Maps hold margins, Captain. Margins hold handwriting. Handwriting holds names." He descended one stair, then paused, half-turned, the lamplight from the window catching the scar on his brow. "I will personally oversee the inventory of this workshop. I will read every margin in every book you own. And if I find what I expect to find, then we will speak again — you and I — about daughters, and drownings, and how much a quiet life is worth, and to whom."

"And if you find nothing?"

"Then I am an old soldier chasing fever dreams," Wren said pleasantly, "and you are a mapmaker with work waiting, and neither of us loses anything but sleep." He clapped the brim of his hat down firmly. "Until tomorrow, Captain Montrose. I look forward to your margins."

His boots went down the stairs in slow, deliberate time, each step a promise. The front door opened; the cold harbor air came up the stairwell smelling of tar and rope. Then it closed, and the soldiers' footsteps faded, and the hooves on the cobbles diminished toward the countinghouse, and Gideon stood alone in her own workshop with her heart going like a hammer on a hull.

She locked the door. She stood with her forehead against the wood.

*Drowned girls are safe girls.* Thirty years he had carried those words, and he had carried them here — not to London, not to some archive, but here, up her stairs, into her room, to lay them on her table like a card played face-up. He had not arrested her. He had not accused her. He had done something far worse: he had shown her his hand, or part of it, and waited to see hers.

She climbed the stairs and stood at the drafting table and looked down at the blank vellum, at the place where his finger had rested. Then, because her hands wanted work and her mind wanted silence, she began finishing the coastline she had abandoned — some indifferent shore, soundings and shoals, a lighthouse that existed only because the plate demanded one. Ink flowed. Her hand steadied. This was the gift Corvus had actually given her: when the world became unbearable, make the world smaller. Make it a page. Draw the water. The water never asks what you are.

But halfway down the sheet her pen slowed, stopped.

Because she was thinking of margins. Wren had said it himself, casually, precisely: *maps hold margins.* And Gideon knew — had known for twenty years, since the night of the storm she did not let herself remember — exactly what crowded the margins of the atlas lying wrapped in its oilcloth three feet from where Wren had stood. Forty names, give or take. Forty families who had passed fugitives through Harborwick under cover of Corvus's charts, recorded in the master's small cipher hand because Corvus believed — madly, magnificently — that a map was the safest hiding place in England, because no soldier ever looks at a map except to find the road.

Wren would look. Wren had spent six years learning how Corvus thought, and thirty years to sharpen the memory, and he had come here today not for the requisition but to tell her so.

Tomorrow he would read the margins. Tomorrow the atlas and her identity would begin to braid together into a single rope, and she did not need a chart to know what ropes were for.

Gideon cleaned her pen with steady fingers, laid it in its place among its brothers, left to right by width, exactly as he had kept them, and looked out the window at the harbor where the army's boats rode at anchor and the tide was turning.

Three days. She had told herself she had three days.

She gave herself until dark, and then she began to count the names.
