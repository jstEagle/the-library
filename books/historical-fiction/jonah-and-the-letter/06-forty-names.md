## 6. Forty Names

The Hales' house stood at the crook of the harbor road where the cobbles gave way to packed earth, a low stone dwelling with salt-bleached shutters and a garden gone wild with rosemary. Gideon had passed it a thousand times in twenty years without ever once stopping. That was the way of the names in the margins: they lived alongside you, borrowed your weather, bought your bread, and you never once let your eyes rest on them longer than any other eyes would.

She carried nothing but a rolled chart — a legitimate commission, an inland survey of the mill brook that she had invented two nights ago and drawn well enough to be real. If anyone asked, she was delivering work. Mapmakers delivered work. It was the most ordinary thing about her.

The town was not ordinary this morning. She felt it before she saw it, the way one feels a change in pressure behind the ears when a storm stands up over open water. Women clustered at the well in knots of three and four instead of the usual loose pairs. The fish-market stalls were half-abandoned. And everywhere, moving through the conversation like a current under ice, the same two words: *next parish*. Arrests in the next parish. A cooper taken from his yard before breakfast. Soldiers on the Marsh Road with a list.

Gideon walked slower. She counted doorways between herself and the Hales' gate and made her face do what it had learned to do twenty years ago — nothing at all.

At the gate she stopped and looked down at the rosemary as though she'd come for cuttings. Then, quietly: "Mistress Hale. I've brought the survey of your water meadows."

It was a lie so thin a child could see through it. Tamsin Hale had no water meadows. But through the window came the sound of a latch, and then the door opening just wide enough, and Tamsin's voice, flat as slate: "You'd best come in before the wind takes it."

* * *

Tamsin Hale had been beautiful once, in the way the town remembered and would not let her forget. Now she was forty-one and hard-used, with grey coming through the black of her hair like frost through turned earth, and hands that had gutted fish and buried a husband and kept a household standing on nothing but will. She closed the door. She did not bar it — bars invited questions — but she stood against it a moment as if its weight were the only thing holding her upright.

"You've come about the names," she said.

Not a question. Gideon set the rolled chart on the table and did not answer quickly. Twenty years of silence had taught her the value of a pause. "What names would those be?"

"Don't." Tamsin's voice cracked only slightly, like ice underfoot. "Don't do that to me in my own kitchen. Jonah Hale's name is first on whatever list is written in that book of yours. I've known it since the night he didn't come home. I've been waiting six years for someone to knock on that door with paper in their hand." She moved past Gideon to the hearth, where a pot of something plain sent up its small steam, and stirred it without looking at it. "I only thought it would be soldiers. I didn't think it would be you."

"You knew it was me?"

"I knew it was Corvus's shop." She turned then, and her eyes were dry and terribly steady. "He used to come here, your master. Afterward. To sit with me and drink one cup of tea and say nothing, which was more comfort than anyone else managed. He had ink on his fingers every time, even in his mourning coat. Ink on his fingers and a look like a man carrying water in his hands across a crowded room." She gestured at the table. "Sit down, Montrose. Or whatever I ought to call you. Sit down and tell me how bad it is."

Gideon sat. The chair was worn smooth by Jonah Hale's years of sitting, and she thought of his name in the margin, written in her own dead master's hand: *J. Hale — the third boat — trust the tide, not the man.* Six words that could hang him if he were alive, and could hang her if he were not.

"It's bad," she said. "The army has requisitioned the ledgers. There's a clerk inventorying everything in the workshop, and he's not a fool. And yesterday a captain arrived — Wren. Aldous Wren. He served with Corvus in the old campaign, and he's asking questions that have nothing to do with cargo manifests."

"What kind of questions?"

"About my master's family."

Tamsin went still, the wooden spoon suspended above the pot. Steam rose between them. "You haven't any family," she said slowly. "That's what everyone says. The mapmaker took an apprentice and no one speaks of—"

She stopped. Her eyes went to Gideon's face and stayed there, reading it the way one reads a chart — the soundings, the hazards, the deep water close inshore. Gideon watched her arrive at it and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that her face wasn't already saying.

"God in heaven," Tamsin whispered. She set the spoon down very carefully, as if it might go off. "Twenty years. In my own parish. We passed you at market. You sold my Jonah a chart of the estuary two months before—" Her hand came up to her mouth, and for one moment the hardness broke and Gideon saw underneath it the woman who had waited up all night for a boat that the tide brought back empty.

"Yes," Gideon said quietly. "And now you know the thing that would hang us both, so we may as well speak plainly, because neither of us can afford the other as an enemy."

That steadied her. Fear did that; Gideon had seen it do it before. Terror scattered people, but shared terror gathered them, drew tight the little circle of the doomed until they stood shoulder to shoulder. She had hated learning that lesson. She was grateful for it now.

Tamsin sat down across from her. Outside, a cart went by, and both women listened to it pass with identical attention, identical stillness, and when it had faded Tamsin laughed — a short, terrible laugh with nothing in it of mirth.

"They're saying the next parish already," she said. "Martha Cooper's husband was taken this morning. They put him in a cart in his shirtsleeves. His wife ran after the cart for a quarter mile and a soldier pushed her down into the ditch, and no one who saw it will meet her eye today because meeting her eye would mean admitting they saw." She folded her hands on the table. Scarred knuckles, a wedding band worn thin as wire. "How many, Gideon? How many names?"

"Forty."

The word sat between them like a stone on the table. Tamsin nodded slowly, once, twice, a woman counting.

"Forty families," she said. "Forty gallows, or forty transportations, which is the same thing slower. And every one of them thinking themselves safe because it was six years ago and the sea keeps its secrets." She looked up. "Does it? Keep them?"

"No. Paper keeps them. Paper remembers everything." Gideon heard her own voice go flat and cold and recognized her master in it. "That atlas has forty names in its margins, written in a cipher of Corvus's own devising, and I am the only person alive who can read it whole — and the army has it in an inventory crate in my own workshop."

* * *

From the window of the house across the lane, Tamsin Hale had watched the mapmaker come up the road that morning, and she had known — before the lie about water meadows, before anything — that the day she had dreaded for six years had finally sent someone.

She had rehearsed this, in a manner of speaking. Not the particulars, but the posture of it: what she would do when the reckoning came for Jonah's name. She had decided long ago she would not weep and she would not beg and she would not give anyone the satisfaction of being surprised. Jonah had died doing what he believed was God's own work, ferrying frightened people across a dark channel to a coast where no one would ask their names, and Tamsin had spent every day since deciding, over and over again, that she did not regret him and would not apologize for him even to the gallows.

But she had not rehearsed this: the mapmaker sitting across her kitchen table with a man's name and a man's clothes and twenty years of silence in her face, and finding that what Tamsin felt was not dread but a fierce, scalding relief.

Because someone else knew. All these years she had carried Jonah alone — his name, his risk, the nights he'd come home smelling of other people's fear and gone straight to the hearth to burn his boots' mud off before bed — and carrying it alone had been its own slow dying. Now here was Corvus's ghost's child, keeper of the same secret, and Tamsin could have kissed her.

"The clerk," Tamsin said now, back in the kitchen, back in the present tense where things could still be acted upon. "The one inventorying. Can he read the cipher?"

"No. But he doesn't need to. The colonial office has men who read everything. Codes are their trade the way charts are mine. Once it reaches them, it's a matter of weeks."

"Weeks." Tamsin rose and began moving about the kitchen — not usefully, Gideon understood, but because her body needed somewhere to put its energy. She banked the fire. She moved a jar. She straightened a cloth that was already straight. "Then we have weeks. Is there a way to get it out?"

"There's a way to get *the truth* out. Not the atlas itself — it's guarded now, or will be within days. But I know every name. Every mark, every annotation. I could copy them."

"Copy them onto what?"

"Chart offcuts. Scraps small enough to sew into a coat lining."

Tamsin stopped her pacing. She stood by the hearth with the firelight making copper of the grey in her hair, and Gideon watched her think — watched her run the arithmetic of it, the weights on either side. Forty families against one woman's neck. One woman's neck against a town full of children. Tamsin Hale had done this arithmetic every night for six years; it took her no time at all.

"Where would they go, these papers? Where does a thing like that become safe?"

"Away. Out of the country, past the reach of the colonial office, into hands that hate the Crown enough to protect its enemies' evidence — or at least to profit by it. Somewhere the names become history instead of verdicts."

"And how do things leave Harborwick?"

Gideon leaned back. Here it was, then — the reason the visit had to be here, the question beneath the question. "Boats," she said. "Except the army seized every boat in the harbor on Tuesday."

"Seized the harbor boats, yes." Tamsin smiled, and for a moment Gideon saw the young woman Jonah must have married, the one who'd waved him off from this same shore a hundred times. "The army seized what the army could see. But Harborwick's been running things past revenue men since before the Crown had a navy worth the name, and there isn't a hole in this coastline your master didn't chart and Jonah didn't use. There's a ship. The *Meridian*, out of Amsterdam, riding at anchor beyond the point where the army's boats don't bother to go because the draft's too shallow for their keels. She takes on her last cargo the day after tomorrow and sails on the morning tide three days hence."

"Three days." Gideon said it aloud to hear the size of it. Three days to copy forty names in cipher onto scraps, hide them, move them past three checkpoints, and get them aboard a ship whose captain she'd need to convince to take them. "Who commands her?"

"A Captain Voss. He owes me." Something crossed Tamsin's face, quick and private — grief wearing debt's clothing. "Jonah carried more than fugitives on that channel, some nights. Things worth money to men in Amsterdam. Voss was one of the men the money reached. He'll take your papers. He'll grumble and charge double and swear he's risking his neck for a stranger, and he'll take them, because Jonah Hale's widow asking is not a stranger asking." She came back to the table and sat, and took Gideon's hands across it, her grip hard as a man-o'-war's rigging. "Now listen to me, because I'll say this once. You are not to carry those papers yourself. Whatever you're planning — and I can see you're planning, you have your master's whole face when you plan — you are not the person to move them. Your face is known at every door in this town as the mapmaker. The soldiers will be watching the shop. Let me think on who moves freely, who the red coats don't see because they've decided not to see them."

"There's no one else who knows what the papers mean—"

"They don't need to know what they mean. They need to know where to put them and keep their mouths shut, and that's all anyone needs to know, if you think on it. Isn't that the whole of your master's method? Nobody carries the whole map." Tamsin's eyes glinted. "He split the knowledge so no one person could hang everybody. Smart old spider. So. Three days. You copy. I'll find the carrier. And Gideon—"

"Mistress Hale."

"Tamsin." Her grip tightened. "Six years ago my husband went out on a black-water tide because he believed a person's life was worth more than his own safety, and I have regretted him not one morning since, including the mornings I woke to the knock on the door. Don't you dare be braver than he was. If it comes to choosing between the papers and yourself, you choose yourself, and you deliver them anyway, and you let the rest of us carry our own share. Do you understand me?"

Gideon did not answer at once. Through the window, past Tamsin's shoulder, she could see the harbor, grey under a scudding sky, and the masts of the seized boats standing in a row like a forest of bare trees, and somewhere among the countinghouse windows a flag that had not flown over Harborwick in her lifetime. Forty names. Forty gallows. And a widow's hands around hers, rough and warm and absolutely certain.

"I understand you," she said.

"Good." Tamsin released her and stood, briskly, the audience over. At the door she pressed something into Gideon's palm — a small coin wrapped in cloth, a token, a captain's recognition piece. "Show Voss this. He'll know it." Then, softer, with the door already opening onto the bright cold street: "Three days. Morning tide. And Gideon — Martha Cooper's husband is in a cell right now because a soldier wrote his name on a list. See that yours ends better."

* * *

Gideon walked home the long way, along the water, and the town whispered around her like tidewater through gravel.

They were saying it in doorways now, no longer hiding it: arrests in the next parish. A name spoken and hushed. A shutter closing early. Harborwick going quiet the way towns go quiet before fever or press-gangs — not silent, but *careful*, every word weighed, every neighbor re-measured. She passed the cooper's wife's sister standing white-faced at her gate, and no one spoke to her, and Gideon understood that she was watching the future arrive early, the shape of her own town three days from now if she failed.

At the harbor's edge she stopped and looked out at the *Meridian*'s anchorage, invisible past the point but as real to her now as the stones under her feet. Three days. The morning tide. Forty names to move from her memory to her pen to a ship's hold to a world where they could no longer kill anyone.

She put her hand in her pocket and felt the wrapped coin, and thought of Tamsin's voice: *nobody carries the whole map.*

When she reached the workshop, the lamp in her own window was burning where she'd left it — and beside it, on the sill, sat a folded note sealed with the army's stamp.

Elias Finch would return in the morning. The inventory continued.

And somewhere in the crate marked for the colonial office, forty names lay waiting to be read.
