## 7. The Copying

The workshop after dark was a different country.

By day it belonged to trade — the smell of hide glue and cut vellum, the clutter of weights and pens, the great table scarred by twenty years of blades. But when the candle was lit and the shutters drawn, it became the country Gideon had built for herself, plank by plank and rule by rule, and no soldier had yet found the road into it.

She worked best at night. Master Corvus had taught her that much before he taught her anything else: *Daylight is for men who want to be seen, child. Night is for work that must survive them.* She had been eleven, maybe twelve, and had thought he spoke of cartography alone.

Now she pulled the atlas from its hiding place beneath the third floorboard — the one with the knot that looked like an eye — and laid it open on the table, weighting the corners with the brass strips she used for stretching paper. The candle she trimmed twice, then shaded with a tin reflector so that its light fell in one narrow cone and nowhere else. Anyone passing in the street would see nothing but the dimmest suggestion of a lamp behind horn panels, the kind any tradesman might burn while balancing accounts.

Then she began.

The offcuts were the answer; she had known it since Finch left, though she had not let herself think it plainly until the town went quiet. A whole page could be found. A whole page would hang her. But chart offcuts — the trimmings and misrulers, the strips sliced from the edges of fair sheets, the spoiled corners where a blot had ruined a coastline — those were everywhere in a working map-shop, so common as to be invisible. No inventory clerk counts scraps. No searcher looks twice at what he has swept aside a hundred times.

She had gathered a week's worth of them into a drawer: narrow ribbons of paper, squares gone crooked, pieces barely large enough for two names if the hand were small enough. Her hand was small enough. It had always been small enough, and for once in her life she was glad of it.

She cut a fresh quill, tested it on her thumb-nail, and turned to the first margin.

*Hale, Tamsin's Josiah.* His name sat at the head of the list, in Corvus's cramped left-leaning script, tucked into the curve of the Devonshire coast as though it were a sounding — *Josiah Hale, 14 fathoms* — invisible to any eye that did not know to look. Beneath him, following the shoreline south: the ferryman Cobb. The chandler's widow Prentice. The two brothers at the salt-works whose names Gideon had never spoken aloud in twenty years because speaking them even silently felt like carrying coals through fire.

Forty names. She had counted them the night before, lying awake in the dark above the shop, and the counting had been a kind of prayer, or the ruins of one. Forty names, forty gallows. Tonight they would begin to have copies.

She set the quill down beside the first offcut and made herself slow. This was the discipline, the real discipline — not the drawing of coastlines, which came to her now as naturally as walking, but this: the deliberate slowing of a hand that wanted to hurry. Fear makes for bad ink-work. Fear thickens the strokes, blots the descenders, leaves the tell-tale tremor that any examiner reads as easily as a heading. She breathed the way Corvus had taught her to breathe before ruling a long line — in through the nose, four beats; hold, four; out through the mouth, eight — and when her pulse had settled into the rhythm of the lamp-flame, she wrote.

*Cobb, ferryman.*

Small. Smaller than she usually worked, small as a fly's footprint, and yet clean — every letter formed, none abbreviated into suspicion. She blew across the wet ink and did not touch it. While it dried she studied the next name, memorizing not the letters but the shape of Corvus's hand around them, because the copy must be hers and yet must carry no signature of hers either; these names, if they survived, would be read by strangers who might someday be questioned, and a stranger must never be able to say *this hand belongs to the mapmaker of Harborwick.*

So she altered. A loop opened here, a serif dropped there, the angle of the downstrokes shifted half a degree toward the vertical. It felt like murder, slightly — like killing off a version of herself — and she supposed that was exactly what it was.

*Prentice, chandler's widow.*

*Salt-brothers, both.*

One strip filled. She laid it on the warming stone near the chimney-wall, where the residual heat of the day's fire would set the ink without cockling the paper, and took up the next.

And so the night took its shape, and the shape was sanctuary.

There was a mercy in it that she did not examine too closely, because examining mercy always felt like inviting its withdrawal. For three hours there was nothing in the world but the cone of light, the rasp of the quill, the forty names marching off the old atlas onto their new small lives. Not soldiers. Not Wren, with his long look and his questions about daughters. Not even the fear itself — fear required a future, and the work allowed no future beyond the next stroke. This was what the craft had always given her, she realized, though she had never named it: not a living merely, but a room with one door, and the door barred against everything.

She destroyed each failure instantly. Twice her hand slipped — once when a dog barked in the street, once for no reason at all except that a body cannot be ruled forever — and both times she fed the spoiled scrap to the candle flame and held it over the slate till the last fleck died, then crushed the ash to powder between her fingers and scattered it in the grate among the ordinary leavings of charcoal and dried moss. Waste was evidence. Corvus had burned his mistakes too; she remembered standing on a stool at six years old, feeding him spoiled charts one by one, solemn as an acolyte, understanding even then that the burning mattered more than the drawing.

*Eleven names,* by the time the candle had burned down to its second inch. She flexed her fingers, knuckles cracking, and permitted herself the smallest survey of the work: eleven thin papers on the warming stone, each no wider than her palm, together lighter than a love letter. Sewn flat between the lining and the canvas of her coat, they would feel like nothing — a stiffness, a patch. Coats were searched at checkpoints, but coats were searched quickly, roughly, by hands looking for steel and paper packets, not for eleven whispers distributed along a seam.

Twenty-nine to go. Two nights' work, perhaps three, and the ship sailed on the morning tide—

The knock came at the street door.

Gideon did not move. That was the first discipline, learned in the smuggling years: when the world knocks, be furniture. Her hand stayed flat on the table, the quill lifted clear of the paper, her eyes on the shuttered window where lamplight would betray nothing. The knock came again — twice, apologetic, official. Then a voice, muffled through oak and horn, that she knew worse than she would have known a shout.

"Mistress Montrose? Master Montrose — I beg your pardon — it's Finch. Elias Finch. Of the requisition office."

*God's teeth.* Her heart slammed once, hard, against the ribs, and then she made it stop, made it settle, because the man outside was nervous and nervous men read nervousness through doors. She looked at the table. The atlas lay open under the lamp, margins bare. Eleven drying strips sat on the warming stone in plain view of anyone who stepped past her. The quill in her hand was wet.

"Master Montrose? I can see the lamp."

"One moment." She pitched her voice low and put an old man's gravel in it, the gravel she had worn like a coat for twenty years until it had grown into the skin. "The shop is closed, Mr. Finch."

"I know — I know it is, and I'm sorry for it." A pause; boots shifting on cobbles. "It's the inventory. Colonel Wren wants the ledgers carried to the countinghouse tomorrow at first light, all of them, and there are entries in your master's hand I can't make sense of — abbreviations, soundings mixed with figures — and if I bring them up wrong he'll have my hide, and I thought — that is, if you could just look at—"

He was going to talk himself through the door whether she opened it or not; that was Finch's way. She had three seconds and spent them well. The atlas closed with a whisper — the brass weights kept the boards from clapping — and slid beneath the false bottom of the plan-chest, and her palm came down on the eleven strips of the warming stone and swept them into her apron pocket in one motion that twenty years of lifting plates and pulling proofs had made fluid. The wet quill went into the spill-jar. The only thing on the table, when she crossed the room, was a half-finished chart of the marshes east of the bridge — honest work, commissioned work, the very thing a mapmaker *should* be burning midnight oil over with the army in town — and she set her lamp beside it as she unbarred the door, so that the light's whole meaning was rewritten in the space of a breath.

Finch stood in the street with his cloak collar up and his ledger hugged to his chest like a breastplate, and behind him Harborwick lay silent under a sky the color of wet slate. He looked, she thought, like a man who had been sent to do wrong and had not yet decided whose wrong it was.

"You'll forgive the hour," he said, ducking past her into the warmth. "I couldn't sleep for it, truth told. Figures go round and round in my head like—"

He stopped.

It was only a heartbeat — less — but Gideon watched it happen with the terrible clarity of the sleepless: his gaze snagging on the table, on the marsh chart, and something in his face tightening, not with suspicion exactly but with *recognition*, the way a man who has handled a thousand papers knows paper. Because the chart lay open and the corner of it — the lower right, where she had been testing the quill before he knocked — held a single practice stroke. Not a name. Not a word. Just a looping flourish, ink still glistening, the kind of mark a pen makes when a hand is warming up.

Or when a hand has just stopped writing something.

"You've been at work late," Finch said. His voice had gone careful.

"The army takes my harbor, my boats, and my ledgers, Mr. Finch." She let dryness do the work. "A man may as well finish his maps. It's the only thing left they haven't nailed a notice to."

"Yes." He came toward the table slowly, and she calculated distances without wanting to — three strides, the weight of him, the depth of her apron pocket against the doorframe — and then he did the last thing her racing mind had prepared for. He reached out and turned the marsh chart a quarter-inch, squinting at it professionally, and his finger hovered over the wet flourish and moved past it.

"Marsh ground east of the bridge," he murmured. "That's tidal, isn't it? All that ground. My father kept sheep there, before the great flood of '31. Said you could walk on it in the morning and row over it by noon." He looked up, and for a moment the clerk's anxiety fell away and something older and sadder looked out of his face. "You'd know the tides better than anyone, I suppose. Being who you are."

"Being a mapmaker," Gideon said evenly.

"Of course." He straightened. "Yes. Only a mapmaker." And there it was again, that half-second where he seemed about to add something, some sentence standing fully dressed in his mouth — *I saw the margins, Master Montrose, I saw the names* — and then not saying it, swallowing it whole, his ears going red in the lamplight.

He spread his ledger instead and walked her through the muddle he had invented, or mostly invented; some of the abbreviations genuinely perplexed him, Corvus having compressed *fathoms* and *freshets* and certain less innocent words into private marks no clerk could parse, and Gideon explained them one by one, keeping her voice level, keeping her back angled so the apron pocket hung away from the lamp. Twice Finch glanced at the grate. The second glance lasted longer than the first. She saw him notice the ash — fresh ash, paler than charcoal leavings, the ghost of paper rather than wood — and she saw him decide, visibly, deliberately, in real time, not to have noticed it.

"Well," he said at last, closing the ledger. "That will serve. I'm much obliged for the hour, Master Montrose. I hope I haven't—" He stopped at the door, cloak in hand, and looked back at the marsh chart, at the little drying flourish at its corner. "That's a fine piece of ground, they say. Hard going, but it holds what's put into it. Ground like that doesn't give things up easy."

"Ground keeps secrets better than men do," Gideon said. "It's why I draw it."

Something passed over his face — gratitude, or its exhausted cousin — and he bade her good night and was gone, his footsteps fading down the sleeping street, quicker at the end than a tired man's needed to be.

Gideon barred the door and stood with her forehead against the wood until her breath came slow.

Then she crossed to the table and looked at the marsh chart for a long time.

It was a fair sheet, half-finished — the eastern channels drawn and tinted, the western reaches still blank paper waiting for tomorrow's soundings. Large. Stiff. Destined for the colonel's own office, which meant it would be handled by clerks, rolled and unrolled, admired and filed away — and no one, no one on God's earth, searches the inside of a finished map. She thought of Finch's strange parting words, *ground like that holds what's put into it*, and wondered — not for the first time tonight, and with a feeling she refused to name — whether the man had just handed her the idea, or confirmed one she'd already had, or was playing a deeper game than his sweating face could hold.

It didn't matter. It was the only door left, and it was open.

She fetched the paste pot and the bone folder, laid the eleven strips from her apron pocket along the blank western margin of the marsh chart, and began to fix them down beneath a facing of clean backing paper, thin as an onion skin, so that the names vanished into the body of the sheet — hidden in the very ground, drowned in the very tide, exactly where Corvus would have put them. When the chart was delivered to the countinghouse, as deliver it must, the names would sit in the army's own strong-room, folded into its own paperwork, safer than any bolt-hole in Harborwick. And when she was ready — when the ship and the tide and God concurred — she would ask to verify her soundings for the colonel's office, a professional courtesy, and lift them out again with a mapmaker's clean hands.

Twenty-nine names remained uncopied. Two nights. Perhaps less, now, if Wren's men came for the ledgers sooner than ledgers warranted.

Outside, far off toward the bridge, a drum beat the watch change, and Gideon dipped her pen, and went back to work.
