## 8. Checkpoint One: The Bridge

The river at Harborwick was never more honest than at low tide. Gideon had learned that from Corvus in her first year — *the water tells you what the land is hiding* — and she stood now on the shingle below the workshop with her boots in the wet sand, reading the channel as it emptied toward the sea, the way a woman reads a face for what is not being said.

The bridge crossed the river at its narrowest throat, where the estuary pinched between the mill race and the old stone quay. There was no other way out of town by road. The marsh track to the east flooded twice daily and swallowed carts whole; the cliff path south was a goat's fancy and nothing more. Everything that left Harborwick on wheels or hooves or human legs passed over those five arches, and since Tuesday morning, everything that passed was stopped.

She could see the checkpoint from here even without her glass: two barrels of sand dragged to the bridge's near end, a plank laid across them, four red coats, and a fifth man in a corporal's sash who spent most of his time sitting on the barrel with his hat over his eyes. A brazier smoked beside them. Behind the barrier, a growing heap of opened parcels lay like afterbirth — paper and string torn away, boxes gaping, a hamper of eels spilled across the planks and nobody bothering to gather them.

Gideon counted. She was always counting; Corvus had beaten arithmetic into her bones until numbers were a kind of breathing. Four soldiers visible. Likely two more at the far end or dozing in the toll-house. One corporal. One hour past dawn, and already eleven travelers turned back or delayed. The parcels searched were all outgoing — she watched a carter argue, lose, and haul his unopened inbound goods straight through while the soldiers barely glanced at him.

So. Wren had not simply closed the town. He had made it a mouth that could swallow but not speak.

She climbed back up to the lane and went to fetch the delivery.

* * *

It was an honest errand, which was the whole point of it. Widow Pressen at the rope-walk had owed the workshop a coil of tarred twine for a fortnight, and Gideon had promised it before the soldiers came; promising it again this morning cost her nothing but the walk. She wrapped the twine in brown paper, tied it with plain string, and addressed it in her own careful hand — *Mrs. E. Pressen, Rope-Walk Lane* — because if she was going to learn how the checkpoint worked, she would learn it carrying something no soldier alive would want to open.

She also carried, sewn flat against her ribs inside her waistcoat, a single sheet of chart offcut bearing nine names in a hand so small it might have been fly-speck. Not the forty. Nine, as a test of her own nerve. If she could walk past a bayonet with nine names against her heart and her pulse steady, then perhaps she could do it with forty. Perhaps.

Her pulse was not steady. She noted that too, the way she noted wind speed and barometric fall, filed it, and kept walking.

The morning was raw, the sky the color of pewter gone dull, gulls screaming over the seized fishing fleet where navy men clambered through the boats with their boots unlaced, taking inventory of other people's livelihoods. Gideon joined the trickle of traffic moving toward the bridge — a milkwoman, two lads with a barrow of kelp, old Hobb the cooper dragging a handcart of staves. Nobody spoke. That was new. Harborwick was a town that traded insults across streets the way other towns traded gossip, and now it moved in silence, eyes down, the whole population become suddenly fascinated by the ground.

The corporal lifted his hat as they approached. Under it he was young, sunburnt, and bored, which Gideon thought might be the most dangerous combination in the army after cruelty.

"Bridge is closed to goods," he said. "Persons may cross for lawful business. Parcels are subject to search."

"Since when does His Majesty fear a coil of twine?" Hobb grumbled, and got a rifle-butt across his handcart for his trouble, not hard, just enough to make the point.

Gideon waited her turn. When the corporal beckoned her forward she held out the parcel at arm's length, letting him take it, keeping her hands empty and loose — she'd watched Finch do it, the little clerk, the way a man shows he is harmless: offer everything, want nothing.

"What is it?"

"Tarred twine for Mrs. Pressen at the rope-walk. She'll be wanting it before the spring rigging."

He weighed it in one hand. Turned it over. And then — and this was the thing Gideon had come three miles of cold morning to learn — he tore the paper.

Not gently. Not with ceremony. He ripped the wrapping as a child rips a present, shook the twine out onto the plank, prodded the coil with two fingers, and held the brown paper up to the light as though names might be written on it in lemon juice.

"You'll be paying for the wrapping," he said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Paper's government property now, requisitioned. You want it back, it's a penny." He said it without a smile, and behind him one of the younger soldiers snorted and was elbowed into silence.

Gideon paid the penny. She rewrapped her twine with fingers that did not shake, though it cost her something, and walked the length of the bridge with the corporal's eyes between her shoulder blades the whole way. At the far end, a second soldier looked at her face, looked at a paper pinned inside his hat brim, and waved her on.

A paper inside his hat brim. Descriptions, then. Wren was circulating descriptions of someone.

She did not look back. She had trained herself out of looking back twenty years ago, in the weeks after the night the soldiers came to the workshop, when every glance over her shoulder felt like a confession. *A mapmaker walks forward*, Corvus used to say, *because he already knows what's behind him.*

What was behind her was nine names and a penny poorer, and a plan in pieces.

* * *

She delivered the twine. She drank tea with Widow Pressen and let the old woman talk about her late husband's knees and the price of oakum and the scandal of the navy men spitting on respectable decking, and all the while Gideon sat very still and did her arithmetic.

They opened everything. Not some parcels, not suspicious parcels — *everything*, and they opened them carelessly, which meant they weren't looking for contraband brandy or smuggled lace. Careless searching finds bulk. Careful searching finds secrets. This was neither. This was thorough-by-order: tear the paper, look inside, find nothing ordinary, move on. It was a search conducted by men who had been told *something* was going out of this town and had not been told what.

Except the paper. They'd held the wrapping up to the light.

So Wren had told them more than nothing. He'd told them to watch paper.

She thought of the atlas lying in its chest beneath her floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath it the half-finished map of the marshes with its hollow backing, and inside the hollow, as of last night, fourteen names copied onto scraps small enough to hide in a seam. Twenty-six to go, and each name took her an hour because she copied them not once but twice — once onto the offcuts, once into memory, writing the letters over and over in her mind until they were less like words than like coastlines she could draw blind.

Forty names. Forty gallows. And a bridge between her and the sea where every scrap of paper was torn apart by a bored boy with a rifle.

"Your tea's going cold, love," said Widow Pressen.

"It's very good tea," Gideon said automatically, and the widow laughed and patted her hand and told her she was a good boy, and the words landed wrong today, landed like the penny, like the torn paper, like the description pinned in the soldier's hat. *A good boy.* Twenty years of being a good boy. It had kept her alive and it had shrunk her life to the size of a borrowed coat, and this morning, walking away from the rope-walk with the harbor full of stolen boats, she felt the seams of that coat begin to strain.

* * *

She came back over the bridge at midday to test the return crossing, and found the rhythm changed.

That was the second lesson of the day, and the more important one: checkpoints were not walls, they were tides. In the morning, bored. At midday, when the officer from the countinghouse came down to inspect them, brisk and vicious — a carter's load of oysters dumped into the river for lack of a permit, an old woman's bundle probed with a bayonet. Toward evening, Gideon guessed, bored again, drunk soon after. A route that failed at noon might pass at dusk. But she could not schedule the tide around a soldier's drinking, and besides—

Besides, the corporal had a list now. She saw him with it: a single sheet, and he checked each outgoing traveler against it, not every one, only the ones who came alone, only the ones with bundles large enough to hold paper. Only, if the set of his eyes meant anything, the ones built narrow through the shoulders.

She crossed, was waved through without a search — she was carrying nothing but her surveyor's satchel, and the satchel had a brass stamp on it, CORVUS & MONTROSE, SURVEYORS, and the stamp worked on soldiers better than any pass. But at the far end she paused at the toll-house as if adjusting her boot, and read the notice nailed to the post at eye level.

BY ORDER OF CAPT. A. WREN: ALL OUTGOING PARCELS, PACKAGES, WRITTEN MATTER, AND POSTAL ITEMS ARE SUBJECT TO INSPECTION WITHOUT EXCEPTION. PERSONS FOUND CONVEYING UNDECLARED DOCUMENTS WILL BE DETAINED FOR EXAMINATION BY THE MILITARY GOVERNOR.

*Without exception.* Written matter. Undeclared documents. He wasn't fishing for brandy. He knew exactly what weight of paper was missing from his net, and he had strung the whole town across the river mouth to catch a single sheet.

She stood there a moment, letting the wind push at her, watching the water slide black and quick under the middle arches. Then she did what she always did when a plan broke, which was to stop being a fugitive and start being a mapmaker. A mapmaker does not curse the mountain. A mapmaker asks: where is the low ground?

The soldiers searched parcels. They searched people, selectively — people who looked like carriers, people on the corporal's list, people alone. Who did they not search?

She ran the morning back through her head like a plate under the roller. The milkwoman, searched, grudgingly, her cans uncapped. Hobb, searched. The two lads with the barrow of kelp — she tried to remember, and could not picture them stopped at all. They'd been waved past both barriers while she stood waiting, waved past with barely a glance, seaweed dripping off the cart's edge, and one of the soldiers had made a face and said something about the smell, and the corporal hadn't even risen off his barrel.

And the fish-boy. The mute boy who came through every morning with the day's catch from the beach-seine, who threaded between the soldiers' legs like a cat between chair legs, who everyone in this town had long ago agreed not to see. Tam, Tamsin Hale called him — no relation, just the town's small stock of names worn smooth with use. He went to the checkpoints the way gulls go to gutting tables, unnoticed, unavoidable, smelling too strongly of mackerel for anyone to want his baskets opened.

Nobody searched the fish-boy. Nobody searched the fish because nobody wanted the fish searched. And the fish-boy went everywhere, every day, past every barrier, out along the marsh road where the tide-surveyors walked, and back again before noon.

Gideon stood at the bridge's end with the wind pulling her collar and understood, with the clean cold click of a lock turning, that the evidence could not travel in her coat. It would have to travel in a coat nobody cared to open. It would have to travel in fish.

She hated it immediately. She hated it the way you hate a correct answer.

Because she remembered being small and useful, remembered Corvus sending her — ten years old, eleven — to carry sealed messages to houses whose doors opened warily, whose occupants looked at her not as a child but as a package. *You're safe*, Corvus had said, hand heavy on her head. *No one suspects a child.* And she had been proud, then. Proud to be trusted with the weight. It had taken her twenty years to understand what he'd done: he had made her complicit before she was old enough to choose it, and called the making a kindness, and maybe it had been, maybe it was the only door out of the burning house, but she had sworn — she had sworn on the night she buried him — that she would never do it to anyone else.

And now the plan, meeting reality at a bridge with a boy corporal and a list in a hat, had just told her the price of the crossing, and the price was a child.

"There's another way," she said aloud, to the water. The water didn't answer. The water knew the marsh road flooded at half-tide and the cliff path was death and the harbor chain was up, and there was no other way.

* * *

She found him at dusk on the shingle below the fish-stakes, mending a net with fingers too quick for his face, which stayed utterly still while the nets flew together under them. He was maybe eleven. Hard to tell with children who don't eat enough. Salt had gotten into everything about him — hair, skin, the rags he wore — and his eyes, when they came up to hers, were the flat grey of the estuary in February.

He knew her, in the way the waterfront knew everyone. He'd delivered to the workshop. She'd paid him fair and once given him a hot pasty on a cold morning, and he had eaten it in four bites and shown no gratitude whatsoever, which she had respected.

Gideon crouched down to his level, which put her face close to the fish-smell and the salt, and laid it out plainly, because lying to children was a debt you paid later with interest.

"There's a packet I need carried, tomorrow evening, out the marsh road past the second checkpoint, to the old tide-gauge hut. Small. Flat. You'd hide it in the fish." She watched his eyes. "Soldiers won't stop you. I can't do it myself. That's the whole truth of why I'm asking."

The boy looked at her. Then out at the water. Then he did something she hadn't seen him do: he frowned, slow, deliberate, and pointed one mending-needle at the bridge, where the brazier glowed and the red coats moved against the last light, and then pointed it at her, and raised an eyebrow with the precision of a barrister.

"They wouldn't find it on me either," she said. "But they'd search me anyway. That's the difference. You're asking why you should be the one."

He nodded once.

"Because it's dangerous for me and not for you," she said, and the honesty scraped her throat raw on the way out. "And because I'm asking instead of telling, which is more than most people in this town will ever do for you. And because I'll pay you. Real coin."

He considered all of it with the gravity of a harbormaster weighing cargo. Then he held up one finger — *wait* — went back to his net, finished the mesh he was on, knotted it off clean, and looked up at her again.

And Gideon, kneeling on the shingle in the falling dark with forty names waiting in her floorboards, thought of Corvus's hand heavy on her own head twenty years ago, and the taste of that pride that had really been obedience, and made herself wait for the boy's answer as an equal waits — because whatever else this cost, he would choose it, and know he'd chosen.

Tam picked up his needle, pointed it at her waistcoat pocket, and held out his other hand, palm up.

Payment in advance. Smart as a whip, and nobody sees him at all.

She counted six coins into his palm, and his fingers closed over them, and somewhere behind her, across the black sliding water, a bell at the bridge began ringing the change of watch.
