## 9. The Boy Who Carries Fish

The boy's name was Tam, though he had no voice to tell anyone so.

He was eleven years old, or perhaps twelve — the salt and the hunger had worn the years off him early, the way water wears a stone — and he had worked the fish carts of Harborwick since he was small enough to hide in a barrel. His mother was three winters drowned. His father had never been anything but a rumor. The fishwives called him *the little ghost* because he came before dawn and vanished by noon, and because he made no sound doing either. Not mute from birth, the oldest wife said — she remembered when he could cry loud enough to wake the street. Something had happened after that. Nobody asked what. Harborwick was a town that understood there were things better left unasked.

He rose before the gulls on the third morning of the occupation, because soldiers or no soldiers, the herring did not care who governed the harbor, and neither did the men who bought them. He loaded his barrow at Brannock's wharf in the blue dark, hands moving by feel, and set off along the quay as the first gray light found the water.

The checkpoint at the harbor mouth had changed overnight. Two soldiers instead of one. A table dragged out onto the stones, a lantern on it, and behind the table a sergeant with a ledger and a face like a shut door. Tam slowed his barrow without meaning to, wheels grating, and the nearest soldier looked up.

"Fish?"

Tam nodded.

"Then it swims through fast, boy." The sergeant waved him past without opening the barrow's basket. "Out of the way."

That was the whole arithmetic of Tam's life: he carried fish, therefore he was furniture. He was a wheel, a post, a smell. Soldiers searched parcels and questioned grown men and wrote down names, but they did not search boys who smelled of herring guts and kept their eyes down. He rolled his cart past the lantern, past the bayonets, past the notice nailed to the customs post that he could not have read anyway, and felt nothing about it at all. Being invisible was not a gift. It was simply the shape of him.

By mid-morning he had sold his herring and taken his usual route home — up Fish Street, past the chandler's, along the lane where the mapmaker's workshop stood with its painted sign faded to a ghost of letters.

The door was open. That was the first thing wrong. The second thing wrong was that the mapmaker — the tall quiet man everyone said knew every inch of the coast better than the Admiralty did — was standing in the middle of his own floor holding a sheet of paper and looking at Tam the way nobody ever looked at Tam: as if he could see him.

* * *

Gideon had watched the boy pass her window four mornings running.

She had learned the habit from Corvus — *watch the traffic of a place for three days before you move in it; everything you need to know about a town walks its streets whether you ask it to or not* — and what she had learned about Harborwick under occupation she had learned largely through this one silent boy with his rattling barrow. He passed the bridge checkpoint at dawn and again at dusk. The soldiers never stopped him longer than a wave of the hand. They searched the baker's boy twice, searched a carter so thoroughly they turned out his tools onto the road, searched a widow's market basket item by item while she stood rigid with humiliation. The fish-boy they waved through like weather.

She had spent the night thinking about that, and about Finch's narrow eyes on the atlas margins, and about Wren's soft questions, and about the marsh map lying flat in its drawer with pages sewn between its linen backing and its chart face — pages that needed to reach the *Merrow*, sailing on the morning tide two days hence, and could not be carried by her own hands past any checkpoint Wren commanded.

She hated what she was about to do. She hated it with a specific, old, familiar hatred, the kind that has a memory of its own.

When the boy's barrow grated past her open door on the third morning, she stepped out into the lane.

"You. Fish-boy."

He stopped. Up close he was thinner than he looked in motion, all wrists and elbows, with a scab of salt rash along his jaw and eyes the color of shallow water over sand. He looked at her and waited, in the manner of someone long accustomed to being spoken at rather than spoken with.

"I need a carrier," Gideon said. "One delivery. Tomorrow, before dawn. Paid in coin." She held up a shilling so it caught the light.

Something moved in the boy's face — not greed. Caution. He glanced down the lane toward the bridge, then back at her, and shook his head slowly.

"Not through the checkpoints," she said. "You carry fish through them every day. You'd carry this the same way."

He shook his head again, harder, and now she read it plainly: he knew. Children always knew more than the town credited them. He had seen the notices, seen the searches, seen men taken away in the night, and whatever words he lacked, he had understood perfectly well that the tall mapmaker was standing in a doorway offering money for something the army would hang people for.

Gideon crouched to his height, which put them nearly eye to eye and which she did partly for kindness and mostly because her knees objected to looming.

"Listen to me," she said quietly. "I'm going to tell you something true, because I won't lie a child into danger. There are papers in my shop that some men want very much. If those men get them, families in this town will die. Not soldiers' families. Fishing families. Families like yours." She let that sit. "I can't carry them myself. They'd search me and find them, and then it wouldn't matter what happened to me, because the papers would burn and the families would follow. But no one searches you. You've seen it. You're a ghost to them."

The boy's jaw worked. His hands, hanging at his sides, curled and uncurled.

"I'm asking," Gideon said. "Not ordering. Asking. And I'm telling you now, before you take a farthing from me, that if you say no, I'll think no less of you, and I'll find another way, and you'll never hear of it again. Do you understand?"

For a long moment the shallow-water eyes studied her face with an attention that made her feel strangely examined — as if the boy were reading her the way she read a coastline, sounding for rocks.

Then he held out his hand. Not for the shilling. Flat, palm up, a question.

"You want to know what you'd carry," Gideon translated. "Fair."

* * *

She showed him in the back room, away from the open door.

The marsh map lay on the trestle table, weighted at the corners — the great unfinished survey of the Saltmere flats, all reed-beds and tidal channels rendered in Corvus's patient hand, the work of two summers. Gideon lifted the top edge of the linen backing where she had stitched it loose, and slid out two leaves of chart paper folded to the size of a psalter, and showed them to the boy, and watched his face while he read nothing, because he could not read, and understood everything anyway, because the fear coming off the paper needed no letters.

"These stay inside the map," she said. She slid them home, pressed the backing flat, and showed him the stitches — small, even, invisible unless you knew. "You don't touch them. You don't need to touch them. All you do is this:" She took the whole map, rolled it, sleeved it in oilcloth, and laid it across his barrow between two baskets. "You've carried ladders. Poles. Oars. It's the same. A roll of paper under the fish baskets. You walk your morning route, same hour, same pace, and at the end of Fish Street you turn up the marsh lane instead of down to the wharf. There's a broken stile by the blackthorn, the one with lightning-scars on it. You lean the map against the stile and walk on. That's all. A man will collect it within the hour. You never saw him. You never saw anything."

The boy frowned and pointed at himself, then toward the bridge checkpoint.

"They'll wave you through," Gideon said. "They always do."

He pointed at the map, then made a fist and struck his own palm — the gesture unmistakable, *and if they don't?*

Gideon looked at him for a moment. Then she told the truth, because she had promised herself she would not lie to him the way Corvus had never lied to her — only failed, in twenty years of silence, to tell her everything.

"If they open it and find the pages, they'll take you," she said. "And it will go badly. I won't pretend otherwise. That's why I'm asking and not paying you to be brave. You can say no right now and walk out that door and I'll say nothing to anyone, ever."

The boy stood very still. Gideon watched him weigh it — really weigh it, the way adults so rarely bothered to imagine a child weighing anything — and she thought, with a grief that arrived like a blow she should have seen coming: *he is doing exactly what I did. Counting the risk against the coin and the being-seen. Corvus stood where I stand and watched me count.*

She had been fifteen, the night Master Corvus had first asked her to carry a letter through a checkpoint — a sealed thing, innocent-looking, that she had later understood to be a warning that saved a family from the press-gangs. He had offered her the truth too. He had said, *you may refuse, Gideon,* and she had not refused, because refusing meant remaining a child, and carrying the letter meant being trusted, and at fifteen trust was worth more than safety. She had understood only years later, holding his last letter, that he had hated himself for asking — that every task he gave her had been both a lesson and a wound, and that he had chosen to wound her rather than leave her unprepared for a world that wounds worse.

Now she stood in his workshop with a silent boy's life balanced on her palm, and understood that the choice did not get easier for being inherited. It got heavier. It gathered weight from everyone who had made it before you.

The boy reached out and touched the rolled map, once, lightly, the way a man touches a door before opening it. Then he nodded.

"No," Gideon said, and he looked up sharply. "Nod means yes. We need to be certain." She crouched again. "Last chance. Yes or no."

He nodded. Then, deliberately, he held out his hand for the shilling, and there was — she would swear it afterward — the ghost of a challenge in it. *Pay me like a man, since you're treating me like one.*

She put the shilling in his palm and closed his fingers over it. "Tomorrow. Before dawn. Same route. And Tam—" She paused, realizing she had used the name the fishwives used, and he had not flinched from it, and perhaps it was the only name he had. "If anything goes wrong at the bridge, drop the map in the ditch and run. Paper drowns. Boys shouldn't."

He looked at her for a long moment with those shallow-water eyes, and then he did something no one in Harborwick had done to her in twenty years: he grinned. Quick, crooked, gone in a heartbeat, but real. Then he hoisted his barrow handles and rolled out into the light, and she heard the wheels fading down Fish Street, and stood alone in the room full of her master's maps feeling as though she had just committed some sin whose name she didn't know yet.

* * *

That night she could not sleep for listening.

Every sound in Harborwick seemed sharpened — the tide turning, a dog two streets over, boots somewhere, though boots were everywhere now and meant nothing and meant everything. She sat by the banked fire until midnight, working the last of the copying by feel more than sight, and found her mind circling the boy the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

She thought: *if they take him, I did that.* She thought: *Corvus, is this what you carried? Is this the weight under your calm all those years — a shop full of children you'd taught to hold live coals?* She thought of the letter she had carried at fifteen, and how proud she had been, and how she had never once considered that pride might have been manufactured in her, deliberately, the way a tool is ground to an edge.

But that was not quite true either, and she made herself face the not-quite-truth, because Corvus had taught her that a mapmaker who flatters the terrain kills travelers. He had given her the choice every time. Every time, the door had stood open. What she had done with her own pride was her own doing, and what the boy had done today with his nod was his own doing too — eleven or twelve years old, silent since God knew what grief, and he had weighed forty strangers' lives against his own skin and chosen the strangers. No one had ground that edge into him. It was native metal.

*Perhaps that's what Corvus saw in me,* she thought. *Not a tool he shaped. A thing already shaped, that he merely refused to waste.*

Near dawn she went to the window and looked down Fish Street, toward the bridge, where the mist was coming off the river in slow rags and the checkpoint lantern still burned. The street was empty. Too early, even for the fish-boy. She told herself that meant nothing. She told herself he would come at his usual hour, rolling his usual noise, a ghost with a barrow, and lean a roll of paper against a broken stile in the marshes, and walk on untouched, and never know that he had carried forty lives between two fish baskets.

She was still standing at the window when she saw him.

He came up Fish Street faster than his usual pace — she knew his pace now, the way she knew tides — and the barrow was empty, and he was looking over his shoulder. At the corner, where the lamplight ended, he stopped, turned, and looked up at her window with an expression she had never seen on that careful face.

It was fear. And behind the fear, urgent and unmistakable, a beckoning.

Gideon took the stairs two at a time.
