## 10. Finch's Doubt

The workshop smelled of linseed oil and old smoke, and by the third night of inventorying it, Elias Finch had come to think of the smell as a kind of accusation.

He sat at Master Corvus's long drafting table with the army's requisition ledger open before him, his pen moving in the small, precise hand that had won him his post — *clerk, third class, attached to the office of the provost* — and which had, in four years of service, never once been praised and never once forgiven. Around him the workshop stood stripped half-bare: drawers pulled out and their contents sorted into lots, copper plates stacked against the wall in gleaming ranks like soldiers themselves, rolls of paper bound with cord and tagged. The soldiers who had done the rough work during the day had gone to their billets. The lamp guttered. Outside, Harborwick lay under the wet black silence of a town that had learned, very quickly, what occupation sounded like when it stopped marching and started listening.

Finch dipped his pen and wrote: *Lot forty-one. Instruments, drafting, brass, assorted. Condition fair.*

*Condition fair.* He had written those words so many times this week that they had begun to lose their meaning, the way any word does when you use it as a shovel. Fair condition. Fair. There was nothing fair about any of it, and he knew it, and he wrote it anyway, because the alternative to writing it was being written about — a report, a reprimand, a name entered in some other clerk's ledger, in some other small precise hand.

He was thirty-one years old and he had come into the army as a boy because his father's chandler's shop had failed and a uniform came with bread. He had never fired a shot at anyone. He had instead spent four years doing the thing he was doing now: converting the world into lists. Boats converted to seized property. Houses converted to quarters. Men converted to prisoners, numbers, entries. Women converted to widows, though there was no column for that; the form did not ask.

It was the forms he thought about at night, lying in the countinghouse loft where they'd quartered him among bales of canvas that smelled of salt and rat. The forms were the war, as far as Elias Finch had ever seen it. And this week the war had come to Harborwick, and the forms were hungry.

*Lot forty-two,* he wrote. *Paper, chart, one hundredweight more or less.*

His pen slowed.

He looked up at the grate.

It was an ordinary workshop grate, iron, set low in the chimney breast beside the hearth where a kettle still hung — someone had been heating water here every evening, the mapmaker or his apprentice, some small domestic ceremony continued in the middle of catastrophe. Earlier that day one of the corporals had poked through the ashes with a bayonet looking for anything burnable worth taking, and had found nothing, and had sworn, and had left the grate open and the ashes disturbed.

But the light from Finch's lamp caught something in that disturbance now. A corner of paper, charmed brown at its edges, curled like a dead leaf, and on the unburned part of it —

Finch rose. His chair scraped, and the sound was enormous in the empty shop. He stood for a moment without moving, listening, the way he had learned to listen this week: for boots on cobbles, for a cough in a doorway, for the particular silence of being watched. Nothing. Only the harbor breathing somewhere below, and the wind finding the gap under the door.

He knelt at the grate.

It was a scrap no larger than his palm, torn rag paper, good paper — the heavy stock of charts, the kind that cost money — and it had been thrust into the fire and not thoroughly enough destroyed. Ink had survived along one edge in fragments, and the fragments were letters, and the letters were names.

*...arren.*

*...sabel C—*

*...the mill at Ashford.*

He knew, by then, what he was looking at. Everyone in the requisition party knew, though nothing official had been said above a mutter. The atlas — the great atlas, Corvus's life's work, the thing Captain Wren wanted found and accounted for more than he wanted the harbor itself — carried more than coastlines. It carried names in its margins. Names of people who had moved fugitives through this town and others like it for twenty years, out past the customs men, out past the press gangs, out onto water and away. Wren called them conspirators. Wren said the word *network* the way other men said *profit.* Forty families, the whisper went, if the margins could be read rightly. Forty gallows, if the reading went hard.

Finch held the scrap over the lamp and read it again, though once was enough. His hands were steady. That surprised him. He had always supposed that in the presence of real consequence his hands would shake.

Someone had tried to burn this. Someone had knelt where he was kneeling now — and he knew who; there was only one person who came and went from this shop with purpose, the tall quiet apprentice, Montrose, who answered questions with exactly as many words as required and no more, whose ink-stained fingers never trembled either — and had pushed evidence of forty lives into the flames, and had missed a piece, and had gone home believing it done.

Or had gone home knowing it wasn't. No. He couldn't believe that. Montrose missed nothing. Finch had watched him all week, professionally, the way he watched everything, and the man — Finch turned the thought over carefully, like a coin he suspected was clipped — the man had the eyes of someone keeping accounts of a different kind.

*Lot forty-three,* Finch imagined himself writing. *Paper fragment, partially burned, recovered from hearth. Bearing marginal notation consistent with the atlas of Josiah Corvus. Recovered by E. Finch, clerk third class, this date.* And beneath the entry, the signature. And beneath the signature, the consequences, unfolding down the page like a coastline drawn by an unforgiving hand: the scrap logged, the discovery reported, Wren informed, the apprentice taken, questioned — Finch had seen Wren question a smuggler in Rye two years ago and had requested afterward to be excused from further duties of that nature, and had been told that such duties were part of the service and the service was not a menu — and then whatever names could be prised loose, and then the arrests, and then the forms, so many forms, each one accurate, each one fair, each one a plank in forty gallows.

He knelt there a long time. Long enough for his knee to ache against the stone hearth. Long enough for the lamp to gutterspit and need its wick trimmed.

Then Elias Finch, clerk third class, who had never disobeyed an order, who had never failed to make an entry, who had converted four years of his life into lists without once asking what the lists were for — folded the scrap of burned paper very small, very precisely, the way he did everything, and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, over his heart.

* * *

He finished the inventory at half past one in the morning. He signed the page, blotted it, closed the ledger, and set it square with the table's edge. Then he took the lamp and made his slow circuit of the workshop, because that was procedure — a final walk-through before securing the premises — and because he wanted, though he would never have used the word, to say goodbye to it.

He had come to know Josiah Corvus these three nights the way a man comes to know a country through its paperwork. He knew now the order of the drawers, which held the finished plates and which the experiments; he knew that Corvus had kept his pencils sorted by hardness in a labeled tray, that he had mended his own pens rather than buy new, that there was a child's copybook on a high shelf with *G.M.* worked into the cover in careful cross-hatching, filled with practice alphabets and, later, practice coastlines, each one corrected in a firm older hand — *observe how the headland lies* — until the corrections stopped being needed. Finch had catalogued the copybook without opening it further than the first page. Some things were not the army's business. He was beginning, uncomfortably, to hold opinions about which things those were.

He paused at the great plan chest where the atlas itself should have lived. It was not there. It had not been there since the first day, and Wren had torn a finger-nail's length off his composure over the absence, though he'd concealed it under courtesy, the way rich men conceal poverty under lace. *The masterwork will surface, Finch. Things of value always surface.* And Finch had nodded and written *atlas: location unknown* in the margin of the master inventory, and had felt, obscurely, glad of its absence — glad that somewhere in Harborwick that beautiful dangerous object was hidden, breathing quietly, keeping its forty names to itself.

On the wall hung the framed maps, and among them, half-finished, a chart of the marshes east of town. Finch had noticed it the first night because it was unlike the others: looser, the ink wet-looking even now, as though its maker had been interrupted mid-line and meant to return. He looked at it again tonight and could not have said why it held him. Perhaps only that everything else in the room was finished — finished and inventoried and lot-numbered — and this one thing remained in motion, unfinished, free.

He trimmed the lamp lower, out of thrift, and stood in the diminished light.

Through the window he could see the harbor, or the idea of it: mast-tips against a starless sky, the seized boats riding sullen at their new moorings. Somewhere out there was the ship Wren kept asking about — outward bound within days, manifest already filed, a captain named something unmemorable sailing on the morning tide. Wren wanted the atlas found before it sailed. Wren wanted many things. Finch had learned this week to read the captain the way he read ledgers, and what he read there frightened him more than the gallows did, because gallows at least killed honestly. Wren wanted the atlas the way a man wants a key he has lost the door for.

And Finch had begun, God help him, to have thoughts. Thoughts about what a clerk's small precise hand might do, or not do. Thoughts with the weight of treason in them, dressed in the plain clothes of caution.

*Wait,* the thoughts said. *Only wait. You don't have to help anyone. You only have to be slow. Slow is not a crime. Slow has never once been court-martialed.*

He blew out the lamp.

* * *

In the countinghouse loft he did not sleep. He lay among the bales of canvas with his coat folded under his head and the scrap of burned paper inside the coat, and he listened to the building settle, and he made his own ledger of the week, because listing things was the only prayer he knew.

Entry: the woman in Rye, two years ago, after Wren had finished with her husband — how she had stood in the street outside and looked at the windows of the provost's office, all of them, one by one, as though memorizing faces, and how Finch had been one of the faces, and had looked away first, and had never managed since to look at a mirror quickly.

Entry: the boy this morning on the quay, a mute fish-boy with a cart, stopped and tipped out by bored soldiers for no reason but boredom — herring sliding across wet stones, the boy gathering them up with hands that did not shake, and Finch watching and doing nothing, and the word *nothing* sitting in his mouth like a coin of a foreign country.

Entry: Montrose. Three nights of Montrose answering questions across this very table — *how long apprenticed; twenty years, sir; your master's habits of correspondence; he kept none, sir; where the master slept; upstairs, sir* — each answer a locked drawer, politely offered for inspection. Twenty years an apprentice. Finch had checked. There were records, of a sort, and the records were old and thin and convenient, and there was something underneath them, some weight pressing up through the paper like damp through plaster, and Finch was a clerk, and clerks know paper, and he knew.

He knew, and he had put the scrap in his pocket anyway.

That was the entry that would not close. He kept trying to write it one way — *I am protecting evidence until such time as* — and it kept coming out another way — *I am choosing a side, late, badly, with no weapon but slowness.* He was not brave. He wanted that recorded too. Bravery was for the woman in Rye, who had memorized faces; bravery was for whoever had knelt at this grate with a handful of forty lives and trusted fire. Finch had only his pocket, and his slow hands, and the small mercenary hope — he was honest enough to see it, even in himself — that mercy, once begun, might somehow spend back upon the spender. That a man who kept a piece of paper in his coat over his heart might, by the same act, keep something of himself that four years of forms had nearly burned away.

Outside, the tide was turning; he could hear it change below the countinghouse, the water sighing as it reversed, patient, immense, keeping its own schedule regardless of armies. The ship would sail on the morning tide three days hence. Or two days hence, now. Time in Harborwick had begun running like sand through a cracked glass.

And tomorrow — he had seen it on Wren's desk, had copied the requisition himself, his small precise hand recording his own diminishing choices — tomorrow Captain Aldous Wren had summoned the apprentice Montrose to the countinghouse. Nine o'clock. *For a discussion of the master's papers.*

Finch lay in the dark with the scrap of paper warming slowly against his chest, and thought: nine o'clock.

And thought: I should warn him.

And thought: I cannot possibly warn him.

And lay there, clerk third class, holding between cowardice and conscience the smallest, most dangerous lot he had ever inventoried, and waited to see which way the tide would take him.
