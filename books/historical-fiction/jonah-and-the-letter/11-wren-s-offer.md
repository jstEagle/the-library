## 11. Wren's Offer

The summons came at noon, delivered by a private too young to shave who would not meet her eyes. *Captain Wren requests the surveyor's presence at the countinghouse. Immediately.* No *please*. No explanation. Gideon read the note twice, folded it along its crease until the ink faced inward, and set it on the workbench as though it were an ordinary order from an ordinary client.

Her hands, she noticed, were steady. It was the rest of her that trembled, somewhere deep beneath the breastbone, in the place where fear had lived so long it had learned to keep house.

She dressed with care, because a man who dressed poorly before authority invited suspicion, and suspicion was a luxury she could no longer afford. Clean shirt, the good coat, hair bound tight beneath the wig she had worn for eleven years — horsehair, greying at the temples to match the face she had taught the mirror to present. She checked the workshop door twice, left the atlas where it always lay, open on its stand like any honest work-in-progress, and walked out into streets gone quiet under occupation.

Harborwick at noon had never been quiet before. There had always been gulls and cartwheels and fishwives singing out the morning's catch, arguments in three languages outside the customs shed. Now there were boots. Red coats moving in pairs along the quay, bayonets catching light, and between them the townspeople flowing like water around stones — heads down, eyes anywhere else. A boy was scrubbing at a requisition notice nailed to the fish-market post, though the ink had sunk into the grain and would outlive them all.

The countinghouse had been hers once, in the way of these things: she had surveyed its foundations for old Master Pell, drawn the plan of its cellar for the wine merchant who leased half of it. Now two sentries flanked its door, and the brass plate — PELL & SONS, IMPORTS — had been unscrewed and set aside like something the building was ashamed of.

"Surveyor Montrose," she said to the sentry, and heard her own voice do what it always did: sit low in the chest, wear boredom like a coat. "I'm expected."

* * *

They took her up to the long room on the first floor, the one with windows on the harbor, and Captain Aldous Wren was standing at those windows with his back to her when she entered, which she understood immediately as theatre. A man who wanted to intimidate began by making you wait inside your own silence.

He was not what she had expected, or perhaps he was exactly what she should have expected: broad through the shoulders still, though fifty must have found him, silver at the close-cropped temples, a scar through one eyebrow that gave his face a permanent suggestion of wry disbelief. He wore his coat well. He wore everything well. That, more than anything, made her afraid — because Corvus had described him to her once, twenty years ago, in a voice she had never heard him use about anyone else, and she had built a monster in her imagination, and monsters are easier than men.

"Master Montrose." He turned. His eyes went over her the way a buyer's eyes go over a horse — not leering, assessing. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. Wine?"

"No, thank you, Captain."

"Suit yourself." He poured for himself, unhurried, and gestured to a chair drawn up facing the empty hearth. On the mantel above it sat a small stack of papers tied in ribbon, and she made herself not look at them, and knew that making herself not look was itself a kind of looking. "Do you know how long I've been in His Majesty's service, Master Montrose?"

"I couldn't say."

"Thirty-one years. Nine of them soldiering, the rest pushing paper, which is soldiering with worse weather." He smiled, sat down opposite her, crossed one ankle over his knee. "Your master — Corvus Montrose — I soldiered with him. Did he ever mention it?"

"He mentioned he'd been a soldier. He rarely spoke of it. He said maps remembered better than men did."

Wren laughed — a genuine laugh, short and surprised, and it was somehow worse than anything threatening he might have done. "That's him. That is *him*, God rest him. We mapped Flanders together in 'twenty-three. I held the chain, he drew. He could make a marsh look like a garden if you paid him, and a garden look like a marsh if you didn't." He sipped. "You inherited all of it, I'm told. The trade, the premises, the commissions. You were with him how long?"

"Fourteen years. Then seven on my own account since he died."

"Mm." Wren set down his glass. And then, gently, almost kindly, he said: "Funny thing, though. Funny thing I've been turning over since I arrived. Corvus Montrose had no sons."

The room was very still. Out on the harbor, a gull screamed once and stopped.

"He had," Wren continued, in the tone of a man working through an arithmetic problem, "a daughter. Born in 'twenty-two, the year we marched. He spoke of her exactly once, drunk, in a tent outside Namur, and then never again, and when a comrade asks after a child and gets that look, you learn not to ask twice. The mother died — I gathered as much. The girl went... somewhere. To family, I assumed. And then the war ate twenty years of my life and spat me out, and I come home to find the great mapmaker dead and his *son* keeping the shop, and nobody in this town can tell me where the son came from. Not the vicar. Not the widow who kept his books. Not the man who buried him." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his eyes — grey, intelligent, patient — rested on her face with the weight of a hand. "Nobody, Master Montrose, except whoever made you."

She had rehearsed for this the way one rehearses for drowning: knowing the drill, doubting it would serve. Her voice, when it came, was flat and slightly offended, the voice of a craftsman insulted by amateurs. "My father was Josiah Montrose, of Penzance. Corvus took me apprentice at fourteen. If you doubt it, Captain, the indentures are downstairs, and the parish register, and—"

"The parish register." Wren nodded slowly. "Yes. Ink is a wonderful thing. Your master was the finest forger of documents I ever met — I don't mean maps, I mean *papers*, the kind that move men across borders. Half his skill with coastlines came from learning to make a coastline lie." He reached to the mantel, untied the ribbon, and lifted the top paper. "These are his letters. To me, from him, over thirty years. Look at the hand."

She looked. It was Corvus's writing, unmistakably — the strong forward slant, the little hook on the ascenders that every apprentice in Harborwick had tried and failed to copy. Letters spanning decades: *Aldous — the survey is finished, tell the colonel the estuary has moved and his memories have not — Aldous, they are asking questions in Rotterdam, burn this —*

And near the bottom of the stack, newer, creased from travel: *Aldous — I have done a thing. Do not ask what. If anything happens to me, there is a person who knows my trade entire. Trust the work, not the town.*

Wren watched her read. He had angled the letters deliberately; he wanted her to see that last line, wanted to watch it land.

"You see my difficulty," he said softly. "An army requisitions a harbor, seizes ledgers, and finds an atlas full of marginalia that reads like a confession — forty names, forty routes, forty years of smuggling fugitives out from under the Crown's nose. And the man who wrote it conveniently dies before questions start, leaving behind a successor nobody remembers, whose handwriting—" he tapped the letter, "—is a fair match for the master's. Almost as though he trained it into someone. As though he *made* someone."

Gideon sat with her hands folded, and inside her chest her heart was going like a hammer in a locked room, and none of it showed, because none of it had been allowed to show for twenty years. Fear, she had learned, was a tide: you could not stop it, but you could chart it, and ride it, and know when it would turn.

"What is it you want, Captain?" she asked. "If you think I'm some impostor, arrest me. If you think the atlas is dangerous, take it — it sits on its stand in plain view, it has always sat in plain view. I am a mapmaker. I draw coasts. What men write in margins after I've gone to bed is not my affair."

"No," Wren agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Which is precisely why I'm offering you this."

He took a second paper from beneath the letters — not old, not Corvus's hand, but official, stamped, written in a clerk's careful script — and laid it on the table between them, face up, the way a gambler shows his winning card.

*Passage for one aboard such vessel as the bearer shall select. Protection of the military governor's office extended to the bearer and the bearer's effects. All inquiries concerning the estate and papers of E. Corvus Montrose, deceased, resolved by testimony of the undersigned.*

"A new life," Wren said. "Passage out of Harborwick on any ship you name, protection from prosecution, a clean ending to every question I've just asked you — and believe me, Master Montrose, I have not yet begun to ask them. In exchange: the atlas, complete, surrendered into my custody tonight. And a sworn statement — your hand, your seal — that the marginalia are your late master's alone. His obsession. His politics. His names. You never saw them. You never understood them. You are merely the tradesman who inherited a haunted house."

The room tilted, very slightly, and righted. She realized she had been holding her breath and let it out slowly, through the nose, the way Corvus had taught her to breathe before a long line of fine work.

"And the families?" she said. "The people those names belong to?"

"What families?" Wren spread his hands, all innocence, all menace. "Names in a margin. Dead man's scribbling. If the atlas comes to me, it goes into my report as seized material of historical interest, and the colonial office files it beside a thousand other curiosities, and no one is hanged." His voice cooled by degrees, like a forge banking down. "But if the atlas reaches the colonial office by another road — if some clerk finds it first, or some zealous major, or if it goes *missing* and someone suggests it was destroyed to protect the guilty — then there will be arrests. Widows. Gallows on the common, and a crowd to watch, because crowds always come. Forty names, I'm told. I haven't counted. I imagine you have."

She had. Forty names, counted by candlelight with her finger on the margin, each one attached to a kitchen she had eaten in, a boat she had drawn, a child who had run errands to the workshop door.

"Why?" It escaped her before she could cage it. "Why offer me anything? You've spent ten minutes telling me what I am. Take it to your superiors and be done."

Something moved behind Wren's face — quick, quickly mastered, but she was Corvus's apprentice and she had been taught to read terrain, and she saw it: not righteousness. Not even, quite, greed. Something hungrier and more private than either.

"Because," Wren said, "your master owed me. Thirty years of letters, and in half of them he asks favors, and in a quarter of them I granted them, and I have been repaid in nothing but grief and a scar. He was the cleverest man I ever knew and he used every ounce of it staying just beyond the reach of everyone who helped him. So now he's dead, and the debt comes due to whatever he left behind." He stood, smoothed his coat, and looked down at her with the expression of a landlord at the end of a lease. "You are what he left behind. The atlas. The talent. The secret. All of it mine now, one way or another — the only question worth asking in this room is whether you'll be paid for it or broken by it."

He slid the passage paper toward her. Beside it he laid a pen, and a sheet of blank foolscap, and uncapped the inkwell himself, with a small ceremony that turned her stomach.

"Take the night," he said. "Think about the widows. Think about the gallows. Think about how quickly a man can be unmade by a question — and how much more quickly a woman." He let that word sit in the air between them, soft as a knife going into a sheath. He had not said it as accusation. He had said it as *inventory*, and that was worse. "Tomorrow evening, I'll call at the workshop for the atlas and the statement. Bring both to the door yourself, and you'll never see me again, and Harborwick will forget your name within a month."

*A month,* she thought, absurdly, *it took me eleven years to build that name.*

At the door, with the sentries opening it and the noon light falling hard across the threshold, Wren spoke once more, quietly enough that only she could hear.

"He trusted the work, not the town. But he trusted you more than either, whoever you are. I could hear it in the letter. It's the only generous thing I ever knew him to write." A pause. "Don't make me regret being gentler than he was."

* * *

She did not go straight home. She walked the long way, along the sea wall, where the wind off the water could take the sweat off her neck and no window overlooked the path. The harbor lay below her, strangled: every boat at anchor under guard, masts bare, the fishing fleet looking like a congregation locked out of its own church.

*Temptation,* her master used to say, *is just a map of your own weaknesses drawn by someone else's hand.* And Wren had drawn hers expertly. Passage. Safety. An ending. All she had to do was hand over the one thing standing between forty households and the rope, swear a lie that cost her nothing — the names *were* Corvus's doing, in a sense; he had never once consulted her — and vanish before the trap finished closing.

And it *was* a trap. She saw its shape the way she saw a sandbar under clear water: the statement would hang the deaths on a dead man, yes, but the moment she signed her name to anything — *any* name, even the borrowed one — Wren would hold a specimen of her hand, a sworn oath, a documented existence he could unravel with a single visit to Penzance or a single question put properly to the vicar. The pass was not mercy. It was a leash, and every yard of slack he allowed would only prove how far he meant to walk her.

And still. Still. Forty gallows on one side of the scale and her own neck on the other, and Wren smiling at the balance like a merchant who owned both pans.

She stopped at the end of the sea wall and looked back at the countinghouse, at the tall window on the first floor where he had stood watching the harbor he'd helped to strangle. He had known. Not guessed — *known*, or near enough, and he had told her anyway, calmly, because telling her gave him power and secrecy gave him nothing. A man who wanted justice would have arrested her. A man who wanted truth would have pressed her. Wren wanted *leverage*, and leverage must be fed and kept alive, and so here she was, alive, walking home with her heart hammering, while somewhere in his coat pocket her whole life sat folded in thirty years of letters.

The wind came hard off the water, smelling of salt and tar and the coming evening, and she thought of the last line of the last letter. *Trust the work, not the town.*

Corvus had known Wren would come someday. Had perhaps written that sentence with this exact afternoon in view. Which meant that whatever he had left her — in the atlas, in the margins, in the habits he had drilled into her hands — was meant to survive precisely this kind of summons.

She turned toward the workshop, and her stride lengthened. Tomorrow evening Wren would call for the atlas and a confession.

She had three nights' copying still ahead of her, a coat lining half-sewn, and — she realized, quickening — a decision to make about which of them would arrive at that door holding the leash.

Behind her, at the countinghouse window, a silhouette watched her all the way to the corner, and did not turn away until she was gone.
