## 12. The Night Search

The marshes at night were a country Gideon had mapped twice over, once with her eyes and once with her feet, and it was the second map that kept her alive now. She knew where the ground pretended to be solid. She knew which channels ran shallow enough to wade and which would take a man to his knees in cold black silt. She knew the sound the wind made combing through the samphire — a dry whisper, like paper being turned — and she knew that when that whisper stopped, something else was moving.

She had been crouched in the hollow behind the old tide-mill for three hours when the whisper stopped.

Lantern light bloomed in the workshop windows across the water, four of them, then six, swinging and doubling themselves in the harbor's still surface until it looked as though the whole town had caught fire from below. The soldiers had come quietly — no drums, no boots on the cobbles in any rhythm a sleeping street would notice — but they had come in numbers. She counted the lanterns again. Eight. Wren had not sent men to search; he had sent men to *dismantle*.

Her hands, wrapped around her knees, had gone numb an hour ago. She did not move them. Movement was noise, noise was light, light was everything she had spent twenty years learning to smother like a candle flame between wet fingers.

*They will find the atlas,* she thought, and made herself finish the thought instead of flinching away from it. *Let them find it.*

That was the arithmetic she had come out here to perform in the dark, alone, with nothing but the cold for company: the atlas was lost. It had been lost the moment Finch had stood in the workshop running his thumb along the margin of the northern coast and narrowing his eyes like a man reading a letter written in a hand he almost recognized. Perhaps before that. Perhaps the moment Corvus had died with his secrets half-inked and left them to her like a debt dressed up as an inheritance. The atlas could not be saved. It could only be survived.

So why did her chest ache as though someone had reached into it and taken hold?

Across the water, the workshop door burst open — even at this distance she heard the wood give, a crack like ice shifting — and the lanterns spilled out into the yard, spreading. They were emptying the place into the moonlight. She saw a soldier carry out the drafting table's drawers one by one and upend them. She saw another emerge with the roll of blank charts under his arm and fling it down like something dead. Small figures moved with the brisk, unthinking violence of men who have been told that nothing in a building belongs to anyone anymore.

And then, unmistakable even at two hundred yards, carried across the flat water with terrible clarity: the sound of the great press cabinet opening. The sound of plates being lifted out and stacked. Her master's copper. Twenty years of her own hands on those plates — burnishing, repairing, re-engraving the worn lines until the coastline of Harborwick lived in her fingertips better than her own face did, because her own face she had long ago stopped studying, having no use for a face that must never be recognized.

*They are taking the atlas.*

She watched a figure — broader than the rest, unhurried, giving orders with small gestures rather than shouts — descend the steps and stand in the yard while the others ferried things past him. Even at this distance, even in this light, she knew the set of those shoulders. Captain Aldous Wren had come to see it done himself.

He held something in both hands. He turned it over slowly, the way a man turns over a purse found on the road, weighing what is inside against what it cost the person who dropped it.

The atlas. He was holding the atlas. Forty names stitched into its margins like a seam of ore running through worthless rock, forty gallows folded inside calfskin boards, and he was holding it up to a lantern as calmly as a merchant appraising cloth.

Gideon's breath came out of her in a long white thread and the cold took it. Her heart was not pounding. That surprised her. Her heart was very quiet, the way it went quiet in the last hour before a difficult line was finished, when the ink was mixed and the pen was loaded and there was nothing left in the world but the stroke and the steadiness required to make it.

*Good,* said some cold clear voice at the bottom of her, a voice she recognized as Corvus's and was no longer frightened to find living in her. *Now you can see it plainly. Now you know exactly what is gone and what remains.*

What remained was sewn into the lining of a coat hanging in the marsh-map rack, hidden inside a half-finished chart of these very flats, forty-one pages of copied marginalia in a hand so like her master's that she herself had wept checking it. What remained was Tam's fish cart and Tamsin Hale's captain and three days that were now two.

* *

The search went on for hours. She had not expected that. She had thought they would find the atlas in the first quarter-hour, as indeed they seemed to, and be done. But Wren's men turned the workshop inside out with the patience of men being paid by the finding. They pulled up floorboards. They drained the ink barrels and stirred the sediment with a bayonet. She heard — or imagined she heard — the splinter of the false back in the drafting cabinet that had hidden Corvus's letters for fifteen years, and winced at the small death of each secret.

At some point a soldier came out and was sick in the yard, loudly, and another laughed at him. She could not imagine what they had found to sick over until she remembered the store of pigments below: the ox blood and oak galls, the jars of ground shell, the long dried eel she'd kept for sizing and never gotten round to burying. A city man's stomach turning over a workshop's honest filth. She found, to her own alarm, that she was nearly laughing — a hard, silent shaking in her chest that was grief wearing comedy's coat, and she pressed her fist against her mouth until it passed.

The tide was turning. She felt it before she saw it, the way you feel a held breath released — the black channels beginning to run backward, the smell of the mud changing from iron to salt. She had perhaps two hours before the flooded path home became impassable, and she did not move, because Wren had not yet left.

He came out of the workshop at last and stood in the yard a long while, the atlas tucked under one arm now like a ledger, like a thing already filed and forgotten. One of his men said something to him. He shook his head. Then he raised his lantern and turned a slow circle, letting the light rake the yard, the fences, the water's edge — and Gideon sank lower into the samphire until the cold mud pressed her cheek, and the light swept over the hollow where she hid and moved on.

When she dared look up, he was speaking to the sergeant, gesturing — she could not hear the words, but she could read the grammar of a hand she had studied across two interviews and one polite trap. Point at the workshop. Point at the grate. Point at the man's own chest. *Take it apart further. I want every scrap. Nothing leaves.*

Then the lanterns withdrew, all but two, and the night closed over the workshop like water over a dropped stone.

She stayed until her limbs unlocked and her teeth stopped their chatter, and then she began the long cold walk home along the paths only she and God and the curlews knew, and by the time she reached the back fence of her own property, soaked to the knee and half-dead with cold, she had stopped grieving and started counting.

* *

They had left a sentry at the front, but the back gate stood open, and open gates tell their own stories. She slipped through the garden where the rosemary was flattened by boots and let herself in through the kitchen as if she were a burglar robbing herself, which in law, she supposed, was precisely what she was.

Inside, the workshop looked like the aftermath of a storm that had happened indoors. Drawers lay gutted. Charts were strewn and stamped with muddy prints. Her good brushes had been snapped — not used, *snapped*, deliberately, one by one, which told her something about the men and about the order they had been given. You do not break a man's brushes out of duty. You break them out of contempt, or on instruction, and either way the message is the same: *you are finished here.*

But they had found what she meant them to find, and not what she meant them to miss. She went first to the grate and knelt. Ashes, scattered, pawed through — the burned waste-scraps of her copying nights had been sifted, and her stomach dropped to see how thoroughly. Something had been taken from the ash. She could see the sweep-mark in the grey where a careful hand had lifted a fragment free. A corner of burned margin-paper, perhaps. A letter or two. Not enough to hang anyone. Enough to confirm suspicion.

*Finch,* she thought. Or one of Finch's sort. Someone patient enough to sift ashes at two in the morning wanted more than duty satisfied him.

She made herself stand. Made herself cross to the map rack, where the great half-finished chart of the marshes hung in its frame, and her heart performed its one lurch of the night: the frame was askew, lifted, replaced — but replaced by hands that had looked for a hollow behind canvas and found only backing board and tacking. They had searched the map itself, run fingers over the painted flats, held it to the lantern hunting for watermarks and secret inks.

Not the lining of the coat slung carelessly over the rack's end, damp with marsh fog, smelling of mud and samphire, exactly like every other garment a working surveyor wore into the field.

Forty-one pages, sewn flat, riding against her own coat's shoulder-blades, and the most dangerous officer in Harborwick had brushed past them twice.

She sat down on the floor amid the wreckage of her life and let out a laugh with no joy in it at all.

* *

Dawn found her still there, or found her there again — she had lost the hours somewhere. Grey light came through the broken shutters and showed her the ruin honestly: the gouged workbench, the emptied cabinets, the place on the wall where the atlas's shelf-box had hung for twenty years and now showed only a paler rectangle, like the ghost of the thing itself.

And in that grey honest light, the clarity she had walked into the marshes chasing finally arrived, and it arrived without mercy, which was the only way clarity ever came to her.

The original was gone. Gone was not a thing to be prevented or mourned into return; gone was simply a fact, like the tide, like her father's death, like the shape of her own body beneath its binding. And since the original was gone, the question that had tormented her for three days — *how do I save it?* — dissolved, and left standing behind it the only real question: *how do I save what it says?*

She already had. That was the terrible, ordinary truth. Forty-one scraps of chart offcut, inked by lamplight in her own best counterfeit of a dead man's hand. All that remained was the carrying, and the carrying was impossible, and impossible was simply a word people used for things that hadn't been worked out yet.

There was no going back — not to the workshop as it had been, not to the trade as she had practiced it, not to the careful gray life of Gideon Montrose, mapmaker, who signed another man's name and kept his ghosts in his margins and asked nothing of the world but to be left unexamined. That man had died tonight in his sleep while soldiers pulled his house apart around him. What woke in his place was somebody smaller and harder, somebody with nothing left to protect except a coat full of names and a boy with a fish cart and whatever remained of three days — two days now, and the tide tables pinned inside her skull where no requisition could reach them.

Somewhere across the town, Tamsin Hale would be waking to the same grey light, and Gideon prayed — not having prayed in twenty years, and doing it badly — that the soldiers' list had not yet grown a widow's name.

She rose. She took the coat from the rack and put it on. The sewn pages pressed against her back like a spine that did not belong to her, like a second skeleton, and she stood straighter under the weight of them.

Outside, the sentry at the corner shifted and coughed. The harbor lay flat and silver under the coming sun, and at anchor beyond the seized boats, half-hidden in the morning haze, rode the masts of the ship that sailed on tomorrow's tide.

She had one day to get forty families off her back and onto that deck — and the whole town between here and the water was waking up full of soldiers.

Gideon buttoned the coat to the throat, picked her way through her ruined life to the door, and stepped out into the dawn.
