## 13. Tamsin's Arrest

The knocking began before the gulls woke.

Gideon heard it from her cot in the back of the workshop — three hard raps, a pause, three more — and knew the sound of it the way she knew the sound of rain against the north-facing glass. Not the polite knock of a neighbor come to borrow flour. The knock of men who did not need to be answered.

She rose without lighting a candle. Twenty years of habit moved her body through the dark: hand on the loose board beneath the workbench first, confirming that the hollow behind it was empty, that the marsh-map pages had already gone where no soldier would think to look. Then to the window, the one with the warped shutter that left a finger's width of view onto Cooper's Lane.

Lanterns. Six of them, maybe seven, bobbing in the grey hour like swamp-lights. Red coats beneath, dulled almost to black in the pre-dawn, and the wet gleam of bayonets. They were gathered at Tamsin Hale's door across the lane, and one of them was hammering at it again as though the door had personally insulted the Crown.

Gideon pressed her forehead to the cold shutter slat and breathed.

*Not me,* she thought, and hated herself for the relief that came with it, quick and animal and shameful, arriving before grief could get its boots on.

The Hales' door opened. A bar of candlelight fell across the cobbles, and Tamsin Hale stepped into it — fully dressed, which meant she had not slept either, which meant she had heard the boots coming up from the quay and had chosen to meet them standing rather than dragged. She was seventy if she was a day, a narrow woman made mostly of sinew and stubbornness, who had buried a husband and two sons to the sea and one son to something she never named, and she stood on her own threshold now with her hands folded at her waist like a woman waiting for a coach.

Gideon could not hear what was said. The wind carried only fragments — a sergeant's voice, flat and procedural; the word *questions*, which every soul in Harborwick had learned in the past week was a word that meant *rope*. Then a soldier went past Tamsin into the house, and another after him, and Tamsin did not cry out or clutch the doorframe. She simply turned her head, once, slowly, and looked across the lane.

Straight at the workshop window. Straight at the finger's width of dark where Gideon stood.

Gideon could not say whether she was seen. In the grey light, at that distance, with an old woman's eyes — probably not. Probably Tamsin was only looking at the place where help should have come from, the way a drowning woman looks at a shore she knows is too far.

Then they put a shawl around her shoulders, which was the army's idea of mercy, and walked her down toward the countinghouse, and the lanterns went with her, and Cooper's Lane sank back into the dark, and the door of the Hale house stood open behind them like a mouth mid-sentence.

Gideon stayed at the window until the light came up. She did not move. Moving felt like agreement, somehow — like conceding that the world had gone on and could be gotten on with. So she stood, and the sky went from slate to pearl to the pale washed yellow of an old chart, and the fishing boats that should have gone out sat dead at their moorings because their masters were all standing in their doorways looking at the Hale house, and nobody spoke, and nobody lit a cooking fire though it was long past the hour for it, and Harborwick performed the oldest ritual a frightened town knows, which is silence.

She knew this silence. She had grown up inside it. There had been a night twenty years ago when soldiers came for Master Corvus and did not find what they wanted, and the next morning the whole street had been quiet in exactly this way — not empty, but *held*, the whole town holding its breath between its teeth, every door a closed eye. A town under occupation does not weep in public. It goes quiet the way a wood goes quiet when the hawk crosses over it, and it stays quiet until the shadow passes or until someone is taken, and then it goes quieter still, because quiet is the only shelter left that cannot be requisitioned.

*The first name on the list.*

That was the thought that finally moved her. Jonah Hale's name, inked in his master's careful hand in the margin of a coastal survey of a coastline Jonah Hale had never sailed — a name put there because Corvus owed him, because Jonah had carried a fugitive in a fish barrel through this very town while Corvus drew the patrol routes that let the barrel pass. Forty names. Forty gallows, she had counted once in the dark, and now the count had begun, and it had begun not with Gideon Montrose, keeper of the list, but with the widow across the lane whose only crime was being married to a good man for thirty years.

She dressed. Breeches, shirt, coat — the coat, with its false lining not yet sewn, its secret still living in the marsh map out beyond the sea-wall. She bound herself the way she had done every morning for twenty years, quick and unthinking, the linen pulls tight under her jaw, and caught her own eye in the cracked looking-glass by the door, and her own eye said: *you did this.*

No. That was not true either, and lying to herself was a luxury the day could not afford. She had not written the names. But she was the reason the army had come sniffing through ledgers, the reason Wren was in the countinghouse going through Corvus's correspondence with a candle and a patience that frightened her more than any number of bayonets. And she was the one still free, still breathing, still able to walk out her door while Tamsin Hale could not.

There was a debt forming. Gideon had kept accounts all her life — inks, papers, hours, favors — and she knew a ledger's arithmetic better than she knew most scripture. What was accumulating against her name now could only be paid one way: the names had to reach the ship. All of them. Before the tide on the morning after next.

She went out into the silent street.

* * *

Tamsin Hale had expected iron and darkness, and was surprised to find only a table and a chair and a young clerk with ink-stained fingers who would not quite look at her.

They had put her in the back room of the commandeered countinghouse, and whatever Captain Wren intended by her, it was not yet underway. The clerk — Finch, he had said, in a voice like an apology — had brought her tea. Actual tea, in an actual cup, chipped at the rim. He set it down and retreated to his inventory lists at the far end of the room and pretended very hard to count barrels.

Tamsin drank the tea and waited. She had outlived a husband, two sons, and one particularly vicious winter; she could outwait an army.

It was near noon when the door opened properly and Captain Aldous Wren came in, and Tamsin understood within four seconds of looking at him why Jonah had never spoken of his army years. Some men carry their history in their faces; this man carried his appetites there. He smiled at her the way a man smiles at a strongbox he has not yet got open.

"Mrs. Hale. I apologize for the early hour. And the accommodations." He took the chair opposite without being offered, unrolled a paper she recognized with a lurch as a page torn from some ledger — hers, the shipping manifests Jonah had kept, God rest him, for the herring trade. "Your late husband's business interests were… varied."

"My husband sold fish," Tamsin said.

"Your husband sold fish," Wren agreed pleasantly, "and occasionally mislaid entire cargoes, and once received payment from a Bristol merchant for forty barrels that were never packed. Curious, the things ledgers remember." He tapped the page. "I am not interested in hanging you, Mrs. Hale. I am interested in names. Men your husband corresponded with. Houses he delivered to after dark. Give me those, and you will walk home tonight, and I will personally ensure these proceedings forget your address."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the clerk, Finch, who had gone very still over his lists, his pen hovering above the paper like a bird afraid to land.

"Captain," she said, "my husband has been dead six years. If I could remember half of what you want, I'd have sold it long ago for firewood money." She folded her hands. "You've taken my tea things' worth of patience. You'll get nothing else before supper."

Wren studied her, and the pleasantness drained out of his face by degrees, the way water leaves a lock. "Sixty years old," he said, almost to himself, "and willing to swing for other people's sins. Loyalty at your age is a waste of a good commodity." He stood, rolled the manifest, and tucked it inside his coat. "We will try again tomorrow. Perhaps a night on a hard floor will improve your memory."

At the door he paused. "You have a friend across the lane. The mapmaker." His voice stayed mild, but Tamsin felt the floor tilt beneath her chair. "Montrose. Odd man. Keeps to himself. Does he keep to himself, Mrs. Hale?"

"He mends my roof tiles," Tamsin said. "Men who climb ladders for widows are rare. I don't ask them questions."

"No," Wren said softly. "People in this town don't seem to ask questions. It's becoming a theme." And he was gone.

Finch did not move for a full minute after the door closed. Then, without looking up, without any change in his flat clerical voice, he said: "There'll be more tea if you want it. They forget about the kettle."

Tamsin looked at the young man's bent head and thought: *there is a crack in this wall.* She had lived long enough to know that every wall had one. She said only, "Thank you," and drank her tea, and began composing, for the second time that day, exactly how much of the truth a person could tell without dying of it.

* * *

By evening the silence in Harborwick had acquired texture.

Gideon walked the long way to the chandler's for tallow she did not need, and counted the town's fear the way she counted fathoms. The baker's shutters closed an hour early. Two fishermen mending nets by the sea-wall worked without speaking, which among fishermen was louder than screaming. At the corner by the Zed and Anchor, old Marta Croy stopped her mid-stride with a look, said nothing, glanced toward the countinghouse, glanced away. The whole town was a chart with a new feature inked across it: *here be arrests*, and every soul navigating accordingly.

Tam found her at the chandler's counter.

The boy materialized the way he always did, smell of fish and tar, a basket of sprats on his hip he was supposedly delivering. He set the basket down, looked at her with those dark unreadable eyes, and made his small quick signs: *soldiers — took — the — widow — everyone — watching.*

"I know," Gideon said quietly.

*Tomorrow?* he signed. *Ship?*

"The morning after. Tide's at first light." She glanced at the chandler, who was studiously weighing nothing in the back. "Tam. After today, they may watch the carts closer. If it turns bad — if anyone stops you — you know nothing. You're carrying fish. You've always only ever carried fish. Do you understand me?"

The boy looked at her, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes that was far older than his years — the look of someone who had learned young that the world asks children to carry things they shouldn't. He signed back, slow and deliberate: *I know — nothing — I carry — fish.*

And then, after a pause, with the terrible economy of the mute: *Like — you.*

Gideon's chest tightened. She wanted to tell him it wasn't the same, that she chose this, that he was only carrying paper — but that was precisely what Corvus had told her at eleven years old, ink-stained and solemn, teaching her which documents to carry past which doors, telling her it was only maps, only paper, only a game with high stakes played for keeps. She had believed him. She had *thanked* him. She was, she realized, doing to Tam exactly what the old man had done to her, right down to the lie of small words for large dangers.

"Go home," she said roughly. "Stay off the lanes tonight. I'll find you tomorrow at the tide-pools, before dusk."

He went. She watched him thread between the shuttered houses and disappear, small and quick as a sandpiper, and she stood a long moment in the chandler's doorway feeling the full weight of what she had just done settle onto her shoulders like wet canvas.

Then she walked home through the held breath of the town, and barred her door, and did not sleep.

* * *

She tried, in the dark, to make the arithmetic come out clean.

If she surrendered now — walked to the countinghouse, gave Wren the atlas, gave him the testimony he wanted, blamed the dead man for everything as the dead man could not object — then Tamsin might yet hang anyway, because Wren already had the manifests and would squeeze until something broke. Or Tamsin might go free, and forty other families would hang instead, slowly, by process of law. Either way Gideon would live, and either way she would live as a woman who had spent other people's blood to buy her own quiet.

If she ran tonight — alone, no copies, no Tam, no ship — the atlas remained, the names remained, Wren remained, and everything she had suffered these three days would purchase nothing but distance.

There was no clean arithmetic. There never was. Corvus had taught her that too, in his way, though she had needed twenty years and a widow's arrest to hear it. Every map is a lie agreed upon, he used to say; the coast is not the line, the line is all we can carry. You draw the line you can live inside.

Very well. This was the line she could live inside: the pages hidden in the marsh map, retrieved tomorrow at falling tide; the transfer to Tam's cart; the flooded road past the second checkpoint by dark, dressed as a tide-surveyor with her father's brass instruments and her own twenty years of knowing where the ground would hold a woman's weight and where it would swallow her. And the morning after, Finch's problem to solve or fail, and the quay, and the ship, and the sea.

Tomorrow, whether she was ready or not. *Especially* whether she was ready or not — because readiness was a luxury the arrested could not extend to the free, and every hour she delayed was an hour Wren spent with Tamsin Hale in a locked room, learning patience from her the way water learns a crack.

She rose before midnight, lit one candle, and took out the one thing she had not dared touch since the copying began: the single finished sheet bearing all forty names in her finest hand, sewn already into the offcut of chart that would go with her if all else failed. She spread it on the bench and read down the margin of it, name by name, and at the top, first inked, most damned, she found what she knew she would find:

*Jonah Hale, Harborwick, and Tamsin his wife, who asked no questions and carried much.*

Her wife. Corvus had recorded Tamsin himself, in his careful cartographer's script, because the widow had ferried messages between safe houses for nine years and never once faltered. Gideon had read the line a hundred times. Tonight it read differently. Tonight it read like a sentence passed in absentia, with Gideon's own handwriting on the warrant — for it was her copying that existed, her plan that had drawn attention, her survival that was being purchased at retail across the lane.

"Forgive me," she said to the candle, to the names, to the sleeping silent town. "Not yet. But soon, and truly."

She pinched out the flame, and in the darkness she made her decision final, the way she drew a coastline — firmly, once, without lifting the pen.

At dawn she would go to the marshes.

What she did not know — could not know, walking the last of her rounds in the black hour before first light, checking the loose board, oiling the hinges of the scullery window, laying out the tide-surveyor's coat with its brass instruments gleaming — was that across the lane, in the house with the door still standing open, soldiers were searching Tamsin Hale's kitchen by candlelight, and that under the loose brick behind the bread oven they would find, before the sun cleared the sea-wall, a bundle of letters in a hand they had been ordered to recognize.

Letters from a mapmaker. Addressed to a widow. Dated within the week.

By the time Gideon reached the sea-wall with the tide running out gold before her, Harborwick was already waking to the sound of boots — moving not toward the countinghouse this time, but toward the marshes, ahead of her, along the very road she had to take.
