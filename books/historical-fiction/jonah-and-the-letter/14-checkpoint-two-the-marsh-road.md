## 14. Checkpoint Two: The Marsh Road

The tide turned at half past nine, and Gideon Montrose felt it before she saw it — a loosening in the air, the faint exhale of the marsh as the water began its slow retreat from the samphire flats. She had been waiting since dusk behind the sea-wall, wrapped in Corvus's old oilcloth coat, watching the last light drain out of the sky over Harborwick and listening to the town behind her make its small terrified noises: a dog, a shutter, a cart that moved too fast.

She knew this ground better than she knew her own hands. Twenty years of drawing it had seen to that — every channel, every gut, every treacherous shelf of mud that looked solid and was not. The marsh south of the harbor road was a place where the army's maps failed, because no surveyor had troubled to wade it, and it was a place where the water kept its own schedule with an honesty no soldier possessed. If you understood the tide, you could walk where men on horseback could not follow. If you understood the tide, you could arrive at a checkpoint at the one hour when the soldiers were standing in six inches of cold water and thinking of nothing but their boots.

That hour would come at two o'clock. The spring tide, drawn by a full moon three days gone, would flood the causeway past the second checkpoint's doorstones and hold there for perhaps forty minutes — long enough that the sentries would withdraw up the bank to the dry ground by the toll-house, long enough that anyone moving through the flooded road would be a shape in the dark, unsearchable, unwinnable, beneath their notice or above their patience.

She had drawn this tide herself, once, in ink. Plate eleven of the atlas. *Harborwick South Marshes, with Soundings at High Water Springs.* Master Corvus had made her sound every foot of it in a leaky punt when she was sixteen, shouting readings against the wind while she wrote them into a greasy notebook with a pencil stub. She had hated him for it then. Tonight she blessed him with a fierceness that hurt her throat.

*He taught you the ground so the ground could carry you,* she thought. *Everything he ever taught you was for this night, though neither of you knew it.*

At half past midnight she rose from behind the sea-wall and went down onto the flats.

* * *

The mud took her boots to the ankle and held them with a wet, sucking reluctance, as though the marsh had opinions about what left its body tonight. She moved by the moon's low light and by memory, following the spine of firmer ground that ran between the outer channel and the old saltings — a ridge no wider than a cart track, invisible from the road, which she had walked a hundred times in daylight and twice before in darkness. Samphire brushed her shins. Somewhere to her left the channel breathed, filling.

She carried nothing but the coat on her back and the knowledge in her head, and yet she had never felt heavier. In the lining of that coat, sewn flat against the left side beneath her arm where a search would find only ribs, lay forty names copied in her smallest hand onto four scraps of chart offcut — the work of two nights, each scrap destroyed in the grate as soon as its successor was begun, each letter formed slowly enough to keep the ink from feathering. But the marsh map still held the rest. Half-finished, rolled and hidden in the hollow of the dead alder at the marsh's edge, the chart of the southern marshes carried its secret cargo: pages slipped between paper and backing cloth, invisible unless you knew to lift one from the other, holding the whole of the marginalia entire — dates, initials, the routes of boats, twenty years of quiet salvation written in the hand of a dead man and the hand of a woman who did not exist.

She had to retrieve those pages tonight and move them beyond the reach of whatever Wren planned next. Finch's visit two evenings ago — his eyes on the grate, his too-long silence — had told her the workshop was a dying thing. And last night, from her window in Tamsin Hale's back room, she had watched lanterns cross the market square in a pattern she recognized from her childhood: not a patrol but a cordon. They were tightening around something. Perhaps only the harbor. Perhaps everything.

Her master had taught her that a map is never finished; it is only abandoned at some acceptable degree of error. So too, now, with plans. You worked until the tide came in and called the error acceptable.

The alder stood where it always stood, a black claw of a tree against the paler black of the sky. She knelt in the mud beside it and worked her arm into the hollow at its root, and her fingers found the roll of paper exactly as she had left it, dry, wrapped in oilcloth, patient. There was a moment — her hand closed around it — when the whole marsh seemed to go silent and listen, and she waited for a shout, a lantern, a shot out of the dark. Nothing came. Only the channel, breathing. She drew the roll out and pressed it to her chest like a child.

*Forty names,* she thought. *And every one of them heavier than lead.*

* * *

She stopped in the lee of the salting shed to open the roll and check it, because Corvus had taught her never to trust a thing she had not verified with her own senses, and because her hands needed something to do that was not shaking. By feel rather than sight — light here would be suicide — she lifted the backing cloth from the chart and confirmed the pages sat where she had placed them, four sheets folded eighths, thin as regret. Then she did the harder thing. She took the topmost sheet, the one bearing the first ten names with Hale at its head, and slid it free, and sewed it into her coat lining beside the copies with the needle and waxed thread she carried for exactly this purpose, working the stitches blind, pricking her thumb twice and being glad of the pain because it sharpened her.

The remaining pages went into Tam's keeping. That was the arrangement, made yesterday in the fish-smoke gloom of the boy's uncle's shed, conducted mostly in gestures and the few words of instruction Gideon judged safe to speak aloud. The cart would come to the head of the marsh road at half past one. The pages would go beneath the false bottom of the brine tank, under a layer of herring that had been rotting there since morning for the purpose — no soldier alive would dig past that smell without orders and a bayonet. Tam would take the coast road north to Cley, delivering fish to the inn there as he did every week, untouched and unnoticed, a mute boy with a mute cargo. From Cley, Thursday, a wherry. Gideon would give him nothing else, because he needed nothing else: the less he knew, the safer they both were, and the thought of how well she knew that truth — of who had taught it to her, and how it had felt to be the child who knew too little to refuse — sat in her chest like a swallowed stone all the way up the saltings to meet the road.

The cart was already there when she reached the track, a darker shape among dark shapes, and Tam a smaller shape beside it, still as a post, his breath steaming. He did not startle at her approach. She wondered sometimes whether the boy's silence had taken his fear along with his words, or simply given him nowhere to put it.

She lifted the brine tank's lid, laid the pages beneath the herring, and closed it. Her eyes were watering from the reek and she let them.

"You remember the inn at Cley," she said softly. "You remember Mr. Quill. You say nothing. You give him nothing." She had taught him that much: *give him nothing.* "Thursday."

Tam nodded once. Then, unexpectedly, he caught her sleeve, and pointed — north, along the coast road — and shook his head, and pointed again.

"I know," she said. "I know the watch is doubled at the bridge. This isn't the bridge." She put her hand briefly on his shoulder, the closest thing to an embrace either of them could afford. "Go home. Sleep. Be ordinary tomorrow. That's the bravest thing anyone can do this week."

He looked at her for a long moment in the dark — a look she could not read and did not try to, because reading it would have meant admitting what she had done, making a child complicit in the saving of forty families, exactly as another man had once made a child complicit in the saving of a map. Then he climbed onto the cart and clicked his tongue, and the pony leaned into its collar, and the wheels crunched away northward carrying forty lives under a layer of rotten herring.

Gideon stood in the road until the sound of them was gone. Then she turned south, toward the checkpoint, and became a tide-surveyor.

* * *

The transformation had taken an hour that afternoon, in Tamsin's back room, and it was the finest piece of forgery she had ever produced, because the material being forged was herself. Corvus's oldest surveying coat, brushed and mended. His leather satchel, scarred by thirty years of fieldwork, containing instruments real and hers: the brass sextant, the sounding line coiled just so, a notebook genuinely filled with tidal readings in her own careful hand — genuine because she had spent three hours copying them from plate eleven, because a lie built around true things is a load-bearing lie, and a searched man with authentic papers is merely tedious while a searched man with perfect papers is impossible. A lantern with a shuttered slide. A military pass that read *J. Montrose, Surveyor, attached to the Office of the Harbormaster, upon His Majesty's Service*, bearing a signature she had practiced forty times until Finch's own hand flowed off her nib without her thinking about it — poor Finch, whose guilt she was spending like stolen coin, and whom she intended to repay if ever God gave her the chance.

She had even, in a flourish she half despised herself for, rubbed mud into the coat hem and salt-stained the cuffs, so that she smelled like her own profession.

Now she walked the flooded causeway with her lantern shuttered to a slit, and the water came up over her boot-tops, black and glass-flat, mirroring a sky full of hard indifferent stars, and she was afraid, and she mastered the fear the only way she had ever mastered anything: by measuring it.

The flood stands four fingers deep at the doorstone at two o'clock, she told herself, counting her steps. It drains by a foot by half past. The sentries quit the road for the toll-house bank at the first stand of the tide and do not return while their boots are wet. I have forty minutes. I have walked this road in my mind five hundred times. The distance from the salting shed to the toll-house is one thousand two hundred paces. I am at pace four hundred. The channel on my left is filling. The ground underfoot is gravel over clay, firm, surveyed, known.

Fear is only ignorance of the ground, Corvus used to say, tapping her knuckles with a ruler when her hand wavered over the pen. Know the ground and the fear has nowhere to stand.

She knew the ground. She had made it. At pace eight hundred the drowned road bent around the old oyster beds and she saw, ahead and slightly elevated, the checkpoint: the toll-house with its single window, a fire glowing behind the shutters, two shapes visible on the bank above the drowned road, one of them pacing, boots in hand, the other sitting very still in the manner of a man negotiating with the cold. A third shape moved at the far end where the road rose dry again. Three men. All of them miserable. None of them looking at the water, because the water was the army's problem tonight and not the army's opportunity.

Gideon slowed to the pace of a man doing his job. She opened her lantern's slide a finger's width, letting fall a coin of weak light on the road ahead, and she raised her sextant in its plainest, most bureaucratic gesture, sighting at nothing — at the stars, at the water, at the whole indifferent apparatus of the heavens — and she walked.

A hundred paces. Fifty. The sentry by the far end stirred and shaded his eyes against her little coin of light.

"Who goes?" His voice was young and cracked with cold. "Road's flooded. No passage till the ebb."

"Surveyor," Gideon called back, and made her voice into something bored, official, and slightly hoarse — J. Montrose, attached to the Office of the Harbormaster, a man who had been standing in cold water since midnight and resented everyone responsible. "Springs won't wait for your road. Harbormaster wants soundings on the ebb and I'll have them if I drown for it." She let the exasperation do the work. Anger, she had learned long ago, was the most masculine thing a voice could carry; no one searches an angry man, they apologize to him.

"Says who?"

She was close enough now to see his face, nineteen years old at most, chapped raw. She stopped, set her feet deliberately in the flooded road, and produced the pass from her breast pocket with the deliberation of a man who produces documents eleven times a day and finds the question insulting. The paper gleamed in the lantern light. Finch's signature sat at the bottom, fluent and doomed.

The sentry squinted at it, and at her, and she watched his eyes travel over the coat, the satchel, the sextant, the mud — cataloguing, dismissing. She was, to him, furniture. An instrument of the same army that owned him, arriving out of the dark to add inconvenience to misery. She could see the exact moment his attention surrendered: his gaze sliding off her to the fire in the toll-house window, his shoulders dropping.

"Soundings," he said, with the contempt of the cold for the comfortable. "At two in the morning."

"The tide keeps no garrison hours," Gideon said, pocketing the pass. "Nor do I."

He almost smiled — a flicker of kinship, soldier and servant both ground between the same millstones — and stepped aside, waving her through with two fingers. "Mind the culvert at the bend. Dropped a crate down it Tuesday, lost the rum and all."

"Noted," said Gideon Montrose, tide-surveyor, and walked on, and did not run, though every nerve in her legs screamed to run, and it was the hardest thing she had done in twenty years of hard things: one thousand two hundred paces at the pace of a tired professional man, boots splashing, sextant glinting, while behind her the sentry called something to his fellows about the mad buggers the harbormaster employed, and laughter followed her thin across the water, harmless, ignorant, already forgetting her.

Only when the toll-house fire had sunk below the bend of the road did she allow herself to stop, and lean against the sea-wall, and discover that her hands, gripping the satchel strap, would not immediately open.

* * *

She came off the marsh road an hour before dawn, at the point where the sea-wall met the pasture behind the smokehouse, and she stood a long time looking back the way she had come. The marsh lay gray and breathing under the fading stars. Somewhere out there, the tide was turning again, faithful, punctual, keeping the schedule she had written for it in another life. She had passed the second checkpoint. The pages were under herring, bound for Cley. The names were against her heart. One road remained, and it was the worst of the three: the quay, in daylight, among searching hands, with Wren himself somewhere in the streets behind her like a smell of smoke.

She should feel triumphant. Instead she felt the peculiar hollowness that follows fear mastered — the sense of having spent something that does not renew. Twice tonight she had been within a shout of the gallows, and twice her craft had carried her through, and the craft was real and the skill was hers, earned in a leaky punt and at a candlelit desk across twenty borrowed years. But craft, she thought, walking on through the wet grass toward Tamsin's dark window, had gotten her past the sentries. It would not get her past Wren. Wren was not a flooded road. Wren was a man, and men changed their schedules.

She let herself in through the smokehouse door, sat down on Tamsin's kitchen floor with her back against the cold range, and allowed herself, at last, one minute of shaking.

Then she heard the bells.

Not the church clock — the harbor bell, and after it another, and men's voices in the lane outside, running, and boots passing the shuttered windows in numbers no night patrol had any business possessing. Gideon rose slowly to the gap in the shutter and looked out into the graying street, and saw red coats moving house to house in the dawn, and saw them stop at the widow Hale's gate.

Tamsin's name led the list. Everyone in Harborwick knew whose name led the list.

They were waking her now.
