## 15. What Wren Remembers

The guardhouse at Harborwick had been a countinghouse before the army came, and it still smelled of tallow and old salt rather than justice. Captain Aldous Wren noticed such things. He had spent thirty years learning to notice what rooms remembered — the business they had conducted before soldiers arrived to make their business his.

He sat now behind a table that had once weighed cargo, with Tamsin Hale on the opposite side of it, her wrists bound loosely in front of her, her chin lifted in the particular way of a woman who had buried a husband and decided there was nothing left worth lowering her chin for.

"You understand," Wren said, "that this is an informal conversation."

"I understand you've had me in a cell since dawn and no one's told my daughter where I am."

"Your daughter is with the minister's wife. She's eating bread and butter and asking when you'll be home." He folded his hands. "I should like that answer to be soon, Mrs. Hale. It depends entirely on you."

Tamsin Hale looked at him the way one looks at weather — assessing, unafraid, already calculating how long it would last. Wren found himself admiring it. Fear made prisoners talkative; contempt made them careful. This woman was giving him contempt, which meant she believed she had something he wanted badly enough to trade for.

She was not wrong.

* * *

He began, as he always began, far from the point.

"You knew Elias Corvus."

"I knew of him. A mapmaker. My husband bought charts from him for the *Merrow*. Everyone in Harborwick knew him."

"And did everyone know him to be a kind man? A generous one?"

"A quiet one." Her eyes narrowed slightly. "Why does the King's army care whether a dead mapmaker was kind?"

Wren smiled and let the smile sit a moment before taking it away. "Because kindness leaves marks, Mrs. Hale. Paper trails. Debts of gratitude. I knew Corvus myself, you see — before he was a mapmaker, before he was anyone's neighbor. We served together. Verran's campaign, the marsh years. He drew the maps that kept three hundred men from drowning in the fens, and I carried the dispatches that made him famous enough to retire here and grow old in ink." He leaned back. "So when I discovered his atlas full of names — forty names, forty families, a whole quiet industry of treason conducted under cover of geography — I took it personally. A friend's betrayal stings longer than a stranger's."

"He's dead twenty years. Whoever he betrayed, he betrayed himself first."

"Perhaps. But treason doesn't die with its author. It just goes on being treason." He produced the atlas from beneath the table — it had been recovered from the workshop two nights past, its boards scored by the search — and set it down between them without opening it. "The first name in these margins is Hale. Josiah Hale, master of the *Merrow*, who carried eleven souls out of this country between the years '31 and '36 and was paid, I suspect, in nothing but the satisfaction of it. Kindness leaving marks."

Tamsin's jaw tightened. For the first time something moved behind her eyes that was not contempt.

"My husband is four years drowned," she said. "You can hang a name, Captain, but you'll need a neck."

"The Crown disagrees. The Crown has always been willing to hang a memory if the flesh isn't available. Widow's estates forfeit. Children lose their standing. There are more ways than rope to punish a family, as I'm sure a woman of your intelligence already knows." He watched her absorb this, watched her refuse to flinch, and thought: *Corvus chose his people well.* Then he corrected himself. *Corvus chose his people the way I choose mine — for what they could carry.*

But it wasn't the names he had summoned her to discuss. Not really. Forty smugglers were forty problems, and problems were merely the currency of any officer's career. What Wren wanted was the other thing — the thing that had been tickling at him since the first day he'd stood in that workshop and watched the mapmaker's apprentice grind ink with those long, deliberate hands.

"Tell me about the apprentice," he said.

Something shifted in the room. He saw it happen — a stillness coming over Tamsin Hale like frost over a window, her face closing not in guilt but in *decision*.

"Gideon Montrose," Wren went on, pleasantly. "Corvus took him in as a boy, I'm told. Trained him in the trade. Left him the workshop and the atlas entire. Curious arrangement for a man with no kin — unless the apprentice was kin in every way but paper." He tilted his head. "Was he?"

"Why ask me? You've met him."

"I've met him, yes." Wren rose and walked to the narrow window, looking out at the gray harbor, the seized boats riding at anchor like patient cattle. "A careful young man. Very good hands. Doesn't drink at the tavern, doesn't court, doesn't gossip. Signs his master's name to everything and his own to nothing — I checked the guild registries, Mrs. Hale. Twenty years of work and not one signature of his own anywhere. Not on a marriage record, a lease, a tithe roll. Nothing before the year '27 at all. It's as though Gideon Montrose was *born* in Corvus's house, fully grown, with a graver in his hand."

Behind him, silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"I knew Corvus's daughter," Wren said quietly, still facing the water. "Did you know that? In the army years, before the mother died and he sent the child away. He spoke of her the way misers speak of gold — never showing it, never spending it, but you could tell it was there, weighing on him. A little girl with his own stubborn chin. He called her—" Wren paused, tasting the memory. "Well. That name isn't in any registry either, is it. Because when Corvus left the service he didn't come home alone with his maps. He came home with a child who became a boy who became an apprentice, and somewhere in that alchemy a daughter stopped existing and a son began. And every soul in Harborwick decided, all together, without ever saying a word, not to notice."

He turned around. Tamsin Hale had gone very pale, but her eyes were dry and level, and Wren understood suddenly and completely that he was right, and that she had known, and that she would have gone to the gallows before confirming it.

He found, examining himself honestly, that he felt no triumph at all. Only appetite.

* * *

They gave him a private room above the customs shed and a lamp, and he sat long into the night with Corvus's letters spread across the boards — the old army correspondence he had requested from the regimental archive weeks ago, ostensibly to establish provenance for the requisitioned papers, actually because a man who intends to leverage another man must first learn what that man loved.

There was not much. Corvus had been a poor correspondent and a worse sentimentalist. But there was enough.

A letter from '19, complaining of fever in the camp and thanking Wren — *Aldous, you old shark* — for the loan of a horse. A letter from '21, brief and cold, announcing his intention to sell his commission: *the service has taught me all the ways a man can be used and none of the ways he can be spared.* And one letter, undated, tucked among the others, in a hand shakier than the rest:

*A. — If anything should happen to me, there is a chest in my workshop with a false bottom. What it contains concerns a person, not a prize. I ask you, as the only man from those years whose word I ever trusted, to see it kept from official hands. Do this and I remain your debtor. Sell it, and God forgive you, because I will have forgiven you already, which will be worse.*

Wren had read it perhaps forty times in the past fortnight. He read it again now, and again it performed its strange arithmetic on him. *A person, not a prize.* Corvus had known even then — had known exactly what his daughter would be worth, to whom, and why — and had trusted Aldous Wren, of all people, with the knowledge.

That trust had been the old man's last foolishness. Wren had not been trustworthy at nineteen, when they'd met in the mud of Verran. He had simply been *useful*, which Corvus, brilliant with coastlines and hopeless with men, had never learned to tell apart.

And yet. Wren turned the letter over, and over again, and admitted to the empty room what he had been refusing to admit all week: that some small, rusted mechanism in his chest had objected, these past days, to the shape of his intentions. That the objection had been growing louder since he'd stood in the workshop and watched Gideon Montrose's hands move over the copper plate — hands that reminded him of another pair of hands, smaller, guiding a horse's mane through his own fingers while a girl of eight informed him, with Corvus's exact stubborn chin, that the marshes weren't flat, they were *layered*, and any fool could see it.

He had liked her instantly. That was the trouble. He had liked her the way one likes a reflection of a better self.

He snuffed the feeling out with the ease of long practice. Sentiment was a luxury purchased by men with pensions. Wren had debts — gaming debts, promotion debts, the quiet crushing mortgages of a career built on other men's misfortunes — and a woman pretending to be a man was not merely a scandal. It was an instrument. Twenty years of fraud against the guild, the parish, the Crown itself; forgery of documents, impersonation, unlawful inheritance of a freeman's trade. Any one of the charges would destroy her. Together they would make her do anything. Testify as he directed. Sign what he required. Become, in short, what Wren had been to his superiors for thirty years — a tool with a face.

Forty smugglers were worth a commendation. One false man was worth a fortune.

And there was the atlas to consider — the atlas, which Finch reported missing pages from, which the searches had failed to account for entirely. Where there were copies, there was a copyist. Where there was a copyist, there was leverage already moving through the town like blood through cloth. He would find it. He would find it in her coat lining or her fish-boy's cart or the marsh mud itself, and then the instrument would be complete.

Unless she sailed. The tide tables lay open on the desk — he had pulled them from the seized ledgers, because tide tables were maps too and Wren had learned from the best to read them. The morning tide, three days hence. He had closed the harbor, doubled the quay watch, ordered every departing soul stripped and searched. It should have been sufficient.

It was not sufficient, and he knew it, because he had taught her everything she knew about reading ground and water — no. Not her. *Him.* Damn it, he had taught *the girl*, twenty-five years ago, one golden afternoon on the Verran dikes, and she had remembered, and the remembering had been passed through twenty years of disguise like a flame carried in a shuttered lantern, and now it was going to walk past his soldiers wearing his own lessons for a face.

Wren laughed aloud, once, without humor, in the lamplit room.

"All right, little mapmaker," he said to the harbor beyond the window, to the black water and the sleeping masts. "Layered, not flat. Show me where you'll surface."

He took up a pen and began, in his neat quartermaster's hand, a list of instructions: the fish carts to be stopped at the bridge; the boy questioned separately from the men; the surveyor's credentials of every person crossing the marsh road to be verified against the guild rolls — knowing, even as he wrote it, that the guild rolls held no Gideon Montrose at all, and that the absence was the loudest signature in the book.

At the bottom of the page, after the orders, he wrote a single line meant for no clerk's eyes:

*Do not harm. Detain only. The value is alive.*

Then he sat back, and blew out the lamp, and let the dark harbor tell him nothing, thoroughly, until dawn — when a runner arrived soaked with spray and out of breath, to report that the widow Hale had said nothing under questioning, that the bridge watch had searched seventeen fish carts and found only fish, and that a burned scrap of paper bearing a fragment of marginal handwriting had been handed in that morning by Clerk Elias Finch, who wore, the runner said, a very strange expression indeed.

Wren held the scrap to the window light. Four words survived the fire. Four words in a hand he now recognized as well as his own — the same hand, disguised for twenty years, writing someone else's name.

He smiled, and it reached nothing above his mouth.

"Well then," he said softly, to the girl in the lantern-memory, to the man she had become, to the debt and the prize and the person Corvus had begged him to protect. "Let's see which of us the tide favors."

He sent for his horse.
