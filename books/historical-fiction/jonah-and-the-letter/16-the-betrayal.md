## 16. The Betrayal

The fish came up silver in the grey morning, and Tam counted them the way he counted everything — by weight, by shape, by the way light moved across a thing. The herring were small this week. Small fish meant small coins, and small coins meant Mrs. Pellow at the market house would look through him again as though the mute boy were a stray dog that had learned to carry a basket.

But today the fish did not matter. Today the map mattered.

It lay beneath the third layer of burlap in his cart, wrapped in oilcloth, folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth itself. He had not opened it. He could not read it anyway, not the letters and not most of the lines, but he knew its geography by heart now because the mapmaker had made him learn it: here the fold, here the crease, here where the paper had been mended with a sliver of paste so old it had turned the color of tea. *You put nothing on top of it heavier than the fish,* she had said, in her voice that was low for a man and steady for anyone. *And you do not stop at the bridge longer than a soldier's boredom lasts.*

A soldier's boredom. Tam thought about that as he pulled the cart down the lane from the shambles, wheels creaking, gulls screaming over him like the town had gone mad and only the birds knew why. Soldiers were bored in the mornings. They were cruel after noon, when the rum came out, and dangerous at dusk. Mornings were best. Mornings they wanted only to be somewhere warm.

The bridge at Harborwick was old stone, older than anything else in the town except the sea, and since the red coats had come it had grown teeth. A barricade of barrels and iron-edged posts narrowed the road to the width of one cart. Two soldiers stood there always, and a sergeant, and beyond them the harbor spread out grey and busy-wrong, full of naval boats where fishing boats should be, full of men with muskets where men with nets should be.

Tam joined the little queue of carts and women with baskets and a drover with two sheep that kept trying to lie down in the road. He looked at nothing. That was the trick of looking at nothing — you saw everything. The soldier on the left had new boots and was proud of them. The one on the right had a cold and hated the morning for it. The sergeant was eating an apple and had already decided to be in a bad mood about it.

*Nothing on top but the fish. Do not stop longer than a soldier's boredom.*

The queue moved. A woman argued about eggs. A soldier dumped a basket of rags onto the stones and prodded through them with his boot while the owner went white and silent, and Tam watched her hands shake as she gathered them back up, and he understood something without having words for it: that the soldiers were not really searching for anything. They were searching for the feeling of being obeyed.

Then it was his turn.

"Fish boy." The sergeant tossed the apple core into the water. "Open it."

Tam opened the cart. He did it slowly, the way you open a door you know is safe, and he began lifting the layers of burlap with both hands, showing herring, showing the ice-melt and the brine-dark wood, showing the second layer, and the soldier with the cold made a face and stepped back.

"Christ, it reeks."

"It's meant to," said the other, laughing. "Go on, boy, all of it."

All of it. Tam's hands did not tremble — hands never trembled; it was a point of pride with him, one of the few things he owned outright — but inside, where no soldier could put a boot, something curled tight. The third layer. Under it, soft as cloth, the folded country of forty names.

He lifted the burlap.

Herring. Silver and dead and stinking, a carpet of them, and beneath the herring — he had checked it eleven times in the dark before dawn — the flat stone from the fish-press, the one every cart in Harborwick carried to weigh the catch down. The oilcloth bundle lay under the stone, and the stone lay under the fish, and no hand that was not looking for it would ever lift three layers for a mute boy's cart.

The sergeant prodded the herring with his stick. Pressed down once, hard, and Tam felt the pressure travel through the fish and the stone like a hand pressing on his own chest.

"Ugly little life you've got," the sergeant said. "Go on, then."

Tam closed the cart. He nodded — he did not say thank you; he had never in his life said thank you aloud and he was not going to start with a sergeant — and led the mare forward across the bridge, and the harbor opened before him, and behind him the soldiers had already forgotten him, turning to the next cart, the next basket, the next small humiliation.

He delivered the fish. He collected the coins. And he would have gone home clean, gone home whole, gone home the invisible boy forever, if the woman at the fourth house on Cooper's Row had not been standing in her doorway watching him leave.

* * *

Gideon heard it secondhand, then third, then fifth, each telling worse than the last, which was how Harborwick digested news — passing it from mouth to mouth like a bone each gnawed a little thinner. By the time the story reached her at the workshop door, where she stood pretending to scrape salt off a windowpane, the tale had grown teeth of its own: the fish-boy stopped at the bridge, the cart searched, papers found — no, a map found — no, the boy taken in irons —

She made herself stand still until the teller, a cooper's wife with a face like a boiled ham, moved on down the lane. Then she counted to fifty. Then she walked, unhurried, a man with an errand, toward Cooper's Row, and found the fourth house, and found the woman in the doorway still enjoying herself.

"—and I said to my sister, I said, that boy's too clean for a fish-boy, did you notice? Never a word, mind, but he looks at you like he's counting your teeth—"

"Madam." Gideon touched her hat brim, the little bow of a tradesman. "I'm told a cart was searched at the bridge this morning."

"Searched and nothing found, more's the pity." The woman brightened at having an audience. "My sister saw it all. But Captain Wren himself came down within the hour — *himself*, mind, not ten days in the town and he comes running for a fish cart — and ordered the watch doubled. Doubled! There's twelve men on the bridge now and every soul going over gets turned out, man, woman, and child. And the harbor—" she dropped her voice with relish, savoring it, "—the harbor's shut. No boat leaves Harborwick, not a dinghy, not a gull, until the captain has what he says is *owed to the Crown*. Those are his very words. My sister heard him."

Gideon thanked her, bought nothing, and walked away with her heart going like a smith's hammer under her ribs.

*Too clean for a fish-boy.* A neighbor's eye, idle and hungry, and a boy who had done nothing wrong except be seen doing it well. She should have known. Twenty years she had survived by being dull, by being forgettable, by giving the world nothing to hold onto — and she had handed a child a secret and sent him through the one place in town where secrets got weighed.

By noon she had confirmed the rest. Twelve men at the bridge. A picket at the marsh road, doubled since morning, torches already stacked for the night watch. Boats at anchor in the roads with spring-loaded crews, the *Providence* among them, her topsails loosed and ready and going nowhere. The harbor master's office flew the black board that meant closed water. Dawn tide. Her dawn tide. Sealed shut like a jar.

She stood at the top of the cliff path above the marshes with the wind pulling her coat and did the arithmetic of ruin, because arithmetic was what she had instead of weeping.

The copies existed. That much was safe — the pages sewn into her coat lining, warm against her body even now, forty names riding above her heart like a second pulse. But a coat could be cut off a body in thirty seconds by a man with a knife, and after tonight there would be no cart, no boy, no marsh road, no bridge. Every door out of Harborwick now had a soldier behind it, and behind every soldier, somewhere, Aldous Wren, patient as rising water, waiting for the atlas to surface so he could take it — or waiting, some colder voice suggested, for *her* to surface, whatever it was he truly thought she was.

She had planned for checkpoints. She had planned for searches, for suspicion, for the ordinary stupidity of armies. What she had not planned for was Mrs. Cooper's-Row-sister, the thousand eyes of a frightened town all turned informer at the smell of reward. You could map tides and ground and stone. You could not map envy.

Below her the marshes lay silver-brown under the afternoon, channels braiding through the flats, and she watched them the way she had watched them since she was nine years old, reading the water. The tide would come in over the road by dark. It always came. That was the one thing in Harborwick that had not changed and would not change and did not care about captains.

The tide would come in. And tomorrow at dawn it would go out again, whether she was on the ship or in a cell or hanging from the crossbeam of her own workshop.

*Very well,* she told the marshes, and the marshes said nothing, being wiser. *Then it will not be the road, and it will not be the bridge, and it will not be the quay gates. It will have to be the water itself.*

* * *

She found Tam at dusk, in the fish-smoke alleys behind the shambles where the light didn't reach and neither did authority. He was cleaning his cart with the thoroughness of someone scrubbing away a sin, and when he saw her his shoulders did the smallest drop — relief, she realized, that she had come to him and not the soldiers.

"You did well," she said quietly. "Better than well. You gave me a day I wouldn't otherwise have had, and I'll not forget it."

He looked at his boots.

"But the road's closed now, and it's my fault as much as anyone's mouth in this town, and I want you to hear me say it: you owe me nothing further. Nothing. Go home tonight and don't walk past the bridge for a month." She pressed coins into his hand — too many, deliberately. "And if any man with a red coat asks you questions, you know nothing. You carried fish. That's all you've ever done."

Tam looked up then, and his eyes in the smoke-dark were older than his years, and he shook his head once. Firm. Then he pointed at the cart, and at her, and made a gesture — small hands folding, unfolding, the motion of a page turning.

*No,* Gideon wanted to say. *No, absolutely not, you are eleven years old and you are mute and if they take you there is no one—*

But he had already turned back to scrubbing the cart boards, jaw set, and she recognized the set of that jaw because she had worn it herself at nine years old, holding a pen in a candlelit workshop while Master Corvus said *you are the only pair of hands I trust*, and how she had glowed, God help her, how she had *glowed* to be trusted with ruin.

Corvus had made her complicit and called it teaching. Now she stood in a fish alley doing the same to a boy who couldn't even argue back, and the argument she was having with herself lasted four seconds and lost, because there was no one else, and the tide waited for no conscience.

"The north stairs," she said at last, low. "Under the customs house. Tonight, after the watch changes — midnight bell. There's a boatman called Hedgerow who owes me; you'll find him by a lantern with green glass. Tell him nothing. Carry nothing. Just be there, and be seen carrying your empty crates home the long way, past the north stair, so that anyone watching sees a boy with a cart and thinks nothing of it. Can you do that?"

Tam nodded. Then, hesitating, he touched his own chest, and pointed seaward, and raised his eyebrows — and she understood the question, and it nearly broke her: *will you be on the water? Will you get out?*

"Yes," she lied, gently, because hope was the only wage she could pay him. "Now go. Midnight. Don't look back."

He went, pulling the cart, small against the last of the light, and Gideon stood alone in the smoke-smell and let herself feel, for exactly sixty seconds, the full weight of the trap closing: the harbor sealed, the bridges thick with bayonets, Wren circling with his patient smile, Tamsin Hale in a cell for the crime of a name, and a child walking into the dark because she had asked him to. Sixty seconds. She allowed it the way she allowed herself to remember her father's face — briefly, completely, and then never again until it was safe.

Then she buttoned her coat over forty names and walked down toward the waterline to find out, by lamplight and lead-line, exactly how deep the channel ran beneath the customs house stairs — and whether a woman who had spent her life charting coastlines could steal one.

Behind her, unseen, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the shambles and followed at a distance: not a soldier, not yet. A clerk, with ink-stained fingers and something burning a hole in his pocket, looking for her before Captain Wren found her first.
