## 17. Finch's Choice

Elias Finch had not slept.

He sat at the inventory table in the workshop's back room with the lamp turned low, and before him, laid flat on the scarred oak where Master Corvus had once cut his plates, was a scrap of paper no larger than a thumbnail. He had carried it in his breast pocket for two days. He had taken it out at night in his lodgings and held it to the candle. He had put it under his pillow like a child with a stolen sweet, and had slept worse for it, and had risen each morning telling himself that tomorrow he would place it on Captain Wren's desk and be done with it.

Tomorrow kept refusing to come.

The scrap was charred at one edge, but the ink had survived well enough — ink made well always did; he had learned that much in this workshop whether he meant to or not. Three letters of a name. *Hal*. And below it, in the same cramped cartographer's hand, a number: *1*.

He knew what the number meant now. He had spent three days among these ledgers and margins, and he knew that Corvus had numbered his ghosts, and that the first ghost was named Hale, and that Tamsin Hale had been marched out of her own kitchen at dawn with her hands tied in front of her like a common cutpurse while her neighbors watched through their shutters and pretended to be chimneys.

Finch had watched too. He had stood in the column of soldiers with his ledger under his arm and his stomach turning over itself, and he had thought: *I could stop this. One word. One scrap of paper.*

And then he had thought of the flogging he'd witnessed on the *Resolute*'s deck a week ago — a boy of seventeen, half the army's age, beaten for a slow count of stores — and of the woman in the next parish whose house had been burned on suspicion alone, the fire reported to him secondhand in a clerk's flat voice as though it were a shipment of oats. He had thought of what Wren would do with a scrap of paper that proved forty families guilty, and what Wren would do to the man who withheld it.

Both thoughts had kept him awake. Both, together, had made him what he now was: a man sitting in an enemy's workshop at midnight, waiting for the enemy to come home.

He heard her before he saw her. The side door — she never used the front; the front door had been splintered by soldiers and stood leaning against its own frame like a drunkard — the side door opened on its careful hinge, and Gideon Montrose came into the room and stopped as if she had walked into glass.

She was dressed as a man, as always: the coat, the breeches, the surveyor's satchel. But in the lamplight, with her guard down for the half-second before it came up, Finch saw past the costume to the exhaustion beneath it. She looked, he thought, like a map drawn on paper too thin — every line showing through to the other side.

"Finch." Her hand had moved toward her coat — toward whatever she kept sewn there, though he didn't know that yet, wouldn't let himself know it — and then stilled. "You've no business here at this hour."

"No," he agreed. "I don't."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Neither of them sat. Somewhere outside, a sentry called the half-hour in a bored voice, and the sound seemed to remind them both that the town was full of men with muskets and that this room was very small.

"You've been going through my grate," she said at last. Not a question. Her voice was level, but Finch had spent three days learning to read the small tremors in other people's composure, because his life among soldiers depended on reading them, and he saw this one plainly: she was measuring the distance to the door.

"I have." He turned the scrap on the table so it faced her. "Burned paper travels badly. It smears. You'll want to press what remains between boards before the char flakes further."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, slowly, she crossed the room — not toward the door, he noticed; toward the table — and picked up the scrap between two fingers, the way one handles something both precious and diseased. She read it. He watched her read it, watched her face do the terrible arithmetic: *Hale. Number one. He knows.*

"If you've brought this to trade," she said quietly, "you should know I've nothing worth having."

"That's not why—"

"Then you've brought it to hang me, and we're wasting each other's time." She set the scrap down and squared her shoulders, and Finch understood suddenly that she was preparing to fight him — this soft-bellied clerk with ink-stained cuffs — with whatever she had, which might be nothing but nerve. It was, he reflected, exactly what Corvus must have been like at the end. The same stillness before the blade.

"I'm not hanging anyone," Finch said. And then, because it was true and because truth was the only coin he had left: "I should have. It would have been simpler. Wren would have promoted me. I'd have gotten out of Harborwick before winter." He laughed, short and ugly. "I came here to tell you instead that you're going to die if you stay, and that I am apparently the sort of coward who can't even do villainy properly."

* * *

Gideon did not trust him. That was the first thing, and she held onto it the way a drowning man holds a spar that may be pulling him under.

Twenty years of keeping herself alive had taught her that kindness arriving at midnight, uninvited, from the enemy's own table, was most often bait. Corvus had said it plainly once, sharpening a burin by candlelight: *When the wolf brings you a lamb, girl, count the wolf's teeth before you thank him.* But Corvus was twenty years in his grave and the wolf's clerk was standing in front of her with his hat wrung in his hands like a schoolboy who had broken a window, and the scrap of burned margin-paper lay between them with *Hal* facing the ceiling, and somewhere across the town Tamsin Hale sat in a cell because of that paper's brothers.

"Tell me," Gideon said, "why I shouldn't call the sentry. Tell me why a man of the requisition office sits alone in my shop at midnight with a piece of evidence he's concealed for two days, unless he means to use it."

"Because I've seen what they do," Finch said, "and I find I can't help them do it anymore."

He said it simply, without performance, and that more than anything unsettled her. She knew men who performed conscience — she had watched smugglers weep piously on Sunday and run cargo on Tuesday. This man's voice had the flatness of someone reporting a measurement. He had weighed himself and found the answer wanting.

"Wren means to search every soul at the quay," he went on, and now his words came faster, the dammed-up thing released. "Every soul, every parcel, every crate. Not just the outgoing traffic — he's ordered the quay closed from four o'clock tomorrow, cordoned, and the search conducted in ranks. Nobody boards without emptying their pockets and being handled. Hands, ribs, boot-heels, coat seams." His eyes flicked, once, involuntary, to her left side — to the lining, Gideon realized, or rather not to any lining he knew of, but to the idea of linings, the whole species of them. "They're looking for the atlas. They'll take anything that looks like evidence and ask questions after. There's a woman already in custody who will confirm whatever they want confirmed by Thursday, and when they have their proof, Wren won't need the original at all. He'll need only you."

"And you're telling me this out of the goodness of your heart."

"I'm telling you," Finch said, with a small, bitter precision, "because I inventoried this workshop. Because I catalogued a dead man's life into columns and found that the dead man had spent his whole fortune, near enough, on other people's escape — passage fees, bribes entered as 'freight,' 'canvas repair,' 'pilotage' — forty lines of it, year after year. I know what this atlas is, Montrose. I've known since the second night. And I sat with that knowledge in my pocket—" he touched his breast, where the scrap had lain, "—and discovered that I am not built for the work they've given me. Some men aren't. It took me thirty-one years to find out, which I grant you is slow."

Gideon looked at him for a long time. Outside, the tide was turning; she could hear it in the harbor's changed voice, the water dragging at the pier pilings, and some cold practical part of her mind noted it automatically — *low water at half past three, the marsh road passable from two until five* — while the rest of her stood in her master's workshop listening to an army clerk confess to cowardice in the shape of mercy.

"Why come to me?" she asked. "Why not simply lose the scrap? Let it fall in the grate. Walk away clean."

"Because walking away clean isn't enough anymore." Finch drew a breath. "I tried that arithmetic. Scrap lost, conscience quiet, no harm done by my hand directly. But the names are still in the margins, and Wren is still hunting, and the only person who can carry those names out of Harborwick is standing in front of me about to walk into a cordon that will strip-search her at the gangplank." He met her eyes — the first man in twenty years, she realized, who had looked at her steadily without either knowing or not-knowing, but simply seeing a person in trouble. "I don't hold with what you are, Montrose, or what's hidden in those margins. I hold with the fact that forty families shouldn't hang because a captain wants a decoration. That's the whole of my politics. It isn't much. It's what I have."

* * *

It was then, Gideon thought later — in the hours when she would replay this night the way one probes a healed wound — that Finch reached into his coat.

Her whole body tightened. Twenty years of instinct said: *weapon*, and her hand was already moving toward the knife in her boot before her mind caught up and understood that what he drew out was a folded paper, creased and soft with handling, and that he was not pointing it at her but laying it on the table beside the burned scrap, and smoothing it flat with one ink-stained thumb.

A travel pass. Army issue. The seal unbroken. *Permit the bearer, Elias Finch, clerk of requisitions, to pass all checkpoints and embark upon any vessel departing His Majesty's port of Harborwick, by order of Capt. A. Wren.*

His name. His description. His rank.

"Finch," she said carefully, "do you know what this is?"

"It's mine." He was looking at the wall behind her, at the racks of engraving tools, at the half-finished plate of the northern coast that still sat in its press where she had left it — where she had been working, she understood now, on the last night of her old life. "Wren signed it for me a month ago, when he thought I'd be shipping records back to the colonial office. I won't be needing it. A man who stays in Harborwick after tonight is either going to be hanged as your accomplice or promoted as Wren's creature, and I find I want neither."

"You'd be deserting."

"I'd be spending myself," Finch said. "There's a difference, though it's small and I couldn't explain it to a court-martial." He pushed the pass another inch across the table, and his hand was shaking — not much, but honestly, the way a man's hand shakes when he has finally stopped pretending. "The pass gets you past the cordon. Your face doesn't match the description — no description would, would it — but seals open gates faster than faces. Tell them you're carrying documents for the requisitions office. You're good at documents. God knows you should be; you've forged a whole man out of nothing for twenty years."

The words landed harder than he could know. Gideon kept her face still — she had long practice at stillness — but inside, something old and buried turned over. *Forged a whole man.* Said casually, almost admiringly, by this soft clerk who perhaps knew more than she'd credited, or perhaps knew nothing at all and had simply stumbled against the truth with his eyes shut. Either way, he was handing her the one thing she could not make herself: legitimacy, stamped in wax.

"Why?" she asked. It was the question underneath all the others. "Truly. No man spends himself for strangers."

Finch was quiet a moment. When he answered, he did not look at her.

"My father was a cooper in Rye," he said. "Small man, honest, dull as ditchwater, and I loved him. When I was nine, a fugitive hid in our yard three days — a woman, running from debt or worse, I never learned which. My father fed her and asked her nothing and rowed her out to a collier himself, at night, in a leaky boat, though he was terrified of open water. I asked him why afterward, why risk everything for someone we didn't know, and he said—" Finch's mouth worked. "He said, *the day will come when you're the one in the yard, lad, and what happens then depends on who's watching from the window.*" He straightened. "I've been the man at the window for three days, Montrose. I'm tired of watching."

The lamp burned steady between them. Gideon found, to her surprise, that her eyes were hot, and that frightened her more than the cordon, more than Wren — this sudden treacherous softness, this feeling of a hand catching her when she had planned to fall alone. She had trusted no one wholly since Corvus died. Trust was a door, and doors admitted wolves. But this man was no wolf. He was something rarer and more useless and more precious: a small honest man doing a large dishonest thing for the right reasons, and paying for it with his own neck.

"The marsh road," she heard herself say. "Tomorrow night, low water. If anything goes wrong at the quay — if they take me — there's a fish-cart at the second marker post, and under the false bottom—" She stopped herself. "No. Forget I said it. The fewer who know—"

"—the fewer who hang," Finch finished. "I know how it works. I've spent three weeks in the business." He allowed himself the ghost of a smile, the first she'd ever seen on him. "I'll not take your secrets, Montrose. Just give me one instruction and I'll follow it like a soldier, which is ironic, but there it is."

Gideon looked at the pass. At his name written where hers should go — where no name of hers had gone in twenty years. Tomorrow, if she lived, she would sign herself onto that ship with another man's identity, wearing another man's permission, and it struck her with sudden force that her whole life had been borrowed papers, borrowed names, a self constructed out of other people's blanks filled in.

"When it's done," she said, "get out of Harborwick however you can. Don't wait for me. Don't look back at the quay. Whatever happens at the gangplank, you walk away and you keep walking, and you never write to me, and you never speak my name."

"I don't know your name," Finch said mildly. "Only your master's."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Then he took up his hat, settled his coat, and became once more the gray little clerk she had first taken him for — except that she would never take him for that again, and they both knew it.

"One more thing," he said at the door. "Wren's search begins at four o'clock. He'll start at the far end of the quay and work toward the ship — pride, you see; he likes to inspect his own net as it closes. Which means the last soul searched stands nearest the gangplank, and the search takes better than an hour to reach her." He paused, hand on the latch. "Be the last soul on the quay, Montrose. Arrive late. Arrive calm. And whatever he says to you — and he will say things, the man can't help himself — remember that a man who talks is a man who hasn't yet decided."

The latch clicked. The side door closed. Gideon stood alone in her master's workshop with a dead man's travel pass in her hand and the tide turning in the dark below, and through the window she could see, far off at the harbor mouth, riding lights swinging at anchor: the ship that would sail on the morning tide, with or without her.

Four o'clock. She had eleven hours, one pass, one page of copied names sewn against her ribs, and one stranger's name to wear out of her own life.

Somewhere across the sleeping town, a bell began to ring — and stopped, cut short, as though a hand had been clapped over its mouth.
