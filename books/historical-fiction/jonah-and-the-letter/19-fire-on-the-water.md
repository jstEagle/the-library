## 19. Fire on the Water

The *Meridian's Grace* smelled of tar, salt pork, and the particular staleness of a hold that had carried too many seasons of cargo without ever once being properly aired. Gideon stood at the rail and let the wind have her face — the first honest thing to touch her in three days.

Harborwick shrank behind them, or rather, it did not shrink, for the tide was slow and the ship was slower still, warping out past the mole with the patience of something that knew it had all morning. The town sat where it had always sat: the gray stone of the countinghouse, the red tile roofs gone brown with dawn shadow, the church spire stitching sky to earth. From this distance it looked harmless. From this distance she could almost pretend it had always been a place she merely visited.

Her coat hung heavy on her shoulders. Against her ribs, sewn flat between lining and wool, forty names rode in ink and candle-smoke, each one copied twice over in her smallest hand. She had not slept. She did not intend to sleep yet. Sleep would mean lying down, and lying down would mean stillness, and stillness would give her body permission to begin shaking, and she was not ready to shake. Not here. Not within sight of the quay.

A sailor passed behind her with a coil of rope over his shoulder, gave her the brief nod men give a surveyor — she was still dressed as one, Finch's pass folded in her breast pocket like a second heart — and moved on. Nobody looked twice at her. That was the whole architecture of her life, built beam by beam across twenty years: nobody looking twice. She had spent the night learning what it cost when somebody finally did.

*He looked at me,* she thought, for perhaps the hundredth time since the quay. *Captain Aldous Wren looked straight at me, at arm's length, with his men turning out pockets ten feet behind him — and he let me walk.*

She turned it over the way she'd turn an unfamiliar chart, checking its bearings, looking for the flaw in the coastline. There was no kindness in Wren's face. She had catalogued that face carefully during their two meetings: the army neatness of him, the way his eyes moved over a room taking inventory of exits and persons and debts owed. Men like Wren did not extend mercy. They extended credit. And credit came due with interest.

So either he had not recognized her — which meant she was free and would remain so only until he did — or he had recognized her and wanted something. Both possibilities were unbearable. She settled for watching the water instead.

The harbor light slid past to larboard. The channel narrowed between the shingle bar and the old powder buoy, and the helmsman — a Cornishman with forearms like hawsers — brought the ship round with small unhurried corrections, and then Harborwick was behind the headland and gone, and there was nothing in the world but gray-green water and a white line of gulls and the enormous indifference of morning.

Gideon discovered she was gripping the rail hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and made herself let go, finger by finger.

*You are alive,* she told herself, in the voice Master Corvus had used for correcting her pen-work. *The names are off the paper and into wool. Forty families will wake tomorrow and be nobody again. This is what winning looks like. It looks like nothing.*

* * *

The captain's cabin was offered for the crossing because the *Grace* carried no passengers of rank and Captain Merrow found the company of a mapmaker less tedious than most. Gideon accepted a cup of small beer she didn't want and sat across the scarred table from him while he worked through his papers, and it was there, somewhere off the Warner shoals, that the news caught up with her.

A pilot boat came alongside just after noon — navy launch, flying requisition colors — and handed up a packet for ships clearing Harborwick. Merrow read it, grunted, and read it again.

"Trouble?" Gideon asked, keeping her voice at the bored register of a tradesman inquiring about customs delays.

"Order from the port commandant." He tossed the paper onto the table. "Requisitioned materials deemed compromised are to be destroyed rather than forwarded. Something about securing the record." His mouth twisted. "Which is army language for *someone's about to be asked questions they'd rather not answer*."

Gideon picked up the notice. Her hands were steady. Twenty years of steadiness, called upon once more.

It was signed *A. Wren, Capt., Port Commandant.* Beneath the boilerplate, in the clerk's hand, a summary: seized documents from the Montrose workshop, being a marine atlas and associated papers, assessed as evidence and **ordered burned at the quay this day**, the destruction witnessed by two officers, no further inquiry required.

No further inquiry required.

She read that phrase four times. It was the sound of a door being locked from the inside. Wren had taken the atlas at midnight, held the proof of forty crimes in his hands for less than a day, and now he was going to burn it — not because the army wanted it destroyed, but because *he* did. Because an intact atlas reaching the colonial office would invite questions about how long it had sat unsecured in a requisitioned workshop. About why the harbor had been closed too late. About what else Captain Wren might have overlooked, or been paid to overlook, or owed from twenty years ago when he and Corvus Dunmore had worn the same uniform.

"He had it," Gideon said quietly. "The atlas. They found it."

"Seems so." Merrow sharpened a pencil with a knife, not looking up. "Commandant's having a bad week, if the gossip aboard the pilot boat means anything. Lost a prisoner for lack of evidence, lost his cargo manifest, now burning his own seizure. Man's covering himself like a hedghog." He glanced up. "Why? Friend of yours, this Montrose fellow?"

"I knew his work," Gideon said. "Every coast he ever drew."

* * *

They burned it at four in the afternoon, and she watched.

The ship had warped around to take on fresh water from a lighter, and so the *Grace* lay at anchor barely half a mile off the quay — close enough to see faces on the pier, close enough to smell the low-tide reek of the town she'd given her life to escaping. Gideon stood at the stern rail with a spyglass she'd borrowed from the captain, and she told herself she was watching for pursuit, and she believed herself about half the time.

Two soldiers carried the atlas out onto the quay between them, and even at that distance she knew it by the way they handled it — gingerly, resentfully, like men carrying something that had already judged them. A third soldier brought the brazier. An officer stood apart with his hands clasped behind him, and through the glass she found his face, and it was exactly the face she expected: composed, correct, watchful.

Wren did not look at the fire. He looked at the harbor. At the ships at anchor. At the *Meridian's Grace*, riding half a mile out with her surveyor standing at the stern rail — and Gideon lowered the glass a fraction, absurdly, as though he could see her eyes through brass and half a mile of evening air.

They fed the atlas to the brazier page by page. That was regulation, presumably; burning a book whole leaves whole pages half-consumed, legible, dangerous. So they destroyed it the way the army destroyed things: thoroughly, methodically, in front of witnesses. She thought of the North Sea plate she'd finished three nights ago by candlelight, the last coastline, the ink still tacky when she'd sanded it. She thought of the margins crowded with small careful names, forty of them, written in Corvus Dunmore's hand across fifteen years of quiet treason. She thought of the fire taking the vellum first, curling it like a living thing flinching, then going black, then going to flakes of ash that the sea breeze took out over the water — some of which, she understood with a strange clarity, were drifting toward her, toward the ship, toward the rail she leaned on.

She did not cry. She had expected to, and the not-crying felt like a kind of theft.

*Bitter*, she thought. *This is bitterness, then. I kept waiting for grief, and grief hasn't come. Only this green taste, like biting the pith of something.*

Because here was the truth of it, laid out on the water as plainly as soundings on a chart: the atlas was not destroyed by the men who feared it. It was destroyed by the man who *needed* it gone. Every name in those margins was now freer than it had been in fifteen years, freed not by courage but by corruption — freed because Aldous Wren's survival depended on the evidence of his own failure never existing. He had burned forty families' danger away to save his own skin, and it had worked, and it was the same act as mercy, indistinguishable from mercy, and Gideon stood at the rail with the spyglass hanging forgotten against her chest and hated him for it with a purity that frightened her.

If she had stayed. If she had fought. If she had stood on that quay with a pistol and made a speech — none of it would have accomplished what his cowardice had just accomplished in twenty minutes.

Survival, she was learning, was not a heroic arithmetic. It was the sum of everyone's small self-interests aligning for a moment, like stars that meant nothing, pointing nowhere, and then drifting apart again. Finch's conscience. Tam's silence. Tamsin Hale's stubborn widow's grief. Merrow's mild greed for passenger fares. Wren's rot. All of it had conspired, without conspiracy, to carry her and her sewn coat past three checkpoints and out to open water. She owed her life to a committee of accidents and appetites, and not one member of that committee had lifted a finger for her sake.

Except Finch. She corrected herself. Perhaps Finch.

And except Corvus, who had arranged the only part he could arrange: the training. The steady hand. The lesson she was only now, watching smoke come off the water, beginning to understand. *A map is a promise you make to strangers,* he used to say. *Make it true, and it will carry people safely through country you'll never see.* She had thought he meant coasts and channels. He had meant everything.

* * *

Evening came down soft. The lighter finished and pulled away; the anchor came up groaning; the *Grace* leaned her shoulder into the ebb tide and began, at last, properly, to leave. Harborwick became a smudge, then a suggestion, then a few lights. The burn-smell lingered longest.

Gideon stayed at the rail past dark. Her body had finally begun to shake, the great delayed tremor working up from her knees, and she let it, alone in the dark where no one could see the surveyor come apart. She shook for the atlas. She shook for Tamsin Hale in a cell somewhere in the town going dark below the horizon. She shook for the boy Tam, mute and complicit, carrying fish and secrets in the same cart. She shook hardest, maybe, for the girl she had buried in 1727 under a man's name — the girl whose name was in none of the margins, protected by no one, smuggled out of the country inside her own body.

When the shaking stopped she felt scoured. Empty in the way a chart is empty before the first line goes down.

"You'll want to log your position eventually." Merrow's voice beside her, unhurried. He had come up quietly or let her hear him coming; with sailors it was impossible to tell. He set something on the caprail: her borrowed spyglass, and beside it a stub of pencil and the back of a spare sailing card. "Man who loses his instruments in a fire should start fresh. Where are you bound?"

"Somewhere with no name I know," Gideon said. "I'll know it when I see the coastline."

"Hm." He studied the dark water. "That officer. Wren. You watched him a long time."

"Professional interest."

"Was he watching you?"

The question hung in the rigging. Gideon thought of Wren's face through the glass, turned toward the anchored ships while his evidence burned. Thought of *no further inquiry required* — and of how men like Wren closed doors behind themselves and counted locks, and how a woman pretending to be a man, walking free with his knowledge or without it, was worth more to such a man loose than caught. Leverage keeps. Justice doesn't.

"No," she said. "He was watching the fire."

Merrow nodded as though that settled something, and went forward to his anchors and his ledgers.

Gideon remained at the stern. Behind her, Harborwick had become indistinguishable from the night. Ahead, the black water ran in long slow swells toward a coast she had never charted, and would chart, honestly, plainly, with no ghosts in the margins — because the ghosts were all behind her now, every one of them: dead, released, arrested, corrupted, or waved through a checkpoint by a look she didn't yet understand.

She took out the pencil and the sailing card. By feel more than sight, in the dark, she wrote the ship's position and the date. Then, beneath it, because the shaking had left her hollow and hollow places echo, she wrote a single line of practice — the way she had practiced another man's signature for twenty years:

*A. Wren, Port Commandant. No further inquiry required.*

She looked at it a long moment. Then she snapped the card in half and dropped both halves over the rail and watched the dark water take them.

There was one name left unwritten in the world. One signature never once set down in twenty years, waiting at the bottom of a blank title page somewhere ahead of her, in a port with no name she knew.

Tomorrow, she thought. Or the day after. When the coastline came into view.

She went below, and slept, and did not dream of fire.
