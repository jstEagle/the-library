## 20. The Morning Tide

The harbor let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long — slowly, grudgingly, leaving a mark.

Gideon stood at the stern rail and watched Harborwick shrink. The town sat low against the grey water, its rooftops dark with morning rain, its chimneys bleeding thin smoke into a sky the color of old pewter. From this distance she could not see the soldiers on the quay. She could not see the requisition notices still nailed to the posts, paper gone soft and sagging in the damp. She could not see the countinghouse with its new flag, or the bridge checkpoint with its upturned cart and its bored sentries. Distance did what distance always did: it made occupation look like ordinariness, made a strangled town look like any other town waking to any other day.

The ship rolled beneath her feet in the long swell beyond the mole, and she gripped the rail and let it move her. Twenty years of maps had taught her the grammar of coastlines — every headland a sentence, every bay a pause — but she had never once read one from the moving side of the water. Harborwick from the land was a place she had drawn; Harborwick from the sea was a place she was leaving. The difference between those two things was the width of her whole life.

*You are free*, she told herself, and the words sat strangely, like a coat cut for someone else's shoulders.

The tide was with them. It had been with them since before dawn, lifting the ship off the harbor bed where it had leaned all night, turning it, patient as anything Corvus had ever taught her, toward the open water. She thought of his voice — *the tide forgives nothing and forgets nothing, girl; learn it early and it will carry you* — and she thought that he had been wrong about at least one thing. The tide forgot plenty. It was people who remembered.

"Landfall in four days if the wind holds." The captain's voice came from behind her, and she turned to find him studying her with the frank appraisal of a man who had already decided not to ask questions he would regret hearing answered. He was a Leeward man, weathered to the color of his own deck, and he had accepted Finch's pass without reading it twice, which told Gideon either that he was honest or that he was very well paid to be. "There's a bunk below if you want it. You look like you haven't slept."

"I haven't."

"You will," he said, "or you'll fall down, and either way the ship doesn't care." He touched his cap and moved off along the rail, and Gideon understood that this was the last conversation anyone on this vessel would initiate with her for four days. That was a mercy. Strangers who knew nothing were a kind of country, and she intended to immigrate.

She turned back to the rail. The harbor mouth was passing now, the black rocks of the mole head sliding by to starboard, and beyond them the open sea unrolled — grey-green, flecked white, enormous. Her breath caught at it, an old reflex she hadn't felt since childhood. The sea had no margins. There was nowhere on all that water to write a name.

She was still looking at it when the pier came into view around the last shoulder of the mole — the long wooden arm where the fishing fleet clustered, where Tam's cart had stood a hundred mornings, where the fish-wives shouted and the gulls wheeled in their dirty spirals. The ship drew past it at half a cable's distance, close enough that Gideon could see individual faces on the stones, could see the soldiers there in their red lines, could see—

Her heart stopped and then started again, hard, against her ribs.

Tam.

The boy stood at the very end of the pier, at the spot where the wood gave way to water, small and straight-backed in his oilskin coat. He should not have been there. The watch had been doubled; Wren had closed the harbor; a mute fish-boy loitering at the quay's edge three days after being stopped at the bridge with a cart full of mackerel and nothing else was either a fool or a signal. But there he stood, and as the ship slid past, he raised his hand — not waving, exactly, not at first. Holding it up. Palm out. The way a person stands when they are keeping a promise they cannot speak.

Then, as the gap widened, he waved. A child's wave, quick and whole-armed, and even at that distance Gideon saw him smile, and something broke loose in her chest and went to pieces.

She lifted her own hand. She held it there until the pier was a smudge, until the boy was a speck, until the whole town was a low dark line dissolving into rain, and her arm ached, and her eyes stung with more than the spray. He didn't know what he'd carried. She had made sure of that. Pages sewn into a marsh map, forty names passed through small hands like so much fish-paper — he knew only that the quiet mapwoman had needed him, and that she had paid him honestly, and that she had looked at him once with an expression he had probably spent the days since trying to name.

He would name it someday, she thought. Someday, in some tavern, some old woman would tell the story of the forty families, and Tam would go still over his cup, and he would understand what his hands had done. And Gideon prayed — she who had not prayed in twenty years — that the understanding would sit easy on him, that it would feel like a medal and not a scar.

Because it would be a scar. She knew the weight of it. She carried the twin of it herself, twenty years heavy: the memory of being eleven years old and holding a lamp for Master Corvus while he burned papers, knowing without being told that the burning mattered, that she was now inside the secret, that there was no outside anymore, ever again. *I have made him what I was*, she thought, watching the water slide past below. *Corvus made me complicit and called it an apprenticeship. I made Tam complicit and called it wages.* The coin changed hands but the transaction was the same. Children were how secrets survived. Children were the vessels the old men poured themselves into when they felt death coming.

And yet.

And yet the boy had waved.

* * *

They let Tamsin Hale go at nine o'clock that same morning. Gideon learned this two days later, in the middle of the Atlantic, from a sailor who had taken a pilot boat out of Harborwick on the evening tide with a bundle of letters, and who was willing to talk for the price of an audience.

"There was a widow woman," the sailor said, leaning on the rail beside Gideon, working a wad of tobacco as though it owed him money. "Took her up three days back. Name of Hale. They said she was head of the whole smuggling ring, the army did. Had the whole parish in her pocket." He spat over the side, ceremonially. "Let her go Thursday morning. No evidence, they said."

"No evidence," Gideon repeated.

"Not a scrap. And there's them saying—" he lowered his voice with the relish of a man lowering a bucket into a well, "—there's them saying the evidence *was* found, right enough. Atlas and all, every name writ fair in the margins, just like the informer said. But Captain Wren ordered the whole thing burned. In the courtyard of the countinghouse, in front of witnesses. Said it was contaminated. Said no court could touch it now." The sailor shook his head with something that was almost admiration. "Burned forty families' worth of hanging evidence on account of it got wet, or some such. Strange kind of soldiering, if you ask me."

"It isn't strange," Gideon said quietly. "It's arithmetic."

The sailor looked at her sideways, decided she was mad, and wandered off. But she stayed at the rail a long while after, working through the sum herself, because she had promised herself she would understand it fully before she allowed herself to feel it — and because feeling it was the thing she most wanted and most dreaded to do.

Wren had burned the atlas.

Not lost it. Not mislaid it through the chaos of a search conducted in haste. Burned it, deliberately, publicly, with ceremony — and in doing so he had done everything Gideon had crossed checkpoints and sewn pages into her coat lining and put a child at risk to do. He had freed the forty names. He had destroyed the evidence that would hang Tamsin Hale and thirty-nine others. Every purpose of her three desperate days, accomplished by her enemy's own hand, in her enemy's own courtyard.

Unless she understood why.

And she did understand why. She had understood it since the quay, turning it over during the first sleepless night in her bunk, during the second, worrying it the way her tongue worried a cracked tooth. Wren had searched every soul at the water's edge. He had been close enough to touch her — had looked directly into her face, into eyes he must by then have known belonged to Corvus's daughter, the daughter no one spoke of, the apprentice who had lived twenty years as a man — and he had let her walk onto that ship.

A man like Wren did not show mercy. She had met his kind in Corvus's stories: the soldiers who came back from the wars with their honor left on some foreign field and their appetites intact. What Wren had shown her was not mercy. It was bookkeeping.

If he exposed her — if he arrested the woman masquerading as a man, the forger of identities, the heir to a smuggling network — then everything she was became testimony. And testimony traveled upward. There would be an inquiry. There would be questions about how such a fraud had operated for twenty years in a port under army administration, questions about who had signed the requisitions, who had overseen the inventory, who had let a known associate of Corvus's slip through a sealed harbor on a forged — no, worse, a *borrowed* — travel pass. Questions about the atlas, and about why the atlas had burned. Questions, reaching back decades, into whatever debts and divisions of spoils had passed between Captain Aldous Wren and Master Corvus in the army years — the debts Wren had hinted at with his velvet threats, the history he kept in Corvus's old letters like a man keeping knives in a drawer.

Exposing her would have exposed himself. So he burned the atlas to bury his failure, and he let her sail to bury his past, and he would file his report about the smugglers of Harborwick with language vague enough to survive any future audit, and he would go on rising in the army's ranks on the strength of a requisition that had found, regrettably, nothing.

She had escaped Harborwick on the tide of another man's corruption. That was the truth of it, stripped bare. Not courage. Not craft — or not craft alone. Finch's grace had opened the quay, yes, but Wren's rot had kept it open. She owed her life to a good man's weakness and a bad man's self-interest, braided together, and there was no unpicking the braid. If she cursed Wren she cursed the thing that saved her. If she thanked him she gagged on it.

*Power corrupted even your rescue*, she thought, to the grey water, to the whole indifferent ocean. *You are alive because a rotten man protected his rot.*

And beneath that thought, quieter and more dangerous, the question she could not stop asking: *what did Corvus do, that Wren had so much to hide?* She had built a saint out of the old man's bones. Twenty years of grief will do that; the dead improve in storage. But the letters on Wren's table had been real, and the fear in Wren's eyes when he spoke of old debts had been realer. Whatever her master had been before he was a mapmaker — whatever he had done in the king's coat that gave a captain leverage over him and gave a captain terror of exposure — it was part of her inheritance too. She carried it the way she carried everything else he left her: silently, in the lining.

* * *

On the third night the wind backed around and freshened, and the ship began to move properly at last — the long rhythmic plunge of a vessel finally answering the sea — and Gideon, who had not slept in she could not remember how long, went below and slept like ballast.

She dreamed of the workshop. In the dream it was whole, unransacked, candlelit; the plates of the atlas lay drying in their racks, forty coastlines in fine brown ink, and Corvus stood at the bench with his sleeves rolled, inking a shore she did not recognize, and he said, without looking up, in the tone he used for the most ordinary instruction: *the margin, girl. Mind the margin. That's where the true country lives.*

She woke with salt light in the porthole and the dream still on her like wet clothes, and she lay in the narrow bunk and did the accounting she had been avoiding since the pier.

What had it cost? The workshop — gone, torn apart, and even if she could return, the returning would be watched forever. The original atlas — ash in a courtyard, her master's last work destroyed by the corruption it had been meant to outlast. Tamsin Hale — three days in an army cell for a crime of which the whole town was guilty and only the brave were accused. Tam — a debt of complicity she could never repay and never explain. Finch — his career, perhaps his liberty, spent on a stranger's escape like a coin flipped into dark water. And herself — twenty years of a borrowed name, and no way back to the one she'd buried, and no certainty she would recognize her own face if she found it again.

Against that column, the other: forty families breathing free in their beds because a page of chart offcuts reached a ship's hold and a copy would reach a kitchen wall. Tamsin Hale walking out of custody into the morning because the proof against her had gone up in her enemy's smoke. A boy on a pier, waving.

It did not balance. Nothing balanced. Survival never balanced; that was the lesson Harborwick had taught her above all others. You did not come through a thing like this clean or even square. You came through lopsided, weighted with what others had paid, and you carried the imbalance the rest of your life, and you tried — this was the whole of morality as far as she could now see it — you tried to spend it well.

She got up, unsteadily, and dressed, and went up on deck into the morning.

The sea had changed in the night. It ran deep blue now under a sky scoured clean, and the ship walked over it with a confidence Gideon envied. Ahead, faint on the horizon, a coastline was assembling itself out of haze — not Harborwick, not home, not anywhere she knew. Just land. New land. A line she had never drawn.

She stood at the bow and watched it come, and took out the single copied page she had kept apart from the ones hidden in the marsh map — the master page, the one written in her own careful hand, folded once against her heart where it had ridden through three checkpoints and a search and a burning. Forty names. She did not need to unfold it. She knew them the way she knew tides.

Behind her, far behind, past the curve of the world, a widow was walking out of a cell into the light, and a boy was mending nets on a pier, and a clerk was staring at a scrap of burned paper in his pocket and deciding, perhaps, what kind of man he would be. The ship carried her away from all of them, and toward none of them, and the strangeness of that — of being saved by people she was leaving — settled over her like the salt on her coat.

The coastline ahead sharpened. She could make out a headland now, and the pale thread of surf beneath it, and a harbor mouth opening like a held breath.

She had no charts of it. Nobody did, yet.

That, at least, was hers to change.
