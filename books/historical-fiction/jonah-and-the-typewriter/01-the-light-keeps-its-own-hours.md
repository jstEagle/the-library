## 1. The Light Keeps Its Own Hours

The light did not care that it was Hazel Montrose's birthday. It never had cared for calendars, or grief, or the small ceremonies by which people on shore persuaded themselves their lives had shape. It cared only for its hours — lit at dusk, trimmed through the dark, extinguished at dawn — and it had taught her, in three years of widowhood, to keep its hours as her own.

She climbed the ninety-seven steps at half past four in the afternoon, because the sky had told her to. The barometer in the kitchen — Edmund's barometer, its brass case polished so often by his thumb that hers had worn the shine deeper — had been falling all day, and falling fast, the needle sagging like a tired shoulder. By noon the sea had turned the color of pewter with a green bruise beneath it. By two the gulls had gone inland, all at once, the way they did, abandoning the headland as a crew abandons a ship they know better than she does. And now, standing in the lantern room with the mullioned glass rattling in its frames, she watched the western horizon swallow itself whole and understood: this would be no ordinary dark.

"Right," she said aloud, to nobody, which was how she said most things. "Right then."

History, she had long ago decided, happened elsewhere. It happened in London, where men in good coats stood up in the Commons and made the world over with their voices. It happened in Berlin, in Rome, in Madrid — places names she read in the newspaper that arrived, when it arrived, ten days damp and late on the supply boat. It happened to ministers and admirals and to other women's husbands. It did not happen here, on this black fist of granite thrust out from Cornwall's knuckle into the Atlantic, where the only events were weather and the only politics were the tides. Her rock stood outside time. That was what she had loved about it, once. That was what she told herself still, though the telling had begun to wear thin as an old coat brushed too often at the same elbow.

She hauled the oil can up the last flight, shoulders burning, and set about preparing the lamp.

* * *

Edmund had taught her the mechanism before he'd taught her anything else about himself, including how to love him, which he had done clumsily and mostly through instruction. *Watch the wick, Hazel. Feed it steady or it'll gutter and sulk.* He had stood where she stood now, his big hands sure among the brass fittings, and she had thought — God help her, she had actually thought — *we will do this forever, the two of us against the sea, and it will be enough.*

The lamp took the flame. She adjusted the wick until the light burned white and even, and the great lens above her caught it, gathered it, and threw it out — one flash every seven and a half seconds, out across the water, out to wherever the ships were. She liked to imagine the flashes arriving. Some helmsman in the rain, some tired second mate lifting his head: *There. There's the light. We're alright.* Nobody on those decks would ever know her name. That was the bargain of the work, and she had made her peace with it, or believed she had.

By six the storm arrived properly, announcing itself not with wind first but with a sound like a door slamming somewhere under the island, deep in the rock, and then the wind came indeed — came off the sea in long screaming volleys that leaned against the tower and made the whole structure hum like a struck bell. Rain went horizontal. The sea, when she dared the gallery between gusts, was not breaking but detonating, forty feet of gray water collapsing against the rocks below with impacts she felt through her boot soles.

She went down for soup and came back up with it cold, because there was no point heating anything she couldn't guarantee to eat while watching the clock. The clock mattered. Everything here was clocks: windings and rotations and the metronome tick of the mechanism that turned the lens, night after night, whether she slept or not. She wound it. She checked the wick. She wiped salt spray from the inside of the glass where it crept in through seams no surveyor had ever found, and outside her window the world had been reduced to violence and more violence.

Loneliness, she had learned, was not a feeling so much as a uniform. You put it on. You buttoned it to the throat. You wore it until it stopped chafing and began instead to hold you together, and eventually you could not tell whether you wore it or it wore you. Three years of practice had made her excellent at it. She could stand an entire watch alone with a dead man's voice in her ears and let nothing show on her face, because there was no face to show it to.

*You should have come off the rocks,* she said to him sometimes, in the evenings, to the shape of him that lived in the corner of the lantern room. *You should have seen the swell running and stayed ashore like a sensible man.*

But Edmund had never been a sensible man, and that — she had always assumed — was why the sea had taken him. The boat had foundered crossing from Penzance with stores. Bad weather, the inquiry said. A sudden squall. An accident, they called it, the way they called things accidents when the paperwork needed finishing and nobody wanted to look twice.

* * *

At half past nine the mainspring of the rotation mechanism jammed.

She heard it happen — heard the tick falter, stutter, stop — and her body moved before her mind did, down the iron stairs two at a time with the lamp above her flashing its useless steady glare, fixed on one bearing like a man staring. A fixed light was worse than none; a helmsman could mistake it for an anchor light, a house, anything. She had perhaps minutes before it mattered.

The gear housing sat below the lantern room in its own small chamber, and she got the cover off with shaking hands and found the fault at once: a seized journal bearing, dry, the oil gummed to tar. Cold had thickened it and grit had gotten in — how, she'd never know — and the whole train of gears had ground itself to a halt on the failure of one small thing.

"One small thing," she repeated, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong in the dark.

She worked by the light of a hand torch clamped between her teeth. Solvent first, then the pick, then the fine wire, clearing the race while the tower shuddered around her and somewhere above her the lamp burned on, patient, blind. Her fingers went numb. Her knees screamed against the iron floor. Twice the wind hit with such force she felt the masonry flex, a slow groan traveling up through the stone like the building itself was afraid.

She did not think of Edmund while she worked. That was the trick of it, the mercy of competence: there was no room in a crisis for grief. Grief was for the quiet afterward, and she kept the afterward as short as she could.

At eleven o'clock precisely, the mechanism turned over, caught, and resumed. Tick. Tick. Tick. The lens began to sweep again, and the beam swung out across the black water like an arm remembering how to reach.

Hazel lay back on the iron plates, filthy, oil to the elbows, and let herself shake for exactly one minute. Then she climbed back up, cleaned the glass, checked the wick, and wrote in the logbook, in her neat keeper's hand:

*Night of 14th–15th October. Whole gale, backing ENE, gusts hurricane force. Rotation mechanism seized 21:30, cleared by hand, service restored 23:00. Light exhibited without interruption.*

Without interruption. She looked at the words a long moment. If only all failures could be logged so tidily.

* * *

Toward dawn the storm spent its worst and settled into a long exhausted roaring, the sea still mountainous but the wind dropping by degrees, the way a fever breaks. Hazel kept her post. She trimmed the wick four times, wiped the glass eleven times, drank tea gone cold in a tin mug, and watched the eastern sky decide, slowly, grudgingly, to exist.

The sun came up red behind the scud, a hard ugly sunrise, the kind that meant more weather behind it. The light was extinguished at 7:04. She stood a moment in the sudden gray daylight, listening to the ringing absence where the mechanism had been, and felt the familiar emptiness open under her pride like a trapdoor. Another night survived. Another night alone. The two facts sat side by side in her chest and refused to be reconciled.

She went down the stairs slowly, counting them out of habit — ninety-four, ninety-five, ninety-six, ninety-seven — and let herself out onto the leeward rocks to see what the sea had brought her.

It always brought something. Spars, crates, rope, once an entire ship's door, green-painted, hanging off its hinges like a question. The villagers from the mainland village — the few times she saw them — called the beach below the tower the sea's pocket, because it turned everything out eventually. She walked the wrack line with her skirts kilted up, boots sinking into weed-strewn sand, cataloguing the night's deposits: timber, a shattered crate stenciled with foreign letters, a dead gull with its wing folded neatly as if asleep.

And then, at the high-tide mark, half-buried in bladderwrack at the base of the seaward wall —

Not flotsam.

A child.

He was curled against the stone with his knees drawn up, a boy of seven or eight perhaps, soaked to the skin, his lips blue-gray, his small hands clenched in his sleeves. His hair was full of sand. His eyes were closed, and for one obscene heartbeat she thought he was dead — and then she saw the shallow stutter of his breathing, saw one bare foot twitch against the cold rock.

No boat had reached this beach in that sea. No boat could have. The cliffs on either side were unscalable, the paths washed out since midnight. And yet here he was, asleep against her door as if the tide had delivered him — and clutched to his chest, gripped so tightly his knuckles had gone white even in unconsciousness, a single sheet of paper, typed, its ink already beginning to run in the wet.

Hazel stood over him in the wreckage of the storm with the wind pulling at her hair, and felt the solid ground of her ordered life shift, very slightly, beneath her feet.

The boy did not wake. But the paper, when she bent closer, bore corrections in the margins — small, precise, penciled marks she knew the way she knew her own hands.

She knew that shorthand anywhere. It was Edmund's.
