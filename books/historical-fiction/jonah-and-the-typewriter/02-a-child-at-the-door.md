## 2. A Child at the Door

The storm spent itself by dawn, as storms do on that coast — not gently, but the way a drunk spends his last coin, recklessly and all at once. Hazel Montrose stepped out of the lantern room at half past six, her knees stiff from ten hours of winding the clockwork and trimming the wick and watching the sea try its teeth against the rock, and she stood for a moment on the gallery with the wind gone soft and confused around her, like a dog that has forgotten why it was barking.

The water lay strewn with the storm's leavings. Spars. A hatch cover, split lengthwise. Weed torn up from depths she preferred not to think about, flung across the rocks in black ropes. She had seen this after every blow for eleven years, first beside Edmund and then without him, and she had learned to read it the way other women read letters from home: here is what the sea took, here is what it grew bored of, here is what it decided to give back.

She climbed down the stair, made tea she did not want, ate bread that tasted of nothing, and told herself what she always told herself the morning after: tonight the lamp will need me again, and thank God for it. The thought had a worn groove in her mind, smooth as the stone step outside the door where Edmund's boots had worn a hollow she still avoided stepping in.

It was when she went out to clear the wreckage from the causeway path — because if the supply boat could not land cleanly at the next spring tide she would be eating salt mackerel until May, and there were limits — that she saw the gull first. It was worrying at something near her door, something slumped against the frame in the lee of the wall, and she raised her voice to drive it off before she understood what it was defending itself from.

Not wreckage. Not weed.

A child.

* * *

He was asleep — or she prayed he was asleep, because the alternative was too terrible to hold in a mind already crowded with the night's arithmetic of wind and tide. Seven years old, perhaps eight, though he was so thin the age seemed negotiable. He sat wedged into the angle of door and wall, where the stone caught what little warmth the house gave off through its seams, and he was soaked to the skin in clothes that had once been good ones — a sailor-collared coat, small brass buttons greened with salt, trousers that had belonged to a boy dressed by someone who loved him and had money to show it.

His hair was dark and plastered flat to his forehead. His lips were the color of the slate under the rain barrel.

Hazel knelt, and the cold of the wet granite came up through her skirts, and she put two fingers to the side of his throat with the detached efficiency she had learned from the medical pamphlet the Trinity House sent round every few years. A pulse. Slow, but present, steady as a second hand.

"Boy," she said. Then, louder, foolishly, to a child unconscious on a headland forty minutes' row from anywhere: "Boy. Wake up."

He did not. His head lolled against the doorframe, and something slipped from his fingers onto the step — a folded paper, sodden, its edges pulped soft.

She looked at it a long moment. Then she gathered the boy up under the arms and hauled him inside, and the effort of it surprised her; he weighed no more than the winter's store of candles.

* * *

She got him out of the wet coat and into Edmund's oldest jumper, which hung on him like rigging on a mast, and she wrapped him in the blanket from her own bed and set him near the stove, and all the while her hands moved with competence while her mind moved somewhere else entirely, circling one question like a gull over flotsam: *where did you come from?*

There was no boat on the rocks. She had walked the whole causeway; there was nothing but storm-leavings and the gulls complaining about her intrusion. The nearest village was Penlowe, four miles by the cliff path, and no seven-year-old walked that path in last night's weather unless God carried him. The sea did not give up living children. In eleven years it had never once done her that kindness.

And yet here he was, breathing in her kitchen, smelling of seawater and coal smoke and something else beneath it — lamp oil, she thought, oddly. Paraffin. As though he'd been kept in a room full of lamps.

On the table, the paper he'd carried had begun to dry, curling at its ruined edges. There was writing on it in pencil, a single line in a cramped adult hand:

*Forget the face.*

Hazel read it three times. It made less sense each time, and each time it frightened her more. It was not the note of a desperate mother abandoning a child to whoever might find him. It was an instruction. An order given to the boy himself, or perhaps — and this thought arrived cold, the way the sea comes up through floorboards — an order meant for whoever found him. Meant for *her*.

Forget the face. Whose face?

She went to the window and looked out at the empty morning, at the grey water smoothing itself over all its crimes, and saw nothing but gannets working the swell. When she turned back the boy's eyes were open.

They were dark, and they were fixed on her with a focus that no sleeping child should have, and he said nothing. He watched her the way she watched the barometer: waiting to see which way the needle would jump.

"You're safe," she said, and heard how stiff the words were, unused, like machinery left out all winter. "You're at the lighthouse. The Montrose light. Do you know where that is?"

Nothing. He pulled the blanket higher on his chest. His gaze went past her shoulder, to the lamp-oil can by the door, then to the stove, then back to her face, cataloguing her the way she had catalogued him.

"Hungry?"

At that, a fractional nod, and she was grateful for it, grateful for any transaction at all, and she set bread and dripping before him and watched him eat with the terrible control of a child who has learned hunger is not to be trusted to show restraint. He ate everything. He drank the milk she warmed. He never took his eyes fully off her hands.

When he finished, exhaustion took him like a wave taking a dinghy, and he slid down against the settle and slept, properly this time, his breath whistling faintly through a nose raw from salt.

Hazel sat across from him for a long while, her tea going cold, and felt something move in her that she had not permitted to move since the day the Penlowe constable had come to her door with his hat in his hands and the word *drowned* in his mouth. She had sealed that thing away behind routine and duty, behind the clean grammar of the keeper's life: trim the wick, wind the clock, log the weather, keep the light. It had worked for fourteen months. It had worked right up until this morning.

Now it was a child in Edmund's jumper, and the seal was broken, and she resented him for it with a fierceness that shamed her even as she felt it.

* * *

The paper had dried enough to handle by afternoon. She spread it on the table under the window's grey light and smoothed it flat with both hands, and read it.

It was typed. Typed, on a proper machine, the letters crisp and machine-even despite the water damage — and that alone was strange enough, out here where the only typed documents were Trinity House circulars and the odd invoice. It was a list. Ships' names down the left margin: *Corvina. Merrivale Rose. Saint Ives Belle. Andromache.* Dates beside them, October through March, this past year and the year before. Cargoes beside those: *coal, coal, tin plate, coal, machinery, coal* —

Coal. Coal, coal, coal, and yet one entry read *Andromache — Dec. 14 — coal (manifest) / spirits, tobacco, rifles? (actual)*, and the word *rifles* had been struck through and rewritten twice, and it was there, in that struck-through word, that the world Hazel had carefully arranged into a place where nothing happened reached in and took hold of her wrist.

Because the corrections were in the margins. Pencil, small, angled hard into the paper by someone pressing through weather or hurry. Quantities amended. Dates corrected — *Dec. 14 should be Dec. 12, tide wrong for the harbor watch rotation*. And beside *Merrivale Rose*, in shorthand she had not seen in fourteen months and would have known blindfolded, in the shorthand Edmund had invented himself, aged nineteen, because standard systems were, in his words, built for clerks with no imagination—

Her husband's shorthand. Her dead husband's private shorthand, the one he used in his own notebooks, the one no other living soul could read, sitting in pencil in the margin of a page naming ships that carried coal and landed God knew what under God knew whose flag.

The kitchen tilted, very slightly, the way the tower leaned in a gale. She put her palm flat on the table and waited for it to right itself.

Edmund had not always been a keeper. That was the story she told herself — that he had come to the light young and whole, salt-blessed, born to it. But there had been five years before the light, five years he spoke of rarely, when he had clerked ashore in Falmouth and then in Truro, keeping books for men whose names she half-remembered from dinner conversations she had not listened to closely because she was young and in love and the names of shipowners were not romantic. Vane. The name surfaced now like a body coming up under a hull. Ambrose Vane. Vane Line. Vane & Partners. The man whose steamers she had counted from the gallery a hundred times, riding heavy in the water, riding *too* heavy, and she had logged them and waved and thought nothing, because history happened in London, in Parliament, in the newspapers she got four days late — not here, not on her rock, not in the margins of her dead husband's handwriting.

*Forget the face.*

She looked at the sleeping boy, and the fear that had been sitting quietly in her chest since morning stood up and began to walk. Someone had typed this page. Someone had wanted it carried somewhere, to someone — and someone else had wanted it stopped, and the boy between those two somethings had been soaked, starved, and delivered by a sea that does not deliver children, to the one house on this coast where a man's shorthand could be read.

Which meant it was no accident he had washed up at her door. Whatever force had put him there — a woman's plan, a desperate gamble, Providence with its old cruel humor — it had known what she could do with this page. It had known better than she did.

Hazel folded the paper along its original creases, crossed the kitchen, and knelt at the bottom of the linen cupboard, behind the sheets, where the mortar was loose in the north wall because the tower breathed with the wind and shook the weakest seam loose every winter. She pressed the page into the gap, replaced the plaster dust and the linens, and stood with her heart going hard.

Behind her, the floorboard creaked. The boy was awake again, propped on one elbow, watching her with those dark unblinking eyes. And in a voice rusty and small, cracked from salt and disuse, he spoke his first words to her:

"She said you'd know."

Then his eyes rolled back, and fever took him, and the long night began again.
