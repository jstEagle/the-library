## 3. What Edmund Left Behind

The storm had taken three days to die, and in its wake the headland lay scoured and silent, like a room after a quarrel. Hazel Montrose spent the morning of the fourth day doing what she always did after weather: walking the gallery rail, counting the panes, running her thumb along the glass to feel for cracks. The lamp itself was sound. The lamp was always sound. It was everything else that rattled.

Inside, Jonah sat where she had left him, on the low stool by the stove, wrapped in Edmund's old cardigan with its sleeves turned back twice at the wrists. He had been with her two nights now. He ate what she gave him — slowly, watchfully, as though food might be rescinded — and he slept in bursts, waking at every groan of the wind with his eyes open and his body very still. He had not spoken a word since *please*, which he'd said at the door on that first drowned night, holding out the typewritten page like a ticket he hoped she would honor.

She had pinned the page to the inside of the logbook cabinet, behind the door where the daily records lived, and twice in the night she had taken it out and read it again by candlelight. *Corinthian Star. Merrowmaid. The Adela V.* Dates. Tonnages. Cargo entries that read as coal and meant nothing of the kind — she had begun, in the small hours, to suspect they meant nothing of the kind, though she could not have said how she knew. And in the margins, in a hand so faint it might have been breath on glass, small corrections. A transposed figure here. A date struck through and mended there. Notations in shorthand she recognized the way one recognizes a voice through a wall.

Edmund's shorthand. Edmund's corrections.

She had told herself it was coincidence for exactly as long as she could bear to, which was not long at all. Then she had gone to the sea chest.

* * *

It stood at the foot of the bed she still could not sleep in the middle of, this chest that had crossed from Falmouth with her as a bride and never been opened fully since the day the coastguard brought her his coat, sea-heavy, one sleeve torn. She had gone through it once, eleven months ago, with the methodical numbness of a woman settling an estate: clothes to be given, papers to be kept, keepsakes to be looked at once and put away facing down. She had found nothing then. But she had not known then what she was looking for.

Now she knelt on the cold flags and lifted the lid, and the smell rose up to meet her — camphor and wool and, beneath it, faintly, lamp oil, though he had never tended the light with her; that had been her post alone even when he lived.

His shirts first. His spare boots, the heels worn on the outside edges the way he always walked, toeing inward toward the sea as if the ground itself sloped there. She set these aside with hands that did not quite obey her. Then, wrapped in an oilcloth tied with string, at the bottom, beneath a false lining she had never thought to lift — a packet.

Not letters. Ledgers. Or pages of ledgers, torn out, perhaps thirty of them, covered edge to edge in columns of figures. And clipped to the front page, a letter of engagement on heavy cream paper bearing a letterhead she knew the way everyone on this coast knew it: **VANE SHIPPING & COAL, ST. AUSTELL AND LONDON**.

*This is to certify the employment of Mr. Edmund Montrose as junior clerk and assistant in accounts...*

Junior clerk. Her husband, who had told her he was going up-country to keep books for a grain merchant in Truro, had been keeping books for Ambrose Vane. For two years. Two years during which he had come home to her at intervals, smelling of ink and train smoke, laughing at her stories of the light, saying nothing — nothing — about the fleet whose ships she guided past her own rock on their way into harbor. Ships whose names were now typed on a child's page with dates beside them that she was beginning, sickeningly, to match against days Edmund had been home. Days he had kissed her forehead and asked about the weather and said *nothing*.

She knelt there a long time. The stove ticked. Somewhere below the island the swell worked at the rocks with its patient stone hands.

And then she began, properly, to read.

The figures made a pattern once she stopped trying to read them honestly. Coal in, coal out — and then coal in, and something else out, something carried under naval registry numbers that no collier had any business holding. Entries marked with Edmund's shorthand in the margins, the same shorthand as on the typed page: *not 3rd — see my hand*. *Duplicate manifest kept*. *V. signs but does not read*. And on the last sheet, dated six weeks before his death, three words underlined twice:

*If anything happens—*

The sentence ended there. Whatever came after it had been written somewhere else, or never written at all.

Hazel sat back on her heels. Eleven months of grief rearranged itself around her like furniture in a dark room — same shapes, all of them wrong now. She had built her mourning carefully, brick by brick, a fine solid house of it: *the sea took him, the sea takes everyone, the sea is not cruel it is only the sea*. She had said as much to the vicar. She had said it to herself at four in the morning, trimming wicks, and it had held her up the way a railing holds you up, and now she put her weight on it and it swung loose in her hands like a gate.

The sea had not taken Edmund. The sea did not tear coats on one sleeve. The sea did not leave a man's boots dry.

She was still kneeling there, the packet open across her lap, when she heard the small feet behind her and did not turn fast enough to compose her face.

Jonah stood watching her. In the grey light his eyes were the color of harbors, and he looked from her face to the papers and back with an expression far too old for seven — the expression of someone checking whether the grown-up had seen too much to be safe, or just enough to be useful.

"You knew him," Hazel said. It was not what she had meant to say. "The woman who wrote your page. She knew Edmund."

Jonah's mouth opened. Closed. He came closer, one slow step, then another, until he stood at the edge of the scattered ledger sheets as though they were tide pools he'd been warned about. His finger came out and touched the letterhead. He traced the little anchor in the crest, round and round.

"House," he said.

Hazel went very still.

"Big house." His voice was rusty, a hinge unused. "Cellar. Cold cellar. Boxes like—" he made a shape with his hands, stacking, "—like church. Rows."

"The cellar had boxes," Hazel said gently. "Rows of boxes. Papers?"

He nodded, then frowned, digging for words the way a child digs for worms, not knowing which ones will hold. "Woman. She come down. Nighttime. She write, write, write." He mimed it, fingers dancing on invisible keys, and Hazel's heart turned over: the typewriter. The typed page had been made in that cellar, in that house, by lamplight, while a boy hid among Vane's boxed duplications. "She say, run. She give me paper. She say find—" His brow creased. He looked at Hazel, at the oilcloth, at her hands. "Smell," he finished helplessly, touching his own shoulder, then pointing at hers. "You smell like her. Like burn-y lamp."

Lamp oil. Whatever else the secretary of Ambrose Vane had been, she had smelled of a lighthouse, and a drowning child had trusted the next person who did.

"She said find the keeper," Hazel said quietly. "Didn't she. Find the keeper of the light."

Another nod. Then, unprompted, in that broken rusted voice, the thing that stopped Hazel's breath: "Man shout. Down stairs. Woman hide me. Man say—" Jonah's face worked; he pressed his fists to his eyes. "Man say clerk fix it or clerk go in water."

* * *

She fed him bread and dripping afterward and watched him eat, and she thought about accidents.

She thought about how Edmund had been found off Gull Rock, four miles from where his boat was moored. She had asked about it at the time, and been told currents were treacherous, and had accepted it because accepting things was how she survived. She thought about the torn sleeve — snagged, she'd assumed, on his anchor line. She thought about the engagement letter, dated two years back, and the ledger sheet dated six weeks before his death, and the unfinished sentence: *if anything happens—*

Something had happened. Something had been arranged to happen.

And a woman in a big house with a cold cellar had spent a year — how long? months at least — teaching a child to run to the light, carrying the half of the proof that would make Vane answer for it. Half. Because the other half, the duplicate manifests Edmund had promised in his careful marginal hand, the boxes stacked like church pews, still sat in Ambrose Vane's own cellar, under his own floor, guarded by his own name.

Hazel banked the stove and pinned the typed page back inside the cabinet, and beside it, deliberately, she pinned the engagement letter. Two documents now instead of one. Two children of the same secret, reunited on the door of her logbook like siblings found.

Outside, the afternoon was clearing in the way of Cornish winters — hard blue slashes between racing cloud, the sea beaten silver. From the gallery she could see the mainland, the thin grey line of the coast road, and beyond it, invisible but perfectly imaginable, St. Austell, and the Vane offices, and the big house with the cellar. Somewhere down there men were paid to watch harbors. Somewhere down there a magistrate's court sat in stone comfort, and a shipping magnate signed papers without reading them, and a woman who smelled of lamp oil was either dead or hiding or waiting to see if a child had reached the light.

Jonah came and stood beside her at the rail, small and warm in the borrowed cardigan. He did not look at her. Together they watched the water that had supposedly taken Edmund Montrose, and Hazel understood, with a clarity that felt almost like fever, that history did not live in cities and parliaments after all. It had been living in her sea chest. It had been sleeping in her bed. It had walked up to her door soaked in seawater and seven years old.

"Jonah," she said. He turned his harbor-colored eyes up to her. "The woman in the cellar. Did she ever say where the papers should go? If not to me — where?"

He thought. His lips moved silently, rehearsing. Then: "Truro," he said. "Big building. She say inquiry." He tugged her sleeve, urgent now, words coming faster as the dam broke. "But road is stop. Men at harbor. Men at road. I see them from boat. Three mens, three places." He held up three fingers, small and certain. "She say *they know the keeper's wife has it*."

The wind came off the sea and pushed Hazel's hair back from her face, cold as a hand. They knew. Which meant the hunt had already begun — and the first of the three places, she understood, watching the low grey shapes of customs boats working the harbor entrance two miles off, was already being watched.

They would come for the page. They would come asking questions at the harbor, polite men in customs collars, and sooner or later they would ask about the widow at the light.

Hazel put her arm around Jonah's shoulders and drew him in against the wind, and found, to her surprise, that her fear had settled into something harder and more usable.

"Then we'd best decide," she said to the boy, to the sea, to the ghost of the man who had lied to her for two years, "what story we're going to tell."
