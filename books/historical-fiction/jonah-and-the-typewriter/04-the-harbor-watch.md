## 4. The Harbor Watch

The boat came for her on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to the sea and everything to Hazel Montrose.

She had sent word to Truro on Friday — a letter carried by the grocer's boy, addressed with deliberate dullness to the Board of Trade inquiry office, requesting an appointment regarding *irregularities observed in the course of duty*. She had spent two days choosing those words. Not *crimes*, not *smuggling*, not *Vane*. Bureaucrats, she reasoned, moved toward the irregular and away from the dramatic. A woman who wrote *crimes* was hysterical. A woman who wrote *irregularities* was an official witness, and officials protected their own.

The reply came back by the same boy: present yourself at the inquiry annex, Quay Street, Truro, Thursday the fourteenth, at half past ten. Bring any documentation in your possession.

*Bring any documentation.* As though it were a ration book. As though it were not the single most dangerous piece of paper on the Cornish coast, folded now into quarters and living in the breast pocket of her coat, against her heart, where she could feel its small stiff weight when she breathed.

It had lived in other places first. Under the flagstone by the stove. Inside the hollow of the rain gauge. Sewn — badly, then better — into Jonah's teddy bear, until she thought better of it, because if anything happened to the boy the page would go wherever he went, and she could not allow that. For three nights it had sat inside the lens room, wedged behind the spare wick box, and for three nights she had climbed the stairs every hour to confirm it still existed. This was no way to live. So now it lived against her heart, and she had decided that whatever happened to her would happen to it simultaneously, which was either cowardice or strategy depending on how the day went.

Jonah did not want her to go.

He stood at the window of the keeper's cottage with his palms flat against the glass, watching her dress for the mainland as though she were going to war — which, she supposed, in the smallest possible sense, she was. He had learned perhaps thirty words since coming to her in November. He hoarded them like coins, spending them rarely and only on necessities. This morning he spent freely.

"Harbor," he said. "Men. Boots."

"Yes." She tucked her collar up. "There'll be men at the harbor."

His hands made the shape he used for *bad* — fingers curled, knuckles forward. Then he pointed at her breast pocket.

"I know," she said. "I know where it is, love."

He crossed the room and took hold of her coat hem with both fists, and looked up at her with that terrible gravity children carry, the gravity of someone who has seen the grown world do exactly what it is capable of and has never been lied to about it. Something passed between them there in the grey morning light — not words, because he had few and she suddenly had fewer. She crouched so they were level.

"If I'm not back by the evening boat," she said slowly, "you light the lamp at sunset. You know the turns. You've watched me a hundred times."

He shook his head hard.

"You can. I've seen you count the turns in your sleep." She took his hands from her coat and held them. "The light doesn't care who winds it, Jonah. It just has to be wound."

That, at least, he understood. The light was the only thing in the world he trusted without reservation, and she had built it that way deliberately, showing him the mechanism, letting him oil the carriage wheels, teaching him that some things kept burning no matter what happened to the people tending them. She had thought she was comforting him. Only now, pulling on her gloves in the doorway, did she understand she had been preparing him.

Mrs. Penhale's nephew rowed her across the sound in his fishing boat, hunched and silent, spray coming over the bow in cold sheets. Hazel sat in the stern with the logbook on her lap, wrapped in oilcloth, and rehearsed.

This was the part no one told you about courage: it was mostly rehearsal. Since Tuesday she had been running the harbor in her mind, over and over, the way she ran the lamp mechanism — checking every gear for wear. *Where are you bound? Truro. What business? Board of Trade. What's in the book? The log, obviously; look bored; keepers are always hauling logs somewhere; nobody in history has ever wanted to read another person's logbook.* The logbook itself was the whole trick, and she had spent Sunday constructing it.

Because here was what she knew, from six years of watching men lie to each other on docks: customs men did not fear paper. They feared *official* paper — documents with authority behind them, documents that could make trouble flow uphill onto their own superiors. A smuggled ledger would make them search her. But a lighthouse logbook, stamped by Trinity House, carried by the keeper herself in the open, was the most tedious object imaginable. No one had ever committed a crime inside a logbook, because logbooks were read by clerks, and no criminal in history had ever willingly faced a clerk.

So she had done it in plain sight. Page 214, between the entry for the gale of the ninth (wind SSE, severe, lamp turned without interruption) and the entry for the eleventh (fog, signal gun fired twice), she had pasted a false page — glued at the top edge only, laid over the typed sheet so that it lifted like a flap. On the false page, in her neatest hand: *Stores received: colza oil 40 gal., cotton waste 2 lb., glass chimney spare 1.* Utterly, magnificently boring. Beneath it, hidden, Edmund's shorthand corrections marching across the typed names of ships that had never carried what their manifests claimed.

She had admired her own work at midnight with something close to glee, and had then been frightened by the glee. When, she wondered, had she become a woman who enjoyed this?

* * *

Newlyn harbor wore its new masters the way a dog wears a muzzle — resentful, constrained, wrong.

Hazel saw them before the boat even touched the quay: three men in customs coats that were a shade too new, standing with the particular idleness of men being paid to watch rather than work. Real customs officers developed a slouch, a cigarette, a way of looking through people. These three stood like soldiers off duty, weight balanced, heads tracking movement along the quay with the unconscious discipline of hired men. And one of them — the tall one, grey-moustached, doing nothing at all near the fish scales — she recognized. He had been on Vane's pier in Falmouth the year Edmund died. She had seen him once across a crowded landing and never forgotten him, the way one never forgets a man who watched a coffin being loaded with his hands in his pockets.

Her mouth went dry. Her heart began its stupid, animal hammering, loud enough she was sure the whole harbor could hear it.

*Rehearse,* she told herself. *You have done this a hundred times since Tuesday.*

She paid the nephew, gathered her bag and the logbook, and stepped onto the quay with what she desperately hoped was the walk of a woman on tedious official business: brisk, slightly put-upon, eyes already looking past the customs men toward the railway ticket office as though the only thing in the world she wanted was to be rid of this errand and on her train.

The tall man let her get within ten feet before he moved.

"Morning, ma'am. Where are you bound?"

"Truro." Perfect. Flat. Slightly impatient. Where had this voice come from? "And I'm late for the ten-forty, so if you'll forgive me—"

"Business?"

"The Board of Trade." She shifted the logbook under her arm and produced, from her handbag, the letter from the inquiry annex — she had brought it precisely for this moment, folded to show the official letterhead, the date, the reference number. She held it out with the weary confidence of a woman who had shown it eleven times already today. "I'm expected at half past ten. You're welcome to read it properly if you enjoy standing in this wind, but I don't."

Something flickered in the man's face. Not suspicion — hesitation. She watched, with a sensation like ice water, the exact moment his arithmetic failed him: *customs man detains lighthouse keeper en route to Board of Trade inquiry* was a sentence that ended careers, possibly his own superior's. Vane's men were thugs, but they were thugs employed to be invisible, and invisibility required knowing which women not to touch.

"Lighthouse keeper, are you?" he said.

"Hazel Montrose. Montrose Light." She said her own name the way she said the light's — as one word, a fact, older than his employment. "Six years. You can wire Trinity House if you like, but they don't answer quickly and my train won't wait."

A silence. Wind snapped the tarpaulin on the fish cart behind him. Somewhere down the quay a gull screamed its idiot scream, and Hazel thought, absurdly, of Jonah counting the turns of the lamp in his sleep.

The man stepped aside. "Mind you don't miss it, ma'am."

"I never miss it," she said, which was a lie, because she had missed three ferries in her life, and walked on without hurrying — hurrying was confession — while every nerve in her body sang like a struck wire.

She did not look back. Looking back was the thing that undid them all, in every story; she kept her eyes on the ticket office and her breathing even and her arm pressed tight against the logbook, and only when the train doors closed behind her and the platform began to slide away did she permit herself one long, shaking exhale, fogging the window glass.

Then — God help her — she laughed. Alone in a third-class compartment outside Penzance, a widow of forty-one laughed until her eyes stung, not from relief exactly but from revelation. *I can lie.* She had spent six years believing herself transparent, a woman whose grief showed through her like a lamp through glass, and instead she had stood in the wind and dismantled a man's resolve with nothing but a letterhead and her own name delivered like a fact older than his employment.

Edmund, she thought, watching the coast unspool backward, had never known this about her. He had protected her from everything, including — evidently — herself.

* * *

Truro took her testimony in a low-ceilinged room that smelled of ink and damp plaster. The examiner was a dry man named Polwhele who wrote down *irregularities in cargo manifests, observed in the course of duty* without once asking what she had actually observed, and told her the matter would be referred, and that documentation should be submitted to the registrar.

"Submitted," Hazel repeated.

"In duplicate, ideally."

She walked out of the annex at one o'clock with the logbook still under her arm and the whole enterprise suddenly, horribly clear to her: they wanted papers. Men like Polwhele believed in papers the way other men believed in God, and she had just promised to deliver the evidence directly into a registry where Ambrose Vane's solicitors would know its location within the week. The inquiry would not come to the storm-battered rock. The papers would have to come to the inquiry — past the harbor, past whoever waited between here and there, past everything.

On the platform waiting for the return train, she bought a newspaper she didn't read and found herself studying the shipping notices instead. *MV Corvina, Vane Line, arrived Falmouth.* Edmund's handwriting had been all over ships like that once. Ships that docked at night. Ships whose cargoes she now understood.

When the train reached Penzance the light was already failing, gold going to iron over the water, and she knew before the boatman spoke that something was wrong. He wouldn't meet her eyes. He pulled at his cap.

"There's been a gentleman out to the light, Mrs. Montrose. Yesterday. Left a card." He handed it over reluctantly, as though it might burn him.

Grey cardstock. Expensive. A printed name — **A. VANE** — and beneath it, in ink, in a hand that was elegant and unhurried:

*A pleasure to know your husband once. Perhaps we might renew the acquaintance. — A.V.*

Hazel stood on the darkening quay with the sea wind pulling her hair loose, and understood that the checkpoint behind her had been the easy one.
