## 5. Jonah Speaks

The woman's name was Miss Pell, and she smelled of ink.

Jonah knew this the way he knew the shape of his own hands — not because anyone had told him, but because he had pressed his face into her sleeve so many times that the smell had gone into him and stayed. Ink and cold tea and something underneath like rain on hot stone. When he tried to remember his mother — his real mother, the one before the cellar — she did not have a smell anymore. She was only a voice now, and a hand, and a song with no words that he could hum but never sing.

Miss Pell had told him once, crouching in the dark of the linen cupboard with a candle shielded behind her apron, that names were dangerous things. *You are Jonah when I say you are Jonah*, she had whispered. *When anyone else asks, you are nobody's. You came from nowhere. Do you understand?* He had understood. He was very good at understanding things that frightened grown-ups, because frightened grown-ups whispered, and whispers carried further than shouting if you were small enough to be near the floor where all the listening happened.

The Vane house was called Greywater, though there was nothing grey about it in summer. It sat above its own private cove like a fat cat on a wall, all cream stone and green shutters, and at night the windows went gold one by one as the servants went round with the lamps. Jonah had lived in the top of it, in the nursery corridor, for as long as he could remember knowing anything at all. Before that — before — there was a cottage and the sea-smell of a different coast, and a woman humming, and then there was a long dark ride in a cart under sacking, and then there was Greywater, and Mr. Vane saying to someone whose face Jonah never saw: *He'll do. He has no one to ask after him.*

Jonah had not known what that meant then. He knew now. It meant: *this child can vanish, and no one will come looking.*

* * *

He had been useful. That was the thing he did not tell Hazel, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Children in great houses are invisible; they are furniture that breathes. Jonah had learned to carry trays through corridors where gentlemen stopped talking when adults passed but kept talking when a boy of five or six went by, because what harm could he do, and who would he tell? He had stood in doorways holding a dusting cloth while men said words like *manifest* and *naval cover* and *the third Friday, always the third Friday,* and he had not understood any of it except that the words made the air go heavy, like weather coming.

And then one night — the night everything changed — he had been in the study where he should not have been, fetching back a dropped pencil-case that belonged to Mr. Vane's son, and he had heard the argument. Two voices. Mr. Vane's, which was smooth as poured cream even when it was angry, and another, thinner voice, a woman's, saying: *It is murder dressed as shipping, sir, and I will not carry the numbers of drowned men in my ledgers.*

Silence then. The kind of silence that has weight.

Jonah had frozen in the doorway with the pencil-case in his fist, and Mr. Vane had looked straight through him — through him, the way you look through a chair — and said, *Miss Pell, you will mind your station.* And she had looked at Jonah, just for one flicker of a second, and he had seen that she knew he was there, and that she was afraid *for* him, not of him.

Three days later she had found him crying in the boot room because one of the footmen had struck him for breaking a cup, and she had knelt down on the cold flagstones in her good skirt and taken his face in her cool hands, and that was when the secret started. That was when she became the cupboard and the candle and the whisper.

* * *

She hid him properly after that. Not all at once — it was done in pieces, careful as mending. First she arranged for him to sleep in the east attic instead of the nursery corridor, saying he'd been troublesome, saying the housekeeper wanted him out from underfoot. Then she taught him the sound of Mr. Vane's boots on the stairs (slow, deliberate, heels first) versus the boots of everyone else, so that he could be silent and folded away inside two minutes whenever those particular footsteps started upward. The cellar came later. The cellar was where she kept the papers.

He remembered it exactly: the smell of apples going soft in their crates, the drip of something far off in the dark, the little desk she had made from an upturned barrel with a lamp on it that she shaded with a biscuit tin. She would bring him down when the house was full of guests, when the noise upstairs was thickest, and he would sit on an apple crate wrapped in a horse blanket while she typed.

The typewriter was black and gleaming and terrible to him, like some insect with silver teeth. Her fingers moved over it fast and light, and the keys struck up at the paper, and the sound — *tick-tick-tack, tick-tick, ziiiip* as the carriage went back — was the sound of safety. As long as the typing went on, he was hidden and she was there and nothing bad could reach either of them. She typed for hours some nights. She let him hold the pages afterward sometimes, still warm from the roller, and he could not read more than a few words but he understood that each page was a small stone thrown at a giant, and that someday, if enough stones were thrown —

*What is it?* he had asked her once, tracing the neat rows with a finger.

*The truth,* she had said. *Which is worth nothing locked in a cellar, and everything delivered to the right hands. That is the whole trouble of truth, Jonah. It needs a courier.*

Then, one wild night in October, she had come down the cellar steps two at a time with her hair half-down and her eyes shining wet-bright, and she had packed him into the space behind the coal chute with his coat and a paper parcel of bread and cheese, and she had put into his hands the page — the page, folded into quarters, wrapped in oilcloth — and she had held his head between her hands and pressed her forehead against his.

*There is a lighthouse west of Porthmora,* she said, very fast, very quietly. *Do you hear me? West. Follow the water with it always on your left. A keeper lives there. If it is the keeper I think it is, the truth will be safe there, and so will you. You knock, and you show the page, and you do not give it to anyone else. No matter what they promise you. No matter what they threaten. Swear it.*

He had sworn it. He had sworn because her forehead was hot against his and because she was crying without any sound at all, which frightened him more than Mr. Vane's boots ever had.

*Why me?* he asked. *Why don't you take it?*

Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. *Because they watch me now. And because a child on a road is pitied, and a woman on a road with papers is searched. Go, my love. Go west.*

She had kissed his forehead, right where she used to press hers, and pushed him up the chute and into the screaming dark, and that was the last time Jonah ever smelled ink.

* * *

The storm had taken three days off his life, he thought. Or maybe given them back; he couldn't decide. He had walked with the sea on his left just as she told him, except the sea did not stay in one place in a storm, it climbed hills and swallowed roads and once it had crossed a field ahead of him like a grey flock of sheep, and he had run. He had slept in a hay barn with cows breathing near him like warm engines. He had eaten the bread the first day and the cheese the second and on the third day he had crested the headland in the washed-clean morning and seen the tower standing white against the sky with its gallery rail black as ink — like ink — and he had known, the way you know your own name, that this was the place.

The keeper had opened the door before he knocked twice.

She smelled of lamp oil.

His mother had smelled of lamp oil. Not Miss Pell — Miss Pell was ink — but the mother before, the humming one, the one from the cottage that lived now only in songs without words. She had trimmed wicks every evening in the window, and the smell of the burning had gotten into her cardigan and her hair, and when she held him at bedtime that was what love smelled like: oil and smoke and warmth. And this tall, tired, salt-faced woman had opened the door with the wind tearing her skirt flat against her legs and the light blazing behind her, and the smell had come out to meet him on the wind, and Jonah's whole body had decided before his mind caught up: *this one. This one keeps things safe.*

So he had slept against her door, because the storm had beaten him to it and won, and in the morning she had found him, and he had given her the page the way he swore to give it — into her hands and nobody else's.

* * *

Now it was weeks later, and Jonah sat on the floor of the lantern room with his knees drawn up, watching Mrs. Montrose polish the brass. She thought he didn't notice things. Grown-ups mostly thought that. But he noticed how she talked to Edmund — that was her husband, the drowned one — she talked to him while she worked, quietly, telling him the weather and the state of the stores and, lately, small things about Jonah, as though introducing them. *Your boy ate all the porridge again. Your boy hums in his sleep.* He noticed how she checked the door latch four times before bed now, and how she had moved his sleeping mat closer to the stove without saying she'd done it, and how she read the typed page every night by lamplight, touching the margins where the small slanted writing was, and weeping without sound, which was a thing Jonah knew about, being expert in it himself.

The margins. He should tell her about the margins. He should tell her that the slanted writing wasn't printed, that Miss Pell wrote it herself, in the cellar, in pencil, correcting the numbers, and that meant whoever wrote it had access to the true books, and that meant — well, he didn't know what it meant, but Mrs. Montrose's face when she found the writing had gone very still, like water before ice, and he thought maybe she knew the handwriting, and that seemed important, that seemed like a stone in the giant's shoe.

But telling was hard. His words had gone into hiding the same week he had, and they came out slow and crooked, one at a time, like creatures venturing from a burrow. *Cellar,* he managed that evening, while she trimmed the wick. *Barrel. Upside down. Typewriter. Lady. Black lady, iron lady. Papers. More papers. Down there. Still there, I think.*

Mrs. Montrose's hand stopped on the wick-trimmer. The flame leaned and righted itself.

*Still there,* she repeated softly. *In Vane's cellar. Edmund's—* She did not finish. She looked at Jonah for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her come and sit down on the floor beside him, which no grown-up had done since Miss Pell knelt on the boot-room flags. She smelled of oil and salt and brass polish, and Jonah leaned sideways until his shoulder touched her arm, and she let him.

*You're keeping me,* he wanted to tell her. *That's backwards. I chose you. I walked three days to choose you.* But the words wouldn't come, so he said the important part instead:

*Don't send me back there. Even if they ask. Even if they give you things. Don't trade me.*

Something in her breath changed. He felt it through his shoulder.

*Jonah,* she said, and her voice was the voice she used for talking to Edmund, quiet and certain, *no one is trading anybody. Not while I keep this light.*

Above them the lamp turned, steady as a heart, sweeping its beam out over the black water and hauling it back again. And Jonah believed her — wholly, dangerously, the way children believe — which is why, three nights later, when the sound of Mr. Vane's kind of boots came up the rock-cut stair below the tower, slow and deliberate, heels first, Jonah knew the truth had finally come asking after its courier, and his blood went cold as seawater.
