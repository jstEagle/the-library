## 6. The Magistrate's Son

The house at Penmerrick stood back from the road behind a wall of escallonia gone wild, its hedges reaching over the stone like hands trying to close around the windows. Hazel had passed it a hundred times on her way to market and never once thought of it as anything but what it had always been: the magistrate's house, solid and grey and safe as an oath. Now, with the typed page folded against her ribs and Jonah's cold little hand swallowed in her own, she saw the ivy for what it was — cover, or cage, depending on which side you stood.

"You'll wait outside," she told him for the fourth time. "There's a bench by the gate. You can see the whole front of the house from there. If anyone comes out but me, you walk toward the churchyard. Don't run. Walk."

Jonah nodded. He did not argue; he never argued. It was one of the things about him that both broke her heart and made him easier to carry through this winter than any child had a right to be. Seven years old, and he had already learned that survival was quiet work.

She left him on the bench with the wind worrying his hair and went up the path alone, rehearsing as she walked. *Mr. Tresidder. I've come about the boy. I need your counsel, and your discretion.* Two words she had never in her life asked anyone for, and here she was prepared to beg them from a man she'd met four times.

The housekeeper showed her into the study, where Mr. Pascoe Tresidder sat behind a desk too large for the room, a small man made smaller by the furniture he'd chosen. He rose when she entered, which she took for courtesy until she saw his eyes flick past her shoulder to the window, to the drive, to the road beyond — checking, the way a man checks a tide table before launching a boat.

"Mrs. Montrose." He did not offer his hand. "You keep the light at Carrick Vean."

"I do."

"Alone now." It wasn't a question. Everyone in the parish knew how Edmund had died; grief was the only currency they'd ever let her spend freely. "Sit down, then. What brings you off your rock?"

She sat. She began the speech she had built like a sea wall, word by word, and got perhaps six words into it when she saw the letter lying open on his blotter.

She was a keeper's wife and then a keeper herself; she had spent nine years reading water, reading cloud, reading the particular silence of a lamp about to fail. She read that letter in three seconds flat. The crest at the top. The heavy, expensive paper. And the signature — *A. Vane* — looped and confident, the signature of a man who signed things the way other men breathed.

She could not make out the body of the letter from where she sat. But she could make out Mr. Tresidder's face as he realized what she was looking at, and that told her everything: the flush climbing his collar, the hand that moved to cover the page and stopped halfway, caught between concealment and the knowledge that concealing it would confirm what her eyes had already done.

"Bad news?" she said mildly.

"Business," he said. "Shipping business. Nothing that concerns the parish." He squared the letter, turned it face down, and put a book on top of it — a Bible, of all things, as if weight alone could hold the thing shut. "You were saying. About a boy."

And there it was, laid out plain between them: she could not tell him. Not now. Whatever counsel she'd come for, whatever protection, it belonged to Vane before it belonged to her, because a debtor's discretion is not his own. She thought of Edmund's shorthand in the margins of that typed page, the careful corrections of a clerk who knew the ledgers were wrong and couldn't say so while alive — and understood, with a cold settling in her chest, that she had just watched the law of the coast turn its face away.

"A foundling," she said instead, smoothly, astonishing herself. "Washed up after the storm. The authorities will want him registered. I wanted to know whether that was your office or someone else's."

It was a good lie because it was nearly true, and because it gave him nothing to act on. Tresidder frowned, consulted a memory that seemed to cost him effort, and said the registration of foundlings was a matter for the Board of Guardians in Truro, and that she ought to make inquiry there.

"Truro," Hazel repeated. "That's a long road in winter."

"The roads are watched this season," he said, and then seemed to hear himself, and added quickly, "smugglers. Customs business. Nothing for you to trouble over." He stood, and she stood, and the interview ended the way so many things ended now: with everyone knowing and no one saying.

Outside, the light was going the colour of pewter. Jonah was not on the bench.

Her heart did something physical, a fist closing in her chest — and then she heard the voices, high and bright, from behind the escallonia hedge. She came around the corner fast and found them: Jonah, and another boy, the same age or near it, in a coat too fine for the weather, holding out a conker on a string as if it were a crown jewel.

"He's got no name," the boy announced, delighted. "Your boy. He says he's got no name. Is he a gypsy?"

"He's my ward," Hazel said. "His name is Jonah."

"Jonah," the boy tried it out, tasting it. "I'm Charles. Charles Tresidder. My father's the magistrate. Do you know what a magistrate does? He puts people in prison." He said it with enormous satisfaction, and Jonah looked at Hazel with those wide, careful eyes that held so much and gave away so little, and she saw the fear move through him like a shadow under ice.

"We're expected," she said. "Say goodbye, Jonah."

But Charles followed them to the gate, chattering — about his pony, about the storm, about the men in uniform who had been staying at the big hotel and driving motorcars through the village at all hours, which his father told him not to mention to anyone. "So I'm mentioning it to you," he concluded triumphantly. "Why aren't I s'posed to mention it? Do you know?"

Hazel knelt to fix Jonah's collar, which did not need fixing, so that her face was hidden and her voice could drop below the boy's hearing.

"Charles. If a stranger asks you about us — about Jonah and me — what will you tell them?"

"That I don't know you."

"And why will you say that?"

Charles considered her with the frank blue eyes of a boy who had never yet needed to lie and found the exercise novel. "Because it's a secret," he said. "Secrets are better than conkers."

"It isn't a game."

"I know." And then, with a sudden gravity that startled her, a gravity she had wrongly believed lived only in children who had been hurt: "He looks sad. Your Jonah. Like something happened to him and nobody fixed it." He glanced back at the house, at the study window where his father's silhouette moved behind the glass. "Nobody fixes things here either. They just talk about them."

She should have gone. Every prudent instinct said go. Instead she heard herself ask, "Charles, if I needed to get a message to Truro — to the Board of Guardians, properly, without it passing through your father's office first — could you carry it? Could you be trusted?"

The boy straightened inside his expensive coat. "I go to Truro every fortnight with Nanny. On the train. Father thinks the train is safer than the roads since the customs men came." He said it easily, innocently, and Hazel felt the ground shift under her — the whole rotten arrangement of the coast laid bare by a child repeating what he'd been told at dinner. Roads watched by Vane's men; trains still free; and the magistrate routing his own son around his own creditor.

"Then perhaps," Hazel said slowly, "you'll carry something for me next fortnight. If I bring it to you quietly, at the gate, and nobody sees."

"Is it a secret?"

"It's the realest secret there is."

Charles grinned, and for one unguarded moment he looked so much like Jonah might have looked, in some other life, in some unbroken childhood, that Hazel had to look away.

* * *

They walked home along the cliff path in a wind that tasted of iron, and Jonah held her hand the whole way, gripping harder than the path required.

At the turn above Carrick Vean, where the lighthouse stood up out of the darkening water like a held breath, he stopped. He did this sometimes — planted his feet and looked at her with his whole face, gathering himself for words the way other children gathered stones.

"The man in the big house," he said. "He asked questions."

"He's a child. Children ask questions."

"He asked about the men in the cars." Jonah's fingers tightened. "You told him nothing. But he knows where we live now. He watched us walk away and he knows."

Hazel looked down at him — seven years old, weighing risk like a quartermaster — and felt the full cost of the winter arrive in her chest. She had spent two years learning that isolation kept her safe. She had been wrong. Isolation had only kept her alone. One child at a gate, one conker on a string, one pair of innocent eyes — and suddenly her fate rested not on her own competence, which she could trust, but on the goodwill of a magistrate's son, which she could not.

"Then we don't wait for the fortnight," she said. "We send the message sooner. There's a fishing boat running to Falmouth Thursday — Tam Bellow's boat. He owes Edmund his life and he's never once mentioned it." She heard her own voice steadying as the plan assembled itself. "And if the boat doesn't sail — if the weather turns, or Vane's men are watching the harbor too closely—"

"Then Charles carries it," Jonah finished.

"Then Charles carries it," she agreed, "and we trust a seven-year-old with the future of half the shipping coast, God help us."

Jonah almost smiled. It was the closest he came, and she had learned to treasure these almosts the way other people treasured photographs.

That night she climbed the tower and lit the lamp, and while the great lens gathered its light and threw it seaward, she sat on the iron stair with the logbook open on her knees and wrote, in the small neat hand she used for weather:

*Wind SW freshening. Visibility poor. Trust extended to T.'s son, Truro run. Outcome unknown.*

She read it back and almost laughed — almost — because it sounded exactly like what it was: a woman logging her own leap into darkness, with no light of her own to steer by. Below her, Jonah slept in Edmund's old chair, his breath a small steady rhythm against the roar of the sea, and she thought: *this is what he is keeping safe, and this is what I am risking him for.*

Thursday was two days off. The message — copied in her own hand, the original page sewn into the lining of Jonah's coat where no checkpoint would think to search a child — would need to reach Tam Bellow before dawn.

And it was somewhere in the middle of that calculation, watching the beam swing out across black water, that she saw it: far down the coast road, past the harbor lights, the headlights of a motorcar moving slow, stopping, moving again — methodical as a searchlight, working its way up the coast toward her rock.

She snuffed the stair lamp and stood very still in the lantern room, letting the great light sweep over her, and counted the seconds between flashes, and waited to see where the car would stop.
