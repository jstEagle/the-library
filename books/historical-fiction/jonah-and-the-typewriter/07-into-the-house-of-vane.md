## 7. Into the House of Vane

*Hazel*

The oil barrels went out on the morning cart twice a year, and the invoice went back twice a year, and in six years of widowhood Hazel Montrose had never once set foot inside the house that consumed her lamp oil by the gallon. Vane House did not buy its light from the harbor chandlers. It bought it from the lighthouse — an arrangement Edmund had made in his first year of clerkship, she now understood, an arrangement designed to put a Montrose at the door of Ambrose Vane on a schedule no one would ever question.

She wondered sometimes, dressing in the dark, whether Edmund had planned even then. Whether the contract had been a key left in a lock for a wife he never expected to use it.

Tonight the key turned.

She wore her good black wool, the one with the high collar, and her keeper's greatcoat over it because the wind came off the water like a blade that evening, straight and cold and certain of its business. In the cart beside her sat two gallons of kerosene in their brass-bound cans, exactly as invoiced, exactly as expected. The third thing in the cart was not on any invoice: a canvas satchel containing a shuttered storm lantern, a pry bar, and the page Jonah had carried through the storm — folded now, soft as cloth from handling, its marginal corrections in Edmund's cramped shorthand worn nearly to shadow.

Jonah stood at the gate with Peter Tresidder, the magistrate's son, both boys stiff with borrowed solemnity. She had rehearsed this part too many times; there was nothing left to say that rehearsal could improve.

"Back before the tide turns," she said.

"Yes, Mrs. Montrose." Jonah's voice was small and steady. Then, quieter, only for her: "The cellar has a door that pretends to be a wall. Third stone from the corner is loose. Mama showed me."

*Mama.* The word still landed in her chest like a dropped tool.

"Third stone," she repeated. "Go on, now."

He went. He did not look back, which was worse than if he had, because she knew he was learning not to — learning the keeper's trick of walking forward into weather without checking what followed. She turned the cart onto the cliff road and let the wind push her toward the lit windows of Vane House, and she thought: *I am not brave. I am simply too far along to stop.*

The dinner party glowed against the December dark like something stranded. Carriages in the forecourt, footmen in livery, windows throwing amber light across wet gravel — all of it paid for, she reminded herself, gripping the satchel strap, by ships that ran contraband under naval colors and by men who drowned other men when arithmetic required it. She handed the horse to a stable boy, presented her delivery docket to a footman who looked at her oil-stained knuckles and then at her face and found nothing worth remembering, and was directed around the side of the house to the cellars.

That was almost disappointingly easy. She should have known better. Nothing in Ambrose Vane's house was easy; some things were merely patient.

* * *

*Jonah*

The Tresidder house smelled of bread and ink. Jonah sat on a kitchen stool with his hands wrapped around a cup of cocoa so sweet it made his teeth ache, and Peter's mother asked him questions the way people drop stones down a well — gently, listening for the splash.

"And where did you live before? Before Mrs. Montrose found you."

"Big house," Jonah said. "Lots of stairs." He drank the cocoa. It was true and it was useless and that was the best kind of answer. Mrs. Tresidder nodded as though big houses and stairs were an adequate geography, and Peter pulled him upstairs to a room with a model schooner hanging from the ceiling and a tin of lead soldiers poured out across the floor like a battle already lost.

Peter arranged soldiers. Jonah watched the window instead. From here he could see the headland, or where the headland would be if the fog allowed it, and beyond the fog the light — his light now, hers, theirs — turning and turning. But tonight he was not watching for the light. Tonight his whole body was aimed at the dark shape of Vane House on the far shore of the bay, where Mrs. Montrose had gone with his mother's page folded in her coat.

"She'll be all right," Peter said, not looking up. "Your mum. My father says keeper's widows are the toughest breed in Cornwall. Says they've done their grieving already and everything after is interest."

Jonah didn't know what interest meant, in that sentence. He picked up a soldier, felt its weight, set its feet in the sand Peter had spread on a tray.

"My mama typed fast," he said. "Faster than anyone. She said words were the only things that couldn't be drowned."

Peter considered this the way he considered chess moves, jaw working. "Then she'd like Mrs. Montrose. Father says she writes a logbook like scripture."

Downstairs the clock struck eight. Jonah counted the strokes with his heart, and when they finished he said, very quietly, to the soldiers and the sand and the boy beside him: "She's at the cellar door now."

* * *

*Hazel*

The cellar of Vane House ran the whole length of the building, brick-floored and barrel-vaulted, stacked with wine, with cordage, with crates stenciled NAVY STORES — PORTLAND that made her teeth set hard enough to hurt. A cellarman showed her where the empty cans were to go, grumbled about women delivering oil, and climbed back up the stair to the warmth of the kitchen, leaving her alone among the shadows and the smell of paraffin and salt pork.

Third stone from the corner. The corner where the coal chute met the north wall.

Her hands knew the work before her mind caught up — she had pried frozen fittings loose on the gallery rail in January gales; a stone was nothing. It came away easier than it should have, the mortar crumbled like old bread, and behind it a cavity lined with oilcloth, and in the cavity a packet wrapped in sailcloth and tied with the kind of knot sailors tie and nobody else.

Edmund's knot. She knew it the way she knew his handwriting. Her fingers shook so badly she could barely work the fold.

Inside: two ledgers, bound in gray board, unmarked. She opened one by shuttered lantern-light and there it was — dates, ship names, cargo entries in Edmund's hand, and beside them, in a second hand, the true manifests. *Meridian, 14 March: wool, 400 bales.* And beneath, in pencil: *opium, 60 chests; rifles, 200, naval escort H.M.S.—*. Page after page. Winter after winter. Her husband's neat, doomed honesty preserved in a cellar while he himself went into the sea.

She had thought she was past weeping for Edmund. She learned otherwise, kneeling on another family's cold floor with his ledgers in her lap. She gave herself thirty seconds — she counted them, keeper's discipline — and then she wrapped the ledgers, hid them in the false bottom of her delivery basket beneath the oil cans' straw packing, and replaced the stone.

Which was when the cellar door opened above, and light spilled down the stair, and a voice she had heard only once before — at the funeral, offering condolences with his hat in his hands — said:

"I'm told my lamp-oil supplier is delivering personally tonight. At half past eight. On the longest night of the year." Footsteps on the stairs, unhurried. "Mrs. Montrose, I believe. Edmund Montrose's widow."

Ambrose Vane came down into the cellar light the way cold comes under a door. He was broader than she remembered, silvered, beautifully tailored, and his eyes went past her to the north wall — to the third stone from the corner, which she had replaced a half-inch proud of its neighbors — and then came back to her face, and she watched him decide, in real time, whether he had seen anything at all.

"Mr. Vane." She kept her voice level. Six years of shouting over engines and gales had given her a voice that carried without strain; she used its lower register now. "Your oil, sir. Two gallons, as invoiced. Your man signed the docket upstairs."

"He did." Vane stopped perhaps four feet from her. Close enough to shake hands. Close enough to strike. "Forgive me. I confess I asked for you to be shown down rather than kept waiting above. I wanted—" he smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced on better faces than hers— "I wanted to look at Edmund Montrose's widow. He worked for me, you know. Three years. A careful young man. Careful men are rare."

"He was careful," she agreed.

"The sea wasn't. It takes the careless and the careful alike, doesn't discriminate. Terribly democratic, the sea." His gaze drifted again to the wall, rested there a moment, returned. "You keep his light, I'm told. The lighthouse. You must have found his effects. Papers. A clerk brings his work home, however carefully."

The lie assembled itself somewhere below her thinking, in the same place courage lives, which may be the same place.

"Bills mostly," she said. "Oil invoices. Receipts for bootlaces. If there's anything you want, Mr. Vane, you're welcome to it — I keep his boxes in the store room and I've never had the heart to sort them. Come in spring, when the road's open, and take whatever a dead man owned that's of use to you."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not belief. Suspension of disbelief, purchased cheaply because the alternative was inconvenient before a houseful of guests. She understood then, with a clarity that frightened her more than discovery would have: he suspected, but suspicion cost him nothing tonight, and certainty would require effort, and powerful men defer effort until it suits them.

"Perhaps I shall," he said softly. "In spring."

He stood aside to let her pass. She gathered her basket — the weight of the ledgers dragging at her arm like a second child — and climbed the stairs with her spine held straight by pure spite, and at the top step his voice followed her up, pleasant as a host refilling a glass:

"Give the boy my regards, Mrs. Montrose. When you find him."

She did not turn around. She did not trust her face. She walked through the warm brilliant hallway, past laughing guests and candlelight and a string quartet tuning, out the front doors her own deliveries had never been permitted to use, down the steps into the wind — and only when she reached the stable yard, only with the horse between her hands and the gates ahead, did she allow herself to breathe.

*When you find him.*

He knew. He had known before she came. Which meant the roads, the harbor, every checkpoint between here and Truro, had gone from watchfulness to hunt sometime today — and the worst storm in ten years was due before midnight, and Jonah was a mile away behind a kitchen door that would not hold against determined men.

She harnessed the horse with fingers that would not quite obey.

* * *

*Jonah*

The knock came at nine o'clock, three blows, and Peter's mother went to the door with a candle, and Jonah's whole body went tight as wire — until he heard the voice outside, roughened by wind but unmistakable, the voice that smelled of lamp oil even through a closed door.

He was down the stairs faster than dignity allowed. Mrs. Montrose stood in the doorway with sleet in her hair and a red mark on her cheek where the wind had struck her, and she was looking at him the way she looked at the lamp when the weather turned — measuring, fierce, already calculating.

"Get your coat," she said. "Both of you. We're leaving tonight, all the way, and we're not stopping."

Behind her, through the doorway, the darkness over the sea had begun, very faintly, to groan.
