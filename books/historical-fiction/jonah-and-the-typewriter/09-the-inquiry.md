## 9. The Inquiry

The roads opened on a Tuesday, and the magistrate's son arrived before the mud had finished thawing.

Hazel saw him first from the lantern gallery — a small figure on the cliff path, leading a shaggy pony whose hooves sank to the fetlock in the saturated ground. Thomas Penhale, eleven years old and carrying a canvas satchel strapped across his chest like a soldier's pack. He had ridden through two nights from Truro, sleeping in barns, lying about his father's name at every toll, because a woman he'd met once in his father's study had looked him in the eye and asked him to be brave.

She went down to meet him at the door, and Jonah was there before her, flinging it open so hard it cracked against the wall.

"You came," Jonah said.

"Course I came." Thomas was wind-burned and filthy and trying very hard not to look frightened. He pulled the satchel around and held it out with both hands, the way one hands over something alive. "I didn't open it. Not once. Not even when the man at Bodmin asked what was in it and I said it was my schoolbooks and he believed me because—" he paused for breath, "—because nobody believes a boy would lie about schoolbooks."

Hazel took the satchel. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against the weather she could still smell on him, were Edmund Vane's ledgers — three of them, their spines cracked, Ambrose Vane's own initials tooled into the leather. Ships that never carried what they claimed. Naval papers forged by lamplight. Dates and manifests that matched, entry for entry, the half-page she had kept folded against her own heart since autumn.

"Your father," she said carefully, "does he know where you've been?"

Thomas looked at his boots. "He knows now. He read your letter." A pause. "He cried, Mrs. Montrose. I never saw him cry before."

* * *

Truro smelled of wet stone and coal smoke and other people's lives pressed close together, and after a winter on the rock the sheer density of the city unnerved Hazel — carts and voices and windows stacked upon windows, all of it indifferent to her. She wore her good black dress, the one she'd been married in nine years ago, let out at the seams because grief had not made her thinner but the winter had. The logbook rode in her bag like a heartbeat. She had not let it out of her hands since Penwith station.

The inquiry was held in the county hall, in a room designed for grander things than this — oak panelling gone dark with decades of pipe smoke, a ceiling painted with Tritons blowing conch shells over a sea that had never known a storm. Three commissioners sat behind a raised bench. Clerks sat below them with pens poised. And along the wall, in a chair placed as if by accident where every eye would find him, sat Ambrose Vane.

He was smaller than she remembered. That was the first shock. The second was that he looked *well* — rested, barbered, his grey suit cut close and correct, his hands folded on his knee like a man waiting patiently for a delayed train. He did not look at her when she entered. He looked through her, the way one looks through weather.

"He thinks he's already won," murmured the man beside her — an inspector from the Board of Trade, sent down from London, tired-eyed and kind. "They always do, at the start."

"I don't want him ruined," Hazel heard herself say, and it surprised her, how true it was. "I want the ships stopped. The rest is God's business."

The inspector studied her a moment. "You understand what you're walking into, Mrs. Montrose. Your ledgers are corroborated in part by the customs seizure at Falmouth. But the heart of this case — the connection between Vane's fleet and the naval cover, the forgery of protection papers — that rests on you. Your testimony. Your logbook. No clerk of yours will stand behind you. No documents of your own making. When you finish, his counsel is going to take you apart piece by piece, and there will be nothing between you and him but your voice."

"Yes," said Hazel. "That's rather the shape of my life."

* * *

They swore her in at half past ten, and the oaths echoed strangely in all that carved wood, and then she was in the chair and the room had gone very quiet, and she understood that this — this bare, stripped moment, no lamp, no rock, no keeper's authority to shelter behind — was the most exposed she had ever been. Not even the night of the storm, alone in the lantern room with the glass flexing in its frame. Then, at least, the sea had been honest about wanting to kill her.

She told it plainly. That was her only strategy, decided somewhere off the Cornish coast in the dark of the train: plainness. She told them about the child at her door, though counsel for Vane objected that hearsay was not evidence, and the commissioner overruled him with visible reluctance, saying the circumstances of discovery went to provenance. She produced the typed page. She identified the marginal corrections — small, slanted, precise — as the hand of her late husband, Edmund Montrose, and gave the dates of his employment at Vane & Sons, and watched Ambrose Vane's face while she did it.

Nothing moved in it. Nothing needed to. His stillness was itself a kind of answer, and she saw the senior commissioner see it too.

Then she opened the logbook, and this was the part she had rehearsed on the train with her lips moving silently over the words like a woman at prayer, and it went worse and better than rehearsal. Worse, because her voice failed entirely on one passage and she had to begin again, and the room waited without mercy until she could. Better, because when she found her footing, she found she had more than facts. She had a keeper's habit of exactness, drilled by eight years of writing down wind and weather and the passing of every light, and exactness has its own authority. She walked them through the nights. The *Cormorant* logged inbound with naval colours flying, cargo entered as coal, discharged at the old tin quay between two and four in the morning by men who did not behave like coal-heavers. The *Providence Girl*, listed as lost with all hands off Ushant — a loss Vane had mourned publicly, insured heavily — logged instead passing the Point light alive and darkened, running without lamps, on the fourth of November.

"She wasn't lost," Hazel said. "She was hiding. A ship hides either from a storm or from the law, gentlemen, and I have the weather record for the fourth of November. There was no storm."

A clerk's pen scratched and stopped. Somewhere behind her a chair creaked. And Ambrose Vane unfolded his hands at last and leaned forward, and spoke for the first time all morning, addressing the bench with the mild courtesy of a man correcting a ledger error.

"With respect, Commissioners — this woman keeps a lamp. She is not a surveyor of shipping, nor a customs officer, nor any manner of expert. What we are asked to accept is the word of a grieving widow, isolated for months, in the company of a child of unknown parentage, reading significance into entries she is not qualified to interpret. Grief does strange work on the mind. I say this with genuine sympathy—" and here he turned to look at her directly, at last, and his eyes were exactly as cold as she remembered from a cellar doorway in another life, "—Mrs. Montrose has suffered a great deal."

The room tilted slightly. This was the moment she had feared in forty different shapes on the train: not the argument, but the *tone* of it, reasonable and sorrowful, wrapping contempt in sympathy so neatly you couldn't grasp either. She felt Jonah's absence like a missing rib. She felt Edmund's letters burning all over again, ash on the winter wind, every scrap of proof that might have shielded her gone by her own choice.

And then she found she was not afraid.

It came to her whole, the way answers come in the lantern room — not reasoned but simply *there*. She rose halfway out of the chair, uninvited, and the commissioner did not stop her.

"You're right, Mr. Vane," she said. "I keep a lamp. I am not an expert in anything but what passes beneath it. But consider what that means. In eight years I have written down every vessel that rounded my headland — four thousand nights, gentlemen, and I could tell you the fog density on any of them. Mr. Vane's empire rests on the assumption that no one watches. But someone always watches. That is the entire profession. Someone sits in the cold and writes down what is true, whether or not anyone ever reads it." Her voice steadied as it went, filling the paneled room the way lamplight fills water. "You may call me a grieving widow. I am. You may say I have only my word. I do. But my word has kept ships alive for eight years, and no one aboard any of them ever asked to see my credentials."

Silence. The Tritons blew their conches overhead. And then the middle commissioner — the quiet one, the one she had privately decided was merely furniture — removed his spectacles and said, "The Board notes, for the record, that the Falmouth seizure includes manifests bearing the *Providence Girl*'s registry number, recovered two weeks ago, which correspond to the entry this witness has just described." He put the spectacles back on. "Corresponds exactly, in fact."

Vane's counsel asked for a recess. It was denied.

* * *

It was in the afternoon, while counsel for the Board was reading the Falmouth exhibits aloud in a voice gone dry with triumph, that the doors at the back of the hall opened.

Hazel turned with everyone else, and saw a woman come up the aisle — thin, grey-faced, wearing a coat too good for her present circumstances and a hat that had been mended with thread the same colour as the felt. She walked like someone who had decided, sometime during the journey, that turning back was no longer survivable, and was now operating on that decision alone.

"I have a statement," the woman said to the bailiff, and her voice carried further than she seemed to intend. "My name is Ruth Carne. I was secretary to Mr. Ambrose Vane for six years. I typed the page these proceedings have discussed. And I can tell you where the rest of it came from, and who taught Edmund Montrose where to hide the ledgers, and why Mr. Vane sent a child to drown on the north coast rather than let either of us talk."

At the respondent's table, something happened to Ambrose Vane's face. It did not collapse — men like him did not collapse. It simply, finally, stopped being maintained. The stillness drained out of it, and underneath was only an old man discovering that the tide had been going out for some time.

Hazel did not hear much of what Ruth Carne said. She found she was watching the woman's hands — capable hands, ink-stained at the cuticle, hands that had struck keys in a cellar by lamplight while a child slept against her shoulder — and thinking: *she smells of it too, I'd wager. Lamp oil. We always do, we watchers.*

* * *

They adjourned at six. The commissioners would deliberate through Friday; the newspapers had already gotten the shape of it, and outside the hall a crowd had gathered under the gas lamps, and someone recognized her and someone else shouted her name, and Hazel kept her head down and her bag tight against her ribs and walked toward the lodging house where Jonah was waiting with Thomas Penhale and a landlady paid to ask no questions.

But in the doorway of a chandler's shop across the street, a figure peeled away from the crowd and fell into step beside her. Ruth Carne. Up close she smelled of cheap soap over the ghost of ink, and her eyes had the raw, scrubbed look of someone who had wept everything out days ago and arrived at a terrible calm.

"They'll indict him," Carne said quietly, matching Hazel's pace. "Whatever the commissioners decide publicly, the Director of Prosecutions had a man in the gallery today. I know because he approached me this morning." She hesitated. "There's something else, Mrs. Montrose. Something I couldn't say in there, in front of them all. About the night Jonah was sent away. About what Vane's men were ordered to do with him — and why they didn't." She glanced sideways, and her voice dropped almost to nothing. "Someone on that boat disobeyed an order. And I think you should know who, before the papers find out first. Because it isn't over. He has brothers in the trade, and one of them has just learned your name."

The gas lamps hissed. Somewhere behind them, in the darkening hall, clerks were locking the ledgers away for the night.

"Walk with me," Hazel said. "We've a long way back to the light."
