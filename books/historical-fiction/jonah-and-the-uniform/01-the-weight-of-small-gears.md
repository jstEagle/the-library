## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

There is a particular sound a workshop makes at six in the morning before anyone has spoken: forty-odd clocks disagreeing about the hour, each one certain it alone is right. Master Corvin's shop on Lampwick Row had been making that argument for thirty years, and I had learned to read it the way sailors read weather. That morning in March of 1938, two clocks in the window had stopped overnight — a carriage clock and a bracket clock that had kept faithful time through two governments and one flood — and I remember thinking, with the clarity that comes before coffee, *that's an omen, if I believed in omens.*

I did not believe in omens. I believed in mainsprings. A thing stops because something in it has failed or worn through, and you can find it, and you can fix it, and that is the whole of the world's honesty as far as I was concerned. I was twenty-two years old, an apprentice clockmaker with ink-stained fingers and a mother's cheekbones that marked me on every street corner, and I believed absolutely in the repairability of everything.

This belief would take some beating out of me over the following months. But that morning I still had it whole.

The apprenticeship piece sat under a cloth on my bench where I'd left it the night before: a small regulator movement, eight days' running, deadbeat escapement, the kind of exercise Master Corvin called "the examination they give you before life gives you the real one." Every apprentice at the guild finished one. Most of them were ugly. Mine was going to be beautiful, which Corvin said was a flaw I'd have to grow out of, since beautiful things invite attention and attention, in his experience, was how trouble found your door.

"Plate's true," I murmured to it, lifting the cloth. "Jewels seated. Now we see whether your heart beats."

I set the balance cock, eased the pallets into engagement, and gave the winding key three careful turns. The escapement ticked once — hesitated — ticked again, and then settled into that dry whisper that clockmakers hear in their sleep. I sat very still and listened the way other people pray.

Behind me, the door to the back room opened and Master Corvin came shuffling out with his coat already half on, though it was barely past dawn. He was seventy-one that spring and his hands had begun to betray him — a tremor in the right one that he pretended not to notice and I pretended not to see. He could still diagnose a movement by ear from across the room. He could no longer hold a steady hand to a pivot. This arrangement of losses had made him quiet in a way I didn't like.

"You're oiling too heavy on the third wheel," he said, without looking.

"I'm oiling exactly enough on the third wheel."

"Hm." He poured himself coffee from the pot I'd set on the stove, which meant he'd smelled it from the back room and timed his entrance accordingly. My master was a great believer in appearing to arrive by coincidence. "Heavy hands make heavy clocks."

"Yes, Master."

"And don't 'yes, Master' me, girl, it makes me feel old."

"You are old."

"That makes me feel worse." He lowered himself into his chair by the window, the chair with the cushion shaped to his body after three decades, and looked out at Lampwick Row while the light came up gray over the rooftops. Vellmark mornings were all gray; the city sat in a river valley where fog collected like debt, and it burned off by nine if it burned off at all.

I went back to my piece. There is a stage in finishing where you do nothing but look — turn the movement under the loupe and hunt for the flaw you know is there because you haven't found it yet. I found it at half past seven: a burr on the escape wheel, so small it would have taken a year to matter and then killed the clock in a day. That's the whole trade in one sentence, if you want it. Everything fails slowly and then suddenly, and your job is to be present for the suddenly.

* * *

The customers started coming at nine, and with them came the census.

The first was Frau Hessler, who brought in a carriage clock that struck thirteen and stayed twenty minutes talking about it before she mentioned, in passing, as though remarking on the weather, "They say the forms come next month. The counting. You'll want your papers in order, dear, with your — well. With your circumstances."

"My circumstances," I said, "are a bench, a bed, and a master who won't let me finish a wheel without commentary."

"Mm," said Frau Hessler, who had known me since I was eleven and loved me in the way of people who express affection primarily through warnings. She paid for the repair in exact coin, counted twice, and left.

The second was a man I didn't know, a surveyor's clerk with a soft voice and new boots, who wanted a watch regulated and stood too long at the counter looking at the wall of ticking faces. "Big job ahead of you people," he said. "With the new law. They'll need every clock in the city agreeing."

"People have needed that for centuries," I said. "It's why we're here."

"Not like this, though." He took his watch, wound it once as if testing whether I'd returned it honest, and left without another word.

And the third was young Tomas Brandt from the bakery, who was seventeen and terrified and didn't bother hiding it, which made him the only honest customer of the morning. "Paloma. Paloma, my father says they're doing households first. Households and businesses. And there's — they ask about grandparents. All four. Names and places." He turned his cap in his hands. "You'd write down all four?"

"I'd write down what's true," I said, which was a lie, because even then I understood that some questions are designed so that truth is the wrong answer.

Tomas nodded and looked at his shoes, and I looked at mine, and neither of us said the word that hung between us — the word for people like us, the four-grandparents word, the classification word that had been creeping north through the border provinces all winter like frost through a wall. In Vellmark we told ourselves the river made a difference. Borders make people religious about geography. The river was a hundred meters wide and we believed in it the way drowning men believe in rope.

Afternoon came, and with it more clocks and fewer words. A mantel clock with a broken suspension spring. A pocket watch whose owner had died and whose son wanted it running "for sentiment" but haggled over the price anyway, which Corvin said was also sentiment, of a kind. And through it all, beneath it all, the low murmur: *have you heard, they say, my cousin in Harsfeld says, the forms come next month.*

Master Corvin heard none of the murmur, or appeared not to. He worked on a church clock gear too large for his tremor but not too large for filing, and he said nothing all afternoon except once, when the surveyor's clerk's words must still have been sitting in him: "A clock that lies is worse than no clock."

"Whose lie?" I asked. "The maker's or the owner's?"

He gave me a long look over his spectacles, the look he used when I'd asked a question bigger than the room. "Yes," he said, and went back to filing.

* * *

At seven, when the shutters were closed and the sign flipped, we did the thing we had done every evening since October, when the first decrees came down the river from the capital. We packed.

It takes perhaps forty minutes if you don't rush it, and we never rushed it, because rushing is how you forget things and forgetting is how you lose them. Corvin's tools went into two leather rolls that had belonged to his late wife's father, who had also been a clockmaker, who had also, family legend held, packed them nightly during some earlier trouble nobody named aloud. The good loupes went into a wooden case lined with felt. The reference books — Breguet, the guild manuals, the water-stained volume on complications that Corvin treated as scripture — went into oilcloth and then into the false bottom of the delivery trunk, which we had built ourselves one grim Sunday in November, sawing and gluing in silence like men building a coffin.

My own kit was smaller. Three screwdrivers, a pair of pliers, my files, my staking set, the loupe Corvin had given me at fourteen that was good but not his best — his best sat locked in a drawer upstairs and I had never once been allowed to touch it. My mother's ring went into the same pouch as my guild papers, because documents and keepsakes weigh nothing and both fit in a palm. That was the arithmetic we lived by now. Not what a thing cost. What a thing weighed, and whether it could be carried at a walk, at night, by someone who might be crying.

"The Hessler clock goes home tomorrow," Corvin said, wrapping the last of the benches. "Don't let her talk you into re-casing it. She'll try. She's been trying for six years."

"She'll want to discuss the census."

"Then charge her for the discussion. Time is what we sell." He paused, his hand flat on the bench top, and I watched him decide whether to say the next thing. He decided to. He always decided to; discretion was not among his vices. "You heard what they're asking. Grandparents."

"I heard."

"Your mother's people are in the records as river-folk. Unregistered." River-folk: the polite word, the word the city used for the families who had lived along the banks for two hundred years, trading and repairing and marrying across every line the cartographers drew, keeping their papers thin because papers cost money and the river asked fewer questions than the land did. "That will come up."

"It may."

"Don't 'it may' me. It will. So." He straightened, and for a moment the tremor in his hand was still, as if the subject itself steadied him. "When they come, you answer slow. Slow answers cost less than fast ones. And you come get me before you sign anything."

"Master—"

"*Before* you sign anything." He looked at me the way he looked at a movement he suspected of hiding a crack. "I've watched governments change the rules before, child. The rules change. The paperwork is how they make the changing look like your fault."

We finished packing. We ate bread and hard cheese standing at the counter like thieves in our own shop, and then Corvin climbed the stairs to his rooms and I banked the stove and stood a moment in the dark among the clocks.

Forty of them, ticking in the blackness, each one certain of its own hour. I thought about the surveyor's clerk. *They'll need every clock in the city agreeing.* It had sounded like small talk when he said it. Standing there in the dark it sounded like something else — like a warning delivered by a man too cautious to warn anyone, or like prophecy wearing the dull coat of bureaucracy. When all the clocks agree, someone has decided what time it is. And whoever decides what time it is decides when the trains leave, when the curfew falls, when the offices open to stamp the forms that sort the keepable from the removable.

I lit my candle and went up to bed. On the landing I paused, because through Corvin's door I could hear him moving — not settling, moving. Drawer by drawer, deliberate, unhurried. Checking his own packing. Or adding to it.

I lay awake a long time listening to the shop below, the forty small arguments about time going on without us, faithful in the dark whether anyone witnessed them or not. That was the part of the trade I loved most and could never explain to anyone: the faithfulness. A good movement doesn't care who owns it or what the government decrees. It releases its seconds one at a time, honestly, to everyone alike. I fell asleep believing I could spend my whole life inside that honesty, behind a counter, among gears, untouched by whatever the forms next month would ask.

In the morning there was a letter on the mat, and the letter had an official seal, and the seal was blue.

Blue was the color of the Observatory.
