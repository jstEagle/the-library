## 2. Summons from the Observatory

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which I have since decided is the sort of day governments choose for ruining things. Mondays are for hangovers and lies about the weekend; Wednesdays leave time to reconsider. Tuesday is decisive, and decisiveness is rarely merciful.

Master Corvin was at the counter when the postman's knock came, squinting through his loupe at a carriage clock that had belonged to three generations of a family named Hollis and would, with any luck, outlast all of them. He did not look up when I took the envelope from the tray. Official envelopes were arriving often enough that autumn to have stopped being an event — thin gray things stamped with the eagle-and-gear of the new Directorate of Records, each one a small tax on the heart.

"This one's heavier," I said.

"Everything official is heavier." He set down his tweezers and held out his hand, then thought better of it and nodded for me to open it myself. His hands had been shaking for two years now — not all the time, but in the mornings, and lately in the afternoons too, and always when it mattered. It is a cruel joke of anatomy that the disease chose a clockmaker's hands. Or perhaps not a joke at all. Perhaps anatomy reads the same newspapers we do and knows where to strike.

The paper inside was thick, cream-colored, expensive. Expensive paper from a government office is never good news; cheap paper means they don't care enough to impress you.

*By order of the Directorate of Civic Timekeeping,* it began, in the flat bureaucratic hand that seems designed to make even sentences about weather sound like indictments, *the workshop of A. Corvin, licensed horologist of Vellmark, is hereby directed to dispatch a qualified technician to the Municipal Observatory without delay, there to assess and repair the master regulator, currently in a state of failure.*

"In a state of failure," Corvin repeated when I read it aloud. "That's one way to describe it."

"You knew?"

"I know everything that happens to clocks in this city, girl. The master regulator stopped Sunday night. Or rather—" he corrected himself, as he always did when precision mattered, "—it didn't stop. It began losing. Badly. Six minutes by Monday noon, eight by evening. Nobody's admitting it because nobody admits a thing like that until they've hanged someone for it."

I looked at the letter again. *Without delay.* Underneath, the signature: a name I didn't recognize above a title I did — Director of Synchronization, which sounds like something out of a bad novel and was, in the Vellmark of 1938, simply a job. There was a second paragraph I read twice before understanding it fully.

It said: *This summons constitutes designation of priority service under the Census Compliance Act. Noncompliance will be noted.*

"They've tied it to the census," I said.

Corvin took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose, and for a moment he looked every year of his sixty-three. "Of course they have. Everything's tied to the census now. You can't buy sausage without someone asking where your grandmother was baptized."

* * *

You should understand what the master regulator was, because I did not, fully, until that week. Every apprentice learns about it the way children learn about the sun — as a fact of the sky, too large and too permanent to think about. The observatory sat on Kessel Hill at the city's eastern edge, and inside it, in a room kept at a temperature regulated to a fraction of a degree, ticked the clock by which every other clock in Vellmark set itself. The stationmaster's chronometer. The exchange bell. The factory sirens, the church towers, the telegraph office. When a jeweler adjusted a watch in my master's shop, he was, knowingly or not, adjusting it to whatever the regulator said, and whatever the regulator said was whatever the heavens said, or close enough that no one had ever been entitled to argue.

Which meant that when it failed, the whole city drifted. Not all at once — that would have been noticed, shouted about, fixed in a day. It drifted the way we drift, gradually and then suddenly, each institution quietly trusting its own error until the errors disagreed with each other. Corvin said trains had been leaving the station by guesswork since Monday morning. He said it the way he said most important things, quietly, into a movement he was working on, so that I could pretend not to hear if I wished.

"Why us?" I asked. "There are four other shops."

"There are four other shops whose masters aren't the best in Vellmark." He said it without vanity, the way you'd report the weather, and it was true, and being true made it worse. "And three of those masters are members of the Loyalty Register. They'll be offered the work and they'll refuse it, politely, because repairing government time in this season is like defusing a shell — honorable only if nothing goes off." He picked up his tweezers, considered them, put them down again. His right hand gave its small treacherous shiver. "They want the best hands available. Unfortunately for both of us, the best hands are twenty-four years old and classified as — what is it this week? Second-category persons of mixed descent, pending verification."

"Pending," I said. "I like pending. Pending has possibilities."

"Don't be clever with me." But there was no heat in it. There hadn't been heat in his scolding for months, and I missed it more than I would have guessed.

* * *

We talked it through over supper, which in our shop meant bread, hard cheese, and pickles eaten standing at the workbench because neither of us had ever acquired the habit of sitting down to eat like people with futures. Outside, the lamplighter was making his rounds early — the Directorate had ordered lights lit before dusk now, part of some directive about civic visibility that nobody explained and everybody obeyed. From the window I could see the notice board on the corner where the new census regulations were posted, the paper gone soft at the edges from rain and from people touching it the way you touch a sore tooth.

"If I go," I said, "and I do it well, it looks good. On the record. Priority service. Maybe it buys us something when the classification comes through."

"And if you go and do it badly—"

"I won't do it badly."

"No," he agreed. "You won't. That's what frightens me." He wiped pickle brine from his fingers and looked at me with the expression he reserved for movements that were beautiful but wrong — a tourbillon in a cheap case, say, all show and no torque. "Paloma. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once and then we're going to speak of tools. The observatory is not a workshop. It's a stage. Whatever you do in that building will be watched by men whose entire profession is writing down what people do. You will finish the job, and you will finish it fast, and you will ask nothing — not about the clock's history, not about who requested us, not why a regulator maintained by the Directorate itself failed in the same month they needed an excuse to station people there. Nothing."

"Clocks fail," I said. "It's practically their vocation."

"Clocks fail," he said. "Governments arrange for clocks to fail. I've lived under three of them in this city and I will tell you the difference between them: the first ignored time, the second sold it, and this one worships it. And when a government starts worshipping something, it starts sacrificing to it, and the sacrifices are always people, and the priests are always clerks." He turned back to the bench, and his shoulders did the thing they did when he'd said more than he intended. "Finish fast. Ask nothing. Be home before dark."

"And if the repair takes longer than dark?"

He was quiet a moment. In the case before him the little Hollis carriage clock ticked on, indifferent, faithful, stupid in the way only perfect mechanisms can afford to be.

"Then be home before anyone notices you were gone," he said.

* * *

I packed the tool roll that night with more care than I had ever packed it, and here I must confess something that will seem small until you remember what the world was like: I enjoyed it. There, I've written it. Amid the dread and the arithmetic of danger, some traitor part of me was *glad*. The summons was addressed to our shop by name — *the workshop of A. Corvin* — but everyone in Vellmark's trade knew whose hands actually moved beneath that name now, and the letter was an acknowledgment, in cold official ink, that the work I did mattered. That it mattered to precisely the men I feared most did not, apparently, dull the pleasure. Pride is an ungrateful organ. It accepts blood supply from anyone.

I laid out the pieces on felt: the escapement files, the oilers with their goose-neck tips, the staking set, the loupe Corvin had given me at the end of my third year — a plain German glass, nothing like his finest, but mine. I wound my travel chronometer against the shop regulator and found it half a second proud. I adjusted it. Then I sat looking at my hands, flat on the felt, and thought about what they were about to touch, and whether the touching of it would be recorded anywhere besides the job sheet.

Corvin appeared in the doorway in his nightshirt, holding a candle, looking like a painting of a clockmaker that someone had left out in the weather.

"The observatory keeps its own records," he said. "Whatever you sign there, sign small. Initials, not names."

"Yes, Master."

"And Paloma." He hesitated. I waited. With Corvin the unsaid sentence always weighed more than the said ones, and I have wondered for years afterward which sentence it was he swallowed that night. If you'd asked me then, I'd have guessed it was something practical — *beware the assistant director*, or *check the temperature compensator first*. Now I think it was simpler and worse. Now I think it was something like *I am sorry to be sending you where I cannot follow*, because his hands had already resigned, weeks before either of us said the word, and he knew it, and pride was a family trait in the Whitlock line of teaching that ran straight into mine.

"Sleep," he said instead. "Steady hands win arguments."

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of river. I walked east through streets that were already rehearsing their obedience — shopkeepers angling their new compliance placards just so, two men from the Records Directorate measuring the doorway of the tailor's on Wexford Lane for reasons they declined to share with the tailor. At the foot of Kessel Hill I stopped and looked up. The observatory dome caught the first weak light and held it, and somewhere inside that white stone drum the great regulator was losing seconds like a man losing blood — steadily, silently, and, if no one intervened, fatally.

I climbed the hill. My bag was heavy with tools and my chest was heavy with the particular mixture that I would come to know very well over the following weeks: fear and vanity in equal parts, shaken, not stirred. At the top, a uniformed porter checked my letter against a list, checked my face against my papers, and waved me through into a marble hall where my footsteps came back to me doubled, as though someone else were walking beside me, keeping pace, taking notes.

The head keeper met me at the stair. He was brisk, relieved, incurious in the way of a man who wants the problem solved and the solvers gone before anyone asks how the problem came to be. "The regulator room," he said, leading upward. "It's all yours. Sign the access log on your way in."

The log lay open on a lectern by the door. Above my blank line were three days of entries in a small, upright, unusually legible hand — not the keeper's, not any of the staff's. Someone had been visiting the dead clock, morning and evening, entering times and initials like a doctor checking a dying patient. The last entry was dated that very morning, barely an hour old.

And as I stood there with my pen hovering over the empty line, I heard footsteps on the stair behind me — unhurried, even, the steps of a man who has never once in his life needed to run — and the head keeper said, with a note in his voice I couldn't yet read:

"Ah. The census taker is early."
