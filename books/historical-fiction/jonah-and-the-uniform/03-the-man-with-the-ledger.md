## 3. The Man with the Ledger

The observatory sat on Kessel Hill like a hat someone had set down and forgotten, and I climbed toward it that October morning feeling like an insect crawling up the brim of the world.

I had taken the tram as far as it went, then walked the rest, my toolbag swinging heavy against my hip. Master Corvin's parting words were still lodged in my ears like a splinter: *Finish fast. Ask nothing. Sign nothing you don't have to.* He had pressed three silver thalers into my palm besides the tram fare, which from Corvin was an act of generosity so unprecedented I nearly checked his forehead for fever.

"The clock is dying," he'd said, when I asked why they couldn't send someone else — anyone else — because surely the city had a dozen journeymen who would leap at observatory work. "And dying clocks make strange demands. They want whoever they want." He said it the way he said most things, as if quoting some ancient text on horology that only he had read.

So there I was, my breath fogging in the thin hill air, the city spread out below me in its autumn colors, all those rooftops and chimneys and little lives going about their business according to a clock that had stopped keeping honest time three weeks ago. You could see the whole of Vellmark from up there. You could see how small everything was. I remember thinking that this must be why governments loved high places — not for the view, but for the way it made people look like arithmetic.

A guard at the iron gate examined my letter of summons twice, once with his eyes and once with his finger moving under the printed lines like a man following a recipe he didn't trust. Then he waved me through with the particular contempt guards reserve for people carrying tools rather than weapons, as though a screwdriver were merely an ambition that hadn't found its purpose yet.

The interior smelled of cold stone and lamp oil and, beneath it, the faint metallic sweetness of old brass — a smell I knew better than my own mother's kitchen. Every serious clockroom smells this way. It is the smell of patience under pressure.

The keeper's assistant, a nervous young man named Pell whose collar was too tight for his neck, led me down a spiral stair into the clock chamber itself, talking all the way about how grateful the administration was, how urgent the matter was, how the Director had personally authorized unrestricted access.

"Unrestricted," I repeated. "That's generous."

"It's necessary." Pell lowered his voice as though the walls might inform on him. Which, given the year, they might. "Nothing in Vellmark runs without this clock. The trains, the mail, the factory shifts, the court sessions. When it stopped—" He made a vague gesture suggesting collapse, famine, the end of civilization. "People set their watches by the church bells instead. The church bells! Do you know how inaccurate—"

"I repair church clocks," I said. "Sometimes."

He had the grace to look embarrassed.

And then we were in the chamber, and I forgot him entirely, because the master clock of Vellmark rose out of the floor like something between a grandfather clock and a cathedral pillar — three meters of oak case and glass, brass wheels visible behind the panes like organs in a chest, a pendulum hanging motionless for the first time in forty years. Dead. It looked dead in a way furniture doesn't; it looked *stopped*, which is worse, the way a stopped watch on a dead man's wrist is worse than any empty holster.

"Well," I said to it. "What did you do to yourself?"

Pell cleared his throat behind me. "You'll find the maintenance logs in the—"

"I'll find the problem first. Logs lie. Gears don't."

I opened the case panel and put my hands inside the movement, and I will confess something now that I confessed to no one then: I was happy. For perhaps two full minutes, standing in a government building under a regime I distrusted, with a census law folding the city into lists like laundry, I was simply and completely happy, because the great wheel was misaligned by less than the width of a hair and the escapement's pallets had worn into shallow scoops and nobody in the world knew it but me. There is a privacy to diagnosis. The machine tells you its secrets and no one else can hear.

That was when the man came down the stairs.

I heard him before I saw him — measured steps, unhurried, the kind of walk that expects doors to be held open. Then he appeared at the bottom of the spiral with a leather ledger under one arm and a briefcase in hand, dark coat, fair hair combed with more attention than the morning strictly required, and a face built for official neutrality. He had the look of a man who had practiced looking like nothing in a mirror until he got good at it.

"Brand," Pell said, with the enthusiasm of a man greeting a tax. "You're early."

"Census takers are always early." The man set his briefcase on a side table and surveyed the room. "It's the late ones who end up in reports."

His eyes found me then — I was still elbow-deep in the clock's vitals, sleeves rolled, a smudge of oil somewhere along my jaw that I could feel like a brand of its own. He looked at me the way I'd just been looking at the escapement. Assessing. Reading wear patterns.

"You're the clockmaker," he said.

"I'm the clockmaker's apprentice."

"You're alone."

"My master's hands shake. The administration prefers precision to seniority, apparently." I pulled my arm free of the movement and wiped it on a rag. "Paloma Underhill."

"Underhill." He took out the ledger, opened it, and wrote my name in a hand so neat it was almost hostile. Then he paused, pen hovering. "Underhill is a Palatine name."

"And Brand is a border name. We're both very far from wherever our names started."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile that had heard rumors of itself.

"Lineage will be addressed on form eleven-C," he said. "Which is not today."

"Then today we're just two people who dislike each other professionally."

"I don't dislike you yet. I haven't had time." He clicked his briefcase open and began removing folders with the ceremony of a surgeon laying out instruments. "I'm here to verify the observatory's staff records against the new registry. You may ignore me entirely."

This proved impossible, but I gave it an honest attempt. I worked. He worked. The chamber filled with the small sounds of two kinds of precision — the click of my calipers, the scratch of his pen, the tick of Pell's nervous throat-clearing until Pell finally fled upstairs and left us alone with the dead giant and each other.

It took him forty minutes to break.

I counted. That's what apprenticeship does to you; you count everything. Forty minutes of watching me dismount the escapement bridge before he set down his pen and drifted over with the studied casualness of a man pretending he wasn't drift-ing at all.

"That tooth," he said, nodding at the escape wheel I held up to the lamplight. "It's chipped."

"It's worn. Chipped teeth are accidents. Worn teeth are biographies." I tilted it so the light caught the metal. "Every tooth on this wheel has been letting time out in tiny amounts for forty years. Someone decided long ago exactly how much imprecision this city could tolerate per second, and built it in."

"That sounds like a criticism of engineering."

"It's a compliment to engineering. Imprecision, controlled, is the whole art. Anyone can make a clock run. Making it run *wrong* by exactly the right amount — that takes character."

He considered this longer than a man considering horology should. "You talk like the machines are people."

"People pretend to be machines. It seems only fair to extend the courtesy."

The almost-smile again, closer to actual this time. He returned to his table. I went back to my wheel. And here is where I should record, honestly, what I felt, because I have promised myself this account will be honest even where it is unflattering: I watched his hands. He had careful hands, deliberate, and I noticed them the way you notice anything well-made, and I told myself that was all it was — professional appreciation of craftsmanship in unexpected places. My mother used to say that about my father. Professional appreciation. It ran in the family, apparently, this habit of dressing fascination in work clothes.

Around noon, Pell sent down bread and cold sausage on a tray, and we ate standing on opposite sides of the clock like two armies observing a truce over a neutral mountain range.

"They say the clock broke on its own," he remarked, tearing his bread with unnecessary precision. "But the maintenance log shows the last service was signed off six months early. Someone wanted it healthy on paper."

"Someone wanted their bonus on paper, more likely. Clockmakers are capable of ordinary sins. We're not all poets of sabotage."

"I didn't suggest sabotage."

"You thought it loudly."

He chewed, unhurried. "Do you always tell officials what they're thinking?"

"Only the ones who ask questions like confessions."

That landed somewhere. I saw it land. He looked down at his bread and I understood, with the sudden clarity that sometimes arrives uninvited, that he hadn't come to the observatory only for staff records — or rather, that he had, but that being here troubled him in a way he hadn't sorted out yet, and my sharpness was finding grooves in him that had already been worn.

I softened whether I meant to or not. "The clock failed honestly," I said. "Brass fails honestly. That's the one nice thing about it."

"Humans?"

"Humans fail creatively."

He laughed then — one short syllable, surprised out of him, immediately regretted. He glanced up the stairwell as though the laugh might be climbing back down with witnesses.

We worked through the afternoon. He verified records; I rebuilt the pallets. And every hour or so he would wander past my workbench on some transparent errand — consulting a folder he'd left deliberately nearby, examining the wall, studying the pendulum as though it owed him testimony — and every time, he would linger a moment too long watching my hands move.

"You're staring," I informed him, the fourth time.

"I'm verifying."

"Verifying what?"

"That you are who the summons says you are. An apprentice. Alone. Qualified." He picked up Corvin's loupe from the bench — my cheap one, not the good one — and turned it over. "This is apprentice equipment. But the work isn't apprentice work."

"My master says talent is just stubbornness with better tools."

"Your master sounds like a man who's been investigated before."

The accuracy of this stopped me for half a second. I covered it by reaching for a screw. "My master sounds like every clockmaker over sixty. We're a suspicious trade. We assume everything is wound too tight and will eventually spring."

"And are you? Wound too tight?"

"Ask me at midnight. That's usually when it shows."

Another glance toward the stairs. Another almost-smile, this one with something apologetic folded into it, as though he were sorry for every regulation that made him check the stairwell before allowing himself a reaction.

Near dusk, he packed his folders back into the briefcase with the same surgical ceremony, and stood a moment with his hand resting flat on the closed case, like a man taking an oath or suppressing an impulse.

"The escapement," he said, nodding at the parts laid out on my cloth. "How long until she runs?"

"'She' now. This morning you called it a mechanism."

"This morning I hadn't watched anyone care about something for six straight hours."

I had no answer prepared for that, so I gave none. He seemed to expect none. He collected his coat from the chair, and at the foot of the stairs he paused.

"I'll be back Thursday. Form eleven-C requires the clockmaker's personal details entered into the registry. Lineage, residence, classification." He said it flatly, officially, but his eyes stayed on mine a beat past official. "You'll want your documents in order."

"I keep my documents in a drawer marked *documents*. I'm very organized."

"Underhill." He said my name like he was testing the weight of it. "Thursday. Don't be late."

"Census takers are always early."

The almost-smile arrived, stayed a full second this time, and then he climbed the stairs and was gone, his footsteps fading into the stone, and I stood alone in the chamber with the dead clock and realized my heart was doing something arrhythmic that no escapement I'd ever serviced would have tolerated.

I turned back to the movement and made myself diagnose the great wheel all over again, hands steady, mind elsewhere.

Thursday, then. Form eleven-C. Lineage, residence, classification.

I would spend the next three days discovering exactly how many ways there are to dread a single word on a government form — and how much stranger the dread becomes when part of you is hoping the man holding the pen comes back at all.

* * *

Master Corvin listened to my report that night with his hands wrapped around a mug of tea he never drank, and when I reached the part about the census taker who lingered to watch me work, he was quiet long enough that one of his shop clocks ticked twice before he spoke.

"Brand," he said. "Elias Brand."

"You know him?"

"No." He set the mug down with exaggerated care. "But I know the type. Careful men are either the safest kind or the worst kind, girl, and there's no telling which until it's too late to matter." He looked at me across the lamplit bench, and his eyes, milky at the edges now, were sharper than his hands would ever be again. "Which do you think he is?"
