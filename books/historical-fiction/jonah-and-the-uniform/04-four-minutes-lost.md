## 4. Four Minutes Lost

There is a particular kind of silence that a government building makes when it has decided not to answer you. I had heard it before — in the records annex on Kessler Street, where the clerks kept their voices at a volume suitable for funerals, and in the transport ministry, where the telephones rang and rang with the specific patience of machines that knew no one intended to pick up. The observatory had its own version. It was the silence of a thing that considered itself above explanation.

I stood in the dome room with my ledger under my arm and listened to it not tick.

The master clock of Vellmark was supposed to be the loudest honest thing in the city. Everyone said so. You could set your watch by it from the harbor to the freight yards, they said, and they did say it, constantly, the way people repeat things they have decided to believe rather than things they have checked. In truth most citizens had never seen the clock. They knew it the way they knew the moon — as an assumption, something that would continue its business whether or not they looked up.

Its business had stopped.

I walked around it slowly, the way I had been trained to walk around anything that might be evidence. That was the whole of my profession, when you reduced it: walking around things, looking at them correctly, and writing down what the Ministry of Records required to be true. Elias Brand, census taker, second grade. My stamp was brass and my salary was modest and my conscience, until recently, had been the flattest surface in Vellmark.

The clock itself was a magnificent corpse. It stood better than two men tall, a walnut case gone dark with decades of polishing hands, its face a white enamel disc cracked in one hairline place like old porcelain. Behind the glass, the pendulum hung dead still — and there is something obscene about a still pendulum, something that puts a cold finger against the base of the throat. A stopped watch is a private grief. A stopped master clock is a public one. This machine did not merely tell time; it *issued* it. Every railway departure, every factory shift whistle, every official document timestamped in this city drew its authority, however indirectly, from the swing of that rod.

And the rod was not swinging.

I did what any careful man does first. I looked around for someone to blame.

* * *

Her name was Underhill — the apprentice — and I had watched her for the better part of an hour before speaking to her, which is a thing I told myself I did for professional reasons. She worked with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her tools laid out on a cloth with the kind of geometric devotion that would have moved a drill instructor to tears. She had not noticed me, or she had noticed me and decided I belonged to the furniture, which was worse, or better. I hadn't decided.

She was lying half inside the clock's movement chamber, and she was talking to it.

Not loudly. Not insanely. The low, continuous muttering of a person negotiating with a superior who refuses to be reasonable.

"You are not," she was saying, "going to do that."

I coughed. She extracted herself from the mechanism with the unhurried dignity of a cat leaving a room it regrets entering, and looked me over: the coat, the ledger, the stamp case in my breast pocket. I watched her perform the identification in about a second and a half. Census taker. Category: nuisance, possibly threat. I saw the assessment close behind her eyes like a shutter.

"The observatory is closed to unregistered visitors," she said.

"The observatory is registered," I said. "I've just come from the registration office downstairs. The building itself is in order. It's the people in it we're less sure about."

"How reassuring. For the building."

I held up my credentials and she examined them with more suspicion than most people gave to counterfeit money, which flattered me slightly. Elias Brand, it said, Ministry of Records, authorized to conduct verification of persons and premises under the New Enumeration Act. The Act had gone into force eleven days earlier, and already it had changed the texture of ordinary conversations all over Vellmark. People now spoke the way they crossed icy streets — weight forward, eyes down, no sudden movements.

"Paloma Underhill," she said finally, because I was waiting, and giving your name to a census taker had become either an act of compliance or a small declaration of war depending on how you held your mouth when you did it. She held hers carefully neutral. "Apprentice to Master Corvin, Clockmakers' Guild, third bench. If you're here to count me, count me. If you're here to count the clock, it's dead, and you'll need a bigger form."

"Is it dead? Or merely resting?"

"It's dead. The recoil escapement failed Tuesday night — the pallet stones wore past their engagement and the escape wheel ran free until the weights bottomed out. Which means—" she pointed at the pendulum with a pair of tweezers, a gesture of pure disgust, "—for approximately sixty-one hours, this machine hasn't been wrong. It simply hasn't been."

"And the difference matters?"

"The difference, Herr Brand, is the difference between a lie and an absence." She said it without heat, the way you'd explain rain to a child. "A wrong clock can be argued with. An absent one leaves everyone guessing. Half the city is setting its watches by the cathedral bell now, and the cathedral bell is rung by a sexton who reads the time off his own wristwatch, which he last calibrated in spring."

I wrote none of this down, though my hand twitched toward the ledger out of pure reflex. She noticed the twitch. I could tell she noticed, and I could tell she filed it away, the way I filed things away, and for a moment we were two professionals recognizing each other's trade across a dead machine.

"You're the one sent to repair it," I said. "Underhill. Corvin's shop, on the Weavers' Lane."

"I'm the one sent to repair it." She turned back to her cloth of tools. "You'll want to note that I began work yesterday morning and expect to require several days. You'll want to note the parts must be made by hand; nothing off a shelf fits a movement this old. You'll want to note a great many things, I imagine, so I'll leave you to it."

It was a dismissal. I did not take it, partly from contrariness and partly because — though I would have needed several days and better light to admit it — I did not want to leave the room while she was in it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about working for the Ministry of Records: you spend years believing that paper is inert. Paper sits in drawers. Paper waits. Paper is the patient, harmless sediment of other people's lives. Then one day you follow a sheet of paper to its conclusion and find that it has grown teeth somewhere in the filing system, and by the time you hear the growling it has already closed its jaw on someone's street address.

My work that week was simple enough to be sinister. Verify the observatory's staff roster. Confirm residence registrations for the district. Cross-reference against the new classification schedules. Tick, tick, tick — I was, I realized, standing in that dome room, only another escapement. The Ministry released me in controlled increments through the machinery of the state, and every release advanced some wheel I never saw.

The clock's failure, I learned from the registrar downstairs, had consequences that radiated outward like cracks in ice. The harbourmaster could not coordinate the tide tables with the shipping manifests. The hospital's shift changes had collapsed into argument. And the railway —

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only honesty available to a man holding a falsifiable document, and I am trying, in this account, to be honest even where it costs me.

The railway.

The Vellmark Central Yard sequenced its departures off the observatory signal — a telegraph pulse sent on the hour and half hour, corrected daily against the master clock. With the clock dead, the yard had reverted to local stationmasters' estimates, and the estimates disagreed with each other by margins ranging from three minutes to eleven. Freight sat on sidings. Passenger platforms filled with people reading timetables that no longer described reality. And among those trains, waiting in the confusion like sharks in churned water, were the special transports. The scheduled ones. The ones whose departures, under the new Act, were timed to the minute and justified to no one.

If the clock came back, the schedule would resume. Cleanly, efficiently, on the minute. Trains would leave when the paper said they left, carrying whom the paper said they carried.

I stood in the corridor outside the dome room with my ledger open and my pencil sharp, and I understood, with the clarity that comes only at the worst moments, exactly what my next entry could do.

*Report:* master timepiece non-operational since the fourteenth; repair in progress; estimated restoration within five days. Upon verification of completion, standard synchronization to resume.

That was the accurate entry. That was the entry a good civil servant writes. And if I wrote it — if I wrote it and forwarded it through channels, as procedure demanded — then somewhere down the chain a transport officer would breathe a sigh of relief, ink a revised timetable, and the machinery of removal would begin again, punctual and blameless, each gear engaging the next.

Or.

Or I could file nothing. Not falsify — I wasn't there yet, I want that on the record, whoever's record survives — simply *delay*. Note that inspection of the observatory's timekeeping installation was incomplete. Pending further review. The phrase was beautiful in its bureaucratic emptiness; it could sit in a drawer for a week without anyone asking why, because everyone at the Ministry had more drawers than time.

I told myself it was procedure. I have always been able to tell myself things; it is my single greatest talent and I suspect it will be the death of me. *Pending further review* is a legitimate category. A conscientious official does not certify an instrument he has not fully inspected. The master clock was not yet repaired; therefore the synchronization could not yet be verified; therefore the report could not yet be filed. I was not obstructing anything. I was being thorough. The trains' confusion was not my confusion. The delay was the clock's fault, and the clock's fault was metallurgy, and metallurgy was God's department.

Every link in that chain of reasoning was true and the chain as a whole was a lie, and I stood there in the smell of dust and machine oil holding the difference in my hands like a coin I had found on the pavement and did not intend to turn in.

* * *

She found me an hour later, or came looking for me — I never established which, and the ambiguity mattered more than I let on. I was sitting on the observatory's exterior steps in the late light, the ledger closed on my knee, doing an impression of a man thinking about weather.

"They told me you hadn't filed," she said. No greeting. She stood a step above me, arms crossed, so that she had the rare advantage of height and used it.

"Filed what?"

"Don't. Whatever we are to each other, we're not stupid to each other. The registrar expected your verification report by four. It's nearly seven. Either you're incompetent, or you're slow, and you don't move like an incompetent man."

"You've known me six hours."

"Long enough." She came down one step. "What happens when you write it down? What actually happens, downstream, when the record says the clock runs?"

I looked up at her. The question was a door left open a hand's width, and we both knew that doors like that got people shot, and we both stood there anyway.

"Schedules resume," I said.

"That's all?"

"That's never all."

She studied me for a long moment, and whatever she was looking for, she apparently found enough of it, because she nodded once, curtly, and turned back toward the door. At the threshold she paused.

"The escapement has to come out entirely," she said. "It'll take me most of a week. Perhaps longer, if I'm careful." A beat. "One wants to be careful, with a mechanism this important."

Then she was gone inside, and I sat on the steps until the streetlamps came on, and in my ledger, under the day's date, I wrote: *Inspection of observatory timekeeping installation — ongoing. Further review required.*

Nine words. The first lie of omission in a career built on inclusion. I blotted it, squared the pages, and told myself — thoroughly, conscientiously, pending further review — that I had done nothing at all.

Above me, through the dome's ribs, I could see her lamp still burning in the clock room, and beyond it, invisible but everywhere, a whole city running on guesses, three minutes adrift, five, eleven, nobody knowing whose watch to trust — and for the first time in my flat, orderly life, I found that I did not want the guessing to end.

I would pay for that sentence eventually. But that night it was simply true, and I filed it nowhere at all.
