## 5. The Question

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which I have always considered the most bureaucratic of days. Mondays still carry the smell of the weekend; Wednesdays have the dignity of the hump; but Tuesdays exist solely for forms and summons and men in gray coats who want to know your business. So it was fitting, I suppose, that the Census Verification Office chose a Tuesday to send Elias Brand to my door.

I was at the bench, elbow-deep in a carriage clock with a temperamental balance spring, when Corvin's head came up from his own work like an old hound catching a scent. Two sharp knocks. Not the apologetic tap of a customer with a broken watch — the measured, official knock of someone who has been instructed to knock exactly twice.

"Underhill," Corvin said quietly, which was unnecessary, since there was no one else in the room it could be, but old men like to say names when they are afraid of them.

I wiped my hands on the leather apron — a useless gesture, since my fingers would never be clean enough for this kind of visitor — and opened the door.

Elias Brand stood on our step holding a ledger under his arm the way a priest holds scripture, except that scripture doesn't have carbon paper. He wore his census taker's badge slightly crooked, as though he had pinned it while walking and couldn't be bothered to fix it, and his expression was the particular blankness of a man who has learned that his face is government property.

"Apprentice Underhill." He consulted a card he clearly did not need to consult. "Master Corvin's establishment. I'm here for household verification. It will take ten minutes."

"It took you four words longer than necessary to say that."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the ghost of one that had been detained at the border. "May I come in?"

"You're going to whether I say yes or no."

"Yes," he agreed, and stepped past me into the workshop.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being sorted: it doesn't feel like anything dramatic. There are no trumpets. No men with measuring calipers taking the shape of your skull. There is only a thin man at your workbench, moving your polishing cloths aside with two fingers as if they might contaminate him, laying out a form with printed boxes, and asking you questions that begin innocently and end somewhere over a cliff.

He asked the address, which he wrote. He asked how many persons resided at the premises, which was two, which he wrote. He asked Master Corvin's full name, date of birth, trade classification, and Corvin gave each answer in the flat voice of a man reciting his own inventory, and Elias wrote it all in a hand so neat it looked typeset.

I stood by the window and pretended to be furniture. This is a skill apprentices develop early — the ability to occupy a room without doing so — and I flattered myself I was executing it well until Elias said, without looking up:

"And the apprentice. Full name?"

"Paloma Underhill."

"Parents' names?"

"Marta Underhill. Deceased."

"Father?"

The word sat in the room the way a dropped gear sits on a floor — small, hard, impossible to ignore. I watched Elias's pen hover above the box marked *Father, Name*. The pen did not move.

My father's name was Andrés Vela. He had been gone eleven years, and in eleven years I had built a careful architecture around his absence: he lived in no photographs, appeared in no stories Corvin told customers, existed in no document within city limits. My mother had married a watchman from the eastern districts who died before I could remember him, and after her came the quiet years, and after the quiet years came Corvin, who asked no questions because he recognized in me the same instinct for erasure that he practiced himself.

But the form wanted a name. And under the new law, the form's wanting was not a request.

"Andrés Vela," I said.

The name sounded strange in my own mouth, like speaking a language I'd been told to forget. Elias wrote nothing. He looked up instead — the first time he'd looked directly at me since entering — and his eyes did the arithmetic before his face did. *Vela.* Southern name. Dock-district name. The kind of name that, entered into a ledger in the autumn of 1938, traveled onward to offices where men drew lines through names with red pencils and sent other men with trucks.

"Occupation of father?" he read from the card.

"Dock hand. Deceased."

"Place of birth of father?"

"Port Sarelle," I said, and heard my own voice go very steady, the way it goes when I'm working on a hairspring and my hands must not shake. "South of the border."

Corvin, at the far bench, had stopped pretending to work. His loupe hung against his chest, forgotten. In the silence I could hear every clock in the shop ticking out of phase with every other — twenty-odd heartbeats, none of them agreeing, all of them loud.

Elias looked down at the form. The final question, question nineteen, was printed in bold type, because it was the question that mattered and everyone knew it. I had seen a copy of the form nailed to the post office wall between a notice about rabies vaccinations and one about curfew hours, and I had read question nineteen three times and then walked home without buying the stamps I'd come for.

*State the lineage classification of the household head as defined by Statute 44.*

Statute 44 had pages of definitions involving grandmothers and blood fractions and residency dates, all of it drafted by men who believed heredity could be tabulated like currency. By any honest application of those pages, my household — Corvin the household head, myself the resident apprentice of mixed parentage — fell into the category the red pencils liked. The category that went on lists. The category that went on trains.

Elias's pen hovered.

I want to be precise about those seconds, because precision is my trade and I owe it that much. I counted them afterward, the way I count everything I cannot bear to simply live through. Four seconds. In four seconds a master clock falls behind by less than a tenth of a beat — imperceptible, unmeasurable, gone. But some intervals contain more time than they hold. In those four seconds, Elias Brand read question nineteen, looked at the answer he had already written — *Vela, Andrés, dock hand, Port Sarelle* — understood exactly what statute required him to write next, and made a decision.

His pen touched paper.

He wrote *Mixed — exempt, artisan household, verified per Schedule C.* Schedule C did not apply to anyone. I knew this because I had memorized the statute out of the particular terror that masquerades as diligence. There was no exemption for artisan households. There was no Schedule C. He had invented it, in the same neat typeset hand, in front of me, in ink that would dry permanent by dinnertime.

"There," he said mildly, blotting the entry. "That resolves it. Artisan households were flagged for expedited review precisely to avoid backlog." He turned the form toward us with the bored efficiency of a man closing a transaction. "You'll receive a certification stamp within the week. Keep it posted near the door."

Corvin said, "Thank you," in a voice like gravel settling.

"Routine," said Elias, and began gathering his papers.

* * *

I walked him to the door because it seemed the thing to do, and also because my legs had decided independently that they needed to move or something in me would break visibly. At the threshold he paused, adjusting the ledger under his arm, and spoke without looking at me, quietly enough that Corvin at his bench could not have heard.

"Schedule C exists as of this morning. I drafted it at breakfast." A pause. "It applies to anyone I decide it applies to."

"That's not how laws work," I said.

"No," he agreed. "It's how ledgers work. The law is what's written down. Everything else is weather." He settled his collar. "You should know, apprentice, that I verify perhaps six households a day. I am thorough and slow and my supervisor finds me tedious. Tedious men are left alone. Remember that about me."

"I don't know anything else about you."

"Then we're even." He stepped down onto the cobbles. Then, half-turned, almost an afterthought: "The carriage clock on your bench. The one with the balance spring wound too tight. You'll break it if you keep forcing the amplitude."

"How do you—"

"My father repaired clocks," he said. "Before he took a position counting people instead of seconds. One of those professions is respectable." And he walked off down the lane with his crooked badge and his dangerous ledger, a thin gray man among the morning carts, leaving me standing in the doorway holding a doorframe I hadn't noticed gripping.

* * *

Corvin did not speak for a long while after the door closed. He went to the case of finished repairs, took out a customer's regulator, set it down, picked it up again, put it back. A man rearranging reality until it fit a new shape.

"He falsified a census record," Corvin said finally. "In our presence. With us as witnesses, which makes us complicit, which means our safety now depends entirely on his silence and his nerve." He sat down heavily on his stool, and for a moment he looked every one of his sixty-eight years, and then some. "Do you understand what happened in this room, girl?"

"Yes."

"Tell me anyway."

"A man wrote four untrue words in triplicate," I said, "and one of them was *exempt*, and the word was about me."

Corvin nodded slowly. "And what do we do with a debt like that?"

I looked at the form's ghost, invisible now but present everywhere in the shop — in the space where Elias had stood, in the faint smell of his cheap tobacco, in the carbon-paper future that would travel from his office to the archive to wherever ledgers go to become destiny.

"We keep it wound," I said. "Carefully. So it doesn't explode."

That night, for the first time in a month, I packed my tools not out of habit but out of plan. Carry, I thought. What can be carried. I was halfway through wrapping the loupe when I stopped, unwrapped it, and left it in the drawer — because packing implies flight, and I was not ready to admit that a stranger's handwriting was the only wall between my life and a list.

I slept badly, which is to say normally, except that at some hour past midnight I woke with absolute clarity into the dark and thought: *he hesitated.* Before he wrote the lie, he had hesitated — and hesitation, in a census taker, is a crack in the mechanism through which a whole life might leak.

I did not yet know whether that frightened me more than the soldiers did.

Three days later the certification stamp arrived, cream paper, official seal, *Schedule C — Verified.* I hung it by the door as instructed. And two mornings after that, a second letter came from the Census Verification Office, typed this time, unsigned — informing Master Corvin's establishment that premises of classified households were subject to routine physical inspection of inventories and contents, date to be announced.

Corvin read it twice, went very pale, and looked at the wall of clock cases along our back room — every one of them, as of last night, hiding things that were not gears.

"Well," he said. "We always did need a deadline to work well."
