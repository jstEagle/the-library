## 6. What Can Be Carried

The notice was pasted to the door of the workshop the way all official notices are pasted: crookedly, and with an optimism about glue that the glue did not deserve. One corner had already curled loose, so that the paper breathed in the wind like something alive, and I stood on the step reading it twice because the first time I read it I was too busy not being sick to absorb the content.

*By order of the Bureau of Enumeration and Classification, the premises of Corvin & Underhill, Horologists, shall be subject to inspection of inventory, records, and personnel, per the requirements of the Census Act. Compliance is mandatory.*

Personnel. That word did more damage than any of the others. Inventory they could count; records they could burn; but *personnel* meant that when the inspectors came, they would come for the hands as well as the tools. A workshop without its workers is just a room full of metal. They knew that. The whole architecture of the law knew that.

I went inside.

Master Corvin was at his bench, which surprised me until I saw what he was doing. He was not working. He was sitting very still with a movement cradled in his palm — the little eight-day carriage clock he'd been fussing over since spring — and he was looking at it the way men look at photographs of people who are already gone.

"You saw it," I said.

"I saw it." He set the clock down with exaggerated care, as though the bench might resent being trusted with it. "Took them long enough. I'd begun to feel neglected."

That was Corvin's way. Sixteen years at his bench had taught me that the worse the trouble, the drier the joke, until you reached a certain threshold past which he said nothing at all and you knew you were in truly deep water. We were not yet at silence. But we could see it from here.

"When?" I asked.

"Notice says ten days."

"Ten days." I hung my coat on its peg, an ordinary gesture performed by a person who was no longer entirely ordinary, if she ever had been. "Then we have work to do."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about confiscation: it does not arrive all at once, a wave that takes the house whole. It arrives in categories. First your papers are examined. Then your property is inventoried. Then your person is classified. And somewhere between the second category and the third, you stop being a citizen with possessions and become an inventory item yourself — one entry among many, awaiting valuation.

We had known this was coming. Everyone in Vellmark's mixed quarters had known it since spring, when the census posters went up with their cheerful sans-serif promise: *A Counted City Is a Cared-For City.* Master Corvin had responded to the poster by teaching me, over several unremarkable evenings, how to disassemble a clock case and reline its interior walls with false bottoms shallow enough to escape notice but deep enough to hold calipers, tweezers, three jeweling tools, and a lifetime of small sins. He called the lesson "case restoration technique." He never once said the word *hiding*, and I never once asked why we were learning it, because some educations require no syllabus.

Now the lessons became practice. On the second evening after the notice, he cleared the display shelf of its finest pieces — the regulator with the mercury pendulum, the astronomical clock with the orrery, the twin carriage clocks with the engraved cases — and laid them out on the bench like patients awaiting surgery.

"Hand me the lining felt," he said. "The green."

"The green is out."

"The grey, then. And the blade."

I watched him work. His hands, which had shaken badly enough for two years now to keep him off fine repair work, were almost steady tonight. Fear, it turned out, was a better treatment for his tremor than any doctor in Vellmark. There is a particular stillness that comes into the body when every motion matters, when a slipped knife means a visible seam and a visible seam means questions and questions mean the end of everything. He cut the lining panels with the concentration of a man defusing something, fitted them into the carriage clock's case, then lifted the false floor into place with two fingers and closed the case.

From outside: a handsome clock. From inside: a cavity holding a folded packet of jeweled screwdrivers.

"There," he said, and his voice cracked slightly on the word, and neither of us acknowledged it.

One by one, through the evening, the best of our tools vanished into the innards of our finest clocks. The staking set went into the regulator's base. The balance-staff gauges went beneath the orrery's false drawer. It struck me, watching a pair of dividers disappear into a clock whose whole purpose was telling truth about position and distance, that there was a joke in it somewhere, and that neither of us would laugh even if either of us found it.

"What happens," I asked, "if they open them?"

"They won't open them." He pressed the case shut. "Inspectors look at what inspectors expect to see. A clockmaker's shop full of clocks. They'll check the ledgers, count the stock, glance at the benches. Nobody dismantles a finished piece to see where it keeps its secrets. Only we do that."

"And if they do?"

He was quiet for a moment. "Then we'll have larger problems than hidden tools."

* * *

My own task was different, and harder.

While Corvin hid the trade, I had to hide the life. Everything we owned that wasn't tool or stock — the personal, the domestic, the accumulated sediment of two existences conducted above and beside a workshop for decades — had to be sorted before the inspectors arrived, because a life left lying about is evidence, and evidence gets classified right along with persons.

I gave myself three piles.

**Carry:** what could go into two bags and my coat pockets without inviting a search. Documents that would pass. Photographs that wouldn't, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked inside the hollow heel of a boot I never wore. My mother's ring, which I wore now at all hours, having concluded that the safest place for contraband gold was on the finger of a girl whose paperwork made her, provisionally and precariously, keepable. Corvin's letters from his late wife, eleven of them, soft as cloth from handling. Small things. Dense things. Things whose loss would be survivable but only barely.

**Bury:** the middle category, the one that required hope and planning in equal measure. The good silver that had been in Corvin's family longer than the Bureau had been in anyone's. The ledgers predating the new government — forty years of honest accounts, each page a record of transactions that might be read, by a suspicious clerk, as evidence of commerce with the wrong sorts of clients, meaning anyone. My father's books, in his own language, which I had never fully learned and which the law now insisted I pretend did not exist. These went, over three nights, into a hole behind the coal cellar, wrapped in waxed canvas against the damp, laid flat like the residents of a small underground city. Burying things is an act of faith. You are betting on a future in which someone digs them up again. Some nights the bet seemed sound. Other nights I stood in the dark of the cellar with cold hands and a shovel and thought: *if they deport us, these books will lie here until the building falls, and no one will ever know they waited.*

**Burn:** the last pile, and the cruellest, because burning requires you to decide, item by item, what cannot be allowed to fall into their hands. Letters that named names. Corvin's old correspondence with colleagues across the border, warm and funny and damning in the wrong filing cabinet. My father's business papers, which proved, in careful ink, that a man of his classification had run a successful enterprise in this city for nineteen years — proof of exactly the kind of competence the new forms were designed to erase. I burned those papers in the workshop stove on the fourth night, a handful at a time, feeding the fire slowly because rushing felt like disrespect.

The stove ate them without comment. Stoves are indifferent to history. That may be the thing I envied most about them that winter.

* * *

On the fifth night, Corvin found me sitting on the floor of the back room amid the carry pile, holding a teacup.

Not a valuable teacup. Not silver, not porcelain worth inventorying. A thick white cup with a chip on the rim, purchased by my mother at some market before I could walk, used every morning of my apprenticeship, chipped further by me at fourteen when I knocked it against the bench vise and Corvin said — I can hear it now — *"Careful, girl, that cup has seniority."*

It didn't fit any pile. Too humble to carry, too worthless to bury, too much mine to burn. It sat outside the system, and I sat outside the system with it, and somewhere down the street a tram bell rang four minutes' worth of notes into the cold air, and I understood, finally, in my hands and chest rather than my head, what the census actually was.

It was a sorting of objects into piles. That was all. Carry, keep, remove. They had simply decided to apply a clockmaker's triage to human beings, and they had done it with less care than we took with teacups, because we would never have thrown away a working mechanism merely because its serial number had been filed oddly.

Corvin appeared in the doorway. He looked at the piles, then at me, then at the cup.

"That cup has seniority," he said.

"It doesn't fit anywhere."

"Everything fits somewhere." He lowered himself onto the crate opposite mine with the careful economy of an old man's knees. "You've just defined the categories wrong. There's a fourth pile."

"There isn't room for a fourth pile."

"There's always room for the fourth pile. It's the one you don't pack, because you're carrying it already." He nodded at the cup. "That goes wherever you go. In your hand, if necessary. Inspectors can't classify what they don't think to ask about, and no inspector in this city will ask about a chipped cup."

I turned it over. The chip sat under my thumb like a small scar.

"When did you learn this?" I asked. "The fourth pile?"

Something moved behind his face — I saw it clearly then, in the lamplight, and I have seen it clearly every time I have thought of it since. A door opening onto a room he kept locked.

"A long time ago," he said. "Under a government you're too young to remember. Keep sorting, girl. I'll make tea."

He rose, and paused, and added, quietly, as if to the room rather than to me:

"We'll need the cup. Trust me on that."

* * *

We worked until nearly midnight, and it was near midnight — the hour when the workshop was at its most itself, all the day's ticking braided together into one long breath — that Corvin stopped at his bench and did not move for so long that I looked up from the waxed canvas I was folding.

He was looking at the wall of clocks. Our wall. Forty years of acquisitions and repairs and rescues, every one of them running, every one of them set to the observatory signal like all honest clocks in Vellmark. A whole civilisation of dials agreeing with one another.

"They'll take the wall," he said. "Whatever else happens, whatever we hide, they'll inventory the wall and requisition half of it for their offices. Bureaucrats love a well-run clock. It flatters them."

"Some of them will survive it," I said. "Clocks are patient."

"Yes." He came to stand beside me, and for once his tremor was gone entirely; perhaps his hands had finally accepted that shaking was no longer the worst thing available to them. "But listen to me, because I'll only say this once and then we'll both pretend I didn't. Whatever they ask you, at the inspection — however they phrase the questions about lineage and classification — you give them the answers we've prepared. Exactly. No improvisation, no cleverness. Precision, Paloma. Precision has kept you alive at this bench for sixteen years and it will keep you alive at that table."

"And after the table?"

"After the table," he said slowly, "we'll see what's left to be precise about."

He turned back to his bench, and I stood alone in front of the wall of clocks, and I noticed — the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song — that the observatory regulator, the one we always checked first, the one through which all official time entered the shop like blood through an artery, had drifted.

Three minutes slow. Maybe four.

I frowned at it. I had wound it Sunday; I wound it every Sunday; it had never drifted more than a minute between windings in all my years of winding it. Either something in the movement had finally given way, or something upstream of us had — some failure at the source, in the great master clock above the city, the one I was due to climb toward soon enough.

Or, whispered a voice in me that I did not yet recognize and would come to know very well: *or someone has decided what the time should be.*

I wrote it in the maintenance log — *regulator losing, investigate* — because that is what an apprentice does. What an apprentice does not do is stand in front of a wall of clocks at midnight, holding a chipped teacup, feeling the first cold touch of an idea that will change everything.

Down at the station, they said, the trains had been running late all week.

Nobody could say why.
