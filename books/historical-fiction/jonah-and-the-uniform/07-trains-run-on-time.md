## 7. Trains Run on Time

I had always believed that the worst thing a man could discover about his work was that it was pointless. I learned otherwise on a Tuesday morning in the transport office, standing beneath a timetable painted on enamel the size of a barn door, when a clerk named Hessler explained to me, with the pride of a man showing off his mother's jam preserves, exactly what my signature set in motion.

"Everything keys off the observatory," he said. He tapped the painted board with a pencil, tap, tap, as though the board might answer. "Master clock gives us the hour signal three times daily. We sequence departures from it. Freight in the morning, passenger at midday, and the—" he consulted a slip of paper he did not need to consult, "—special services in the evening."

"Special services," I repeated.

"Resettlement transports." He said it the way one says *roof repairs*. A phrase worn smooth by use until nothing sharp remained in it. "They've been sitting on the sidings for nine days because the observatory clock is dead and nobody will authorize departures off pocket watches. Regulations." He smiled, inviting me to share in the comedy of regulations. "You can't run a railway on wristwatches, Brand. The whole city would drift apart like — well." He gestured vaguely, a man surrendering a metaphor before finishing it.

Nine days. Nine days of trains standing in the yards like animals that had forgotten how to be slaughtered, because the heart of Vellmark's official time had stopped and no one downstream had the authority to disagree with silence.

I should note, for the record I was increasingly unsure I believed in, that I had come to the transport office on legitimate business. The census required coordination with municipal schedules — verification visits were to be conducted during hours when subjects could reasonably be expected to be home, which required knowing what hours meant, which required, apparently, a forty-minute tram ride and a conversation with Hessler. This was the bureaucracy's great joke on all of us: every cruelty was reached via paperwork that was itself entirely reasonable. No single form ever asked you to be a monster. The forms only asked you to be accurate, punctual, and thorough, and the monster assembled itself out of the aggregate, the way a crowd assembles out of individuals who are each merely walking.

"So the repair," Hessler said, "is rather urgent from our side of the fence. You're out there often, aren't you? Verifying the building?"

"I'm verifying the personnel assigned to the building," I said. "The clock is a matter for the works department."

"Mm." He found something on his desk, a requisition, and initialed it without reading it. "Well. When they fix it — and they will, the Underhill girl has been up there a fortnight, my wife says her family knows the workshop — the schedule resumes. And I'll tell you, Brand, between us, there are men in this office who've been made very unhappy by nine days of nothing running. Very unhappy." He looked up, and for a moment the clerk fell away and something older and colder looked out through the same mild face. "There are people in this city whose classification papers expire. Who are meant to be *elsewhere* by now. Every day the clock stands still is a day someone upstairs asks why, and I would very much prefer to stop being asked."

*Elsewhere.* The word did its little dance, dressed in its little euphemism, and I nodded along like a sensible man.

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only honesty left to me and even that arrived late. In that moment, standing in the transport office smelling of coal smoke and ink, I did not think: *these trains carry people away to be killed.* I knew the word *resettlement*. I had written it myself on classification forms. What I thought, insofar as I permitted myself thought, was something smaller and more cowardly: *the faster the clock runs true, the sooner I stop being noticed.*

That is the mechanism, if you'll forgive the vocabulary I've picked up from watching an apprentice work. Horror does not arrive in a city wearing banners. It arrives as duty. It arrives as a clerk's mild unhappiness, a superior's raised eyebrow, a schedule waiting to be honored. It arrives wearing your own face, holding your own pen, and it says: *you're already halfway done; it would be a shame to stop now.*

* * *

I walked back toward the observatory through the old quarter instead of taking the tram, which I told myself was because the day was fine and really was because I needed the extra twenty minutes to become a person who could climb those stairs again.

The old quarter was where the classifications lived and breathed. You could read the census off the buildings now, if you'd been trained as I'd been trained. Fresh paint over a doorway where a nameplate had been removed. A butcher's shop shuttered not for scarcity but for lineage. The Weiss family's window boxes, absurdly cheerful, geraniums going on as though botany were exempt from legislation. And everywhere the new placards, the categories, the neat stenciled letters that looked so orderly from a distance and so insane up close, the way any system looks once you've seen the human hands inside it.

A boy was chalking hopscotch squares on the cobbles outside the bakery. He drew them crooked, counted them wrong — six, seven, eleven — and hopped anyway, delighted, landing wherever his feet decided. I stood there longer than a grown man with appointments ought to, watching him refuse the entire concept of a standard interval.

Then I climbed the observatory hill, and the tower stood against the sky with its dead face, the clock face motionless at some abandoned hour, and I understood that I was looking at the most honest object in Paloma Underhill's city. Everything else in Vellmark told you a lie about time. Told you there was no urgency, no schedule, no train with your name stenciled on its manifest. The stopped clock at least admitted time had broken.

She was in the gallery when I came in, sleeves rolled, a jeweler's loupe pushed up on her forehead like a second eye sleeping. She didn't look up.

"You walk like a man rehearsing an apology," she said. "Either you're here to arrest me or you've bad news about the coffee ration."

"Neither. I'm here to verify that you continue to exist at your listed address of employment."

"How thorough of you. Write that down. 'Subject exists, grudgingly.'"

I had learned by then the grammar of her. The wit was not decoration; it was load-bearing. Take it away and the structure of her would collapse under everything it held up. I leaned on the rail and watched her hands do whatever small violent thing they were doing to a wheel the size of a coin, and I tried to locate, in my chest, the appropriate response to what I'd learned that morning, and found instead a terrible quietness, the quietness of a man watching weather approach from very far away and calculating whether it will reach his house or pass to the north.

"The transport office says hello," I said. "Or rather, it says *hurry*."

Her hands paused. Just barely — a stutter in the rhythm, the kind I'd learned meant something had landed.

"Do they," she said.

"The master clock sequences the departures. All of them. They've been paralyzed without it. There are men in that office who take it personally."

"And when it runs true again?" She set down her tweezers and looked at me directly, and her eyes did the thing that unnerved me most about her, which was to see me completely while revealing almost nothing. "The trains go back to their timetable."

"Yes."

"Whose timetable includes the evening services."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't insult her by pretending it was one. This was the other thing about Paloma Underhill: she had done the arithmetic everyone in this country was doing in some back room of the skull, and she refused the comfort of not saying it aloud.

"Hessler seems like a decent enough man," I offered, which was the stupidest sentence I produced that autumn.

"He's a decent enough man who files timetables," she said. "The timetable doesn't care about his decency. That's the whole genius of it, isn't it? Nobody has to be a monster. Everybody just has to be on time." She picked the tweezers back up. "Go away, Mr. Brand. I have four hundred parts in this movement and each one of them is more honest company than you are."

She was wrong about that, but I went.

* * *

That night I sat in my rooms with the window open — October coming on, the air gone metallic — and conducted the sort of inventory that passes, in a certain kind of man, for a conscience.

Here is what I knew. Item: the observatory clock, once repaired, would restore the departure schedule. Item: the departures included special services, evening, carrying persons whose classification papers had expired, a phrase I had written variants of in my own hand perhaps two hundred times. Item: the woman in the tower was herself of mixed heritage, which I had known since the third verification visit, which knowledge I had entered into my ledger as — I took the draft page out of the drawer and read my own falsification again, my own tidy lie in my own tidy script — *lineage verified, maternal line documented per annex B.* Annex B did not contain what I said it contained. Annex B contained a birth record from a parish that the ministry had declared unreliable, and I had declared it reliable, and the ink had dried, and the world had continued spinning at approximately its customary rate.

Item: I had, as of this morning, a professional incentive to see her succeed quickly.

I put the page down and looked at my hands. They are not remarkable hands. Clerk's hands, ink-stained at the second knuckle, steady enough for forms. I had always assumed that hands like mine were incapable of real harm — harm requiring rifles, boots, shouting, all the theater of it. But no. Harm, it turns out, requires only accuracy. The ledger was a weapon and I was its trained hand and the trigger was a question mark placed after a surname.

And here the night split into its two arguments, the way it would split again and again in the weeks to come, and I lay awake refereeing.

Argument one ran thus: you are a small man. Small men survive by keeping the machine oiled and their heads down. The clock gets fixed regardless — she is plainly capable, the works department wants it fixed, history will record that the repair happened with or without your enthusiasm. Your enthusiasm changes nothing except the date of your own arrest. Be accurate. Be punctual. Be thorough. Go to sleep.

Argument two was quieter, and it did not argue so much as it showed me things. It showed me the enamel timetable with its neat rows. It showed me the word *elsewhere* doing its dance. It showed me a girl of maybe ten I had once seen on a platform in the capital, years ago, before any of this had a name, holding a paper boat over the edge of the platform into the wind of a departing train, and it asked me, in the tone of a patient teacher, whose timetable that train had kept.

I got up and drank water that tasted of the tin cup. I looked out the window at Vellmark laid out under the gaslight, a city of forty thousand clocks — church bells, factory whistles, station boards, kitchen mantels — all of them secretly listening, I now understood, for one voice on a hill. When the master spoke, they would all agree again. The trains would breathe, the schedules would knit themselves back together, the city would resume being exactly what it had been built, recently and deliberately, to be.

And somewhere in that resumed machinery would be a woman with a loupe on her forehead who had never done anything to anyone but keep time honest.

I thought — and I record the thought because it shames me and shame is instructive — I thought: *she is fast. She may finish within the week. Perhaps something will delay her.*

I thought it, and then I sat down hard on the edge of the bed as if the thought had weight and had struck me. Because I knew what I had just wished, in the privacy of my own skull, and it was not *perhaps the parts won't arrive*. It was: *perhaps she will fail.* I had wished incompetence onto the finest craftsman I had ever watched work, because her competence was now a timetable and her timetable was a train and that train had expired classification papers stapled to every soul aboard.

There is a particular nausea that comes from catching yourself mid-fall — not from having fallen, but from seeing the moment your foot left the ledge. I had it then. I had wanted, for one clean second, to sabotage a woman I — and there the sentence stopped, because I did not yet have permission to finish it, and would not for some time.

What I could wish instead, I reasoned, pacing, was ambiguity. Not failure. Delay. A clock that ran, but imperfectly — no, that was madness, that was a thought for a different man, a braver one, a man whose hands did not shake when he imagined it. Put it away. File it. Annex C. Unreliable source.

I filed it. It did not stay filed. That is the trouble with thoughts of that species: they breed in the dark of a sleepless head, and by morning there is a whole litter of them, and they look at you with your own eyes.

* * *

Morning brought Hessler a telegram and brought me, by noon, the news of it, because Hessler had apparently decided I was the kind of man one shares things with, God help me.

"Works department confirms end of week," he said, beaming, tapping his pencil against the enamel board. "Friday, the new escapement goes in. Signal test Saturday morning, full schedule Monday." He wrote *MONDAY* on a slate beside the timetable with the satisfaction of a man writing his own salvation. "And not before time. The evening services alone — fourteen hundred souls across three departures. Do you know what it costs to hold a transport even one day? Guards, provisioning, the paperwork multiplies, Brand, the paperwork *multiplies*."

Fourteen hundred souls. He said it as tonnage. I heard it as tonnage too — that was the trick of it, that by then I could hear it both ways simultaneously, the euphemism and the arithmetic underneath, and stand there nodding at both.

"Monday," I said.

"Monday." He underlined it twice. "On time, at last. You know what they say." And he said it, the slogan, the one painted on the station wall in letters a meter high, the one that had seemed, in the early days, like mere administrative vanity and now sounded like a threat wearing a motto's clothes: *the trains run on time.*

I walked back up the hill in a state I can only describe as composed dread, the dread sealed under glass like a museum exhibit, labeled, dated, viewed daily by its owner without being touched.

At the observatory door I met Corvin, the old master, come up the hill with a wrapped parcel of tools despite hands that shook like autumn leaves. He looked at me a long moment — the old ones see everything; it is their last privilege — and said, in his cracked voice:

"She'll have it finished by Friday, Mr. Brand. Finest work I've seen in forty years. Whatever it is you're worried about" — and I hadn't said a word, my face must be a public notice board — "you should decide soon whether you're the kind of man who winds clocks or stops them."

He went in past me, leaving the smell of oil and metal polish, leaving me on the step with the tower overhead and its blind stopped face staring down at the city it was about to wake.

Inside, up the spiral stair, I could hear her already at work — the small tick and click of a mechanism being persuaded, part by part, toward obedience. Friday. Saturday a signal. Monday the world.

And I stood there in the stairwell's cold light, a census taker with a falsified page in his drawer and a wish in his skull he dared not finish, understanding at last that the question was no longer whether the clock would run true.

The question was what I would do, between now and Friday, about the possibility — the mad, criminal, unavoidable possibility — that it shouldn't.
