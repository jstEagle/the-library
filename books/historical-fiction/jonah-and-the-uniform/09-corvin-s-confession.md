## 9. Corvin's Confession

There is a particular kind of silence that lives inside a clock workshop. People imagine ticking — they imagine a hundred little hearts all beating at once — but the truth is stranger. A workshop full of clocks is mostly held breath. Each mechanism gathers itself, releases one second, gathers again. Between those beats there is a sliver of nothing, and if you sit among them long enough, as I have sat for fifty-one years, you begin to live in that sliver. You begin to feel like a man who exists only in the pauses between someone else's heartbeats.

It was in one of those pauses, on an evening in late October of 1938, that I decided to tell the girl what I had done.

I had been deciding for eleven years, you understand. Deciding is a thing I do well. I once spent four months deciding whether to replace the third wheel in the observatory's strike train or simply true it on the lathe, and in the end I did neither, because by then the decision had become a kind of companion. But the census had changed the arithmetic. When a government starts asking whose grandmother was born where, a man's unfinished confessions stop being private property. They become loose tools left out on the bench — dangerous to everyone who walks past.

Paloma was at the far bench, working on the Weiss girl's music box.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only inheritance I have worth giving, and I intend to give it accurately: she was not repairing it for money. She had brought it home in her toolbag two days after the Weiss family stopped coming to the shop, and she had said nothing about it, and I had asked nothing, because in Vellmark that autumn the things we did not say to each other had grown into a whole second language, fluent and terrible, and we were all of us native speakers.

She worked with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her dark hair pinned back with whatever had come to hand — a broken hairpin, I think, and beside it, absurdly, a jeweler's broach. The lamp threw her shadow long across the floorboards, so that it seemed she was twice her size, a giantess bent over a toy. Her hands were steady. Of all my sins, I will say this: I never gave her unsteady hands.

"Corvin." She didn't look up. "You're hovering."

"I am supervising."

"You're hovering. There's a distinction. Supervising involves adding value."

"Witnessing," I said, "involves adding value. Hovering is what old men do when they have something in their throats."

That got her attention. She set down her tweezers — laid them parallel to the bench edge, exactly, because I had beaten that into her in her first month the way other masters beat arithmetic into children — and turned on her stool, and looked at me with those level dark eyes that had unnerved every inspector and customs man who ever leaned over our counter. Half of Vellmark could not decide what she was, and the half that thought it had decided was drafting laws about it.

"Sit down," she said. "You only confess while sitting. It's in your manual."

"There is no manual."

"There would be, if you could forgive yourself long enough to write one."

* * *

I sat. It took some arranging. A man of seventy-three does not lower himself onto a stool so much as negotiate with gravity, and gravity has excellent lawyers.

Behind me, the regulator clock on the wall ticked through its beat — the good one, the one we trusted, though we no longer trusted anything official enough to hang a seal on. Outside, the street was quiet in the way streets are quiet now: not empty, but listening. Since the census notices went up in the post office window, Vellmark had developed the hush of a room where someone is reading your letter over your shoulder.

"When I came to this city," I began, and then stopped, because it was the wrong beginning. Beginnings are like escapements — release too much too fast and the whole works jams. I started again. "You have noticed my hands."

"They're on the ends of your arms," Paloma said. "Noticed them years ago."

"Humor me."

"They shake when you're not working. They're steady when you are. I've never seen the point of mentioning it."

"The point," I said, "is that they were not always so. I shook them myself, if you follow me. No man shakes at sixty-two without assistance, and my assistance was a bottle of plum brandy kept in the parts cabinet behind the dead seconds movements, where no apprentice would ever look, apprentices being constitutionally incapable of looking anywhere but forward." I watched her take that in. She was too well-trained to interrupt a mechanism mid-explanation; I had taught her that faults must be allowed to fully present themselves before diagnosis. "The year was 1927. You'd have been nine. The old government — the one they've now taught the schoolchildren to call chaotic, corrupt, weak, all the words that make the new one look like a haircut and a hot meal — that government ran its own sorting, you see. Quieter about it. Same scissors."

Paloma's face did not change. But her hands, resting in her lap, folded into each other, finger over finger, the way they did when she was holding something still that wanted to move.

"There was a ministry office on Kesslerstrasse," I said. "Third floor. Blue blinds. They paid cash, which should have been my first warning, since honest institutions always pay late. And they sent clocks. Not their own clocks — confiscated ones. Seized from houses, from shops, from lodges and associations that had been declared illegal on a Tuesday and emptied by Wednesday. Grandfather clocks with family names painted inside the case doors. Carriage clocks with monograms. Wall regulators that had hung in the same hallway for three generations and been wound every Sunday by the same arthritic hand. All of it impounded, all of it ticking in crates in that blue-blinded room, and all of it — here is the part I need you to hear precisely — *running*. Confiscators do not wind clocks, Paloma. Someone in those houses wound them right up to the morning they were taken. Every seized clock arrived still counting the seconds of a household that no longer legally existed."

The regulator ticked. I let it tick through perhaps forty of those seconds, which is a respectable length of time for a man to look at his own past if he keeps his eyes on his hands.

"And the ministry wanted them running why?" Paloma asked quietly.

"Inventory," I said. "Appraisal. Some of the pieces went to officials' houses as gifts. Some went to auction in the capital. A clock that has stopped is a curiosity worth pennies; a clock that runs is furniture worth crowns. My job was to make the stolen goods presentable. To clean the pivots of a music box that had played a lullaby in a nursery from which the child had been removed, and oil it, and regulate it, and send it back to the ministry so that some captain's wife could set it on her mantel and be charmed by how sweetly it tinkled." I heard my own voice go dry, which is my habit, and which I did not correct, because correcting it would have required feeling the full weight at once, and at seventy-three one carries such things in installments. "Eleven months. I repaired two hundred and six clocks for that office. I know the number because I kept a ledger. I told myself the ledger was for tax purposes. What it was, actually, was a list of names — because the family names were inside the case doors, or engraved on the backs, or written on paper labels glued beneath the bases by mothers who apparently believed, as mothers do, that a name in ink might survive anything."

Paloma said nothing. In the pause, somewhere down the street, a boot heel struck cobbles in the deliberate rhythm of a patrol, passed, faded.

"You taught me precision," she said at last.

"I did."

"Why?"

And there it was — the question I had been waiting eleven years for her to ask, delivered with the flat calm she used on difficult customers, which is the voice she used on everything that mattered. I reached into my waistcoat pocket.

* * *

The loupe is French; the case is English; the lens itself came to me from a master in Prague who inherited it from a man nobody remembers. It is a doublet, ten power, and in fifty years it has not gathered a scratch, because I have never let it touch anything but light. I have looked through it at more beauty than most men see naked-eyed in a lifetime — the whorl of a blued screw head, the frost of a properly finished flat, the impossible architecture of a lever escapement no bigger than a grain of rice. And I have looked through it at serial numbers I was polishing out of confiscated silver.

I put it in her palm and closed her fingers over it.

"That's yours," I said.

"Corvin—"

"It is not a reward. Do not mistake it. It is a transfer of debt." My hands, on my knees, were shaking — the ordinary shaking, the brandy's long farewell, thirteen years gone and still billing me. "Here is the confession entire, since we've begun. After the ministry, I could not work for a year. The shaking got so bad I couldn't hold a graver. Then one day I read in the paper that the new director of the observatory wanted the master clock overhauled, and something in me decided that if I had spent a year making stolen time run beautifully, I would spend the rest of my life making honest time run perfectly. As penance. Precision as penance — that is the phrase I used to myself, alone, in this shop, night after night, to justify being exact about things no one would ever notice. Because that's what penance wants, Paloma. It wants to be *unseen*. Punishment you can display is just theater."

"So every tolerance you drilled into me—"

"Was a prayer," I said. "Yes. Every tenth of a millimeter. Every surface you polished past specification. Every time I made you redo a jewel setting four times and told you it was because you were talented enough to get it right — that was me, kneeling. You were my rosary, girl. I don't recommend it. It's a heavy thing to be someone's absolution, and you never volunteered."

She looked down at the loupe in her fist. Her thumb moved across the brass case, reading it, the way she read everything.

"Two hundred and six clocks," she said. "Did any of them come back? Ever? Did anyone ever walk in and say, this was mine?"

"Once." I had not said this aloud in decades, and saying it now, the room seemed to tilt a degree, as though one of the shelf clocks had leaned in to listen. "A woman. 1929. Brought in a carriage clock — a LeRoy, good movement, badly treated — and asked if I could fix the strike. Said it had belonged to her father and it hadn't struck correctly since it came back from 'repairs.'" I made the quotation marks in the air, an ugly gesture. "I opened the case door. Inside, on the paper label beneath the base, was a name from my ledger. Number one-forty-something. I don't permit myself to remember which. And I looked at her, and she looked at me, and neither of us said one word about it, because what was there to say — *madam, I oiled your dead father's clock so an officer's wife could enjoy it?* So I fixed the strike. Took me three hours. Charged her nothing. And when she left, I took the ledger out behind the shop and burned it in the coal bin, and the shaking started that same evening and has not entirely stopped since."

"Thirteen years," Paloma said softly. "That's when it started."

"You noticed that too. You notice everything. It's why I chose you — no. That's a lie of the tidy sort. I chose you because you walked in off the street at fourteen, having read in some library book that clockmakers existed, and asked if you could watch, and then stood absolutely motionless by the bench for four hours until I gave you something to hold. Nobody had ever stood that still in my shop. Stillness like that is either vocation or grief, and I knew both, and I knew I could teach either one."

The lamp hissed faintly. Down at the far bench, the Weiss girl's music box lay open, its tiny cylinder gleaming, waiting for pins that Paloma would raise and shape by hand until it played whatever lullaby it had played in a room that was no longer legally a room.

"Why tell me now?" she asked.

"Because the lists went up," I said. "Because a census taker has taken an interest in this shop that I am too old to believe is bureaucratic. Because if they come for us — for you, let's be honest, they'll retire me to a filing cellar and be done — then you will carry this shop's memory, and a memory with a hole in it is a forged document. I won't leave you holding clean paper. You'll know what you inherited. Both halves. The skill and the stain."

"And the loupe?"

"The loupe is so you can see clearly," I said. "That's all it does. All it's ever done. It shows what is truly there, at ten times the size, and leaves the judgment to the eye behind it. I looked through it at wickedness and called it work. Look through it at what's coming, and judge better than I did."

She turned it over once, twice. Then she lifted it to her eye — the practiced gesture, elbow tucked, and I felt something in my chest turn over like a mainspring reaching the end of its barrel — and she aimed it, of all things, at me. Ten power. Fifty-one years of bench work examined at close range.

"Hm," she said. "Worn. Scrupulously maintained. Some sentimental value to the previous owner." She lowered it and hung the cord around her neck, tucked the lens under her collar against her heart like a pocket watch. "Accepted. With conditions."

"Conditions," I echoed. Apprentices.

"You'll tell me the rest as I ask for it. Not in one lump. And—" she hesitated, and for a moment the flat calm cracked and I saw the twenty-year-old underneath, frightened and furious and wide awake, "—and you'll stop drinking the cooking sherry and pretending it's for the cases. If we're going to lose things, Corvin, I want you sober for it."

"Merciless," I said, and meant it as praise, and she took it as one.

She picked up her tweezers again, aligned them to the bench edge, and bent back over the music box. I remained on my stool in the held breath between ticks, lighter by one confession and heavier by one loupe, which is roughly the exchange rate the soul gets offered in this life.

It was nearly midnight when she spoke again, not looking up, in the tone she reserved for observations that were not questions but would be answered as questions anyway.

"Corvin. This census taker. Brand. He came back today while you were at the bank."

"I heard."

"He asked me how long a full overhaul of the observatory master takes." Her tweezers hovered over the cylinder, perfectly steady. "I told him the truth. Three more weeks. He wrote it down. And then he said — very quietly, not writing — *make it take longer than that.*"

The regulator ticked on the wall, honest to the second, marking time for a city that had begun measuring something else entirely.

"Well," I said, reaching for the sherry after all. "Perhaps precision isn't the only thing I've taught you."
