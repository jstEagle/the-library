## 10. Inspection Day

The notice came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for cleaning the ultrasonic bath and re-hinging the cabinet under the window; nobody sane schedules an inquisition on a Tuesday.

*By order of the Office of Civic Inventory, this premises will be subject to verification of declared assets and registered personnel. All instruments, tools, and mechanisms must correspond to Schedule C of the Workshop Registry.*

Corvin read it twice, holding it at arm's length the way he did now, as if distance might soften what words said. His hands, I noticed, were perfectly steady while he read and shaking again by the time he set the page down. Fear worked on him in reverse. It steadied him for the crisis and deserted him after.

"Schedule C," he said. "Do we have a Schedule C?"

"You made one," I said. "Three years ago, for the fire insurance. It lists every tool we own."

"Does it list all the tools we own?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Because for two weeks now — ever since the inspection placard went up on the Weisses' door, ever since Corvin started sleeping with his coat on — we had been hollowing out clock cases and lining them with contraband. A jeweling tool inside the case of the Vienna regulator. Calipers nested in the barrel of the tall hall clock like bones in a coffin. Corvin's father's loupe kit, the one with the ivory fittings, tucked behind the chime springs of a mantel piece that had not run since 1929 and was therefore, by every inspector's logic, beneath notice.

Under the new ordinances, unregistered precision instruments were presumptive evidence of undeclared work. Undeclared work by an unregistered apprentice suggested unregistered *personnel*. And unregistered personnel, in Vellmark in the autumn of '38, was a phrase with a trapdoor in the middle of it.

"We could declare everything," I said. "Reopen the schedule, add the new pieces."

"And explain where the money for new pieces came from? While my apprentice isn't on any guild roll because the guild stopped admitting—" He stopped. He never finished that sentence anymore; he just let the shape of it sit between us like a tool neither of us would pick up.

"So we hide it better," I said.

"Paloma." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger. "They're not looking for tools. You understand that. Tools are the excuse. They're counting our hands."

I did understand it. I understood it the way you understand weather coming — not because you're wise but because your teeth ache.

* * *

We worked until two in the morning, and then we sat among the clocks and listened to them disagree about the hour, which is the closest thing a workshop has to a conscience. At half past two Corvin took down the Vienna regulator, opened its waist-length case, and began unwrapping the jeweling tool from its bed of chamois.

"No," I said.

"It's the most incriminating thing in the building. Serial number, guild mark, everything."

"It's also the most visible. If they open that case, they'll find it in four seconds. The mantel clock doesn't run — nobody services a dead clock's insides." I took the tool from his hands and put it back behind the chime springs, then re-wedged the panel so the seam showed honest dust. "The regulator stays empty. Empty and clean. If they want to look at something, let them look at something boring."

He watched me do it with an expression I hadn't seen on him before. Not fear. Something closer to appraisal, the way he used to look at my filleting work when I got a curve right on the first pass.

"When did you learn to think like a smuggler?" he asked.

"I'm a clockmaker," I said. "Everything I do is hiding small things inside large things and hoping the tolerances hold."

* * *

They arrived at nine sharp, which told me something. Bureaucracy keeps its own time, and men who arrive exactly when the paperwork says are men following a script, not hunting. Hunters come early, or late, or in twos from opposite doors.

There were three of them. A senior inspector named Vogel, gray in the way that furniture is gray, with a leather folio under his arm and the particular patience of a man who has done this forty times and been disappointed thirty-nine. Behind him, a junior with a clipboard and the unfortunate habit of touching things. The third man didn't touch anything at all, which worried me more. He stood by the door with his coat buttoned and his hands folded, and he looked at *people* — at Corvin's hands, at my face — the way the other two looked at ledgers.

"Herr Underhill?" Vogel said, consulting his folio.

"Apprentice Underhill. Master Corvin is the proprietor."

"He's also listed as your guarantor of residence." A thin checkmark. "Convenient arrangement."

"Expensive arrangement," Corvin said mildly from the bench. "You'd be surprised what rent costs even from family."

Vogel almost smiled. That was the dangerous part of him — he wasn't cruel, or he was cruel the way winter is cruel, without appetite. He simply wanted the day's form completed, and forms are completed either by finding nothing or by finding enough.

They began at the front of the shop and moved back, and I learned quickly that the junior with the clipboard was the real instrument of the inspection. Vogel asked questions; the junior opened drawers. Every drawer. He had a system — lift, tilt, run a finger along the underside — and I stood three feet away doing arithmetic in my head: how long since I'd cleaned the bench drawer where the extra pin-vise lived (nine days), whether the rag I'd used to wipe it smelled of oil (everything smelled of oil), whether Corvin's ledger of repairs could be cross-checked against parts inventory before lunch.

It could. Of course it could. We bought parts for jobs we'd already finished and cannibalized donors for jobs we hadn't recorded, because recording meant taxes and taxes meant questions and questions, in Vellmark that autumn, meant doors kicked in at four in the morning.

"Your stock of mainsprings," Vogel said, "is eleven units per the registry. I count fourteen."

"Three are donor movements," I said. "Stripped for parts. They're not functional mainsprings, they're—"

"Show me."

I showed him. Two of the three genuinely were stripped; the third was intact because I'd been saving it for the observatory job, and I watched the junior's finger hover over it while my pulse performed a small drumroll against my collar. Vogel wrote something. Whether it was *violation* or *note to self*, his handwriting declined to say.

Then the junior reached the tall hall clock, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards into whatever cellar Vellmark keeps for bad apprentices.

I had forgotten the calipers.

Not forgotten-forgotten. I had decided, at some point in the small hours, that the barrel of the hall clock was safe because the case was heavy and the door stuck, and I had filed that decision somewhere sleep couldn't reach it. Now a young man with a clipboard and a taste for tilting drawers was working the stuck door open with both hands, and six inches of calibrated steel were sitting inside it like a confession.

"Careful," I said, and my voice came out wrong — too high by a quarter tone. Clockmaker's ear; I heard it myself.

The junior looked up. So did the quiet man by the door. In inspections as in surgery, the moment the patient's voice changes is the moment everyone leans in.

The door swung wide. The junior leaned his head into the case, and I had perhaps two seconds, and in those two seconds my mouth did something my brain had not authorized.

"That regulator's out of beat," I said loudly, pointing at the Vienna regulator on the wall. "Badly. Look at the recoil — hear it? Listen."

There was no recoil problem. There was no sound at all; I hadn't wound it in days. But Vogel was a thorough man and thorough men cannot walk past a claim they haven't checked, and he turned toward the wall clock, and the junior straightened up out of the hall clock's barrel to see what his superior was looking at, and the calipers stayed in the dark like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"There's nothing wrong with this clock," Vogel said.

"Not that one. There." I crossed the room — too fast, I knew even as I did it, but slowing down would have taken nerve I was spending elsewhere — and put my hand flat on the Vienna regulator's case. "The crutch. It's bent a hair. Someone moved the clock without leveling it, probably the delivery men last spring, and now the escapement drops unevenly on the left bank. It loses about ninety seconds a week. I've been meaning to correct it."

"You've been meaning to."

"Workshop policy," Corvin said dryly, without looking up from his bench. "We fix customers' clocks before our own. Bad for morale otherwise."

Vogel studied the regulator. Then me. Then, briefly, the ceiling, in the manner of a man asking some higher authority for patience. "Open it."

My hands, I am pleased to report even now, were steady. Whatever curse afflicts Corvin had settled on me in mirror image: the worse the moment, the truer the fingers. I opened the case, swung out the movement, and pointed to the crutch wire, which was — by genuine accident, by the accumulated kindness of entropy — very slightly out of true. Entropy, occasionally, takes payment in advance.

Vogel leaned in. He looked for a long time. Long enough that I had leisure to think about the fact that he might know nothing about clocks and be waiting for me to sweat, or might know a great deal about clocks and be waiting for me to sweat, and that either way sweating was the losing move.

"Ninety seconds a week," he said finally.

"Closer to a hundred in humid weather."

He straightened. Something in his face shifted — not warmth, but the next thing down from it, which is the recognition of a fellow technician. "And you service the observatory's master clock," he said. "The letter went out under this shop's name."

"The master sent me. I finished the escapement work there last month."

"Finished it?"

"Running within tolerance," I said. "Within official tolerance." Four minutes, said the part of me that never stops talking. Four minutes, and every train in Vellmark agreeing with a lie I put there with a screwdriver and a steady hand. I kept my face arranged into apprentice blandness and thanked whatever gods govern liars that Vogel's folio was thick with other people's sins.

He closed the case himself, gently, the way you close a case when you respect the mechanism inside it. Then he nodded at the hall clock, and my heart performed its second drumroll of the morning —

— but the junior only latched it shut, because the moment had passed, because attention in an inspection is a river and you can only stand in it once.

They found the discrepancies in the parts ledger anyway. Of course they did; I'd known they would; that was the arithmetic I'd been running all morning. Vogel spent twenty minutes over the book while Corvin explained, in the flattest tones available to a man of sixty, why twelve repair tickets referenced nine purchased mainsprings. Donor movements. Cannibalization. Waste not. The explanations were true, mostly, and boring, entirely, and boredom is the best armor ever devised.

At eleven minutes past noon — I remember because the shop's least reliable wall clock said ten past and I corrected it in my head, a reflex I'd have laughed at any other day — Vogel closed his folio.

"The workshop passes," he said. "Subject to a corrected Schedule C within fourteen days, and a fine for inventory irregularity." He named a figure that would hurt but not kill us. "Herr Corvin. Your registration is current. Your apprentice's status is..." A pause, a page turned, the smallest hesitation, like a hand hovering over a question on an exam. "...under review. Standard procedure. You'll receive notice."

Under review. Three words, weightless as paper, heavy as iron. I thought of Elias Brand's pen hesitating over a lineage form, of the answer he'd written that wasn't true, of the way we'd both stood afterward not looking at each other while the room filled with the sound of a lie settling into place like a jewel into its setting.

"Thank you for your thoroughness," Corvin said.

"Thank your regulator," said Vogel. "Most people don't maintain anything anymore."

They left the way they'd come, in single file, the quiet man last. At the threshold he turned and looked at me for exactly one second longer than courtesy required — cataloguing, filing, carrying my face off to some drawer of his own — and then the door shut and the bells above it shivered and stopped.

* * *

For a full minute neither of us spoke. Then Corvin got up, walked to the tall hall clock, opened the barrel, and took out the calipers. He held them in his palm like something recovered from a wreck.

"Ninety seconds a week," he said. "In humid weather."

"I panicked."

"You redirected a state inspector onto a flaw you invented, using a flaw you didn't know existed, in a room containing evidence that could have gotten us both on a train east." He set the calipers down. "If that's panic, Paloma, I'd hate to compete with your calm."

He went to the till and counted out the fine without comment, which was its own kind of grief — a life measured now in what the regime could take from the drawer. When he turned around his eyes were wet, but his voice wasn't.

"Fourteen days for the corrected Schedule C. And an apprentice status 'under review.'" He took off his spectacles and polished them on his apron, a gesture I'd seen ten thousand times and suddenly understood as prayer. "You should know that reviews don't go the way of the reviewed. Not lately."

"I know."

"And yet you're smiling."

I was. I couldn't help it — it wasn't joy, it was the awful giddy relief of a held breath released after three hours, and it shook something loose in my chest that had been braced since Tuesday's letter. "We passed," I said. "Barely. But barely counts. Ask any pendulum."

Corvin snorted, which from him was applause.

I spent the afternoon doing what I always did when the world reminded me it was made of paperwork: I cleaned tools and put them where they belonged, all except the ones that belonged nowhere, which went back into their hiding places with fresh wedges and honest dust. As the light went gold behind the window I rehung the Vienna regulator, straightened the crutch properly, wound it, and set the pendulum swinging. Ninety seconds a week, no longer. Some lies I intended to retire.

A knock came at the door just past dusk — soft, twice, in a rhythm I'd begun to know the way you know your own heartbeat. Through the glass, in the blue evening, stood Elias Brand with his coat collar up and a paper bag of chestnuts in one hand and something else in his eyes that I couldn't read yet and already feared.

"They posted the next classification list today," he said, when I opened the door. "Paloma. You need to see where the names are."
