## 11. A Slow Kind of Love

The Weiss file weighed almost nothing. That was the obscenity of it — three generations of a family reduced to ninety grams of paper, light enough to balance on one finger, heavy enough that my hand shook holding it.

I had told myself, after the Underhill girl's verification, that the first falsification was an aberration. A lapse. A moment of weakness brought on by her hands in the lamplight and the specific way she had said *my mother's people were clockmakers too*, which was not a confession and was not a denial and was exactly the kind of answer the forms were designed to make impossible. I had written *lineage: verified* beneath her name, and I had walked home that night telling myself it would never happen again, because men who tell themselves things like that are usually lying, and I have never enjoyed being an exception to a rule.

Now here were the Weisss: Marta and Josef and the daughter, Anneliese, aged six, whose entry required only a single line. And here was the classification schedule, freshly distributed, with its new column headed *Disposal Category* as if human beings were surplus inventory to be auctioned in autumn.

I sat at my desk in the census bureau long after the others had gone home. The lamp made a small warm island of my desk and left the rest of the room in bureaucratic darkness, rows of empty chairs like witnesses who had turned their backs. I took out my pen. I uncapped it. I performed the entire theater of hesitation, even though there was no one to perform it for, because apparently I needed to believe I had deliberated before I did what we both knew I would do.

In the box marked *Heritage Classification* for the Weiss family, I wrote the word that would keep them whole. My handwriting did not waver, which surprised me. It came out looking exactly like every other entry in the file — competent, neutral, the handwriting of a man who has no opinions about anything. That is the real skill of a census taker, it turns out. Not accuracy. Invisibility.

I blotted the page. I filed the folder back among four hundred identical folders, and I understood, sitting there in the dark, that I had crossed from a man who had once lied into a man who lies. The distinction seemed important. It seemed like the kind of distinction Paloma Underhill would appreciate, being a woman who spent her days distinguishing between gears that differed by a tenth of a millimeter.

Then I went to find her, which was, I grant you, an insane thing for a careful man to do.

* * *

The observatory gallery runs in a circle above the great instrument room, a railed walkway where visiting dignitaries once stood to look down at the telescopes. Now the dignitaries had been replaced by clerks and the telescopes by the master clock's open cabinet, but the gallery still had the quality of a place designed for watching without being seen. Which is why I chose it, and why I was not entirely surprised when Paloma appeared at the far curve of it, toolbag over her shoulder, and stopped walking the moment she saw me.

"You're here late," she said.

"So are you."

"The clock doesn't keep office hours." She came closer along the rail, keeping a careful arm's length between us, as though the space itself were a form she was filling out correctly. "Neither does the census, I suppose."

"The census keeps all hours. It's very patriotic that way." Below us the instrument room lay in shadow, the clock's pendulum hanging dead in its case while she worked on its replacement. "I filed the Weiss verification today."

I hadn't meant to say it. It came out the way a gear slips — one wrong angle and suddenly everything downstream moves differently than intended. She went very still. In the dimness I could see her weighing me, the way I had seen her weigh a movement earlier that week, turning it over, checking it for flaws.

"And?" she said.

"It's filed. Complete. Accurate." I let the last word sit there between us until it did its work. Accurate was not a word that could survive scrutiny, and she knew it, and I knew she knew it, and this was how we spoke now — in words that carried more than their dictionary weight.

"Josef Weiss," she said quietly, "fixed our shop's roof after the storm two winters ago. Wouldn't take payment. Said a clockmaker who can't hear rain on a dry ceiling isn't much use to anyone."

"I'll remember that," I said, "if anyone ever asks me why."

"Will they ask?"

"No," I said, because lying to her felt different from lying to the ledger, and also because I wanted it to be true badly enough that it nearly was.

She set her toolbag down on the gallery floor. Below us, the dead clock stood with its case open like a patient awaiting surgery, and she looked at it the way surgeons look, I imagine — with affection and grievance in equal parts.

"Do you know what an escapement is?" she asked.

"I process forms about ancestry and shoe size. Educate me."

She moved to the rail and looked down at the clock, and I moved beside her, and if anyone had come up the stairs we would have looked like two professionals discussing municipal timekeeping, which was the point of everything we did now.

"A pendulum wants to swing forever," she said. "That's its nature. Gravity pulls it down, momentum carries it through, and it would just — go. Faster and looser and wilder until it tore itself apart. The escapement is what stops it. Or rather—" she corrected herself with a small frown, the same frown I'd watched her give a stubborn mainspring, "—it doesn't stop it. Nothing can stop it. It *releases* it. A little at a time. Tooth by tooth. Every swing, the escapement lets the wheel advance by exactly one tooth, no more, and that controlled release is what makes it a clock instead of just a falling object."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"I don't know," she said, which was a lie, and a poor one, and we both let it stand.

We stood there in the half-dark with the city spread out beyond the gallery windows, ten thousand windows already going dark under curfew, and I thought about what she was actually saying, which was that everything wild wants to run and must be meted out in survivable portions. Time. Feeling. Whatever it was that happened to the air between us whenever we occupied the same room, which had been happening since the first afternoon at the observatory and which neither of us had named, because naming it would be a tooth released all at once, and the mechanism would tear itself apart.

"I nearly wrote the truth today," I said. "On another form. Not yours. Mine, in a sense."

"What stopped you?"

"Fear," I said. "And then the thought that fear is just the escapement of cowardice. It releases it a little at a time so you never notice you're falling."

She laughed — short, surprised, quickly smothered, because sound carries in an observatory. "That's a terrible metaphor. Escapements are precise. Fear isn't."

"Fear is extremely precise. It measures everything. It's the only thing in this country that does."

She turned to look at me then, fully, and the distance between us — the disciplined arm's length we had maintained for weeks — suddenly seemed like the most ridiculous engineering flaw I had ever encountered. I could see her deciding something. I could see her decide against it.

"There's coffee in my kit," she said instead. "If you're staying."

"I'm staying," I said.

* * *

We drank terrible coffee from the same tin cup because she had packed only one, packing light, the way everyone sensible was packing light now, and we talked about nothing for an hour — the weather, the price of brass, the inspector general's suspicious mustache — and it was the most honest conversation I had all week, because nothing in it could be used against anyone.

Past curfew, when the risk of her presence became a thing with actual weight, she began packing her tools, and I watched her wrap the loupe Corvin had given her — no, not Corvin's yet; her own, well-worn — and I heard myself say, "You should know that your name will come up. On a list. Eventually. The system doesn't tolerate exceptions; it metabolizes them."

She didn't stop wrapping. "I know."

"And you should know that when it does—"

"Don't finish that sentence." She closed the toolbag with a click like a case snapping shut over a finished movement. "Sentences like that, you can't take back. And I need you taking nothing back right now. I need everything exactly where it is."

"That's not—"

"It's a pendulum, Elias." She picked up the bag and slung it over her shoulder, and for a moment she was silhouetted against the window and the city beyond, and I understood that I would memorize this exact image and carry it somewhere no ledger could reach it. "Everything swings. You hold it. You release it. One tooth at a time."

She went down the stairwell, and I listened to her footsteps fade, and then I stood alone in the gallery above the dead clock and admitted to the dark what I had been refusing to admit for weeks: that I was not falling. Falling is fast. This was slower than that, and therefore worse, and therefore better.

Below me the master clock waited, silent, its pendulum hanging like a held breath. When it ran again, everything would run with it — the trains included.

And I had begun, only begun, to wonder what would happen if it ran a little wrong.
