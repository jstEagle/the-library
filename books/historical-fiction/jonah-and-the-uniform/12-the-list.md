## 12. The List

The list went up on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult to Tuesdays everywhere.

I had gone out for lamp oil and bread — the two commodities Vellmark still pretended were plentiful — and I passed the census office the way you pass a dog you suspect of biting: quickly, eyes forward, with a respectful margin. Except that morning the window was crowded. People stood shoulder to shoulder in the gray light, reading something posted behind the glass, and nobody was talking. In my experience, silence in a crowd is worse than shouting. Shouting means someone has already been found. Silence means everyone is still looking.

I should have kept walking. Master Corvin had a saying about curiosity — he claimed it killed more clocks than rust — but my feet had opinions of their own, and they carried me to the edge of the window where I could read the posted page at an angle, over the shoulder of a woman in a coal-seller's coat.

It was a classification list. The third one since the law took effect, each longer than the last. Names in two columns under two headings, the regime's favorite trick: *Registered — Retained* and *Registered — Removed*. The bureaucratic poetry of it. As if we were books the city was deciding whether to keep on its shelves.

I read the left column first, out of some animal instinct to check whether the people I knew were safe. Herr Weiss was there. Frau Weiss. Marta Weiss, age nine. Retained, all three, though I knew for a fact their papers said otherwise, because Elias Brand's careful hand had made it so, and the knowledge sat in my chest like a swallowed coin — heavy, cold, and impossible to spit out.

Then, because vanity is a stronger instinct than self-preservation, I looked at the right column.

*Underhill, P. — Apprentice, Horological. Classification pending review. Scheduled: [date].*

The date was eleven days off.

Eleven days after the master clock was due for completion.

I did not faint. I want that on the record. I have read enough novels where women swoon at bad news, and I would like it known that what I actually did was count the coins in my pocket, realize I was four hellers short of the lamp oil, and walk home in the rain without any oil, any bread, or any visible expression. Corvin always said I had a clockwork face. That morning I was grateful for it.

* * *

The workshop smelled of brass and cold tea when I came in. Corvin was hunched over his bench, working a file with the careful, trembling strokes that had become his signature — the shake had gotten worse all autumn, and he compensated by slowing everything down until his work looked almost deliberate again. He glanced up when the door opened, saw my face, and set the file down.

"Out with it," he said. "You've got the look of a mainspring about to let go."

"There's a new list."

"I know. Saw it yesterday." He went back to his filing, which meant he wanted me to say it anyway. Corvin believed that fear spoken aloud lost weight, the way a rumor loses detail with every telling. He may have been right about rumors. He was wrong about fear.

"My name is on it," I said. "Right column. Pending review."

The file stopped moving. For a long moment the only sound in the shop was the wall of clocks — twenty-three of them, all ticking slightly out of phase, so that the room never had one heartbeat but many, a small crowd of hearts that disagreed about when the next second should arrive. I have lived with that sound my whole life. It has always sounded like safety to me. That morning it sounded like mockery.

"Pending review," Corvin repeated. He turned around slowly. His hands were steady now, which is how I knew how bad it was. Terror works on him like a brace; the shaking stops when something matters. "That's not removal yet. That's the antechamber. There are appeals."

"Appeals take months. The list says scheduled. Scheduled for the twelfth of next month."

"The clock finishes on the first."

"Yes," I said. "I noticed."

He took off his spectacles and polished them on his apron, a gesture I had seen ten thousand times and suddenly understood completely. He wasn't cleaning the lenses. He was buying seconds. A man who had spent sixty years among timepieces, hoarding them even now.

"You'll finish early," he said at last.

"Or I won't finish at all." I heard my own voice come out flat and precise, the voice I used on stubborn escapements. "Corvin. Do you understand what that date means? They've set my classification for the day after the observatory work is due. Either that's coincidence —"

"It isn't. This government doesn't believe in coincidence. It believes in schedules."

"— or someone decided that the apprentice who repairs the master clock shouldn't be removed until the clock runs. They're keeping me the way you keep a tool. Use it, then put it back in the drawer. Only their drawer is a train."

The word *train* landed between us and neither of us picked it up. We both knew which trains. Everyone in Vellmark knew which trains, in the way you know the direction of the wind — not because anyone explains it, but because you feel it on your face.

Corvin put his spectacles back on. Behind the glass his eyes were wet, but his voice, when it came, was dry as a bearing housing. "Then you'll be careful. You'll be precise. You'll finish the clock exactly when they expect it finished, no sooner, no later, and you will give them nothing to notice between now and then."

"And after?"

"After," he said, "is not a country I can see from here."

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat up in my room above the shop with the lamp turned low, disassembling and reassembling a carriage clock I'd repaired twice already, just to have somewhere to put my hands. Around three in the morning I gave up on the fiction of work and did what I'd been avoiding since the census office: I thought.

Here is what thinking gets you at three in the morning, in case you've never tried it. It gets you arithmetic. Cold, clean, merciless arithmetic.

Eleven days between completion and classification. Eleven days in which I could appeal, bribe, forge, flee, or trust — in ascending order of impossibility. Appeal: to whom? The same office that printed the list. Bribe: with what? An apprentice's wages and a drawer full of hidden tools. Forge: papers existed, I knew they existed, because the Weiss family's new ones existed, drawn by a forger whose name I was carefully ignorant of. And flee — well. Fleeing meant abandoning Corvin, and the shop, and every clock I'd ever brought back from the dead, and it also meant the roads were watched, and it also meant I would be running forever, and I was twenty-four years old and tired at three in the morning in a way that sleep does not fix.

Trust. That left trust. Trust in Elias Brand, who wore the regime's gray coat and carried the regime's ledger and had, on one afternoon in October, looked at a form requiring my lineage and written down a lie in a clerk's beautiful hand. He had done it without hesitation, which frightened me more than hesitation would have. Hesitation means a man is deciding. No hesitation means he decided long ago, before the question was ever asked, and I did not know yet what else he had decided, or what he was willing to spend.

And there was the other arithmetic, the one I kept turning over like a coin with two identical faces. My name appeared on the list dated eleven days after the clock's completion. Which meant the clock mattered. Which meant the clock's accuracy mattered, because the transport office had been howling about schedule failures since the mechanism died, because the trains ran off the observatory's time, all of them, including the trains I could not bear to think about.

A clockmaker knows what a clock can do. A clock can run fast. A clock can run slow. A clock that runs slow by a small, invisible amount looks exactly like a clock that runs true, right up until the moment someone checks it against the stars, and nobody in Vellmark checks anything against the stars except me.

I put the carriage clock down. Its little heart ticked away, faithful and stupid, keeping perfect time for a world that had stopped deserving it.

"Eleven days," I said to the dark. And then, because precision is a kind of prayer: "Nine hundred fifty thousand, four hundred seconds. Give or take."

* * *

Elias came the next evening, ostensibly to deliver a receipt for the lineage verification, actually because he always came, twice a week now, with pretexts growing thinner and thinner until last week he'd simply arrived holding a paper bag of chestnuts and no explanation at all. Corvin retired to the back room with theatrical promptness, muttering about inventory, and left us alone with the twenty-three disagreeing heartbeats.

"You've seen it," he said. Not a question. He stood by the bench with his hat in his hands — he'd taken to removing it indoors lately, another thin pretext, since no census taker removes his hat for a receipt.

"The whole street's seen it. Half of them can't read past their own names, but they've seen it."

"Paloma." He said my name the way he entered figures into the ledger, with care, as if a mistake here would propagate through every line below it. "There's a process. Review boards. I've seen cases reopened."

"Have you? Or have you seen cases postponed?"

He didn't answer, which was an answer, so I told him the other part — the dates, the eleven days, the tidy scheduling of my usefulness. I watched his face while I did it. He has a good face for hiding things, which sounds like a compliment and isn't; it's the face of a man who practiced. But around the eyes he goes slack when something lands hard, like a dial with a loose needle, and the needle swung.

"They're using the deadline," he said quietly. "Your hands finish the clock, the clock restores the schedule, and the schedule moves the —" He stopped himself. Even alone, in a room full of ticking witnesses, he couldn't say it.

"Trains," I said. "You can say trains. Trains are just trains."

We both looked at each other and agreed silently to pretend that was true.

"What do you need?" he asked.

It was such a plain question. Not *what do we do*, not *God help us* — what do you need, as if I were a mechanism he intended to service properly this time. I almost answered honestly. I almost said: I need you to stop wearing that coat. I need you to tell me what happens to the people in the right column after the trains leave, because I've built a cathedral of not-asking and it's collapsing. I need you to say the thing you don't say when you bring chestnuts and receipts, because if my name is on a list with a date beside it, I have developed a sudden appetite for words that cost something.

What I actually said was: "Time. I need time."

"How much?"

"That depends entirely on what it's for."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide not to press, the way you watch a man decide not to open a door with smoke coming from under it. "The review board sits on the ninth," he said instead. "Before your date. Cases go through in alphabetical order. Underhill is late in the alphabet."

"Underhill," I said, "is always late in the alphabet. It's the story of my life. U comes after everything worth being."

That almost made him smile, and seeing it almost arrive, I understood something with the terrible clarity of a freshly cleaned lens: I would do whatever it took to keep this man standing in my workshop taking his hat off for no reason. Not the grand romantic whatever of novels — the small, mechanical whatever. The whatever of gears. Each tooth engaging the next, each release controlled, each motion inevitable once started.

"Paloma," he said, low, "whatever happens with the clock, with the board, with any of it —"

"Don't," I said, gently. "Not yet. If you say it now, it becomes something we could lose. Say it when it's safe, and until then I'll assume."

"*Assume?*"

"Assumption is a legitimate horological technique. All the best mechanisms run on it."

* * *

After he left I sat at the bench until the lamps burned low, and I took out my notebook — the private one, the one with the escapement calculations — and beneath a page of figures I wrote three lines, small, in pencil:

*They need the clock finished by the first.*
*They need it accurate.*
*Nobody has defined accurate.*

I looked at those lines for a long while. Outside, the curfew bells rang ten o'clock across the rooftops of Vellmark, every tower obedient to the dead master clock's last honest hour, and I thought about what a four-minute error looks like from inside a mechanism — invisible, deniable, patient. I closed the notebook before the thought could finish forming, because thoughts, like springs, will snap if you wind them too fast.

But the notebook doesn't forget what's written in it. And neither, now, did I.
