## 13. Sabotage or Salvation

The Transport Office keeps its files in alphabetical order and its conscience in no order at all. I learned this on my third day of employment, when I watched a clerk stamp DEPARTURE AUTHORIZED on a manifest he had not read, because the clock above his desk said quarter-to-five and quarter-to-five meant his coat was already on.

That was before the master clock died. Now there is no quarter-to-five. There is only a hole in the ceiling of Vellmark's schedule, through which everything falls.

I know this because I spent Thursday afternoon in the transport annex, ostensibly verifying residency records for the yard workers — which I did, faithfully, four names to a page, my pen as honest as it has been in weeks, which is to say not very — and actually reading everything else that crossed the desks around me. Bureaucrats assume a man with a ledger is blind to everything but the ledger. This assumption has kept me alive twice now.

What I read was this: the departure sequence for all rolling stock out of Vellmark Station is keyed to the observatory signal. The master clock drops an electrical impulse at the hour; the stationmaster's board advances; trains are cleared in order of priority. No impulse, no sequence. The military transports carrying classified persons south have been sitting on sidings for nine days, engines cold, while the office runs on pocket watches that disagree with one another like witnesses to a crime.

Nine days. The Weiss family's papers say they left eleven days ago. Arithmetic is a kind of mercy, if you don't look at it too closely.

* * *

Here is what I could not stop calculating on the walk home, past the shuttered bakery, past the poster of the Director looking like a disappointed schoolmaster carved from tallow:

A dead clock delays the trains. A slow clock delays them longer, and deniably. Four minutes of error, propagated across every departure board in the city, would produce a schedule that never agrees with itself — trains held, connections missed, orders countermanded, the whole apparatus tangling in its own arithmetic like a man trying to run upstairs in a dream. And nobody would call it sabotage. They would call it *mechanical fault*. There is a form for mechanical fault. I have seen the drawer it lives in.

And here is what followed that thought, arriving the way consequences do — late, inevitable, wearing its own uniform:

If I helped devise such a thing — if I so much as stood in the room while Paloma Underhill considered it — then I am no longer a census taker who has told some lies. I am a saboteur. The distinction matters to no one but me, and soon it will not matter to me either, because both men hang from the same beam.

I climbed the stairs to my rooms, lit the lamp, took off my coat, put the coat back on, took it off again, and sat down to spend the night losing an argument with myself.

* * *

*Consider it rationally,* said the first voice, the one that sounds like my father, may he rest, though he never rested a day. *You are a small man in a small office. Your job is to record the world, not to rearrange it. Every act of heroism you have committed so far has been a clerical error dressed up as courage. Falsified lineages. A burned draft. You have been sliding down this slope for months, and the slope ends at the river, and the river in Vellmark in November is where they find people.*

*Yes,* said the second voice, which sounds uncomfortably like Paloma, dry and level, the voice of someone who repairs things by understanding exactly how they break. *And consider who is standing at the bottom of the slope already. The Weisses. The Ostrers. Half the families on Kessel Street whose names I wrote into the wrong columns because the right column was a cattle car. They didn't choose the slope either. It was built over their houses.*

*That is sentiment. Sentiment is not a defense at tribunal.*

*Neither is accuracy. Ask the last man who filed a perfectly truthful report about Kessel Street.*

I got up and made coffee I did not want. The apartment has one window, and beyond it the city lay in its usual November murk, and somewhere in that murk the observatory dome, invisible tonight, housing a dead heart that an apprentice has been keeping alive by hand for weeks. Housing Paloma, probably, at this hour. Working alone, as apprentices do, with those careful hands and that face that gives away nothing except, occasionally, everything, if you happen to be watching for it.

Which I am. Have been. Since the first afternoon, when I asked whether the escapement regulated time or merely rationed it, and received the answer: *Rationed. Time is infinite. Rations are political.* I have thought about that sentence more than I have thought about most people I've known.

*Sabotage,* said the first voice, when I sat back down, *is discovered. That is its defining feature. Clocks fail honestly every day — that's why the excuse exists, that's why it will work. But a deliberate error requires a deliberator, and deliberators leave traces. Notes. Glances. The specific quality of silence between two people who know something. Someone will notice that the two of you look at each other like conspirators.*

*We don't look at each other like conspirators.*

*You look at each other like something worse. Something a jealous colleague could describe to an investigator in one sentence.*

This was true, and it sat in the room with me for a long time, taking up most of the available air.

Because let us be exact, since exactness is the only virtue I have left: I am in love with Paloma Underhill. I have been in love since approximately the second week, which coincides precisely with the moment I falsified the lineage entry, so I cannot even establish which came first, the cowardice or the feeling, and I suspect they are the same thing viewed from different sides of the ledger. I have never said it. Saying it would convert a private weakness into evidence. As long as it is unspoken, it is deniable. Deniability is my native country and I am its most loyal citizen.

*A slow clock saves strangers. A silent mouth saves you. Choose.*

*Those aren't the only options.*

*Name another.*

I named several. None survived inspection.

* * *

Toward two in the morning I did what any reasonable man does at an impasse: I went through my files. Not the official ones — the drafts I should have burned weeks ago and did not, because destroying them felt like confessing and keeping them felt like insurance, and I have been unable to determine which instinct is more dangerous. Insurance, I decided, watching my hands shake over the pages. Cowards keep receipts. Heroes burn them. I have been telling myself I'm gathering courage for the burning.

Page after page of classifications. *Underhill, P. — apprentice — mixed heritage — pending review.* Pending review, dated the twelfth. The twelfth is three days after the master clock's scheduled completion. I had noticed this before and filed the noticing in the place where I keep things too large to think about: the completion date, the list date, the tidy bureaucratic coincidence by which the woman who fixes the city's time will learn, the same week, what her own time is worth.

*There it is,* said the first voice, gently now, which was worse than cruel. *You can't save her with a clock. A slow clock delays trains. It does not amend a classification list. If you want to save her, you'd need to alter the record itself — again, further, deeper — and every alteration is another thread an auditor can pull, and someone is already pulling your threads. Voss noticed the Weiss entry. Voss smiled at you Tuesday. Voss does not smile.*

*I know.*

*Then you know the question isn't whether you'll hang for small crimes or large ones. It's whether you'll hang for nothing, or for something. Because Voss has enough for nothing already.*

I put out the lamp, because there is a particular clarity that comes with darkness, being essentially honesty without witnesses.

*For nothing,* I said aloud, to the dark. *Or for something. Those are the terms.*

*Terms,* said the dark, which had stopped sounding like my father and started sounding like everyone.

* * *

I slept perhaps ninety minutes and dreamed of pendulums. In the dream the pendulum swung wider and wider until it was not measuring time but beating it, and the tick became a gavel, and the gavel became a coupling rod, and the train pulled out of Vellmark Station on time, perfectly on time, every carriage full, and I stood on the platform checking my watch and saying *accurate, accurate* to no one, the way a man says his prayers.

I woke at five with the answer, which is to say with no answer, only the shape of one, the way you wake knowing you dreamed a solution and holding only the residue.

But residue was enough to arrange the argument into its final form, so let me set it down while the nerve holds.

Argument for safety: I owe the state nothing, but I owe myself my life, and life is the raw material of every future good act. A hanged man falsifies no more ledgers. If I stay clean — visibly, verifiably clean — I might survive Voss, survive the investigation, survive long enough to protect Paloma through channels, paperwork, quiet erasures. Slow resistance. The resistance of the patient clerk.

Counter-argument: the patient clerk is a fiction we tell ourselves while the trains load. There are no channels. The paperwork IS the machine. My quiet erasures have saved perhaps thirty people and each one increased the chance of saving none. Cleanliness is not a strategy; it is an aesthetic.

Argument for action: a clock running four minutes slow is the smallest possible crime with the largest possible yield. It is not dynamite; it is friction. Friction kills machines the way doubt kills tribunals — slowly, invisibly, from inside the mechanism. Hundreds of deportations delayed, scattered, desynchronized. Some escaped entirely. And it looks like wear, like weather, like the ordinary incompetence of matter.

Counter-argument: *looks like.* Every safeguard I have relied upon so far has eventually required another. Today a slow clock, tomorrow a forged transit pass, the next day a body between a soldier and a door. The ladder only goes down. And Paloma — Paloma would bear half the risk for none of my reasons. Has anyone even asked her? Have I proposed this, or am I, in the privacy of my cowardice, planning to let her arrive at it herself, so that the idea is hers and the guilt is distributed and my hands stay warm?

God. That one I had to sit with.

Because yes. That is exactly the shape of it. I have spent three nights constructing arguments for a thing I intend to suggest to her so gently she'll believe she thought of it, and I have done this not to protect her but to protect my account of myself — so that afterward, whatever afterward holds, I can say *she decided.* The census taker's soul: always filing, never signing.

Well. Not this time. If the thing is worth doing it is worth doing under my own name, which is worth nothing anyway, which is precisely the point — worthless things should be spent on worthwhile ones. That is the entire secret of economics and possibly of grace.

* * *

So: dawn found me at my table with a blank sheet of paper, doing sums like a man writing a suicide note in arithmetic.

Four minutes. Not six — six reads as design. Not two — two passes unnoticed, saves no one. Four sits at the exact threshold: large enough that departures slip and slide and collide, small enough that a technician swears on his mother's grave the mechanism is within tolerance, because it nearly is. Error, not treason. The difference between murder and manslaughter, expressed in seconds.

Delay compounds. A four-minute error cascades through a sequenced schedule the way a dropped stitch climbs a stocking. By mid-morning the stationmaster is dispatching by guesswork. By noon the military liaison is shouting. By evening someone demands the clock be inspected — and here is the beautiful, terrible hinge — an inspection finds *nothing*, because there is nothing to find, only a mechanism in perfect health that happens to disagree with the truth by four minutes, and the inspector signs the form, and the trains stay tangled, and the tangled stays plausible, week after week, until someone thinks to check the calibration against a chronometer brought from outside.

Weeks. Perhaps a month. Perhaps enough.

It is not salvation. Let no one mistake it. It is a wedge, not a door. But wedges are how doors open, and doors are how people leave, and leaving is the only salvation currently stocked in the shops of Vellmark.

And the risk? State it plainly, Elias, the way you'd state a figure in a column. If discovered: for me, the investigation I am already under acquires a verdict. For Paloma — already listed, already pending — discovery means the list moves from pending to final. The clock meant to save her neighbors would be the instrument of her ruin. That is the equation. That is what I am proposing to multiply by her name.

Unless. Unless the list itself could be dealt with, and here my professional knowledge, my filthy specialized knowledge, whispers its suggestion: lists are paper. Paper burns. I have burned before; the archive is flammable; the archive is where I work. But that thought leads down its own stairwell into its own cellar, and I am not ready to descend it tonight. One crime per dawn. I am a moderate in all things except the ones that matter.

I folded the paper with the sums into quarters and put it in my breast pocket, against the chest, where I could feel it like a coal.

* * *

At seven I was at the observatory, earlier than any inspector, earlier than sense. The porter let me in with the indifference porters reserve for men who have become fixtures. Up the iron stairs, along the gallery, past the great orrery hanging motionless in the gray light, to the clock room — and there she was, sleeves rolled, loupe in one eye, hands inside the open case of the master clock as if she were taking its pulse.

She heard me and didn't turn. "You're early," she said. "The regime doesn't start until eight."

"The regime," I said, "is going to have a very confusing autumn." And I took the folded paper from my breast pocket, and laid it on the bench beside her tools, and waited, heart going like the worst regulator in Palatinate, for her to finish the escapement, wipe her hands, unfold my treason, and tell me whether we were about to be heroes or corpses — and whether, whichever it was, we would finally have to say so out loud.
