## 14. The Weiss Family Leaves

The note came on a Tuesday, folded inside a receipt for a mainspring that Herr Weiss had never actually ordered. That was the point, of course. Paper that could be burned without raising questions, arithmetic that looked like arithmetic. I unfolded it at the workbench while Corvin ground a pivot across the room, and read four lines in Herr Weiss's cramped, careful hand — the handwriting of a man who had spent his whole life making things legible for other people to check.

*Thursday. The 6:40 freight takes livestock east through the junction. We will be the livestock. If you can, come say goodbye. If you cannot, we understand better than you know.*

I read it twice and then put it under the bench blotter, which is where I kept everything those days that I couldn't afford to look at: the second census summons, Corvin's medical certificate with its stamp of *unfit for classification*, the list of tools I'd memorized in order of what would hurt most to lose.

"Bad news?" Corvin asked without looking up. He always knew when I'd received paper. He said my breathing changed, that I read documents the way I'd once read love letters — as if the words might rearrange themselves if I blinked.

"The Weisss leave Thursday," I said.

His grinding wheel stopped. For a moment the workshop was so quiet I could hear the carriage clock on the shelf ticking out its small stubborn life, and beneath it, faintly, the grandfather clock in the corner that hadn't told correct time since 1931 but which neither of us would sell, because some lies you keep running out of loyalty.

"Thursday," he repeated. "So it's real now."

"It was always real. Now it has a date."

Corvin nodded slowly and went back to his wheel. He didn't offer condolences or courage or any of the things people say when they want to feel they've done something. He was seventy-one years old and had learned, in a way I was only beginning to learn, that most of what happens to us offers no handle to grip. You polish what's in front of you and you try not to think about the drawer full of things you can't fix.

But that night, after we'd packed the tools into their satchels — the nightly ritual that had become as automatic as winding, our hands moving over bench vise and calipers and the good screwdrivers with the hornbeam handles — Corvin said, "Take them something."

"Like what?"

"Whatever fits." He closed his satchel buckle. "People leaving with nothing walk slower. I've seen it. A thing to carry changes how a body moves through a train station. It gives the eyes somewhere to be."

He said it lightly, but I knew there were years underneath it — years I still didn't fully understand, years of clocks repaired for men whose names he wouldn't say, precision performed as a kind of penance I wouldn't learn the shape of until later. He gave me the loupe that week too. But that comes in its own turn; grief keeps its own ledger, and I'm getting ahead of it.

* * *

Thursday morning I closed the workshop half a day early for a "delivery to the eastern quarter," which even our new clerk — the indifferent one who had replaced the first indifferent one, indifference apparently being a heritable trait in government clerks — did not bother to question. I took the long way to the Weiss apartment, past the bakery where Frau Weiss used to buy the stale loaves at discount and split them with the widow upstairs, past the schoolyard where little Anneli Weiss had once shown me a dead beetle she was convinced was only sleeping, and asked me if I could wind it back up.

I was nine. I told her no, that beetles didn't have springs. She said everything must have a spring, or how would it walk? I have thought about that argument roughly every day since, and I have never won it.

Frau Weiss opened the door before I knocked. The apartment behind her was already a ghost of itself — the walls bare where photographs had hung, the good linens gone from the cupboard, the piano sold weeks ago to a buyer who paid a third of its worth and knew exactly what he was doing. What remained was arranged with terrible tidiness: two suitcases by the door, a carpetbag, and Anneli sitting on the floor amid a scatter of toys she had been made to choose between.

That was the detail that undid me before anything else did. Not the empty walls. The choosing. Someone had told the child she could bring three things, and she had spent days conducting auditions among her possessions, and the ones that lost were stacked in a shoebox labeled, in her round eight-year-old letters, *FOR WHEN WE COME BACK.*

"Paloma." Frau Weiss kissed both my cheeks and held my face a moment longer than comfort allows. Her hands smelled of yeast and kerosene. "You came. Herr Weiss said you wouldn't — he said it's dangerous to be seen near us now. I said Paloma comes anyway."

"Herr Weiss underestimates my stubbornness."

"He doesn't underestimate anything. He's a man who counts rivets." She almost smiled. "Come in. Stand away from the windows. And don't look at me too closely, I've been crying since Monday and it shows no sign of stopping."

Herr Weiss emerged from the bedroom carrying Anneli's coat and an expression I recognized from mirrors: the careful blankness of a person holding themselves together with procedure. He shook my hand formally, which was its own kind of heartbreak. Two months ago he would have embraced me. But he had begun practicing being unmemorable, rehearsing the version of himself that a border guard would glance at and forget, and embrace was not in that man's vocabulary.

"The papers are good," he said quietly. "As good as such things are. Elias Brand's contact in the river district — you know him?"

"I know him," I said, and did not let my voice do anything interesting with the words. Though of course my heart did what it always did at the sound of that name, a small irregular tick, an escapement slipping a tooth.

We spoke in the code of ordinary things. Frau Weiss pressed bread on me that I didn't need and couldn't refuse. Herr Weiss explained, loudly enough for any listening neighbor, that he was relocating his dry goods business to his cousin's establishment in the south, an opportunity, regrettable to leave Vellmark, family is family. Anneli sat among her judged toys and watched me with the frank stare of children who have understood far more than the adults agreed to say aloud.

Then she got up, crossed the room, and tugged my sleeve.

"My music box broke," she said.

"Anneli—" her mother started.

"It's *broken*." The word cracked in the middle, and I saw the whole architecture of the child's composure — built over weeks, mortared with obedience — show its first fault line. "It was going to come with me, it was one of the three, and the crank is stuck and the ballerina doesn't dance anymore and Mama said there's no time to fix anything ever again."

There was a silence of the specific kind that forms around a child's grief when adults are too depleted to lie to it.

"What kind of music box?" I asked.

Anneli stared at me. "What?"

"What kind. Cylinder or disc. Does it have a comb — metal teeth, like this?" I held up my fingers, curved. "Or a bell?"

"Cylinder," she whispered. "It plays the swan song. From the ballet Mama took me to. Before."

"Show me."

She fetched it from the depths of the carpetbag, cradled like a wounded bird: a cheap tin affair with a painted lid, the ballerina frozen mid-pirouette, the little crank bent nearly double. I turned it gently. Nothing. Something inside had seized, or a tooth had slipped, and the mechanism that held all her music was silent.

"There's no time," Frau Weiss said again, softly, helplessly. This time it wasn't about the box.

"No," I agreed. "There isn't." I took the box out of Anneli's hands. "Which is why you're not going to watch me fix it. Watching makes repair take twice as long — everyone knows that. Ask any clockmaker." I knelt and put my forehead briefly against hers. "Here's what will happen. You'll put the box in my toolbag, because my toolbag is going where your family is going, and I'll see it delivered safely into your hands at the station. And then, when you're settled wherever you're settling, you send it back to me through whatever clever channel your husband arranges, and I'll return it fixed. That's the arrangement. Broken things stay in circulation, Anneli. That's how they get mended. Understood?"

She looked at me with the gravity of a judge weighing testimony. Then she nodded once, went to her shoebox, and removed the music box's replacement — a wooden horse — placing the horse in exile and the box's fate in mine.

I put it deep in the toolbag, beneath the satchel of screwdrivers, where it lay against my hip for the rest of the day like a second heartbeat with the wrong rhythm.

* * *

The freight yard at the eastern junction was not a place civilians went for sentimental reasons. It was mud and iron and sodium light, and the men who worked it moved with the flat economy of people who load things onto trains and are not paid to wonder what's in them. At ten past six, a livestock train stood at platform three, steam up, cattle cars rattled and waiting, and along the fence line drifted perhaps thirty people with luggage, walking — as Corvin had promised — not like refugees but like travelers, each of them carrying something, each of them with eyes trained carefully on middle distance.

Elias had done his work well. I never learned the whole chain — the clerk in the transport office, the guard at the gate whose brother-in-law owed whom — but I knew the first link, and I knew what each forged manifest cost him. Every document was a signature that wasn't his attached to a risk that was. I had watched him weigh that arithmetic at the observatory table, coffee gone cold beside the escapement, and I had never once been able to tell whether the scale in him tipped toward terror or toward something fiercer that terror was merely the shadow of.

I found the Weisss near the rear car. Herr Weiss had transformed: collar up, hat down, the counting-rivets man wearing the borrowed posture of an anonymous merchant. Frau Weiss gripped Anneli's hand with whitened fingers and smiled at me in a way that cost her visibly.

"You shouldn't be here," Herr Weiss murmured.

"Delivery," I said, and lifted the toolbag. "Special courier."

Anneli took the box with both hands and did not open it. She understood, with the terrible instinct of children, that hope handled carelessly spoils. "You'll fix the ballerina?"

"I'll fix everything except the world," I said. "The ballerina especially. She's just tired. Ballerinas rest between performances."

The whistle came — a short, impatient bark — and the fence line stirred. Around me, thirty families became thirty departures. There were no speeches. Herr Weiss shook my hand again and then ruined his own rehearsal by gripping my shoulder hard, once, and saying nothing at all. Frau Weiss kissed my cheeks and left salt on them. Anneli hugged the music box to her chest and said, "Send it back quick," with the imperiousness of a woman who has placed a commission with a craftsman and expects results.

They climbed into the car marked for livestock, and the door rolled shut behind them, and I stood at the fence with my empty-feeling hands while the train gathered itself and pulled east into the dark.

I stayed until the last of the steam had thinned. I stayed longer than was safe. And then I walked home through streets I barely saw, unlocked the workshop, lit a single lamp, and took off my coat.

The toolbag was lighter without the box. I felt its absence against my hip like a pulled tooth.

And then I remembered.

The music box was still in the bag. Anneli had hugged the wooden horse.

No — worse. I set the bag on the bench and dug down past the screwdrivers and there it was: the tin box, bent crank, sleeping ballerina. She *had* taken it. I had handed it to her myself at the fence, watched her clutch it. My memory was not wrong. The box in my bag was —

I turned it under the lamp and saw the scratch along the base that I didn't remember, and understood, with a lurch, that there had been two boxes: the one she carried daily and the duplicate from the shoebox, *FOR WHEN WE COME BACK,* and in the chaos of the door and the coat and the whistle, the wrong twin had ended up in my keeping. Or perhaps she had traded them deliberately — given me the broken one to mend and kept the horse, or given me the one that meant *come back to us* — and I would never ask, because asking was a letter that couldn't be sent.

Either way, the box was mine to mend, and the girl it belonged to was forty kilometers east and climbing, and the lamp hummed, and the workshop was very quiet.

I sat down at the bench. I got out the fine pliers and the loupe — Corvin's loupe, though that gift was still a few days in the future in my telling of it, and grief does not respect chronology — and I straightened the crank, and freed the seized gear, and reset the cylinder's comb tooth by tooth, and the whole time I worked I wept.

Not gracefully. Not the single dignified tear of fiction. I wept the way mechanisms fail, suddenly and structurally, over the tin box and the shoebox and the piano sold for a third of its worth and the bread split with the widow and the beetle that must have a spring or how would it walk. I wept because survival, it turns out, is not measured in what you save but in what you carry away from the saving — absences, mostly, packed tight as tools, heavier than any suitcase. I wept because Herr Weiss had shaken my hand instead of embracing me, and because that handshake had been the bravest thing I'd seen all week, and because bravery and loss had become, in that year, the same object viewed from different sides.

When the mechanism ran true — the little ballerina turning, turning, the swan song thin and sweet and slightly sharp in the second octave, the way it had always been, cheap tin, honest flaw — I closed the lid and did not wind it down. I let it play to the end of its spring, alone, in the lamplight, and then another time after that, and then I wrapped it in cloth and put it in the drawer under the bench blotter, with the summons and the list, where I kept everything I couldn't afford to look at and could not stop looking at.

It was nearly two in the morning when I noticed the envelope that had been slid under the workshop door. No name. My name on it, though, in a hand I knew almost as well as my own.

I tore it open. Four lines, in Elias Brand's careful, official, ruinous script:

*Your name is on the next classification list. I saw it an hour ago. The date given is the day after the master clock is due. Paloma — I don't think that's a coincidence, and neither should you. Come to the observatory tomorrow. Burn this.*

I read it three times. Outside, somewhere over the eastern junction, a train whistle blew, or I imagined it did.

The clock was due in eleven days. So, apparently, was I.
