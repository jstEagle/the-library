## 15. Betrayal Suspected

The trouble with lying on paper, I have come to learn, is that paper is patient. Paper does not sweat. Paper does not look away when you enter the room. Paper simply sits in its drawer, in its ledger, in its neat bureaucratic rows, holding every falsehood you have ever fed it, waiting with the serenity of a trap already sprung for someone to come along and read it aloud.

I had been a census taker for nine years before the spring of 1938, and in those nine years I had made perhaps four mistakes — a transposed street number here, a misspelled surname there — each corrected within the week by my own hand, initialed in the margin like a good little functionary. My error rate was the pride of the district office. Supervisor Halm once held up one of my completed ward books at a staff meeting and said, *This is what thoroughness looks like, gentlemen,* and I had felt an actual, physical warmth at the praise, which tells you something depressing about what nine years of counting other people's chimneys does to a man's ambitions.

Now my error rate was zero. Perfectly, immaculately zero. Every lineage clean. Every classification consistent. Every household in my district either reassuringly keepable or conveniently absent from the record entirely.

And if there is one thing the men in the district office love more than thoroughness, it is a statistic that stops making sense.

* * *

Her name was Fräulein Ott. Grete Ott. She sat two desks down from mine by the tall window that looked out onto the customs yard, and she had been there eleven years to my nine, which gave her the peculiar seniority of people who have never been promoted. She wore her hair in a bun so tight it seemed to be pulling her eyebrows upward into an expression of permanent inquiry, and she kept her pens in a wooden rack sorted by nib width, and she was, in the way of certain small meticulous creatures, the most dangerous person in the building.

She caught me at the kettle on Thursday morning.

"Brand." She said it the way one says *rat in the pantry.* "Your Weiss entry."

I did not turn around immediately. This was deliberate; a guilty man turns too fast. I let the water run another moment over the grounds and said, "Which Weiss? There are Weisses all over my ward."

"Wawel Street. The glazier's household." She came to stand beside me, ostensibly waiting for the second kettle, though the second kettle had not yet been asked for by anyone. "Four in the household. You've recorded the daughter as born under the mother's first marriage name."

"Yes."

"But the parish record lists the girl under Weiss from birth."

"Does it." I poured the water. The smell of cheap coffee rose between us like a small honest thing in a room full of dishonest ones.

"The registry cross-indexes," she said. "Since March. You'd know that if you read the circulars instead of stacking them."

"I read the important ones."

"Mm." She took the other kettle, which had not been hers to take, and poured her own cup without asking, which was its own small declaration of territory. "It's the third one."

"Third what?"

"Third entry of yours that doesn't reconcile." She sipped. She had the patience of a woman who sorts pens by nib width; she was going to say exactly as much as she intended and not one syllable more. "The Wawel Weisss. The Feldman widow, whose age you've adjusted downward by six years, which flatters her but doesn't help her classification. And the Kessler brothers, whom you list as residing in your ward in April when the ferry manifests have them crossing to Lirgen in February."

I stirred my coffee. The spoon clicked against the cup, and I made myself watch the swirl rather than her face.

"Clerical fatigue," I said. "The ward book runs four hundred households. I've requested an assistant twice."

"Mm," she said again. It seemed to be her entire vocabulary beyond accusation. Then: "I've always admired your work, Brand. That's what makes it noticeable. A sloppy man's errors you expect. Yours—" she set the cup down on the counter with terrible precision, "—yours have a shape to them."

*A shape.* I walked back to my desk carrying my cup like a man carrying nothing at all, and behind me I could feel her eyes doing what her eyes did, which was filing.

That afternoon she requested the full year's ward books from records. I signed the requisition slip myself, because refusing would have been the second mistake, and the first rule of drowning gracefully is to swim toward the boat.

* * *

Here is what I had, by Thursday evening, sitting alone in my flat with the shutters drawn and the gas turned low:

One: the original field notebooks. Three of them, pocket-sized, oilcloth covers, filled in pencil in my own hand at doorsteps across the ward. These were the true records — or truer, anyway, containing as they did the actual names, ages, and lineages of the people I had chosen to protect. If Grete Ott laid a notebook beside a ledger page and compared, the discrepancies would not have a shape. They would have a signature.

Two: my draft pages. The working copies where a falsified entry began its life as a falsified entry, before being recopied fair for submission. I kept drafts out of habit — procedure demanded retention for ninety days — folded into a leather folio at the bottom of my desk drawer, under the spare nibs and the tin of blotting sand.

Three: approximately four days. Maybe five if Halm was drunk enough on his own importance to double-check everything twice. Ott would want corroboration before she went up the chain — a woman like that never reports until the report is bulletproof — and corroboration meant pulling parish registries, ferry manifests, employment rolls. It meant doing properly, over several days, what she had done improperly, brilliantly, in a single morning over the kettle.

Four: no way out that ended with everyone alive and me still employed.

I laid the three field notebooks on my kitchen table and looked at them for a long time. There is an old superstition among census men that the notebooks are sacred — the raw gospel, the thing you swear on at tribunals, the difference between a clerk's opinion and the state's memory. Burning one was not falsification. It was heresy. It was also, I noted with the detached interest of a man watching himself from a slight distance, extremely easy. Paper burns. It wants to burn. All these years I had thought of paper as patient, and it turns out patience and flammability are not mutually exclusive.

But I didn't burn them. Not yet. Because burning them on Thursday night, five days before anyone formally accused me of anything, would itself be evidence — a man destroys nothing he has nothing to hide. No. What Thursday night required was arithmetic.

I took out a fresh sheet and wrote, in my best fair hand, the kind of hand Halm praised at staff meetings:

*Circular 41-C (March) requires cross-verification of entries against parish and transport indices. In re-examining Ward 9 field notes, I find transcription irregularities affecting [x] households. I request permission to conduct a corrective survey beginning immediately.*

It was beautiful. It was, I reflected, the single most elegant lie of my career, and I include in that count every lie I had told about the Weisss and the Feldmans and the Kesslers, because this one had style. I would report myself. Before Ott's memorandum reached his desk, Halm would receive a confession dressed as diligence: your most thorough man, horrified by his own fallibility, requesting emergency leave to correct the record. And the corrections would be corrections — real visits, real amendments — only the amendments would move in the direction I needed, smoothing the anomalies rather than exposing them. The Kesslers were genuinely gone to Lirgen; I could amend their entry to reflect departure, which explained the residency problem and removed them from classification consideration in the same stroke. The widow Feldman's age could be restored to truth, which hurt her classification slightly but closed the discrepancy, and a closed discrepancy is invisible forever while an open one gets circled in red ink.

The Weisss were the problem. The Weisss were gone too — gone on forged papers through the eastern gate on a Tuesday morning while I stood at my post in the observatory gallery pretending to count chimney smoke — and a corrective visit to an empty house raises questions that corrective surveys exist to answer. *Household departed, date unknown, forwarding address unrecorded.* Acceptable. Common, even, in a city where people had begun leaving the way weather leaves, gradually and then all at once.

The daughter's name, though. The daughter's name was the splinter. Born under Weiss per the parish, recorded under the mother's prior name per my ledger — why? Because the mother's first marriage had been to a man the new forms classified, and a child carrying his name inherited the classification like a debt. So I had given the girl her mother's cleaner maiden line, and now the paper trail ran backward from my lie straight to the church register, and no amount of corrective surveying would unpick it, because I couldn't amend the parish records. They weren't mine. Nothing was mine anymore. That was the whole discovery of the week, really: I had spent a season believing I was bending the record, and in fact the record had been quietly wrapping itself around me the entire time, joint by joint, like a hand closing.

I worked until two in the morning drafting the memorandum, revising, reading it aloud in Halm's voice to hear how it would sound to him, sanding away anything clever — Halm distrusted cleverness the way he distrusted foreign coffee. What remained was dull, penitent, and bulletproof for approximately the length of time it took Ott to compare three notebooks against three ledgers, which is to say, not long enough.

* * *

Friday I submitted the memorandum at nine sharp, before Ott had finished arranging her nibs. Halm read it twice, tapped it against the desk edge to square the pages — his gesture, the little square-maker, the man who believed the world improved with alignment — and peered at me over his spectacles.

"Tired, Brand?"

"Spring," I said. "The light changes and the figures swim."

"Happens to the best of us." He said it with the complacent generosity of a man who had never once been tired in his life. "A month's corrective survey. Overtime authorized. And Brand—" he squared the memorandum again, "—Ott flagged some of these same items yesterday. Good instinct on your part getting ahead of it. Reflects well."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me. Correct the record." He waved me out, already reaching for the next document, and I understood that I had purchased not safety but a stay of execution, and that the governor was named Grete Ott and did not grant clemency so much as postpone paperwork.

Because here is the thing about Ott that I understood standing in the corridor with my hat in my hand: she had not reported me. She had flagged me. She had pulled the year's books and begun building her case with the methodical hunger of a woman who had waited eleven years at a window overlooking a customs yard for something — anything — to actually happen. When she finished, she would not go to Halm. She would go over Halm. There was a new bureau forming, we all knew it, the verification bureau, the auditors of auditors, and women like Grete Ott were its natural clergy. My memorandum bought me days because it gave her a rival explanation for her anomalies — clerical fatigue, self-reported, self-corrected — and a woman like that would not strike until she could prove my corrections were themselves false, which required her to redo, from source documents, every correction I made over the next month.

A month, then, if I was brilliant. A week, if I was merely careful. Either way: days. Everything now was days.

* * *

I did burn the drafts. Friday night, in the stove in my rooms, feeding the folio pages in one at a time so the flames wouldn't leap and show in the window gaps. The draft copies were pure liability — they showed the falsifications in sequence, my handwriting moving from true to false in strokes any examiner could follow like footprints in snow. Procedure demanded ninety days' retention. Procedure also assumed its clerks were honest, and I had stopped qualifying sometime in April, standing in an observatory doorway watching Paloma Underhill's hands move inside a clock and understanding, with the calm of a man falling, that I would do whatever was necessary.

The last page to go was not a census draft at all. It was a sheet of observatory stationery on which, weeks ago, in a moment I had told myself was idle, I had written out the escapement diagram she was struggling with — the anchor, the pallets, the teeth of the escape wheel releasing time one measured bite at a time. Across the bottom, in my hand: *A mechanism can only give out what it holds back. Ask her what happens when it holds back too much.*

I had never asked her. We had talked about pendulums instead, in the gallery, our voices pitched low under the dome, and there had been a moment — her face lit by the brass glow of the master clock, my heart going like something badly regulated — when either of us could have said it, and neither did, because we were both keeping time in four-minute increments now and there was never a full minute left over for the truth.

I watched the diagram curl and blacken. Then I cleaned the grate, scattered the ash in the coal scuttle, and sat down at the table to plan a month of corrective surveys that would need to survive the scrutiny of the most dangerous woman in the district office.

Saturday I began. Door to door, notebook in pocket, amending the record one merciful lie and one necessary truth at a time. The Kesslers: departed, February, ferry manifest attached. The widow Feldman: age restored, her eyes on me with something that was either gratitude or contempt, possibly both, certainly deserved. House after house, name after name, my fair hand laying down a version of Vellmark that would pass inspection and cost as few people as possible, and all the while at the back of my skull the arithmetic ticking: Ott in records, pulling sources, narrowing. Four minutes slow, I kept thinking, absurdly. Four minutes buys a train. What does a week buy?

Sunday evening I made my rounds end at the observatory hill, off my route, telling myself it was to check the public clock against my pocket watch like any dutiful servant of municipal time. The city lay below in the blue hour, lamps coming on along the river, and above it the dome held the last light like a hand cupped around a flame. The master clock's chime moved across the rooftops — six strikes, unhurried, four minutes behind the evening star and nobody in Vellmark the wiser.

She was expecting me at the stairwell after curfew. I checked the street twice, crossed the square, and climbed.

Halfway up, in the cold stone dark, I heard footsteps above me descending — measured, official, boots with authority in them — and flattened against the wall by instinct, and heard them pass: two men, uniformed, talking in the low bored voices of men carrying orders they hadn't read yet, and the last words that reached me on the stair, before the echo swallowed everything, were *"the apprentice,"* and *"tomorrow morning,"* and *"both files."*
