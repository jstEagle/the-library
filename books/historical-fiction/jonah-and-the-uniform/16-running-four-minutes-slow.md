## 16. Running Four Minutes Slow

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that happens at three in the morning, when the lamps burn low and the mind has exhausted every respectable thought and begun, out of boredom or desperation, to count things it should leave uncounted.

I had been doing that arithmetic for four nights.

It went like this: the master clock would be finished in six days. My name appeared on the classification list in seven. The transport office had posted notices announcing the resumption of the full schedule — *departures to resume upon restoration of official city time* — and those departures included, though the notices did not say so in any language a decent person could read aloud, the trains that carried away the newly classified.

Seven days after my own name was read, if the schedule held, there would be a train for people like me.

And I was building the instrument that would make the schedule hold.

I sat on my stool at the observatory workbench with the escapement cradled in a cloth on my knees, and I looked at it the way you look at a knife you've used your whole life and have only just noticed is a knife. It was beautiful. I had made it beautiful. Eleven teeth cut and polished over three nights, each one finished to within a hair's tolerance of its sisters, each one designed to do exactly one thing: to release time in measured, obedient increments. Tick, and then tick, and never a moment more than the mechanism allowed.

A clock does not give time away. That's what Master Corvin taught me first, before he taught me anything else. People think a clock tells time, but it doesn't. It *rations* it. Every tick is a small permission. The escapement holds the entire weight of the mainspring — all that furious stored energy wanting to spin free — and it lets go one tooth at a time, at exactly the pace the maker decides. A clock is not honest. A clock is disciplined. And discipline, like any other law in Vellmark that winter, could be bent by whoever held the tools.

I put the escapement down on the bench and pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes until the dark behind them bloomed orange.

"You look like a woman arguing with God," Elias said from the doorway.

"It's a short argument. He keeps winning."

"Only because He cheats." He came in carrying two cups of coffee that smelled like it had been brewed sometime around the coronation of someone's grandfather, and set one within my reach without asking whether I wanted it. He had stopped asking weeks ago. That was its own kind of sentence, though neither of us had diagrammed it yet.

He pulled up the second stool — the observatory kept two, as though anticipating that every repair would require an audience — and looked at the escapement, and then at me, and then back at the escapement, with the careful blankness of a man who reads forms for a living and knows better than to ask questions whose answers he'd have to write down.

"The Weiss train leaves Thursday," he said quietly.

"I know."

"If the schedule resumes Wednesday—"

"I know, Elias."

He drank his terrible coffee. I drank mine. Above us, through the dome, the stars performed their ancient indifference, wheeling around a point some astronomer had calculated to the second, because even the heavens in this city were required to keep records.

* * *

Here is what I want you to understand about sabotage, since apparently I am now an authority: real sabotage is loud. Real sabotage breaks things — snaps arbors, cracks jewels, files a hidden notch into a pivot so the mechanism seizes at the worst possible hour. Sabotage announces itself. Sabotage gets investigated. Sabotage gets the saboteur's name written in ledgers, and I had spent enough evenings watching Elias fill ledgers to know exactly how permanent ink is.

But a clock that runs slow? A clock that runs slow is a tragedy. A clock that runs slow is old age, wear, temperature, the shame of mechanical things. No clockmaker alive can make a master clock perfect; every clockmaker will tell you, mournfully and at length, how far short of perfection even the finest movement falls. The inspectors knew this. The transport office knew this. There were tolerances written into the contracts — I had read them, because reading other people's tolerances is the sort of thing apprentice clockmakers do instead of sleeping — and the tolerances said: *the master clock shall keep time within such-and-such seconds per day.*

The tolerances assumed honesty. They assumed a mechanic trying her best and falling a little short.

They did not assume a mechanic trying her best in one direction only.

Four minutes. I turned the number over the way Corvin taught me to turn a gear under the loupe, looking for flaws. Four minutes was too much to hide in a day — the inspectors would catch it immediately. But spread across the calibration period, introduced as a cumulative drift, a fraction here, a fraction there, each adjustment landing just slightly past true — no single measurement would prove anything. Any one inspection would find a clock running within a hair of spec. Only the accumulated sum, only time itself, would reveal the difference, and by then the error would look like entropy, which forgives everyone.

And four minutes was enough. I had done the sums on that too, on the back of a gear-cutting worksheet I later fed to the stove. The transport office sequenced its departures off the observatory signal — the noon drop of the time ball, the telegraphed hourly pulse. Trains left on the quarter-hour, staggered along the eastern line so no two consist met at the junction. If official time drifted four minutes behind the sun, then every departure happened four minutes late by the sky's reckoning, and the junction scheduling — which ran on the railway's own chronometers, wound by men who still trusted the sun, because railwaymen are sentimental that way — would refuse to cooperate. Conflicts at the junction. Held signals. Delayed sections. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that pointed at one clock in one tower. Just a system developing a stutter, the way a machine develops a stutter when something invisible has worked loose inside it.

Days, perhaps. Perhaps a week of delays. And in that week — trains missed, schedules fouled, the machinery of removal grinding against its own timetable — people could move. The Weisss were already gone, thank whatever watches over fools and clockmakers. But there were others. The Kesslers above the bakery. Old Marta who taught piano and whose grandmother had come from somewhere the new maps disapproved of. Names on lists I wasn't supposed to know existed, except that a census taker with tired eyes sometimes talked in his sleep-adjacent way at midnight, and I listened, and remembered everything, because a girl who can cut a gear to a hundredth of a millimeter can certainly remember a list.

I was not saving the world. Let no one write that on my stone, if stones are permitted to people like me by then. I was stealing minutes. Four at a time, sixty times an hour, thousands of them between now and whenever they caught me. But minutes are the only currency I have ever understood, and I knew their value precisely, because I had spent my whole life learning how little of one it takes to change everything. The distance between a gear engaging and a gear stripping. Between a heartbeat and the next one. Between Elias saying a name on a form and writing a different one.

Between the doors closing and a hand slipping through.

* * *

The trouble with decisions made at three in the morning is that they must survive daylight, and daylight brings witnesses, and the chief among my witnesses was currently asleep in the workshop on a cot he pretended was for naps.

Corvin woke at five, as he always did, with the sound of a man winding himself — joints clicking, breath settling into rhythm. He shuffled to the stove, put the kettle on, and stood watching me where I sat at the bench surrounded by the disassembled future of Vellmark's time.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"I slept."

"Liar. Your father lied better." He poured two cups of the workshop coffee, which was worse than the observatory's, which took dedication. He set mine down beside the great wheel and lowered himself onto his stool with the care of a man descending a ladder he no longer trusted. His hands, when they settled on his knees, trembled faintly, the way they had trembled for two years now. Penance hands, he called them once, and refused to explain.

I picked up the escapement. Put it down. Picked up a tooth file and set it down again. Finally I said, to the bench rather than to him: "Master. When the clock is done. How long before anyone notices if it runs — imperfectly?"

The kettle ticked as it cooled. In the corner, Corvin's oldest regulator — the one that had survived three governments and one fire — swung its pendulum with the patience of something that had outlived every person who first wound it.

"Notices," Corvin said. "In a week, maybe ten days. The transport office compares the master against the railway chronometers monthly. Sooner, now, with everyone nervous. Ten days." He paused. "Why imperfectly? Which direction?"

The question beneath the question. He had always been able to hear the question underneath my questions; I suppose it was only fair that I return the courtesy by letting him hear mine.

"Slow," I said.

Silence. The regulator ticked. Somewhere outside, early in the gray morning, a truck went past carrying soldiers or furniture or people, and none of us looked up to find out which, because we had all gotten very good, that winter, at not looking up.

"Four minutes?" Corvin asked.

I stared at him. He examined his trembling hands with an expression of profound scholarly interest.

"How—" I began.

"Because it's the right number," he said. "Two minutes, the schedule absorbs it; the junctions carry slack. Six, and it's obvious within days — someone walks up the tower stairs to see what's broken. Four sits in the seam. Four is the gap between what a machine should do and what a machine can be blamed for." He looked up at me then, and his eyes were terribly clear, clearer than they'd been in months. "I did arithmetic like that once. For the other side. Their trains also ran on time. Someone built them clocks that ran perfectly, and I want you to understand, Paloma, that whoever built those clocks was proud of the work. Precision doesn't ask what it's for. That's the whole horror of it. Precision is innocent the way a knife is innocent."

"And so?"

"So." He reached into his waistcoat, slowly, and brought out nothing at all — just touched the pocket where the loupe lived, his finest, the one he'd promised to no one. "So precision in the other direction is equally innocent. Equally exact. A clock four minutes slow requires the same craftsmanship as a clock true. More, maybe. You'll have to make the error *look* like decay. Wear patterns. Temperature response. You'll have to lie in brass, and brass is a poor liar unless the hand is excellent." His voice roughened. "Your hand is excellent."

"Master—"

"Don't thank me. Thanking me implies I'm on a side. I'm on the side of the work, and the work today is this: if you're going to betray your government, you will do it to specification." He stood, joints crackling, and carried his cup to the stove, and that was the end of the speech, because Corvin believed confessions were like clocks — best opened rarely, and closed tight again before dust got in.

But at the door to the back room he stopped, without turning around, and said: "Ten days of delay. Then they'll send a different clockmaker. Make sure the ones who need the time are already gone by then."

"Yes, Master."

"And Paloma." Still not turning. "When they eventually ask — and they will — I taught you everything about clocks and nothing about conscience. Say it loudly. Say it stupidly. Stupid is believable."

He went to bed. I sat alone in the workshop with the ticking of forty-odd clocks, every one of them faithful, every one of them telling the truth in small obedient increments, and I understood that I was about to become the only dishonest thing in the room.

* * *

Elias came at nine, off shift, carrying bread that he set on the bench as tribute, and found me already cutting. Because here was the thing I had decided in the dark and confirmed by lamplight and now committed to in steel: I would finish the clock. Not roughly, not grudgingly, not as an act of sabotage dressed up as failure. I would finish it *well*. The escapement would be the finest thing I ever made. The clock would run beautifully, smoothly, reliably — four minutes behind the truth, forever, or at least until some other mechanic with excellent hands undid me.

Rebellion, I was learning, is mostly bookkeeping.

"You've decided something," Elias said, watching my hands.

"Mm."

"Paloma."

"Your bread is getting stale."

"Paloma." He came around the bench and stood where he could see my face, which was unfair of him, and waited, which was worse. He had this patience — ledger patience, the ability to stand in front of an open page until the truth got embarrassed and wrote itself down.

So I told him. All of it: the drift disguised as wear, the fractions of error seeded through the calibration, the junction conflicts, the held signals, the days purchased. I told him in the flat voice of a craftswoman describing a technique, because that was the only way I could tell it without my voice breaking, and while I talked I kept filing, because my hands had made their own decision and did not intend to consult the rest of me.

When I finished, the workshop was very quiet. Even the regulators seemed to be listening.

"They'll hang you," Elias said. "Or whatever the word is this month."

"They'll have to catch the error first. And by the time they catch it—"

"People will have moved." He said it not as agreement but as verification, checking my figures against his own. Census takers think in lists; he was probably already counting who four minutes could save. Then he said, very softly, "You could lose everything. The workshop. Your name. Your—"

"My name's already on a list. He can only take it once." I set down the file at last and looked at him properly. "You don't have to be part of this. Your records stay clean. As clean as they get. If they audit the clock, they audit me and my tools and nobody else. I'll say I misjudged the temperature compensation. Corvin will swear I'm simply not as good as I think I am."

"He'd enjoy that testimony."

"He's been rehearsing it for years."

Elias laughed — one short syllable, surprised out of him — and then the laugh died and he did something I didn't expect. He reached across the bench and put his hand flat over the escapement, over my eleven perfect teeth, covering it the way you'd cover a candle flame.

"Then I'll bring you the calibration records," he said. "Every inspection report the transport office filed last year, and the tolerance tables, and the names of the two inspectors who'll sign off on your work. If you're going to lie in brass, you should know exactly what truth they'll measure you against."

"That's falsification of—"

"That's *bookkeeping*," he said, using my word back at me, gently, like handing me something breakable. "You taught me the value of four minutes. Let me teach you the value of a well-kept file."

Above us the observatory's interim clock — a stopgap, ugly, accurate to within a minute and hated by everyone — struck the hour. Official time. True time. The time of schedules and classifications and trains that departed when the paper said they would. I felt it pass through the room like weather.

In ten days it would strike, and be wrong, and no one would know why.

I picked up the file again. Elias pulled his stool closer. Outside, Vellmark went about its evening under a sky that kept its own counsel, and somewhere down in the city a thousand clocks ticked toward a Thursday, and a Friday, and all the appointed hours of the appointed days —

— and at dawn, when I climbed the tower stair with my toolbag and my heart going like a fly against glass, I found the tower door already unlocked, and a slip of paper pinned to it in handwriting I recognized:

*They've moved the inspection up. Tomorrow morning. Two men from the Ministry. One of them is the one who reported me.*

*Whatever you meant to do — do it tonight.*
