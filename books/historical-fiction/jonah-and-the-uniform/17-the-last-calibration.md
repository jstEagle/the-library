## 17. The Last Calibration

There is a particular quality to the hour before dawn that clockmakers know and poets only pretend to. The poets call it liminal, hushed, sacred. What it actually is, is cold. The observatory dome holds the night's chill the way a bell holds a struck note, releasing it slowly through the small hours until, by four in the morning, the air itself seems to have opinions about your fingers. I had been up since two, warming my hands over a spirit lamp and watching the master clock's pendulum swing in its long glass case like something asleep and dreaming of ticking.

Tonight — this morning, now — I would finish it.

The word *finish* deserves some unpacking, the way a movement deserves unpacking before you understand why it runs. For three months *finish* had meant *repair*: replace the worn escapement teeth, true the suspension spring, clean forty years of Vellmark's coal-soot grit from the pivot holes until the brass gleamed like the day it was cast. That much was craft, and craft I could do in my sleep, which was fortunate, because sleep had become a rumor I'd heard mentioned but never personally verified.

But *finish* also meant the other thing. The thing I had decided at three o'clock on an afternoon when the light came through the observatory windows sideways and made everything look like evidence. Four minutes slow. Not broken — nothing a regulator check would catch immediately, nothing that smelled of files or malice. Just a clock that had developed, in the honest course of its long life, a modest disagreement with the sun.

Four minutes. Enough to push a departure into the next slot, tangle a schedule, make an inspector doubt his own watch. Not enough to hang anyone — I hoped, I prayed, I did arithmetic about it at odd hours the way other people counted sheep.

I opened the case and breathed the smell of oil and old iron, which is the closest thing my family has to incense.

My hands were steady. I want to be honest about this, because everything else in those weeks was dishonest by necessity: my hands were steady, and it felt like a betrayal of the fear I carried, as though the fear should have earned at least a tremor. But hands are trained things. Master Corvin had spent nine years drilling steadiness into them — *the work does not care what you are feeling, Underhill; the work cares whether you are accurate* — and so while the rest of me conducted its private negotiations with terror, my fingers picked up the timing weight and moved with the calm of a surgeon or an executioner, whichever metaphor you prefer. I have tried both. They are more similar than either profession advertises.

The final adjustment was not dramatic. Sabotage rarely looks like sabotage from inside; it looks like patience. I let the pendulum run for the ten-thousandth beat and listened. A master clock speaks to the person who rebuilt it — tells you where it aches, where it hesitates, where a tooth meets a pallet a half-hair proud of where it should be. I listened, and I adjusted the rating nut by a quarter turn, and then another eighth, easing the period longer, longer, a sliver of a second stretched across sixty beats, accumulating the way debts accumulate, quietly and then all at once.

Four minutes a day. By evening the city's official time would lag behind truth by exactly as much as I needed it to, and no one would see it happen, because time does not announce itself when it is being stolen. It only notices afterward, when it's gone.

* * *

Elias arrived at half past five, which meant he had crossed the city in the dark, past the curfew patrols, carrying nothing visible except a thermos of coffee that he had told his landlady was for a census deadline. He had told her this without blinking. I had stopped being frightened by how easily he lied and started being frightened by what that ease was doing to him, the way water works on a foundation — nothing you can point to, until one morning the wall has a lean to it.

He did not come all the way into the dome. He took up his post at the head of the stairwell instead, where he could hear boots coming up three flights away, and settled himself against the stone with the thermos balanced on his knee like a man waiting for a train he had no intention of boarding.

"You've been here since two," he said. Not a question. He knew the hours I kept better than I did by then.

"The clock doesn't calibrate itself."

"No," he said. "It takes a specialist."

I passed him the spare loupe on its chain — Corvin's second-best, the one he'd lent me for observatory work — and Elias hung it around his own neck without using it, which was his way of wearing something of mine in public where no public existed.

"Patrols?" I asked.

"Changed at four. The next rotation comes at six-forty." He consulted his pocket watch, and I watched him do it, and I thought — not for the first time — that there was something obscene about a census taker consulting a timepiece calibrated against the very clock I was bending. He caught me looking.

"It'll hold," he said.

"That's what you said about the Weiss entry."

"And it held. They're on a train today because their paperwork says they were never the people we both know they are." He said it flatly, the way you set down something heavy so your hands are free. "Paperwork doesn't have to be true, Paloma. It only has to be consistent. That's the whole religion of my profession, and I've become its worst heretic."

"You're its best convert," I said. "You lie with perfect documentation."

For a moment he almost smiled, and then didn't, and the almost was worse than either alternative. From the stairwell came the sound of the city beginning below us — a cart, a dog, the first tram testing its route like a tongue finding a sore tooth. In an hour the transport office would read the official time off the observatory's telegraph line and begin sequencing the morning departures, and every minute I put between the master clock and the truth would become a minute of the world's business, delayed.

Elias turned his collar down and looked at me over the rim of the coffee cup.

"How long?" he asked, meaning the calibration.

"Done within the hour. Then she runs. She'll drift back toward true eventually — a week, maybe ten days — unless someone re-rates her. By then it won't matter."

"By then," he repeated, and did not finish the sentence, because finishing it required naming the thing that was coming for both of us, and we had gotten very good, the two of us, at building our conversation around a hole shaped exactly like the future.

* * *

I worked. He watched the stairs. This was the liturgy we had fallen into without ever drafting it: I bent over the movement and he stood guard, and between us we held open a small country with two citizens, whose borders were the sound of boots on stone.

At some point he spoke without turning around.

"My father had a watch."

I waited, wrench in hand, not looking up, because there are things people can only say to the top of your head.

"A cheap one. Nickel case, stem-wound. He wound it every morning at seven and set it against the church bells, which were wrong, incidentally — the sexton never corrected them after the war. So my father went through his whole life four minutes early and believed it was noon when it was quarter-to." A pause. "He said a man who knows the wrong time absolutely is better off than a man who doubts the right one."

"Your father sounds like a philosopher."

"He sounded like a clerk. Same thing, in that generation." Elias turned the thermos cap in his fingers, round and round. "I keep thinking about that watch. About how much of my life I spent believing numbers on paper were the same thing as the truth, because a man who winds a wrong watch every morning believes it completely. And then I met you, and you took the back off the whole apparatus and showed me the escapement, and now I can't unsee it. I can't wind anything anymore without thinking: *who decided this? Who rated it? Against what?*"

"That's called becoming a clockmaker," I said. "You should apprentice. The pay is terrible and the regime wants you dead, but the hours are flexible if you sabotage correctly."

This time the smile came all the way, briefly, in the dark at the top of the stairwell, and I looked away from it because some things are too heavy to look at directly when your hands need to be steady.

Quarter turn. Eighth. Listen. The pendulum swung its long slow arc, and I thought about what Corvin had told me — that an escapement is just a heart, letting time out one controlled beat at a time, and that everything a clock is, is a promise about the future kept in increments too small to see. I had spent my whole apprenticeship learning to keep promises. Now I was learning to break one beautifully.

* * *

The sky beyond the dome's shutter began to change at six, going from black to the bruised grey that Vellmark wears eleven months of the year. My breath had started showing. I finished the last rating adjustment, closed the case, and stood with my palms flat against the glass like a doctor confirming a pulse.

"There," I said quietly. "She's done."

Elias came into the dome then, properly, for the first time all morning. He stood beside me and we watched the pendulum together, and the silence between us had changed texture — the working silence replaced by something with weight and edges.

"Paloma."

Just my name. I did not turn around.

"I need to say a thing," he said, "and I need you to let me say it badly, because I've rehearsed it and the rehearsal was worse."

"Say it badly, then."

He looked at the clock instead of at me. Maybe that was easier. Clocks don't answer, and they don't get arrested, and they keep whatever confidence you tell them for exactly as long as they run.

"I am not a brave man," he said. "I want that on the record, in triplicate, properly filed. Everything I've done — the forms, the Weisses, the ledger entries I've buried — none of it was courage. It was you. Every falsified line traces back to the moment I stood in this room and watched you put a clock back together and thought: *if she can do that with her hands, I can do this with mine.* You are the reason I'm capable of any of it, and I have been trying for four months to find a category for what you are to me, and the categories don't exist. The form has no field for it."

"Elias—"

"Let me finish. I may not get another chance to be thorough." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I love you. There. Filed. Witnessed by a clock, which is fitting, because clocks are the only things in this city that tell the truth anymore, except this one, which lies by four minutes, for which I will be grateful every day I'm alive."

The pendulum ticked. Somewhere below, the tram bell rang the hour — six-thirty by the city's reckoning, which as of this morning was already a fiction.

I had prepared, in the months of stairwell conversations and stolen coffee, approximately four hundred responses to this moment, ranging from wit to gravity, and every one of them deserted me. What came out instead was the truth, unedited, which is what happens when you spend enough time around precision instruments; they shame you into accuracy.

"I love you too," I said. "I have since the ledger. Since you hesitated over that last question and wrote down a lie with my name in it. I thought: *this man's hands shake when it matters, and stay steady when it doesn't, and I know exactly what that costs.*"

"It doesn't cost anything yet."

"It costs everything eventually. That's the design." I finally turned and faced him, and his face in the grey dawn light looked younger and older than it ever had, simultaneously, the way faces do when they stop performing. "We have perhaps a week. Perhaps days. You know that."

"I know that."

"And you said it anyway."

"I said it anyway." He took my hand — the right one, the clock hand, still smelling of oil — and held it the way you hold something you are already memorizing for later. "A man who knows the wrong time absolutely," he said softly, "is better off than a man who doubts the right one. I'd rather have this and lose it than file the correct empty form for the rest of my life."

Above us the master clock ticked on, four minutes behind the sun, carrying our secret in its beating like a letter sewn into a coat lining.

And that was when Elias's head turned toward the stairwell, sharp as a bird dog's, and his hand left mine, and everything we had just built was folded and pocketed in the space of a heartbeat.

Boots. On the stairs. Two sets, heavy, climbing fast, taking the flights two at a time — and the sky outside was barely light, and the patrol rotation wasn't due for another ten minutes, and nothing about the sound of them was routine.

"Don't move," Elias said, already reaching into his coat for papers that would not cover this. "Whatever happens — don't follow me down."

They were on the third flight.
