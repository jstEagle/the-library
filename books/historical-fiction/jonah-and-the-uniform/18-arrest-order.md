## 18. Arrest Order

There is a particular quality to a morning when you know it will be your last honest one. I don't mean your last morning alive — death is a democrat and visits everyone eventually, usually at inconvenient hours. I mean the last morning before you become, permanently and officially, the thing you have only been in secret.

I knew it the way one knows weather through a window. Not by evidence. By pressure.

I had been at my desk in the census office since six, which was itself a confession; an innocent man sleeps. I sat with my draft copies — the ones Herr Kessler had noticed were *corrected*, his word, delivered with the delicacy of a man laying out cutlery for an autopsy — and I fed them to the small iron stove in the corner one page at a time. The office was empty. The stove made its modest, apologetic sounds. Paper burns differently depending on what's written on it, did you know that? Blank paper goes quickly, almost eagerly. A page bearing names, ages, lineage classifications — that resists. It curls around its own ink as though trying one final time to be read.

I watched eleven years of careful falsification curl and go grey and let them go without ceremony. Sentiment is for men with alibis.

The cover story was prepared. I would say what any bureaucrat might say: errors of transcription, fatigue, the crush of the autumn enumeration, my sincere intention to file amendments. It might hold three days. Four if Kessler's superiors were lazy, which they were not, because laziness in this government gets you sent somewhere that makes Vellmark's prison look like a spa. I gave myself five days and considered myself an optimist.

What I had not accounted for — what no amount of stove-side arithmetic accounts for — was speed.

* * *

They came at nine-forty by the observatory clock, which is to say nine-forty-four by every honest timepiece in the city, though no one in authority knew that yet, and God willing no one ever would.

I was crossing the observatory's ground-floor hall — I had taken to passing through it daily, a habit I told myself was about verifying records and knew perfectly well was about the stairwell, about the faint chance of a glimpse of dark hair bent over the escapement — when the doors opened and four soldiers came in with the unhurried confidence of men carrying paper that outranks everyone in the room.

A lieutenant led them. Young, clean-shaven, with the terrible freshness of someone who still believed in the enterprise. He consulted a document. I watched him do it and understood, with the peculiar clarity that arrives precisely too late, that my name was on it.

"Census taker Brand," he said. Not a question.

"Reporting," said my mouth, while the rest of me performed rapid, useless calculations.

"You're under investigation for irregularities in the district seven enumerations. You'll come with us to—" and here he named a building I had driven people toward and never once entered from the other direction. The irony was so tidy it felt authored.

"Of course," I said. "Might I collect my coat? The mornings are cold."

He considered this. Somewhere behind his eyes a regulation was located and found permissive. "Quickly."

And there it was — the whole architecture of my survival reduced to whether a young man believed I wanted a coat.

Because here is what he could not have known: the observatory's main stairwell descends past a half-landing where a workroom door stands open during repairs, and down at the bottom of that stairwell, in the clock room itself, Paloma Underhill was at this moment elbow-deep in the master clock, finishing work that was scheduled — officially scheduled, stamped and filed, the regime's own paperwork — to conclude tomorrow morning. And Paloma Underhill's name sat on the classification list dated the day after completion, which meant that these soldiers, or soldiers very like them, were coming for her too. The lieutenant had said nothing about an apprentice. But lists are long, and lieutenants are thorough, and I had spent enough evenings reading this government's forms to know that *unnamed apprentice, mixed lineage, observatory access* was a sentence already being typed somewhere with relish.

If they took me now, walking me out through this hall, they would pass the stairwell door. Someone would glance down. A soldier's eyes are trained to notice things out of place, and a girl with Paloma's face in a building like this was, under current law, a thing out of place.

The coat. Right. Think, Elias. You have perhaps forty seconds and one coat.

* * *

I want to record, for whatever record survives, that I did not think of anything noble. There was no swell of principle, no vision of the Weiss family safe in some other country, no echo of my better angels. What I thought of was the sound Paloma's tools make — that small bright industry, tick and tap and the whisper of the broach through a hole — and the unbearable fact of it stopping mid-note. That was all. Love at its most basic is just this: the refusal to let a particular sound end early.

I crossed the hall toward the coat rack at a pace I calibrated carefully: brisk enough to seem cooperative, slow enough to buy seconds. The stairwell door stood open, as it always did now, propped with a wedge of wood Corvin had contributed decades ago and no one had thought to remove. From below came the faint, steady heartbeat of the pendulum, and beneath it, tinier, the music I was listening for.

I took my coat off the hook. I put it on. And as I shrugged the second shoulder into it — a gesture that required exactly two seconds and covered the movement of my head turning — I looked down the stairwell and let my voice carry the way it carries in stone corridors, pitched not at the lieutenant but at the depths:

"The observatory keeps irregular hours this week! All appointments are postponed — tell the workshop master his afternoon is free!"

Silence from below. One beat. Then the sound of a tool being set down, deliberately, audibly, the way one answers.

"Who are you talking to?" the lieutenant asked.

"Myself," I said. "It's a habit of men who work alone. You'll find it noted in my file, I'm sure — along with everything else you've found." I gave him a smile I had practiced on difficult informants, the smile that says *I am tedious, lose interest*. "Shall we?"

He did not lose interest. But he was twenty-three and I was thirty-six and I had been lying to professionals since before he could grow the moustache he was attempting, and so he saw a cooperative clerk with an irritating sense of humor rather than a man who had just shouted a warning down a stairwell in bureaucratic code. *Postponed.* *Afternoon free.* *Go.*

Paloma would understand. Paloma, who had learned to read mechanisms, who understood that a warning is only a signal if the receiver knows the language — Paloma would hear it. I had to believe that with the same total, unexamined faith with which I had, over these months, handed her my entire life in installments.

We walked out through the great doors, four soldiers and one census taker, and the doors swung shut behind us, and I did not look back at the stairwell because looking back is how guilt learns your address.

* * *

The interview room in the security building was painted a green that has clearly been selected by someone who hates the human face. They sat me across a table from a captain whose file-thickness told me everything: my folder, when he opened it, made a sound like a small mattress being turned.

"Elias Brand," he said. "District seven."

"Captain."

"Your entries show corrections. Amendments. Inconsistencies." He laid three pages side by side like a card trick. "Household counts that change between draft and final. Lineage entries revised downward. One family — Weiss — classified labor-eligible in your draft and unclassified in your final submission. Unclassified, Brand. Do you know what happens to the unclassified?"

"They wait for re-review," I said, "under section—"

"I know the sections. I wrote two of the sections." He leaned back. "Here is what I think, Brand. I think you are either incompetent or compromised, and I have read your aptitude scores, so incompetence would embarrass us both."

Outside, a tram bell rang. I thought: that bell is governed by the observatory clock. I thought: that bell is currently four minutes wrong, and the man responsible for noticing is sitting in this chair. I thought: good.

"I'd like counsel," I said.

"You'd like a miracle. Neither is available." He gathered his pages. "You'll be held pending verification of your drafts against the field records. Your residence will be searched. Your associations will be reviewed." A pause, delicate as a knife being set down between courses. "Your recent associates, Brand. List them for me."

And there it was — the question underneath the questions, arriving on schedule like everything else in this country. My associations. One association, if I was honest, which I had stopped being on paper but found myself unable to stop being anywhere else.

"A landlady," I said. "A tailor who overcharges me. Colleagues."

"Names."

I gave him three names of men I barely tolerated, and watched him write them, and calculated what each would say under questioning, which was nothing useful, which was the point. Every hour he spent on the tailor was an hour not spent at the observatory.

But hours were the one currency I could not mint. The verification of my drafts against field records would take — I ran it while he wrote — a day, perhaps two, assuming the district offices cooperated, which they would, because cooperation is the national sport. Two days. Then the discrepancies would resolve themselves into a shape, and the shape would lead, inevitably, up a certain stairwell to a certain clock room.

Unless the records didn't exist to verify.

My draft copies were ash; I had seen to that. But the field records — the working ledgers, the master volumes, the great red-bound censuses in the archive — those lived in the records room of this very building, three floors down, catalogued and cross-referenced and waiting like witnesses for the trial to begin. As long as those pages existed, every falsified entry in my hand was a signed confession and a map.

I looked at the captain's green wall and understood, with the calm of a man who has finally run out of smaller choices, what remained.

* * *

They held me overnight in a room with a bench and a light that never fully committed to darkness. I slept, actually — a shallow, administrative sleep, the kind where you dream in forms. Near dawn a guard brought water and I asked, in the tone of a man concerned with his own comfort, whether detainees were permitted to send for personal effects.

"For what?"

"Warm socks. Books. I'm told detention involves waiting, and waiting involves feet."

He looked at me the way guards look at clerks, which is to say with a contempt that conveniently includes compliance, and grunted something that history would remember as neither yes nor no, and left the door unlocked for the corridor sweep, which is how I learned that this facility's security budget had been trimmed in favor of the transport office. Everyone's resources were flowing toward trains now. Toward schedules. Toward the resumption of departures that a certain master clock, running sweetly and dishonestly, was doing its quiet best to postpone.

I dressed. I listened at the door. The corridor held the pre-dawn emptiness of institutions — one distant footsteps, a radio murmuring in a room somewhere, the building's pipes clearing their throats.

Three floors down, the records room. Its lock was institutional, which is to say decorative; its real security was procedure, and procedure was asleep until seven.

I have done many small dishonest things in my life and most of them were, in the end, rehearsals. Falsifying a lineage here, misfiling a household there — each act felt enormous at the time and revealed itself later to be practice, scales run before the piece. This was the piece.

I will not describe the walk down. Some corridors deserve to be forgotten by everyone who used them.

The records room smelled of dust and binding glue and the particular staleness of paper that no one has loved. Shelf upon shelf of red bindings. District seven occupied the third bay, third shelf, and my own neat hand ran through eleven volumes like a thread through fabric, holding together — I realized, pulling the first one down — far more than anyone had counted. Not just my falsifications. Every taker's entries, every classification, every family reduced to a figure in a column. Hundreds of names. Thousands. The Weisss were in here. Half the families who had slipped away on the last weeks' trains were in here, their files intact, ready to be hunted whenever the machinery resumed. The ledger was not just evidence of my crimes. It was the instrument of everyone else's.

The choice made itself with the ease of a mechanism reaching detent. That is the thing no one tells you about irreversible decisions: they do not feel like cliffs. They feel like clicks.

I took down the volumes, one after another, and carried them to the stove in the corner — the rooms of this government all keep stoves, as though the whole apparatus runs on small fires — and I worked with the methodical calm I had learned from watching a clockmaker's apprentice, who taught me without knowing it that panic is just precision applied too late. Pages into the fire. Names into heat. The room filled with smoke and the smell of burning ink, and somewhere above me the building began, at last, to take notice: a door, a voice, running boots on stairs.

I fed the fire faster. Volume nine caught with a whump that I choose to remember as applause.

When the door burst open I was standing in smoke beside a stove full of eleven years of classifications, and I confess my first thought was not fear but professional satisfaction: the field records were gone, and with them the proof, and with the proof — a day, maybe two, bought for a girl at the top of a stairwell who needed every one.

"Hands up!" — of course. There is always a man shouting *hands up*, as though hands were the operative organ.

I raised them. Smoke curled between my fingers. Behind the soldiers, through the little window, the sky was going pale at the edges, dawn approaching, and with it the hour when Paloma would make her final calibration, and with it — if the fates of clocks and men aligned — the morning trains, delayed, delayed, delayed.

"It was an accident," I told the soldiers pleasantly, as they took my arms. "Clerical error. You'll find it noted in my file."

* * *

They marched me up through the building as the alarms woke properly, and I went willingly, almost gaily, a man finally at peace with his paperwork. Past the ground floor, through the great hall, toward the doors — and there I dug in my heels for one last theft, small even by my standards.

"Lieutenant," I said to my escort, "one kindness. Tell whoever manages the observatory that the census verification is suspended — all site access revoked, effective immediately, until further notice. Official wording matters. Make sure the observatory staff are informed directly. Today. Before eight."

He frowned. "Why before eight?"

"Because after eight it's yesterday's news," I said, "and in this country yesterday's news gets people killed."

He wrote it down. Of course he wrote it down; give a bureaucrat a sentence and he will carry it across the city. By noon, some clerk would deliver those words to the observatory, and Paloma — sharp Paloma, who read warnings in the language of postponements — would know what they meant: *suspended, revoked, until further notice.* Would know that I was taken. Would know to finish, to calibrate, to run — and to run four minutes slow.

The doors opened onto cold morning air and the noise of a city waking up on time, or believing it was. Somewhere across the rooftops the observatory dome caught the first light. Somewhere beneath it a pendulum swung, keeping a truth that belonged to us now, four minutes removed from theirs.

They put me in a car. I looked back once — allowed myself the luxury, since looking forward no longer held anything I needed.

The car pulled away toward the buildings with the green walls, and I sat back among the soldiers, hands cuffed, coat warm, and began composing in my head the statement I would give: incomplete, contradictory, tedious — a mechanism designed to release information in controlled amounts, one escapement tooth at a time, for as many days as Paloma needed.

She would need at least until morning.

I intended to give her every second I had.
