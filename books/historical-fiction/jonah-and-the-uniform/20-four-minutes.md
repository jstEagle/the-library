## 20. Four Minutes

The master clock ticked behind me like a heart that had learned to lie, and I stood on the observatory steps with my collar turned up against the October wind and listened to Vellmark disagree with itself.

That is the strange thing about a city governed by one timekeeper: nobody ever checks their own watch. They check the church bells, which follow the observatory. They check the station whistles, which follow the bells. The whole apparatus of a morning — the bakers lighting ovens at the hour, the clerks sprinting for the eight-fifteen, the soldiers reporting precisely when precision was required — all of it hung from the pendulum swinging above my head, four minutes behind the truth, and not one soul in ten thousand knew it.

I had done that. Me, Paloma Underhill, apprentice clockmaker, who had spent six years being beaten about the knuckles for letting a regulator drift thirty seconds in a week. Master Corvin would have called it the finest work of my career. He might also have called it heresy, treason, and a very efficient way to die, but he'd have said it with pride, the way he said everything that mattered.

The sky over the rail yards was going the color of dishwater. Somewhere down there, Stationmaster Krebs — a man built like a filing cabinet and roughly as warm — was consulting the official time and finding, no doubt, that the official time had developed opinions of its own.

I checked my own pocket watch. The honest one, the one Corvin had given me at the start, the one I kept in my waistcoat where a heartbeat would be. Six minutes past seven by true reckoning. By the city's new reckoning, it was two minutes past. Four minutes. It doesn't sound like much. Four minutes is the length of an egg boiled soft, a song played through once, a goodbye cut short. Four minutes was also, as of this morning, the difference between a train departing on schedule and a train full of classified persons sitting on platform three while a clerk ran up and down the platform shouting at the sky.

I hadn't slept. Neither had Elias, presumably, wherever he was — at the stairwell door at dawn he'd looked at me the way you look at the last match in the box, and then he'd gone down to the records room to do whatever a man does on the last morning before they come for him. He'd said, "Whatever happens down there, don't wait for me." He'd said it lightly, the way liars say true things.

So I didn't go inside. I sat on the cold stone steps with a view of the whole gray city and did the only useful thing left to me, which was to be a witness.

* * *

At seventeen minutes past seven by the honest watch, the first whistle blew from the yards, and even from the hill I could hear something wrong in it — too long, too urgent, the sound of a man arguing with machinery. The eight-fifteen southbound was, according to every timetable printed in Vellmark, due to depart in fifty-eight minutes. According to the observatory clock, whose time the transport office was required by regulation to telephone for and obey, it was still nearly an hour away.

Krebs, I would learn later, telephoned the observatory three times between half past seven and eight. The first two times, the night attendant — bribed with nothing more than fear and a plausible shrug — told him the master clock read such-and-such and could the Stationmaster kindly not question the official timekeeper of Vellmark, as questioning it was, per regulation eleven, an offense. The third time, Krebs asked to speak to whoever maintained the instrument.

That would have been me. I let the bell ring.

There is a particular pleasure, I confess, in refusing to answer a telephone. It is a small, mean, magnificent pleasure, and after the year I'd had I took it with both hands.

By eight o'clock true time — four past eight by the city — the yards had worked themselves into the particular chaos that regimes produce whenever reality fails to fill out its forms correctly. I could see figures on platform three through Corvin's spyglass, which I had brought up to the gallery along with a thermos of coffee Elias had left two nights ago and never reclaimed. Soldiers, mostly. A line of civilians standing the way people stand when they've been told to stand: still, close together, hands quiet. The Weiss family wasn't among them — the Weisss had gone out on Tuesday with forged papers and their daughter's music box ticking in my toolbag — but I knew some of the faces. The tailor Ostrer and his brother. Old Mrs. Halloran, who once paid Corvin in plum jam for fixing her carriage clock and had been overpaying him in jam ever since because she believed in settling debts generously or not at all.

They were supposed to be moved at eight-twenty. They were now, officially, moving at eight-sixteen — except that eight-sixteen had not yet happened, because according to Vellmark it was only twelve minutes past, and so the order went out: hold the train until the scheduled time, and the scheduled time refused to arrive, and somewhere in the transport office a clerk was discovering that you cannot arrest a pendulum.

I drank the dead man's coffee — not dead, don't say dead, just vanished, just burned, just gone into a crowd the size of a city — and I watched, and my hands were steady, which surprised me. I had expected them to shake. Instead they lay in my lap like tools set down after a job well done, and I thought: this is what Corvin meant. Precision isn't steadiness. Precision is knowing exactly what you've done and refusing to flinch from the measurement.

* * *

The trouble, when it came, came on foot, up my hill, in the person of a junior transport officer with a clipboard and the expression of a boy sent to tell the schoolmaster the stove won't light. He hammered at the observatory door. When nobody answered — the night attendant having been given the morning off via a note I had written in Corvin's hand, which I doctored better than most forgers doctor money — he came around the side and found me on the steps.

"You there. You're the clock people?"

"I'm the clock person," I said. "The rest of us are clocks."

He blinked at that, decided it was above his pay grade, and held out the clipboard like a summons. "The stationmaster reports a discrepancy. The master clock is running slow. Four minutes, give or take. That's a regulatory violation. The schedule can't—"

"Then your schedule is wrong," I said pleasantly.

"It isn't wrong. It's *official.*"

"Yes," I said. "It matches the clock exactly. Four minutes slow, both of them, all the way down. Bells, whistles, timetables. Everything agrees with everything else. There has never been a city in finer agreement." I let him stand in the wind with that for a moment. "Unless you'd like the official time to disagree with the official time. In which case, which one would you like to keep, and who signs the form saying so?"

He looked at his clipboard. He looked at the observatory dome. I watched a young man run the arithmetic of his own career and come up with a number he didn't like. To report the master clock faulty was to admit the entire city's schedule had been built on sand for days; to report it accurate was to explain why the eight-fifteen hadn't left. Either road ended in a room with senior men and bad lighting.

"There'll be an inspection," he said, without conviction.

"Bring a form," I said. "I'll sign anything."

He went back down the hill at a pace suggesting he hoped the problem would resolve itself through the intervention of higher authorities, meaning anyone but him.

I let the spyglass rest on platform three again. The train was still there. Steam rising. Mrs. Halloran had sat down on somebody's trunk, which struck me as exactly right. And here was the thing I had not dared to hope for: nobody was loading them aboard. The delay I'd manufactured didn't just slow the departure. It slowed the *paperwork*. Every additional minute was another minute in which the classification orders, the transfer manifests, the escort assignments — all of it chained to official time — failed to converge. The regime had spent years building a machine that could only act on schedule, and I had stolen the schedule, and the machine stood in the yard huffing steam at a horizon that wouldn't come.

Elias had argued with himself for a whole night before agreeing to it, and I understood now that he'd been right to argue and wrong to doubt. Not because sabotage was safe. Because it was *legible*. A stopped clock screams sabotage. A slow clock whispers error, and error is everywhere, error is forgivable, error gets filed.

* * *

At nine minutes past nine, true time, the whistle finally blew long and low — the departure signal — and the southbound began to move.

I counted the carriages through the glass. Six. Full. Whether full with whom, I couldn't say from that distance, and I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only inheritance I'm sure of: I don't know that everyone on platform three made that train. I know the escort detail was short — two soldiers had been pulled away at half past eight to chase a discrepancy at the freight office that may or may not have existed before Elias walked past it that morning. I know that when the train finally rolled, there were fewer people on the platform than there had been at eight, and that some of the missing had faces I knew, and that missing-in-that-direction could mean the train, could mean the tunnels, could mean the marsh road north that the fishermen used. I know that Mrs. Halloran boarded, because I watched her climb the step slowly, with dignity, carrying her jam-stained carpetbag as if it were state luggage, and I put both hands on the cold stone of the parapet and held on.

And I know that the train was forty-one minutes late, and that when the inquiry comes — and it will come — every document will show that the departure occurred at the correct scheduled interval from the moment the transport office received the official time. The clock will testify for me. The clock is the one witness in Vellmark everyone believes, and it lies.

The last carriage cleared the points and the smoke thinned and I sat back and discovered I was crying, which annoyed me, because I had planned to feel triumph, and instead what I felt was arithmetic. Four minutes bought. Six carriages gone. One old woman aboard with her bag. Some number of neighbors scattered along the marsh road with forged papers and borrowed hours. One census taker somewhere below the city with smoke on his sleeves if he'd done what he said, or irons on his wrists if he hadn't. One master in a workshop with shaking hands, waiting to hear whether his finest pupil had disgraced the trade or saved it, since from where Corvin sat those had lately become the same thing.

Four minutes a day, the clock would stay slow. I'd built the error into the rate, not the reading — a hair off the pendulum's length, a whisper of friction in the escapement I'd rebuilt with my own hands at midnight while a wanted man held the lamp. Any inspector who checked the mechanism would find a tired, aging movement losing time gracefully, the way movements do. It would take weeks of observation to prove intent, and by then — well. By then was the part we hadn't solved. By then was the open door in every plan we'd made, and through it I could see either Elias walking in with smoke-smell on his coat, or nothing walking in forever.

I packed the spyglass. I poured the last of the coffee out on the steps, a libation to whatever god keeps accounts of small gears, and I started down the hill toward the workshop, because there were clocks to wind and a list with my name on it dated tomorrow, and because if the soldiers were coming for me I intended to be found doing my job, sleeves rolled, loupe in my eye, precisely where precision lived.

Halfway down, at the corner where the hill street bends and you can see the whole lower town spread out gray and ordinary, I stopped. From the direction of the civic quarter rose a column of smoke — black, fat, wrong-colored for chimneys, wrong place for a fire. My stomach dropped through the cobbles before my mind caught up and supplied the obvious address. The records hall. The archive. The ledgers with their columns of names and classifications and lineage codes, hundreds of pages of who-could-be-kept and who-could-be-taken, and someone was feeding them to it.

He'd told me not to wait for him. He'd told me lightly, the way liars say true things, and I had nodded, the way cowards agree.

I stood at the corner with the smoke rising and my name sitting on a list like a knife on a table, and I thought about a note I had no business hoping for, and a workshop with a lamp lit, and a word I had said once at dawn on a stairwell and meant with my whole blood. Then I turned downhill and walked faster — not toward the smoke, though God knows every step of me wanted to run at it, and not away from it either, but toward the only fixed point left in a city whose time I owned.

Behind me, above me, the master clock ticked on, four minutes slow and gaining, patient as any good liar, keeping Vellmark's stolen hours wound tight in its spring — and ahead of me, past the smoke, past the soldiers, past tomorrow's date on tomorrow's list, a small brass bell over a workshop door waited for a knock.

I meant to be there when it came.
