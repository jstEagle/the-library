## 21. What the Workshop Remembers

The new clerk's name is Herr Foss, and he has the singular gift of being forgettable in every direction. He came to the district office three weeks after the smoke over the archive had been officially attributed to "an electrical fault of unprecedented scope," and he does not care about clocks, or lineage, or me, which makes him the finest bureaucrat Vellmark has produced in years.

He visited the workshop once, on a Tuesday, wearing a coat too heavy for the season and an expression suggesting he'd already forgotten why he'd come. He looked at Corvin's empty stool. He looked at me.

"You're still here," he said, as though I were a piece of furniture that had survived an inventory.

"I'm still here."

"The master clock is functioning?"

"Within tolerance," I said, and it was even true, if your tolerance was generous enough to hold four minutes.

He wrote something in a ledger — a new ledger, one of hundreds that have replaced the ones that burned, all those clean pages waiting to be dirtied again — and left. At the door he paused and said, "Corvin. That was the old man's name? There was some question about his classification." Then he shook his own head slightly, like a man waving off a fly. "Closed file. It doesn't matter now."

It doesn't matter now. Four words that would have killed Master Corvin to hear, he who believed that everything mattered, that the whole moral architecture of a life could be read in whether you bothered to true up a escapement nobody would ever see. But Foss said it the way people say such things now, with the tired relief of a city that has decided remembering is more expensive than forgetting, and I let him believe it, because letting officials believe convenient things is a skill this workshop taught me well.

So the workshop stands. I still don't entirely understand why. Perhaps the regime has larger concerns than an apprentice clockmaker of the wrong ancestry; perhaps the burned ledgers took my file with them; perhaps there is simply a limit to how much cruelty any bureaucracy can administer before its paperwork chokes on itself. Whatever the reason, I open the shutters each morning, I take the day's work, and I wind the clocks.

That last part is not obligation. That is liturgy.

* * *

There are nineteen clocks in the shop now, counting the regulators on the wall and the carriage watch in the locked drawer that no one has claimed since its owner boarded a train we did not see depart. Every evening, half an hour before closing, I go through them in order. The mantel clocks first, then the longcase in the window whose pendulum bob is brass gone soft-gold with handling, then the wall regulators, then the small alarm clock by the bench that Corvin kept though he never once used an alarm, because — he told me exactly once — a man who wakes when he means to wake has no need of bells.

I wind them and I listen. Each one has a voice. The longcase has a low, patient heartbeat; the little alarm clock ticks like rain on a tin roof; the carriage watch, when I hold it to my ear, sounds like something very small running very fast to get somewhere important. For twenty minutes each evening the shop is an orchestra of insistence, every mechanism saying the same thing in its own dialect: *still here, still here, still here.*

I think Corvin knew that. I think that's why he stayed in this cramped, dust-walled room for forty years while governments changed around him like weather. A workshop full of ticking is a workshop full of witnesses. You cannot erase a street that keeps time.

His stool sits where he left it, at the bench by the north window. I have not moved it. Some days I catch myself almost speaking to it — *Master, the third regulator loses a minute a week, I've adjusted the rating nut but I think the suspension spring is tired* — and the habit of addressing him is so deep that grief barely registers anymore; it has become structural, load-bearing, part of the frame.

He did not die. I want to be clear about that, because the version of events that circulates now is vague and merciful and wrong. He was taken — two days after the inspection, quietly, between the afternoon customers and the evening curfew, by two men who did not knock because they did not consider our door a door. I was at the observatory. When I came back the stool was empty, the lamp was cold, and on the bench sat his loupe, wrapped in a chamois cloth, centered with a precision that was itself a message: *this is deliberate; this is inheritance; do not waste time mourning when you could be working.*

I have never found out where they took him. I have written inquiries in my careful apprentice hand and received back nothing, which is at least honest. But I wear his loupe at my eye six hours a day, and through its lens every gear is a little sharper than God made it, and I choose to believe that somewhere in this country's sprawl there is an old man timing his own breathing against a prison wall and finding it, professionally speaking, irregular but serviceable.

That is what memory becomes when it cannot afford monuments. It becomes a loupe. It becomes winding. It becomes four minutes.

* * *

Because yes. The master clock still runs slow.

I calibrate nothing else that way — every clock in the shop keeps honest time, to the second, because dishonesty in small mechanisms felt like a corruption of everything Corvin taught — but the great clock above the city, the one whose pendulum swings in the observatory tower and whose hands govern the trains and the factories and the morning whistles and the schedules of the departed, that one runs four minutes behind the truth, and only I know it.

Weeks have passed. The discrepancy should have been caught. Any competent horologist with a marine chronometer and ten spare minutes could find it. But the observatory's senior astronomer was reassigned in the reshuffle after the fire, his replacement is a party man who believes stellar observation is a form of insubordination, and the transport office has stopped asking why their timetables feel wrong and started simply adjusting around it. Four minutes has seeped into the city's bones like damp into plaster. Trains leave when the tower says they leave. The tower says what I say it says.

Some mornings I climb the observatory hill before dawn — I am still nominally its contracted maintenance technician, the only person in Vellmark with the clearance and the nerve — and I stand in the clock room with the pendulum swinging its great slow scythe beside me, and I check my father's old pocket watch against the master dial, and I confirm the gap. Four minutes. The width of a held breath. The length of time it takes a family to move from a doorway to a freight car when everyone pretends not to see them.

The Weiss family is in Frejstadt now, or beyond it. Their daughter's music box plays again; I repaired it the week after they left, alone in the shop, and when the little mechanism finally turned over and produced its thin, warped waltz I put my head down on the bench and cried in a way I had not cried at Corvin's empty stool, because grief for people is louder than grief for people's absence, and because the box's tune was four beats out of phase with the shop clocks and I could not bear to correct it. It sits in the locked drawer now, keeping the carriage watch company. Two orphans of inaccurate time.

* * *

I found the note eleven days ago. I have not told anyone, because there is no one to tell, and because some things you turn over in private the way you'd turn a stolen jewel.

It happened like this. The master clock had developed a faint irregularity in its strike train — a hesitation on the hour, a tiny stammer before the bell — and though I suspected the cause, proper diagnosis required opening the case panel I had sealed myself on the last morning, the final calibration, the dawn when Elias stood watch at the stairwell and we said, in fewer words than most people spend ordering coffee, everything we had spent months not saying.

I went up with my tools on a grey Thursday. The clock room smelled of oil and cold stone. I removed the panel screws — four turns each, my seals undisturbed, no one had looked inside, no one ever looks inside, that is the whole quiet miracle of machines, that everyone trusts them blindly — and there, wedged against the inner face of the case where only a horologist's hand would ever reach, was a twist of paper no bigger than a pill.

I knew the handwriting before I unfolded it. Blocky, deliberate, the letters of a man who had learned to write forms and never unlearned it. Elias Brand wrote everything as though it might be audited.

*Underground line. Northern depot. 61 kilometers. These coordinates.* And beneath a small drawn arrow: *wait.*

No name. No signature. Nothing that would hang him if it were found, nothing that would hang me. Just coordinates in the northern hills, sixty-one kilometers by the rail line, and one word that has been running through me ever since like a gear through a train of wheels, transferring motion to everything it touches.

Wait.

He got out. That much the word confirms — a dead man leaves no appointments. The soldiers came for him the same morning they came for Corvin; I heard the boots on the observatory stairs as I went down mine, and I have spent weeks refusing to finish the sentence that began *he was*, because unfinished sentences are the only kind I can afford. Now the sentence has an ending after all, just not the one I feared: *he was faster than they were.* The census taker who falsified three hundred records and burned the archive that held them walked out of Vellmark inside a crowd of smoke and confusion, and somewhere north of here he is alive, and he thought of the inside of a clock case as the one post office the state could never censor.

Of course he did. We met inside a mechanism. It was always going to be our medium.

* * *

I have done nothing with the note. That deserves explaining, because eleven days is a long time to hold a lit fuse.

The first night I nearly went. I packed a bag — the carry pile, resurrected from those early weeks when Corvin and I sorted our lives into what we could lift — and I got as far as the door. Then I stood in the shop with my hand on the latch and listened to nineteen clocks ticking *still here, still here,* and understood that if I left, the master clock would drift. Not immediately. But within a month someone would recalibrate it, or replace me, or simply let it run true out of neglect, and the four minutes would close like a wound, and whatever small slack I have woven into this city's schedule — the slack that lets documents be misfiled, that lets patrols arrive late, that lets the occasional family evaporate between one census and the next — would tighten.

The clock is a hostage I have taken, or that has taken me. Both feel true. Either way, leaving means releasing it.

So I wait. Which is, I notice, precisely the instruction. He did not write *come.* He knows me better than to write *come.* He knows that I will not abandon a mechanism mid-repair, that I cannot leave the city's time lying crooked behind me, and so he wrote the one word that honors both the woman I am and the future he is holding open: *wait.* Wait until the war's shape changes, until the reshuffles finish eating themselves, until a northern depot appears on no one's list of places worth watching. Wait the way a pendulum waits at the top of its arc — not inert, but gathered. All the energy in the world, held at the turning point.

I am trying to learn to wait like that. It does not come naturally. My whole training is against it — a clockmaker's sin is delay; every gear I cut was a promise that the next second would arrive on schedule. But Corvin also taught me that the finest movements are the ones that know when to hold the escape wheel locked, letting the spring's full force press harmlessly against stone, spending nothing, until the precise instant of release.

Perhaps that is what this whole year has been. One long lock. And somewhere north, sixty-one kilometers, the release is already cut and fitted, waiting in another man's pocket for the moment my hand comes to the wheel.

* * *

Tonight I closed the shop early. Herr Foss had come by to inform me that the district's inspection schedule was being "simplified," which I take to mean abandoned, and he lingered on the doorstep with the lost look of a man who misses being feared and cannot remember what he was feared for. "You'll keep the place in order," he said. "Until matters are regularized."

"Within tolerance," I said, and he nodded as if this were an answer, and left.

Now the lamps are lit, and I make my rounds. Mantel clocks first. The longcase in the window, its brass bob glowing like something alive. The regulators. The alarm clock that ticks like rain. Nineteen voices, one message.

Then I sit at Corvin's bench — my bench — and take out the note, and unfold it on the wood under the lamp, smoothing the creases with the side of my thumb as carefully as if it were a leaf spring. The coordinates. Sixty-one kilometers. North, where the rail lines fray into hills and the regime's attention frays with them. The arrow. The word.

*Wait.*

Outside, high on the hill, the observatory strikes nine — which is to say, it strikes eight fifty-six, and the city adjusts without knowing, and somewhere in that four-minute margin a door opens softly and stays open a little longer than it should.

I fold the note along its creases and return it to the drawer with the music box and the carriage watch — the museum of what we carried out with nothing. Then I take up the loupe, Corvin's loupe, warm from my eye, and set it down beside the half-disassembled third regulator, tomorrow's penance, tomorrow's prayer.

The clocks tick. The lamp hisses. Winter is coming down off the hills, and the nights are getting long enough to hold everything.

Sixty-one kilometers. One word.

I am learning to wait the way a spring waits — and some evening soon, when the knock comes at the workshop door, I intend to be ready to let the wheel go.
