## 22. The Hour That Is Ours

The first snow of winter came on a Tuesday, which felt fitting. Tuesdays were the day Master Corvin had always chosen for cleaning the escapements — he claimed dust settled differently after the weekend, though I never found a single almanac that agreed with him. The snow came sideways, the way snow does in Vellmark when it means business, and it piled against the workshop door in a white shoulder that muffled the whole street.

I had been keeping the shop open out of something between stubbornness and grief for six weeks by then. Six weeks since the smoke over the records office. Six weeks since the last train pulled out four minutes behind schedule, carrying half my street into a country whose name I couldn't pronounce but which had agreed, through whatever channels the Weisss had negotiated, to take them.

Six weeks since Elias vanished into a crowd I couldn't follow him into without getting us both shot.

The new clerk came every Thursday. Herr Fessler was his name — a soft man with soft hands who wore his regime like an ill-fitting coat he'd been handed secondhand. He counted the clocks. He counted the tools. He wrote numbers in a ledger with a pencil he licked before sharpening, which told you most of what you needed to know about him. He did not ask questions, because asking questions meant writing answers, and writing answers meant responsibility, and Herr Fessler had built a life on the principle that responsibility, like rust, should be avoided wherever possible.

"Shop looks well," he said last Thursday, peering at Corvin's regulator clock as if he could tell time from looking at it. "Quiet street now."

"Quieter," I said.

He nodded at that, satisfied, and made his small mark, and left, and I stood in the doorway watching his breath dissolve and thought: *this is what survival looks like from the inside. It looks like Thursdays.*

* * *

The workshop itself had become a kind of argument I held with the past. Corvin's bench sat exactly where he'd left it, the loupe still hanging from its hook — *my* loupe now, though I still reached for it some mornings expecting to find his hand already there, arthritic and certain. I wound his clocks each week. All eleven of them, plus the regulators, plus the carriage watch that had belonged to his wife and which neither of us had ever discussed. They filled the shop with their ticking, and because I had calibrated them all to match the master clock at the observatory — because I could not help myself, because precision was the only prayer I knew — they all ticked four minutes slow.

Four minutes slow, together, in the dark. A room full of machines agreeing to lie.

Some nights I found this unbearable. Some nights it was the only comfort there was.

I had repaired the Weiss girl's music box in those first terrible days — the one I'd smuggled out in a toolbag while her mother wept into her coat collar. It played a waltz, slightly flat in the third phrase where the comb tooth had been chipped and I'd had to file it true. I kept it on the shelf above the bench and played it sometimes with the lamps off, and I will not pretend I didn't weep, because I did, and because a woman who repairs timepieces ought to be honest about at least one mechanism.

The note I found in the master clock's case — *coordinates, and the word 'wait'* — lived inside my shirt now, folded to the size of a stamp, worn soft at the creases from being read too often. The coordinates pointed north, toward the lake country, toward a village so small it appeared on maps only as a printer's apology. Wait. As if waiting were a thing you could do deliberately, a discipline, a craft. As if it were not simply the thing I had already been doing, hour upon hour, in a shop full of lying clocks.

* * *

On the evening the snow came, I closed early. There was no reason not to; customers were thin as the light, and the ones who came were mostly buying timepieces they could not afford because owning something that measured time made them feel it might be rationed less cruelly. I banked the stove, trimmed the lamp, and set to work on a pocket watch that belonged to a widow on Kessler Street whose late husband had wound it every morning for forty years and whose son wanted it running again "for the memory." For the memory. As if a movement could hold a man the way a case holds a spring.

I worked until my eyes burned. Outside, the snow thickened from weather into statement. And somewhere in the middle of fitting a balance staff — the smallest, most unforgiving piece of the whole assembly, the part that decides whether a watch lives or dies — I heard the knock.

Three raps. Evenly spaced. Not the hurried double-knock of a customer escaping the cold, and not the heavy official cadence of men who arrive knowing the door must open. Three raps, patient, deliberate, spaced the way a person spaces knocks when they have rehearsed them, when they have stood outside a door in the cold composing themselves.

My hands went still over the movement. In the silence, the clocks ticked on, all of them, four minutes slow, filling the workshop with their conspiratorial whisper.

There is a moment — every apprentice learns it — just before you commit to a delicate adjustment, when you understand that what comes next cannot be undone. Cut the pivot wrong and the staff is ruined. Set the beat wrong and the watch runs crooked forever. You learn to live inside that moment, to breathe in it, to let it stretch. Corvin called it *the held breath*. He said the best craftsmen weren't the steadiest hands but the ones who could stand longest inside the held breath without flinching.

I stood in it now. I heard the snow against the shutters. I heard the stove tick as the iron cooled. I heard my own heart doing its crude, unregulated work.

Then I took down the lantern.

Why the lantern, with a lamp burning right there on the bench? I couldn't tell you. Perhaps because the lantern was how you went to doors in stories, in the old tales Corvin told while soldering — the wife at the window, the traveler in the storm, the light carried toward the unknown knock. Perhaps because I wanted my hands full so they wouldn't shake. Perhaps because some part of me had understood, from the moment of those three even raps, exactly who stood on the other side, and understood that such a moment deserved fire, not gaslight. Deserved ceremony. Deserved everything I had.

I lit the wick. The flame stood up yellow and steady. I crossed the workshop floor — past Corvin's empty stool, past the shelf with the music box, past the three piles I no longer kept because I no longer sorted my life into carry, bury, burn — and I put my hand on the latch.

Through the door's thin wood I said nothing, and the figure outside said nothing, and the clocks ticked, and the snow fell, and the held breath stretched until I thought it would break me.

I opened the door.

Snow swept in across the threshold in a white breath. The lantern flame bent and recovered. And there — dark against the dark street, snow caught in hair gone thinner than I remembered, shoulders carrying six weeks of roads I could not imagine — a figure raised one hand against the lantern light, and opened his mouth to speak, and —

Behind me, all eleven of Corvin's clocks, and the regulators, and the carriage watch, arrived together at the top of the hour they did not believe in, and began, in perfect four-minutes-slow unison, to strike.

* * *

I want to record what happened next precisely, because precision is the only inheritance worth passing on, and because whoever finds these pages deserves better than sentiment.

But here is what precision costs: it admits how little happened. He did not finish speaking. Neither did I. The clocks struck twelve notes that belonged to eight o'clock while the true hour was four minutes further along, and in that gap — that stolen, manufactured, deliberate gap that I had built into the heart of Vellmark's official time with two steady hands and a clear conscience — the distance between us closed and failed to close and closed again. He stepped onto the threshold or he didn't. The lantern trembled in my grip or the wind took it. Snow melted on the floorboards between us in a spreading dark map of everywhere he'd walked to reach this door.

What I know is this: the door stayed open. I did not close it. Whatever the world had planned for us — Fessler and his Thursday counts, the classification lists, the trains, the ledgers, the ash — none of it entered that doorway. The doorway belonged to the clocks, and the clocks belonged to me, and I had taught them to keep a different kind of time.

"The shop runs slow," he said at last, his voice roughened by six weeks and cold air. "All of it."

"All of it," I said. "Four minutes."

"I know. I've been listening to it for miles." And here he almost smiled, the dry wit surfacing through exhaustion like a coin through floodwater. "Do you know what it does to a man, hearing a whole city strike the wrong hour and call it right?"

"It does to a man exactly what it was meant to do," I said. "It gives him back four minutes."

The snow fell. The flame stood. Somewhere north, under other stars, the Weisss were lighting a stove in a language I'd never learn, and the girl's music box waited on my shelf to be carried to them someday, across borders that no longer existed in quite the same way. Somewhere south, an archive smelled forever of smoke. And here, on a threshold in Vellmark, a door stood open between a clockmaker and a census taker who had burned his own name rather than write ours down wrong.

He reached out and touched the lantern, not me — his fingers closing over the wire bail beside mine, so that we held the light together, the way you hold anything that matters: jointly, precariously, both aware it could gutter.

"May I come in?" he asked. "I've been keeping your hours. I'd like to keep them properly."

And the clocks, striking the last of their twelve — the twelfth note falling into the snow-silenced street four minutes behind itself, four minutes behind truth, four minutes ahead of everything — seemed to me to answer before I could.

I stepped back. I held the lantern higher. The light went past him, out into the falling snow, and found the road north, and held it.

"Yes," I said. "Come in. We're slow here — you'll have to learn to wait."

He laughed — actually laughed, the sound cracking the cold like ice on a pond — and crossed the threshold, and the door stood open behind him, and the clocks ticked on in their beautiful conspiracy, and the hour, at last, was ours.
