## 1. The Weight of Small Names

There were three hundred and eleven names in the master ledger of the Halloway Trading Company, and Silas Ashgrove knew every one of them by heart. She knew which ones were written in her own careful hand, which ones had been entered by old Merrow Halloway before his hands went to shaking, and which ones had been invented entirely — born at a desk by lamplight, given false debts and honest-looking flourishes, so that certain divers might appear on paper as property of the company rather than children of men the crown had decided were traitors.

Paper could drown a person just as surely as water. Silas had learned that at fourteen, when she'd signed herself over to the company with an X she'd practiced making look confident. Seven years of indenture later, she could write a bill of lading in three different hands, balance a column to the half-penny, and forge a magistrate's seal well enough that the seal itself would have wept with pride.

She was useful. More than that, she was *unremarkable* — a plain, quiet woman who smelled faintly of ink and river, whom merchants forgot five minutes after speaking to her. In Marrow's Bend, being forgettable was a kind of wealth. She had spent years accumulating it.

By day she worked the counting-house: tallied pearls by luster and weight, recorded each diver's take, deducted the company's share with arithmetic so scrupulous it was almost an apology. By dusk, when the ledger was locked away, she walked down to the shallows where the divers worked, and there she became something else entirely. Not the company's clerk. Just Silas — the woman who brought bread still warm from Tam Ovett's oven, who knew whose mother had the coughing sickness, who could hold a lantern steady while a diver's catch was counted honestly among friends instead of on paper.

The divers had made room for her the way water makes room for a dropped stone — without ceremony, without question, until she simply belonged to the shape of things.

"You're doing it again," said Maren Coate, surfacing with a wet gasp and pushing her gray braid out of her eyes. "That face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're counting something that isn't pearls." Maren spat into the river, which was less rude than it sounded; the river and Maren had an understanding. "You've been staring at Jude for a quarter hour. He's mended his net twice now just to look busy under your attention. Boy's going to wear a hole in it."

Silas hadn't realized she'd been staring. That was the trouble with attachments: they crept up on you like damp through stone, and by the time you noticed, the damage was done and the wall was cold all winter.

Jude was eleven, or perhaps twelve — his mother had never been sure of the date, and the company ledger said only *Jude, son of Edele, diver, no surname entered*, because enemy blood didn't merit ink wasted on a family name. He had his mother's long hands and his dead father's stubborn chin, and he dove deeper than any child should, coming up with river pearls in his teeth some evenings like a pirate of very small ambitions.

He also, Silas had noticed, watched the road.

Every boy in Marrow's Bend watched the road lately. There were rumors — there were always rumors — that the crown's recruiters would come through before winter, taking sons for the army the way the company took pearls: nine parts of every ten, and gratitude demanded for the remainder. The boys watched the road the way you watch a cloud that might or might not rain on your harvest.

Jude watched it differently. If the recruiters came, they wouldn't stop at conscription for him. His father had been branded an enemy of the crown seven years ago, and the brand did not wash off a family. For Jude, the road held not soldiers but gallows-adjacent things: questioning, chains, worse. Everyone on the waterfront knew it and nobody said it, the way nobody says *drown* on a boat.

* * *

She found him at dusk on the breakwater, three days later, skimming stones across the low tide with a skill that suggested he ought to be doing chores instead.

"Your net," Silas said, sitting down beside him on the barnacled stone. "Maren says you'll wear a hole in it."

"Maren says a lot of things."

"She does. It's why we keep her. Cheaper than a town crier." She skipped a stone herself — four hops, which he pretended not to be impressed by. Out past the breakwater the river went molten with sunset, gold running to copper, and far downstream, barely visible in the haze, stood the dark line of the old bridge. No traffic crossed it anymore. The far bank was another country now, closed since the border edicts, its customs houses empty, its gates rusted open to nothing. Children dared each other to walk halfway across and spit off the edge. Nobody went further. There was nothing on the other side except elsewhere.

"Silas." Jude turned a stone over in his long fingers. "If they come for me. The soldiers."

"They won't come this year."

"If they come." He wasn't looking at her. He threw the stone flat and hard and it flew clean across the current, six skips, seven, gone. "What do I do?"

The honest answers presented themselves in order: *Run. Hide. Hope.* All true, all useless, all things he'd already thought of alone in the dark, which is why he was asking a clerk on a breakwater instead. What he was actually asking was: *will anyone help me, or will everyone find reasons to be busy?*

Silas looked at the boy beside her and felt the particular terror of caring about someone — the sudden, vertiginous knowledge that here was a person whose death would actually be a death, hers too in some measure, and that she had let it happen without noticing, the way you let a season change.

She should have kept her distance. She knew the arithmetic of it. A clerk who loved no one could forge a ledger with a steady hand forever. But the evening was warm, and he was eleven-or-twelve, and he had asked her.

"Listen to me," she said. "Do you know what I do all day? Truly?"

"You count pearls."

"I count everything. Names, debts, weights, measures. I know what every person in this town is worth on paper, including the magistrates, including Captain Vane of the garrison, including the Halloways themselves. Paper is how the world decides what's real." She picked up a stone of her own, weighed it. "And paper can be written twice."

Jude frowned at her. "You mean lies."

"I mean *better truths*. There's a difference. A lie is a thing that gets caught. A better truth is a thing that was always supposed to be in the book and somehow got left out, and you're only correcting the omission." She smiled despite herself. "It's a fine art. I'm told I have a talent."

"If they come for me—"

"If they ever come for you," Silas said, and put enough iron in it that he finally looked at her, "you will come to me first. Before you run, before you hide, before you do anything brave and stupid, which at your age brave and stupid are the same thing. You come to the counting-house, or you send me a sign — a pearl in my bread loaf, don't ask me how, boys manage these things. And I will get you across that bridge if I have to carry the entire Halloway ledger on my back and set fire to it on the way."

She hadn't meant to say the last part. It came out whole, fully formed, like something she'd been building in secret even from herself. Across the river, the old bridge stood black against the last light, patient as a promise neither of them had known they were making.

Jude stared at her. Then, with the terrible gravity of a boy accepting a knight's oath, he nodded once. "A pearl in your bread loaf."

"A pearl in my bread loaf. And mind it's a good one — I'm risking my neck, not my standards."

That earned her half a smile, quick and crooked, and then he was a boy again, skimming stones, and she was only a woman on a breakwater in the cooling dusk, telling herself the vow meant nothing, that war was rumor, that the bridge would rust another thirty years before anyone needed it.

* * *

The recruiter's drum was heard on the north road the first week of autumn.

Tam Ovett brought the news himself, still in his apron, flour to the elbows, bursting through the counting-house door without knocking for the first time in eleven years. Behind him, down at the docks, the company's own flag was coming down off its pole, and in its place, rising slow against a gray sky, went the banner of the crown.

"Soldiers," Tam said, breathing hard. "A whole column of them. And Silas—" He swallowed. "They're saying they haven't come for boys. They've come for the books."
