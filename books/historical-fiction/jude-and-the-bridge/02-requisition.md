## 2. Requisition

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, which Silas thought was a poor choice of day. Tuesdays were ledger days, the one morning she sat openly at the counting-house desk with the company's whole history spread under her hands. Had they come on a Friday, when she hauled kelp for the dye-works, or a Sunday, when she slept like a stone, she might have been merely inconvenienced. Instead she was at her stool, pen wet, when the doors banged open and the century of His Majesty's Third Infantry marched into the Halloway Trading Company as if it were a tavern with an unpaid tab.

There were thirty-two of them, boots ringing on the floorboards Silas had mopped not four days past. Dust rose. Settled. Rose again.

"By order of the Crown," announced the sergeant, a broad man whose voice seemed to come from somewhere behind his chest, "these premises are requisitioned for the war effort."

Silas dipped her pen and kept writing.

The thing about being useful and unremarkable, she had learned over eleven years of indenture, was that people talked around you. The sergeant's men began hauling crates of salt-pork toward the door while Mr. Halloway himself came puffing down the stairs in his good coat, sputtering about letters of marque and his cousin in the Assembly, and nobody so much as glanced at the small woman at the desk who was, with great calm and even greater speed, doing arithmetic.

She was calculating how long it would take them to reach the ledger room.

Three hours, if they were thorough. Two, if they were soldiers, which meant impatient and fond of smashing rather than sorting. She gave them two.

The master ledger lived in the back vault, and it was not merely a book. It was a confession with covers. Every diver who'd ever taken an advance from the Halloway Company was entered there—names, debts, terms of service, the whole architecture of obligation that kept half the waterfront politely enslaved. And threaded through those honest columns, in ink matched so carefully that even she had to squint sometimes, were forty-one entries Silas had forged over the years. Debts marked paid that hadn't been. Indentures shortened by three years here, five there. A dead man's name transferred to a living widow so she could draw his rations. Little mercies, written in a hand she'd practiced until it could pass for old Halloway's own on a bad-light day.

If the army audited those pages—and armies always audited; it was the one thing armies did well—the penalty for falsifying company accounts was hanging. Per count. Forty-one counts. Half the divers whose names she'd cleaned were still in Marrow's Bend, still diving, still easy to find.

Silas finished her column, blotted it neatly, and went to fetch tea for the officers, because invisible people brought tea.

* * *

It was near dusk before she saw him, and even then she knew him wrong first: a tall officer in a captain's coat, standing in the doorway of the counting-house with his thumbs hooked in his belt, wearing the particular expression of a man surveying property. Then he moved his head in a certain way, and eleven years fell away, and he was eight years old again, throwing river stones at her window because he'd heard she could get extra bread from the kitchen.

"Edmund," she said.

"Miss Ashgrove." Captain Edmund Halloway smiled, and it was his father's smile exactly, which was somehow worse than if it had been his own. "They told me there was still a clerk on the premises. I didn't believe them. And then I thought—who else could it possibly be?"

"You've grown," she said, which was true, and insufficient.

"I'm auditing my father's books." He stepped inside and ran a finger along her desk, inspecting it for dust, finding none, looking faintly disappointed. "The Crown requires a full accounting of all labor debts in occupied territory. Every contract. Every advance. Every name." He said the last word lightly, but she felt it land between her shoulder blades. "You'll assist me, of course. You know where everything is."

"Of course," said Silas, and poured his tea with a hand that did not shake, because she had trained it not to, and hated it a little.

He talked while he drank—cheerfully, the way his father had talked while raising rents. The town was under martial administration now. The bridge was closed to civilian traffic; there were enemy sympathizers crossing into the northern provinces, and the Crown intended to catch them. Pearl stocks were being inventoried. Diver contracts were being reviewed for irregularities.

"Irregularities," Silas repeated.

"My father," Edmund said, with the fondness sons reserve for fathers they have outmaneuvered, "was a sentimental bookkeeper. All these little kindnesses scattered through the accounts. It'll be my pleasure to tidy them up." He set down the cup. "First thing tomorrow, we start with the master ledger. All of it. Front cover to back."

* * *

She walked home along the water, the way she always did, past the drying racks where the divers hung their nets, and found Maren Kell waiting for her on the seawall with the look of a woman who had been rehearsing bad news.

"They took the warehouse stores," Maren said. "And they posted notices. Conscription rolls for men sixteen to forty." She paused, and the pause did the rest of the work.

Jude was seventeen. Jude Kell, son of Tomas Kell, who had been branded an enemy of the crown for the crime of carrying letters across the bridge for people who needed them carried, and who had vanished north two winters ago, leaving his boy behind with nothing but his father's reputation like a brand on him too.

"He's not here," Silas said. "He went north in the spring."

"That's what everyone will say," Maren said. "What everyone says and what's true aren't always diving partners." She looked out at the darkening water. "There are soldiers at both ends of the bridge already, Silas. They're not checking cargo yet. Just faces."

Silas thought about a promise made in shallow water on a summer evening, a girl's easy vow to a frightened boy: *if it ever comes to that, I'll get you across.* Promises made in peacetime were cheap as river glass. It had never occurred to her, then, that keeping one would cost this much.

"The ledger," Maren said quietly. "Your names in it. Ours. What happens when they read it?"

Silas watched the lamplighters working their way down the waterfront road, small flames blooming one by one against the dusk, each one a small surrender to the coming dark.

"I don't know yet," she said honestly. "But tomorrow I'll be sitting beside the man reading it. That's something."

"It isn't much."

"No," Silas agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

She was up before the garrison drums, though what she intended to do in the vault before Edmund woke she could not have said—steal a two-hundred-page ledger and hide it where? In a town crawling with soldiers? She got as far as the counting-house door and found it already open, already lamplit, Edmund already seated at the master table with the great book before him, turned to the first page.

"Early," he said pleasantly. "I remember that about you."

He worked methodically, and he worked fast, which she had not expected. He ran his finger down columns of names—Kell, Ostrey, Fennimore, Brightwater—and made small ticks in the margin. Silas stood at her own desk copying inventories he'd assigned her, and watched him sideways, and counted.

Page nine. Nothing. Page ten, the Ostrey debt marked *paid in full*, dated four years early, and Edmund's finger stopped.

Silas kept copying. Her pen did not slow. Her heart, however, had climbed into her throat and begun taking up more room than was strictly polite.

"This entry," Edmund said. "Paid in full. My father's initials."

"He was sentimental," Silas said. "You said so yourself."

"Mm." He held the page closer to the lamp. Turned it. Found the Fennimore indenture, shortened without cause. Turned again. And Silas watched him arrive at the same conclusion she had arrived at years ago alone in this room—that the handwriting was very nearly right and not entirely right, the way a pearl can be very nearly round.

When he looked up, he was no longer smiling his father's smile. He was smiling his own, and it was far more intelligent.

"These aren't my father's marks," he said softly. "These are someone else's. Someone good." He tapped the page twice, almost admiringly. "Someone still in this building, I'd wager."

Outside, down by the water, a gull screamed once and cut off. Somewhere beyond the drying racks a lantern moved where no lantern should be, low and quick, heading for the reeds below the seawall—and Silas, cold to the marrow, thought of a boy who was supposed to be a hundred miles north.
