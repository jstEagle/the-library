## 3. What the Ledger Owes

The counting-house smelled of tallow and river damp, as it had for eleven years, but these days it also smelled of boot leather and gun oil, which changed the arithmetic of everything.

Silas Ashgrove had learned long ago that the safest place in any room was the corner where nobody looked. She had made herself furniture at the Halloway Trading Company — a woman of tidy columns and unremarkable face, useful the way a good stool is useful, present the way weather is present. For eleven years this had kept her fed, dry, and free of the stocks. Now an army sat in her counting-house drinking the company's coffee, and the ledger she had tended like a garden lay open on the requisition table under the eyes of Lieutenant Halloway himself.

Edmund Halloway. She had known him when he was nine years old and afraid of eels. He did not appear to be afraid of anything now, least of all her.

"Master Ashgrove," he said — he had never once managed *Mistress* without stumbling on it, so he simply didn't — "these entries in the third register. Who keeps them?"

"I do, sir."

"They're very neat."

"Thank you, sir."

"That wasn't entirely a compliment." He turned a page with one finger, gentle as a man handling a pearl shell. "Neatness can hide things. A crooked bookkeeper writes crooked lines. An honest one writes straight ones. But a clever one—" he looked up at her, and there was something almost apologetic in it, "—a clever one writes *very* straight lines indeed."

Silas kept her hands folded on the counter and thought about tide tables. The tide went out. The tide came in. Neither of them cared what was written down about it.

"The divers' accounts run through my hands because I don't lose money," she said. "You may audit every column twice."

"I intend to."

Behind him, through the window, she could see the shallows where the divers worked, pale backs bent to the water, and among them — no. Not among them. Her heart did something foolish and enormous before her eyes caught up with it: not a diver at all, just a heron standing in the eelgrass, tall and thin and wearing its stillness badly.

A heron that had once been a boy who couldn't hold his breath long enough to dive, and so had learned instead to stand motionless in the shallows for hours, waiting for soldiers to stop looking.

*Jude.*

* * *

Lieutenant Edmund Halloway had been three days in Marrow's Bend and had already formed the opinion that rivers made people dishonest. There was something about a border town — the water moving past with half of it belonging to nowhere, carrying other people's property through your hands — that loosened a man's grip on the idea of ownership.

The Ashgrove woman troubled him.

Not because her books were wrong. He had found errors in ledgers his whole career and had developed an ear for them, the way his mother could hear a false note in a hymn. These books rang true. Every page. That was precisely the trouble. Eleven years of accounts and not a single smudge of hesitation anywhere, not one corrected figure, not one entry where the ink told a story of doubt. Real books were messy the way real lives were messy. These were too clean by half.

And there was the matter of the pearl-divers' rolls. He had gone through them four times now, looking for the name on the warrant — *Jude, son of Corven, diver, enemy of the crown* — and finding only the father's line, closed out neatly six years ago with a settlement paid in full, and beneath it nothing. No son entered. No apprentice listed. As though Corven the diver had sprung from the riverbed fully grown and childless.

Corven had drowned with debts, according to the town. Debts meant children, usually. Children meant names written in ledgers, because the company never let a debt go unwritten.

Someone had been here before him. Someone with very steady hands.

He dipped his pen, wrote in his private notebook: *Ask the woman about Corven's boy*, and then sat looking at the sentence for a long while, because he remembered the Ashgrove woman from summers here as a child — remembered her, improbably, teaching him to skip stones off the pilings, patient with a small solemn boy, never once laughing when he fell in.

He crossed out the sentence. Then he wrote it again.

* * *

Silas found Jude after dark, in the salt shed where they cured hides, sitting on a barrel with his boots off like a boy visiting his grandmother instead of a name on a death warrant.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You should see the other fugitives." He was nineteen now and had his mother's cheekbones and his father's habit of grinning at exactly the wrong moment. The grin flickered and died. "They took Tam's boat at Willow Reach. They're asking at every crossing. Silas — they have my description. *Diver's build*, it says, like I'm a horse."

"Diver's build is honest. You've got your mother's shoulders; you'll never pass for a clerk." She sat down across from him, and the fear she had carried folded up neat and small for eleven years unfolded all at once, filling the shed. "The bridge is watched. Both ends. And the lieutenant has the rolls out every night looking for you."

"Then I'm done. I'll go upriver and swim—"

"You'll drown. You've always swum like a thrown cat." She heard her own voice go flat and practical, the counting-house voice, and hated it and was grateful for it. "There's one way. The ledger."

He frowned. "What does a book do for me?"

"It says who owes and who is owed. It says who exists." She looked at her hands, which had done quiet illegal work before — a date moved here, a debt forgiven there, small mercies smuggled in under columns of numbers, enough to hang her twice over if anyone ever read the pages the way Lieutenant Halloway was reading them. "Your father's line is closed. Paid in full. If you'd ever been entered under it — apprentice, son, anyone — you'd be in their book, and their book says you're theirs to take. But you're not in it. Do you understand? Six years ago somebody made sure you weren't."

Understanding came over his face slowly, the way dawn comes over water. "That was you."

"Somebody," Silas said.

"So I'm already free. I just walk past them—"

"No. Because a boy who doesn't exist in the ledger but exists in the warrant is a *mistake*, and mistakes get investigated, and investigations pull threads, and those threads are stitched through every diver in Marrow's Bend." She stood up and began pacing between the hanging hides, and the words came faster now, the breaking point arriving right on schedule, right on time, punctual as debt. "Half the waterfront would swing if he reads those pages carefully. So it isn't enough that you're absent. There has to be a reason for your absence that satisfies a clever man. Absence needs a story."

"What story?"

She stopped pacing. She had known the answer since afternoon, since the lieutenant said *very straight lines indeed*, and she had been trying all evening to find a kinder version of it, and there wasn't one.

"A clerk who falsified a record," she said quietly, "and fled before discovery. A clerk who took money to erase a name. Me, Jude. I write myself into your story tomorrow morning — an entry in my own hand, a confession in the margin: erased the son's name for payment, absconded with company funds, whereabouts unknown. Dated tonight. He'll hunt a thief instead of a diver's boy. Thieves are common. Nobody burns a town looking for one."

"That's — no. That's your whole life. That's the counting-house, that's eleven years, that's *you*—"

"It's ink," Silas said, and was surprised at how light her voice came out, how much lighter than she felt. "Do you know what I've learned in eleven years of books? Everything in them is only true because we agree it is. The army agrees I'm a clerk. The crown agrees you're an enemy. Well. I withdraw my agreement."

Jude got up off the barrel and stood there barefoot in the dark with his jaw set like his father's, and for a moment he was going to argue — she could see the argument assembling itself behind his teeth — and then he didn't argue. He hugged her instead, hard, the way you hold on to a piling in a current.

"Then I'm not crossing alone," he said into her shoulder.

Silas had planned many things for the morning. She had planned the forgery, timed to be discovered mid-audit. She had planned the road, the ferry schedule, the lie about a sister in Greywater. She had not planned on the word *alone* landing in her chest and staying there, warm and inconvenient, refusing to be filed away.

"There's a bridge," she said at last, "and it goes both directions."

* * *

At dawn, Edmund Halloway unlocked the counting-house and found the ledger open on his desk, exactly where he'd left it — except that it wasn't. A fresh entry sat in the third register in that impossibly neat hand, dated the previous night, describing a falsified erasure, a bribe taken, and a clerk named S. Ashgrove who had fled toward the river.

He read it twice. Then he looked out the window at the bridge, gray in the morning mist, and saw a small tidy figure walking toward it carrying the second register under her arm like a woman delivering laundry.

Every instinct he owned told him to shout for the guard at the far span.

Instead, Edmund Halloway stood very still in the doorway of his father's counting-house, watching her walk, and understood — with the clarity of a man hearing a false note in a hymn — that whatever happened next, the books were already wrong, and he was going to have to decide, in the next thirty seconds, whose side of the ledger he stood on.
