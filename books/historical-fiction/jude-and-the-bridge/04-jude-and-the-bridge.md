## 4. Jude and the Bridge

The ledger weighed less than Silas expected.

She had carried sacks of pearl shell heavier than this. She had carried buckets of river water up from the shallows until her arms shook, and she had carried, for eleven years, the particular weight of being nobody in particular in a town that liked its nobodies quiet. But the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth and pressed against her chest beneath her coat, felt like carrying a sleeping child—something warm and breathing and terribly easy to drop.

The dawn was the color of unpolished silver. Mist stood on the river in slow columns, and the old bridge rose out of it the way it always had, stone legs planted in the current, spanning the border that half the divers in Marrow's Bend had been born on one side of and lived on the other. Jude was somewhere ahead of her. He had left the smokehouse an hour before she did, moving through the reeds like the eel-catcher's son he was, and she had watched him go with her heart in her throat and thought: *I promised him when he was twelve years old. I promised a boy with river mud on his chin that I would walk him over this bridge if it ever came to it.*

It had come to it.

* * *

She had spent the night making her last entry, and it had taken longer than any forgery she'd ever done, because it was the first honest work she'd done in the book.

Officer Halloway—young Tam, who used to steal dried plums from the counting-house crates and blame the gulls—had grown suspicious of her neat columns. She had seen it in his eyes two days ago, that flicker of a boy remembering he was clever. He kept returning to the autumn pages of nine years back, running his thumb down the margins where her ink sat a shade too confident, where debts were forgiven a little too generously and names appeared and vanished like herons. He hadn't accused her yet. He was saving it, the way boys saved plums.

So she had written, in her own plain hand, on the ledger's final leaf:

*I, Silas Ashgrove, clerk of the counting-house at Marrow's Bend, have falsified entries in this book since the year of the great flood. Every name altered is mine to answer for. The divers owe nothing. They never did.*

Then she signed it, and her hand did not shake, which surprised her. Eleven years of invisible arithmetic, and here at the end her figures finally balanced.

* * *

The soldiers at the bridge were four, stamping their boots against the cold, their breath rising in little ghosts. A sergeant with a red nose and a bored posture held a paper—a description of a boy, sixteen, dark-haired, diver's build—and scanned each figure that came down the river road toward the crossing.

Silas came down the road slowly, an old woman in a man's coat, bent under the weight of nothing much at all. She had put pebbles in her shoes so her gait would stoop. Invisibility, after all, was a craft like any other, and she intended to practice it one last time before retiring from the trade.

Jude was already there.

He stood at the bridgehead as if he'd simply stopped to watch the mist, hands in his pockets, and only she knew how his heart must be going like a fish in a net. One of the soldiers had drifted near him, idly, the way a cat drifts near a gap in the fence. The sergeant looked up. Looked at his paper. Looked at Jude.

"Boy," the sergeant called. "Where are you bound?"

Jude opened his mouth, and Silas heard in the silence before he spoke the whole shape of his fear, and she found—to her own mild astonishment—that she was not afraid anymore. There comes a point past which dread simply runs out of road.

"Bound across," she said loudly, before Jude could answer, "same as me. My nephew. Taking him to his mother's people." She shuffled forward. "Got the family papers here."

The sergeant's eyes narrowed. "Papers, is it."

"Papers," Silas agreed, and drew out the ledger.

It was, admittedly, an unusual sort of family paper. The sergeant frowned at the oilcloth, at the size of the thing, at the company seal stamped on the cover. His boredom sharpened into interest, and his interest into duty. "That's requisitioned property," he said. "That's the Halloway book. That book's meant to be on an officer's desk."

"It was," Silas said. "Now it isn't."

"Now it—" The sergeant's hand went toward it, and something in Silas, some small bright reckless thing that had waited eleven years in a counting-house window, moved first.

She stepped back onto the span of the bridge, held the ledger high over the water, and struck a match against the stone balustrade.

"Hey—" said the sergeant.

"Hoy!" said another soldier, already moving, too late, the way everyone was always going to be too late—

The flame caught the oilcloth, then the cover, then the first hundred pages of every debt ever owed in Marrow's Bend. Silas held it a moment longer than she needed to. Long enough to feel the heat kiss her knuckles. Long enough to read, in her mind's eye, the names as they went: Marta Osei, freed. The Vane brothers, freed. Old Tomas who dove with a bad ear and sang anyway, freed. Her own forged entries, her own careful crimes, her own small name among all the small names—freed, freed, freed.

Then she let it fall.

The ledger dropped end-over-end, burning like a lantern loosed from its string, and hit the river with a hiss and a bloom of orange light that floated downstream between the mist columns, shedding pages as it went—little burning birds, each one carrying somebody's name out toward the sea.

"No—" The sergeant lunged to the rail and watched three years of requisition orders and eleven years of indenture records sink and scatter and blacken. He turned on Silas with his mouth open, and what came out was almost a wail. "*That was evidence.*"

"Yes," said Silas pleasantly. "It was."

"You'll hang for that."

"Very likely," she agreed. "But not today, I think, and not here, because you can't hang me twice for it and my nephew and I are expected across." She took Jude's arm—he took hers, actually, gripping hard enough to bruise, which she forgave him—and they walked.

The soldiers looked at the sergeant. The sergeant looked at the burning page drifting serenely under the bridge, illuminating the water below like a small sunken festival. What, precisely, was he to do? Arrest a woman for destroying proof that would have hanged half the town—including, if young Tam Halloway's audit went as it might, several men whose testimony would reflect very badly on certain collaborating officers? The sergeant's face performed a series of calculations, none of them ending anywhere he wanted to live.

"The boy matches the description," one soldier offered weakly.

"The boy," said Silas, not breaking stride, "is a free-born ferryman's apprentice crossing with his aunt on family business, and the paper that said otherwise is currently fertilizing the river. Good morning."

And because dawn was cold, and the mist was thick, and authority without its paperwork is only a man standing in the rain, they walked past. Four soldiers and a sergeant parted around them like water around a stone, and the stones of the bridge rang under Silas's boots—her boots, bought with her own wages, worn on her own two feet—and halfway across, Jude began to laugh.

It was a helpless, hiccupping, sixteen-year-old laugh, the laugh of a boy who had rehearsed a dozen grim and noble farewells and gotten none of them. "You *told* him good morning," he wheezed. "You set the whole ledger on fire and told him *good morning*."

"Manners cost nothing," said Silas primly, and then she was laughing too, laughing until the tears came, standing in the middle of the bridge with a boy she had once loved quietly and now loved out loud, while behind them the last embers of the book went dark on the water.

They stopped at the crown of the span, where the border ran—an old carved line in the stone that both governments pretended not to see and every diver in Marrow's Bend could find blindfolded. Jude put his toe on it.

"Together?" he said.

"Together."

They crossed the line side by side, and nothing happened. No bell, no shout, no hand on the shoulder. Just the river going on below and the sun coming up thin and gold through the mist, and Silas Ashgrove—clerk, forger, arsonist, aunt by invention—standing for the first time in her life on ground no ledger claimed.

"What do we do now?" Jude asked, looking at the far shore, at the road, at everything.

Silas considered it. Behind her lay every name she'd freed; ahead lay no name at all yet, which was nearly the same thing as any name she chose.

"We get useful somewhere new," she said. "Quietly, at first."

And they walked on into the morning, unhurried, unowned, the bridge creaking its old song beneath their feet.
