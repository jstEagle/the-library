## 1. The Summoning

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, folded twice and delivered by a boy who did not wait for a tip. Ulyana Blackwood read it standing in the doorway of the workshop, letting the winter light fall across the paper so that anyone watching from the street would see only a woman squinting at a bill.

*The master clock at the Clementinum observatory has failed. Present yourself Thursday at seven. Bring your own tools.*

No signature. No courtesy. The imperial seal in the corner had been stamped slightly off-center, as if the hand that pressed it had been thinking of other things.

She read it three times, and by the third reading she had stopped seeing the words and started seeing the mechanism behind them—the way a summons was itself a small machine, with levers of obligation and springs of consequence, designed to move a person from one place to another whether she wished to go or not. Her father would have laughed at that. *Everything is a clockwork, Ulya,* he used to say, tapping his temple with a screwdriver held like a cigar. *Some are just better oiled than others.* But her father had been dead four years now, and the workshop was hers alone, and there was no one left to laugh with except the clocks.

She set the letter down on the bench beside the half-repaired carriage watch and did not think about it again until Wednesday night, when she cleaned her tools.

That was the ritual. Every tool laid out on the felt in order of use: the eyelet files first, then the pliers, fine as heron's beaks; the oilers with their glass reservoirs; the tweezers in three sizes; the loupe, its brass rim worn silver where her thumb had polished it over ten thousand hours of looking closely. She cleaned each one, inspected each one, put each one back into the roll of leather that had been her grandfather's before it was hers. The leather was cracked along one fold and dark as old chestnuts, and it smelled of machine oil and walnut shell and time itself, if time had a smell—and Ulyana had long ago decided that it did. Time smelled of brass dust and whale oil and the faint sourness of human hands.

A person could hide inside that smell. She had done it for four years. The name Blackwood was good for exactly two things in Prague: fine repair work, and trouble. The family had come from England two generations back with nothing but talent and a talent for making enemies, and the second talent had outlived the first in the memory of certain offices along the river. So she worked under the sign that read simply *Hodinářství*—Clockmaker's—in unadorned black letters, and let the guild registry list her as *U. Blackwoodová*, and hoped that the initial would be enough to keep her anonymous among the dozens of journeymen and masters scattered through the city's winding streets.

It had been enough. Until now.

Thursday came down gray and hard off the Vltava, snow that was more grit than crystal rattling against the shutters before dawn. Ulyana wrapped herself in the gray wool coat that made her shape indistinct, wound a scarf to the bridge of her nose, and carried her toolbag close against her hip like something stolen. The city was still mostly asleep. Milk carts cut parallel lines through the fresh fall. A lamplighter worked his way up Smetanovo nábřeží with his pole on his shoulder, and the little flames died one by one behind him as the sky brightened reluctantly, the color of dishwater.

The Clementinum rose out of the river mist the way cathedrals do—not suddenly but gradually, first the suggestion of towers, then the towers themselves, then the astronomical flags stirring at their peaks. The observatory tower stood at the complex's southern corner, older than the baroque skin stretched over it, and near its base a door waited with a soldier beside it.

That was the first wrongness. Clockmakers were summoned to observatories all the time; mechanisms failed in cold weather the way old men's hearts do. But mechanisms did not require soldiers at the door. Soldiers required something worth guarding—or someone worth watching.

Ulyana adjusted her grip on the toolbag and reminded herself of the first rule of her trade, which was also the first rule of her life: *a clock does not care who winds it.* She was here to fix a mechanism. Whatever else lived in this tower—secrets, suspicions, men in uniforms—was gearwork she did not have to touch.

The soldier checked her papers against a list without reading her face. "Blackwoodová," he said, pronouncing it wrong, and waved her through into a vestibule that smelled of candle wax and stone cold enough to ache.

An official met her there—a minor functionary with ink-stained cuffs and the harried expression of a man who had been awake since four making other men's problems legible. He talked while leading her up the spiral stair: the master clock had stopped twice in a week; it kept erratic time; the astronomers were furious because their observations depended on it, and the observatory reported to the university, and the university reported to the governor's office, and so the failure of one escapement had climbed the hierarchy of Prague like water rising through walls until it reached people whose names appeared in newspapers.

"You will understand," the functionary said, pausing on the landing to catch his breath, "that repairs will be observed."

"Observed," Ulyana repeated.

"A formality." He said it the way people say *it won't hurt*, which means it will. "An officer of the customs administration will attend you in the clock room. You may ignore him. Most people manage."

*Most people.* She filed the phrase away with all the other small warnings she collected, the way her roll held spare mainsprings.

* * *

The clock room occupied the top of the tower, a tall stone chamber open nearly to the rafters, where the master clock hung in its great wooden case like a saint in a shrine. It was a magnificent thing even in failure—a regulator of the old school, weight-driven, with a nine-foot pendulum and a deadbeat escapement that some forgotten craftsman had finished with love visible in every polished angle. Through the tall windows the whole of Prague lay below in its winter shroud, rooftops smoking, the river a dull pewter band stitched with bridges. Somewhere beneath that gray cloth, forty thousand clocks were telling forty thousand different times, and this machine in front of her was supposed to be the one they all answered to.

Ulyana set down her bag, unwound her scarf, and forgot everything else.

Work was the one country where she had never been a foreigner. She opened the case, and the smell came out of it—that particular breath of an old clock, oil gone faintly rancid, brass oxide, wood resin, the ghost of every hand that had ever serviced it. She let the weights down gently on their drum, listening to the click of the ratchet, counting. Then she began.

The failure announced itself within the hour: the pallets of the escapement had worn unevenly, and someone—years ago, by the look of the oil—had "corrected" the wear by bending the crutch instead of replacing the pallets, a sin against the mechanism that had compounded quietly ever since, like a lie told often enough to become a personality. She would need to fit new pallets. Fine. That was why she existed.

It was while she was working at the depth of the movement, cheek almost touching the cold brass plates, loupe screwed into her eye, that she saw the letter.

It lay wedged behind the escapement bridge, in the shadowed gap between the plate and the case wall where no functional part of any clock should ever be—an envelope of heavy cream paper, folded flat, sealed with wax that had once been red and had faded toward the color of dried blood. Dust had drifted over it in a fine gray fur, which meant it had been there a long time. Years. It had not fallen in; the geometry of the case made that impossible. Someone had put it there deliberately, wedging it into the one space in the whole mechanism where vibration would hold it rather than shake it loose.

A hiding place. Hidden things in clock cases were usually valuables—coins, sometimes, or a jeweler's stone. But no one hid a letter behind an escapement for safekeeping. Too awkward. Too specific. People hid letters behind escapements the way traitors hid confessions under floorboards: because they never wanted them found.

Her hands went still inside the movement. She became very aware of the room behind her—the draft from the window, the creak of boards, the fact that she had not yet heard the customs officer arrive, though the functionary had promised him. Her fingers rested an inch from the envelope. All she would have to do was draw it out, turn it over, break the seal—

And what? Read it? Know whatever it knew?

Ulyana thought of her father tapping his temple. *Some clocks are better oiled than others.* And underneath that, quieter, from the last winter of his life, when he had seemed to spend most of his time looking out the workshop window at nothing: *There are documents, Ulya. There are always documents. A clever woman leaves them where they are.*

She withdrew her hand. She took up her file instead, and went back to shaping the new pallet steel, letting the thin silver scream of the file fill the tower and crowd out every other sound. The letter stayed where it was, dust settling on it another grain at a time, patient as everything else in that room.

Whatever it was, it belonged to the clock's past, not its future. She was here to make the future run true. That was the whole of her commission, and she intended to leave the tower having touched nothing else, seen nothing else, been nobody at all.

* * *

She was fitting the last pallet when the door at the top of the stairs opened without a knock.

Footsteps—measured, unhurried, the steps of a man who had decided in advance how long this room was going to take him. Ulyana did not turn around. In the loupe's magnified circle the pallet seat gleamed, and she held her file to its final angle and pretended, with the whole of her body, to be nothing more than a woman absorbed in honest work.

"Ulyana Blackwood," said a voice behind her. Not asking. Reading, probably, from a paper. "Apprentice clockmaker. Summoned by order of the observatory directorate."

She eased the loupe from her eye. The world snapped back to its ordinary size: the gray window-light, the hanging weights, the stranger standing six paces off with his hands clasped behind him and the posture of a man cataloguing her for a ledger.

"That's me," she said, and turned, and made herself smile the small closed smile she used for customers who asked too many questions. "If you're here to watch, sir, there's a chair by the stove. Fair warning—it's a long job, and I don't talk much while I'm working."

The man looked at her for a moment longer than politeness required. He was youngish, dark-haired, with the kind of careful face that gave away nothing except the fact that it was withholding something. His uniform was customs administration, immaculate, and his eyes went—not to her face first, but to her toolbag, lying open on the floor with its rolls of steel glinting like a nest of sleeping snakes.

"I don't mind long jobs," he said. "I count seconds for a living."

Behind him, unnoticed by either of them, sealed in cream paper and dust, the letter waited in the dark of the case—and Ulyana understood, with a cold settling in her stomach that had nothing to do with the weather, that her quiet country of work had just posted border guards.
