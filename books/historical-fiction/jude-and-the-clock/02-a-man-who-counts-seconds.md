## 2. A Man Who Counts Seconds

Jude Havel had been counting seconds since he was nine years old, when his father—a customs clerk of the old school, a man who believed that boredom was the tax honest men paid for order—set him at the window of their flat above the river gate with a brass pocket watch and told him to time the barges. How long from the first sight of a mast to the scrape of hull against pilings. How long for a manifest to travel from deck to desk if the captain was cooperative, and how long if he was not. It was meant as punishment for some childhood transgression Jude could no longer remember. Instead it became a discipline, then a habit, then something closer to a religion. He knew to the breath how long it took a lie to form on a smuggler's face: three-fifths of a second of stillness before the mouth began.

So when the summons came down from the Finance Ministry—*all repairs to the observatory's master clock to be observed by an officer of the Imperial and Royal Customs Service; Officer J. Havel assigned; duration indefinite*—Jude read the order twice, folded it into his coat, and felt the familiar tightening in his chest that meant either trouble or opportunity, though he could not yet have said which.

The name on the repair docket was what did it. *U. Blackwood.* Blackwood. An English name in Prague like a splinter of foreign oak in Bohemian grain. He had pulled her family's file the night before, by lamplight, and read it standing up because sitting would have felt too much like surrender. Grandfather a horologist admitted to the guild under dispensation—foreigners were rarely admitted, which meant someone powerful had wanted him admitted, which meant debts existed somewhere. Father dead under circumstances the file described with bureaucratic delicacy as *an incident at the mint, matter resolved*. And Ulyana Blackwood herself, twenty-three years of age, apprentice to Master Vojtěch Randa of the clockmakers' quarter, no record, no debts, no petitions, nothing. A page of clean paper where a life should have been.

Nobody in Jude's trade trusted clean paper.

He arrived at the observatory an hour before she did, because arriving early was half of observation. The tower rose out of the December fog like the mast of a drowned ship, and the caretaker, a stooped man named Prokop who smelled of linseed oil and cabbage, let him in with the resigned air of a man accustomed to being invaded by ministry paper. The stair wound upward through cold stone, and at the top, behind a door banded in green iron, the master clock filled the room the way a heart fills a chest—not merely occupying the space but animating it, every wall trembling faintly with its pulse.

Jude stood in the doorway and listened to it. Eleven tons of gears and weights and ambition, built sixty years ago to keep the empire's astronomers honest, and now failing—the docket said—by four seconds a day, then seven, then eleven, a sickness progressing like fever. The pendulum swung in long silver arcs, and the tick of the escapement was so deep it was less a sound than a sensation, felt in the sternum, felt in the teeth.

*Four seconds a day,* Jude thought. *In my line of work, four seconds is the difference between a manifest and a confession.*

She arrived at eight o'clock precisely, which he noted with professional approval and immediate suspicion, because punctuality in a woman whose file contained nothing was either virtue or rehearsal. She came up the stairs carrying a leather toolbag in one hand and a wrapped loaf in the other, and she stopped two steps below the landing when she saw him, taking him in the way he had seen sailors take in a change of weather: quickly, completely, without visible alarm.

"Officer Havel," she said, reading the ministry order he held out before he could offer it. "Customs. They've sent customs to watch a clock."

"The clock governs the signal that governs the river traffic," Jude said. "River traffic is customs business. If the master clock lies to the harbor pilots, ships meet bridges, and bridges are very expensive." This was true, or nearly true; he had extracted it from his superior that morning and suspected neither of them believed it mattered. "You're the Blackwood girl."

"Apprentice Blackwood," she said, setting down her bag. "Or Master Randa's apprentice, if you prefer accuracy to lineage."

"I prefer files to charm," said Jude. "Yours is remarkably thin."

For the first fraction of a second—three-fifths, perhaps, he was never sure afterward whether he imagined it—her face went perfectly still. Then she smiled, and the smile was warm enough that a less practiced man would have missed the stillness entirely.

"Then you'll be bored," she said, and knelt at the clock's access panel.

* * *

She worked, over the following days, the way water works—finding the low places, filling them, moving without apparent effort toward some level only she could see. Jude had expected a tinkerer, all elbows and intuition. What he got was method. She disassembled the escapement train in sequence, laid each component on a wool cloth in an order that never varied, and cleaned sixty years of congealed oil with a patience that made his own father look impatient. She spoke to the mechanism under her breath—not endearments, exactly, but arguments, negotiations: *not you, not yet, hold, there.*

And she ignored him with a thoroughness that he recognized, after two days, as itself a kind of attention. She always knew where he was. When he shifted from one side of the case to the other, her hands adjusted around him like a stream adjusting around a stone, without looking, without breaking rhythm.

"You count my movements," she said on the third morning, not turning from the pallet knife in her hands.

"I count everything."

"That must make funerals very expensive."

"It makes smuggling very difficult," said Jude. "Which is the point of me."

She set down the knife and finally looked at him directly, and he understood that until now she had been deliberately not doing so, and that the directness was a weapon being unsheathed. Her eyes were gray, the gray of river ice, and they held him the way a jeweler holds a stone to the light.

"Tell me about your regulations, Officer Havel. You've been reciting them to yourself for three days whenever I touch anything larger than a screw. I'd like to know which ones I'm violating."

"None," he admitted. "Yet."

"Yet." She almost laughed. "You're waiting for me to commit a crime against the crown inside a clock tower. Do you imagine what that crime would even look like? Smuggling minutes? Conspiring to make the trains late?"

"I've seen men conspire over less than a clock," Jude said, and heard his own voice come out flatter and harder than he intended. "I watched a grain merchant in Tábor ruin forty families by shaving eleven minutes off the arrival time of a shipment so his broker could sell short. Minutes are money. Seconds are empires. That's why I'm here, and that's why you're being watched, and if that offends you, take it up with the century we live in."

The silence between them lasted four ticks of the great escapement. He counted.

"Eleven minutes," she said at last, quietly. "That's a real number. My grandfather used to say a clockmaker's first duty is to be boring—to be so exact, so unremarkable in her honesty, that no one ever thinks to check her work." She turned back to the movement. "I have spent six years learning to be boring, Officer Havel. You will find nothing in this tower worth watching. But watch, if it pays your wages."

It should have ended there. It did not, because that afternoon the failure revealed itself, and everything changed.

* * *

The fault, when she found it, was beautiful in the way disasters are beautiful—simple at root, catastrophic in expression. A hairline fracture in the suspension spring of the pendulum, invisible until flexed, flexing with each swing, stealing microseconds the way a corrupt clerk steals kopecks. By evening she had the replacement fabricated and fitted, and the great clock's heartbeat steadied audibly, the room settling like a ship finding even keel.

They stood together in front of the open case, both of them listening, and Jude realized he could not remember when he had stopped standing across the room from her.

"There," she said softly. "Listen. It forgives itself now. Before, every swing was a small argument with gravity. Now it's a conversation."

"You talk about it like a person."

"I talk about everything like a person. People are the mechanisms I understand best." She glanced at him sideways, and there it was again—that flicker of assessment beneath the warmth. "You included, though your gearing is more complicated than most. Something is wound too tight in you, Officer Havel, and something else is worn nearly through."

"And what would you replace first?"

"The mainspring of certainty," she said. "It's overdriven. Men who are certain of everything break at the first thing they're wrong about."

Jude should have been offended. Instead he felt something shift in his chest, a component seating into place, and it alarmed him far more than offense would have. He had spent eleven years in customs houses learning to read strangers, and somewhere in the last four days he had stopped reading Ulyana Blackwood and started simply watching her, the way one watches a fire in winter—not for information but for warmth—and the distinction between those two activities was, professionally speaking, catastrophic.

Because there was also the other thing. The thing he had not put in any report.

On the second day, while she worked at the recoil escapement, Jude had taken his turn inspecting the upper movement—the ministry required him to log the interior condition of the case daily, a regulation so obscure he suspected he was its only enforcer—and wedged behind the escapement bracket, folded to the size of a playing card and sealed with black wax, there had been a letter. He had seen it clearly. He had noted its position in his logbook with the neutral phrase *foreign object, sealed correspondence, location recorded*, and he had closed the case and said nothing, because saying something would mean opening an inquiry, and an inquiry would mean the file marked *Blackwood* growing thicker, and he wanted—he discovered with genuine physical dread—to see what she would do about it first.

He had been watching her not touch it for three days. Watching her work around that section of the mechanism with a precision that was almost reverence, her hands never once drifting near the bracket, her eyes never once lingering. It was the most conspicuous avoidance he had ever witnessed, and it confirmed everything his instincts had been telling him since he read her clean, thin, empty file.

She knows it's there. She's deciding what to do. And whatever's in that letter touches her family, or she wouldn't care.

The clock struck seven—its voice restored now, full and even—and the sound rolled out over the frozen city, over the bridges and the burning gas lamps and the snow coming down in slow theatrical flakes. Ulyana closed the case with the tenderness of a nurse tucking in a patient, and began packing her tools in their unfailing order.

"Tomorrow I finish the strike train," she said. "After that, the clock needs nothing from me for months."

"After that," Jude said, "the observatory has no reason to keep either of us here."

"No," she agreed. "Reasons are in short supply everywhere, I find."

She lifted her bag, nodded to him—civil, cool, final—and went down the spiral stair with her footsteps ringing behind her, and Jude stood alone in the trembling room and took out his logbook.

He wrote: *Repair proceeding satisfactorily. Mechanism restored to standard.* His pen hovered. Three-fifths of a second of stillness before the mouth begins—his whole life measured in that interval, the moment where truth and falsehood hang balanced on a pivot, and he had built a career on knowing which way the weight would fall.

He added one line, small, in his own private shorthand that no superior would parse: *Subject has located the foreign object. Awaiting her move.*

Then he closed the book, and looked at the great clock ticking steadily toward midnight, toward the new year, toward whatever was folded behind the escapement like a splinter working toward the skin, and understood that for the first time in eleven years he was not observing a mechanism.

He was inside one.
