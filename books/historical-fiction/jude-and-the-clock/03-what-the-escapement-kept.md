## 3. What the Escapement Kept

The observatory at dawn was a different country than the observatory at dusk.

Ulyana had learned this over three days of work, arriving before the assistants lit the lamps and staying until the cold drove her fingers stupid. In the morning the tower belonged to her alone—or nearly alone, for Jude arrived with the first bell, punctual as a complication wheel, and took up his post by the stairwell door where he could see her hands and she could not see his eyes. She had stopped minding it. That was the danger she did not yet recognize: that a person can grow accustomed to being watched until the watching becomes a kind of company.

But this morning she had asked for—and been granted—one hour before he came up. The great clock's striking train was disassembled across the bench in ordered ranks, and the fault she had hunted for two days was finally cornered. It was not in the escapement itself. It was behind it.

She had known since yesterday evening, when the anchor escapement gave its thousandth reluctant tick and something shifted in the case with a whisper of paper against old pine—a sound no clock should make. A clock's heart is brass and steel; it has no business carrying anything soft. She had gone home, slept badly, and returned resolved.

Now she stood on the ladder with the movement cradled against her shoulder, working the back plate free with a screwdriver her father had ground down herself, its handle wrapped in red thread for luck. The screws came away one by one, each released with a small complaining cry of metal. The last one she caught in her palm before it could drop into the works below—dropped screws were how apprentices learned humility, and she had learned that lesson twice already in her life.

The plate lifted free.

Behind it, wedged into the cavity between the frame and the case wall where no honest mechanism required space, lay a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord.

Ulyana did not move for a long moment. The clock ticked above her head, the remaining train still running on its temporary weight, each beat falling into the silence like a stone into a well. Somewhere below, the observatory's night assistant coughed once and went quiet.

*Leave it,* said the voice of every lesson her mother had ever taught her. *You are here to repair a clock. Clocks do not carry letters. Men put letters in clocks, and men who do that do not want them found by girls with screwdrivers.*

But she had already seen the edge of what was inside the oilcloth—not paper only but paper with a printed border, the kind used by chanceries and banks, and the wax seal, broken and rejoined carelessly, carried an impression she recognized from her apprenticeship under the mint's own contract clocks: the double-headed eagle of the imperial treasury.

Her hands moved before her caution did. That was the truth of it, and she would remember it afterward with shame—that all her careful invisibility came apart not through someone else's discovery but through her own fingers' hunger to know.

She drew the packet out. The oilcloth crackled. Inside was a letter, four pages, folded thrice, in a hand so regular it might have been engraved. And at the head of the first page, beneath the treasury eagle, was written:

*Memorandum concerning the conspiracy of certain persons of Prague, being a true account of their designs upon the Imperial Mint, with names.*

She read the first name and felt the tower tilt.

*Blackwood.*

Not Ulyana Blackwood. Her father, Elias Blackwood, clockmaker, of the house on Karmelitská. And after him, in the same engraved hand, her uncle Tomáš, and the widow Nováková who sold them lamp oil, and a printer named Krejčí whose shop she had passed ten thousand times without once going in, and eleven more names besides, some she knew and some she did not, all threaded together in a single accusation: that they had designed, financed, or concealed a scheme to introduce false dies into the imperial mint at Kutná Hora, to debase the coinage, to shake faith in the crown's money in the very months when faith in the century itself was failing—and that the plot was set to flower at the turning of the year.

She read it twice. The second reading was worse, because the second time she understood the dates. The memorandum was three years old. Three years it had sat behind the escapement, sealed in oilcloth and darkness, while her father repaired the town hall clock and taught her to cut wheels and died believing himself forgotten. Whoever wrote it had never delivered it—or had delivered a copy elsewhere, and hidden this one where only a clockmaker would ever find it. Where *her family* would find it, if they found it at all. A trap laid in the one place a Blackwood could be trusted to open.

Or perhaps not a trap. Perhaps simply cowardice: an informer who could not bring himself to post the thing, and so had posted it into the dark of a clock case, and let time keep his secret for him.

It did not matter which. What mattered was that the letter existed, that it named her dead father among conspirators, and that she was holding it forty feet above a city where a customs officer with patient grey eyes sat waiting for his hour to end so he could come up and watch her hands.

*The invisibility is gone.* The thought arrived whole, the way ice arrives on a pond—all at once, though you watched it coming for weeks. She had built her life the way she built a silent escapement: every surface polished, nothing protruding, nothing that caught the light or the ear. Blackwood was a name to be spoken quietly or not at all, ever since the business with uncle Tomáš's pamphlets, ever since the constable who came to the shop and looked too long at the shelves. She had become the girl who fixed things and asked nothing, whose face was difficult to remember. Three days at the observatory and she had been so careful. And all the while her family's name had been lying behind the very clock she was paid to heal, patient as rust.

She heard his step on the stairs.

There was no time to think and barely time to move, and so she moved the way she had been trained to move—by procedure, by the body's memory of a hundred thousand repetitions at the bench. The letter went back into its folds. The folds went into the flat tin box that held her spare files, the box that lived at the bottom of her toolbag beneath the coil of gut line and the roll of buckskin. The lid closed with a click no louder than a pallet dropping. The oilcloth and cord she folded small and pushed deep into her breast pocket, against her heartbeat, where it seemed to burn through wool and linen alike.

Then she was on the ladder again, back plate in hand, aligning it over the pillars as the door at the top of the stairs opened and let in a draft of colder air and the man who counted seconds.

"You're early," Jude said. "Or the clock's late."

"The clock is neither." She did not look down at him. Looking down meant looking up later, and later there would be things in her face she could not govern. "Early or late is a judgment clocks leave to people. I only try to keep them indifferent."

He came to the foot of the ladder and stood there with his ledger under his arm, and she felt his attention settle on her the way frost settles on glass—invisible in the moment, undeniable after. She set the plate. She started the screws. Her hands, thank God, were steady; her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook somewhere underneath, in a place no regulation could reach.

"The striking train," he said. "You've found it?"

"Found it, yes." Screw, quarter-turn, seat. "Whether I've cured it is a conversation for tomorrow. These old trains hold grudges."

"Grudges." He said the word as if tasting it. "I'd have said springs. Everything wound tight wants to unwind."

"Everything wound tight wants to *strike*," she said. "That's rather the point of a striking train."

She heard him write something in the ledger—the small scratch of his pencil, unhurried. Recording her words? Recording her hours? She thought of the page that must already exist somewhere in the customs house, her name entered beside her father's, and had to swallow before she could speak again.

"I'll need the case open another day," she said, as lightly as she was able. "The strike side wants cleaning. Years of dust in the gathering rack."

"Of course." A pause, measured out like one of his seconds. "Miss Blackwood. May I ask you something that isn't about the clock?"

*No.* "You may ask."

"When you were a girl—did your father teach you this, or did you learn it against his wishes?"

The question was so far from what she feared that it took the ground from under her for a moment. She finished the screw in her fingers before answering.

"He taught me," she said. "He said a craft is the only inheritance that can't be seized. He said it laughing, mostly." *And he is named in a treasury memorandum as a traitor, and I am carrying the proof against my heart.* "Why do you ask?"

"Because you handle the tools like they're letters from someone." He said it plainly, without flattery, and somehow that made it worse. Then he glanced at the opened cavity behind the escapement—at the clean rectangle of lighter wood where the packet had lain, outlined in three years of undisturbed dust.

Ulyana saw him see it. Time did not stop; she wished it would; it went on ticking overhead with perfect indifference while the pencil in his hand lowered toward the empty space and stopped.

"Dust shadow," he said. Not a question.

"The previous keeper stored a spare weight there, most likely. Brass throws off its powder when it sits." Her voice was a masterpiece. She despised it. "I'll note it in my report."

"Yes," Jude said slowly, and his eyes came up the ladder to her face, and for the first time in three days she could not read them at all. "Yes, I imagine you will."

He wrote one more line in the ledger. She would give a year of her life to know what it said. Then he wished her good morning and went down the stairs, his footsteps even as a pendulum, and Ulyana remained alone in the tower with the great clock counting out its seconds above her head—each one now a coin spent from a purse that was nearly empty.

New Year's Eve was nine days away. The cordon would go up around the Old Town by order of the police president, checkpoints at the gates, manifests inspected, names compared against lists. And somewhere in the customs house there might already be a list with *Blackwood* on it, three years older than her caution, waiting for her name to walk up to it and sign itself.

She took the toolbag down from its hook and felt the tin box shift inside it, heavy as a verdict.

Nine days. One letter. One road out of the city, past men who knew her face or feared her name.

She slung the bag over her shoulder and went down into Prague to begin planning how to disappear a thing that had already found her.
