## 4. Three Checkpoints

Ulyana did not go home.

She told herself it was prudence—the observatory's workbench was better stocked, the light from the meridian window kinder to fine work than her rented room above the chandler's shop on Na Kampe. But prudence was a lie she allowed herself, and beneath it lay the simpler truth: home had a door, and doors could be knocked upon, and the men who would come knocking knew her address because her family name was written in a letter that weighed nothing in her hand and everything in her chest.

So she stayed. She banked the fire in the tower's little stove, lit the shaded lamp over the bench, and laid out her tools in their worn velvet grooves with the deliberate slowness of a woman pretending to herself that her hands were steady.

The letter sat where she had hidden it, wrapped now in an oiled cloth inside the false bottom of her toolbag—a compartment her father had built for smuggling jeweled movements past Habsburg customs men who counted screws but not conscience. *Blackwood*, the letter said. *The Blackwood workshop, Prague. Complicity in the design of the sabotage mechanism intended for the imperial mint.* A signature she did not recognize. A seal she did. Her grandfather's mark, pressed into wax twenty years dead.

She had not read it again. Once was enough for dread; twice would be for study, and study meant planning, and planning meant she had already decided what kind of person she intended to be tonight.

She decided. She unfolded the letter by lamplight and read it as a clockmaker reads a broken movement—not with horror but with attention, noting each tooth of accusation, each sprung pivot of implication. The handwriting belonged to someone educated, hurried, frightened. Dated March of 1881, the year of the assassination attempts, the year her father had closed the workshop for a season and taken Ulyana, then nine years old, to live with cousins in Plzeň while he "attended to affairs." The year the Blackwood name had begun its slow descent from respected to merely tolerated.

*So that was it,* she thought, with a strange, cold clarity that surprised her. Not shame exactly—shame required believing the charge, and she did not know yet whether it was true. Perhaps her father had designed something wicked. Perhaps he had refused to and been named anyway, which was nearly as fatal. Either way, the paper in her hands was a loaded mechanism: spring wound, train engaged, waiting only for the right hand to release it.

The question—the whole of the question, stripped of sentiment—was whose hand.

She folded the letter along its old creases and sat very still while the tower ticked around her, the great master clock overhead keeping its patient time, and outside the windows Prague lay under early darkness like a city holding its breath before the last week of the century.

Then she took a fresh sheet of paper and began to draw.

Not a map, precisely. Clockmakers do not draw maps; they draw mechanisms. What she drew was the route from the observatory to the printing house of Karel Vondráček on Spálená Street rendered as a diagram of obstructions—each checkpoint a pallet stone, each guard a locking tooth, and somewhere at the end of the train the escape wheel that would let the whole thing run or jam forever. She drew three circles down the left margin, and inside each circle she wrote a name, and beside each name she wrote what that name wanted, because men are moved by want the way trains are moved by springs.

Three checkpoints stood between her and safety. She knew them the way she knew the escapement's three sources of friction.

* * *

The first was the river gate.

The Svatováclavská gate opened onto the coal wharves below the water tower, where barges loaded through the night and the guards were paid partly from the imperial treasury and partly from the grain factors' own purses—which meant they were diligent about cargo manifests and incurious about anything that traveled without one. Ulyana knew the sergeant there, a broad Czech named Hašek with a game leg and a widow sister who sold pies near the Charles Bridge. Hašek had repaired watches for the wharf masters for years until his eyes failed him, and twice a year since, Ulyana had serviced his watch for free—a silver cylinder movement, English, far too fine for a river sergeant, inherited from some ancestor who'd been better with money than Hašek had ever been.

He knew her face. That was the danger and, if she thought about it correctly, the instrument. Men fear strangers; they wave through friends. If she went through the river gate as Ulyana Blackwood, clockmaker, with a broken pendulum bob in her bag and a plausible errand—the observatory's regulator needs a new suspension, and this bob must go to the gilder on Dlouhá—Hašek would not search her. He would ask after his watch. He would complain about the cold in his leg. He would wave her through and feel pleased with himself for knowing a craftsman.

The risk was the weighing. Barge cargo passed over the great balance scales at the gate, and sometimes, when the guards were bored, they weighed a pedestrian's bag out of mischief. A brass bob filled solid weighs what a bob should weigh. A brass bob with a letter sewn into a cavity cut through its base weighs more—and weight is the one thing a clockmaker cannot lie about, because every man who has ever carried luggage knows what heaviness feels like, even if he knows nothing else.

So: the cavity must be small, the sewing done with wire, the letter folded to the size of a communion wafer. And she must carry something else in the bag that was honestly, provably heavy—a broken mainspring barrel, a jar of mercury for a compensation pendulum, things with legitimate mass—so that if Hašek lifted the bag, his hand would find the weight it expected and stop asking questions. Deception, she wrote in the margin of her diagram, is a matter of counterweights.

She looked at what she had written and felt something turn over in her chest—not remorse, not yet, but the first cold tooth of a gear engaging. She thought of Jude's face when he'd caught her at the escapement, the particular alertness in him, the way he counted everything: seconds, steps, the pauses between her answers. He would see the manifest discrepancy eventually. He counted too well not to.

*Later,* she told herself, and the word tasted like the mercury she handled—bright, mobile, poisonous in quantity.

* * *

The second circle on her page she labeled simply: *the customs house.*

The customs house on the Královská cesta was the true fortress, the place where papers were examined rather than glanced at, where the stamp mattered more than the man. Its night officer was a Bohemian German named Vogel, and Vogel feared the Blackwood name—not disliked it, feared it, the way a dog fears a street it was once kicked on. Ten years ago Ulyana's father had testified before an imperial commission on guild standards and made Vogel's superior look a fool in open session; the superior had been pensioned off, Vogel had been passed over twice for promotion, and the whole customs house had learned that a Blackwood's testimony could end careers. They watched her family's shipments with scrupulous, vindictive care. Every crate from the workshop got opened. Every invoice got queried.

There was no charm that would work on Vogel, no counterweight heavy enough to buy his incuriosity. His want was simple and ugly: he wanted a reason. Give him a reason and he would pull the whole edifice down on her gladly, with stamps and signatures, all perfectly legal.

Therefore the customs house could not be passed—it had to be *turned*. And there was only one way to turn it that she could see, and the way ran through a man with grey eyes and a customs officer's coat who had spent two days watching her hands and asking questions that were almost interesting.

Jude.

She set down her pen. This was the part of the diagram she had drawn twice and crossed out twice, because drawing it made it real: the plan required him. Not his ignorance—his knowledge. He was assigned to observe her; his countersignature legitimized her movements; his access to the customs house was total. If she told him nothing, he would find the discrepancy in her manifests on his own—she had seen him find discrepancies smaller—and then the machinery would jam with her inside it. If she lied to him completely and was caught, she would take him down with her, and something in her flinched from that with a force that startled her, because it was not calculation at all, just feeling, raw and inconvenient as a seized arbor.

But if she gave him half the truth—if she showed him enough to make him choose—

She was not fool enough to think she could predict what he would choose. She had watched him for two days the way he had watched her, and what she had learned was that he was a man at war with his own posting, that he treated regulations the way she treated cheap movements—with professional respect and private contempt. He might arrest her. He might burn the letter. He might, if the half-truth were shaped exactly right, become the strongest gear in her whole train: a customs officer's stamp is worth more than any smuggler's nerve.

It would mean making him complicit. It would mean lying to him in order to tell him the truth, choosing which truths to withhold the way she chose which weights to add to a bag. She understood, sitting alone in the tower with the lamp burning low, that whatever she did to Jude at the customs house would be the first truly unforgivable thing she ever did—or the first thing that proved forgiveness was possible. There was no third outcome. Mechanisms do not offer thirds.

*If he turns me in,* she wrote beside the second circle, and then could not finish the sentence, and left it hanging on the page like a pendulum stopped mid-swing.

* * *

The third circle she drew slowly, and her pen pressed so hard the nib nearly tore the paper.

The New Year's cordon.

Every December thirty-first, the governor's office threw a reception at the Rudolfinum and the streets between the Old Town Square and the river were closed to traffic from ten o'clock until long past midnight. Checkpoints rose at every corner—lanterns, chains, gendarmes checking invitations against a printed list. It was theater, mostly, a display of imperial order for the century's turning, and everyone in Prague complained about it and attended anyway.

But this year the cordon would not be theater. This year, whoever had hidden that letter in the observatory's clock—whoever had used her family's name as either weapon or shield—would know by now that the letter was gone. Letters in escapements do not remove themselves. The search would begin quietly, in records and registers, and it would end at the obvious choke point: a city sealed for the holiday, every exit watched, one woman with a Blackwood face trying to leave with the evidence in her bag.

They would not need to hunt her. They needed only to stand at the cordon and wait, and the century itself would close around her like a trap.

She sat back and looked at the whole diagram at once—all three circles, the route threading them like a pinion through a gear train, and at the bottom, the escape wheel: Vondráček's print shop, where a letter could become a thousand broadsheets by morning, where ink once loosed could never be recalled by any stamp or sergeant in the Empire. The conspirators could silence a woman. They could not silence a printed page multiplied beyond counting.

That was the design. That was the whole mechanism: pass the river gate among honest weights, turn the customs house with a borrowed conscience, and survive the cordon—but the cordon had no solution she could see, not yet, only a blank space on the page where the answer should have been, and four days left on the calendar, and the master clock above her ticking down toward midnight with the indifference of all machines to the fates of the people who wind them.

Ulyana blew out the lamp and stood in the dark tower, listening to it tick, and made herself calm the way she calmed a runaway train: weight by weight, wheel by wheel. Panic was friction. Fear was play in the gears. What remained, when those were removed, was work—clear, sequenced, possible.

Tomorrow she would cut the pendulum bob and sew the letter in wire. Tomorrow she would walk to the river gate with her bag full of honest heaviness and lie to a kind man about why she smiled at him.

And tomorrow evening, Jude would come to check her manifests, because he always came in the evening, and she would have to decide, with the letter warm against her hip, how much truth a man could be given before the giving destroyed them both.

She heard boots on the tower stairs—early, hours early, unhurried and exact, a man who counted seconds climbing toward her.

Her hand closed over the toolbag's handle.

Jude was here.
