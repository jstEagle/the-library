## 5. The River Gate

The letter had spent two days beneath the floorboards of her workshop, and Ulyana had spent those same two days not looking at it.

She knew its weight the way she knew the weight of any tool left out of place—by its absence from order, by the way her eye kept snagging on the loose plank as though the wood itself had begun to speak. She cooked supper over it. She sharpened her files above it. She slept with her cot dragged to the far wall and told herself it was for the draft, when the truth was that she could not bear to lie directly over a document that named her family traitors to the Empire.

By the third morning, she understood that dread was a poor material to work with. It had no grain, no temper. But fear, hammered flat and folded, made something else: a plan.

* * *

The pendulum bob lay on her bench like a severed heart—brass, cracked along its seam, hollow where the regulating weight should have been. It had come off the Nový Town chapel clock three winters ago, and she had kept it because brass was brass and war came in cycles of its own. Now she turned it under the lamp, thumbed the crack open until the two halves gaped like an unshelled nut.

It would hold the letter. Folded to the size of a wafer, wrapped in oilcloth against the river damp, seated in the hollow with wax packed around it so it could not shift and whisper—the bob would hold it perfectly. A pendulum bob was dead metal to any inspector. Nobody weighed the past.

The sewing was the part that took craft. She split the leather of her toolbag's inner flap and restitched it with the bob sewn flat against the frame, positioned so the bag's balance would not change. Ulyana Blackwood had learned needlework from her mother before she'd learned files from her father, and her mother's rule came back to her now with an almost painful clarity: *a seam is only as honest as the thread you hide it with.* She used waxed linen the color of the leather. She pulled each stitch tight and even. When she finished, she ran her thumb along the flap and felt nothing—not a ridge, not a lie.

Then she sat back and looked at what she had made, and felt sick.

Not at the smuggling. The smuggling was clean work; she could respect clean work even when it was her own ruin being threaded through it. What sickened her was the manifest lying beside the bag, written in her own careful hand, listing tools and parts for a repair job at the customs annex on the riverfront—a job that did not exist, requested by an inspector who did not exist, on paper she had bought from a stationer who asked no questions because she had overpaid him not to.

And Jude would read that manifest. Jude, who counted everything—who had counted the seconds of her breathing in the clock tower, who noticed when a gear tooth was cut a half-degree off true, whose suspicion was not cruelty but a kind of attention so complete it had felt, embarrassingly, like being seen for the first time in years. He had signed the pass that let her carry tools out of the observatory district. His stamp would sit at the bottom of her false paper like a signature on a confession he didn't know he was making.

*You've made him complicit already,* she thought, snuffing the lamp. *You did it the moment you smiled at him across the escapement and let him believe the Blackwoods were only clockmakers.*

* * *

The Vltava wore its winter skin that morning—grey-green, sluggish, laced with plates of ice that turned slowly like coins dropped into dark water. Fog stood on it in columns. The river gate was really two gates: the stone arch where the road met the landing, and the water gate itself, a timber barrier across the barge channel manned by four customs men in coats too heavy for their frames.

Ulyana came down to the landing with the barges, which was the whole point of coming with the barges. Cargo moved on New Year's week the way blood moves from a wound—everything the city would consume before the century turned: coal, salted fish, casks of Bohemian beer, bolts of wool, crates of glassware from the north. Six barges rafted together, and among the stevedores and the cargo clerks she was one more figure in a grey coat with a toolbag and a reason to be there.

Her contact was a woman named Marthe, who ran the second barge and owed the Blackwood workshop nine years of repairs paid in favors rather than coin. Marthe didn't greet her. That was the arrangement. She simply looked at Ulyana once, then at the crowd of guards at the arch, and spat into the water, which meant: *bad morning, keep your head down.*

They waited an hour in the fog while the guards worked through the first barge. Ulyana felt the letter against her hip the entire time—not the letter itself, which was sealed in brass and wax, but the fact of it, the way one feels a cracked tooth. Every sound made her flinch and every flinch made her furious. She had repaired clocks through thunderstorms. She had held her father's hand in a room full of secret police when she was eleven and had not trembled. This was different. This was her own heartbeat she could hear now, counting faster than any escapement she trusted.

*Steady,* she told herself. *You are a tradeswoman going to fix a clock. Tradeswomen are boring. Be boring.*

The second barge cleared the arch. Then it was theirs.

A sergeant with a ledger and a mustache frosted with fog waved them toward the weighing table at the water gate. That was standard—all personal baggage went over the scale, cargo went by manifest and sample. Ulyana had planned for this. Her bag declared eleven pounds of tools; she had built it to weigh eleven pounds and a half, because a bag exactly at its declared weight smelled of preparation, and a bag slightly over smelled of human error. She joined the line of deckhands and cooks and one nervous fur trader, and watched the scale's brass pointer swing and settle, swing and settle, and tried to think of nothing but the number.

The man ahead of her was overweight by two pounds of undeclared tobacco and made up for it with a bribe so smooth that the sergeant didn't even change expression. Then it was her turn.

"Name."

"Ulyana Blackwood. Clockmaker." She set the bag on the scale plate. "Repair call at the customs annex. Papers there in the outer pocket."

The sergeant opened the outer pocket without interest. Jude's stamped pass sat on top of her false manifest, and seeing his handwriting—the small, precise numerals, the way he crossed his sevens—produced in her chest a sensation she refused to name. The sergeant glanced at the stamps, grunted, and reached for the bag.

The pointer swung.

Eleven pounds. Eleven and a quarter. Eleven and a half—

The pointer shivered and hung between gradations, and the sergeant's eyes came down from the papers to the dial with the mild affront of a man interrupted mid-yawn.

"Heavy," he said.

"There's a broken flywheel in there," Ulyana said. "Cast iron. I declare it at the annex—it doesn't leave my custody." Her voice came out steady, and she marveled at it from somewhere outside herself, the way one watches a pendulum maintain its arc through a trembling building.

"Sergeant." A second guard drifted over, younger, with the raw knuckles of a man who liked his work. "Annex manifest says nothing about a pickup today."

"The annex manifest," Ulyana said, "is posted inside the annex, constable, which is presumably why they pay you to stand out here in the cold where nothing is posted." She let a trace of weariness color it—not insolence, exactly, just the flat patience of a craftsman kept from her work. "If you'd like to walk me the four hundred meters to confirm, I'll wait while you find your coat."

The young guard flushed. The sergeant did not smile, but something at the corner of his mustache shifted. He lifted the bag off the scale, set it on the table, and began opening compartments with thick, deliberate fingers.

Ulyana had rehearsed this moment forty times in her workshop, and in all forty rehearsals her heartbeat had behaved. Here it did not. She watched his hands move through the file drawer, the lamp oil, the roll of wire, and behind her own sternum something ticked louder and louder, and she understood suddenly, viscerally, what her father had meant when he said that a clockmaker dies twice—once when the mechanism stops, once when she stops trusting her own work.

The stitching held. His fingers passed over the inner flap and found leather, and only leather.

He repacked her things worse than she had packed them—he put the wire where the files went, an atrocity—and shoved the bag back across the table. "Annex. Straight there. If anyone asks, you were waved through by Havel."

"Thank you, Sergeant Havel."

"Don't thank me. Weighing iron gives me a sore back."

She walked through the water gate onto the barge without running. Running was impossible; walking was nearly as hard. Behind her the fog closed over the arch, over the scale, over the young guard who had almost been clever enough to ruin her. Ahead, the river opened southward, and the city fell away bank by bank—the customs house roofline, the spires, the observatory hill where a master clock was ticking without her and a man was watching the door she hadn't come out of.

Marthe brought her a tin cup of something hot and bitter and sat down beside her without asking anything, which was the kindest transaction of the whole morning.

"You look like a woman who got away with something," Marthe said finally.

"I look like a woman who hasn't yet."

"Hm." Marthe drank. "Same thing, on a river. Different thing, in a city."

Ulyana looked at the water sliding past, ice plates turning their slow silver revolutions, and let the exhilaration come—the real thing, dizzy and bright, her body singing *alive, alive* the way it only did after a mechanism she'd been afraid of first ran true. She had done it. First checkpoint. Two remained.

Then, riding in on the wake of the joy, quiet as fog: the memory of Jude's numerals on her false paper. His stamp. His faith, if that's what it was, folded neatly around her lie like the oilcloth around the letter.

She had told him she'd return to the tower on the second of January to regulate the strike train. She had watched him write it in his ledger, believing her. And somewhere behind her in the city, when the discrepancy surfaced—as discrepancies always did, as gears always found the flaw cut into them—Jude would discover that the arithmetic had never balanced, that she had used his precision against him, that the first thing she had ever stolen from him was the truth.

The barges rounded the bend. The river gate vanished.

Ulyana drank Marthe's bitter tea and began, coldly, carefully, to plan how she would lie to him next—and whether, when the moment came, she would be able to watch his face while she did it.
