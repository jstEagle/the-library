## 6. Confessions at Closing Time

The customs house on Křižovnická Street closed its shutters at six, but Jude remained at his desk long after the clerk had snuffed the lamps, the manifest spread before him like a confession he could not make her sign.

He had read it eleven times. He knew the way a man comes to know a face—first the whole, then the particulars, then the small betrayals of detail. The manifest was written in Ulyana Blackwood's precise, upright hand: *one pendulum bob, brass, defective; one set of pinion wire; sundry tools; one oilstone*. Everything accounted for. Everything correct.

That was the problem. It was too correct. He had held her toolbag himself on Tuesday—he had insisted, some instinct overriding courtesy—and the weight of it had settled into his palms wrong. A bag carrying what she listed should have pulled light as a hymnal. This had dragged like a sack of river stones. He had said nothing then. He had watched her climb back into the tower with that bag and told himself he was mistaken, that grief for his own dead ambitions had made his hands unreliable witnesses.

But Jude had been raised in a customs family, three generations of men who weighed cargo and weighed men and learned to trust only the scales. His grandfather used to say: *The lie is never in the goods, boy. The lie is in the arithmetic.* And the arithmetic here did not close. Four hundred grams of discrepancy, perhaps five hundred—the difference between an honest toolbag and one carrying something dense and folded flat against the canvas.

A letter would weigh that much. Folded twice, sealed, carried against the grain of the bag's lining.

He pressed his thumb against the bridge of his nose until colors bloomed. Three days he had known. Three days he had walked beside her through the tower's cold spirals, handed her wrenches, listened to her explain the escapement's temperament as though it were a difficult child, and said nothing. Each hour of silence had been a coin from a purse that did not refill. He was spending himself, and he knew it.

And worse—far worse—was the thing he would not write even in his own thoughts without flinching: he did not want her to be guilty. He wanted the discrepancy explained by anything else. Solder. Spare weights. Sentiment—a keepsake, a locket, a lock of her mother's hair. He had interrogated himself at two in the morning over this wanting and found no defense. Somewhere between the second day's sparring over gear ratios and the moment she had laughed at his stiff Viennese regulations—"You count seconds like a miser counts coins, Herr Offizier"—she had stopped being an assignment and become a person, and persons, unlike assignments, could not be filed.

The clock on the mantel struck six. Below in the street, Prague was lighting itself for evening, gas jets popping alight one by one down Karlova, the smell of roasting chestnuts drifting up through the window gap. Five days until the century turned.

Jude took the manifest, folded it once, and put it in his coat pocket. Then he put on his hat and went to find out what kind of fool he was going to be.

* * *

Her workshop above the clockmaker's shop on Liliová was lit when he arrived, and through the frosted glass her silhouette moved with the economy of someone doing work she loved—no wasted gestures, shoulders relaxed, hands always in motion. He stood in the street for a full minute watching, feeling like the thief he was about to become.

She opened the door before he knocked. She had heard him, of course. Or seen his shadow, or simply felt the change in the building's breathing. Her eyes went to his uniform first, then to his face, and whatever she found there made her step back—not in fear, he noted, but to give herself room.

"Herr Offizier. Have I broken a regulation? Is there a form for visiting after hours?"

"There is." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Form 44-B. It requires me to ask you questions you will not want to answer."

"Then ask them somewhere warm. I have tea, and it is terrible, and you look like a man who has been standing in doorways deciding things."

He followed her up. That was the first betrayal, though he would not count it until later: the simple act of climbing her stairs when every oath he held said to turn around, file the discrepancy, request a formal inspection, and let the machinery do what machinery does.

* * *

The workshop smelled of oil and cedar and the iron bite of filings. Gears hung from pegs like a hunter's trophies; a half-disassembled carriage clock lay open on the bench, its heart exposed and beating. She poured tea into two cups chipped at the rims and sat across from him, and she did not pretend not to know why he had come. That was the thing about her, he was learning. She never wasted movement, not even in conversation.

"You found it," she said.

"I don't know what I found. I know your bag weighs four hundred grams more than your manifest says it should. I know you're careful enough that this wasn't carelessness. And I know"—he set the manifest on the table between them, unfolded, smoothed flat with both hands—"that I have been sitting in my office for three days trying to build you an innocent explanation, because the alternative is Form 44-B."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the shop's regulator clock ticked with the patience of stone.

"And what happens at Form 44-B?" she asked quietly.

"I write what I observed. My superior writes what he concludes. Men whose faces I do not know arrive at your workshop and your observatory and your family's home, and they are not like me, Ulyana. They do not drink bad tea first."

She looked at the manifest for a long time. When she raised her eyes again, all the wit had gone out of them, and what remained frightened him more than any evasion: exhaustion, and beneath it a resolve like bedrock.

"If I tell you," she said, "you cannot unhear it. And then you will have to choose, and I have watched you choose duty your whole life, Jude. It's stitched into you like your name. Don't ask me to be the reason you tear it out."

"My name," he said, "is on an oath I swore to an empire that taxes my countrymen and calls it protection. I have made my peace with it the way one makes peace with a limp. But I find lately—" He stopped. Started again. "I find I cannot reconcile the accounts anymore. So yes. Tell me. Or don't, and I'll decide alone, and God help us both with what I decide."

She laughed then, short and bleak. "You are either the most honest man in Prague or the most dangerous. I honestly can't tell."

"Neither can I. Ask me again at midnight on the thirty-first."

So she told him half of it. He would learn later, in the fullness of everything, that it was half—but that night, and for several nights after, he believed he had all of it, and he would love her a little for how completely she lied.

"The letter names my family," she said, staring into her cup. "It was hidden in the master clock's case, behind the escapement, where no one would look unless they already knew to strip the movement down to the arbor. It implicates the Blackwoods in a plot against the mint. I don't know if the accusations are true—I was nine years old when whatever it describes was supposed to have happened. But I know my father fled because of something, and I know my mother died with that flight in her lungs, and I know that if that paper reaches the right desk in Vienna, there will be nothing left of my family but a line in an archive."

Jude sat very still. *Half-truths are engineered,* his grandfather used to say. *They carry their own weight and no more.* But he checked her story against what he knew—the timing of the old mint irregularities, the Blackwood name in the customs ledgers from '81, the father who had vanished across the border like water through fingers—and it closed. It closed beautifully. That was what he would not forgive himself for: not that she deceived him, but that she deceived him so well, because he wanted so badly to be deceived.

"And you kept it," he said. "Instead of destroying it or turning it over. Why?"

"Because whoever hid it there wanted it found by someone. And because destroying evidence of my own family's guilt—or innocence—is exactly what a guilty person does." She met his eyes. "I'm taking it to someone who can read it properly. Someone who knows which accusations are imperial fiction and which aren't."

"A printer," he said, before he could stop himself.

Her face went white, then carefully, deliberately neutral. "Why would you say that?"

Because I have been reading the manifests of every printing house on this side of the river since Tuesday. Because Novák & Sons ordered an unusual quantity of rag paper last week. Because I am already halfway to being the man who ruins himself for you, and I did it in my office, alone, at midnight, without ever touching your hand. He said none of this.

"Lucky guess," he said. "Printers deal in paper."

Silence stretched between them, taut as mainspring wire. She was deciding, he could see, whether to run, whether he had trapped her, whether the tea had been a mistake. And he understood—with a clarity that felt like falling—that whatever he did in the next thirty seconds would be the hinge his whole life swung on.

He picked up the manifest, folded it along its original creases, and struck a match from the dish on her bench.

"Wait—" she started.

"What manifest?" he said. "I reviewed your declaration and found it complete and accurate. The bag's weight variance is within tolerance for damp winter canvas. I noted this in my journal, which I will leave in my office, where it will be found if anyone looks, and it will say nothing useful to anyone." He touched the flame to the paper and held it until the fire reached his fingertips, then dropped the last corner into the dish and watched it curl into black lace. "There. Now neither of us has committed anything to writing."

Ulyana stared at the ash. "You've just destroyed imperial property to protect the daughter of a man your empire wants for treason."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He looked at her—at the soot smudge on her wrist, the ink stain on her collarbone, the steady dark eyes that had ruined his arithmetic—and gave her the only true answer he had.

"Because when this century ends, I don't want to be inside it anymore. Whatever we carry into 1900, I'd rather carry it with you than for them."

For a moment he thought she might cry, and then he saw that she would not allow it; she was her father's daughter, built of sprung steel and restraint. She reached across the table and covered his burned fingertips with her hand, and her palm was warm and calloused, and neither of them spoke for a long while.

"You can't come with me to the cordon," she said finally. "On the thirty-first. Whatever I'm carrying, wherever it goes—you'll be at your post, and I'll be in the crowd, and we cannot be seen together."

"I know."

"And if they take me—"

"They won't."

"How do you know?"

He turned his hand over and laced his fingers through hers, feeling the strength in them, the certainty of a woman who fixed things that were broken and refused, on principle, to believe anything could not be mended—including, perhaps, him.

"Because I'll be counting seconds," he said.

He left before full dark, going out through the shop's rear stair and the alley beyond, because a customs officer seen leaving a suspect's rooms at night was a loose thread, and threads, in his experience, got pulled. Behind him the workshop light burned in the frost-black window, and in his chest, where his oath had lived for thirty-four years, there was now a hole shaped exactly like her hand.

He did not see, as he turned onto the Charles Bridge, the figure detach itself from the doorway opposite her building—a heavyset man in a plain coat who had been posted there since noon, who had watched the lamp in the workshop burn through two pots of tea' worth of hours, and who now walked unhurriedly toward the offices of the men who were already hunting the letter, and who had begun, that very afternoon, to wonder aloud whether the young customs officer assigned to watch the clockmaker's apprentice could be bought—or broken.
