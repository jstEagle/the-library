## 7. The Customs House

The customs house on Křižovnická Street smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and the particular staleness of paper that had been handled too many times by hands that did not love it. Jude had worked beneath that smell for eleven years. He had thought it neutral, the way a man thinks his own house is neutral, until Ulyana Blackwood walked through his door at a quarter past nine in the morning with snow in her hair and a manifest he had already read four times.

She did not look at him. That was the first thing he noticed—that she was very carefully not looking at him, her gaze fixed on the brass rail that separated the public counter from the officers' desks, her chin lifted at the precise angle of a woman who has decided that dignity is the only coat she owns.

"Apprentice clockmaker," announced the clerk, Váňa, in the bored drone of a man who had said the same sentence nine hundred times. "Repair papers, observatory commission. Second inspection."

"I know," Jude said, and heard how the words came out too quickly.

His superior, Councillor Brandl, looked up from the ledger at the far desk. Brandl was a heavy man built like a filing cabinet, with small eyes that had spent thirty years learning to find the flaw in any document, any face, any morning. He closed the ledger with one finger marking his place.

"Blackwood," Brandl said. It was not a question. The name itself was an interrogation.

Jude felt the floor tilt beneath him, though of course the floor was perfectly sound. It was everything else that had come loose from its moorings.

"The clockmaker is expected at the observatory by ten," Jude said. "The master clock cannot be left stopped past—"

"I know what a master clock is, Novák." Brandl rose, unhurried, and came around the desk with the ponderous grace of a man who had never once needed to hurry because everyone else hurried for him. "Bring the papers to my desk. Both sets."

*Both sets.* There were not two sets. There was one set and its shadow—the manifest Ulyana had filed and the manifest as Jude had amended it two nights before, adding a weight entry where none belonged, a discrepancy so small it might have been clerical error. Might have been. He had told himself, lying awake in his narrow bed on Na Příkopě, that he had made the error deliberately so that he could *find* it, so that he could summon her here under legitimate pretext, so that he could ask her questions no officer was permitted to ask a suspect. He had told himself many things. At three in the morning the lies wore thin as cheap paper, and what remained underneath was simpler and far more damning: he had falsified a record to create an excuse, and now he would falsify another to bury it, and between those two acts lay the whole distance of his life from now on.

He laid both manifests on Brandl's desk.

Brandl read them the way he read everything—slowly, lips moving slightly, one thick finger tracing the columns of figures. Outside, a sledge runner scraped past on the cobbles, and somewhere down toward the river a barge horn sounded, long and low, like something mourning. Snow ticked against the tall windows.

"Weigh entry here," Brandl said, tapping the first manifest. "No corresponding entry here." His finger moved to the second sheet. Then his eyes came up—not to Ulyana, standing straight-backed at the counter with her hands folded over her toolbag, but to Jude. "Your hand, Novák?"

Jude's heart performed a single enormous beat and then went very quiet, the way his father's watch had gone quiet the day it died, all its accumulated motion spent at once.

"Mine," he said. "An error in transcription. I corrected it this morning."

"You corrected it." Brandl's voice carried no inflection at all, which was worse than suspicion; suspicion could be argued with. "In the original file. Which is against regulation 41, subsection—"

"Subsection three. Yes, sir."

Silence. The stove in the corner ticked and settled. Váňa found something fascinating to study in his stamp drawer.

Brandl looked at Jude for a long moment, and Jude understood that he was being weighed, measured, assessed for the thing Brandl valued above competence and above loyalty: *usefulness*. An officer who made errors was a problem. An officer who covered errors was a lever. And Brandl collected levers the way other men collected snuffboxes.

"Stamp it through," Brandl said finally, sliding both manifests back across the blotter. "The lady's time is valuable." A pause, delicate as a blade being turned. "And log the correction properly this evening. In your own hand. I'll initial it myself."

"Yes, sir."

Which meant Brandl now owned a piece of paper with Jude's confession already half-written on it. Jude took the manifests with steady fingers—he would remember afterward, with something like awe, that his fingers had been steady—and carried them to the counter where Ulyana stood.

Their eyes met for the first time.

Hers were dark and level and terrified. Not of him. Of everything behind him—the whole grey apparatus of the Empire arranged in filing cabinets and ledgers and men shaped like filing cabinets, all of it balanced on the contents of a toolbag sewn into a pendulum bob. He saw all of that in the space between one breath and the next, and he saw also, underneath the fear, something that undid him completely: trust. Frightened, reluctant, furious trust, extended like a hand into empty air.

"Your papers, Fräulein Blackwood," he said aloud, in the flat voice of the service. "All in order."

* * *

She waited for him by the church of St. Francis, in the little square where the old women sold chestnuts from braziers and the pigeons made their grey argument against winter. She stood with her back to the Charles Bridge, and when he came to her she did not move closer or farther away, only watched him come as if he were a variable in an equation she was still solving.

"You lied in there," she said quietly. "For me. With him watching."

"I lied in there for a transcription error I invented myself. Which makes it worse, not better. I've understood that since roughly four o'clock this morning."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if it survived the season. "Four o'clock. You woke at four o'clock thinking about regulations?"

"I woke at four o'clock thinking about you," Jude said, and immediately wished the words unsaid, because they were true and truth was now the most dangerous cargo in Prague.

Ulyana looked away, toward the river, where ice floes ground against the bridge piers with a sound like slow applause. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone urgent, stripped of the counter's formality. "You shouldn't have summoned me here. Whatever you meant to ask me—you shouldn't. Every hour you spend near me, you spend inside the same fire. Don't you understand what's coming? They know the letter exists, Jude. Someone talked. My mentor sent word last night—a man came to the shop asking after me by name, describing my face, describing my *bag*. If they're hunting it through official channels, then your stamps, your logs, your name in Brandl's ledger—" She stopped. Drew breath. "There is no way back for you after today. None. I need you to hear me say that plainly."

"I heard you say it plainly on Thursday," Jude said. "When you told me half the truth on the tower stairs and asked me not to follow you. I'm following you anyway." He took off one glove, bare-handed in the cold, and touched the edge of her toolbag—just the edge, just the leather, not her hand, because if he touched her hand in daylight in a public square he would not be able to stop. "I processed your papers personally this morning. My handwriting is next to yours in a government book. Whatever you're carrying out of this city, I carried it past the second checkpoint myself. Do you think I did that planning to walk backward out of the fire?"

"It isn't bravery, it's arithmetic," she said fiercely. "One letter against one officer's whole life—"

"One letter," Jude said, "that names your family. One letter that will hang people, or be used to hang them, depending on whose pocket it sits in at midnight tomorrow. I've done the arithmetic too, Ulyana. I simply refuse to arrive at their answer."

A chestnut seller coughed nearby; an icicle let go of the church eaves and shattered musically on the steps. Ordinary sounds. The world insisting on its ordinariness while the two of them stood in the middle of it conducting treason like a courtship.

"There's a printer," Ulyana said at last, so low he had to lean in to catch it, close enough to see that her lashes held melted snow. "On the far side of the New Year's cordon. Tomorrow night, when the whole city is in the streets—if the letter reaches him before midnight, by morning it isn't evidence anymore. It's ten thousand broadsheets. Evidence can be buried. Ten thousand broadsheets cannot."

"Then it reaches him before midnight."

"*We* can't both carry it. They'll be watching us both now—me especially, they have my description, and you—" Her hand came up, quick and involuntary, and gripped his sleeve, and then neither of them moved. "If anything happens at the cordon, if they take me, you don't stop, you don't turn around, you don't—"

"Ulyana."

"Don't argue arithmetic with me, Officer."

He wanted to laugh. He wanted, absurdly, in the freezing square with the century dying around them, to take her face in both bare hands. Instead he covered her gloved fingers with his bare one, there on his sleeve, and said: "Tomorrow night, then. You'll take the north lane by the hospital wall. I'll take the crowd along Karlova. We converge at the tower steps at quarter to twelve exactly, whether either of us arrives or not." He paused. "That's the plan. It's a bad plan."

"It's a terrible plan," she agreed.

They stood another moment, snow beginning again in earnest, softening the edges of every roof in Prague. Then footsteps crunched behind them—deliberate footsteps, unhurried—and they separated as smoothly as two strangers who had been discussing the weather.

Councillor Brandl stood six paces away, consulting a small notebook he did not appear to be reading.

"Novák." His breath steamed. "Forgotten something at the office?"

"My gloves, sir," Jude said, raising the bare hand. "I seem to leave pieces of myself all over the city lately."

Brandl's small eyes moved from Jude to Ulyana and back, patient as a ledger line. "See that you collect them all before New Year's Eve," he said. "The year-end inventory is thorough this cycle. Everything accounted for. Everyone." He tucked the notebook away and continued on toward the customs house, boots printing slow holes in the fresh snow.

Ulyana's grip tightened once on Jude's sleeve—hard, brief, a whole speech compressed into five fingers—and then she was walking south toward the observatory with her toolbag swinging at her side, alone among the chestnut sellers and the pigeons.

Jude stood in the falling snow and understood, with the terrible clarity of a mechanism seen open for the first time, that Brandl had not followed him by accident. That someone had talked, and it had not been Ulyana, and it had not been him. That the leak ran through the observatory like a crack through a mainspring, and by midnight tomorrow it would reach the people who had taken her mentor, and those people were no longer waiting for the letter to surface.

They had begun, tonight, to hunt.
