## 8. Hunters in the Belfry

The knock came at half past nine, and it was wrong.

Ulyana knew every sound her workshop made—the sigh of the settling joists, the tick of the six clocks she kept wound out of habit rather than need, the scrape of the tram bell two streets over. This was none of them. This was a fist, measured and patient, three strikes and a pause, as though whoever stood outside had all the time in the world and wanted her to know it.

She had been at her bench for four hours, working by lamplight on the decoy. It lay before her now: a letter aged with tea and smoke, folded to the same creases, sealed with wax she had colored to match and pressed with a button ground flat to imitate the original's crest. The real letter rode against her ribs, sewn into the lining of her waistcoat where no manifest would ever think to look. She had told herself, sewing those stitches by candlelight, that she was being careful. That care was the same thing as safety.

The knock came again.

She opened the door with the chain on, and for one absurd heartbeat she thought it was Jude—his shoulders filled the doorway the same way—but the man wore no customs grey. He wore civilian black, badly, coat cut for a broader man, and behind him stood another, thinner, holding his hat against his chest like a mourner.

"Fräulein Blackwood." The first one smiled without warmth. "We regret the hour. There has been an accident at the observatory."

Her hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the December draft. *An accident.* In Prague, in this last week of 1899, delivered by men whose coats didn't fit, the word meant exactly what it was meant to hide.

"What kind of accident?"

"Herr Novák fell. On the tower stairs." The smile held. "He is asking for his apprentice. You will come."

Novák. Seventy-one years old, hands steady as a lathe, who had not asked for anyone in his life except once, quietly, to have the kettle kept warm. She thought of him bent over the escapement with her, the day they'd found the letter's hiding place empty of everything but dust because *she* had already taken it. She thought of how she had said nothing. How she had walked home through the snow composing lies with a craftsman's attention to detail, and how every lie had been built on the assumption that the danger would come looking for *her*.

"He fell," she repeated. "On stairs he has climbed twice a day for thirty years."

"Old buildings," the thin one said, "are treacherous in winter."

She could refuse. She could bolt now, out the back, down to the river, and be gone before they finished knocking on her shutters. But they had said *asking for*, and whether it was true or bait, it worked identically: she was already reaching for her coat.

"Bring your tools," the broad one said pleasantly. "If there is damage to the clock, who better to repair it?"

* * *

The observatory at night was a different animal than the observatory of her mornings. By daylight the tower belonged to workmen and scholars, to chalk dust and the smell of oil; the great clock overhead was a heartbeat so constant it became silence. Now the halls were stripped bare. Doors she had never seen locked stood locked. And on the stairs of the north turret, where the lamplight failed, two more men waited—one leaning against the wall picking his nails with a knife, one standing very straight in the manner of someone who had once worn a uniform and missed it.

But it was the workshop at the top that stopped her breath.

They had taken it apart the way a child takes apart a beetle. Novák's benches overturned, drawers emptied onto the floor in glittering heaps, forty years of calipers and files and half-finished movements kicked across the boards. The long-case regulator he treasured above all his works stood open like a gutted fish, its weights dangling, its pendulum snapped clean at the suspension spring. Someone had gone through it gear by gear, wheel by wheel, hunting.

And in the center of the wreckage, tied to his own chair, sat Master Novák.

His left eye was swollen shut. His right found her the moment she crossed the threshold, and in it there was no fear—only a furious, ancient irritation, the look he gave apprentices who forced a wheel instead of fitting it properly.

"You let them in," she said to the room, though she looked at the straight-backed man. "You had no warrant. He is a subject of the Emperor same as any of you."

"And loyal to the Empire," the straight-backed man agreed. "Which is more than can be said for his late employer's family." He stepped forward into the lamplight, and she saw he was older than she'd first thought, silver at the temples, the kind of face that would be respectable again the moment he put on better clothes. "Sit down, Fräulein Blackwood. We haven't much time, and I dislike repeating myself to intelligent people."

She did not sit. "You've broken his hand."

"One of his fingers. The others remain functional. Whether they stay that way—" he gestured, almost courteously, at the wrecked bench, "—depends entirely on you."

Novák spoke for the first time, voice cracked but level. "Ulyana. Don't." A wet breath. "It's only a letter. Paper doesn't outweigh a life—"

"*Hush*, Herr Meister," the silver-haired man said softly, "you'll get your turn." To her: "You took something from this clock in November. A document concerning certain... financial arrangements. Its contents, if published, would ruin men who are presently arranging the fiscal future of this empire. We want it back. You will bring it to the New Year's cordon tonight—"

"I know about the cordon."

Something flickered in his eyes. Recalculation. "Then you know it is the only gate still open to you after midnight. Every other road out of Prague closes at eleven. The river is watched. The trains are watched. But the cordon—" he spread his hands, generous, "—the cordon will admit you. Personally. My men will be waiting at the Charles Bridge barrier. You hand the letter to no one else, you tell no one, and by morning your mentor is drinking his coffee and your name is nowhere but in the clockmaker's guild rolls. A clean trade."

And there it was. Not a checkpoint—a snare dressed as one, the third gate of her route turned inside out like a glove. She had spent days mapping the city as a mechanism, every guard a tooth on some great escape-wheel, and all the while these men had simply *removed* the wheel and put their own hand in its place. The cordon wasn't a way out. It was the one door they were certain she would walk through, because they had hung a life on the far side of it.

She thought of Jude then—not tenderly, but with terrible clarity. Of the stamp he had forged under his superior's eye, sweat shining at his hairline. If these men owned the cordon, did they own more? Had her route been reported not leaked but *sold*, checkpoint by checkpoint, until every kindness shown to her had been a coin passing from one hand to another? No. No—she had seen his face. Whatever else in this city was bought, that had not been. She would build on that or build on nothing.

"Say I agree," Ulyana said. "What proof do I have he lives past midnight? Men who break a craftsman's fingers over paper are not famous for honoring trades."

The silver-haired man sighed, as if her caution personally wearied him. He nodded at the knife-man, who crossed the room, seized Novák's bound wrist, and held it up into the light like something offered for sale. Swollen knuckles. A ring the old man had worn since before she was born.

"You see? Alive. Annoyed. Entirely restorable." He smiled. "Midnight, Fräulein. The bridge barrier. Come alone, come unarmed, come *unaccompanied*—we will know if a customs officer walks beside you, and we will take it poorly."

*We will know.* They had eyes at the customs house. Her stomach dropped through the floor of her, down through the tower, into the frozen river below the city.

* * *

They let her gather Novák's coat and a blanket, and then they walked her down the turret stairs and out into the night with the courtesy of men finishing an appointment. At the observatory gate the broad one paused.

"A happy new century to you," he said. "However many years of it you're permitted."

Then the street swallowed her, and she was alone with the cold and the bells beginning somewhere across the river, practicing, rehearsal chimes rung raggedly for tomorrow's century.

She walked without direction for six streets, seven, until she found a churchyard with a bench beneath a bare linden, and sat, and let herself feel it.

Grief came first—for the old man's broken hand, which was her doing; for her workshop's safety, spent; for the girl who had believed, eleven days ago, that a person could be invisible if only her work was good enough. Then, underneath the grief, something harder surfaced, something that did not weep. She recognized it because she had been training it her whole life without knowing: the part of her that looked at a stopped clock and did not despair but *listened*, hunting the single fouled tooth among hundreds.

Very well. They had designed a trap around her obedience. So she would not be obedient. She would walk to the bridge barrier at midnight carrying exactly what they demanded—and it would be the decoy, the tea-stained counterfeit she had built with a counterfeiter's patience, and when they opened it in triumph she would be watching their faces, learning them, while somewhere across the crowd the true letter traveled in hands they were not watching. Because they had made one error, the unforgivable one, the one she would have flunked an apprentice for: they had assumed she would trade a life for her own safety, and so they had never considered what she might do if the trade ran the other way.

She would give them her body at the barrier, her name, her freedom if it came to that. Let them seize her. Let them hold her in the torchlight of the last hour of the nineteenth century while the crowd sang and the real letter slipped through the press of coats and fireworks toward the printer on Ječná Street.

The risk was Jude. Everything now hung on Jude—whether his oath had more bend in it, whether his tenderness could survive becoming conspiracy. She had half a truth to give him and no time to give it gently.

She rose from the bench. Above the rooftops the observatory tower stood black against the stars, and faintly, impossibly, she could feel it—the master clock up there in its case, still turning over its great wheels, counting down the hours to midnight as though nothing in the world had changed. It did not know it had been betrayed from within, just as she hadn't. But a clock could be cleaned. A clock could be set right.

She turned her steps toward the customs house, and began, in her head, to count the seconds.
