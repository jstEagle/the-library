## 9. Midnight Mechanism

Ulyana had counted the seconds of her whole life in the language of gears, and tonight she finally understood why. A clock did not hurry. A clock did not hesitate. It simply moved through the interval allotted to it, tooth by tooth, and if a wheel broke—if a spring snapped and everything came crashing down—it broke on schedule all the same.

Eleven forty-one. She stood at the mouth of Celetná Street with the decoy letter pressed against her breastbone, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of her coat where a woman might hide anything worth hiding. The letter was a forgery she had built herself in her mentor's back room, aged with tea and smoke, sealed with wax she had colored to match the original. She knew every flaw in it. That was the point. A trap must be legible to the people walking into it, or they would not walk.

The crowd around her was already thickening, flowing toward Old Town Square like water finding its level. Paper lanterns bobbed above the heads of children riding their fathers' shoulders. A vendor sold roasted chestnuts from a cart, and the smell of them—warm, ordinary, absurd—rose over the cold stone of the empire. Somewhere ahead, past the barricades and the grey coats of the cordon, the observatory tower stood black against the stars, its dial glowing faintly, its hands crawling toward the century's end.

Her hands were steady. She noted this the way she noted the beat of an escapement: without judgment, simply as information. Inside her chest something else was screaming, but the screaming part of her had been locked out of the workshop hours ago, when she had kissed Jude once, hard, at the door of the printing house and said, *Not you. They know your face too well.* He had argued for exactly four seconds—she had counted—and then he had taken the real letter from inside his coat, pressed it flat against his heart as if sealing an oath, and gone.

*Every second accounted for.* That was the design. At eleven fifty she would present herself at the cordon, visibly nervous, visibly carrying something. The conspirators watching for her—the ones who had taken Herr Novák, who had left his spectacles broken on the observatory floor as a message—would take her instead of searching further. And while they held her under the tower's shadow, believing they held the century in their hands, Jan Marek's press would be rolling, and the truth about the mint, about the forged imperial seals, about the men whose signatures lined that stolen letter—would be drying on a thousand broadsheets in a thousand wet stacks across the city.

She thought of Novák's hands, trembling when they took him. She thought of the pendulum bob, still sitting in the evidence locker at the river gate, empty now, its lead core drilled out and refilled with nothing. She had made so many empty things this week. It was almost restful, in a terrible way, to finally make one on purpose.

Eleven forty-four. She began to walk.

* * *

Jude had never been good at crowds. As a customs officer he had learned to read them the way other men read ledgers—flow rates, pressure points, the places where contraband pooled—but tonight Prague refused to be read. The square was a single animal, ten thousand hearts beating toward midnight, and he moved through its body with the real letter flat against his chest, dressed not in his uniform but in a stevedore's coat two sizes too large, a worker's cap pulled low.

The printer's shop lay three streets east of the square, behind the Church of St. James. Marek had promised the first run would come off the press at eleven fifty-five; the distribution boys were already stationed at six corners of the city, waiting for the signal bell. Not the tower bell—that would be too late, too watched. The carillon of St. James, which struck its own quarter-hour four minutes before the observatory, an old disagreement between two clocks that no one had ever bothered to reconcile. Ulyana had found the discrepancy herself, days ago, tracing the gear trains in her head while Jude pretended to inspect her tools. *Prague runs four minutes late everywhere except the sky,* she had said, and laughed—the first true laugh he'd heard from her—and in that laugh the whole plan had been born.

He loved her with a ferocity that frightened him more than the cordon did. There was no regulation for it. No manifest he could stamp, no weight he could check, no seal he could break to verify what was inside him. Every hour since the customs house—since he had pressed a false stamp onto her papers while Brandl watched over his shoulder and his own pulse roared in his ears—he had been living outside the law of himself, and he found, shamefully, that he did not want to go back.

A patrol crossed his path at the corner of Ungelt. He turned his shoulder, became furniture, became freight. The soldiers passed. In the pocket of the stevedore's coat his hand closed around a spare fuse he carried for no reason except that holding something made the shaking less visible.

Eleven fifty-two, by the church clock ahead of him, which meant eleven forty-eight in the tower behind. Four minutes of grace. The whole city lived inside those four minutes tonight, and only two people in it knew.

He thought of her walking toward the cordon alone and made himself keep walking east.

* * *

They stopped her exactly where she had calculated they would—at the second barricade, beneath the tower, where the light from the guard brazier fell full on her face and the faces of the men behind it. Three of them in plain coats. One of them she recognized from the observatory, from before, from a visit that had never been explained: a heavy man with a scarred knuckle who had once stood in Novák's workshop asking about deliveries.

"You're the clockmaker," he said. Not a question.

"Apprentice," said Ulyana. And then, because fear performed best when it leaked: "Please. I have to get home before midnight."

"Open your coat."

She did it slowly. Her fingers found the oilcloth packet and lingered on it—one heartbeat too long, a hesitation machined to tolerance—and saw his eyes fix there like a pawl dropping into a notch.

Inside her ribs, her heart kept perfect time. She had trained it for twenty-three years on the great regulator in her master's shop, breathing to its tick, and it would not fail her now. Eleven fifty-six, said the tower above them, its hands luminous against the dark. In eight minutes, by the false church time the whole crowd trusted, the century would end. In four minutes, by the true time, the presses would roll.

The heavy man took the packet from her lining with gloved fingers and weighed it in his palm, and something in his face went hungry and satisfied, and Ulyana let him see her flinch—let him believe, wholly, that he had just taken everything.

"The Blackwood girl," he said to the others, softly, like a man confirming a debt. "Bring her."

* * *

At eleven fifty-three by St. James's clock—eleven forty-nine by the tower—the first broadsheets came off Jan Marek's press, still warm, ink smelling of iron and lamp oil. Jude stood in the doorway of the printing house and watched the boys take the stacks and run, six directions, six corners, folding the future into their jackets like boys stealing apples.

Marek put the last proof sheet in Jude's hand himself. THE MINT CONSPIRACY, it said across the top in letters like hammer blows. Below it, transcribed line for line in careful type: the letter, the names, the seals, the plan to flood the century's first day with counterfeit crowns and blame it on the Bohemian guilds. And at the bottom, in smaller type, the words Jude had insisted on adding himself, because some debts must be paid in public:

*Verified and attested, Sebastian Novák, master horologist, this 31st day of December, 1899.*

"He dictated it to me at gunpoint's memory," Marek said grimly, wiping ink on his apron. "They released him an hour ago. Said he'd written nothing worth killing him for." His eyes flicked to the window, to the dark shape of the square. "Your clockmaker is still carrying the decoy."

"I know." Jude folded the proof and put it inside his coat, against his heart, where the real letter had ridden all night and now rode nowhere, having given itself entirely to ink. "I'm going to get her."

"Through a cordon?"

"Through a cordon," Jude said, "at midnight, in a crowd of sixty thousand people who are all, in about seven minutes, going to be reading."

He stepped out into the cold. Above him the church carillon gathered itself to strike, and somewhere behind him, four minutes adrift in the honest dark, the observatory's great clock turned its patient wheels toward the hour, keeping the only time that had ever mattered: the time it would take him to reach her.

* * *

They marched her up the tower steps, two guards before her and two behind, the heavy man leading, the forged letter clutched in his fist like a warrant. Ulyana counted the stairs—forty-seven, forty-eight—because counting was prayer and engineering both, and because she needed to know precisely how long it would take the news to climb down to her.

Below, beyond the arrow-slit windows, the square seethed, lanterns and breath-smoke and ten thousand upturned faces. On a corner near the Josefov arch, she saw one of Marek's boys reach the lamplight, unfold a broadsheet, hold it up to a stranger, then another, then a third—and she saw the stranger grab it, saw heads bend together, saw the paper begin to move through the crowd the way fire moves through dry grass: not fast, not yet, but in every direction at once.

Fifty-two steps. The belfry opened above her, cold and vast, ropes hanging like hanged men. The heavy man turned, lifted the oilcloth packet high so she could not fail to watch him open it—

—and below, all across the square, the first voices began to rise, confused at first, then angry, then many, a sound like a gear train catching: *the mint—the seals—look—the names—*

The heavy man tore the packet open. Tea-aged paper. Smoke-cured wax. Her own forged hand, every flaw placed like a jewel.

His face went through recognition slowly, terribly, the way frost crosses a window. And Ulyana Blackwood, apprentice clockmaker, standing in the belfry of the century's last minute with four men between her and the stairs, smiled at him with perfect, machined calm and said:

"Count the seconds. You haven't many left."

Below them, four minutes early and utterly correct, the bells of St. James began to ring.
