## 10. The Name Inside the Case

The bells began before the smoke had cleared.

Ulyana heard them first as vibration—through the cobblestones, through the iron railing of the observatory stairs, through the soles of her boots where she stood with the decoy letter crushed in her fist and the whole of Prague shouting itself into a new century around her. St. Nicholas first, then Týn answering, then every small parish church waking in sequence like a row of gears catching teeth, until the air itself was nothing but bronze and thunder and the century's turning made audible.

The conspirators did not hear their own ruin arrive. That was the beauty of it—the one clean, perfect beauty in a night that had cost her everything else. They stood on the tower steps with Major Aldous Voss's gloved hand closed around her wrist and his men closing around her like a watch case snapping shut, and they were still certain of victory, still certain that the letter in her bag was the only copy in the world, because arrogant men always believe the mechanism turns only for them. Voss tore the envelope from her with the tenderness of a man unwrapping a gift he had paid for in blood. He read it by lantern light. His face went through three distinct expressions—triumph, confusion, and then the long grey settling of a man who has just heard the escapement slip.

*It is a forgery,* Ulyana thought, watching him. *And I forged it better than you ever could.*

Down the hill, through the crowd, she saw the printers' boys already running with armfuls of paper, broadsheets flapping white as gulls, and she heard the word pass through the crowd the way fire passes along a fuse: *the mint—the mint—the names—the names.* Jude had done it. Somewhere in that press of ten thousand celebrating strangers, Jude Blackwood—not yet Blackwood, not yet anything of hers, but close, so close—had carried the true letter through the cordon on his own body and delivered it to the one man in Prague who could turn ink into judgment before dawn.

Voss's grip tightened. She felt the bones of her wrist grind and did not cry out, because he wanted her to cry out, and she had spent a year learning exactly which of her pains to spend and which to keep.

Then the soldiers came up the steps from below—and they were not Voss's soldiers.

She would learn the details later, in the days when details were possible again: how the police commissioner had received an anonymous broadsheet at nine o'clock, how two of Voss's own captains had chosen the winning side with the swift pragmatism of men who had never truly chosen anything, how the mint clerk whose testimony anchored the whole conspiracy had been found at half past eleven sitting in a café on Mostecká, drinking plum brandy with the fatalistic calm of a man who had decided that if the century was going to end anyway, it might as well find him comfortable. But in the moment Ulyana knew none of this. She only knew that hands were pulling Voss away from her, that his lantern fell and guttered on the wet stone, that his voice climbed into registers she had not thought ambition could reach, and that above it all the master clock—her clock, broken and silent these six weeks—hung in its tower like a heart waiting to be restarted.

Mentor Arnošt came out of the belfry leaning on two men and looking, as always, mildly inconvenienced.

"They searched my workshop," he said, when she reached him. "They knocked over my flux pot. The one from 1874."

"I know."

"And they let the regulator run down." He said it the way other men said *they burned my house.* Then his eyes found hers over the tops of his spectacles, and beneath the dryness there was something she had never seen there before—not pride exactly, or not only pride. Recognition. As if he were seeing, for the first time in twelve years of apprenticeship, the full dimensions of what he had been training.

"Go home, girl," he said. "Sleep. Tomorrow we will have a great deal of work."

But home, that night, was a room she did not go to.

* * *

Dawn on the first of January, 1900, arrived the colour of hammered tin, and Ulyana Blackwood walked up the observatory hill carrying her toolbag as if it were an ordinary morning, because it was an ordinary morning now, because the extraordinary thing had survived the night and become simply the world.

The guards at the door saluted her. Not stopped-and-questioned, not watched-her-name-written-in-a-ledger. Saluted. One of them, a boy of perhaps nineteen with chapped cheeks, said, "They say it was you, madam. What's in the papers." And she did not correct him, did not deflect, did not perform the small shrug of anonymity she had worn for years like a second coat. She said, "It was not only me," which was true, and let him believe what he needed to believe, which was also a kind of truth.

Inside, the tower smelled of cold stone and old oil, and the master clock waited.

Six weeks she had circled it. Six weeks of repairs conducted with her eyes down, of wheels trued and pivots polished while refusing to look at the whole of what she tended, because to look at the whole was to claim it, and claiming was dangerous, and the Blackwoods had learned across three generations what happened to those who built beautiful things too loudly. Her grandfather had engraved his name inside cases and paid for it in exile. Her mother had taught her to sign work where no one would ever look—a scratch code under the great wheel, initials filed nearly smooth—and Ulyana had absorbed the lesson so completely she had almost forgotten there was another way to exist.

She set down her bag. She opened it. The tools lay in their fitted pockets, and beside them, wrapped in chamois, the true letter—the evidence, the thing that had nearly killed her, that had unmade a major and remade a city—folded now to the size of a prayer book.

She had thought all night about what to do with it. Burn it, some part of her argued; it had done its work, and papers like that outlive their usefulness dangerously. Give it to the archives, argued another part, where it would be catalogued and boxed and gradually forgotten. But both of those answers belonged to the woman who had walked up this hill in November with her head down and her tools sharp, the woman who repaired things without asking what they meant.

She knelt at the open case instead, and she began to build a compartment.

It was delicate work, and she was glad of that, because delicate work left no room for trembling. A shallow recess behind the striking train, invisible unless the entire movement was lifted out—which required knowledge, patience, and hands that loved the mechanism more than they feared it. She cut the wood with a knife older than she was. She lined the recess with felt. She fitted the letter inside, folded flat, and above it she fixed a thin brass plate, screwed flush, that would hold the movement's weight and bear, in time, the small honest wear of a hundred years of maintenance.

Then she took out her graver.

The steel was warm from her pocket. She tested its edge against her thumbnail the way Arnošt had taught her when she was fifteen and all elbows and hunger, and she positioned the tip against the inside of the case, on the main plate, where no inspector would look and no thief would steal and only someone who opened the heart of the thing—someone like her, someone who loved it—would ever read.

She engraved her name.

*ULYANA BLACKWOOD.*

Not scratched in secret code. Not filed nearly smooth. Cut deep, in letters as clear as a bell's inscription, the full name, the family name, the name that had been a weight and was now—she felt it shift even as the graver moved, felt the whole load redistribute like a bridge accepting traffic—a foundation. Her grandfather had signed his work and suffered for it. Her mother had hidden hers and diminished herself by inches every year. Ulyana signed hers because the clock deserved to know whose hands had saved it, and because she deserved to be known by the thing she loved, and because silence had bought her survival once and she was finished paying that particular price.

The door at the base of the tower opened and closed. Footsteps on the spiral stair—she knew them now, would have known them in a crowd of thousands, in a city of bells. Even, patient, slightly longer on the left foot than the right. She did not turn around. She kept engraving, because the second line was already begun, and it seemed suddenly very important not to stop.

Jude reached the top and stood in the doorway with snow melting on his shoulders, wearing civilian clothes that fit him badly, as if his body had not yet agreed to the change. His customs commission had lasted precisely four days past the arrests—long enough for the inquiry board to take his statement, long enough for him to confess to falsifying a stamp and destroying manifests and failing to report a known fugitive, long enough for the board to conclude, in the marvellously bureaucratic language she had later read herself, that Officer Kade's irregularities were *subsumed within services rendered to the Crown*, and to accept his resignation with a relief that everyone present pretended not to notice.

"You're supposed to rest," he said. "Arnošt told me you'd be here anyway. He bet me a bottle of slivovitz."

"Which way did you bet?"

"That you'd be here." He came closer, stopping at the edge of the case, where the smell of brass filings and lamp oil rose between them. "What are you cutting?"

"An inscription."

His eyes moved over the plate, reading her name in the new-bright steel, and she watched him understand it—not just the words but the act, the size of it, the whole history of hiding ending in a single line of graver-work. Something moved through his face that she had seen there once before, in the tower in November, when she had handed him half the truth and he had held it like something holy.

"There's room," she said, "for two names."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, far below, the city was sweeping up the ashes of the old year and arguing loudly about the future, and somewhere Voss sat in a cell designing his defence, and somewhere Arnošt was probably already cleaning the flux pot, and the river ran grey and patient under the bridges as it had run under every turning of every century this city had ever survived.

"My father," Jude said slowly, "spent thirty years stamping other people's names into ledgers. He used to say a man's name was just the sound the state made when it wanted him." He looked at the graver in her hand, then held out his own hand, palm up, asking. "Show me how to make it mean something else."

She put the tool into his hand and guided his grip, her fingers over his, the blade finding the brass, and together—they argued pleasantly throughout about the depth of the strokes and the width of the letters, and she won most of the arguments, as she privately intended to keep doing for the rest of her life—they cut the second line:

*JUDE KADE.*

Below it, smaller, because she insisted and he surrendered, a date: *1 January 1900.* Below that, because he insisted and she did not surrender so much as stop resisting, a single additional line that he cut himself, tongue between his teeth, with the concentration of a man defusing something:

*Repaired by love. Keeps excellent time.*

"It's sentimental," she said.

"It's accurate. Wind it."

She wound the great weights up their cables, set the pendulum swinging with two fingers against the bob, and adjusted the escapement until the tick settled into that perfect, seamless rhythm that no one notices until it stops—the sound of time being taken seriously. Above them the hands, which had hung dead at twenty past four for forty-one days, shuddered, crept, and began again to move.

At noon the master clock struck the hour for the first time in six weeks, and the sound rolled down over the rooftops and the river and the sleeping celebrants of Prague, and inside the case, in brass and steel, where only the worthy would ever look, two names rode the vibration like passengers on a ship, going wherever the centuries were going, together.

Ulyana closed the case door and latched it, and stood a moment with her palm flat against the wood, feeling the heartbeat under it.

"Well," said Jude. "What do we fix next?"

She smiled, took up her bag, and told him.
