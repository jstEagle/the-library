## 1. The Weight of Glass

The lamp took eleven minutes to wind, and Orsen Delacroix had never once needed all of them.

She could have done it in seven if she hurried, six if she was angry — and there had been a season, two winters past, when anger was the only fuel the house had in plenty. But hurry was a habit for young keepers, and Orsen had learned that the glass did not care whether you rushed or lingered. The mechanism turned at its own pace. The sea came in at its own pace. The only thing in the world that responded to her urgency was her own heart, and she had long ago stopped asking much of it.

So she wound the clockwork slowly, listening to the brass teeth take up the weight, and let the sound fill the tower room the way other people let hymns fill a church.

"Eleven minutes," she said to the lamp, as she said every evening. "You vain old eye."

The lamp, being glass and flame and forty pounds of carefully balanced machinery, said nothing, which was one of the reasons she had kept its company these nine years. Things that talked back had a way of leaving.

Outside the lantern room, October had come down on the harbor like a lid on a pot. The water that morning had been the color of unpolished pewter, and by afternoon it had gone darker still, and the gulls had done that thing they do before weather — not crying so much as complaining, a low administrative grumble, as though filing grievances with the sky. Orsen had read the signs the way she read everything: quickly, without sentiment, and twice.

Storm season. The third blow of it was out there somewhere past the point, gathering its opinions.

She climbed the last steps to the gallery rail and stood a moment with the wind worrying the hem of her coat. From up here the harbor looked small enough to hold in one hand — the curve of it, the breakwater, the fishing fleet riding at anchor like spoons left in a bowl. Beyond it, the town: a smear of lamplight beginning along the front street, smoke standing straight up from chimneys until the wind bent each column sideways, all in the same direction, like a congregation leaning toward something they couldn't name.

And beyond the town, nothing. Water, and the edge of the world, or near enough.

Nine years ago she had come up here with a husband and no idea how to run a light. Six years ago she had come up here alone with no idea how to run a life. The light had taught her both times, patiently, the way large slow things teach: *this is how you hold the weight; this is how you let it fall; this is how you do it again tomorrow.*

She went back inside, checked the wick, trimmed the wick, and cursed the wick, because that too was ritual, and a keeper who abandoned her rituals might as well be a fisherman's widow with a matchbox.

*Which,* she thought, trimming anyway, *is precisely what I am. The lamp simply hasn't noticed.*

* * *

The house attached to the lighthouse by a low stone passage had three rooms downstairs and two up, and it was furnished in a style Orsen privately called Early Widow. There was one good chair, positioned where it could see the door and the sea at once, because she had never decided which of them she expected more. There was a table scarred with rings from mugs Niklas had refused to use coasters under, and she had never sanded the rings out, because grief keeps strange relics and a coaster ring is as good as a locket if you're poor.

There were books, mostly, and charts, and a shelf of logbooks going back thirty years in three different hands — the keeper before her, then Niklas, then hers. She sometimes took down the oldest volumes just to touch the handwriting of a man dead before she ever saw this coast. It comforted her in a way she couldn't explain to anyone in town, which was fine, because explaining things to anyone in town was not among her talents.

Margit Halden at the harbor office said Orsen Delacroix was "not unfriendly, only finished," and Margit meant it kindly, and Orsen had laughed harder at that than she'd laughed at anything in a year, because Margit was exactly right. A finished thing. A completed sentence. She had assumed the period at the end of herself had been set down six years ago, ink dry, book closed.

It is astonishing, the length of time a person can go on believing a book is closed simply because she has stopped turning pages.

* * *

Supper that night was barley soup with a rind of salt pork, eaten in the good chair with the logbook open on her knee, because even meals were conducted in duplicate: one for the body, one for the record.

*Oct. 9th. Wind SW freshening. Glass falling since noon. Trimmed wick, wound works, cleaned lens. Gulls restless. Expect the third blow within two days.*

She paused over the page, pen lifted, and considered writing what she had actually thought when she'd seen the barometer that afternoon — *expect trouble, and not only of the meteorological kind* — but the logbook was no place for premonitions. Premonitions belonged in letters never sent, and she had gotten out of the habit of those.

Still. There had been a boat in the harbor yesterday that shouldn't have been. A revenue cutter, gray and neat as a closed fist, riding at anchor with no cargo visible and no business she'd heard announced. Its officers had come ashore in pairs and walked the front street in their clean coats, looking at the fishing boats the way an auctioneer looks at furniture.

And this morning, half the foreign crews had not put out at all. She'd watched through the glass — the long telescope, not the great lens; she was curious, not sacrilegious — and seen men mending nets that didn't need mending, working slowly, glancing seaward whenever the cutter's launch crossed the harbor mouth.

Orsen Delacroix held no brief for politics. Politics happened in towns, and she lived at the exact distance from town where opinions cost more than they earned. But she knew what frightened men looked like. She had been married to a fisherman for three years; she had watched whole crews wear that face when the season turned bad and the money ran thin. Fear moved through harbors the way weather did, and it had settled on this one like fog.

*Not my fog,* she told herself, and wrote instead: *Supply boat due Thursday, winds permitting. Order lamp oil, flour, tea. Do not forget the tea, you forget it every quarter and you regret it every quarter.*

That last line she wrote and then, after a moment, left in. A logbook ought to know its keeper.

* * *

The storm arrived first as a rumor, then as a fact, then as a personality.

By the second evening it had worked itself up from a blow into a proper gale, and the tower sang its old songs — the moan in the stairwell, the rattle of the gallery door, the deep drum of the sea against the rock below, so steady it became less a sound than a pulse. Orsen had made her peace with all of it. A person can grow fond even of a thing that frightens her, provided it frightens her honestly. The storm never pretended to be anything but what it was. That was more than could be said for most visitors.

She kept the light burning through the worst of it, four hours on watch, two hours down, the rotation of a woman whose body told the hour better than any clock. At the turn of the middle watch she stood in the lantern room while the rain went horizontal and the great lens turned its slow circles, throwing its beam out across the chaos, sweeping, returning, sweeping, patient as forgiveness.

And it was there, in the small hours, wrapped in Niklas's old coat over two of her own, that she saw the boat.

Not the cutter. Not the fishing fleet. A small craft, dark, running before the storm with no light showing — which no honest vessel does in a gale, because honest vessels want very much to be seen. It came around the point badly, shipping water, the kind of handling you get from either a dying helmsman or a desperate one, and it drove past the harbor mouth without turning in and vanished eastward into the murk between the point and the far shoals.

Where no sane pilot would go. Where the water over the bar was too thin for anything but a gull's reflection.

Orsen stood at the glass for a long time after it disappeared, watching the empty stretch where it had been, and the beam swept overhead, and the wind leaned on the windows, and she felt — though she would not write this in any logbook, then or ever — the particular cold that comes of seeing something you were not meant to see.

*Someone running,* she thought. *In this. At night. Unlit.*

Then, because she was fifty-one years old and practical to the marrow: *Well. Whoever it is, they've chosen a poor night for it, and it's no affair of mine.* She returned to her stool, wound the works, trimmed the wick, and told the lamp, as she told it every night:

"Burn true, old eye. It's all we're asked to do."

But the words sat differently in her mouth than usual, and the rest of that watch passed slowly.

* * *

Morning came up gray and chastened, the storm blown out to sea to bother somebody else, the harbor strewn with evidence — weed on the steps, a shattered crate on the rocks, the fishing fleet bobbing like survivors. Orsen slept four hours, ate the heel of a loaf with butter she stretched further than butter deserved, and went out to walk her rock, which was another ritual: surveying the damage, salvaging what the sea had offered, cataloguing what it had taken.

It was on the seaward steps, wedged in the corner where the stone met the railing, that she found the bundle.

Her first thought, absurdly, was driftwood. Her second was that driftwood does not come wrapped in oilcloth tied with line, and her third — arriving all at once, the way true thoughts do — was that the oilcloth was moving.

She crouched. Her knees reported their objections and were ignored.

Inside the oilcloth, soaked, shivering, hollow-eyed, impossibly small, was a boy. Perhaps eight years old. Dark-haired, olive-skinned, with the salt-crusted look of someone who had been at sea rather longer than any child should be, and a cut healing crooked above one eyebrow. He did not cry. He did not speak. He looked up at her with the flat, exhausted wariness of a creature that has decided screaming is a luxury, and his hands — she noticed this especially, afterward, when everything about that morning had blurred except the details — his hands were holding the cloth shut at his throat, knuckles white, as though whatever else in the world might be taken from him, the wrapping would not be.

"Well," said Orsen Delacroix, who had faced down gales, grief, and the Harbor Office over a disputed oil requisition, and found her voice failing her now, of all mornings. "You're no driftwood."

The boy said nothing. His lips were blue.

She gathered him up — he weighed less than the spare lamp glass, she would think later, less than forty pounds of trouble — and carried him inside, and set him by the stove, and began with the plain arithmetic of a body: wet clothes off, blankets on, soup warmed, broth coaxed. He drank what she gave him. He let her tend the cut above his brow. He watched her the entire time with those flat dark eyes, taking her measure, deciding something.

When he was color-warmed and the shaking had eased, when the soup bowl was empty and the stove ticked and the tower above them went on turning its slow bright circles as if the world below were ordinary — only then did she notice what had been tucked into the folds of the oilcloth, pinned under his small white grip all along.

A note. Paper-wrapped, waxed against the sea, dry as a sermon.

Orsen dried her hands. Sat down across from him. Looked at the boy; the boy looked back, and there — beneath the exhaustion, beneath the fear — something almost like anticipation. As if he knew what she was holding. As if he had been carrying it not for himself but for her.

She broke the wax and unfolded the paper, and the handwriting hit her before the words did — that quick, slanting, impatient script, letters leaning into the future like a man walking into weather.

She knew that hand. Nine years gone, and she knew it in a heartbeat, the way you know a voice calling your name from a crowd.

And the words themselves, when her eyes finally agreed to read them, were only three:

*Forget the face.*

Orsen Delacroix, finished woman, completed sentence, keeper of the light on the harbor point, sat very still in her kitchen while the boy watched her and the storm's aftermath dripped from the eaves, and understood — with the terrible clarity that arrives only once or twice in a life, usually at the exact moment when everything is about to change — that Tomas Vey had just called in his debt.

And outside, faint on the wind, carried up from the direction of the harbor, she heard the first foghorn of the season begin to moan — though the fog was still hours away.
