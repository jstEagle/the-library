## 2. Orders Come with the Fog

The fog came in on a Tuesday, the way official things often did—uninvited, gray, and full of self-importance.

Orsen Delacroix felt it before she saw it. There was a particular hush that settled over the water when fog was on its way, a muffled quality to the gulls' complaints, as though the whole harbor had been asked to lower its voice. She had kept the light at Gullwrack Point for eleven years, first beside Henrik and then alone after the sea took him, and in that time she had learned to read the weather the way other women read letters: closely, and with an eye for what was being left unsaid.

She was polishing the lantern glass when the first damp tendrils curled around the gallery rail. This was her morning ritual, and she performed it with the devotion of a woman who had very few rituals left. The lens above her—a great Fresnel apparatus of prisms and brass, shipped from France at ruinous expense—demanded constant attention. Salt crusted everything on Gullwrack Point. Salt in the hinges, salt in the hair, salt in the tea if one was careless about the kettle lid.

"There you are, my beauty," she murmured to the glass, working a chamois cloth along the curved face of the central prism. "You'd think after eleven years they'd have found a glass that cleans itself."

The glass, being glass, said nothing, which Orsen had always considered one of its finer qualities.

From the stairwell below came the clatter of boots—two sets, one heavy and familiar, one lighter and unfamiliar. Orsen's hands stilled on the lens. Visitors to the lighthouse were rare enough in fair weather. Visitors who arrived *with* the fog, escorted by the district's harbormaster, were rarer still.

"Delacroix!" boomed Harald Voss from the stairwell, in the tone of a man who believed that volume compensated for his chronic inability to climb stairs gracefully. "Delacroix! You up there? Don't tell me she's gone and fallen down the stairs again."

"I fell down the stairs once, Harald," Orsen called back, not moving, "in 1889, and I begin to suspect you'll be recounting it at my funeral as though it were a weekly occurrence."

Her voice reached him before her footsteps did. By the time she descended to the watch room, Voss was standing by the stove in his good coat—the gray one with brass buttons that he wore whenever he wished to remind the district he represented the Crown's authority and not merely its paperwork. Beside him waited a young man Orsen did not know: narrow-shouldered, clean-shaven, with the particular pallor of someone who worked indoors and considered weather something that happened to other people.

"Keeper Delacroix," Voss said, with a small bow that managed to be both courteous and condescending, which was quite an achievement. "May I present Mr. Aldous Pemberton, clerk to the District Maritime Authority."

"Mr. Pemberton." Orsen looked him over without pretense of subtlety; she was sixty-one years old and had stopped pretending subtlety somewhere around the turn of the century just past. "You look cold."

"I'm quite well, thank you," said Pemberton, shivering.

"The fog does that to mainlanders." She turned to the stove. "Sit down, both of you. There's coffee, and it's only half salt."

Voss laughed too loudly. Pemberton sat on the edge of a chair as though it might be requisitioned out from under him.

* * *

The coffee was drunk. The pleasantries were endured. And then Pemberton drew a leather folio from inside his coat, and Orsen understood that this visit had nothing to do with lamp oil or wick deliveries.

"The Authority has issued new directives regarding coastal security," Pemberton began, in the flat reciting voice of a man reading words he did not write and did not entirely understand. "In light of recent... tensions... there is concern regarding the movements of foreign nationals in our waters. Fishing vessels operating under foreign flags. Crews of mixed origin. The Authority believes certain individuals may be using legitimate maritime commerce as cover for intelligence gathering against coastal installations."

Orsen set down her cup. "Intelligence gathering."

"Espionage, Keeper," said Voss, unable to bear the euphemism any longer than he could bear silence. He said the word the way a boy says *dragon*: with relish and no belief whatsoever. But his eyes, Orsen noticed, were not enjoying themselves at all.

"They mean the fishermen," she said.

"They mean," Pemberton corrected gently, "certain fishermen. Names are being compiled. The Authority requests—and here I must emphasize, *requires*—the cooperation of all coastal personnel. As keeper of Gullwrack Point, you occupy a position of considerable strategic importance. You see every vessel that passes this harbor mouth."

"I see lights and hulls," Orsen said. "At night I see very little else. That's rather the nature of fog."

"You'll be provided with a register," Pemberton continued, undeterred, producing from the folio a ledger bound in green canvas, thick as a Bible and considerably less inspiring. "You will record each vessel passing the point: name, flag, description of crew where observable, time of passage. Weekly reports to the district office. And should you observe anything—" he paused delicately, "—*irregular*, you are bound to report it."

"To whom?"

"To me," said Voss. His voice had lost its performance quality. "Orsen. It's orders. They come down from the capital; nobody at my level voted for them."

"And what happens," Orsen asked, looking at neither of them but at the fog pressing against the windows like a great pale animal, "to the men whose names end up in these compilations?"

Pemberton's expression performed a small administrative maneuver, shifting from clerk to diplomat. "Questioning. Documentation review. In cases where documentation proves... insufficient, detention pending deportation."

"Detention where?"

"That information isn't distributed at my level."

"No," Orsen agreed mildly. "It never is."

She rose, took the green ledger, and weighed it in her hand. It was heavier than it needed to be. Most instruments of bureaucracy were.

"You'll want me to sign something," she said.

"A receipt, yes. And an oath of acknowledgment."

"Then I'll sign the receipt. I keep honest records, Mr. Pemberton—I've kept them for eleven years and no one has ever caught a lie in them. What I *see* from this tower, I will write in your book. But I'm a keeper of lights, not a keeper of men. If a boat passes in the fog and I cannot tell a Danish hull from a Norwegian one, I will not invent the difference to fill your columns."

Voss winced. Pemberton blinked, visibly recalibrating; he had clearly been told the famous widow Delacroix was a cooperative sort, quiet and dutiful, the kind of woman who made clerks' lives easy.

"The Authority," he said carefully, "appreciates candor, madam. But I must inform you that cooperation is not optional. A keeper who fails to report irregularities would be considered..." he consulted the folio, "...negligent in her duties."

"My duties," Orsen said, "are written in my commission. They concern the light. The fog signal. The welfare of anyone wrecked within reach of my ropes." She set the ledger on the table between them with a soft, final thump. "Show me where in my commission it says I am to be a spy, and I'll sign whatever page it's written on."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above them the great clockwork of the lens rotated with a faint, patient whir, indifferent to the affairs of men.

It was Voss who broke the silence, and he did it quietly, which frightened Orsen more than anything else he might have done. He walked her to the door, away from Pemberton's hearing, and stood a moment with his hand on the frame.

"Harald," she said, "you've known me twenty years. Say what you came to say."

He looked out at the white wall of fog swallowing the harbor. "They started with the names last week. Fishermen mostly—Danes, a couple of Finns, some born here but with foreign fathers. Men who've fished these waters longer than the Authority has existed." He lowered his voice further. "There's a list posted at the customs house. Half the harbor's gone quiet since. Men don't greet each other anymore. They count each other."

"Counting," Orsen said. "Everyone's become an accountant."

"There's talk of a magistrate coming. Special commission. Wide powers." Voss rubbed his jaw, and Orsen saw that he had not slept. "Orsen—be careful. Keep your head down. Write your reports dull. Duller than dull. The safest thing on this coast this winter is to be boring."

"I've been boring for eleven years. It's nice to be praised for it finally."

But her joke landed on stone, and Voss left it there. At the door he paused once more.

"One more thing. The Vey brothers' boat was flagged in the second compilation. Tomas Vey."

Orsen felt something shift in her chest, an old plank settling under weight. "Tomas Vey has fished this harbor since before I kept this light. He pulled my husband out of the water in '86 when the *Merrilea* went down. If Tomas Vey is a spy, then so is the church bell, and both of us should hang."

"I know," Voss said simply. "That's why I told you. Be boring, Orsen. Please."

* * *

They left with the fog still holding, their footsteps fading down the rock path until the mist closed over the sound entirely. Orsen stood in the doorway a long while, listening to the foghorn take up its low, patient complaint, and thought about lists, and ledgers, and the peculiar human talent for turning neighbors into entries.

She thought about Tomas Vey.

It had been seven years since she'd seen him—since the funeral, that was, when he'd stood at the back of the church in his one good coat, hat in his enormous hands, and wept without shame for a man who wasn't his brother but should have been. Henrik had called Tomas the best man on the coast, and Henrik had not been generous with superlatives. *If I ever owe my life to anyone,* Henrik used to say, laughing, *thank God it was a debt to Tomas Vey, because that man will never call it in.*

Henrik had been wrong about that, or imprecise. Tomas had never called in the debt. But he had, once, in a letter years ago, written a line Orsen had never forgotten: *A debt like ours isn't owed between people, Orsen. It's owed forward.*

She wondered, watching the fog eat the horizon, what it meant to owe a thing forward, and whether the bill ever came due in fog like this, on mornings like this, when the world went gray and men began counting each other.

She climbed back to the lantern room and polished glass until her arms ached, because the light must burn regardless, and because polishing glass required no decisions about anything else.

It was nearly dusk when she saw it.

Not through the fog—the fog had lifted toward evening into a thin, sour drizzle—but through the telescope, swept along the shore road below the point out of pure habit. A cart. An ordinary fish-cart, horse tired, standing where carts had no business standing, at the bend where the cliff path forked toward the cove.

And then a figure, small—much too small to be out in this weather, much too small to be walking alone—detaching itself from the cart and beginning, hesitantly, to climb the path toward the lighthouse.

Orsen lowered the telescope. Her heart, which had survived storms, widowhood, and forty years of coastal winters, conducted a brief and uncharacteristic rebellion against her ribs.

Someone was coming up her rock. Someone small. Someone, she suspected, who had not come by choice.

"Well," she said aloud to the empty lantern room, to the beautiful indifferent glass, to the fog rolling back in off the sea. "So much for being boring."

She put on her heavy shawl, lit the storm lamp, and went down to open the door before the knocking came.
