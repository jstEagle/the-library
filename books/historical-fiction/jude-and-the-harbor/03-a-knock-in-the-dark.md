## 3. A Knock in the Dark

The fog came in off the water that evening like a guest who intended to stay the winter.

Orsen Delacroix had watched it roll across the harbor mouth a hundred times before and it never once arrived politely. It did not drift. It *arrived*, shouldering its way past the point, swallowing the buoy bell, erasing the far headland so thoroughly that a person might believe the world ended ten yards from her door if she were the believing sort. She was not. Forty-one years of living on this rock had taught her that the world did not end; it merely became inconvenient.

She trimmed the wick at dusk, as she had done for eleven years, first beside Henrik and then alone after the sea took him. The lamp took the flame with its usual small sigh, and the great lens gathered it up and threw it out across the water in one long patient arm of light. She patted the brass housing the way another woman might pat a horse's neck.

"There," she said. "Do your work. I'll do mine."

Her work, that evening, was bread. She had learned long ago that a keeper who did not keep herself fed kept nobody, and so Thursday nights were bread nights, and the oven in the little stone kitchen behind the tower made the whole house smell of something human in a place where everything else smelled of salt and oil and, in storm season, worry.

The worry was new. Eleven years of keeping the Gullvåg Point light and she had never worried about anything larger than weather — and now there were gunboats in the harbor, and men with lists, and a magistrate named Brandt whose handshake felt like being frisked. The coastal authorities had decided, sometime in September, that the empire's troubles could be solved by arresting every foreign sailor within forty miles, and they were going about it with the enthusiasm of men who had found a job suited to their talents.

Orsen had opinions about this. She kept them mostly to herself, which was a discipline, and mostly failed, which was a character.

* * *

She was pulling the loaf — too soon, she knew, but patience had never been her strongest virtue — when she heard the dog.

This was remarkable for two reasons. First, because she had no dog. Second, because the dog appeared to be barking at her front door, which meant either that the fog had grown inventive or that someone was standing on her doorstep trying to be quiet and failing at it badly.

She set the loaf down, listened, and heard beneath the imagined bark a real sound: a small, rapid knocking. Not the confident rap of the supply boat's pilot or the two-thump signal of a fisherman needing the light verified against his charts. This was the kind of knocking a person does when they are afraid to knock and more afraid not to.

Orsen took down the lantern from its hook by the door, lifted the bar, and opened up.

The night fell into her doorway in the shape of a boy.

He was perhaps nine years old, though it was hard to tell, because he was soaked to the skin and shaking so hard his outlines wavered like something seen through the wrong side of glass. His hair was dark and plastered flat to his skull. He wore an oilskin coat three sizes too large with the sleeves rolled back four times, and he was barefoot, and between knocks his fists had been tucked under his arms for warmth, so that the knocking had come out arrhythmic and apologetic, like a stutter given a fist.

He looked up at her. His lips were going blue.

"I'm told—" he started, and then his teeth chattered over the rest of it.

Orsen did not waste time on questions asked of boys who couldn't answer them. She hauled him inside by the collar of the enormous coat, kicked the door shut behind him, and put the bar back down — a thing she had never done in eleven years, and the sound of it settling into its bracket made a small cold thought settle somewhere behind her ribs as well.

"Kitchen," she said. "Move."

He moved. He left a trail of seawater across her clean floor and she found she could not summon one grain of anger about it.

* * *

The kitchen was warm and smelled of slightly ruined bread, and Orsen set about the business of thawing a child the way she set about all tasks: methodically, with muttered commentary.

"Boots," she said, then looked at his feet. "Well. Nothing to take off, that simplifies matters." She fetched the blanket from Henrik's old chair — the good wool one she never used because using it would have been a concession to the fact that some chairs stay empty — and wrapped him in it, all but his face. Then she sat him directly in front of the stove and put a tin mug of warmed milk in his hands and folded his fingers around it.

"Drink. Slowly. If you gulp it you'll lose it on my floor and then we'll both be sorry."

He drank slowly. He was an obedient boy, or too frightened to be otherwise, and she watched the color climb back into his face by degrees, from gray to pale to something approaching the territory of pink.

While he thawed, she thawed too — enough to actually look at him.

He had a face built for trouble and trying to look innocent about it: a narrow chin, eyebrows that sat at a permanent angle of skeptical inquiry, and dark eyes that catalogued everything in the room while pretending to look only at the milk. He counted things, she noticed. His gaze ticked from the stove to the shelf to the window to the lamp-hook and back, quick little movements, like a man taking inventory. Or like a boy memorizing exits.

"You walked here," she said. It wasn't a question. There was no boat tied at her landing stage; she'd have heard the hull knock the pilings.

He nodded.

"From where?"

A shrug, very small inside the large blanket.

"From *where*, boy. The village is four miles. The fishing camps are six, and the road between here and them is under a foot of water tonight." She crouched to bring her eyes level with his. "Nobody walks four miles barefoot in a November squall for the pleasure of my company. Somebody sent you."

His jaw worked. "I'm supposed to give you a note."

"Are you."

"It's wet," he warned, with the gravity of a courier delivering terrible news about the war.

"It would be."

He dug under the oilskin, under the sweater beneath it, and produced a piece of paper folded into a square small enough to hide in a palm. It was, as promised, wet — the edges translucent, the fold soft as cloth. But the innermost corner had been protected, tucked the way careful hands tuck things, and when she unfolded it by lamplight most of the message survived:

*Keeper Delacroix —*

*Forget the face. Keep the boy. You know what is owed.*

*— T.V.*

Orsen read it twice.

Then a third time, though the words did not change, and neither did the hand that had written them — a slanted, impatient scrawl, letters leaning forward like men hurrying into wind, the T crossed with a single fierce stroke. She knew that handwriting. She had known it on the envelope of a letter fifteen years ago, and on the ledger of the *Vasa Marie*, and once — the time that mattered — scratched into the margin of a harbor chart that Henrik had brought home rolled up and silent, along with a story he told badly and never stopped telling.

Tomas Vey.

The name arrived in her chest and sat down heavily, the way old debts do.

* * *

The boy watched her read. She could feel him watching, the way you feel weather changing.

"Who gave you this?" she asked, without looking up.

"Tomas." Then, quickly, as if the name had escaped without permission: "I shouldn't say. He said don't say."

"Well, you've said. No harm done — I already knew." She turned the note over. Blank. "Tomas Vey. Fisherman. Big man, laughs too loud, owes me—" she stopped herself, recalculated, went on evenly, "—rather, I owe *him*. Where is he now?"

The boy's face did something complicated. Fear and loyalty wrestled briefly behind his eyes, and fear won on points.

"He said forget the face," the boy recited, clearly repeating a lesson drilled into him. "Keep the boy. You know what is owed."

"Yes, I can read, thank you." She said it gently enough that he didn't flinch, but firmly enough that his shoulders came down half an inch. "I'm asking different questions than the ones on the paper. That's generally how conversation works. You'll get used to it, living among people."

That startled a ghost of a smile out of him. Good, she thought. A boy who could still smile, soaked and hunted and delivered to a stranger's door in the dark, was a boy worth the trouble he was about to cause her.

"Name?" she asked.

A pause. Then: "Jude."

"Just Jude?"

Another pause, longer, freighted. "Just Jude."

"Mm." She rose and busied herself at the cupboard, giving him privacy the way you give people privacy — by pretending to look elsewhere. "Well, Just Jude. There's soup. It's fish soup, because this is a lighthouse and not a palace, and the fish was swimming this morning, which is more than can be said for most things. Eat it, then sleep, and tomorrow you and I are going to have a long conversation in which you tell me everything, including the parts you've been told not to."

"He said you'd say that," the boy admitted.

"Did he." She ladled soup into a bowl and did not let her hands shake, because there was no sense alarming the child before breakfast. "And what else did Tomas say I'd say?"

Jude took the bowl in both hands. Steam climbed past his face. When he spoke, he had the careful, reverent tone of a boy quoting scripture, or instructions for surviving.

"He said, 'She'll grumble, but she'll do it. Her husband was the best man I ever knew, and she's the better half of him.'"

The kitchen was suddenly very quiet. The stove ticked. Outside, the fog pressed its face against the windows, and far out in the dark the light swung its long arm across the water, around, and back again, patient as a heart.

Orsen turned to the window so the boy wouldn't see what her face was doing, and busied herself with nothing, and let the moment pass.

"Eat your soup," she said, when she trusted her voice.

* * *

He ate like a small storm — efficiently, entirely, mopping the bowl with the heel of the bread she'd salvaged — and then, mid-chew, the exhaustion caught up with him the way exhaustion does, all at once, between one bite and the next. His eyes drifted shut. His head tipped toward the table. He jerked awake, alarmed, ate two more spoonfuls with tremendous determination, and went down again.

Orsen watched this performance with her chin in her hand and found, to her surprise, that she was smiling.

Henrik would have liked this boy. That thought arrived uninvited and stayed anyway, the way thoughts of Henrik did — no longer a knife, these days, more an ache that had learned manners. Henrik, who had never met a stranger and never met a rule he considered binding if it conflicted with feeding somebody. Henrik, who had stood on this same kitchen floor fifteen years ago, dripping harbor water, holding a chart with Tomas Vey's furious handwriting in the margin, and said, *Ors, the man pulled me out from under a capsize with a boathook and a prayer. Whatever he ever asks of us, the answer is yes.*

She had promised. She had promised on the worst night of her life, standing in this room with her husband's cold hands in hers, that any debt owed to Tomas Vey was hers now to pay.

She had assumed, in the way people assume about debts, that she would at least get to choose the currency.

She looked at the sleeping boy — the blanket risen and falling with his breath, one hand still curled loosely around the empty spoon — and then at the note lying by the lamp, and then out the dark window toward the harbor, where somewhere beyond the fog a man was hiding, or running, or already taken, and had entrusted her with the single most dangerous object in the district: nine years old, barefoot, and asleep in her kitchen.

"Forget the face," she murmured. What face? Whose?

The boy stirred and muttered something in his sleep. She leaned close. It might have been *mama.* It might have been *Maren.* She couldn't be sure.

Orsen banked the stove, hung the blanket over the chair back, and carried him up the tower stairs to the bunk room, where he weighed almost nothing and gripped her collar in his sleep like a boy who had learned not to let go of things.

On the landing below the lamp room, she paused. The great lens turned above her, steady and blind and faithful, throwing its light out over a sea full of fog and gunboats and one missing fisherman, and Orsen Delacroix stood in the glow of it with a sleeping child on her shoulder and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the top of stairs in the middle of the night, that her life had just changed course.

Whatever the morning brought — the magistrate, the supply boat, the questions she didn't yet know to ask — it would find her already committed.

She looked down at the note, still clutched forgotten in her free hand, and her eyes caught on the signature one last time. *T.V.* Slanted, hurried, familiar as her own heartbeat.

But it was the date written small in the corner — in ink that hadn't smelted in the rain, ink fresher than the boy's journey could allow — that made her stop breathing.

The note had not been written tonight.

It had been written three days ago — the day before Tomas Vey disappeared.
