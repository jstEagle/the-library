## 4. What the Note Did Not Say

The note sat on Orsen Delacroix's table between the bread knife and the oil-stained ledger, and it weighed more than either.

*Forget the face.*

Three words. Nine letters of intent and none of explanation. She had read it eleven times since carrying the boy upstairs — twelve now, she supposed, counting the glance she stole at it while pretending to reach for the salt — and each reading yielded exactly as much sense as the last, which is to say none.

She turned it over again. The paper was good stock, too good for a fisherman's pocket, torn from something larger. A ledger page, perhaps, or a letter. The edge showed a faint ghost of previous writing pressed through from the sheet above it, mirror-backward and unreadable, like a rumor of a message.

But the handwriting. That was the thing that would not let her sit down.

Orsen had spent thirty years reading hands. Lighthouse keepers learn to read everything: weather, water, the mood of gulls, the way a wick takes the flame. But handwriting especially — logbooks, supply manifests, official correspondence with its stiff bureaucratic loops. She knew the coast surveyor's cramped leftward slant. She knew the harbormaster's pompous flourishes, drawn as though each capital letter expected to be knighted.

And she knew this hand. She would have known it blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea.

Tomas Vey wrote the way he mended nets: quick, economical, every stroke doing double duty. The *g*s were open at the bottom, unfinished, as if he couldn't be bothered to complete anything that wasn't going to catch fish. The *t*s crossed in impatient slashes. And there, in the final word — *face* — the small habit she'd noticed once, years ago, on a scrap he'd sent up with her husband's returned coat: the *c* hooked backward at the end, curling toward itself like a man protecting something in his fist.

No one else in the world wrote a *c* like that. No one else in the world, as far as Orsen knew, was still alive to write anything at all.

"Tomas Vey," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to the storm rattling the eastern pane, "you impossible, drowned, resurrected fool."

The storm did not answer. Storms rarely did, and when they did, one regretted asking.

She put the kettle on because her hands required employment or they would begin making decisions without her. This was a known hazard. Orsen's hands had opinions. They had signed her name to a lighthouse keeper's contract at nineteen when her voice had failed her entirely; they had buried her husband's watch in his coffin when every sensible instinct said sell it, sell it, you'll need the money; they had, seven years ago, gripped the harbor rail while a boat went over in the chop and a stranger named Tomas Vey dove after Henrik Delacroix and hauled him back by the collar like a wet cat.

She owed that man a debt. Not the vague, polite kind — the kind with teeth. She had stood on the dock with Henrik coughing half the harbor out of his lungs and said, *Anything, Tomas. Anything you ever need, it's yours.* And Tomas, wringing seawater from his cap, had laughed and said, *Then stay alive, old woman. That's payment enough.*

He had not collected. He had not needed to. Until tonight, apparently, he had decided to collect in the strangest currency ever devised: a boy, a note, and an instruction that was no instruction at all.

*Forget the face.*

Whose face? The boy's? Impossible — the child was asleep upstairs with a face like an open book dropped in the sea, all its pages showing. Some third party's? A mother's? A father's?

And why would forgetting be safer than knowing?

The kettle sang. Orsen poured the water over the leaves and did not pour the tea, but stood with the pot in both hands, letting the warmth work into her knuckles, and let herself think the thought she had been keeping in the far room of her mind with the door shut:

The roundups had started three weeks ago.

Everyone knew it, though nobody said it above a whisper, and the whispers themselves had grown careful. Foreign sailors first — the Lascar crews off the tramp steamers, the Danes, the men who spoke their own languages on the docks where anyone might be listening. Then the suspicion spread outward like spilled lamp oil, greasy and indiscriminate, until a Swede's accent could get a man questioned and a Norwegian's silence could get him detained. The magistrate, a damp little man named Kessler with the eyes of an accountant auditing the world, had posted notices in the harbor office: ALL PERSONS OF FOREIGN BIRTH RESIDING WITHIN THE DISTRICT SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR EXAMINATION. PERSONS HARBOURING UNREGISTERED ALIENS SHALL BE PROSECUTED TO THE FULL EXTENT.

*Harbouring.* Orsen had always disliked the word. It made shelter sound like a crime, which, apparently, it now was.

And here was the arithmetic, then, laid out plain on the table beside the note: she was an employee of the coastal authority. Her wage came from the government whose magistrate was sweeping the waterfront with a net. The boy asleep under her roof had appeared in the company of a foreign-born fisherman — if the handwriting belonged to whom she believed — a fisherman who had vanished from the harbor two months ago, about the same time the notices went up, and whose absence everyone had politely attributed to the sea.

Two plus two. Even a lighthouse keeper, raised on arithmetic of a very simple sort — *keep the light, mind the clockwork, don't drown* — could do this sum.

If Tomas Vey was hiding from Kessler's examinations, then the boy in her guest bed was contraband. And she, Orsen Delacroix, keeper of the light at Harbor Point these twenty-two years, was being asked — no, not asked; *entrusted* — to become a criminal.

The tea had gone cold. She poured it out anyway and drank it anyway, because waste offended her more than bitterness.

* * *

Upstairs, the boy had kicked most of the blankets onto the floor, which struck Orsen as a promising sign of recovery. He slept the way children sleep in stories and almost never in life: deeply, entirely, one arm flung out as though mid-throw toward something just beyond the mattress. His hair had dried into a brown thicket. In sleep, the tension had gone out of his face, and what remained was younger than she'd guessed — nine, perhaps ten. Young enough that someone should be worrying about him besides a stranger.

She righted the blankets without waking him and lowered herself into the chair by the window, which creaked a warning she ignored. The lamp on the sill threw its small steady circle across the room. Out beyond the glass, the great light turned and turned, sweeping the water every eight seconds, and the rain came down the panes in long silver ropes.

She studied his face by lamplight, because the note told her to forget it, and Orsen Delacroix had been contrary since birth.

It was a harbor face, she decided. Broad and open, a nose already broken once and healed slightly sideways — how does a child of nine break his nose? — and a small pale scar through the left eyebrow like a dash of punctuation. Freckles across the nose, the sort that came out in summer. Nothing remarkable, and yet the whole arrangement of it tugged at some drawer in her memory that refused to open all the way. She had seen this face before, or a version of it. An older version. A younger version. Something.

*Forget the face.*

"I forget nothing," she informed him softly. "It's a defect. My late husband complained of it for thirty years."

The boy slept on. Somewhere below, the foghorn spoke its long low syllable into the dark, and Orsen settled deeper into the chair and let memory take her where it had been trying to go all evening: to a dock, fourteen years ago, before the debt, before the widowing. To a night when Tomas Vey had come up to the lighthouse uninvited, the way he did two or three times a year, carrying mackerel and gossip in equal quantities.

Henrik had liked him enormously. This had always mildly annoyed Orsen, who found Tomas exhausting — a man who filled every silence whether or not the silence wanted filling. But Henrik laughed differently around him, looser, younger, so she fed them both and endured the noise.

That particular visit, Tomas had brought his wife.

Orsen remembered her only in fragments now, the way one remembers weather: a thin woman with black hair going silver early, a foreign melody to her consonants — Danish? Frisian? something northern and soft-edged. She had sat very straight at Orsen's table and spoken perhaps twenty words all evening, but her eyes had moved constantly, taking inventory of the room, the lamps, the exits, the sea. A quiet woman built like a held breath.

And she had been pregnant. Heavily, unmistakably, with the particular tired dignity of a woman in her final month who has stopped discussing it.

Henrik had fussed over her — he fussed beautifully, Henrik, it was one of his three talents — and offered her the good chair and the best of the bread, and the woman had accepted both with a nod that managed gratitude and apology at once. Tomas had talked enough for four people. At some point he'd said, hand resting briefly on his wife's shoulder, "Spring, God willing. A sailor wants a son born in fair weather."

Orsen remembered the wife's face at that. Not smiling. Watching the window, where the light swept past every eight seconds, and following it — sweep, sweep, sweep — with her eyes, like a cat tracking something no one else could see.

They had never come back. Word, a year later, drifting down the waterfront: the *Katrine* lost with all hands off the shoals, Tomas Vey among them. Orsen had worn her black armband for a month and felt foolish wearing it, since grief for a near-stranger seemed ostentatious. Henrik had taken it harder. "Best man I ever worked a deck with," he'd said, more than once, and then gone quiet in the specific way of men who are doing arithmetic of the soul.

A boy born in spring, God willing, fourteen years ago. The arithmetic said no — the child downstairs was too young by four years. Unless there had been another. Unless the spring baby had been followed by a second, later, unannounced, in fair weather or foul.

Unless the face she was instructed to forget was the face of a man she had watched eat bread at her own table, and the boy was his son, and the father was alive and hunted or dead and hunted still, and either way the child was the kind of evidence magistrates love best: small, portable, and unable to testify.

Orsen looked at the sleeping boy and understood, finally, the shape of the thing Tomas had done. He hadn't asked her to shelter a fugitive. He had asked her to forget a face — which meant the face mattered, which meant the boy's parentage was the dangerous part, which meant somewhere in her house was a child whose existence could hang a man, and she was expected to keep him as though he were nobody's anybody at all.

"Well," she said to the empty room. "That's tidy."

It was not tidy. It was a catastrophe wearing the borrowed coat of tidiness, and she knew it, and she was already, appallingly, deciding.

Because here was the truth she kept circling: the promise. *Anything you ever need.* Spoken on a dock with her husband alive because of the man across from her, sworn in front of God and three witnessing fishermen, and promises like that do not expire, do not gather interest or penalties, they simply wait — patient as the sea — for collection.

Tomas Vey had collected. He had walked his debt to her door in the body of a frightened boy and paid it over with three words.

Her wage came from the authority. Her promise came from before the authority, from underneath it, from the older law of the dock and the deck, which held that you do not leave a child on a doorstep in a rising storm, and you pay what you owe even if the payment ruins you.

Orsen got up from the chair, quietly, and went down to the kitchen, and took from the drawer beneath the ledger a fresh page, and dipped her pen, and began — because she was a keeper, and keepers log things — a list.

*Item,* she wrote. *One boy, unnamed, age uncertain, arrived by unknown boat during gale. Currently asleep. Eats like a gull.*

*Item. One note, three words, handwriting identified. Contents insufficient. Author presumed dead. Presumption now suspended pending further insolence.*

She paused, pen hovering. Then, in smaller script:

*Item. One promise, aged fourteen years, called due.*

She looked at the list. It looked back. Neither of them was comforted.

There was a knock at the door.

Orsen went absolutely still. It was past two in the morning. The supply boat was not due for days; the fishermen did not cross the point in this weather; the magistrate's men certainly did not climb lighthouse rocks at night unless they intended ruin. The knock came again — twice, soft, deliberate. The knock of someone who did not wish to be heard by anyone but her.

She blew out the kitchen lamp, took down the fire iron, and crossed the room on silent feet, heart hammering out a rhythm entirely unlike her light's steady eight seconds.

Through the door, low, in a voice roughened by salt and disuse and years:

"Keeper. It's blowing up worse than they're saying. And I've one more favor to ask before the fog comes in."

Not Tomas. Too young. Too afraid.

But he knew her. And he knew about the fog coming — the fog that hadn't started yet.

Orsen put her hand on the latch.
