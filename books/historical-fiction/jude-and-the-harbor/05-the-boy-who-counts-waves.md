## 5. The Boy Who Counts Waves

By the third morning, Orsen Delacroix had learned three things about the boy sleeping in her storeroom.

First: he ate like a gull, quick and wary, one eye always on the horizon of the plate as though someone might take it from him. Second: he could gut a mackerel faster than she could, which was frankly humiliating, since she'd been doing it for thirty years. And third — the strangest thing, the thing she kept turning over like a smooth stone in her pocket — he counted.

Not aloud. Not constantly. But she'd catch him at the north window of the lamp room, lips moving, fingers twitching against his thigh, watching the swells come in. She'd timed him once, discreetly, kettle in hand. He went to eleven, started over, went to eleven again. Eleven waves, then rest. Eleven more.

"He's counting the sets," she said to the cat, who had opinions but no answers. "Fisherman's habit." Though no fisherman she'd ever met had been nine years old.

She said nothing about it to Jude. There were questions you fed a boy slowly, like broth through a cracked lip, and there were questions that came out all at once and drowned everything. She had buried a husband. She knew the arithmetic of grief. You did not rush it.

* * *

The storm season had settled into its rhythm by then — three days of gray malice, two days of lying calm, repeat until spring or the end of the world, whichever arrived first. On the calm days Orsen worked. The lens wanted polishing whether the government appreciated it or not, and the fog bell's mechanism had developed a hitch in its swing that she addressed with oil, a file, and language her mother would have boxed her ears for.

Jude helped. That was the trouble, really. He *helped*. He hauled coal up the spiral stairs without being asked, small shoulders set like a man three times his size. He wiped down the glass with the chamois in long careful strokes, never leaving a smear. When she sent him down for the spare wick, he brought back the spare wick *and* the trimmer *and* the little tin of matches she'd been looking for since August, because he had apparently memorized where everything in the lighthouse lived within two days of arriving.

"You're going to put me out of a post," she told him.

He looked up from the wick he was trimming, alarmed, and she realized too late that jokes about losing things landed differently on a boy who had already lost everything visible.

"I'm not going anywhere," she said, gentler. "Neither are you. Eat your bread."

They ate lunch in the lamp room when the weather permitted, which meant they sat on the iron floor among the great glass prisms with their heels hanging over the stairwell, sharing bread and cheese and pickled onions while the light — unlit in daylight — threw little rainbow ghosts across their knees. It was, Orsen reflected, the first time in six years she'd eaten a meal facing someone instead of the sea. She hadn't realized how much she'd minded the arrangement until it changed.

"Eleven," Jude said suddenly, around a mouthful of cheese. He was watching the water.

"What's that?"

"Waves. Before the big ones." He swallowed. "You watch. They come in sevens mostly, here. But this week it's elevens. Bigger set after."

Orsen looked at the harbor, then at the boy, then back at the harbor. He was right. She'd known it instinctively — every keeper knew it in her bones, the rhythm of the sets — but she'd never once thought to *count* it. To make the knowledge sit still and hold still and be useful.

"How do you know that?"

Jude shrugged the shrug of a boy who has been told he imagines things. "Papa taught me. He said if you can count the waves you're never lost. You just wait for your number."

"Papa," Orsen said carefully, and let it hang there like bait on a line.

But Jude's mouth closed like a mussel at low tide, and he went back to his bread, and she reeled in the empty hook without a splash. *Later*, she told herself. Everything about this boy was later. Later she'd ask about the note. Later she'd ask why Tomas Vey — who had never in his life done anything so reckless as father a child, or so she'd believed — had written *forget the face* in his crabbed left-slanting hand. Later she'd ask what face, whose face, forgotten how.

For now there was bread, and light, and eleven waves between the storms.

* * *

It rained on the fourth day, a fine needling rain that made the whole point of land feel like the inside of a washed bottle. Orsen declared it an indoor day, which in a lighthouse meant the same as every other day except with more mending, and set Jude to sorting the spare bolts of canvas by weight while she attacked a sock that had been daring her to give up since June.

He sorted badly. He sorted badly on purpose, she suspected, because sorting badly meant she'd come look, and her coming to look meant conversation, and conversation — she was learning — was the thing he rationed himself like a starving man rations bread. Give him silence and he'd work all day. Ask him a question straight on and he folded up like a wet map.

So she didn't ask. She talked instead. This was a trick she'd learned from her sister Maren, who could extract a life story from a turnip given twenty minutes and no competition.

"That sock," Orsen said, holding it up, "was Henrik's. He wore it the winter we courted and swore it was lucky. I have never in my life seen luck come out of a sock, but I keep mending it, so perhaps the joke is on me."

Jude looked at the sock. "That's not a sock. That's a boat with a hole in it."

Orsen laughed before she could stop herself — a real laugh, rusty, startling the cat off the table. "A boat with a hole in it. Well. Henrik would have agreed with you, God rest him, which is why he bought new socks and left me this one to fight." She pulled the needle through. "He was a fisherman, my Henrik. Did Tomas tell you that?"

The name landed soft, but it landed. Jude's hands stilled on the canvas. "Tomas isn't—" He stopped. Started again. "Tomas is just someone."

"Just someone," Orsen agreed mildly. "Just someones are the best kind. My husband was a just-someone. So am I. We're the backbone of the entire coast." She bit her thread. "Tomas saved his life once, you know. Out past the shoal, in weather like this. Pulled him out of the water when his boat went over. Never let me pay him for it. Said the debt was his pleasure, and debts of pleasure don't get paid back, only passed along."

Jude had gone very still. Not frightened-still. Listening-still. A boy drinking in details of a man he clearly knew, filed under a name he clearly wasn't supposed to say.

"So I owe Tomas Vey a great deal," Orsen went on, conversational, eyes on her needlework. "More than a boy in a storeroom, even. But I don't mind saying — and you can tell him I said it, next time a just-someone rows out here in the dark — that he might have written more than four words on that paper. Four words and no instructions is very like Tomas. He once gave me half a fish for my birthday with no explanation, and when I asked what it was for, he said, 'It's half a fish.'"

The corner of Jude's mouth twitched. It fought. It lost. He laughed — a short, surprised bark of a laugh, as if it had escaped custody — and then pressed his hand over his mouth like he could shove it back in.

"There now," Orsen said, satisfied, and returned to her sock. "Half a fish."

* * *

The counting revealed itself fully on the fifth night, and it broke her heart in the specific way she was beginning to understand this child intended to break it, slowly and without meaning to.

The storm came up from the southeast around dusk, a proper one, with rain that hit the glass sideways and a wind that made the tower hum like a struck bell. Orsen lit the lamp early. Jude insisted on helping with the full ritual — carrying the matches, standing witness, reciting the steps back to her as she performed them, which she allowed because his voice steadied on procedure in a way it didn't on anything else.

Then the watch began, and the storm did what storms do, and somewhere past midnight the boy woke screaming.

She heard him from the foot of the stairs and took them two at a time, which at fifty-eight years old was an act of faith rather than sense. The storeroom was dark. He was sitting up in the cot, not thrashing — worse than thrashing, he was rigid, hands gripping the blanket, eyes open and seeing something that was not the storeroom, not the lighthouse, not anything she could reach.

"One," he gasped. "Two. Three—"

"Jude."

"—four, five—"

"*Jude.*" She sat on the edge of the cot, slow, the way you'd approach a bird. "Count with me, love. Slow ones. Watch my hand." She raised her fist and opened one finger at a time, unhurried, deliberate. "One. Two. Three."

His eyes fixed on her hand. His breathing ragged against the count. "Four," he whispered. "Five. Six." Down to ten, and then he stopped, and then he shook, and then — for the first time since the night he arrived — he cried properly, ugly and gulping, the crying of a child who has been saving it up in a place with no room for saving.

Orsen held him. She was not a natural holder of children; her arms had gone out of practice and felt like borrowed equipment. But she held him anyway, and rocked a little, and after a long time he spoke into her shoulder, words sanded down to almost nothing.

"The boat went over. Papa said count the waves. He said, *count to eleven and then swim toward the number.* And I counted, and I got to the beach, and he didn't."

The lamp above them turned. The storm shoved at the tower. Orsen closed her eyes and held on tighter.

"He didn't count fast enough," Jude said. "Or the eleventh wave was too big. I've been counting ever since. If I count right, maybe—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "I know it's stupid."

"It is not stupid," Orsen said, with a fierceness that surprised them both. "It is the least stupid thing I have ever heard. You counted your way onto a beach, Jude. Most grown men couldn't count their way out of a rowboat." She pushed the damp hair off his forehead. "Your papa taught you well."

"He taught me other things too. Things I'm not—"

"Supposed to say." She nodded. "I know. I know, love. Here's what I know about that: you don't have to say anything you don't want to say. Not to the magistrate, not to the authorities, not to anyone. Not even to me. But whatever it is — whoever it is — it doesn't change what happens in this house. In this house you eat at my table and sleep in that cot and help me polish glass, and that's the whole of the law here. My law. Keeper's law. Very strict. First offense is punishable by extra pudding."

He hiccupped. Half a laugh, half a sob. "What's the second offense?"

"Second offense is *more* pudding. I run a merciless establishment."

He fell asleep eventually, the storm talking itself hoarse outside, his breath evening out against her arm. She stayed until her legs went numb, thinking about a man in the water saying *count to eleven*, thinking about Tomas Vey writing *forget the face* and meaning, presumably, *keep him safe*, and thinking — with a cold sliver of clarity sliding in under all the warmth — that a boy who could read waves like scripture, whose papa taught him things he wasn't supposed to say, was exactly the sort of boy the coastal authorities had started dragging into magistrate's offices this season.

And that the supply boat was due Thursday.

* * *

Thursday came gray and flat, the sea lying to itself like something recovering from a bad night. Orsen stood at the rail of the gallery with her spyglass, watching the supply boat wallow in from the south, and Jude stood beside her on a crate he pretended not to need.

"Who comes on it?" he asked. Not *what comes* — he'd already asked about flour and oil and wicks. *Who.*

"Petty officer Halden usually. He counts my kerosene twice and inspects my logbook like it owes him money." She lowered the glass. "Sometimes the magistrate sends someone along. Depends on his mood. His mood depends on the newspapers, and the newspapers this month are full of foreign spies under every bed on the coast."

Jude said nothing. His knuckles were white on the rail.

"Now hear me," Orsen said, and turned to face him fully, and saw him square up to receive orders like a sailor, which he was, in whatever unspoken way he was. "If anyone asks, you're my nephew from up the coast. Your mother's widowed, she's ill, she sent you to me for the winter. Your name is Jude Eriksen." Her own name, handed over like a coat in cold weather. "You count waves because any sensible person on this coast counts waves. You smile when spoken to, you say 'yes ma'am' and 'no ma'am,' and you do *not* mention boats going over, notes, Tomas, or anything with a face worth forgetting. Can you do that?"

"Nephew," Jude repeated. He tried the word out the way you test thin ice. Then something moved across his face — quick, private, almost like relief, as though *nephew* was a door he'd been waiting outside of for a long time. "Yes ma'am."

"Attaboy." She squeezed his shoulder once, hard, because tenderness would have undone them both. "Now go look busy with a bucket. Nothing says 'legitimate resident' like a boy with a bucket."

He went. She raised the glass again and watched the supply boat come on, and found her stomach doing something it hadn't done in years.

Because there were two figures on the deck, not one. Halden in his petty officer's cap, checking his list.

And beside him, tall and dry under an umbrella he plainly resented needing, in a coat she recognized because she'd buried its owner's twin brother wearing one just like it —

the magistrate himself.
