## 6. The Supply Boat

The supply boat came on the ninth day of the fog, which meant Orsen had nine days to forget she was harboring a criminal and only one morning to remember how to lie about it.

She had not lied well in twenty years. Widowhood had pared her down to a woman of few words, most of them spoken to lamps and gulls and the sea itself, none of whom could catch her in a falsehood. But the *Petrel*, old Brekke's flat-bottomed tub of a supply boat, would bring flour and lamp oil and — if the harbor gossip traveled as it always did — questions. And standing behind Orsen in the kitchen doorway, scrubbed pink and wearing a sweater that hung off him like rigging, was a boy who did not officially exist.

"He'll ask my name," Jude said. His voice was steady, but his hands were working at the cuff of that sweater, rolling and unrolling it like he was trying to wind up something inside himself.

"Then we give him one," Orsen said, testing the bread on the sill for coolness. "Names are cheap. It's faces people remember."

"Mine especially."

She looked at him then — the sharp little chin, the dark eyes too big for his face, the whole architecture of him so obviously *Tomas Vey* rebuilt in miniature that it was a wonder anyone had ever needed a note at all. Forget the face. As if such a thing were possible.

"You'll be my sister's grandson," Orsen said. "Come from upcoast to help me through the winter. Hermsdal. That family breeds tall children with bad manners; you'll fit right in."

"I don't have bad manners."

"Not until you need them." She handed him the bread knife, handle first, an office of great trust. "Cut eight slices, not seven, because I always eat one while nobody's watching, and if you count wrong I'll know you've been counting mine instead of yours."

Something happened to Jude's mouth then that took her a moment to identify, because it had been so rare since the night he arrived. He smiled. Just barely — the way ice smiles when it starts to break — but it was there, and it made him look six years younger than the age he'd claimed, which she suspected was twelve and knew better than to say aloud.

Outside, the fog had thinned to a gray gauze through which the *Petrel*'s lantern swam like a slow fish. Orsen heard the clank of her bell buoy, the slap of water against pilings, and beneath it all the engine cough that meant Brekke was fighting the tide again, as he had fought it for thirty years and lost to it for thirty years, arriving always forty minutes late and forty minutes grumbling.

She pulled on her coat and gave the boy her last instructions the way she gave all her instructions: as though they were remarks about the weather.

"There's dried cod in the barrel by the stairs if you get hungry. Don't touch the lamp room — I'll know. And if anyone comes up those stairs before I do, you're a boy eating bread in a kitchen, nothing more. Boys eating bread have been doing it since the Flood. Nobody arrests a boy eating bread."

"What if they ask what I'm doing here?"

"Eating bread," Orsen said. "It has the advantage of being true. Lies go hard on the stomach, but truth with a slice of cod under it goes down easy."

She went down the spiral stair into the fog, and behind her, faintly, she heard the whisper of the knife through bread, careful and even, eight slices exactly, and thought: *good lad*.

* * *

Brekke came up the rock path with two crates on his shoulder and a third balanced on his head in the manner of a man who had long ago decided gravity owed him money and intended to collect.

"Widow Delacroix." He set the crates down and shook her hand with the damp enthusiasm of a landed haddock. "You're looking well. Fog suits you. Most people it makes miserable, but you it makes mysterious."

"Fog makes everyone miserable, Brekke. Some of us just have practice hiding it. Where's my oil?"

"In the boat. Where's my coffee?" He peered past her toward the tower. "And where's the boy?"

Orsen felt the question land in her chest like a dropped anchor. Nine days of quiet, and the gossip had come ahead of the flour after all.

"What boy?" she said, and heard immediately that it was the wrong answer, delivered too fast and too flat, the verbal equivalent of a door slammed.

"The one the pilot boat talked about." Brekke settled onto her doorstep uninvited, producing his pipe with the ceremony of a bishop producing a relic. "Half the harbor's talking about it. Magistrate's launch put a fellow ashore at Halden's wharf two nights back, asking after strangers. Somebody saw a boy on your point. A light moving where no light ought to be, they said." He tamped his tobacco, unhurried, watching her through the smoke of his own making. "I told them what I tell everybody. Old Delacroix sees things in the fog. Sees her husband, some nights, waving from the water. Sees ships that aren't there. The authority men wrote it down very seriously and got back in their launch, and I dare say they're still writing."

The relief went through Orsen like warm liquor, followed immediately by the suspicion that warm liquor deserves.

"You covered for me," she said slowly. "Why?"

Brekke looked offended. "Because it's a good story. I've been telling it for years and nobody ever listened before. You've given my lies respectability at last, Widow. I should be thanking you."

"Brekke."

"And," he admitted, the bluster dropping away like a coat shrugged off, "because Tomas Vey pulled my brother out of the water in '87 when every other boat had gone home, and because whoever left that boy on your doorstep wanted him found by you and nobody else, and I can add two and two even if the magistrate cannot." He struck a match. The flame lit his face from below, turning all its weathered creases into a kind of map. "But Widow — and I say this as your friend and your grocer, which in a place like this amounts to nearly the same office — you want to be more careful than a slammed door. Careful is boring. Boring doesn't get reported."

Orsen sat down beside him on the step, which surprised them both. From here she could see the whole gray sweep of the harbor, the fishing boats riding low, the magistrate's launch tied up at the far quay like a wasp that had landed and hadn't yet decided whether to sting.

"They're rounding up foreign sailors," she said. "That's what the orders say. Foreign sailors, suspected spies. He's a child, Brekke. Whatever they suspect, he isn't it."

"Children make fine spies. Cheap to feed, quick to climb, and nobody suspects them." Brekke said this mildly, around his pipe, the way he might report the price of coal. "Which is why somebody hid him with you, I expect. Tomas Vey, if the handwriting was his — and I'd bet my boat it was, the man writes like a spider drowned in the inkwell." He puffed twice. "He's in trouble, then. Real trouble. The kind that comes for everyone near it."

"Then he shouldn't have sent the boy to me."

"No," Brekke agreed comfortably. "He should have sent him to me. I'm nobody. Being nobody is the safest address on the coast." He rose, joints crackling like green wood, and hoisted the oil crate. "Come along, Widow. I'll carry the heavy things up, drink your coffee, meet your nephew, and be gone before noon. And on the way back down I'll compose a fresh story about you, something duller. A woman alone with her lamps. Nothing to see."

They climbed together, and at the top of the stair the kitchen door stood open, warm light spilling out, and the smell of fresh bread rolled down to meet them like an apology from another century. Jude stood at the table slicing cod with tremendous concentration, and when Brekke filled the doorway the boy did not bolt, did not freeze — he simply looked up with the polite wariness of any child meeting a stranger, and said, "Morning," and went back to his knife.

Brekke studied him for a long moment over Orsen's shoulder.

"Well now," he said softly, for her ears alone. "Forget the face, was it? Whoever wrote that note must have known the magistrate was blind."

* * *

They ate breakfast the three of them, which was one more than the table had been built for and exactly the number it had secretly wanted. Brekke told the story of the time he'd shipped a crate of live hens marked as glassware and the whole harbor had spent a week listening to the lighthouse tender cluck. Jude laughed — actually laughed, once, before catching himself and folding the sound away like contraband — and Orsen found herself pouring a third cup of coffee without having decided to, and watching steam rise from it while two other people filled her kitchen with noise.

When Brekke stood to go, he paused at the door and became, briefly, entirely serious. It sat strangely on him, like a borrowed hat.

"There's a man aboard the *Petrel* today I didn't invite," he said. "Came down the quay while I was loading, asked could he ride out to the point, look at the light, official business, very polite. I couldn't refuse him without being interesting." He tapped the doorframe twice, an old sailor's habit, half blessing, half warning. "So he's sitting in my boat right now, and he'll be up those stairs within five minutes of my leaving. He'll ask you routine questions and write down your answers, and he'll seem like nothing at all, because that's what he's paid to seem like."

Jude was already moving — not panicking, just moving, quiet as water finding its level, toward the pantry door.

"Under the flour sacks," Orsen murmured. "Breathe through your nose."

"That won't be necessary," Brekke said, and stopped them both with one raised finger. "Pantry's the first place anyone looks, second only to the bed. No — sit him at the table. Give him a pencil. Tell your nephew he's helping you keep the log." He grinned, and it was the grin of a man handing over a loaded weapon. "Nobody in the history of officialdom has ever looked twice at a clerk. We're furniture. I know; I used to be one."

* * *

The man from the magistrate came up the path ten minutes later, and he was exactly as advertised: gray coat, gray manner, a notebook bound in gray cloth, everything about him designed to slide out of memory before he'd finished shaking your hand. He introduced himself as Mr. Renn, assistant to the harbor magistrate, and admired the view, and admired the tower, and admired the bread, in each case with the sincerity of a man reading admiration off a card.

"Routine matters, Keeper Delacroix. We're compiling a census of persons resident on the coast. Names, ages, occupations. Nothing burdensome."

"Resident persons," Orsen repeated, setting his coffee before him. "You'll want the lamp, then. She's the longest-serving resident. Forty-one years. Never missed a night."

Renn's pen hovered. For a moment — only a moment — something human crossed his face, the flicker of a man deciding whether he was permitted to find that funny. He wrote it down.

At the table, Jude bent over the logbook with a pencil stub, copying figures in a careful schoolroom hand, the very portrait of a boy doing sums. Renn glanced at him once.

"Your grandson?"

"Sister's boy. Hermsdal. Upcoast." Orsen refilled his cup without being asked, which was hospitality and also kept her hands busy. "Sent to see me through winter. Tall family, the Hermsdals. Bad manners, but strong backs."

"How long has he been with you?"

The trap was small and neatly laid, and she stepped over it rather than into it. "Longer than the fog and shorter than the winter," she said pleasantly. "Boys lose track of days. So do I. That's what the log's for — you'll find every arrival accounted for there, if the boy's arithmetic holds."

Renn looked at the logbook. Jude, without looking up, turned the page for him obligingly, revealing columns of neat figures — light consumed, oil measured, weather recorded — stretching back weeks. In the margin of the current page, in the same schoolroom hand, someone had drawn a small, careful lighthouse with a cat sitting on top of it.

Renn looked at the cat. He looked at his notebook. He wrote something, closed it, and stood.

"Thank you for your cooperation, Keeper. Routine matters." At the door he paused, and for the first time his voice carried something underneath it, ballast below the waterline. "There was a boy seen on this point some nights ago. Description taken from the witness was vague. Children look alike in fog, we find. Especially to witnesses who aren't sure what they saw." He settled his collar. "The magistrate will be out himself before the storms come. He's less fond of cats."

He went down the stairs. They heard the *Petrel*'s engine cough, fade, dissolve into fog.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Jude pushed back from the table and looked at the page he'd been guarding, and Orsen looked too, and understood what the boy had done. Under the drawing of the lighthouse and the cat, in letters small enough to vanish, he had written across the bottom of the official log:

*He didn't believe the cat either.*

"No," Orsen said quietly. "But he chose to pretend he did, which is worse for him and better for us, and I haven't yet worked out which side of it that puts him on." She took the pencil from Jude's fingers and set it down gently. "Magistrate's coming himself before the storms, boy. That gives us days, not weeks."

Jude looked up at her, and all the steadiness he'd worn all morning finally cracked down the middle.

"Then I should go," he said. "Before he comes. Before it costs you—"

"Sit down," Orsen said, "and eat your bread. Eight slices were cut and I've only had mine. Nobody leaves this kitchen over half a loaf." But her eyes went past him, through the window, to the gray harbor where the magistrate's launch sat waiting, and she found she was already counting boats — which ones sailed soon, which ones sailed far, and which one, before the final storm of the season, might be persuaded to carry a boy all the way out of her keeping and beyond the law's long arm.

The fog pressed against the glass. Somewhere out on the water, the bell buoy rang, patient as a promise.

Three days later, the supply boat would come back early — and Brekke would not be the one steering it.
