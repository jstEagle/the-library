## 7. Salt and Silence

The trick of it, Orsen had learned in her forty-nine years, was that grief and housework kept the same hours. Both arrived before dawn, both refused to be hurried, and both left a body too tired to think by evening if you gave them honest labor.

So she gave the boy labor.

"You'll scrub the lamp glass," she told him on the first full morning, setting a cloth and a tin of whiting powder on the table between them like cards dealt face up. "Not because I need the help. Because a person with busy hands has no time for trembling, and you've been trembling for two days."

Jude looked at the cloth as though it might bite. "I don't know how."

"Nobody knows how. That's what instruction is for." She demonstrated: damp cloth, pinch of powder, small circles, the patience of it. "Watch the way the glass comes clear. There's no feeling in the world quite like watching something come clear under your own hands. Remember that, because most things in life come cloudy first."

He was a small thing, all elbows and wariness, seven years old at most though he claimed eight with the conviction of a customs officer defending a forged manifest. His hair stood up in cowlicks that no water would persuade. He had arrived in Tomas Vey's borrowed oilskins three sizes too large, and Orsen had spent part of the previous evening letting out the seams of a shirt that had belonged — she did not tell him this — to no one, because she had bought it years ago off a peddler's cart against a future that had never asked for it.

Now he scrubbed. Badly at first, then adequately, then with a fierce concentration that made her turn away so he wouldn't catch her smiling at it.

"There," he said finally, holding the pane up to the gray morning light. A single streak ran through the middle like a river seen from a great height.

"That's a start."

"It's wrong."

"It's a start," she repeated. "Wrong and started are cousins. Given a week they'll be living in the same house."

* * *

The rules came next, because rules were how Orsen Delacroix had survived widowhood, winter, and the particular loneliness of a woman married to the sea's worst employee.

She laid them out that evening over barley soup, counting them off on her fingers the way she counted the fog bell's intervals.

"One. You do not go down to the cove. Not for shells, not for gulls, not because the tide left something interesting on the sand. The cove is where boats land and eyes look, and we want neither. Two. If anyone comes to the door — anyone — you are my nephew from the east coast, sent to convalesce after lung fever. Your name is Jonas. Can you say it?"

"Jonas," he said, in a voice so flat it could have leveled a road.

"With more conviction. A sick child wouldn't mumble it like an apology. Again."

"Jonas."

"Better. Three." She hesitated here, because the third rule was the one she hated. "You do not speak of Tomas Vey. Not to me, not to yourself when you're alone, not in your sleep if you can manage it. The less that name exists in this house, the safer it stays in the world."

Something closed in his face then, the way a window shuts against wind. "But he's coming back for me."

"I'm sure he intends to."

"He *promised.*"

"And men who make promises to children are either liars or brave, and Tomas Vey never struck me as a liar." She ladled him more soup before he could argue, which was a trick her mother had taught her: feed the argument before it finds its feet. "Eat. Rule four is that you finish your soup."

"What about rule five?"

"There is no rule five yet. Rules arrive when they're needed, like storms."

He considered this with enormous seriousness, chewing. Then: "Rule five should be that you learn my real name back. When it's safe. When I can be Jude again."

Orsen felt that land somewhere beneath her ribs, in the place where promises were filed. "Agreed," she said. "Rule five."

* * *

The days found their shape after that, the way water finds a channel.

Mornings were the lamp: cleaning, polishing, filling the reservoir with the thick sweet-smelling oil, winding the clockwork that turned the lens in its slow patient rotation. Jude took to the machinery with an appetite that surprised them both. He liked the weights best, the great descending iron of them, and the way the whole apparatus breathed and ticked like something alive.

"It's like a heart," he said, ear pressed to the housing.

"Lighthouses are hearts," Orsen said. "That's the whole secret of them. Everything else is brass and paint."

Afternoons, weather permitting — and the weather permitted rarely, that being the nature of storm season — they walked the headland path, always inland, never toward the cove. She taught him plants. Sea thrift and scurvy grass, the difference between gulls that meant nothing and gulls that meant weather. He learned the names hungrily, repeating them under his breath like a man memorizing scripture, and she understood eventually that he wasn't learning botany. He was collecting things. Anchors. Small certain facts to hold onto while everything else floated loose.

Evenings were the light itself, lit at dusk, and here Orsen discovered the boy's strangest habit.

He counted waves.

She noticed it the third night, watching him at the lantern room's seaward window. His lips moved in time with the breakers below the point. *Fourteen, fifteen, sixteen.* Sometimes he lost his place when a big set came through and he'd begin again without complaint, patient as a monk.

"What are you counting toward?" she asked.

He shrugged. "If I get to a hundred, it's been a long time since the last bad thing."

"How many waves in a bad thing?"

"About thirty."

Orsen absorbed this. Somewhere out in the dark, a storm was assembling itself over the open water, and this child measured safety in units of thirty waves. "And tonight?"

"Ninety-one." He didn't look away from the window. "It's a good night."

"Well then," she said, and pulled her chair around so she sat beside him rather than across, close enough that her shoulder could be a wall if he wanted it to be. "Let's see if we can't make it a hundred."

They sat there while the lens turned its slow circles above them, throwing its beam out over the black water and gathering it back again, out and back, out and back, and when wave ninety-eight came Jude gripped her sleeve without looking, and when one hundred came he let out a breath like a man surfacing.

"A hundred," he whispered.

"So it has," said Orsen, and did not trust her voice to say more.

* * *

*

The trouble with silence, she wrote in the logbook that night, is that it must be practiced aloud or it turns on you.

She had begun keeping a second log — not the official record with its wind speeds and lamp hours, but a private one, in pencil, in a cipher of her own devising that consisted mostly of domestic nonsense. *Nephew improving. Appetite excellent. Glass spotless but streaky. Counting continues.*

It was for Tomas, she supposed, whenever he came. Proof of stewardship. Though some nights, writing it, she suspected it was for herself — proof that the strange new arithmetic of her life balanced, that one lamp plus one boy equaled a household rather than a crime.

Because that was the word for it now. She knew the word. Everyone on the coast knew the word; it hung in the air of every fish market and chapel porch from here to the capital. *Foreigners detained for questioning. Suspicious vessels. Reports of signaling.* The magistrate Halden had been by twice already that season, before the boy, standing in her kitchen with his wet boots and his notebook, asking had she seen any unfamiliar craft, any lights on the water that weren't hers, any strangers passing through.

*I am the stranger now,* she thought, banking the fire. *I am the light on the water that shouldn't be.*

She thought of Tomas Vey in whatever hole or hold he'd vanished into, and of the note in her locked drawer — *forget the face* — in handwriting she'd known at once, having spent a season twenty years ago deciphering it on letters he'd sent her husband, full of fish prices and jokes and, once, a pressed sprig of sea lavender with no explanation. Tomas, who had pulled Erik from the water off Skerry Nose with the storm already eating the boat around them both, and who had never once mentioned it afterward, as if saving a man's life were merely returning something borrowed.

She owed him. The debt had sat quietly in her chest for two decades like a stone in a boot, familiar, almost companionable. Now it had grown legs and walked into her kitchen in the shape of a seven-year-old who counted waves, and the stone had become a heartbeat.

"Rule six," she said to the empty kitchen, and blew out the candle.

* * *

*

On the ninth day the supply boat came, a fortnight early, and Orsen watched it round the point with her heart conducting a brief and unscheduled rehearsal for disaster.

It was only Petersen. Only Petersen, and his boy, and crates of flour and oil and the mail, which was mostly nothing. But she saw now that she would feel this way every time — every sail on the horizon a question mark until proven a comma — and she filed this away as the true cost of sheltering Jude. Not the risk of discovery, exactly. The permanent narrowing of the horizon into a list of suspects.

Petersen unloaded his crates and gossiped and complained about the price of coal, and Jude-as-Jonas stayed in the kitchen as instructed, convalescing loudly enough to be heard coughing if anyone listened, which nobody did. Petersen's boy stared at the closed kitchen door with the frank interest of a twelve-year-old, and Orsen redirected him with a question about his schooling that opened the floodgates of complaint and drowned all curiosity.

"Storm coming," Petersen said at the end, squinting at the western sky, which had gone a peculiar bruised yellow. "Glass is falling like a paid informant. Might be two days, might be four. Big one, they're saying. The kind that rearranges harbors."

"The kind that rearranges harbors doesn't announce itself with yellow skies," said Orsen. "That's just autumn showing off."

"Suit yourself. Oh—" he rummaged in his coat, produced a newspaper folded to the size of a hymnal, "—leftover from town. Nothing in it but misery, but misery passes the time."

She took it, smiled the right smile, waved the right wave, and read it only after the boat was a smudge on the water.

Nothing in it but misery, and half a column of it on page three: the coastal commission's expanded detentions, the listing of harbors under increased scrutiny, and a sentence that made her sit down slowly on the crate of flour:

*Magistrate A. Halden has announced a systematic review of all coastal installations, including lighthouse stations, to commence within the fortnight.*

All coastal installations. Including hers.

She folded the paper along its existing creases, very carefully, and put it in the stove, and watched it curl and blacken, and thought: *he will come with his wet boots and his notebook, and he will want to see every room.*

Then from the kitchen doorway came a small voice, carefully flat, doing its best impression of a boy with nothing on his mind:

"Missus Delacroix? Why did you burn the paper?"

Orsen turned. Jude stood there with the lamp-polishing cloth still in his hand, watching her with those grave dark eyes that missed entirely too much.

"Because it was yesterday's news," she said lightly, "and yesterday's news makes poor reading and worse company." She crossed to him and straightened the collar of the shirt she'd let out for him, an excuse to look at the top of his head instead of his face. "Jonas. How would you feel about learning rule five ahead of schedule?"

"You said rule five was remembering my name."

"It is. But there's a rule six now." She knelt so they were eye to eye, and outside, far off, the first gust of the coming storm moved through the headland grass like a hand through hair. "Rule six is this: whatever happens in the next little while — whoever knocks, however many questions they ask — you and I are going to practice our silences together. And I promise you, on every salt-soaked bone in this body, that when it's finished, there will still be a hundred waves between you and anything bad. Do you believe me?"

Jude studied her the way he studied waves — patiently, thoroughly, counting something invisible.

"Ninety-one," he said at last. "I believe you ninety-one."

From the sea, out past the point, came the low moan of a whistle — a steam whistle, official-sounding, still far off but closing.

Orsen rose and went to the window, and there, dark against the yellowing sky, sat a government cutter making straight for the harbor point, its wake a white accusation across the falling glass.

"Halden," she said quietly. "Two weeks early."

The storm was coming from both directions now.
