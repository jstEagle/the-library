## 8. Halden Returns

The supply boat was three days late, and Orsen had begun to ration her opinions of it.

"He's run aground," she told Jude on the second morning, as they watched the empty horizon from the gallery. "Or married. Either way, we eat the salt cod first and save the flour."

"Is being married worse than running aground?" Jude asked. He had taken to standing beside her with his hands folded behind his back in unconscious imitation, a small boy wearing the posture of an old keeper.

"For the person aground? Considerably."

By the third morning the fog had thinned to rags and the sea lay flat as poured pewter, and out of that emptiness came the *Petrel*, riding low and grumbling, with Halden Brath at her tiller looking like a man who had been arguing with weather for seventy-two hours and lost most of the arguments.

Orsen felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to name. She went down the stair instead of waiting at the top, which was itself a confession, and stood on the landing stage while he brought the boat alongside with the exaggerated care of a man who did not want to admit how tired his hands were.

"You're late," she said.

"The fog was late. I was merely following it." He threw her the bowline before she could reach for it — Halden never handed anything, he always threw it, as if handing implied doubt about your ability to catch — and clambered onto the stage with a creak of oilskins. Up close he looked worse than from distance: gray at the temples where there had been no gray two years ago, a cut healing along his jaw, and eyes that went past her shoulder to the lighthouse before they came back to her face.

"Halden," she said carefully. "You look like a sermon on mortality."

"And you look like the answer to one." But he said it without his usual warmth, and that absence rang louder than any greeting. He turned back to the boat and began hauling crates without asking help, which was also unlike him; Halden's idea of efficiency had always involved directing other people to do the lifting.

Orsen took the second crate anyway. It was heavier than it should have been, and it shifted with a sound of tin against tin, and beneath the tinned goods she saw the corner of something wrapped in oilcloth that had no business on a supply manifest.

"What's all this, then?"

"Government freight." He said it flatly, still not looking at her. "New orders. I'm to deliver them personally."

The word *government* sat between them on the landing stage like a third passenger.

* * *

They climbed to the kitchen because there was nowhere else to put him, and because sending him away would require an explanation, and every explanation Orsen rehearsed on the stairs died somewhere around the word *Jude*.

She had stowed the boy in the lamp room with instructions that were half riddle and half prayer: stay above, count your waves, and if boots sound on the iron stair, you are a shadow and shadows make no noise. Jude had received this with the grave nod of a general accepting a battle plan he privately considered doomed.

Halden sat at her table with his great hands wrapped around a mug of coffee he wasn't drinking, and laid a sealed envelope among the crumbs.

"Read it," he said. "I'm meant to watch while you do."

"That seems unfriendly."

"It is." He almost smiled. Then didn't. "Things have changed on the mainland, Orsen. There's a magistrate now with a commission and a gunboat, and a list of names longer than my arm, and orders about foreign seamen that I won't repeat aloud even in this kitchen."

She broke the seal. The paper inside was dense with bureaucratic copperplate, but the substance was short: registry of all persons landed at or resident near harbor lights; inspection of premises; cooperation with the coastal authority under penalty of dismissal. At the bottom, someone had added a line in different ink — a clerk's afterthought or a warning dressed as one:

*Note: inquiries have been made regarding movements at the Point light.*

She read that line twice. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, faint through the ceiling, a boy's voice was counting — *"forty-one, forty-two"* — so softly it might have been gulls.

Halden was watching her face the way he watched weather coming over the headland.

"Inquiries," Orsen said. "About me?"

"About the light. About landings." He rubbed his jaw, careful of the cut. "There's talk of a fishing boat that put in near here some nights back. Talk of a boy."

The kitchen became very small and very quiet. Orsen set down the letter and made herself pick up the coffee pot instead, made her hands perform their ordinary office, filling Halden's untouched cup though he hadn't asked. Her mother had taught her that hands busy themselves best when the heart is doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic just now was terrible: the distance from this table to the iron stair, the number of steps, whether a seven-year-old could climb down the outside ladder faster than a grown man could follow.

"Halden," she said, "how long have we known each other?"

"Since I was nineteen and you were seventeen and you told me my hat was ridiculous."

"It was ridiculous. You wore it like an apology."

"I wore it because you'd once said you liked the color." He looked at the coffee, then at her, and his eyes were not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "Twenty-six years, Orsen. And in twenty-six years I've never known you to fill a cup that wasn't asked for. So either you're getting soft in your old age, or there's something in this kitchen you don't want me thinking clearly about."

Above them: *"forty-three, forty-four."* Softer now. The boy had heard the change in the room's weather, whatever else he understood.

Orsen sat down across from him. She thought of lying, and knew she couldn't — not to Halden, not well enough, and a bad lie would be worse than any truth. She thought of telling everything, and knew she couldn't do that either, because everything included Tomas Vey, and Tomas was already a name on somebody's list.

"There is a boy," she said.

Halden closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when the wave he's been watching finally breaks over the seawall.

"He was left at my door," she went on, steadily, because if she stopped she might not start again. "Nine days ago. With a note in a hand I knew. His father saved Elias's life once, off the Skerry, and I owed that man a debt I swore on my husband's grave to repay. The magistrate can have my wage, my post, and my liberty if that's the price. But the boy stays fed and warm and unknown until I can get him somewhere safe. That's the whole of it. That's everything."

"It isn't," Halden said quietly. "Whose note?"

She held his gaze. "You know I won't say that."

"I know you won't. That's what frightens me." He leaned back, and the chair complained under him, and for a long moment he studied the rafters as if the answer were written up there in soot. "Orsen, the man they've got doing the rounds — Inspector Rell — he isn't like us. He doesn't drink with the fishermen or owe anybody's husband anything. He counts things. Landings, faces, boats. And there's a rumor he's been given leave to press informants."

"Informants." She tasted the word like brine. "Who on this coast would inform?"

"Anyone frightened enough. Anyone paid enough." Halden turned his mug a slow quarter-circle on the table. "And anyone with an old grudge and new permission to indulge it."

The stove ticked. Above, the counting had stopped entirely, and the silence from the lamp room was louder than the counting had been.

Then, before either of them could speak again, there came the sound of small feet on the iron stair — quick, decided, entirely unlike a shadow — and the kitchen door opened, and Jude walked in.

He had clearly dressed himself in haste: coat buttoned into the wrong holes, one boot unlaced, hair standing up like a startled hedge. He crossed to Orsen's side and planted himself there and looked up at Halden Brath with an expression of ferocious, trembling dignity.

"If you're taking her," he said, "you'll have to take me too, because I'm her nephew, and that's the law."

A silence followed in which several things happened at once. Halden Brath's face, which had spent thirty years learning to keep secrets off it, went through surprise, understanding, and something so much softer than either that Orsen felt her throat close. And Orsen, whose hand had already moved to the boy's shoulder, found herself caught between terror and an absurd, inappropriate urge to laugh — because *nephew*, of all the inventions, delivered with the confidence of a barrister of nine.

"The law," Halden repeated gravely.

"Nephews belong with their aunts." Jude's chin lifted higher. "It's in the Bible."

"I don't recall that particular verse."

"It's in the good part."

Halden looked at the boy for a long moment. Then he looked at Orsen, and something passed between them that had been passing between them for twenty-six years — the old shorthand, the look that said *we will speak of this later and meanwhile I am with you* — except that this time it carried a weight neither of them had ever put into it before.

"Well," Halden said, and reached slowly, deliberately, across the table to take the letter back. He folded it, tucked it into his oilskin breast pocket, and buttoned the flap over it with the air of a man closing a door. "As far as I can report, I inspected the light this morning and found the keeper living alone, as she has these two years, in respectable and melancholy solitude. The boy—" he glanced down, and the corner of his mouth betrayed him— "the boy I never saw. Nephews are notoriously difficult to spot. They hide in the good part of the Bible."

Jude blinked at him. Orsen did not laugh, but it was a near thing, and Halden saw it and seemed, absurdly, pleased.

"You're risking your post," she said quietly.

"I'm risking more than that, and I'd thank you not to itemize it at my age." He rose, joints crackling, and gathered his hat. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"One thing more, Orsen. The supply run next month may be my last for a while — Rell wants his own men doing the circuit, checking manifests himself. If you're going to do anything that needs doing," and here his voice dropped to almost nothing, "do it before then. Because after that, every boat out of this harbor gets counted, and counted twice, and the man counting has never once in his life forgotten a face."

The door closed behind him. His boots went down the iron stair, steady as ever, and out along the landing stage, and presently the *Petrel* drew away from the point, grumbling toward the mainland with its cargo of government freight and government silence.

Orsen stood at the window watching it shrink. Behind her, Jude asked, "Was that true, what he said? About nephews?"

"No," she said. "But it was believed, which on this coast is better."

"And the other thing? About forgetting faces?"

She kept her eyes on the boat. Out on the flat pewter water, the fog was gathering itself again beyond the headland, patient as everything else that was coming for them.

"Then we make sure," she said softly, "that nobody sees yours."

Above them, in the lamp room, the great lens turned and turned, throwing its faithful beam out over water that hid, somewhere beyond the far gray line, a magistrate's gunboat and a man named Rell who had never once forgotten a face — including, Orsen realized with a cold settling in her stomach, quite possibly hers.

For she had seen him before. Years ago, at Elias's funeral. Standing at the back of the church with his hat in his hands, watching not the coffin but the mourners.

Counting.
