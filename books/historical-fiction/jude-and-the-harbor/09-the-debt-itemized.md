## 9. The Debt, Itemized

Orsen had never been a woman who kept ledgers. The lighthouse board kept her ledgers for her — oil consumed, wicks trimmed, hours burned — and she signed her name at the bottom of their columns and thought little of it. Debts of that kind were simple. You owed the government diligence, and the government owed you forty pounds a year, and between the two of you the light got kept.

The other kind of debt — the kind written on no page — she had carried for eleven years without once setting down its terms. But tonight, with the boy asleep in the storage room and Halden's boat due at first light, she found herself doing sums at the kitchen table with a stub of pencil and no paper, tallying against the grain of the wood as though the table itself might hold the figures.

Item one: In the winter of 1882, Tomas Vey had gone into the sea after Eirik.

She had not seen it happen. She had only seen the aftermath — Eirik carried up the harbor steps wrapped in Tomas's coat, blue to the lips but breathing, and Tomas behind him soaked to the bone in water cold enough to kill a man in twenty minutes, wringing out his cap with hands that would not stop shaking. He had laughed about it afterward, in his way. *Your husband fishes like a sack of turnips,* he'd said. *I only retrieved what was mine to mind.* And then he'd eaten half a loaf of her bread and gone home before the fire had even dried him properly, because that was Tomas: he did a thing and then declined to have done it.

Eirik had lived four more years on borrowed time, and Orsen had gotten those years, all of them, every ordinary morning of them. She had tried once to put a price on that. Eirik had caught her counting coins into an envelope and made her stop. *You don't pay a man for a gift,* he said, *you just remember it.* But Eirik was nine years dead now, and remembering felt thinner than paying, and the envelope habit had never quite left her fingers.

Item two: the promise itself. Made on the dock, in the rain, the week of the funeral. Tomas had come to stand beside her — not to speak, because Tomas did not do comfort in words, but simply to be a solid wet shape next to her while the world fell apart — and she had said, because grief makes people formal, *Whatever you ever need of me, Tomas Vey, it is yours. Whatever it costs.*

He had nodded and looked at the water and said, *Mind your stove. It's smoking.*

That was the whole of it. Eleven years, no collection. Until three nights ago, when a boy appeared at her door with a note in a hand she knew better than her own late husband's.

*Forget the face.*

Item three, then, and here the pencil stopped: whatever it cost. Because the cost was no longer abstract. The cost had a name now, and the name was Halden, and Halden would be standing in her kitchen in approximately six hours with a manifest and a set of eyes that missed nothing.

Orsen put down the pencil and picked up her tea, which had gone cold, and drank it anyway, because waste offended her more than cold tea did.

* * *

Jude woke at dawn, which meant he woke the way boys wake — suddenly, loudly, and hungry. Orsen heard him moving in the storage room before she heard the supply boat's engine off the point, and she was upstairs in the lantern room with a rag and a bucket before her own mind caught up with her feet.

*Oil consumed,* she thought, wiping down the lens with the particular tenderness she reserved for glass. *Wicks trimmed. One boy hidden above the ceiling, breath held.*

She had put Jude in the gallery's service closet an hour earlier, with a biscuit, a blanket, and instructions so thorough that he'd begun to yawn halfway through them. *If I knock twice, come down. If I whistle, climb out the seaward window and sit on the gallery rail like you're watching gulls. If I say the word 'weather,' run down the back stairs and into the dune grass and don't stop until the old wreck shed, and stay there until dark.*

*What if I fall?* he'd asked.

*Then fall quietly,* Orsen said, and he had grinned at her, the little wretch, as though fear were a thing that happened to other people.

Now she watched the supply boat nose toward the jetty and saw Halden standing at the bow with his hat squared and his collar buttoned to his chin, which told her everything. Halden dressed for weather like a man dressing for court. He considered the sea a formality.

She went down to meet him.

"Keeper Delacroix." He stepped onto the rock with the exaggerated care of a man who distrusted all surfaces not manufactured. "You're well."

"As well as the fog allows." She did not offer a hand. She never had; he had never seemed to expect one. "You're ahead of schedule."

"The schedule," Halden said, "has been revised." He produced the manifest from inside his coat, unfolded it with ceremony, and held it up — not to read, but the way a clergyman holds a text. "New protocols. All coastal stations to be inspected monthly rather than quarterly. Equipment, stores, logbooks." A pause, measured out like medicine. "And personnel records. Household composition. That last is new."

"Household composition," Orsen repeated. "It's me and the cat."

"You have a cat?"

"He has me. It's a different arrangement."

Something flickered at the corner of Halden's mouth — not a smile, but the place where a smile might have been buried under stricter things. He followed her up to the kitchen, walking the stairs as though auditing them, and sat where he always sat, at the corner of the table nearest the door. She had long ago stopped taking offense at that. Men who wore their caution on the outside were at least honest about it.

"Tea?" she asked.

"Thank you, no. This won't take long." He opened a leather folder. "I'm required to ask certain questions. They aren't personal, Keeper. I want you to understand that."

"They're going to feel personal."

"Yes." He cleared his throat. "Have you had any visitors in the past month?"

"Fog visits this house more often than people do. You've seen the harbor."

"Any visitors," Halden said, with the patience of a man reading terms aloud, "of any age."

And there it was. Not a net thrown wide — a net drawn tight, with a knot in it shaped exactly like a boy. Orsen poured her own tea with a steady hand and let the silence go on one beat longer than politeness allowed.

"Halden," she said, "how long have we known each other?"

"Nine years."

"Nine years. In nine years, how many times have I filed a false entry in my log?"

"None."

"How many times has my lamp failed inspection?"

"Once. The spring of '89. A cracked burner."

"And whose fault was the cracked burner?"

Halden looked at his folder. "The manufacturer's."

"The manufacturer's. So when I tell you something, you may assume it has the same weight as my logs, which is to say the full weight of a woman who has kept a light burning through eleven storm seasons without losing a single ship in her waters." She set down the pot. "Ask me plainly, and I'll answer you plainly, and then we can both get on with our mornings."

He studied her for a moment. She had the uncomfortable sense of being read, the way she read weather — not the visible surface but the pressure underneath it.

"Very well. There is a boy missing. Ten, perhaps eleven years old. Dark-haired. Possibly arrived by fishing boat within the past fortnight. There are men," and here his voice dropped into a register she had not heard from him before, flat and careful, "who believe such a boy may be connected to certain foreign interests, and there are other men — I will be plain with you too, Keeper — who would use any boy found on this coast as proof of whatever they've already decided is true. The difference between those two kinds of men is the difference between an inquiry and a hunt. Do you follow me?"

"I follow you."

"If such a boy were here — if he came to you, if someone left him here — there would be ways of handling it that ended with everyone still alive and dry. And there would be ways that ended otherwise." He closed the folder. "So I am not asking whether you are harboring anyone. I am asking you to tell me if you ever intend to, so that I know how much of the coast I need to walk today."

The kettle ticked on the stove. Above them, through two floors of timber and stone, a boy was sitting in a closet being quiet as a held breath, and Orsen felt the whole weight of the tower balanced on the smallness of her own next word.

"I keep a light," she said. "That's my work, and I've never confused it with anything else. My light doesn't ask where a ship comes from or who owns her cargo. It burns for whoever's on the water. If that's a crime, Halden, arrest the lamp."

Halden stood, tucked the folder away, and settled his hat. At the door he paused, and without turning said: "The spring of '89. You reported the cracked burner yourself, before the inspection, though reporting it cost you a fine you could ill afford. I have wondered for six years why a woman would do that."

"Because a light that lies gets people drowned."

"Yes," said Halden. "That is precisely what I concluded." He stepped out into the grey. "Good morning, Keeper. I'll walk the north beach today. It's a long walk. I imagine it will take most of the day."

She watched him go down the rocks, careful as ever, and stood in the doorway until the fog took his outline, and only then did she realize her hands were trembling — not from fear, she discovered, but from something adjacent to it that she could not yet name. Gratitude, perhaps. Or the vertigo of having been seen through entirely and spared anyway.

* * *

She climbed to the gallery and knocked twice on the closet door, and Jude tumbled out in a rush of blanket and biscuit crumbs, eyes bright with the particular excitement of a boy who has survived his first hiding.

"Did he see the cat?" was the first thing he asked.

"There is no cat."

"That's what makes it good," Jude explained patiently. "A lie with a cat in it falls over. A lie without a cat just stands there looking true." He said it in the tone of a child reciting scripture learned from unreliable priests, and Orsen decided not to ask.

"He walked the north beach," she said. "We have the day. Sit down. I need to talk to you about arithmetic."

Jude frowned. "I can do arithmetic."

"I know you can. This is a special kind." She sat across from him at the kitchen table and turned the pencil over in her fingers. "It's called itemizing. When you owe somebody something very large, you break it into pieces so you can understand it. I owe a man named Tomas Vey your father's life — my husband's life, that is — and I have spent three nights trying to count what that comes to."

"Is it money?"

"It started as money. It became something else. Money is the smallest part of it, which is inconvenient, because money is the part I know how to give." She looked at him — really looked, the way she hadn't let herself before, because looking at the boy's face had become a dangerous occupation since the note. Dark hair, yes. The Vey jaw, unmistakable now that she permitted herself the seeing of it. And the eyes — she had told herself they were only similar, but similarity did not survive honesty. "Jude. What do you remember about the night you were put on the boat?"

His face did the thing faces do when children decide how much truth an adult can carry. He was quiet for a moment, turning his cup in his hands.

"Papa cried," he said finally. "I never saw Papa cry except when Mama's name gets said. He wrote the note at the kitchen table and wouldn't let me read it and his hand was shaking so the letters went crooked. He said—" Jude's voice went carefully flat, a boy imitating his father's imitation of calm. "He said a man keeps a promise even when the keeping costs more than the breaking. And he said you were the only person on this coast who knew what that meant." He looked up. "Was he right?"

Orsen thought of the ledger in her head, all its items tallied: a life pulled from the sea, a promise made in rain, eleven years of interest accruing in silence. She thought of the final line, the one she had avoided writing because writing it made the debt due.

*Forget the face.* But Tomas had known, of course, that she couldn't. He had counted on it.

"Yes," she said. "He was right. And the arithmetic comes out to this: everything I have. Which is less useful than it sounds, because most of what I have is a tower and a lamp, and neither of those fits in a fishing boat."

"What does fit in a fishing boat?"

"One boy," said Orsen, "and one keeper, if it comes to that. Which is why we're going to need help, and why I'm going to spend tomorrow making myself sick talking to the person who can give it." She stood and began clearing the dishes, because motion was easier than the feeling underneath it. "Eat your egg. And Jude — when I say the word 'weather'—"

"Run to the wreck shed," he recited, mouth full. "Stay till dark. Fall quietly."

"Good lad."

Outside, far off along the north beach, a figure in a squared hat was walking slowly through the fog, stopping now and then to examine the sand with great attention — and, she noticed, always seeming to pause a little longer wherever the dune grass grew thickest, as though a careful man might mark, for later use, every place on this coast where something could be hidden.

Orsen drew the curtain, and reached for her boots.
