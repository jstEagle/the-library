## 10. Maren's Kitchen

The path down from the point to Maren Brandt's cottage took forty minutes in good weather and an hour and a half in bad, and Orsen had walked it in every kind of weather the coast could invent. This morning it was neither good nor bad but merely gray — a low, wet sky pressing down on the water like a lid on a pot — and Jude walked beside her with his hands buried in the pockets of the coat she'd cut down for him from one of Eilif's old oilskins, looking as though the coat were the only solid thing in a world made of fog.

"Maren's house has a blue door," Orsen told him, for perhaps the third time since they'd left the lighthouse.

"You said."

"And a garden that's gone wild on one side and tidy on the other, because she can never decide whether she wants order or abundance, so she compromises by having both and pretending it was intentional."

"Is it?"

"Nothing about Maren is intentional. It's all weather." Orsen stepped over the stile. "You'll like her. She talks too much and listens better than anyone I've ever known, which is a rare pairing. And she cooks the way some people pray."

Jude considered this. "Does she pray loud?"

"When the bread burns."

The blue door appeared out of the mist exactly where memory said it would be, though the garden had clearly lost its argument with itself over the summer — the wild side had won several new skirmishes, and something sprawling and yellow-flowered had advanced nearly to the doorstep in open rebellion. Orsen knocked twice, then once more, in the old rhythm.

The door opened on the second extra knock, which meant Maren had been watching for them through the kitchen window for at least an hour and had been forcing herself not to answer immediately, so as not to seem eager. She failed within four seconds, as always.

"Well, finally," Maren said, filling the doorway entirely, which was a considerable achievement given that she stood barely higher than Orsen's shoulder. She was round as a barrel and twice as warm, her gray hair escaping its pins in every direction like something escaping custody. Her eyes went past Orsen immediately to the boy, and Orsen watched her take him in — the thin wrists, the wary stillness, the way he positioned himself half behind Orsen's skirts without seeming to realize he'd done it — and watched, just as quickly, as Maren arranged her face into perfect, unremarkable blandness.

"So," Maren said briskly. "You've brought me a boy. Come in before the fog gets in too, it makes my floors damp and my opinions worse."

* * *

The kitchen was the warmest room on the coast, a fact Maren maintained through stubbornness and an iron stove that hadn't gone cold in nineteen years. There was bread rising under a cloth, a pot of fish stew already murmuring to itself on the hob — of course there was; Maren cooked for imagined guests the way other people kept umbrellas by the door — and dried herbs hanging from every beam until the ceiling seemed to be growing them.

Jude stopped just inside the door and breathed it in. Orsen saw his shoulders come down half an inch.

"Sit," Maren commanded, pointing at the bench by the table. "Not you, Orsen, you'll want to help, and your helping consists of standing in my way and being right about things. Boy. Name?"

"Jude." Then, after a visible hesitation: "Ma'am."

"Oh, none of that. *Ma'am* is for magistrates and midwives, and I've been neither since 1878. You'll call me Maren and you'll eat what I put in front of you and if you don't like it you'll say nothing, because children who criticize my cooking don't get seconds, and everyone likes my cooking eventually." She set a bowl before him, large enough to bathe in. "Except Pastor Ellegaard, who said my stew was 'rustic,' and he got no seconds for six years. Sit up straight. No — don't. Slouch if you want. I only say that because my mother said it and she was wrong about most things."

Jude picked up the spoon. He looked at the stew. Then, very quietly, with the gravity of a boy negotiating a treaty: "I can have all of it?"

Maren's face did something complicated and brief, and then she turned to the stove so her back was to both of them. "You can have all of it, and more after, and bread besides, and if you're still hungry we'll discuss what else is in the pantry, which is considerable, because I bake when I'm worried and I've been worried since Tuesday."

Orsen sat across from the boy and did not look at Maren's back. Some kindnesses were easier witnessed indirectly.

* * *

Jude ate the way small animals eat when they've learned not to trust the supply — steadily, watchfully, one ear cocked for the sound of it being taken away. But somewhere in the middle of the second bowl, the vigilance loosened. He began, haltingly, to answer questions.

He liked herring best of all fish. He could mend a net, sort of, if the holes weren't bad. He could count waves.

"He can what?" Orsen said.

"Count waves," Maren repeated, delighted. "What does that mean, counting waves?"

Jude shrugged into his stew. "On the boat. When it's rough, Papa — " The word caught. He went still for a moment, and both women let him be still. "When the man who owned the boat was scared, he counted waves out loud. How many between the big ones. If it was five or less it was fine. If it got to two you held on." He looked up. "It helps. Counting. It gives the fear a job."

The kitchen was quiet except for the stew.

"That," Maren declared, wiping her hands on her apron with the air of a judge delivering a verdict, "is smarter than anything Pastor Ellegaard ever said from a pulpit, and he charged for those. Fear with a job." She pointed her wooden spoon at Orsen. "You hear that? You should write that down. You write things down, don't you, you keepers are great ones for logs. *Fear with a job.*"

"I heard it," Orsen said. She found she had to clear her throat slightly first. "It's going in the log tonight."

* * *

Afterward, while Jude explored the garden — released like a bird from a cage, though Orsen noted he checked twice that the door stayed where he left it — Maren put the kettle on and the smile off, and the two women stood at the window watching the boy crouch by the yellow flowers and examine something with enormous seriousness.

"Now," Maren said. "Tell me everything, and tell it to me in the right order, because I'm old and my nerves are a ruin."

So Orsen told her. The note. The handwriting. Tomas Vey, and a debt eleven years old, and a promise made over a grave. The magistrate Halden's men in the harbor town, the lists of names, the fishing boats boarded and their crews questioned. A boy with no country on paper and a face someone wanted forgotten.

Maren listened without interrupting, which for her was a physical ordeal, her fingers working the dishcloth like a woman strangling an enemy. When Orsen finished, she was silent for a long moment — three full waves' worth, Orsen thought — and then she said:

"Tomas Vey. The one who pulled Halvar out of the water."

"The same."

"And you owe him."

"I promised him anything. At the graveside. He didn't ask for anything then, and he hasn't asked for anything since, and now there's a boy on my doorstep in his handwriting saying *forget the face.*"

Maren folded the dishcloth. Unfolded it. Folded it again, badly.

"Do you know," she said at last, conversationally, "that when my Niels was conscripted — the second time, the bad time — I went to the harbor master and offered him my grandmother's silver to lose the paperwork. And do you know what he said?" She didn't wait. "He said, *Maren, I can't lose paperwork, I'm a harbor master, losing paperwork is the one thing they check.* And then he lost it anyway, because his wife had birthed half this coast including me, and because some debts are older than governments." She looked up at Orsen, and her eyes, usually so quick and merry, were steady as lead. "This is one of those. I'm telling you so you know it plainly. Whatever comes of this boy, whatever it costs — you came to my kitchen and I fed him and I knew, and that makes me in it. So stop carrying it alone. It makes you walk crooked."

Orsen laughed — unexpectedly, ungracefully, the way one laughs when something heavy shifts. "You've been wanting to say that for twenty years."

"I've been saving it up," Maren agreed. "Like jam."

* * *

They worked out the practicalities over tea, which is to say Maren worked them out and Orsen agreed to most of them. The boy would stay at the cottage during the days when boats might pass on the water below the point — Maren had a legitimate reason for a hired hand, her knees being famously treacherous, and half the village already believed she'd taken on a grandson anyway because Maren couldn't keep a secret longer than a sermon. On fog days he could climb to the lighthouse. Orsen would teach him properly, glass and wick and clockwork, because a boy with duties attracted less notice than a boy with nothing.

"He'll need schooling," Maren said.

"He'll need to not be arrested."

"One leads to the other, in the end. Reading leads to papers, papers lead to legitimacy. Write to your cousin in the city, the one with the connections and the terrible politics. She owes you."

"My cousin's politics got her exiled from two dinner parties."

"Exiled from dinner parties is the safest place to keep a useful person." Maren refilled the cups. Through the window, Jude had moved from examining the flowers to attempting to organize them, pulling weeds with the fierce concentration of a child who has recently learned that usefulness is currency. "Look at him. He's tidying my rebellion. Orsen. He's winning against plants I've fought for years."

"He counts waves," Orsen said. "Weeds are nothing."

And then, because the light was dimming and the kettle had gone quiet and there was no more practical business to hide behind, Maren asked the question neither of them wanted asked, in the sideways manner she used for the truest things:

"Have you thought about the end of this? Not the middle. The end. Boys grow, Orsen. Faces don't stay forgotten. What happens to him?"

Orsen looked out at the garden, at the small figure among the yellow flowers with dirt on his borrowed coat, and found that the answer arrived before she could stop it, whole and inconvenient:

"He stays."

Maren said nothing. She only smiled, slowly, the way sunrise comes — gradually and then all at once — and reached over and squeezed Orsen's wrist hard enough to hurt.

"There," she said thickly. "Now it's said in a kitchen, which is where such things ought to be said. Everything important should happen in kitchens. Weddings, funerals, treason. Tables and bread make people honest."

* * *

It was nearly dark when they walked home, Jude walking ahead a few paces in the way of boys testing the length of their leash, and the fog had lifted enough to show stars in torn patches. He was quieter on the return path, but it was a different quiet — fed, warm, full of Maren's leftovers wrapped in cloth and pressed into his arms like treasure.

At the bend above the harbor path, he stopped.

"Lady Orsen." He never quite managed *just Orsen*, though she'd told him twice. "There's a man down there. By the boats."

She came up beside him and looked. Below, in the last gray light, a figure stood at the edge of the quay where the fishing boats lay moored — a tall figure, hatless, motionless, studying the boats one by one with the patience of a man taking inventory.

Even at this distance, even after eleven years, she knew the set of those shoulders. She had seen them bent over a drowning man once, hauling him back from the dark, and she had promised those shoulders anything in the world.

"Tomas," she breathed.

But even as she said it, the figure turned toward the harbor office instead — toward the lamp burning late in Halden's window — and knocked, and was let in, and the door closed behind him.

Jude tugged her sleeve. "Is that your friend?"

Orsen stood very still in the darkening path, the note's words turning over in her mind like stones in surf — *forget the face* — and wondered, for the first time with a cold weight under her ribs, whether the warning had been meant to protect the boy from strangers.

Or to warn her whom to trust.

"No," she said slowly. "I don't know anymore. Come along — quickly, now. We've letters to write and a light to tend."

And she took his hand, and they climbed.
