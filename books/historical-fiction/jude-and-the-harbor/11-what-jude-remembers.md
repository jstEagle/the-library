## 11. What Jude Remembers

The storm had spent itself by morning, leaving the harbor the color of a rinsed-out sky and the beach strewn with offerings: kelp ropes, a broken oar, one bewildered crab making its determined way back toward the water as though it had merely stepped out to fetch the paper.

Maren's kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and yesterday's bread reheated in a skillet, which Maren insisted was a delicacy and which tasted, Jude thought, like the best thing anyone had ever invented. He sat on the bench by the stove with his feet tucked under him, because the floorboards were cold and because tucking his feet made him feel smaller, and sometimes smaller felt safer.

Orsen sat across from him with her hands wrapped around a chipped mug of coffee she had not drunk any of in a quarter hour. She had that look again — the one where her eyes stayed on him but her thoughts had gone somewhere over the horizon. He was getting used to it. He was getting used to a great many things: a woman who kept a light for ships, a kitchen that wasn't a cabin, a dog named Bristle who had decided sometime in the night that Jude's lap belonged to him now, by right of conquest.

"So," Maren said, sliding another slice of bread onto the skillet where it hissed like something alive. "The fog's lifted, the roads are mud, and nobody official can reach us before noon at the earliest. Which means, little sprat, we have time. And I intend to use it."

"Use it how?" Jude asked, wary. In his experience, adults who said *I intend to use it* were about to make him wash something.

"You're going to tell us what you remember."

He looked at Orsen. She looked back, steady, and did not rescue him, which he understood — somehow, without being told — was its own kind of respect.

"I already told you," he said. "Tomas left me at the lighthouse. There was a note."

"There was a note," Orsen agreed. "And before there was a note, there was a life. A year of it, maybe more. You lived somewhere, ate somewhere, slept somewhere. Somebody taught you your letters — you read that note yourself, didn't you?"

"Some of it. The hard words I guessed."

"Guessed right," Orsen said. "That's somebody's teaching too." She set the untouched coffee down. "Jude. I'm not going to press you. But there may come a day soon when a man with a ledger asks you questions, and it would be better — for you, most of all for you — if you'd already asked them of yourself. So nobody's answer surprises you. Not even yours."

Jue considered this. It was the longest sentence anyone had ever said to him that he'd needed to take apart like a knot, and he found, working at it, that it came undone into something simple: *know your own story before someone tries to hand you a different one.*

"Bristle," he said finally, "is heavy."

"He is," Maren agreed, flipping the bread. "Start there. Work backward."

* * *

So he started there, because it was easier than starting anywhere else, and because Bristle was indeed heavy, and warm, and breathing in that deep doggy rhythm that made thinking feel less like falling.

"We had a boat," he said. "Not a big one. Tomas called it the *Petra*, but I think that was a joke, because it leaked."

"A joke," Orsen repeated softly, and something crossed her face like a bird's shadow.

"Tomas laughs at things that aren't funny," Jude said. "Especially boats. Especially weather. Once the mast cracked and he laughed so hard he had to sit down, and then he fixed it and stopped laughing all at once, like a door closing." He frowned, digging. "We slept aboard sometimes. Sometimes in a house with green shutters. The paint peeled off the shutters in strips, and I peeled more off, and a woman said—" He stopped.

Maren had gone very still at the stove. Orsen had not moved at all.

"What did the woman say?" Orsen asked, and her voice was careful the way ice near the shore is careful.

"That I'd ruin my nails." Jude held up his small hands, inspected them. "They never got ruined. They just got dirty."

"And this woman," Orsen said. "Green shutters. What was her name?"

He knew. He had known it the whole time, the way you know the shape of your own bed in the dark, but saying it felt like handing something over. He looked at Bristle. Bristle looked back with total doggy confidence that whatever happened next would be survivable.

"Mama Lena," he said. "But she wasn't— she wasn't my mama. She said she wasn't, once, when I asked. She said it gentle. She said, *you have a mama, sprat, she's just very far away and loves you across the whole distance.*" He blinked. "I called her Mama Lena anyway. She let me."

The skillet hissed. Maren lifted the bread off it with mechanical care, set it on a plate, and did not slide it toward anyone.

"Lena," Orsen said. Just the name. It landed in the kitchen like a dropped coin and rolled around and around.

"You know her," Jude said. It wasn't a question. Grown-ups were terrible at hiding things; they thought their faces were locked doors and they were actually windows with the curtains open.

"I knew a Lena," Orsen said. "A long time ago." Then, after a pause that seemed to cost her something: "She was married to my brother."

Jude's mouth fell open. Bristle, sensing seismic activity, raised his head.

"I had an uncle?" he said. "Do I have an uncle?"

"Had," Orsen said quietly. "Elias. He drowned eleven years ago, off the point, in the spring squall. Before you were born." Her thumb moved along the rim of her mug, once, twice. "Lena left the coast after. Went south, people said. Went to family. Nobody said anything about a child."

"I'm not her child," Jude said, with the patience of someone correcting a small error. "I told you. I have a mama far away."

"Yes," Orsen said. "You do." And then, so lightly it might have been about the weather: "What was her name? Your mother's."

And here Jude did something he hadn't meant to do, which was go quiet. Because the truth was tangled, and the tangle went like this: he could remember Mama Lena's kitchen, remember Tomas laughing at the leaking boat, remember the smell of tar and the sound of gulls and the exact tune the wind played through the green shutters at night. But his mother — the real one, the far-away one — lived only in fragments. A hand smoothing hair back. A voice singing low, a song about the sea keeping what it loves. A silver thing that caught the light and turned it, spinning, across the cabin ceiling. And a last day, gray and rushed, and Tomas crouching down to his level with his rough hands on Jude's shoulders, saying, *listen to me, sprat, and listen well.*

"Her name is in the song," he said at last. "Sort of. Mama Lena sang it wrong on purpose, to be silly. But I know the right one."

"Sing it," Maren said gently.

"No." His ears went hot. "You'll laugh."

"Sprat, I've been laughed at by fishermen. Nothing you do in this kitchen will crack my top three."

So Jude, feeling ridiculous and doing it anyway, sang the four lines he had. His voice was thin and went flat in the middle and he clutched Bristle tighter through the worst of it:

*Low lies the light where the water's turning,*
*deep keeps the sea what the sea keeps free;*
*sail home, sail home, though the lamp's low burning,*
*Maren waits where the gulls fly free.*

The silence afterward had a strange texture to it, like the air after lightning. Maren had put a hand flat on the table. Orsen was looking at her.

"Well," Maren said, and her voice came out sanded down to almost nothing. "That's an old hauling song. My grandmother's. They sang it in this harbor before either of you was born." She swallowed. "But that third line. That's not the old verse."

"It isn't?" Jude said.

"No." Maren sat down slowly, across from him, and took his hands in both of hers, flour and all. "The old verse says *love waits where the gulls fly free*. Somebody changed it. Changed it to a name." She looked at Orsen. "Somebody changed it to *me*."

* * *

Afterward — after the bread had been eaten and the mugs refilled and Bristle had relocated to the sunniest patch of floor to digest his importance — Jude was sent out to the yard to fetch eggs, mostly, he suspected, so the grown-ups could talk about him in the way grown-ups did, huddled and low, like sailors over a chart.

He didn't mind. The yard was bright and dripping and full of smells washed clean by the storm. The hens regarded him with their usual contempt and surrendered two eggs, warm as fresh secrets.

It was while he was closing the coop gate that the memory came, unasked, the way memories did — sliding up behind him like a wave you hadn't heard building.

The last day. The gray rushed day. He had been carrying his coat, or being carried — no, being carried, on Tomas's shoulders, through rain, and there had been shouting somewhere behind them, men's voices, and Tomas had ducked down an alley between fish sheds and set him down and knelt in the wet, eye to eye.

*Listen to me, sprat, and listen well.* Rain running off Tomas's beard. *If anyone asks, you're mine. Mine and no one else's. Can you hold that in your fist for me? Just for a while. I'll come take it back, I swear it on the Petra.*

*I swear it on the Petra,* Jude whispered now, to the hens, who were unmoved.

And then the other thing. The thing he had packed away so deep he'd nearly forgotten the box it came in. Tomas pressing something cold into his palm that night at the lighthouse door, folding his fingers closed over it, whispering: *keep this where no one looks. Not in your pocket. Never your pocket. Give it to her when she asks you about the sea keeping what it loves.*

*Give it to her.*

Jude stood in the yard with two warm eggs in one hand and his heart going like a bird in a net, because he suddenly understood, with the terrible clarity of eight years old, that *her* was not Mama Lena, and *her* was standing in that kitchen right now, drinking coffee she never touched.

He also understood — this arrived a moment later, quieter, heavier — that he had hidden the thing exactly as instructed, sewn into the lining of his coat where no one looks, and that the coat was currently hanging on a peg by Maren's door, and that a man with a ledger might come asking questions before noon.

He looked at the house. Smoke curled from the chimney, straight up in the windless air, like a finger pointing at nothing, or at everything.

Jude wiped his boots twice on the mat, which he had learned was the custom here, and went inside to give something back.
