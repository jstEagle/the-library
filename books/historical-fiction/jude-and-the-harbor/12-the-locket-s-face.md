## 12. The Locket's Face

The locket had lived in Orsen Delacroix's left-hand drawer for eleven years, beneath a coil of spare wick and a tin of matches gone soft with damp, and in all that time she had opened it perhaps six times. It was not sentiment that kept it there. Sentiment would have kept it on a chain against her breastbone, or buried under the hawthorn where Erik lay. What kept it in the drawer was something closer to arithmetic: it was the last object in the world that connected her to her sister's family, and she had never decided what a person was supposed to *do* with such an object. You could not wear grief like a brooch. You could not throw it into the sea either, though God knew she had stood at the gallery rail some nights weighing it in her palm like a stone.

It was a plain thing, brass gone dull, the size of a sovereign, with a hinge that had to be coaxed open by the thumbnail because the spring had rusted proud. Inside, behind two small panes of clouded glass, were two portraits painted in miniature on ivory: one of Orsen's sister Lise, aged nineteen, on the occasion of her betrothal; and one of the man she'd run off with against every soul's advice in three parishes — a sailor named Vey.

Tomas Vey.

Orsen had known that name for eleven years as a kind of curse and a kind of prayer both, depending on the hour. Curse, because he had taken Lise away to a life of harbors and half-pay and letters that came thinner each year until they stopped altogether. Prayer, because nine years ago, when Erik's boat had gone down off the point in an easterly gale, it was Tomas Vey who had gone out after him in water no sane man would cross, and hauled him over the gunwale blue-lipped but breathing, and rowed him home through sleet with his own hands split open from the oars. Erik had lived four more years after that. Four years Orsen would not otherwise have had. She had told Vey, on the one occasion they had spoken — briefly, awkwardly, on the quay while Erik convalesced — that any debt he cared to name would be honored. He had laughed and said no debt between sailors and keepers, Mrs. Delacroix, only weather. She had not laughed. She had meant it then and she meant it now, and the note in her apron pocket, four words in a hand she had known at once though she hadn't seen its like in a decade, meant it too.

*Forget the face.*

She had spent three days telling herself the handwriting was a coincidence, that many men slanted their f's that way, that grief made pattern-seers of us all. On the fourth morning, with rain ticking against the lantern glass and Jude asleep upstairs in Erik's old clothes rolled twice at the cuffs and waist, she took out the locket.

* * *

She did it at the kitchen table with the lamp trimmed low, which was foolish, because if she was going to learn what she suspected she was going to learn, she ought to have done it in full honest daylight. But some truths are like deep-water fish: they spoil in brightness. So she sat with her back to the dark window and the rain coming down, and she coaxed the hinge with her thumbnail, and the locket sighed open the way it always did, reluctant as an old man rising from a good chair.

Lise looked back at her first. Nineteen forever, dark-haired, with the crooked smile that had gotten her out of trouble and into it in equal measure. Orsen touched the ivory with one fingertip, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child's head.

Then she turned to the second pane, and Tomas Vey looked out at her — young, bearded, eyes set close and quick, with a small scar through his left eyebrow that he'd claimed came from a falling block and everyone else claimed came from a husband.

And Orsen sat very still for a long moment, because the face in the locket and the face of the boy asleep above her head were the same face.

Not similar. Not *a family resemblance*, the way cousins resemble. The same. The same close-set eyes, the same quick geometry of brow and cheekbone, the same stubborn line of jaw — softened in Jude by youth, by his mother's blood, by whatever else the sea had mixed into him, but underneath it, unmistakable, the face of Tomas Vey as surely as if someone had held the locket up to a mirror and let twenty years fall away.

"Well," Orsen said aloud, to nobody, to the rain. "There it is."

Her hands were not entirely steady. She noticed this with a sort of detached irritation, the way one notices a draft. She was a woman who had trimmed a light through eleven seasons of gales, whose hands did not shake on the winding key, whose voice did not shake before magistrates' men. But here, alone, with a dead sister and a living boy and a debt standing in the room like a third person, her hands shook.

*Forget the face.* She had taken it to mean forget Jude's face, forget she'd seen him, deny everything. Now she understood it differently, or feared she did. *The face.* The face in the locket. Forget Tomas Vey, forget the connection, forget that the boy left at your door is the grandson — no. The son.

The son.

She worked it through slowly, the way she worked a sum, twice, three times, because errors in lighthouses cost ships. Lise would be thirty-nine now, if she lived. Jude said he was twelve. Lise had been twenty-seven when the letters stopped. The arithmetic fit with an ugly neatness. And if the letters stopped because Lise died — and children do not post themselves across three hundred miles of coast to the one house in the world where their father's handwriting might be recognized — then the note meant Vey was alive, hunted, and had done the bravest and most desperate thing available to him: he had trusted her.

He had trusted her, and he had told her to forget his face, which was another way of saying *do not come looking, do not ask, do not let the boy tell you anything, because what they do to men they catch is worse than drowning.*

Orsen closed the locket. Opened it again. Looked again, as though the ivory might have reconsidered overnight.

It had not.

* * *

Jude came down mid-morning with his hair doing what it always did — that same uncombable dark thicket, Vey's thicket, she could see that now, she would never *unsee* it — and announced that the rain was a "six" and climbing.

"Six?" Orsen was at the stove, and grateful for the occupation of stirring.

"Six waves." He held up six fingers as evidence. "That's my counting. Little ones don't get counted. Ones that break white past the point get counted. Yesterday was a four. This—" he squinted through the window at the harbor, gray and churning, "—this is a six, maybe a seven if the wind backs around."

"And what happens at ten?"

"Nobody knows." He said it with perfect cheer. "Never got there."

"We'll hope we don't," said Orsen, who privately thought the scale was the finest piece of natural philosophy she'd encountered, and wished she'd thought of it herself during the long watches.

Jude climbed onto his stool — his stool now, apparently, the way the third stair creaking was his creaking and the chipped blue cup was his cup; the boy colonized a place within a day the way barnacles take a hull — and began eating his bread and dripping with the concentration of a stevedore.

Orsen watched him sideways over the rim of her own cup.

She should ask him nothing. That was the instruction. *Forget the face.* Every question she put to the boy was a thread the magistrate's men might follow back through him to Vey, and every answer he gave innocently was rope for his own neck. She knew this. She had rehearsed knowing this.

But he looked up at her with crumbs on his chin and his father's eyes, and she heard herself say, in a voice she tried to make idle and did not quite manage:

"Your mother. What was her name?"

The spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"You don't have to say," Orsen added quickly. "I shouldn't have asked. Eat your—"

"Maren," said Jude. "Her name was Maren."

Not Lise.

The relief and the disappointment arrived together and cancelled each other out into a strange ringing silence, and Orsen stood there holding her cup and understanding, with the particular vertigo of a person who has mislaid a stair in the dark, that she had built her whole chain of reasoning on names and dates — and the name was wrong, so perhaps everything else was wrong too, and the boy was merely some other man's son who happened to wear Vey's face, and—

"Pa called her something else sometimes," Jude was saying, to his bread rather than to her, in the careful voice of a child deciding how much truth a grown-up can carry. "A pet name. He thought I didn't remember. He called her *Liselotte*. Only when he was sad. When he was sad about her he'd sit by the window and say Liselotte, Liselotte, like a bell going."

The cup in Orsen's hand met the table with a click louder than she intended.

"Liselotte," she repeated.

"That's a funny name," said Jude, agreeably. "Is it a real one?"

"It's a diminutive," said Orsen. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, as though someone else were using it. "Of Elisabeth. Of... Lise."

Jude looked up. Something moved behind those close-set quick eyes — not fear exactly, but the animal readiness of a creature that has learned to read rooms before reading books. "You knew her?"

"I knew a Lise once." Orsen sat down across from him, slowly, because her knees had opinions. "Long ago. In another part of the coast. She was..." She found that her throat required clearing. "She was the sort of person who got out of trouble and into it in equal measure. She had a smile that went crooked first on the left."

Jude's face did something extraordinary. It went completely still, the stillness of a held wave, and then the smile broke across it — crooked first on the left — and Orsen felt the locket in her pocket grow hot as a coal.

"She did," Jude whispered. "Ma smiled like that. How did you know that? Nobody knows that. I never told anybody that."

"Because some things," said Orsen, and stopped, and started again, "are worn deeper than others. Some things go all the way down to the ivory."

* * *

They talked for two hours. Orsen broke her own resolution forty separate times and regretted none of them.

Piece by piece, with the patience of a woman untangling rigging, the story assembled itself — not all of it, Jude didn't have all of it, but enough. His mother had died two winters ago, of fever, in a fishing town far south whose name he gave and Orsen did not recognize. After that it had been Jude and his father aboard the boat mostly, moving from port to port, and Jude had understood without anyone explaining it that they moved *because*, the way he understood tides moved *because* — some force you didn't argue with, you just adjusted sail. Then this autumn the moving had gotten faster and quieter, and there had been a night in a harbor town where men came asking questions at the taverns with papers in their hands, and his father had woken him before dawn and rowed him out himself, and given him the note and a coin and instructions: north along the coast road, second day, the point with the light, knock late, give the woman the paper, say nothing else, *forget the face*.

"Which face?" Jude asked now, genuinely puzzled, and Orsen nearly laughed, nearly wept, at the boy having carried the phrase for weeks like a stone in his shoe without once examining it.

"I think," she said carefully, "your father meant mine. He wanted me to forget his face so that if anyone asked me questions, I couldn't describe him."

"Oh." Jude considered this. "But you've never seen him."

"No," said Orsen, which was true in the way that mattered for the magistrate and false in every way that mattered for her.

She went to the drawer while Jude finished the pot of drippings with a spoon — the appetite on the boy, honestly, he ate like the harbor itself — and brought back the locket, and set it on the table between them without a word.

Jude put down the spoon.

He did not touch it at first. He looked at it the way you look at something in a dream that has followed you into waking. Then he picked it up with both hands, coaxing the hinge open with a thumbnail that had doubtless picked apart a hundred gulls' leftovers and crabs and knots, and the locket sighed open, and he saw.

For a long time he said nothing. The rain filled the silence, ticking against the glass. Somewhere below them the sea worked at the rocks, patient, enormous, keeping its own count.

"That's Pa," he said finally. Very quietly. "Young."

"Yes."

"Who's the lady?"

"Look again."

He looked again. And Orsen watched — and would remember watching, in the years afterward, in a different harbor, in a different life — the exact moment when the boy understood. It came into his face the way dawn comes into a harbor: not all at once but in stages, the masts first, then the water, then the whole bright width of it. His eyes went from the locket to her and back again, twice, three times, doing sums of his own.

"That's Ma," he said. "But younger. That's Ma." His thumb trembled against the ivory. "Who are these people, Mrs. Delacroix? Why have you got a picture of my ma in a—"

"Because the lady," Orsen said, and her voice held, barely, "was my sister."

The word *sister* landed in the kitchen and sat there among the bread and the dripping and the rain, changing the shape of everything around it.

"Your—" Jude's mouth worked. "You're my—"

"Aunt," Orsen said. "I expect. If my sums are right. Which they generally are; it's a professional requirement." And because the moment had grown too large for either of them, she reached across and tapped the second pane, the young man with the scarred eyebrow. "And that ruffian once pulled my husband out of the sea with his bare hands, and I owe him a debt I have been carrying eleven years like a stone in my pocket, and it appears he has chosen an interesting manner of calling it in."

Jude was crying now, silently, the way boys cry when they have practiced it, tears running straight off his jaw, and he held the locket against his chest with both hands as though it might be taken.

"I thought I was alone," he said.

"You were never," said Orsen, "for one single day since you knocked on that door, alone. I simply hadn't done the paperwork."

The laugh that came out of him was wet and cracked and crooked-first-on-the-left, and it was so exactly his mother's laugh, so exactly the laugh that had once filled her parents' kitchen in a village fifty miles south, that Orsen had to stand up abruptly and attend to a kettle that needed nothing whatsoever.

* * *

She sent him up for his nap at the usual hour — he fought naps on principle but fell asleep within minutes of winning the argument, a strategy she recognized from Erik and from every fisherman on the coast — and then she sat alone at the table with the locket open before her, both faces looking up, and allowed herself, for the first time in eleven years, to speak to her sister.

"You sent me a boy," she said. "You couldn't have sent a ham. A ham I'd know what to do with."

The rain answered. The sea answered. Neither of them was ever much for conversation.

But the arithmetic had changed, and Orsen Delacroix was a woman who respected arithmetic. She was no longer sheltering a stranger out of obligation to a debt. She was sheltering her nephew — her sister's son, her own blood, the last living piece of Lise — and the law that paid her wage said such sheltering was treason, and the magistrate's men were already sniffing up and down the coast, and Halden would be back, Halden always came back, and somewhere south of here a man with a scarred eyebrow was hiding in the cracks of the world believing his son was safe.

The lamp upstairs needed trimming. There were logs to log, entries to enter, a keeper's tidy paper world to maintain. Instead Orsen sat in the gray light doing sums of an entirely different order: the supply boat due Thursday, the magistrate's circuit due sooner, the final storm of the season due whenever it pleased, and one fishing boat — she'd need to find out which, and whose, and when — capable of carrying a boy beyond the reach of all of it.

She was still sitting there, the locket warm in her closed fist, when boots came up the wet path outside the tower — heavy, deliberate, official boots — and a fist struck the door three times.

"Keeper Delacroix!" A voice she knew, and did not want to know. "Open in the name of the magistrate!"

Upstairs, faintly, the floor creaked: a small boy, awake early, coming to see who had knocked.

Orsen put the locket in her pocket, put the fear in a drawer next to it, and went to answer the door.
