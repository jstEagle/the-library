## 13. A Light for Two

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and burnt oil and, since Jude had come to live at the light, faintly of the mint drops he kept hidden in his coat pocket despite the fact that Orsen had told him twice that sugar near the mechanism was an invitation to ants, and ants near the mechanism was an invitation to ruin.

He climbed the stairs ahead of her that evening, two at a time, the way he always did now, as if the tower belonged to him and he were merely allowing her to accompany him up her own staircase. She let him have it. There were few enough things in the world a boy could own outright.

"Careful at the turn," she said, out of habit more than need. He knew the turn. He knew every one of the ninety-eight steps, had counted them aloud his first week until she'd threatened — gently, and only half in jest — to count *him* over the railing if he said "ninety-one" one more time in that singsong voice.

"Sixty-two, sixty-three," he said now, just to be contrary, and grinned back down at her over his shoulder. He was getting tall. Not tall yet, but getting there, the way a tide gets in: you didn't notice it happening until suddenly the rocks you'd walked across dry-shod all summer were under water.

Inside the lantern room, the great lens turned its slow, patient circles, throwing its beam out over the darkening harbor like a hand smoothing wrinkles from cloth. Orsen set her mending basket by the stool — she had learned long ago that there was no point climbing all this way without something useful for her hands — and began the evening ritual.

"Wick," she said.

Jude produced the spare wick from the cabinet, holding it up like an offering. "Trimmed."

"Glass."

"Polished." He rapped a knuckle against the storm pane, which rang clean and bright. He had polished it himself that afternoon, and she could tell, because he'd done the lower panes last and they still carried the faint ghost of his small handprints, which she had not had the heart to wipe away when she'd found them.

"Oil."

"Full."

"Clockwork."

He wound the weight mechanism with both hands on the crank, tongue between his teeth, counting under his breath. Forty turns. He never lost count anymore. When he'd first come to her, thin as a gull's wing and flinching at slammed doors, he couldn't have counted past ten without his voice going strange. Now he counted everything — waves, steps, turns of the crank, days since the supply boat last came — as though numbers were a rope he could hold onto while the world pitched beneath him.

Perhaps they were. Orsen had never been a boy, but she had been a woman alone in a winter once, and she had counted too.

"Very good, Mr. Keeper's Assistant," she said, and he puffed up so grandly at the title that she had to turn away to hide her smile and fuss instead with the wick adjustment, which did not need fussing.

* * *

They ate supper in the kitchen below, where the stove kept its iron heart beating and the rain worked at the windows like a polite but persistent caller. Jude had developed strong opinions about fish stew, most of them favorable, and he ate three helpings before pushing back his bowl with the deep sigh of a man who has suffered nobly through abundance.

"Maren says," he began, and then stopped, because mentioning Maren had become a careful business between them.

Maren was the fisherman's wife down the point who had taken it upon herself to leave baskets on Orsen's doorstep — bread, salt herring, a jar of cloudberries, once a pair of woolen socks clearly sized for a growing boy and clearly knitted by someone who had guessed at the size rather than known it. Maren asked no questions. Maren left no notes. But Maren's baskets had grown heavier each week, and Orsen understood them for what they were: a village deciding, quietly and without ceremony, whose side it stood on.

"You may say whatever Maren says," Orsen said. "I'm not made of glass."

"She says the magistrate's clerk was at the fish market Tuesday. Asking after boats." Jude turned his spoon over, studying it as if the answer might be engraved on the handle. "Asking especially after the *Petrel*."

Orsen kept her hands steady on her teacup. The *Petrel* was Tomas Vey's boat. Tomas Vey sat, at this moment, in a cell in Halden, and the magistrate believed — or wished very much to believe — that the boy sitting across from her was proof of whatever crime Tomas had been accused of, and that she, Orsen Delacroix, keeper of the Harbor Point light, wage-earner of the coastal authority, was an accomplice.

"Well," she said. "The *Petrel* hasn't put out in six weeks. The clerk will find that a dull answer."

"Will he stop asking?"

"No." She said it plainly, because she had promised herself she would never lie to the boy about the shape of their danger. Soften it, yes. Lie, no. Lies had a way of collapsing under children like bad roofs. "But asking isn't finding. And there are many boats on this coast besides the *Petrel*."

Jude nodded slowly, absorbing this, filing it away in whatever ledger he kept behind his eyes. Then, because he was eleven and supper was finished and dread could only hold a boy's attention so long:

"Can I work the light tonight? All by myself?"

"The whole watch?"

"Start to finish."

Orsen considered him over the rim of her cup. The truth was he already did half the work and most of the climbing; the truth was also that the night watch meant staying awake, and staying awake meant lying in his bunk afterward listening to the wind and remembering, some nights, whatever it was he would not speak of — the face, the note had said, forget the face, and he had never once described it to her, and she had never once asked.

"One condition," she said. "You do the trimming and the winding and the log entry. I sit in the chair. If anything goes wrong that your ten fingers can't mend, my ten fingers are four steps away."

"That's two conditions."

"It's one condition with parts."

He grinned. "That's what Papa used to say about chores."

The words landed in the kitchen like a dropped plate. It was the first time, in all these weeks, that he had said the word *Papa* without his voice going flat and careful. Orsen felt the moment tremble between them — say nothing and let it pass, or reach out and touch it.

She reached out.

"What else did your papa use to say?" she asked, quiet, gathering her mending into her lap as if the question were a small and ordinary thing.

Jude looked at his bowl. The rain filled the silence for a while.

"He said keepers were the luckiest people alive," he said finally. "Because they get paid to stay up all night doing good." He glanced up, quick and searching, the way a boy checks whether a joke has landed or broken something. "Is that true?"

"I've often thought so," Orsen said, and her voice came out steadier than she expected. "Though nobody ever mentions the wicks."

* * *

By nine the storm had settled into a long, steady working rain, and Jude went up to take his watch with the gravity of a bishop entering a cathedral. Orsen settled into the chair he dragged to the foot of the stairs for her — he positioned it precisely where he could see her from the lantern room, and she pretended not to notice — and took out her mending.

It was his coat she mended. It had needed mending since the night he arrived, and she had mended it three times already, and it would need mending again, because boys wore their clothes the way weather wears stone. She worked the needle by lamplight and listened to the small sounds above her: the creak of his footsteps around the lens, the mutter of his voice reading off the log entries to himself, once, unmistakably, the word "*ninety-one*" sung to himself like a charm.

Somewhere past midnight she must have drifted, because the next thing she knew the stairwell was full of light — not lamplight, but the great sweeping beam itself, thrown down the spiral stairs in slow golden blades as the lens turned. Jude stood halfway down, silhouetted, holding the shutter key in his fist.

"There's a ship," he said. "Come look. You have to come look."

She followed him up. The rain had thinned to mist, and out past the point, running low and laboring through the swell, a lugger was standing in toward the harbor — late, off schedule, riding too deep in the water for comfort. The beam swept over her, and Jude counted aloud as it went: one, sweep, two, sweep, three.

"Four flashes and she'll see us clear of the shoal," he said. "Mr. Aldren taught me. Four flashes means safe water, if the keeper holds the light steady."

"And if the keeper doesn't hold it steady?"

"Then she thinks we're another light altogether and stands in on the rocks." He said it without drama, simply as fact, and Orsen looked at him — this boy who had arrived at her door unable to say his own surname without shaking, now reciting the grammar of the sea like his catechism — and felt something turn over in her chest that she had thought was buried with her husband.

The lugger answered with a single lantern hoisted and lowered. Acknowledgment. Thanks. Jude watched it the way other boys watched fireworks.

"They know us," he said softly. "I mean — they know the light. Whoever's aboard, wherever they're going, they know this light is here, and they trust it." He frowned at the fading sail. "Do you suppose that counts? Being trusted by strangers who never see your face?"

Orsen looked out at the black water where the ship had gone.

"I think," she said, "that it might be the only kind that lasts."

* * *

She woke before dawn to the sound of the door — not the front door, which groaned on its hinges and announced every visitor for half a mile, but the scullery door, which she had oiled herself, specifically, so that certain visitors would not be announced.

For one cold instant her heart went to the magistrate, to the clerk at the market, to chains and questions.

Then she smelled bread.

Maren came into the kitchen with a basket on her hip and rain in her shawl, moving with the brisk entitlement of a woman who has decided a matter privately and considers discussion unnecessary. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a skiff, with forearms that had hauled nets for forty years and eyes that missed nothing, least of all a sleeping boy's coat draped over the chair.

"He's filled out," Maren said, by way of good morning.

"He eats like three boys."

"Good. Put him to work hauling my crab pots next week and he'll eat like five." She set the basket down and began unpacking it with the efficiency of a quartermaster: bread, cheese, a crock of butter, dried cod, and — deliberately placed on top, where it could not be mistaken for accident — a folded newspaper.

Orsen looked at the paper and then at Maren, and Maren looked back at her with those net-hauling eyes, and neither of them said anything for a moment, because there are conversations that are safer unwritten even in glances, and this was nearly one of them.

Finally Maren said, "My husband heard, down the quay, that the magistrate's got a new witness coming. Somebody from out of town. Somebody," and here her voice dropped to almost nothing, "who's been paid to remember things."

"A witness to what?"

"To boats. To comings and goings at the point." Maren's jaw worked. "To a light burning steady, night after night, on a stretch of coast where a fugitive might hope to find a keeper with a soft heart."

The stove ticked. Above them, faintly, came the sound of Jude's footsteps beginning his morning round, and the distant, cheerful murmur of a boy counting steps.

"Thank you, Maren," Orsen said.

"Don't thank me. Thank my husband, who decided the fish market clerk didn't need telling about our own comings and goings." Maren pulled her shawl tight and headed for the door, then paused, hand on the frame, not looking back. "Tomas Vey pulled my man out of the water in '87. Off the Kettle Rocks, in February. So whatever's coming," she said, "this house remembers too."

And she was gone into the rain, leaving Orsen alone in the warm kitchen with the bread and the newspaper and the sound of a boy upstairs, counting his way toward morning.

She unfolded the paper. The news was three weeks old and mostly dull — grain prices, a regatta, a shipping notice — except for one small item on the fourth page, tucked between an auction listing and a sermon summary:

*The magistrate's office announces a reward for information concerning the whereabouts of persons harboring individuals sought under the maritime security provisions. Information may be given confidentially.*

Confidentially. Paid to remember. A stranger from out of town, coming to a coast where everyone owed someone something.

Above her head, the light went dark as Jude doused the lamp at dawn, right on schedule, precise to the minute. She heard the clockwork run down, heard him log it — she could picture the careful pencil strokes, *Lamp extinguished 5:52, weather rain clearing, all well* — and she stood there in the gray early light with the reward notice open in her hands.

All well, the boy wrote. She hoped to God he'd keep being able to write it.

Outside, far down the road along the point, a figure appeared in the mist. A stranger, unhurried, carrying a satchel, walking toward the lighthouse with the easy confidence of a man who already knew exactly where he was going.
