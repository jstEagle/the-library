## 14. The Informant

The storm had passed in the night, leaving the harbor swept and gleaming like a coin someone had polished out of guilt. Orsen Delacroix stood at the gallery rail with a mug of chicory coffee going cold in her hand and watched the fishing fleet pick its way back out past the point, sails limp, crews moving slowly, the whole town exhaling after three days of holding its breath.

Behind her, inside the tower, Jude was counting.

He counted everything now. Waves, mostly, but also stairs, gulls, cracks in the plaster, the seconds between the flash of the light and the foghorn's answer when the weather obliged him with both. She had stopped asking why. Somewhere in the fourth week of the boy's residence — she had begun thinking of it as a residence, which was either an act of optimism or self-destruction, she hadn't decided — he had told her, in the flat way he told her most things, that his mother used to count with him.

"And when you get to a hundred," he'd said, "nothing bad has happened yet."

Orsen had put down her mending and looked at him for a long moment. "That's a good system," she'd said, because it was, in its way, the best system for living she had ever heard described, and no one had ever taught it to her.

This morning he was counting boats. He stood at the seaward window with his nose nearly touching the glass, lips moving. "...eleven, twelve..." He paused. "Thirteen."

"Good haul," Orsen said. "After a storm like that, they'll have found the fish all confused and stupid. Easy picking." She said things like this deliberately, small domestic observations about fish and weather, because she had learned that ordinary words were a kind of blanket, and the boy was always slightly cold.

Jude did not answer. He had gone still in the particular way that made the hair on her arms rise.

"There's a boat," he said, "that isn't fishing."

She set down the mug and came to stand beside him. The harbor spread below in its morning silver, and she found what he had found almost at once: a small skiff, riding low, hugging the shadow of the breakwater instead of crossing open water. It sat there without nets, without lines, without any of the honest industry of the other thirteen. Just a man in oilskins, sitting still.

"Fourteen," Jude said.

"Yes," Orsen said quietly. "I see it."

* * *

By noon she had convinced herself of six reasonable explanations, none of which she believed. A man admiring the view. A man waiting on a tide. A man who had lost something overboard and was looking for it with great patience and no equipment.

At two o'clock the supply boat came around the point, as it did every second Thursday, and Orsen went down to the landing to take delivery of lamp oil, flour, and whatever gossip Mrs. Brandt the postmistress had packed into the crates alongside the actual goods, because Mrs. Brandt considered news a staple food group.

Halden was on the supply boat.

Orsen felt it in her stomach first, the drop, before her face had time to arrange itself into anything presentable. The magistrate stood at the bow with his hat squared on his head and his hands folded behind his back, and he wore the expression of a man attending a funeral he had personally arranged.

"Mrs. Delacroix." He stepped onto the landing without waiting for the boatman's help. "Good afternoon."

"Magistrate." She kept her voice level, kept her hands busy with the mooring line, because hands that were busy looked like hands that had nothing to hide. "You've come to inspect the light? After the storm, I'd expect—"

"I've come to talk." He glanced up at the tower, white against the blue, at the gallery rail where a small figure stood watching. Orsen watched him watch, and the counting in her chest began, and got nowhere near a hundred.

"Then we'll talk inside," she said. "It's raw off the water."

* * *

In the kitchen, Halden sat where he had sat before, in the chair by the stove, and accepted coffee he did not drink. Jude was not in the room. She had sent him up the tower stairs with instructions to polish the spare lantern glass, an errand invented entirely for the purpose of putting forty-one steps between him and this conversation, and he had gone without argument, which frightened her more than anything else, because the boy understood errands like that now. He had learned the grammar of hiding from her.

"You know why I'm here," Halden said.

"I imagine there are several things you might be here for, Magistrate. It's been a busy season for your office."

"Don't." He rubbed his eyes, and for a moment he looked less like a magistrate than like a tired man of fifty who wanted to be home. "Don't do that thing where you fence with me, Orsen. You're better at it than I am, God knows, but I haven't the heart for it today. There was a report."

"A report."

"A letter. Sent to the coastal authority in Kessel and forwarded down to me, with a note suggesting I handle it, since I am familiar with—" he gestured vaguely, taking in the lighthouse, the harbor, the general stubbornness of her, "—the local situation."

"What did the letter say?"

"It said," Halden recited, and clearly he had read it many times; it had the worn smoothness of scripture or obsession, "that the lighthouse keeper at Harbor Point is harboring a foreign boy, undocumented, of an age and description matching a child sought in connection with the Vey matter. That the boy does not attend school. That he is seen on the beach at odd hours. That supplies of food sufficient for more than one person are delivered to the light." He looked up. "Whoever wrote it watches you closely, Orsen. They knew about the flour."

The kitchen was very quiet. The stove ticked. Above them, faintly, came the sound of a boy polishing glass, methodical, patient, counting under his breath.

"May I see the handwriting?" Orsen asked.

"Why?"

"Because I promised a debt once, and I keep accounts. If I'm going to be informed upon, I should like to know by whom."

Halden hesitated. Then he took the folded paper from his breast pocket and laid it on the table, and turned it so she could read it, and she understood that this was itself a confession of some kind — that he wanted her to see it, that some part of him hoped she would recognize it and give him a reason for what he was about to do, or a reason not to do it.

The hand was cramped and careful, the letters pressed deep. Shopkeeper's letters, taught by rote.

"It's not familiar," Orsen said truthfully, and felt something complicated move through her — relief, and then disappointment at her own relief.

"No," Halden said. "I didn't think it would be. It's not a friend of yours. It's worse than that, I'm afraid." He took the letter back. "It's paid for. The coastal authority has been offering bounties for reports on harbored foreigners since September. Two crowns for a verified report. Five if it leads to an arrest."

"Five crowns." Orsen laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "That's the price of my family, is it? Five crowns. Less than a decent coat."

"That's the price of information. What it costs afterward depends on me." Halden leaned forward. "Listen to me. The report has been logged. I cannot unlog it. In ten days there is a review in Kessel, and if I report this address as cleared, I need it to *be* cleared, or I go down with you, and then whoever sits in my chair next will not be a tired man who owes your late husband a game of chess and an apology." He held her gaze. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

She understood. She had understood, probably, from the moment the skiff appeared below the breakwater, riding low and patient in the shadow. But understanding a thing and letting it into the house were different acts, and she had gotten good at the space between them.

"The boy's mother," Halden said carefully, "was she—"

"You know who she was." It came out of Orsen before she could stop it, and she saw the flicker in his face, the magistrate's reflex, filing it away. Too late she heard the shape of what she'd admitted. *Was.* Not *is.*

Halden sat back slowly. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and above them the boy polished glass, and the sea worked at the rocks below with its old patience.

"There are stories," Halden said at last, very quietly, "going around the harbor. About Tomas Vey. About who he was, before he was a fisherman. About who his people were." He paused. "About whether the boy looks like him."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a gull screamed once and gave up.

"If you repeat harbor stories in an official report," Orsen said, "you'll start a fire you can't put out. This town will eat itself, Halden. You know how it gets when there's blood in the water and winter coming. Neighbor against neighbor over five crowns. Is that the law you want to administer?"

"What I want," Halden said, rising, putting on his hat, becoming again the office instead of the man, "is not to come back here with men whose faces I don't choose. Ten days, Orsen. Whatever you're going to decide, decide it fast."

He let himself out. She heard his boots on the gravel, the boatman's call, the engine turning over. She stayed at the table until the sound faded, and then she climbed the forty-one steps, and found Jude sitting cross-legged among the lantern stores, the spare glass polished to a shine that hurt the eye.

"Is the man gone?" Jude asked.

"How did you know it was a man?"

"Because you sent me upstairs." He said it without accusation, which was somehow unbearable. "People only send me upstairs when it's about me."

Orsen lowered herself to sit beside him on the cold stone floor, her knees cracking in protest, and for a while she didn't say anything at all, just breathed the smell of brass polish and lamp oil, the smell of the work that had kept her upright through eleven years of widowhood.

"Jude," she said finally. "Someone wrote a letter about you. Someone who watches us."

He absorbed this the way he absorbed everything: silently, completely, like water going into sand. Then: "The fourteenth boat. The one that wasn't fishing. Was that the watcher?"

"Probably."

"Then he'll come back," Jude said. "Watchers always come back. My mother said—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and Orsen did not push, though she wanted to with her whole chest. Instead she put her arm around his shoulders, thin under the sweater she had knitted too large on purpose, so there would be room for him to grow into it. So there would be a reason for him to stay long enough to grow.

"Nothing bad has happened yet," she said. "Have you counted?"

He nodded against her sleeve. "Forty-one steps. Sixty-two gulls. One magistrate." A pause. "One hundred and nine waves this morning. Nothing bad happened yet."

"Then we're ahead of the game," Orsen said, and her voice held steady, and it was the finest acting of her life.

* * *

That evening she walked down to Maren's kitchen with butter she couldn't afford to give away, because there are conversations you cannot have inside the walls where the listening might happen, and Maren's kitchen had two doors and a window over the sink that faced nothing but Maren's own cabbages.

Maren was kneading bread, flour to her elbows, and she read Orsen's face before the door had shut behind her.

"Somebody talked," Maren said.

"Five crowns' worth."

Maren swore with a fluency that would have surprised the congregation, wiped her hands on her apron, and sat down across from Orsen with the finality of a woman clearing the day's schedule in her head. "Who?"

"I don't know. Halden says it's not a friend. Someone taking the bounty money."

Maren was quiet for a moment, working something through, and Orsen watched her do it, and loved her for it, and feared her a little for it too, because Maren was the only person besides Halden who knew the full shape of things, and love under pressure bends toward arithmetic.

"It's Petersen," Maren said finally. "The chandler."

"Petersen?" Orsen frowned. "Why would he—"

"Because he's owed money by half the fleet, and half the fleet won't pay until the season ends, and if the coastal authority starts rewarding informants, Petersen wants to be first in line when the rewards get bigger. He's been rowing that skiff along the breakwater for a week. Ask anyone." Maren's mouth twisted. "Also because twenty years ago your husband caught him watering the lamp oil and never reported it, and some men carry gratitude like a stone in the boot."

Orsen sat with that. Petersen. The man who sold her wicks. Who had stood in her kitchen last spring complaining about his knees. Five crowns. A stone in the boot.

"So what do we do?" Maren asked. And the *we* landed in Orsen's chest like a warm coal.

"We wait for dark," Orsen said, "and then I need you to tell me everything you know about the last boat out before the next storm."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Outside, far off across the darkening water, the great lamp at the top of the tower swung its beam out over the sea, steady, faithful, illuminating nothing it intended to.

"There's a boat," Maren said slowly, "called the *Selma's Pride*. And there's a man who runs her who owes Tomas Vey more than five crowns." She reached for Orsen's hand across the floury table. "But Orsen — there's something else. Something I heard today, down at the fish market, and I didn't believe it, and now I don't know what to think."

"Tell me."

"They took a fisherman off the *Greta* this morning. Quiet, before dawn, no warrant shown. Old sailor, half the harbor says, foreign-born." Maren's grip tightened. "They're saying it was Tomas Vey, Orsen. They're saying they've had him since Tuesday."
