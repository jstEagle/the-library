## 15. Tomas in the Cell

The jail at Thorshavn Point had once been a herring warehouse, and it had never entirely stopped smelling like one. Orsen noticed it the moment she stepped through the door — fish and brine and tar, layered under lamp oil and the sourness of men who slept too close together for too long. The constable on duty, a round young man named Pell whose ears turned red whenever anyone spoke to him sharply, looked up from his ledger with an expression of pure alarm.

"Mrs. Delacroix." He stood so quickly his stool tipped and had to be caught. "You can't — that is, I mean to say, visiting hours are—"

"It's four o'clock in the afternoon, Mr. Pell, and I've brought bread." She lifted the cloth-covered basket as evidence. "Unless feeding prisoners is now against the law as well."

Pell's mouth opened and closed twice. He was perhaps twenty, and he had the particular misfortune of being honest in a dishonest season. Everyone at the harbor knew it. It was why Halden never gave him anything important to do.

"The magistrate said no visitors," Pell managed. "Not to this one especially."

"And did he say no bread?"

"...No, ma'am."

"Then we understand each other." She set the basket on his desk with the firmness of a woman who had spent eleven years arguing with weather and had never once lost. "Lead on, Mr. Pell. My knees aren't what they were."

Her knees were exactly what they had always been. But she'd learned long ago that a small confession of frailty opened more doors than any key, and she was not above spending it.

The cell block was three cells deep, cut into the warehouse's old stone foundation. Two of them stood empty. In the third, a man sat on the edge of a cot with his elbows on his knees, staring at the floor as though it owed him money.

Tomas Vey had aged twenty years in the eight days since they'd taken him. Orsen had known him when he was broad as a door, laughing from the wheelhouse of his boat loud enough to be heard over a gale. Now his shoulders curved inward like something folding itself away for winter, and his hair — always black as a gull's wing — showed white at the temples that hadn't been there in autumn.

He looked up at the sound of her boots. For one moment his face did something terrible and hopeful all at once, and then it smoothed over, and he smiled the way a man smiles when he wants very badly to reassure someone and has almost nothing to do it with.

"Orsen Delacroix," he said. "They still let angels carry bread?"

"They let stubborn widows carry bread. The angels were refused permits." She sat down on the stool Pell had placed — bless the boy, he'd placed it without being asked — and set the basket between them. "Rye and a bit of cheese. Don't tell Maren I brought the good cheese; she'll say you haven't earned it."

"Maren's right. I haven't." But his hands went to the basket, and she saw them tremble, and understood how little he'd been eating. Not out of neglect — Pell would have fed him — but out of some private penance she could only guess at.

She watched him tear the bread and eat the first piece too fast, the way hungry men do, and she waited until he slowed.

* * *

"They told me you'd come," Tomas said finally. "Halden. He sat where you're sitting and told me you wouldn't. He said, 'The keeper looks after her own interests, Vey. She won't risk the walk down.'" Tomas wiped crumbs from his knee with unnecessary care. "I told him he didn't know you."

"He was half right. I didn't come for eight days." She kept her voice level. "I needed to know whether coming here put the boy at risk. I watched who came and went. I counted the constables' shifts. I learned that Pell takes his supper at six and that the night man drinks the medicinal sherry meant for the cells." She paused. "Then I came."

Tomas laughed — a short, broken sound, surprised out of him. "Eight days of reconnaissance. You always did plan a crossing before you left the dock."

"One of us should." She leaned forward. "Now. Tell me what they have on you, and don't dress it up. I've read the harbor notices. 'Aiding foreign agents.' 'Consorting with enemy vessels.' Half of Thorshavn believes you've been signaling the whole coast of the continent from your kitchen window."

"There's no signal," Tomas said quietly. "There never was. What there is, is a name on a shipping manifest from nine years ago that shouldn't have been mine, and a cousin in Bremerhaven who wrote me letters I answered, because he's my mother's sister's son and a man answers his family even if his family writes in careful words that a fool magistrate can twist into knots." His jaw worked. "And there's the boy. That's the true charge, though nobody's written it yet. They think I planted him with you. A spy's child, stashed at a lighthouse like contraband."

Orsen felt the cold move through her that had nothing to do with the stone walls. "And is he? Planted?"

Tomas looked up. And there it was again — that terrible hope, that fear — and beneath both of them, something older and sadder, the thing he'd carried since the day he'd knocked on her door with a sleeping child in his arms and a note in his pocket.

"You know I can't answer that," he said.

"Tomas—"

"I can't," and his voice cracked on it, just slightly, before he mastered it. "Not because I don't trust you, Orsen. God knows I'd trust you with my boat, my life, my grave. But everything I say in this cell goes into Pell's ledger, and Pell's ledger goes to Halden, and Halden—" He shook his head slowly. "Halden isn't looking for spies. Spies are hard to find. Halden is looking for results, and results are easy. You make them out of frightened people."

* * *

Outside, somewhere above them, a gull screamed its opinion of the world. Orsen folded her hands in her lap and made herself wait, because she'd learned that from the sea too: the truth comes in on its own tide if you don't go dragging for it.

It came slowly.

"I saved your Henrik," Tomas said at last, to the floor. "Nineteen years ago. The *Meridian*, the squall off the point. You know this story. You've bought me dinners over it for nineteen years, against my objections."

"Against your loud objections."

"Because it wasn't a favor, Orsen. It was Tuesday." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Any man would have done it. Any man with a boat and a rope. But your Henrik decided otherwise, God rest him, and then you decided otherwise after, and the two of you built this debt out of nothing. Out of a rope thrown from a deck." He pressed his palms flat against his thighs. "So when his note came last month — the arrangement, the place to leave the boy if it went wrong — I did it because of a rope. Do you see? The whole thing hangs on a rope."

Orsen's breath stopped and started again. "His note. Henrik's note."

Tomas went very still, the way a man does when he has said more than he meant to and is measuring exactly how much more the silence will cost him.

"Tomas Vey," she said, and her voice came out low and dangerous and entirely her own, "you told me the note pinned to Jude's blanket was in your hand. You said it yourself: 'forget the face,' your letters, your ink. I recognized it. You knew I would."

"Aye."

"So either you wrote it, or you copied it." She leaned closer. "From something *Henrik wrote*. Before he died. Before either of us knew there'd ever be a boy."

The lamp in the corridor guttered. Somewhere in the building, a door closed and footsteps faded toward the street. Tomas sat with his head bowed, and Orsen watched the fight move across his face — the same fight, she realized, that had lasted eight days, maybe longer, maybe years — and she thought of Henrik at the kitchen table with his maps and his quiet schemes, her gentle schemer, her lighthouse man who loved the coast enough to plan for it like a chess player planning three storms ahead.

"He wrote to me the winter before he died," Tomas said finally. "Just once. A strange letter. He said — I've had eight days in this cell to memorize it, so I can give it to you word for word — he said: *"If anything comes to your door wearing my mother's face, it belongs with the keeper. Whatever else happens, whatever anyone tells you, forget the face and remember the promise. She keeps what she's given. That's the whole of her nature."*" Tomas exhaled. "I thought he'd gone soft in the head, writing riddles to a fisherman. Then a boy turned up wearing your face instead, near enough, and I understood the letter better than I wanted to."

"My mother's face," Orsen repeated. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Tomas. Whose boy is he?"

The old fisherman raised his head, and for a moment the cell fell away and they were just two people in the lamplight, holding something fragile between them that neither had asked to carry.

"That," he said, "is the question Halden would pay gold to have answered. And it's the reason I'm sitting in this cell instead of telling you plain on my own deck. Because there are people living comfortably in this harbor right now, Orsen — respectable people, churchgoing people — who will burn everything down rather than have that answer written anywhere. Including a boy. Including you." He reached through the bars, slow, and rested his rough hand over hers on the basket. "You asked what they have on me. Here it is, all of it: they have nothing, and they know they have nothing, and it doesn't matter. Halden needs a spy by the equinox storm or the commission from the capital dries up. So he'll make one. He's offered me terms already."

Orsen's hand went cold under his. "What terms?"

"He gives me a boat and a pardon. I give him—" Tomas's mouth twisted. "Names. Anyone. The harbor's full of foreign sailors this season, half of them running from worse than we are. I point at three or four, he ships them out, everyone's a hero, and I go home to my nets." He shrugged, a terrible loose shrug. "And the first name on the list, the one he mentioned most warmly, was the woman who feeds prisoners the good cheese and shelters a boy at the light."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The lamp hissed. Orsen became aware, distantly, of her own heartbeat, steady and slow, the way it went when the worst thing arrived and there was nothing left to dread.

"He wants you to buy your freedom with Jude," she said.

"With Jude. With you. With the whole lot of it." Tomas's grip tightened on her hand. "And here is the thing I need you to hear, Orsen Delacroix, before you decide anything foolish, which I can see you deciding right now — your face does a thing when you're about to be foolish, it always has, Henrik used to laugh at it—"

"I am not going to be foolish."

"You are going to offer yourself in trade. I can watch you building the sentence." He actually smiled then, tired and crooked, the smile of a man watching a ship he loved sail straight at rocks. "Don't. Listen to me. If you trade yourself, the boy loses the light and the light loses the boy, and Halden gets everything he wants in one transaction. No. There's another way, but it needs you to do the hardest thing you've ever done."

"Which is?"

"Wait. One more day. Maybe two." He glanced past her shoulder, toward the corridor, and lowered his voice until it was barely more than the sea heard through stone. "There's someone else who knows whose boy Jude is. Someone who isn't in a cell. Someone who's been sitting in this harbor all season, close enough to touch, waiting to see which way Halden jumps before showing their hand. Tomorrow morning, Halden comes back for my answer. What I give him depends on what you find out tonight. Ask Maren who else has been asking questions at the market. Ask her about the woman who came on the supply boat and hasn't left."

Orsen stared at him. "Maren's been—"

"Maren knows more than she says. She always did; it's why you keep her." His eyes were urgent now, alive for the first time in eight days. "Go home, Orsen. Feed the boy. Count his waves with him, or whatever it is you two do. And tomorrow, when Halden sits where you're sitting, I'm going to give the magistrate exactly one name."

"Whose?"

Tomas Vey leaned back against the stone wall, and the smile that crossed his face was the old one — the wheelhouse laugh, the rope-in-the-storm laugh, the laugh of a man who had decided precisely how much trouble he intended to be.

"Theirs," he said. "Whoever they are. Because somebody in this harbor has been playing both sides since before the boy ever landed on your doorstep — and tomorrow, God forgive me, I'm going to help them make their mistake."

From somewhere above them, muffled by stone and distance, came the sound of the harbor bell — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the engine of a single boat putting out from the quay in the dark, hours after the last legal sailing hour.

Tomas's eyes found hers in the lamplight.

"Too late to wait for morning," he said quietly. "They're moving tonight."
