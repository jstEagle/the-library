## 16. The Bargain

The magistrate's office smelled of lamp oil and old decisions. Orsen had been kept waiting on a hard bench for the better part of an hour — long enough for her to memorize the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a gull, long enough for her fear to wear itself out and leave behind something colder and more useful.

When the door finally opened, Magistrate Corren did not look like a man who enjoyed his morning. He was younger than she expected, with the ink-stained fingers of someone who wrote more than he spoke, and a cold that had settled into his voice from disuse rather than cruelty.

"Keeper Delacroix." He gestured her in without ceremony. "You've come about Tomas Vey."

"I've come about several things," Orsen said, taking the chair across from his desk without being invited. In her experience, sitting down first was half of any negotiation. The other half was knowing what you could afford to lose. "But yes. Him."

Corren studied her the way she'd seen harbor officials study a ship riding too low in the water — looking for what she was carrying that wasn't declared. "You're aware I can't discuss—"

"You can discuss whatever you choose," Orsen said pleasantly. "The question is whether you'll find it worth your while. May I?" She set her satchel on the desk before he answered, which had also been her intention.

He eyed it. "What is that?"

"The light station's logbook. Eleven years of daily entries. Weather, vessels sighted, wrecks recorded, salvage recovered." She let that last word sit a moment. "Every storm this coast has thrown at us since 1882, written in my hand and my husband's before me. It has occurred to me, Magistrate, that you have a great many ships coming and going this season that your superiors in the capital would like accounted for. And that the man currently keeping the point light is a woman under suspicion of harboring, whose testimony regarding espionage may be called into question at any moment, whose records might therefore be deemed unreliable."

Something flickered across his face — not alarm, but the weary recognition of a man realizing the fish had teeth.

"You wouldn't falsify government records," he said carefully.

"Heavens, no," said Orsen. "I'd simply stop keeping them accurately. A keeper distracted by legal troubles, worried about her position — mistakes creep in. Times get muddled. A wreck goes unrecorded because she's down at the station house answering questions instead of up in the lantern room. Terribly understandable. Boards of inquiry are so sympathetic to that sort of thing." She paused. "Though they do tend to ask why the light was dark for three hours on the fourteenth of October, and who was responsible."

* * *

To understand what Orsen was doing in that office, you have to understand what had happened the night before, in the cell beneath the customs house, when she had pressed her hands against the cold bars and looked at Tomas Vey for the first time in nine years.

He had aged badly and smiled anyway. That was Tomas all over.

"They told me you came asking after me," he said. His voice was roughened — two days in a cell, and before that, God knew what passage. "I told them we were strangers."

"I know. Halden heard the whole interrogation through the floor, being Halden."

Tomas laughed, and then coughed, and then laughed again, quietly, as if laughter were something he had to ration. "Orsen. Why are you here? You should be a hundred miles from this harbor by now. If they connect you to the boy—"

"They already have connected me to the boy. That ship sailed, Tomas, if you'll forgive the expression." She lowered her voice, though the cell block above them was empty except for a snoring drunk. "I need to know everything. All of it. Not for me. For the bargain."

His face went still. "Orsen. No."

"Yes."

"There's nothing to trade. They think I'm a spy. I'm not, but thinking and proving are different animals, and I can't prove anything from in here."

"You can give me names," she said. "Dates. The route you sailed. Who paid you, and what for, and why you stopped. Everything true, everything verifiable — because here is what I know about magistrates, Tomas: they don't want spies. Spies are work. They want cases that close cleanly and quickly, and they want their superiors pleased. If I walk into Corren's office tomorrow with enough real information to make him look efficient, and enough leverage to make him cautious, then your case becomes something he wants to resolve rather than something he wants to own."

"And what does he get out of it?"

Orsen had thought about this all night, sitting at her kitchen table while Jude slept in the loft above, the boy counting waves even in his dreams — she could hear him murmuring numbers. What did Corren get?

"A story," she said now, aloud, to the memory. "A story where nobody important dies."

* * *

Back in the office, Corren had opened the satchel, glanced at the logbook, and closed it again with the air of a man conceding a chess move. "Suppose I take your meaning, Keeper. Suppose further I'm listening. What exactly are you proposing?"

"Tomas Vey was never a spy," Orsen said. "He was a courier. There's a difference, though I understand the distinction may not flatter anyone. He carried sealed packets between fishing boats and certain merchants here in port — payment for silence, mostly, families on the continent buying their relatives' safe passage. When the roundups started, he stopped. He tried to leave. He was caught with one final packet, which I suspect contains nothing but money and a name, and both of those things embarrass people far more than they endanger the Crown."

Corren's pen had gone still. "And how would you know all this?"

"Because last night Tomas Vey told me all of it, freely and voluntarily, as a man seeking to cooperate with the authorities." She met his eyes. "I'm told cooperation is viewed favorably."

"It is viewed favorably," Corren admitted, "when it produces results. Confessions from frightened fishermen produce very little. Most of them know nothing worth knowing and will say anything to say it." He leaned back. "You're offering me Vey's full confession, names of the merchants, the whole network — in exchange for what? Pardon? He's foreign national. My discretion only stretches so far."

"I'm not asking for a pardon." This was the part she had turned over all night, the stone in her shoe. "I'm asking for deportation. Quietly. On the next boat out, no trial, no record beyond what's necessary. A trial means witnesses, Magistrate. Witnesses mean testimony. Testimony means—" she spread her hands slightly— "me. The keeper of the point light, on the stand, explaining how a boy came to be in her house. Explaining whose handwriting was on that note. Explaining what she knew and when. Trials are inefficient things, Magistrate. They drag in everyone standing nearby. Whereas if Vey cooperates fully, names every name, signs everything — well. Then there's nothing left to try, is there? Just paperwork."

She watched him do the arithmetic. She watched it come out in her favor, and watched him hate that.

"And the boy?"

Orsen had known this question was coming. She had rehearsed three answers and discarded all of them in favor of the truth, or a shape of the truth that could pass in daylight.

"Is my late husband's son," she said.

The silence stretched long enough that she heard the clock on the mantel tick twice.

"That's a serious claim," Corren said at last.

"It is. And provable, in time, by the boy himself — he remembers his mother, Magistrate. He remembers her name, and the town, and the locket she left him. I can tell you whose face is in that locket, but I won't put a child on a witness stand to do it. So here is my offer regarding the boy: ask me nothing, and I swear to you formally, in writing, witnessed, that the child leaves this coast before the season's end. He becomes no one's evidence and no one's burden. He vanishes into a free port where no warrant of yours reaches." She paused. "Or charge me, and I will defend myself loudly, extensively, and with excellent counsel, and somewhere in all that noise, Magistrate, someone will start asking why the roundup list was drawn up before the orders authorizing it were signed. Because I've read those orders. Dates matter."

That was the gamble — the thing she'd pieced together from Halden's gossip and the supply boat pilot's loose tongue and her own long habit of noticing. Someone in the customs service had begun the lists early, which meant someone had known about the policy before it was policy, which meant someone had been selling warnings to the smugglers, which meant the entire investigation rested on a foundation that would not survive daylight.

Corren had gone very pale, then very careful.

"You would ruin this office to save a fisherman and a child."

"No," Orsen said gently. "I would save a fisherman and a child, and if you handle it properly, this office will look very good doing it. Imagine the report: corruption uncovered within the service itself, the informant network dismantled, the foreign courier deported as a cooperative witness, order restored. That's not ruin, Magistrate. That's a career." She stood, took her satchel — leaving the logbook deliberately on his desk — and gave him the small, terrible smile of a woman who had buried a husband and kept a lighthouse alone through eleven winters and was not to be trifled with before lunch. "You have until the tide turns tomorrow. I'll bring you Vey's written statement tonight. Have the deportation papers drafted. And Magistrate — the light on the point must stay lit either way. Whatever else happens to me, that doesn't change. So let's not make it change."

* * *

She walked home along the harbor road with the wind pushing at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades, hurrying her along, and found Halden waiting at her gate with his hat literally wringing in his hands, which could not have been comfortable and served him right.

"Well?" he demanded. "Are we ruined? Are we criminals? Should I flee to my cousin in the north? I hate my cousin in the north, Orsen, but I'll do it if I must."

"We are provisionally not ruined," she said, unlocking the door. "We are possibly heroes of the customs service. It depends entirely on whether Corren is more ambitious or more afraid, and I've laid my money on ambitious."

Halden followed her inside, blinking. "You threatened a magistrate."

"I offered a magistrate an opportunity."

"You threatened him with paperwork."

"Halden. There is no threat on this earth more terrifying to that kind of man than paperwork done correctly by someone else."

Jude appeared at the top of the stairs, barefoot, hair wild. He had clearly been listening at the landing, a habit Orsen had decided not to correct yet because it was serving them all admirably. "Did he agree?"

"He hasn't agreed yet," Orsen said. "He has until the tide turns tomorrow."

"What happens at the tide turns?"

Orsen looked at him — this thin, watchful boy with his mother's locket hidden under his shirt and his father's stubbornness written all over his face — and chose honesty, because he'd had too little of it from adults lately. "Then either Tomas sails away to safety, or I do something much stupider, and we find out how fast a fishing boat can be."

Jude considered this with grave seriousness. "The *Petrel* did the point to the free port in two days once. Papa said. In fair weather."

"In fair weather," Orsen agreed. "And what's the weather doing?"

Jude crossed to the window with the certainty of a boy who counted waves the way other children counted sheep, and looked out at the horizon for a long moment. The gulls were flying inland. The light on the water had gone strange and yellow-green. Far off, the sea had begun to comb itself flat and glassy in that particular way that meant the horizon was holding its breath.

"Storm glass is falling," he said quietly. "It'll come fast. Bigger than the last one."

Above them, in the loft, the barometer hanging by the door gave a small creak as the pressure began its long slide downward — and out past the point, where the *Petrel* rode at anchor, the harbor bell rang once, though nothing had touched it.
