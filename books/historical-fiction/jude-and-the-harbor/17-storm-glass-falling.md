## 17. Storm Glass Falling

The storm glass had been Orsen Delacroix's constant companion longer than any living soul, and it had never once lied to her. It sat on the mantel of the lighthouse's little parlor, a teardrop of blown glass on a brass stand, filled with a clear chemical liquor that clouded and bloomed according to weather she could not yet see. Her husband Elias had called it their "honest parishioner." *Everything else in this house will flatter you,* he used to say, tapping the glass with his pipe stem. *The fire says you're warmer than you are. The clock says the night is shorter than it is. That thing tells the truth.*

By lamplight on the morning of what would prove to be the worst day of the season, the truth looked like a jar full of drowned feathers.

Orsen stood at the mantel in her nightdress, one hand wrapped around a cooling cup of coffee, and watched the white crystals drift down through the liquid like snow falling in reverse. She had seen the glass look sickly before — cloudy before squalls, feathery before gales — but never like this. Never so thick. Never so certain.

Behind her, a floorboard creaked.

"You're up early," Jude said from the doorway.

"I'm up late," Orsen corrected. "There's a difference, though I admit the distinction has stopped mattering."

The boy came and stood beside her, arms folded on the mantel, chin stacked on his forearms. He was eleven, small for it, with a shock of dark hair that resisted every attempt at order and his mother's eyes — though he did not yet know that, and Orsen had not found a way to tell him. He studied the storm glass the way he studied everything: seriously, thoroughly, as if it might be examined later for accuracy.

"It looks like a snowstorm got trapped in there," he said.

"That's precisely what it means, more or less."

"In the glass?"

"In the sky. The glass is only a letter from the sky, opened early."

Jue considered this. "Who writes the letters?"

"The world does. The pressure of it. The weight of all that air leaning on the sea." She set down her cup. "And this morning, Jude, the world is leaning hard."

He was quiet a moment. Then, in the flat practical tone he used for the questions that mattered most to him: "Will the boat still come?"

*The boat.* The last fishing boat out of the harbor before the storm sealed the coast like a lid on a pot. The boat that was meant to carry him away from her, away from the magistrate's men, away from the whole tangled dangerous business of being Tomas Vey's son on a coast where Tomas Vey sat in a cell waiting to be tried for espionage. Three days hence, if the weather allowed. Two days, if the glass had its way.

"It will come," Orsen said. "Boats always think they're stronger than storms. It's their great flaw and their great virtue."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we will be having a much longer conversation about patience."

He almost smiled. He had been smiling more lately — small ones, quick ones, like fish rising to a surface and vanishing again. When he'd first arrived at her door four months ago, wet through and shaking, clutching a note that read *forget the face*, he had not smiled at all. He had not spoken above a whisper for three weeks. Now he counted waves aloud when the sea ran high, and argued with her about whether gulls had opinions, and had claimed the window seat in the parlor as sovereign territory.

She had promised Tomas Vey any debt repaid. She had not expected the debt to arrive wrapped in a boy who hummed when he ate and was terrified of the dark cellar and knew the names of clouds better than most grown sailors.

"Go wake the stove," she said. "If the world is going to lean on us today, I'd rather it lean on a warm house."

* * *

The fog came first, because fog always came first, sliding up over the harbor point like something poured rather than blown. By seven the light in the tower was turning uselessly against a whiteness that swallowed its beam two hundred feet out. By eight, Orsen could hear the swell changing — a deeper note under the ordinary wash of the water, the sound of a sea gathering its shoulders.

She worked the tower in her usual rhythm: wick trimmed, lens polished, clockwork wound, the great lamp turned by hand until the weights kissed the floor and needed winding again. Jude trailed her up and down the spiral stairs carrying things — a rag, an oil can, nothing that required him, but he liked to be useful, and she had learned not to take that from him. Children who had lost everything guarded their usefulness like coins.

"Magistrate Rell came to the harbor road yesterday," Jude said, conversationally, somewhere around the fourth trip. "Maren told me."

Orsen's hands did not stop their work. This was a discipline she had practiced for years — grief, fear, and fury all filed away for later examination while the surface stayed smooth as the lens she was polishing.

"Did she, now."

"He had men with him. They were asking about houses with extra rooms." Jude paused on the stair above her, and she heard him swallow. "Ours has an extra room."

"Every house on this coast has an extra room. Half of them have ghosts in them, which keeps the rents low." She climbed past him and kept climbing. "Jude. Come here."

He came. He stood on the landing with the oil can hugged to his chest like a doll, and she crouched to his height, which her knees protested and her conscience overruled.

"What did Maren say exactly? Word for word, if you can. You have a good memory for words."

"Maren said—" he screwed up his face, doing the work honestly, "—she said, 'He's casting nets, that one, and pulling up names.' And then she saw me listening and made me go watch her salt fish, which was boring on purpose."

"On purpose," Orsen agreed gravely. "That is a Maren trick as old as the hills." She held his shoulders. "Now listen to me. Magistrate Rell may come here. If he comes, you are my sister's grandson from up the coast, visiting during the storm season, and your name is Jonas. Can you carry that name?"

"Jonas," he repeated. Then, after a moment: "Did you really have a sister?"

"No. But dead people can't object, and it seemed a waste of a good lie otherwise."

That earned her one of the fish-quick smiles. But at the top of the stairs, pausing, he said quietly, "You won't forget my real one, though. In case I need it back."

"As if I could forget anything about you," Orsen said, and was surprised by how entirely she meant it.

* * *

The supply boat came at noon, which was itself an omen; Petersen the skipper had not run the point in weather like this since the year Elias died, and he came in fast and gray-faced with the swells chasing him up the channel like dogs after a cart.

Orsen met him at the landing, where the water was already slapping at the stones in an ugly, irregular way. Petersen threw her a line, hopped ashore, and immediately lowered his voice as though the wind were an informer.

"You'll want to hear this inside, Keeper."

"I'll want to hear it standing right here, where no walls have ears. There's not a soul on this point but me and the boy."

Petersen glanced toward the tower, then back, and something in his face shifted — the particular discomfort of a man who has carried bad news too long. "They took Tomas Vey's trial date. Moved it forward. Day after tomorrow, in Halden's court, before anyone can get witnesses up the coast or down it. Rell's calling it efficiency."

"Efficiency," Orsen repeated. The word tasted like bilge water. "Day after tomorrow. And the last boat?"

"Tomorrow morning, if I run her at all. Which is why I'm telling you now, Keeper, plain and without decoration: tomorrow morning or never. After the storm hits, there'll be nothing moving on this water for a week, maybe more. And after the trial—" he rubbed his jaw, unable to finish, and finished anyway, quieter. "After the trial, they'll start looking harder for what Tomas wouldn't tell them he'd done with his boy."

The swell boomed against the outer rocks, close enough to feel through the soles of her boots.

"Petersen," she said. "You've known me fifteen years. Have you ever known me to ask a favor?"

"Once. You asked Elias to stop singing while he cleaned the lens, on account of the acoustics."

"And he ignored me for eleven years. So I am owed one successful request, and I'm spending it now. Tomorrow morning. First light, before the glass drops another inch. Can you take a passenger?"

He looked at her a long moment. Wind pulled at his cap; he let it. "I can take a passenger," he said slowly. "But I'll say the other part, because you raised me honest: whatever you decide, decide it tonight. Storms don't wait on decisions, and neither do magistrates. Rell's already summoned folk to testify at the trial. Harbor folk. And Keeper—" he hesitated, and she braced herself, because she had learned to recognize the pause of a man delivering the true blow after the false one, "—your name's on the list."

The world went very quiet, the way it did sometimes before it went very loud.

"Mine," Orsen said.

"Witness for the prosecution, it says. Whatever that means when the accused hasn't even been charged proper." He shrugged, helpless. "Rell wants keepers talking about boats they've seen at night. Foreign lights. That kind of thing. And everyone on this coast knows Vey used your point as an anchorage back in the day."

Everyone on this coast knew, yes. Everyone on this coast also knew that Tomas Vey had once rowed through a burning squall to pull Elias Delacroix out of the water, and had never once mentioned it afterward, and had let Orsen learn it from a dying man's confession instead of his own mouth. Some debts you paid in money. Some you paid in silence. Some, apparently, you paid by perjuring yourself before a magistrate while a storm tried to drown the whole coastline.

"Thank you, Petersen," she said. "Take your crate up to the tower and rest. You shouldn't cross back until the wind shifts."

"And you, Keeper?"

"I have a boy to feed and a conscience to argue with. Neither task improves with company."

* * *

She fed him, as promised — barley soup with the last of the salt pork, bread baked that morning because baking kept her hands busy and busy hands kept her from thinking, mostly. Jude ate three helpings and hummed, and she let the humming go on longer than usual because it was the sound of a child who felt safe, and she was beginning to understand how rare and fragile a sound that was, and how soon it might be taken off the air.

Afterward they sat by the fire, and she took out the storm glass and held it to the lamplight, and Jude leaned against her side to look.

"It's worse," he observed.

"Considerably worse."

"Is that bad? For the boat?"

"For everything, if it keeps on." She turned the glass slowly. Inside, the crystals had thickened to a solid pale murk, dense as fog pressed into a bottle. "When I was a girl, my mother kept one of these, and before the great gale of '81 it looked just so. The whole sky fell into that bottle two days before the sky fell on us."

Jude was quiet. Then, in a small voice: "Tomas used to read the weather off the water. He'd taste the wind and know things." His fingers found the hem of her sleeve and held it, the way he did when a memory got too big. "Is he cold in the cell, do you think?"

The question went through her like a splinter. She thought of Tomas Vey — broad, sun-cured, forever laughing at something half a second after everyone else understood the joke — sitting in one of the magistrate's stone rooms with the damp coming through the walls.

"Tomas has been cold plenty of times in his life and complained through every minute of it," she said. "Which means he knows how to survive it. Men who complain are men still planning to be alive to do it better."

That was, she reflected, a terrible comfort, and exactly the sort Elias would have approved of.

Jude nodded slowly, accepting it. Then he yawned — the enormous, boneless yawn of a boy finally warm — and his eyes drifted shut for a moment before snapping open with effort.

"I can stay up," he said. "In case you need counting waves."

"There's nobody on earth better at it," Orsen agreed. "But tonight I'll take the count. Off to bed. Jonas needs his sleep too, whoever he turns out to be."

He went, dragging his blanket and pausing at the door. "Keeper Orsen?" — he had never managed simply *Orsen*, nor simply *Keeper*. "If the storm's as big as the bottle says, will the light still work?"

"The light always works. That's the arrangement. The storm rages, and the light doesn't care."

He seemed satisfied with this and padded off, and she heard him settle into the window seat where he'd insisted on sleeping since the first month — *so I can count waves even with my eyes closed* — and go quiet.

* * *

Alone, Orsen built the fire higher than thrift allowed and sat with Elias's old logbook open on her knees, not reading it, just resting her hands on it the way one rests a hand on a shoulder of an old friend. Outside, the wind had begun its work in earnest, moaning down the chimney and setting the tower's ancient timbers ticking like a cooling stove.

Two roads, then. She laid them out before the fire like tracks on snow.

The first: keep Jude hidden through the storm, answer Rell's summons with careful half-truths, hope the trial passed and the search slackened. A road paved with *hope*, which Elias had called the cheapest building material on the coast.

The second: the boat. Tomorrow. First light. Hand the boy to Petersen and the sea and the wide free world beyond the harbor bar, and stand in front of a magistrate the following day and swear she had never seen Tomas Vey's face in her life.

Her eyes went to the shelf above the fire, to the tin box where she kept what remained of her promises: Elias's ring, her mother's thimble, and the note in Tomas Vey's hurried hand — *forget the face* — which she could no more throw away than she could throw away her own heartbeat.

Forget the face. As if she could. As if she hadn't spent four months memorizing it anew, one freckle at a time, in the face that slept in her parlor window and counted waves in his dreams.

The fire popped. The wind rose a full register. And from the mantel, the storm glass — her honest parishioner, her truth-teller, the one thing in the house that never flattered — gave up the last of its clarity and went white as bone.

Orsen sat very still.

Then, through the roar of the rising wind, thin and impossible and unmistakable, she heard it: hoofbeats on the harbor road. More than one horse. Coming up the point.

She rose, snuffed the lamp in the window seat's shadow, and went to wake Jonas.
