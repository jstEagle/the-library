## 18. What the Keeper Swears

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a constable so young his uniform still smelled of the tailor's shop. He stood on the gravel below the lighthouse with his cap in his hands and would not look at the tower, as though it might be watching him.

"Keeper Delacroix. Magistrate Halden requires your statement. At the courthouse. Before noon." He paused, then added, with the awkwardness of a boy reciting someone else's cruelty, "You'll be sworn in proper."

Orsen looked at him for a long moment. She was holding a lamp wick-trimmer in one hand and her dignity in the other, and she found the second heavier.

"And if I'm trimming my wicks at noon?"

The constable blinked. "I don't— I expect you'd best trim them after, ma'am."

"Very sensible of you," Orsen said. "Tell the magistrate I'll come."

He went down the path at nearly a run, and Orsen stood a while watching the fog lift off the harbor, thinking about what it means to be summoned by a man who has never once climbed her stairs.

Behind her, up in the gallery, Jude was counting waves. He had been counting them since before light, when the swell came long and slow out of the southeast — the kind of sea that made old sailors say *weather coming* and spit over the rail. She could hear his voice through the glass, faint and rhythmic as a psalm: *forty-one, forty-two, forty-three.* The boy counted waves the way other children said their prayers, and she had stopped correcting him, because there was no telling which of them the ritual comforted more.

She went inside and put the kettle on.

* * *

Halden had dressed the courthouse for occasion. There were two lamps lit despite the morning, and a clerk at a side table with paper squared before him like place settings, and the magistrate himself behind the bench wearing his better coat — the gray one with the silver buttons that Orsen suspected he polished himself on evenings when no one loved him.

Which was unkind, she reminded herself, and probably true.

"Keeper Delacroix." He gestured to the witness chair as if offering her tea. "Thank you for coming. I trust the journey wasn't troublesome."

"It's four miles by road and the road is mud," Orsen said, sitting. "It was troublesome."

Someone in the gallery — three rows of benches, half-filled with fishermen who had invented reasons to be indoors — made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh. Halden's jaw tightened pleasantly.

"You've been keeper of the Harbor Point light for eleven years."

"I have."

"Appointed upon your husband's death."

"Yes."

"A position," Halden said, drawing the word out like taffy, "which is held at the pleasure of the Coastal Authority, and which carries with it certain obligations. To report. To record. To testify truthfully when asked." He folded his hands. "You understand these obligations."

"I keep a logbook," Orsen said. "Eleven years of it. Every vessel sighted, every weather change, every lamp rotation. You're welcome to read any page of it. Some of the weather entries are quite poetic, if I say so myself."

More coughing from the gallery. A fisherman named Aldous Voss appeared to be dying of something contagious near the back.

Halden waited for quiet the way a man waits for a tide. Then: "Mrs. Delacroix. On the night of the ninth of October, did a boy come to your door?"

The room changed temperature. Orsen felt it — the way the gallery stopped breathing, the clerk's pen hovering above the paper, the fog-gray light through the tall windows seeming to lean in.

"There was a storm on the ninth of October," she said. "A bad one. The Fritjof nearly went on the rocks; you'll find it in my log. In storms, people come to my door. Fishermen, mostly. Once a customs man who'd lost his supper to the sea and most of his dignity besides."

"A boy," Halden said. "Foreign-looking. Dark-haired. Perhaps ten or eleven years of age."

Orsen considered her answer with the same care she gave a wick — not too much, not too little, trimmed exactly to the flame.

"Magistrate," she said, "do you know how many children live along this coast?"

"I'm asking about one."

"And I'm telling you what I know about all of them. Children come to the lighthouse when boats are late. Wives come when husbands are late. Last winter a whole family came when their chimney caught fire and their house filled with smoke, and they stayed three days, and Mrs. Brandt's youngest broke a teacup I'd owned since my wedding, and nobody has ever been arrested over any of it." She let that sit. "That is what a lighthouse is for, Mr. Halden. It is not a watchtower for the Coastal Authority. It is a light, and under a light people are simply — visible. All of them. Equally."

The gallery had gone very still again, but it was a different stillness now. Not fear. Attention.

Halden leaned forward. "I have testimony," he said quietly, "that you were seen in Maren Ostergaard's kitchen with such a boy. Feeding him. That he slept under your roof for days. That you passed him off as a nephew."

"Maren Ostergaard feeds everyone who sits down in her kitchen," Orsen said. "If feeding people is now evidence of a crime, half this county should be transported. Including the magistrate, if his housekeeper is to be believed, and she makes a plum cake that would convict a saint."

This time even the clerk coughed.

"Madam Keeper." Halden's voice dropped into its official register, the one he used for sentencing. "You are under oath. I am asking you directly, plainly, and for the last time: is there a boy living at Harbor Point? Yes or no."

And here was the moment Orsen had rehearsed on the walk over — four muddy miles of rehearsal, arguing with herself in her own head, laying out the truth and the lie side by side like two fish on a slab and trying to decide which one she could gut without ruining the knife.

The truth was: yes. Yes, of course, yes. Jude had slept under her roof for six weeks. He had learned to wind the clockwork and polish the lens and name the clouds. He had a gap where his left eyetooth had been and a habit of standing too close when he was frightened, pressing his shoulder against her arm the way a boat presses against its mooring in rough water. She had promised Tomas Vey any debt repaid, and the debt had arrived at her door weighing perhaps seventy pounds and carrying nothing in the world but another person's handwriting.

The lie would be simple. No, Your Honor. And then she would lose everything slowly instead of quickly — the boy found anyway, the informants already circling, Halden's men with their lanterns and their lists.

But there was a third thing. She had realized it somewhere around mile two, splashing past the Brandt fence line. It was not truth and not lie, and it hung between them like the fog itself.

"No," Orsen Delacroix said.

The word fell into the room and everyone heard its shape.

"Not at present," she continued, before the silence could curdle, "and I will tell you precisely why, since we are all sworn here today and precision seems to be the fashion." She turned in the chair to face the gallery — the fishermen, the two shopkeepers, the customs officer pretending to read a newspaper upside down. "On the night of the ninth of October there was a storm, and in the morning there was wreckage, and among the wreckage I found debris from a foreign vessel. I reported it. Check the log. I also found footprints, cold, going north along the shingle, and no body, and no boy, because whoever had walked that beach had walked away from it under his own power, hours before I ever reached the sand. If a boy was ever at Harbor Point, he is not there now. You may search my station from cellar to lantern. I invite it. Bring tea; it takes an hour."

Halden stared at her. "You're splitting hairs, madam. You just admitted—"

"I admitted nothing except that my grammar is better than yours," Orsen said. "*Is there a boy living at Harbor Point?* No. There is not. Whether there *was* — well. That depends entirely, doesn't it, on whether sheltering a shipwrecked child on a night of hurricane is a crime in this county. And if it is, I'd like to see the statute written down. I'd like to read it aloud in this room. I'd like every mother and grandmother and fishwife on this coast to hear what the law calls a crime, so we all know where we stand."

The gallery was not coughing anymore. Aldous Voss was on his feet, and it took the constable three tries to sit him back down.

* * *

They adjourned for an hour. Orsen ate bread and hard cheese on the courthouse steps, sharing it with a gull she judged honest enough, and watched the southeastern sky the way she had watched it every day for a week.

Storm glass falling. Barometer sagging like a tired shoulder. The fishing fleet knew it — half the gallery this morning had been men who should have been at their nets, staying close to shore, mending gear they didn't need to mend. Everyone on this coast could feel what the instruments confirmed: the last storm of the season was assembling itself far out over the water, and it would be a big one, and it would arrive within days.

Which meant the last boat south would leave within days. And the magistrate knew it too. She could see it in the way he kept glancing at the harbor, quick and proprietary, a man doing arithmetic with other people's lives.

Tomas was still in the cell behind the courthouse. She had seen the window — high, barred, facing the water, because Halden had a sense of humor after all. She had not been permitted to speak to him. She had stood in the street below it anyway, on some pretext about adjusting her boot, and had heard, faintly, a man whistling. An old tune. One Henrik used to hum, back when Henrik was alive, back when Tomas Vey had hauled her husband half-drowned out of the December water and carried him two miles through sleet and never once let him touch the ground.

*Any debt,* she had told him, weeping with relief in the doorway of the fish-house while Henrik shivered under blankets. *Anything you ever ask.*

He had asked for nothing for nineteen years.

Then a note. Four words. *Forget the face.* And a boy.

* * *

The afternoon session went worse for Halden, though he was too careful to show it.

He called the customs officer, who testified to having seen "a juvenile of foreign aspect" near the Ostergaard kitchen and could not say more. He called a shopkeeper who testified to having sold, or possibly not sold, bread to persons unknown. He called the informant — and Orsen watched the room when the man came in, watched the fishermen turn their heads with the slow unified motion of a pod of whales regarding a shark.

His name was Corben Skiles. He kept the toll ledger at the east quay and had the moist, apologetic look of a man who informed for the coin and hated himself in installments. He repeated what he'd apparently already told Halden privately: the boy at Maren's, the boy at the lighthouse, the boy seen on the supply boat dock, and — this part he said slowly, savoring it — the boy seen once in the company of *Tomas Vey's relations*, before the roundups began. A cousin's child, he thought. From across the water. The Vey connection itself.

Something went through the courtroom like current through wire. Orsen kept her face still, but inside her the word landed where it had been waiting to land ever since she'd first read the note's handwriting and felt the floor of memory tilt beneath her.

*Tomas's kin.* She had suspected. Now it was said aloud, in a room full of witnesses, and there was no unsaying it. Whatever blood ran in Jude ran through a family Orsen owed, and Halden had just connected the threads in public — the fisherman in his cell, the boy in the wind, the keeper under oath lying through her excellent grammar.

Halden dismissed Skiles with something almost like tenderness, which told Orsen the man would be paid well and protected poorly.

Then the magistrate turned back to her, and his voice lost its ceremony altogether.

"Mrs. Delacroix. I am going to offer you something, and I want you to hear all of it before you refuse, because I suspect you intend to refuse and you strike me as a woman who likes to finish things." He came around the bench — improper, theatrical, effective — and stood close enough that only she and the clerk could hear. "Confess to harboring. Pay the fine. Lose the post, yes, but no prison, no charges against Vey beyond what he's already earned. Cooperate fully — tell me where the boy has gone — and I will treat it as mitigation. You are, by all accounts, a faithful servant of this station for eleven years. I would prefer to end this cleanly."

"And if I don't confess?"

"Then I charge you with obstruction, and perjury before this court, and whatever else fits, and I take the lighthouse anyway, and Vey goes on the transport list, and the boy—" He shrugged, and the shrug was the cruelest thing in the room. "Boys get found, madam. Especially before a storm, when every boat is counted twice."

Orsen looked up at him. Eleven years of solitary nights had given her a great many skills, and one of the least useful and most cherished was the ability to hold silence until it became unbearable for everyone but herself. She deployed it now, letting the courtroom hear nothing but the tick of the clerk's clock, until Halden shifted his weight like a man standing on ice.

"Magistrate," she said at last, mildly, "when my husband drowned, it took the sea three days to give him back. I sat on that point and watched the water the whole time, because I couldn't help it, and because the light had to be kept, and because grief and duty look identical from a distance. I have been threatened by superior officers, embezzled by supply agents, struck blind by lightning through the lantern glass, and lectured by four successive magistrates on the proper scope of my office." She rose. The constable moved to stop her; the look she gave him could have trimmed a wick at forty paces, and he sat back down. "You will not get a confession from me, Mr. Halden, because there is nothing to confess. I kept a light. People came to it. What they were fleeing is none of my business and shouldn't be any of yours. Swear me out or charge me, but do it today, because there is weather coming and my lamp wants filling, and unlike some institutions in this county, mine has never once failed this harbor."

She gathered her coat. At the door she paused, and — because she was, as Halden had observed, a woman who liked to finish things — she turned back.

"One more item for your records, since your man Skiles raised it. Tomas Vey pulled my husband out of the sea in '74. Carried him through the sleet with broken ribs of his own. Never asked me for a thing until this autumn, and whatever he asked for, I'd have done it with both hands and thanked him for the privilege. Put that in your ledger. Mitigation, I believe, is the fashionable word."

The doors boomed shut behind her.

Inside, in the ringing silence, Halden stood a long moment by the bench. Then he said, very quietly, to no one in particular:

"Charge her. Everything that fits."

Outside, on the steps, Orsen breathed the salt wind and looked southeast, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise, and calculated hours.

Three days, maybe less, until the storm.

Two days, maybe less, until the last boat.

And somewhere between the two, a cell window with a man whistling behind it, and a promise coming due at compound interest.

She started walking. There was packing to do, and a lens to leave spotless, and — if the fates were generous and the tide was right — a bargain to strike with a smuggler named Ostergaard's cousin, who owed Maren a debt of his own.

Behind her, in the high barred window, the whistling had stopped.

Tomas Vey had heard her boots on the cobbles, and he knew that walk, and he knew what it meant when Orsen Delacroix walked away from a courthouse without looking back.

She was going to keep her word.

God help anyone who stood in the doorway of it.
