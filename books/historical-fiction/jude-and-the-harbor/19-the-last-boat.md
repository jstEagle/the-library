## 19. The Last Boat

The storm glass had fallen so low that the liquid in it seemed ashamed of itself, a thin gray thread trembling in the sealed tube above Orsen's desk. She had checked it three times that morning, as though it might reconsider. It did not. Storms at sea are honest in a way people rarely manage; they tell you exactly what they intend to do to you.

"Is it bad?" Jude asked from the doorway. He had learned to read her silences the way other boys read weather vanes.

"It's honest," Orsen said. "Which is worse."

Today was the day. She had known it since Halden left two nights ago with his warrant unsigned and his pride badly bruised, since she had stood before him on her own doorstep and sworn — hand raised, voice steady, heart hammering like surf — that she had never seen the boy he described, that no child slept in her lighthouse, that the face in his papers meant nothing to her. What the Keeper Swears, indeed. She had looked the magistrate in his watery blue eyes and lied with the composure of a woman who had spent eleven years telling ships *all is well* when all was very often not.

The lie would not hold through another visit. And there would be another visit. Halden had said so without saying it, the way officials do, adjusting his hat and mentioning, almost idly, that the supply boat would be making its rounds again within the fortnight.

So it would not be the supply boat. It would be the last fishing boat — Maren's *Katrine*, due at the point this evening on her final run before the season closed and the harbor froze its business for the winter. Tomas had arranged it from his cell, somehow, through a chain of cousins and debt-holders and one deaf fish-wife who communicated entirely in gestures and carried messages better than any telegraph line on the coast. The plan had arrived yesterday, folded inside a mackerel delivery: *Katrine. Dusk. North hold. Tell no one who cannot keep water in a net.*

"You're doing the face again," Jude said. "The one where your mouth is still but your eyes are running around the room."

"My eyes are packing," Orsen said. "There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"The mouth lies," she said. "The eyes just get ready."

* * *

They spent the afternoon pretending it was an ordinary day, which is the hardest work two frightened people can do.

Jude counted waves. He always counted waves when something sat heavy on him — she had learned that in the first week, back when he wouldn't speak of the night his mother put the note in his coat and pushed him up the lighthouse path. One hundred and fourteen waves between the point and the far buoy before he finally asked the question.

"If I go," he said, "will Tomas come too?"

Orsen kept her hands busy with the lamp's brass fittings, though the lamp needed nothing. "He'll follow when he can. That was the bargain."

"That's what adults say when the answer is no."

She turned and looked at him then — this boy, ten years old, who had arrived at her door a bundle of wet wool and silence and had slowly unpacked himself into someone with opinions about her cooking and an uncanny memory for faces and a way of asking questions that landed like small stones dropped down a well. Eleven years she had lived alone with a dead man's photograph and a promise she'd thought was decorative. Now there were boots by her door two sizes too small and a boy who knew her better than her husband had.

"The answer is not no," she said carefully. "But Tomas is in a cell, and cells don't open because you knock politely. He got himself arrested to buy us time, and wasting that time would be rude."

Jude considered this. "You could break him out. I've read about it."

"You've read about it in a book where the hero has a horse and the jailer has a nap."

"I have a boat."

"You have a boat," she agreed. "One boat. Which we will use for its intended purpose, which is you."

"And you?"

The question hung there. She had been avoiding it with the skill of long practice, but Jude had a fisherman's patience; he could simply stand there, hook baited, until something bit.

"I'm the keeper of this light," Orsen said. "The light doesn't leave."

"The light isn't yours anymore. You told me. They took it off you when you wouldn't say anything."

"They haven't taken the keys yet."

"Keys aren't the same as belonging." He said it plainly, the way children deliver truths they don't yet know are sharp. "Maren says a thing can belong to you even after it stops belonging to itself."

"Maren," Orsen muttered, "says a great deal for a woman who lives forty minutes across the water."

* * *

Dusk came early, the way it does before a storm — the sky not darkening so much as being lowered, like a lid on a pot. By four o'clock the gulls had gone inland, which old sailors call the sea emptying its pockets before the robbery. By five the wind had found its voice.

Orsen packed in the space between the lamp's evening trim and the boat's arrival, and packing turned out to be a shorter ceremony than she expected. A change of clothes for the boy. Bread, cheese, the last of the dried apples. The letter from Maren with the address of the free port written in a hand that looped like gulls' tracks. And then she paused, holding the drawer that held everything else: Erik's photograph. Her wedding ring, worn thin. The logbooks — nine years of them, weather and shipping and small notes in the margins. *Fog thick as wool. Trimmed wick twice.* The record of a life measured in oil and attention.

And one more thing. The locket. She had stopped wearing it years ago, but she had never stopped keeping it where she could find it, and now she wrapped it in flannel and put it in the bottom of the bag where the apples had been, next to Jude's few treasures: a carved bird, three interesting stones, and a second note from Tomas that he would not let her read and would not let go of.

"What's in that one?" she'd asked once.

"Instructions," Jude said. "For if things go wrong."

"What sort of instructions?"

"Boat ones," he said, and pressed his mouth shut with the finality of a hatch cover.

* * *

The *Katrine* came into the point at half past six, running dark, her lantern hooded, which against every rule of the sea made Orsen's professional soul itch like wool. Maren herself was at the tiller, which was wrong — the skipper should be aboard, the crew should be hauling, everyone should be where the plan had put them — and Orsen read the wrongness before the bow even touched the rocks.

"There's weather coming that makes this look like a rehearsal," Maren called over the wind, scrambling down to the shingle. She was sixty and built like a capstan, and she seized Orsen's arm with fingers that had spent a lifetime arguing with ropes. "Skipper's daughter's got fever. He stayed ashore. So it's me and my nephew Halvard and God, and God's got other appointments tonight."

"Then turn back," Orsen said. "We wait—"

"We don't wait. There is no waiting. There's tonight or there's spring, and spring is a rumor." Maren looked past her, up the path, and her weathered face did something complicated. "Is that him? Is that the boy?"

Jude came down the rocks carrying his bag like a man reporting for work, chin up, and Orsen saw Maren take in his face — the set of the jaw, the color of the hair — and saw the recognition move through the old woman like a swell under ice.

"Well," Maren said quietly. "Well, then. No wonder they want him." She crossed herself, quick and businesslike, the way one salutes a flag. "Your father pulled my husband out of the water once. Off the Skerries, in '81. Some debts float."

* * *

Getting Jude aboard was easy; the boy moved on boats the way other children moved on stairs. Getting herself to agree to go was the harder labor, and it was Maren and Jude together who accomplished it, in an ambush so gentle Orsen didn't feel the hooks until she was caught.

"Halvard can't manage the north hold alone in that chop," Maren said. "I need hands."

"I'm not crew," Orsen said.

"You're the only person on this coast who can read a barometer and tie a bowline, and you're standing there telling me you're luggage." Maren snorted. "Besides. You think I'm leaving you here for Halden? He comes tomorrow with the supply boat and a signed paper, and you tell your pretty lie again, and this time he checks the loft. Then what? You lose the light anyway, and you lose the boy, and for what — for a job they already gave away?"

"They haven't given it away."

"They gave it away Tuesday," Maren said, not unkindly. "To young Iverson. My sister-in-law's neighbor heard it from the harbormaster's clerk, who talks more than a kettle." She held out her rope-roughened hand. "The light's done with you, Orsen. Doesn't mean you're done with lights."

And Jude — Jude simply took her other hand, the one not holding the bag, and looked up at her with the expression he wore when counting waves, patient and grave, and said, "You promised my father any debt repaid. But nobody ever promised *you* anything. That's not fair. Debts should go both ways or not at all."

"That's terrible economics," Orsen said.

"It's fair," he said. "I learned it from you."

She stood on the shingle one moment longer — the wind pulling her shawl, the lighthouse behind her throwing its great slow wheel of light across the water, *there and gone, there and gone*, the rhythm she had kept alive through eleven winters and one broken heart. She looked at it the way you look at a grave that has also somehow been a home.

"All right," she said, to the tower, to the boy, to the promise she had made to a man eleven years drowned. "All right."

* * *

The north hold smelled of fish, tar, and honesty, and Jude was tucked into it among the crates with a blanket and strict instructions to stay silent as cargo. Orsen climbed to the deck and took the lines Halvard handed her without being asked, and Maren put the *Katrine*'s head to the sea, and the last fishing boat of the season ran north into the rising dark.

The storm found them within the hour, as storms do when they've made an appointment. It was not the great one yet — that was still gathering somewhere out over the deep, the barometers all along the coast agreeing on doom — but it was a serious, working blow, and the *Katrine* took it the way old boats do: complaining constantly, giving ground nowhere.

Orsen worked the lines until her shoulders burned and found, to her surprise, that her body remembered more than her mind did. Erik had taught her knots in their first year, laughing at her fury; she hadn't touched a sheet in a decade, and here her hands were, doing the old arithmetic. The sea, at least, forgave nothing and forgot nothing, and there was a comfort in dealing with an adversary so straightforward.

Halvard, a wide quiet man of thirty with his aunt's hands, nodded at her work. "Keeper's hands," he shouted over the wind.

"Ex-keeper's hands," she shouted back.

"No such thing," he said. "It's like being ex-married. Ask anybody."

Halfway across the open water, between the point and the shoals, the wind dropped for a single minute — the strange held-breath pause some storms carry, like a pickpocket's apology — and in that minute Jude came up out of the hold. He scrambled to the rail beside her, salt-sprayed and wide-eyed, and together they watched the lightning walk along the horizon ahead of them, lighting up the free port's direction like a lantern swung at the end of the world.

"Count the seconds," Orsen said. "Between flash and thunder. That tells you how far off it is."

"One... two... three—" Thunder. "Three miles."

"Good. Keep counting. If it gets to one, we go below."

He counted. He was good at it. He counted waves and seconds and the flashes of lightning, and somewhere in the counting he stopped being afraid, because a boy who can number a thing owns a piece of it. She watched him and thought: *Tomas, you impossible man, wherever they're keeping you, whatever they're taking from you down in that cell — look what you sent me. Look what you trusted me with. I hope I am worth the freight.*

And then, because the sea allows no one to think pleasant thoughts uninterrupted, the lookout cry came down from Halvard:

"Lights! Two points off the starboard bow — riding light, no colors!"

Maren was at the rail in an instant, spyglass up. The pause that followed was longer than any storm pause.

"Coastal patrol," she said at last, lowering the glass, and her voice had gone flat and hard as a keel. "Sloop, armed, running a picket line across the channel. They're not chasing weather tonight. They're looking for something." She turned, and her eyes found Orsen, then dropped to the hatch of the north hold, where a boy was counting lightning.

"Or," Maren said slowly, "they're looking for someone. And we're the only sail on this water."

Behind them, faint and faithful, the lighthouse beam swept the sea one last time — *there and gone, there and gone* — like a hand raised in farewell, or in warning.

"Get the boy ready to run," Orsen said, untying her apron. "If they board us, I'll do the talking. I've had practice lying to important men lately, and I find I'm rather good at it."
