## 20. After the Water

The storm ended the way a quarrel ends between two people too tired to continue it—not with resolution, but with a kind of embarrassed quiet, both parties retreating to separate corners and pretending nothing had happened.

Orsen Delacroix stood at the rail of the *Petrel* and watched the sea perform its morning penance. The water that had spent two days trying to murder everything afloat now lay flat as spilled milk, glittering apologetically in the sun, strewn with the debris of its tantrum: fence rails, a shutter, half a fish crate, one solitary boot turning slowly in the current like a small drowning philosopher.

"You can stop gripping that rail," Maren said beside her. "The boat's not going anywhere without us. Captain Voss has an attachment to his vessel that I'd call unhealthy if it weren't so useful."

"I'm not gripping."

"You're gripping. Your knuckles have opinions."

Orsen loosened her fingers, one at a time, discovering that each of them ached in a distinct and personal way, as though her hands had been through the storm individually rather than collectively. Two days out from the harbor point. Two days since she had stood on the gallery of a lighthouse she no longer owned and watched a fishing boat carry a boy into the gray mouth of the gale, and had turned back to her lamp because the lamp still needed tending, and had kept it tended through the worst night of the season because that was what her hands knew how to do, even for an institution that had disowned her.

She had sworn to the magistrate nothing. She had said, *I know nothing of the boy,* and the words had tasted like copper, and they had been true in the only way that mattered—she knew everything of the boy, which was a different thing entirely.

"He'll be there," Maren said.

"You don't know that."

"I know Tomas Vey arranged a crossing in the teeth of a storm season with a forged manifest, a bribed harbormaster's clerk, and a cousin who owes him eleven years of favors. That man does not arrange things and then fail to attend them. He'd sooner miss his own funeral."

"His own funeral would be easy to miss. He'd probably attend someone else's instead."

Maren laughed—a short, surprised sound, the laugh of a woman who had not expected to find anything funny this far from shore—and then pressed her lips together as though apologizing to the sea for it.

They stood together while the *Petrel* wallowed eastward. Behind them, the free port of Skarven did not yet exist except as rumor and a smudge on the horizon; ahead of them, behind them, everything was in motion. Orsen had spent thirty-one years on a fixed point of land whose entire purpose was to stay exactly where it was, and she found that being in transit agreed with her less than she'd hoped and more than she'd expected. The lighthouse had been hers to keep. Jude was hers to find.

Below them, on deck, the other passengers were emerging from the hold like cautious animals after a forest fire. There were nine of them, and Orsen had catalogued every one during the storm out of pure self-preservation. Old Halvard the cooper, who had spent the worst of the weather cheerfully singing to the bilge pump as though encouraging a slow child. A widow named Fru Sandvik with three trunks and no husband, which explained the trunks. Two brothers from the northern islands who hadn't spoken a word to anyone, or apparently to each other, in two days. A young woman with ink-stained fingers who claimed to be a seamstress and read maps the way other people read letters.

And one magistrate's clerk, or a man wearing the posture of one, sitting apart with his hat over his eyes, whom Orsen had been watching with the patient attention she had once given the horizon.

"That man," she murmured to Maren, "has been asleep for six hours with his hat perfectly straight."

"People sleep strangely."

"People do not sleep strangely in the same position through a following swell. I've watched sailors try. His hat hasn't moved. Either he is dead—and he ate breakfast, so no—or he is listening."

Maren considered this. "To what?"

"To us. To everyone. To whether anyone aboard uses the word *harbor point* with an unfortunate amount of feeling."

"You're suspicious because you have reason to be suspicious," Maren said. "That doesn't make you wrong, but it makes you exhausting."

"Yes," Orsen agreed placidly. "It's one of the few consolations of my situation."

* * *

The situation, taken as a whole inventory, was as follows:

One lighthouse: forfeited. One wage: terminated by letter, delivered with bureaucratic tenderness by a harbor official who could not meet her eye and who left the envelope slightly damp, as though it had been wept on by the department. One house attached to the aforementioned lighthouse: also gone, though the department had graciously allowed her forty-eight hours to remove her personal effects, which amounted to a sea chest, three lamps, a kettle, her husband's coat, a tin of buttons, and a locked drawer containing fourteen years of logbooks that officially belonged to the Crown but unofficially contained her entire adult life written in a keeper's hand, tide by tide, night by night, and which she had packed beneath her petticoats without a flicker of remorse.

One debt to Tomas Vey: paid, she had thought, in full—the boy delivered to the boat, the promise honored, the account closed. Except that accounts, she was learning, did not close. They simply changed currency. She had given Tomas her silence before the magistrate, and he had given her a scrap of paper with a latitude and longitude on it and four words in his blunt fisherman's hand: *Skarven. The blue door.*

One boy: somewhere ahead of her, alive—she had to believe it, the way she believed in the lamp, without evidence required on a nightly basis—with a woman named Signe Aune aboard a boat called the *Gannet*, bound for the same free port, carrying a locket that held a face Orsen had not been able to look at directly for reasons she had not yet confessed to anyone, including herself.

One family of choice, forming itself around her with the unsettling efficiency of driftwood collecting against a hull: Maren, who had quit her post at the harbor office the same week Orsen lost hers, on principle, loudly, and with references.

"You're smiling," Maren observed.

"I'm inventorying."

"You inventorie with a smile when the inventory includes me."

"It includes you under *assets*," Orsen said. "Don't let it go to your head."

* * *

They sighted Skarven at midday, and it was smaller than hope and larger than fear, which is the correct size for a place you are pinning your life to. A horseshoe of gray stone wrapped around a harbor so sheltered it looked like something drawn by a child who loved boats: masts tilted at lazy angles, tar smoke rising, laundry strung between buildings as though the whole town had decided that if it was going to be a refuge it might as well be a comfortable one.

The blue door was not difficult to find. It was, in fact, aggressively blue—a color that had clearly been chosen by someone who wanted to be able to say *the blue door* to strangers forever—and it stood at the end of a lane that smelled of salt, bread, and hemp rope, which Orsen thought might be the most honest combination of smells a town could manage.

Tomas Vey opened it before she knocked.

He had aged, or the season had aged him, or possibly the cell he'd sat in for eleven days had done the work on his behalf; there was a new seam of white in his beard and a stiffness in his left shoulder that he was pretending did not exist. He looked at her for a long moment, taking in the sea chest, the coat over her arm, Maren behind her, the general condition of a woman who had walked out of her own life carrying everything that mattered in one hand.

"You look," he said at last, "like the east side of a breakwater."

"You look like something the tide refused."

"Then we're both home." He stepped back and held the door. "Come in before the whole lane sees me being sentimental. I have a reputation."

"You have a warrant."

"Had. The magistrate's reach ends at territorial waters, and the magistrate gets seasick in a rowboat. In here, I'm merely a fisherman with a suspicious number of cousins."

The room inside was warm and cluttered and alive in the way rooms become when more than one person's life is happening in them: nets coiled in a corner that had never seen a net before, a woman's shawl over a chair, a boy's boots—too large for him, obviously borrowed, obviously being grown into by force of will—by the hearth.

Orsen saw the boots first. Then she saw the boy.

Jude was sitting cross-legged on the floor with a mug of something, talking earnestly to Signe Aune about waves—he was counting them, Orsen realized; he had counted them all the way across the open water, and he was reporting his total with the gravity of a treasury minister presenting accounts—and when he saw her he went absolutely still, the way he always did, the small body deciding whether this was a thing that could be trusted.

Then he was up, and the mug was somehow not broken, and his arms were around her waist and his face was in her coat, and Orsen Delacroix, who had kept a light burning alone for fourteen years and had thought her heart had been buried with her husband in the churchyard above the point, discovered that hearts are not buried so much as stored, and can be issued back out again without warning.

"You came," Jude said into her coat. "Signe said you'd come but people say things."

"I say things," Signe protested mildly.

"But you *know* things. It's different."

"I came," Orsen said. She rested her hand on the top of his head, where the hair grew in a stubborn double crown that no comb had ever negotiated successfully. "I will always—" She stopped, because promises made aloud in front of witnesses acquire weight, and she wanted this one to have all the weight it could carry. "I came. And I am not going back."

"Never?"

"Not even for the lamp."

Jude considered this with the seriousness he brought to waves and totals and other countable things. "The new keeper lets the light go dim sometimes," he reported. "On foggy nights. Petter saw it from the fish shed."

"The light goes *dim*," Orsen repeated, in the voice of a woman receiving news of a personal insult committed by the universe.

"On foggy nights."

"There is a specific procedure for foggy nights."

"I know," Jude said, patting her arm with the weary sympathy of one consoling the bereaved. "I told Petter you'd be angry."

* * *

Supper was fish stew, because it was a fishing town and it was Tuesday, and Orsen suspected it would be fish stew on every day ending in a vowel as well. They ate around a table that had been extended by the addition of a well-scrubbed plank, which suggested that the household had been expecting to grow and had planned accordingly. Orsen found this detail nearly unbearable in its kindness and therefore addressed her stew with great concentration until she could speak normally.

Tomas told the story of the cell with the economical humor of a man who had decided fear was a luxury item. Eleven days. Three interrogations, one of which had been conducted by a man eating a sausage throughout, which Tomas maintained was psychological warfare. A cellmate who snored in a minor key. Release secured when the informant's story collapsed—the informant being a harbor porter who had claimed to see Tomas landing foreign agents at midnight on a beach that, as Tomas's lawyer had gleefully demonstrated, was underwater at midnight that week.

"Drowned testimony," Signe said. "Fitting."

"The magistrate was livid," Tomas said, helping himself to bread. "Not at me. At the porter, at the season, at the weather, at the government for making him work past harvest. He signed my release the way a man slams a door."

"And the roundups?" Maren asked. "Here? Across the coast?"

Tomas's humor settled, the way silt settles when the current slackens. "Slowing. Not stopping. The storm sank two patrol boats, which did more for the cause of justice than any petition ever written. But there are men still in cells who shouldn't be, and names still on lists, and—" he glanced at Jude, and lowered his voice out of habit rather than necessity, because the boy was absorbed in constructing a tower of bread crusts—"and lists have a way of traveling. Which brings me to why I asked you here, Orsen, beyond stew and the pleasure of your face."

"You asked me here. I invited myself onto your doorstep."

"A doorstep is for anyone with feet. I mean the rest of it." He set down the bread. "There's a ship. The *Meridian*, out of Skarven, running to the free ports down the coast—proper ones, with courts that ask questions before they hang people. She sails in four days. Signe and the boy are booked."

Orsen put down her spoon.

"You're booked," Tomas said to her, gently. "Have been since the day you handed that child up the ladder of the *Gannet* in a gale. I don't pay debts halfway, Orsen Delacroix. You taught me that, though you didn't know you were teaching it, the night you told me a man who saves a life owns a piece of it. Well. I own a piece of yours, and I'm spending it on a berth."

"I have money," Orsen said stiffly. "Some. Severance."

"You have a department envelope with three months' wage in it and a smell of apology about it. Keep it. You'll want it where you're going, and you'll want your pride even more, so keep that too, and let me buy the ticket anyway. Consider it interest. Fourteen years of interest compounds impressively."

Across the table, Jude had abandoned the crust tower and was watching Orsen with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the careful face of someone waiting to learn whether a thing is safe to want.

"Four days," Orsen said.

"Four days," Tomas agreed. "Which gives you time for one thing before you sail, and I think you know what it is, because you've been carrying it since the harbor point and it's making you walk crooked."

Her hand went, without permission, to the pocket where the locket sat.

Signe Aune reached across and touched her wrist, once, lightly. "Whenever you're ready," she said. "But Orsen—it's time you looked at her properly. She looks like you."

* * *

Later—much later, when Jude was asleep in the loft with his boots on the wrong feet and the fire banked low—Orsen sat alone in the blue room with the locket in her palm.

Outside, the harbor muttered and clinked, a small domestic ocean. Somewhere a dog made a brief argument about something and lost. The light through the window came not from a lighthouse but from a lantern hung on the quay by an ordinary man for ordinary boats, and it was enough, and it was not, and would never be, hers.

She opened the locket.

The face inside was young—younger than Orsen had ever permitted herself to remember it—and it smiled with the particular crooked smile that Jude wore when he had counted something correctly and knew it. Her sister's face. Her sister, who had died believing Orsen would never forgive her, and who had sent her son to the one person in the world who owed a debt and kept promises, because some part of her sister had known, all those years, exactly what kind of woman Orsen Delacroix was, even when Orsen herself had forgotten.

"Oh, Lena," Orsen said quietly, to the empty room, to the harbor, to the boy sleeping overhead. "You could have knocked. All these years. You could have just knocked."

The floorboard creaked behind her, and Jude's voice came down from the loft, sleep-blurred and matter-of-fact:

"She said you'd say that."

Orsen closed her hand around the locket very tightly, and did not trust herself to answer, and upstairs the boy waited, patient as tide, for her to climb.
