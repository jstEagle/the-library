## 21. What Can Be Carried

The free port of Solvik did not look like much from the water — a crooked crescent of gray houses stacked against a hillside like dishes left to dry — but it smelled like safety, which was a thing Orsen Delacroix had not known a place could smell like. Tar and bread and fish and woodsmoke, all of it mixed together with an undertone of nothing-to-fear.

She stood on the quay with a single canvas bag at her feet and Jude pressed against her side like a barnacle, and she took inventory of her entire life.

One coat. One lamp — the little brass hand-lamp she'd carried up the tower stairs eleven thousand times, pried loose from its bracket on the last morning because she could not bear to leave it. One locket, worn against her sternum where it had lived since her wedding day. One boy, salt-crusted, exhausted, asleep on his feet even as he stood there.

And one debt, repaid. Though Tomas Vey was still three days behind them on the water, and she would not believe him safe until she saw his ugly hat with her own eyes.

"He'll come," Maren said beside her. Maren's arm had been hooked through Orsen's since they'd stumbled off the *Katrine* that morning, as though letting go might mean losing her again after thirty years. "Tomas has survived worse than a crossing. He survived my sister's cooking, and that man once ate an entire herring pie without complaint."

"That wasn't courage," said Halvard, Maren's husband, hauling the bag up onto his shoulder. "That was fear. There's a difference, and I've seen him display neither."

Orsen laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound that startled her own ears — and Jude stirred against her hip.

"Is this it?" he asked, blinking up at the hillside town. His voice still had the raw edge of the storm in it, three days out. "The whole place?"

"The whole place," Maren said proudly, as if she hadn't arrived only two years ahead of them. "Small enough that nobody asks questions, big enough that nobody answers them. Come on. The house is at the top of the hill, and yes, you have to climb it, no, I won't carry you, and yes," she added, cutting off Jude's next question with the precision of long practice, "there will be food when you get there."

"There's always food," Jude muttered to Orsen, but he took her hand anyway.

* * *

The house was small and crooked and leaned slightly toward the harbor, as if it too were curious about the boats. It belonged to a cousin of Halvard's who had gone south years ago, and it came furnished with everything a person needed and several things nobody could explain — including, inexplicably, four clocks in one room, none of them agreeing on the hour.

Jude discovered this within ten minutes and declared it the finest house he had ever seen.

"Listen," he said, standing in the middle of the parlor with his head cocked like a gull's. "They're arguing."

"They're always arguing," said Halvard. "Like most families."

Orsen heard something catch in Maren's breath at that — *families*, said so easily, so casually, with two strangers standing in the room — and watched Maren pretend very hard to be arranging something on a shelf.

Later, when Jude had been fed and installed in the narrow bed under the eaves (he had inspected the window first, checked its view of the harbor water, and pronounced it acceptable), Orsen found Maren in the kitchen, doing unnecessary things to a pot of tea.

"You didn't have to do all this," Orsen said.

"I know." Maren poured without looking up. "I wanted to. There's a difference, and after thirty years you'd think you'd know it." She pushed a cup across the table. "Sit down, Orsen. You've been standing for twenty years. I watched you do it. Sit."

Orsen sat. The chair was soft in the wrong places and she loved it immediately.

"It doesn't feel real yet," she admitted. "The lighthouse. I keep reaching for it. Like a tooth that's gone." She turned the cup in her hands. "Twenty years I kept that light. And they took it in an afternoon with a piece of paper."

"They took the *job*," Maren said. "The light's still there. Look out any window tonight; it'll be burning. Some other hand turns the key now, but the light doesn't care whose hand it is. Light never did care about anything. That's why I liked you — you cared enough for both."

Orsen looked into her tea. Outside, the gulls were conducting their eternal argument over the harbor, and somewhere below a boat horn sounded, low and patient, and she thought: *the sea does not know I am not coming back.* It seemed impossible. She had given it half her life, every night, and the sea had not even noticed her leaving.

"What can be carried," she said quietly, almost to herself. "Halvard asked me on the boat what I wanted to bring aboard, and I stood there in front of that tower and couldn't think of a single thing."

"And what did you end up carrying?"

Orsen touched the bag by her feet. The lamp. The locket. She nodded toward the ceiling, where faint creaking suggested a boy who was supposed to be sleeping was instead cataloguing clocks.

Maren smiled. "Aye. That's what I thought."

* * *

Jude did sleep, eventually — slept like the dead for two days, in fact, surfacing only for meals and one solemn inspection of the harbor from the doorstep before returning to bed. Dr. Andersson, the port physician who examined him at Maren's insistence, pronounced him "thin, bruised, and entirely too stubborn for his own good," and recommended feeding and patience, in that order.

On the third evening, Orsen found him on the doorstep, knees drawn up, watching the boats come in with the lamplight catching his face. She sat down beside him without a word. This had become their arrangement over the past months: silence offered freely, filled only if wanted.

He filled it.

"Do you think he got my wave?" he asked. "At the point. When the boat went past. I waved."

"He saw it," Orsen said. "He'd have told you off for leaning over the rail otherwise, and he didn't, so either he saw it or he was too seasick to notice, and Tomas Vey has never been seasick in his life. He's too stubborn. It would take the ocean a week just to convince him."

Jude considered this and found it sound. Then, quieter: "It's strange. Being somewhere the magistrate doesn't know my name."

"How's it feel?"

He thought about it seriously, the way he thought about everything — waves counted, tides measured, faces studied. "Lighter," he decided. "But also like I'm holding less. Which sounds backwards, but—" He struggled. "When you're hiding, you're carrying it all the time. Every knock. Every stranger on the road. You carry it in your shoulders." He rolled them experimentally, as if checking whether the weight was really gone. "Here I don't. But then my hands feel empty, and I don't know where to put them."

Orsen knew exactly what he meant. For twenty years her hands had known their work — trimming wicks, winding clockwork, logging weather in a ledger until her handwriting was the steadiest on the coast. Now the ledger was gone, the wicks were someone else's, and her hands woke in the night reaching for work that no longer existed.

"We'll find things to put in them," she said. "We're both of us between cargoes right now. That's all."

Jude nodded, accepting this, and then — because he was nine and grief and relief could only occupy a boy for so long — he pointed at the harbor and said, "Maren says there's a ship coming Thursday from the north, and Halvard says it carries timber, and Maren says it carries *something else*, and they argued about it over dinner like it was entertainment."

"It is entertainment," Orsen said. "For them. You get used to it."

"Maren says you used to argue like that. Her and you. Before."

"Before," Orsen agreed. A gull screamed past, offended by something. "Thirty years ago we shared a room above a chandlery and fought about whose turn it was to gut the mackerel. She always won. She cheated."

"She said you cheated."

"Maren," said Orsen, with great dignity, "has never once in her life acknowledged her own cheating. It is her single flaw."

Jude grinned — a real grin, wide and unguarded, the first she'd seen since the storm — and Orsen felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since a note appeared on her doorstep in the fog. *Forget the face.* Well. She had not forgotten any faces. She had carried every one of them across open water, and here they all were, arriving one by one.

* * *

Thursday brought the northern ship, and Friday brought fog, and Sunday brought the sound Orsen had been waiting three weeks to hear: heavy boots on the lane outside, a familiar uneven rhythm — the left foot landing harder than the right, an old injury from a net winch, from a night Orsen knew better than anyone alive.

She was out the door before she'd finished deciding to move.

Tomas Vey came up the hill with his sea bag over one shoulder and his ugly hat jammed low, and he stopped when he saw her, and for a moment the two of them simply stood in the lane like a pair of fence posts, thirty years of history rearranging itself around the fact of survival.

"You look terrible," Orsen said.

"You should see the other fellow," Tomas said. His voice was rougher than she remembered, and his beard had gone fully gray while hers had only gone halfway, which she intended to mention to him at some point when it could wound him properly. "The magistrate's cell had a leak. Dripped all night. I complained to the guards. They were very sympathetic."

"They let you go?"

"Let me go, escorted me to my boat, apologized for the inconvenience." He shrugged. "Turns out when the keeper of the harbor light swears she knows nothing, and there's no evidence but a boy nobody can find, and half the fishing fleet suddenly develops amnesia — well." He set down his bag. "There's only so long a man can be held for suspicion of being suspected."

"You swore nothing?"

"Swore nothing. Confirmed nothing. Complimented the magistrate's penmanship, which confused him greatly." His eyes went past her shoulder to the doorway, where Jude had appeared, frozen on the step. "Though I hear testimony was given elsewhere. Something about a certain keeper telling a certain magistrate exactly where he could store his warrant."

"I may have been clear," Orsen allowed.

"Tore it up in front of witnesses," Halvard reported from inside, with relish. "Dropped the pieces in his tea."

"In his *tea*?"

"It was weak tea," Orsen said. "He deserved better tea and worse news."

Tomas laughed — a great rolling sound that startled gulls off the roof — and then Jude came down the steps like a thrown rope, and Tomas caught him, and whatever was said in that embrace was said privately, though everyone on the hill heard the boy say "you came" twice and the old man answer "told you I would" three times.

* * *

They ate late that evening, all five of them crowded into the crooked kitchen, and the four clocks argued overhead through the whole meal, and Maren declared it the loudest her table had been since her sister visited, and everyone pretended not to notice her wiping her eyes at the potatoes.

Afterward, Tomas and Orsen ended up on the bench by the window with the dregs of the teapot between them, watching the harbor lights come up one by one along the crescent shore.

"Strange thing," Tomas said. "First night in months I haven't had anywhere to be before dawn."

"I was thinking the same."

"Were you." He glanced at her sidelong. "You'll hate it inside of a week. You'll start reorganizing Maren's kitchen by candlelight. You'll count the boats in and out and correct Halvard's arithmetic. You are, and always have been, a woman who needs a watch to stand."

"Probably," Orsen admitted. "But Tomas — I stood mine. Twenty years. I stood it alone, and I stood it well, and when it mattered I stood it against the magistrate himself." She turned her cup slowly. "Maybe what comes next doesn't need to be stood. Maybe it just needs to be... lived in. Like a house that leans a little."

Tomas was quiet a moment. Out on the water, a late ferry crossed the dark, its running lights doubled in the swell.

"The harbor master here owes me a favor," he said, in the elaborate casual voice of a man introducing a subject he'd been circling for days. "Says the pilot station's short-handed. Says they need someone who knows weather, reads water, keeps records nobody has to double-check. Doesn't pay much." A pause. "Pays in keeping."

Orsen looked at him.

"And they'll want two people eventually," Tomas went on, studying his tea with tremendous concentration. "Pilot work being what it is. Someone steady for the log book. Someone good with a lamp." Another pause, longer. "And there's a boy who counts waves like other children count sheep, and I've been thinking the harbor could use counting."

Orsen set down her cup. Her throat was tight and her eyes were stinging and she was absolutely not going to cry in front of Tomas Vey, who would be insufferable for a decade.

"Well," she managed. "I suppose someone ought to keep the light."

"That's the spirit," said Tomas.

From the kitchen doorway, where three people had been eavesdropping with zero shame, Maren said, "I'll make a celebratory pie," and Halvard said, "God help us all," and Jude said nothing at all, but he was smiling at the floor in the particular way of a boy who has just learned that the harbor he lives on is going to need him — that he will matter to it, and it to him, for a very long time.

Orsen sat back on the bench and listened to the water below, and the water, for the first time in her life, sounded less like something she was keeping watch against, and more like something coming to meet her.

It was later — much later, the clocks long since settled into grudging agreement past midnight — that a knock came at the door. Three slow raps, deliberate, unfamiliar. Halvard answered it with the lamp, and in the doorway stood a woman in a traveling cloak beaded with fog, gray at the temples, with a face that made Orsen rise from her chair so quickly it scraped the floor.

The woman looked past everyone in the room, straight at Jude.

"My name is Elise Vey," she said. "I'm looking for my son."
