## 22. Jude and the Harbor

The free port of Solvig smelled nothing like home, which was, Orsen decided on her third morning there, exactly what she needed.

She stood at the window of Maren's kitchen with a cup of coffee going cold in her hands and watched the harbor work itself awake. Fishing boats jostled at the quay like argumentative relatives. Gulls screamed their usual grievances. A boy ran along the breakwater with a bucket, and Orsen caught herself counting — as she had counted every day since the last boat — how many days it had been. Eleven. Jude had been gone eleven days, and she had spent every one of them learning how to be a woman without a lighthouse.

It turned out she was not very good at it.

"You've worn a groove in my floorboards," Maren said from the stove, not turning around. She was a broad woman with flour to her elbows and a voice like a ship's bell, and she had taken Orsen in with the brisk efficiency of someone who had decided years ago that she would and was simply waiting for the occasion. "If you wear through to the joists, you're fixing them."

"I'm thinking."

"You're pacing. Thinking looks different. Thinking sits down."

Orsen sat down, mostly out of spite, and Maren looked satisfied in the way of women who had won arguments for forty years. The kitchen was warm and close and smelled of bread, and through the open door came the sounds of the harbor — the slap of water against pilings, the creak of rigging, men calling prices for herring. It was loud here. Loud and crowded and full of strangers, and Orsen had discovered, to her considerable surprise, that she did not hate it. After nine years of a light and a logbook and the sound of her own voice saying *all's well* to nobody, noise felt like company.

The letter lay on the table where it had arrived two days before, forwarded twice, its envelope soft from other people's hands. She had read it perhaps thirty times. She read it again now, though she knew every word:

*Mrs. Delacroix — We are settled. Tomas is working the yards and I am going to school, which I did not expect and do not entirely trust. Mr. Brandt says I have a head for figures. I told him about the waves. He laughed and said a head for figures is a head for figures.*

*I count eleven days since the boat. I am keeping your share.*

*J.*

P.S. — Tomas says come when the roads dry. He says you promised him a debt repaid but he says he has been doing sums and by his figuring he still owes you one, and he does not like being behind on a debt. It makes him itch. Come before summer so he can stop itching.

Orsen folded the letter along its well-worn creases and looked out at the harbor of a town she did not know, in a country whose language she spoke only in market phrases, and admitted what she had been refusing to admit for eleven days: that she had no intention of staying anywhere else, and never really had.

The plan — if it could be called a plan, which was generous — had been to see Jude safe, then return north and face the inquiry with a clear conscience and whatever consequences followed. She had testified to nothing, as she had sworn to herself she would. Magistrate Halden had asked his questions in a room that smelled of lamp oil, and she had answered them all truthfully: yes, she had kept the light burning through the storm season; no, she had seen no suspicious vessels; no, she had no knowledge of any foreign sailors sheltering on her point. All true. She had simply neglected to mention the boy in her attic, the fisherman in her cellar, or the informant she'd caught going through her things, because Halden had not thought to ask, and Orsen Delacroix had discovered late in life that she possessed a talent for answering precisely the question asked and not one syllable more.

They had taken the light anyway. Administrative review, they called it. A keeper who could not be relied upon for full cooperation, they called her, though they could not say what she had failed to cooperate with. The appointment went to a younger man from inland who would learn, in time, what the winter gales taught everyone, and Orsen had stood on the quay with one trunk and her husband's coat and watched the supply boat pull away from the only home she had left, and found — this was the strange part, the part she still could not explain — that her chest held not grief but something closer to the feeling of setting down a weight she had forgotten she was carrying.

Nine years she had kept that light. For Erik, first, because it had been his post and his pride, and after the sea took him, for the promise she'd made to a drowning man's memory. She had thought the light was what held her together. It turned out the light had merely been holding her in place.

"Post," Maren said now, sliding a plate across the table. Bread, butter, a hard-boiled egg. "And eat all of it. You're built like a mast."

"Maren. I cannot keep eating your kitchen into poverty."

"You're not. You're paying rent, which you insist on, stubbornly, and which I accept, reluctantly, and between us we are keeping this household fed better than either of us managed alone, so stop apologizing for existing." Maren wiped her hands on her apron and finally sat. "Besides. You'll want your strength. If you're sailing north next week like I think you are."

Orsen paused with the egg halfway to her mouth. "I haven't said—"

"You've been reading that letter four times a day and looking at the passenger notices. You're going." Maren's voice softened, just slightly, the way it did perhaps twice a year. "Good. A person shouldn't spend a whole life keeping watch over things. Sooner or later somebody ought to keep watch over her."

*

The roads dried in the second week of May, and Orsen packed one trunk again — she was becoming practiced at it, which she suspected was a lesson rather than a coincidence — and took the coastal steamer north, then east, then followed directions that Tomas had written with the confident inaccuracy of a man who had never once considered that another person might not know which crooked fence he meant.

She got lost twice. The second time, a shepherd pointed her up a track toward a cluster of white houses above a river valley, and said, in the way of people reuniting travelers with their destinations, "You'll be wanting the Vey house, then," and Orsen understood that she had been expected for some time.

She saw the boy before he saw her.

He was in a field beyond the last house, standing very still in the tall grass, and even at a distance she knew what he was doing. He was facing the wind, eyes half-closed, lips moving faintly. Counting. Counting gusts, maybe, or birds, or breaths — the arithmetic he used to make the world manageable, the numbers he had taught her on the nights in the attic when the storms made everything too large and the only way through was to name each wave as it broke below the point and let it pass.

Then he turned, and saw her, and stopped counting.

For a moment neither of them moved. Orsen had rehearsed something on the boat, some reasonable greeting, an adult thing to say. What came out instead was: "You've grown."

"So have you," Jude said, which was not true, and made them both laugh, and then he was running across the field and she was walking as fast as the ground allowed and the distance closed the way distances do at such moments — all at once, unfairly fast, and then his arms were around her middle and her hand was on his hair and the smell of him was soap and grass and something underneath that was simply *Jude*, unchanged, and Orsen held on and did not trust herself to speak for a while.

"You kept my share," she managed finally.

"Forty-one days." He stepped back, solemn, hands in his pockets the way Tomas stood. "I wrote it in the back of my copybook. Forty-one days, and I didn't miss once, except the day we arrived, because I didn't know yet where to put it."

"Where do you put it?"

"In the mornings. When I wake up, before anything else, I think: that's one more." He shrugged, matter-of-fact, as though describing the tides. "It works better than being scared."

Orsen looked at this boy — twelve years old, orphaned twice over, carried across a sea on forged papers and a fisherman's word, and calmly explaining to her the system he had invented for surviving grief — and felt the last small door inside her swing open.

"It does," she said. "That's how it works. That's exactly how it works."

Tomas Vey came up the track with his cap in his hands and gray coming into his beard and the same careful gentleness in his face that Orsen remembered from the worst night of her life, ten years ago, when he had pulled her husband half-drowned from the water and carried him two miles through sleet. He stopped a few paces off, and looked at her, and did not say thank you and did not say sorry, because they were past all that, past the whole ledger of it, and instead he said:

"The debt's paid. By my figuring."

"By yours?"

"Mine. I've been doing sums." He put the cap on. "A life for a life, that was the first entry. Then you kept my son through a winter with the magistrate at your door, which by any honest accounting clears the rest and leaves a balance in your favor. But—" and here he shifted, uncomfortable, a big man wrestling with words too small for what he meant— "a balance in your favor isn't the same as a debt, and I find I don't like the arithmetic anyway. So I'm retiring the whole account. New terms."

"What terms?"

"That you stop being somebody we owe and start being somebody we have." He said it plainly, the way he said everything, and then looked away at the fields because plain men find plain truths hardest to say. "There's a room. Small. Faces east. The boy picked it."

Orsen looked at Tomas, and at Jude standing beside him trying very hard to look as though he were not watching her answer land, and she thought of Erik, ten years dead, who would have laughed until he cried to see where his promise had led her — thought of him with fondness instead of the old raw ache — and she thought of the lighthouse, alone on its point, burning for whoever climbed its stairs now, needing nothing from her that she had left to give.

"The room faces east," she said. "Does it get the morning light?"

"From before dawn," Jude said. "I checked."

"Then I'll take it." She shouldered her trunk before either of them could offer, because some habits were hers to keep. "On one condition. Nobody counts days anymore. Not my share, not anyone's. We start a new book. Page one."

Jude considered this with the gravity he gave all important matters. "What do we count instead?"

Orsen looked out over the valley — the river, the white houses, the smoke rising from Maren-of-Solvig's cousin's bakery two doors down from the Vey house, because the world was smaller than it pretended to be — and then back at the two people waiting for her answer, patient as tide.

"Catch," she said. "We count catch. And good weather. And birthdays, all three of them, including yours, which I intend to establish by summer's end whether or not the paperwork agrees on the date." She started up the track. "Come along, both of you. Show me the house. I've been keeping watch long enough — someone walk ahead of me and tell me what's coming."

Tomas fell into step beside her, grinning into his beard. Jude ran ahead, then doubled back, then ran ahead again, the way boys do when happiness is too much for their legs to hold still, and the evening came on gold over the valley, and no one counted anything at all.

Except, much later, lying in the small east-facing room with the first gray light beginning at the window, Orsen Delacroix did allow herself one number, privately, in the old habit of a keeper logging the night: *Day one. All's well.*
