## 1. The Weight of Ink

The wick wanted trimming at half past four, and Quentin Okonkwo trimmed it, because the light did not care what day it was.

That was the first kindness of Gullwrack Point, if she was honest about it—the way the work refused to acknowledge anniversaries. The lamp burned oil whether a body was mourning or marrying. The clockwork that turned the lens required winding every four hours, rain or grief, and the glass required polishing until the North Sea could see itself coming. There was no room in the machinery for sorrow. Sorrow was not load-bearing. She had checked.

"Good evening to you too," she told the lamp, which had flared and settled as she fed the fresh wick into its jaws. She talked to it more than she ought. Three years alone on a rock with a brass apparatus will do that to a person; the alternative was talking to the gulls, and the gulls were terrible listeners with no respect for a finished thought.

She trimmed, she seated the wick, she turned the key until the flame stood up straight and proud like a chorister. Through the lantern glazing, the sea lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of an old bruise going yellow at the edges. Weather coming. There was always weather coming, in December; the only question was whether it would bother to arrive or merely loom.

Quentin climbed down the iron stairs—eighty-two of them, she'd counted them so many times the count had worn grooves in her mind—and went below to the keeping room, where the stove ticked and the kettle waited and the ledger lay open on the table exactly where she had left it six hours ago.

The ledger. Her other spouse, Emeric used to say. The jealous one.

She sat, poured tea she would forget to drink, and uncapped her pen in the ritual way: two taps against the inkwell's rim, never three. Emeric had tapped three times and gotten ink on his cuff every blessed time, and she had teased him about it for eleven years, and now she could not tap three times herself without her chest doing something structural and unwelcome.

*4 December,* she wrote. *Wind NW, moderate. Swell from the east. Glass falling a little. Trimmed wick 16:30. Lens clean.*

She looked at what she had written. It was perfect. It was exactly what a keeper's log ought to contain, in the order a keeper's log ought to contain it, and it was also a lie of omission so complete that the Admiralty's inspectors, when they came sniffing every spring, had twice commended her records as *exemplary in their precision.*

Because here was what the ledger did not say:

It did not say that she had begun sleeping in the keeping room rather than the bedroom above, because the bedroom still held two hooks on the door and she could not bring herself to take down the second one, nor to look at it, nor to stop looking at it.

It did not say that last Tuesday she had caught herself saying "mind the third stair, it's loose" to nobody, in Emeric's direction, out of eleven years of habit aimed at a man three years drowned.

It did not say that she had not written one word about herself—not a cold, not a birthday, not the night in October when she'd sat on the gallery steps until dawn because she couldn't bear the shape of the bed—in three years, two months, and some days she had stopped numbering precisely so she wouldn't have this problem.

The old ledgers were full of her. *Q. has the sniffles again, E. insists on mustard plaster, abominable man.* *E. fell in the cistern. I laughed. I should not have laughed.* Their whole marriage lived in those pages, in her hand beside his hand, sometimes on the same line where they'd taken turns writing, their arguments conducted in marginalia across entire winters. She could open any volume from those years and hear both their voices.

The new ones held only wind directions.

*Visibility good,* she wrote now. *Nothing in sight.*

There. That was true, at least. Nothing in sight. The most honest sentence in the book, and it looked exactly like weather.

* * *

At six she wound the clockwork, all nine turns, feeling the mechanism take up its tension like a great patient animal waking. At seven she ate bread and cheese standing up, because sitting down to meals alone was a courtesy she had stopped extending herself; a person can't be expected to converse over supper with no one. At eight she made her rounds of the gallery rail in the dark, testing each bracket with a gloved hand, and found the north-east bracket working loose again in the cold.

"You'll want a shim," Emeric said in her head, in the tone he used for observations he believed were helpful.

"I know how a shim works," Quentin said out loud, to the wind, to the rail, to the dark water ninety feet below. "I've known how a shim works since before you did."

This was another thing the ledger did not record: the arguments she continued to have, daily, with a dead man who had grown remarkably agreeable about losing them.

She got the shim. She drove it home with three blows of the mallet—one for the bracket, and the other two because it felt good—and stood back breathing white breath into the dark, and that was when she noticed the sea.

Not anything wrong with it. Just the sea itself, doing its eternal business against the base of the rock, and the sound it made tonight was deeper than yesterday's sound. A long, heavy syllable, repeated. The swell had come around to the east during the afternoon without announcing itself, and easterly swell at Gullwrack meant weather with a capital W, the kind the fishermen at Porthmoor Harbor named after saints and grandmothers.

"Tomorrow, then," she said, and went in to batten what could be battened.

* * *

She worked through the evening by lamplight, and it must be said plainly, because the log would not say it: part of her was glad.

A storm was company. Not good company—it broke shutters, stole roof slates, once took the entire boathouse door off its hinges and flung it into the next parish—but it was *something*. A storm gave the hours edges. During the great gales she was too busy to notice the silence in the second chair, because there was rope to tend and lamps to shield and water to fight, and afterward there was damage to mend, and mending was the closest thing to conversation she allowed herself. Every plank she nailed down was a sentence in an argument she was winning.

So she checked the storm shutters and oiled the hinges and set the spare lamp wicks in their tin within reach, and at ten o'clock she climbed back up and wrote the midnight entry in advance, as keepers do: *Wind increasing. Glass falling steadily.* And then, because her pen was already in her hand and the page was already open and there was no law against it—

She stopped.

Three years, and her hand still tried. Some nights it simply started, the way a foot starts toward a door in a house you've moved out of. *Emeric would laugh at this glass,* the pen wanted to write. *He always said December on this coast is God clearing His throat.*

Quentin put the cap back on the ink.

"No," she told the ledger, mildly, the way you decline a second helping. "We don't."

But she did not close the book. Instead she reached beneath it, where the keeping room's one luxury lived: a flat tin box, painted once with roses and now painted mostly with rust, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, folded soft as cloth from handling, the last letter.

She knew it by heart. That was the trouble with knowing things by heart—they move in. They stop being words and start being wallpaper.

*My dearest Q,*

*I've signed on for the *Meridian*'s coastal run, six weeks there and back, and before you say a word—yes, I know you hate the *Meridian*, yes, I know the captain owes me money, which is precisely why I'm going, because he pays debts in cargo and we need the paint. Don't scold. By the time you read this I'll be halfway to being rich in white lead and you'll forgive me everything, as usual, because you are magnanimous and I am charming, and between us we make one adequate person.*

*Keep the light lit. Mind the third stair. Tap your pen twice and think of me tapping it three times and getting ink on everything I love.*

*—Yrs entirely, E.*

Six weeks. He had been gone three years. The *Meridian* had come home without him, and the sea had given back one boot and a ship's boat with its nameboard splintered, and the harbormaster had made a kind face and used the word *lost* in the careful voice people use for words they don't believe will hold the weight.

Quentin read the letter once, put it away, and did not cry, because she had used up all of that in the first year and discovered that grief, like lamp oil, is finite and must be rationed against the dark.

What she felt instead was the other thing, the quieter thing, the thing the logbooks would never carry: that the letter was *wrong* now, and she couldn't say how. Something in it snagged, like a wick trimmed unevenly, a small crooked flame-flicker she couldn't locate. He'd said *six weeks* so confidently. He'd said *the captain owes me money* —and Emeric, generous, feckless Emeric, had never in his life lent money anyone would owe. He'd been owed nothing but apologies.

She was tired. That was all. Letters didn't change. People did, and then the letters changed in their hands, the way a word repeated goes strange.

Quentin banked the stove, climbed her eighty-two stairs, and kept the light through the small hours, watching the eastern horizon where the stars were going out one by one behind a rising wall of cloud.

* * *

Morning came up gray and wrong. Not storm-gray yet—storm-gray was dramatic, almost theatrical, all banners and drumrolls—but a flat, pressed, holding-its-breath gray, the gray of a sea gathering its argument. The glass had fallen overnight like a stone dropped down a well. The gulls had gone inland, which the oldest fishermen called the surest barometer on the coast, and which Quentin called rude, because even the birds had somewhere better to be.

She wrote the morning entry. *Glass fallen half an inch since midnight. Sea building from the east. Gulls gone inland. Preparations made.*

Her pen hovered.

*Preparations made.* As though she were a person who prepared, rather than a machine that maintained. She looked at the sentence a long moment. Then, in the smallest act of rebellion she had managed in three years, she added one word:

*All preparations made.*

All. As in, everything there was. As in, this is the whole of me now, and the rest went down with a ship's boat off the Skerries, and there is no entry anywhere for it.

She blotted the page, squared the ledger on the table, and went up to trim the wick, because it wanted trimming at half past eight, and the light did not care what day it was, and thank God for that, and God help her for being thankful.

By noon the wind had come around fully east and found its voice—a long, rising note in the lantern glazing, like something enormous whistling thoughtfully to itself while it made up its mind. Quentin stood on the gallery with her coat buttoned to the chin, looking at a sea she had studied for fourteen years the way scholars study texts, and read in it a line she had never quite seen before.

The swell was wrong. Too long between crests, too deep-drawn, the kind of swell that doesn't come from any local blow but travels days across ocean from storms a body will never see. It came in steady as a procession, each wave arriving with the gravity of an ambassador, and it struck the Point with a boom she felt in her back teeth.

"Something's coming," she said. "From a very long way off."

She went down and did everything again that she had done yesterday—shutters, ropes, spares—as though the doing could be doubled up like layers of clothing against cold. She cooked a proper meal for the first time in weeks, out of pure superstition: if the world meant to throw weather at her, she would meet it fed. She ate it sitting down, which felt reckless, almost festive.

And through it all, at the edge of hearing, under the wind and the boom of the swell, the sea kept up that other sound—that long, deep, patient syllable, repeated, repeated—like a knock at a door from underneath the world.

Toward dusk, the light on the horizon: a single smudge where no smudge should be. Not cloud. Cloud did not sit *on* the water. Quentin watched it while her tea went cold beside her, and told herself it was a fishing boat late running for harbor, and knew, in the place where she knew the weight of eighty-two stairs and the exact number of turns in the clockwork, that it was not.

The glass fell another quarter inch.

She opened the ledger and wrote, in a hand that did not shake because she would not permit it:

*Glass falling fast. Unusual swell from the east. Object sighted low on the horizon, bearing northeast. Watching.*

Then, after a moment, because the pen was in her hand and the page was open and some habits are older than grief:

*I do not like the look of it.*

She stared at the sentence. Six words about herself, the first in three years, sitting on the page like a stranger at her own table.

She did not blot it out.

Outside, the first spit of rain came hard against the eastern glass, and far off under the growing roar of the wind—so faint she could tell herself later she had imagined it—there came a sound from the sea that was neither wave nor wind, and the long night began.
