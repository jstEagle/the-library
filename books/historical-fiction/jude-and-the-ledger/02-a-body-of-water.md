## 2. A Body of Water

The first great storm came on a Tuesday, which Quentin Okonkwo considered typical of the sea's sense of humor. It did not arrive on a Sunday when she was rested and prayerful, nor on a Friday when the stores were fresh. It arrived on a Tuesday, three days after she had meant to fix the loose pane in the lantern room and one day after she had decided, with great firmness, that the pane could wait until spring.

By dawn the barometer had fallen so far she could measure her mood by it. By noon the wind was coming off the water in long gray fists. By two o'clock the harbor bell was ringing itself hoarse, and Gullwrack Point had become less a place than an argument between rock and ocean, with the tower standing in the middle like a stubborn relation who refused to take sides.

Quentin worked. That was the whole of it. She worked.

There is a kind of grief that makes a person lie down, and a kind that makes her stand up, and Quentin had been given the second kind. She trimmed the wicks until they were perfect little tongues of certainty. She polished the lens until it threw light the way a good sermon throws conviction — broadly, and without asking anyone's opinion. She logged the weather every hour on the hour: *Wind SW, rising. Sea heavy from the south. Glass falling fast.* Her entries were immaculate. Her entries said nothing about the fact that she had not spoken aloud to another living soul in nineteen days, unless you counted the gulls, and Quentin did count them, but only grudgingly, because they never wrote back.

At four o'clock she made tea she would not finish and stood at the gallery rail to watch the sea do its worst, because watching was part of the work, and because — though she would have denied this under oath — Emeric used to stand there with her in storms, his arm along the rail beside hers, saying nothing useful whatsoever and being missed for exactly that now.

"Come on, then," she told the horizon, which was the closest thing to conversation she permitted herself before supper.

The horizon obliged.

* * *

It was nearly dark when she saw it — saw, then didn't see, then saw again, the way the sea offers things up and snatches them back like a child playing keep-away. A shape in the surf below the point, where the rocks ran out into a shingle beach that was no beach at all, only a place where the stones had given up being cliffs.

At first she thought it was a barrel. The coast was generous with barrels; storms shook them out of hulls the way trees shake down apples. She had a small collection of them behind the boathouse, and had twice fished out crates with foreign stamps, and once a ship's figurehead of a woman whose serene face Quentin had found faintly accusatory ever since.

Then the barrel turned over, and barrels did not do that, and barrels certainly did not have arms.

Quentin did not think. Thinking was for afterward. She took the coil of line from its peg — Emeric's habit, keeping the rope at the door, and she had kept the habit without noticing until this exact moment — and went down the stair inside the tower two steps at a time, out into weather that hit her like a mob.

The wind did not blow at her so much as *at* her, personally, with intent. Rain came sideways, mixed with spray, so that going down the path to the shingle was less walking than wading through air. Twice the gusts picked her up by the shoulders and moved her where they liked. She shouted something — she never learned what; the wind took it as toll — and kept her eyes on the spot where the shape had washed against the stones.

He was face-down when she reached him, half in the surf, and the sea was doing what the sea does with anything it has finished with: pulling at him, patient, certain. Quentin hooked the line under his arms and dug her heels into the shingle, and for a long ugly minute it was tug-of-war with the Atlantic, and she lost ground twice, and then a wave helped her by accident, heaving him up the stones like a fishmonger tossing cod onto a slab.

He was heavier than he looked, or the situation made him seem so. Getting him up the path took everything she had and some things she hadn't known she had. She dragged, rested, dragged again, swore with an eloquence she would have been mortified for her mother to hear, and all the while the storm leaned on her back like a foreman urging speed. Halfway up she stopped being able to tell the rain from the sweat, and somewhere near the top she stopped caring about anything except the arithmetic of the thing: ten more feet, five, the door, the bolt, the sudden shocking hush of indoors where the wind became merely a sound instead of a government.

She got him laid out on the kitchen floor, on top of the good rug, which she noted dimly would never forgive her.

* * *

He was youngish — thirty perhaps, hard to say with the color gone out of him and the salt still running off in threads. Dark hair plastered flat. A gash over one eyebrow that had bled and been rinsed by the sea into looking worse than it was. His clothes were wrong for a sailor: good wool, city-cut, ruined now beyond any tailor's ambition. And his hands —

His hands were what stopped her. Even unconscious, even blue-lipped and rattling, his hands were crossed over his chest, clutching an oilskin packet to him the way a child holds a stolen loaf. Not loosely. Not accidentally. The knot of his fingers had to be pried at, and she tried, and his grip only tightened, and Quentin sat back on her heels, dripping on her own rug, and looked at this stranger who had come through drowning with one fist closed around a package.

"Well," she said, and it was the first word she'd spoken to a person in nineteen days, and it came out rusty. "Well, you're somebody's problem, and I suppose you're mine now."

He didn't answer, being occupied. She got him out of the wool coat — the packet never left his hands, not once; she had to work the coat around it like peeling an orange without disturbing the pit — and rubbed him down with blankets, and built up the stove fire, and did all the ordinary mercies that her body remembered and performed without consulting her. Somewhere in the middle of it he began to shiver violently, which she understood to be good news, and groaned, which she chose to take as gratitude.

She propped him against the settle near the stove and put whiskey to his lips, a spoonful at a time. He coughed, opened his eyes — gray, unfocused, swimming — and looked at her with an expression of profound confusion, as though she were the impossible thing, as though she had washed ashore into his life rather than the reverse.

"You're dead," he said clearly.

"That's often said of me," Quentin replied. "Drink this."

He drank. He slept. Between the drinking and the sleeping there was perhaps a minute when he was neither asleep nor properly awake, and during that minute his hand came up off the packet, hovered, and gripped her wrist — not hard, just enough — and he said, in a voice scraped raw with salt:

"Don't let go of it."

"I haven't," she said, meaning the packet, meaning him, meaning she hardly knew what.

He slept then in earnest, breathing like a set of bellows with a leak, and Quentin covered him to the chin and stood a while in the middle of her kitchen, soaked to the skin, hair dripping a puddle around her boots, feeling something she had not felt in three years and could not immediately name.

It took her until she was changing into dry clothes to name it. It was *use*. Someone needed her, specifically, bodily, tonight — not her signature on a form, not her steady trimming of wicks for ships that passed without a thought, but her hands, her rope, her floor. She had spent three years being diligent, and diligence turned out to be a cold substitute for usefulness, the way a photograph is a cold substitute for a face.

She hated how warm it felt, being needed. She hated it the way you hate a draught under a door: not enough to stop feeling it.

* * *

She did not sleep much, which was normal, and did not mind, which was new. She sat up with the lamp turned low and mended a net she did not need mended, and listened to the storm lean on the tower, and watched the stranger breathe. Around midnight he surfaced, drank the water she offered, and sank again. Around two the tide reached its worst and the whole tower hummed like a struck bell, and she climbed to check the light, and the light burned steady, and she came back down and took up the net again.

Around four, unable to bear her own curiosity a moment longer, she knelt by the settle and looked properly at the oilskin packet.

It was the size of a prayer book, tied with waxed cord, and it had traveled — the oilskin was scarred and abraded, one corner worn white. Whatever was inside had been protected carefully and carried far. She turned it over. There was no writing on it. There was nothing to indicate what it held or to whom it belonged, except that it belonged to a man who had crossed a drowned coast in the worst storm of the winter with his fists locked around it, and who, half-conscious on a stranger's floor, had asked her — begged her, if begging was too strong a word for a whisper, which it wasn't — not to let go of it.

Quentin Okonkwo was an honest woman and the keeper of records, and she did not open it. But she held it. Just for a moment, just to feel its weight — the way you might hold someone else's letter without reading it, which is not eavesdropping, only weighing, and everyone knows the difference even if everyone is lying.

It was heavier than paper alone. There were other things in there. Documents, perhaps. Money. She put it down on the table under the lamp, precisely in the center, where it could be seen, and went back to her net, and pretended that her heart was not conducting business on its own account.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater, and the storm, having spent the night shouting, settled into a sullen drizzle and a long heavy swell, like a guest who has broken something and won't leave. Quentin made porridge, fed the stranger a spoonful at a time when he proved too weak to manage a bowl, and catalogued him in the daylight the way she catalogued wrecks: thoroughly, unsentimentally, item by item.

Item: he was younger than the dark had made him. Item: his hands, freed of salt, were ink-stained at the cuticles — a clerk's hands, or a forger's, or a student's. Item: there was a fine gold chain at his neck with no pendant, as if something had been cut away. Item: he woke fully around eight o'clock and looked at her, and then at the packet on the table, and the relief that crossed his face was so naked and so enormous that it told her more than any interrogation could have.

He reached for the packet. She slid it toward him. He gathered it in against his chest and closed his eyes, holding it, and for a full minute neither of them spoke, and the stove ticked, and outside a gull made its usual argument with the wind.

"Thank you," he said at last. His voice was wrecked but working. "You pulled me out of the water."

"The water was busy."

He almost smiled. It was a tired, careful almost-smile, the smile of a man checking whether his face still worked after a long disuse. "I owe you my life."

"You owe me a rug," said Quentin. "The life was free."

That earned a real flicker of something — surprise, maybe, that a woman alone on a rock in a storm could still find the joke. Good, she thought. Let him be surprised. Surprise was nearly as warming as whiskey, and cheaper.

"What's your name?" she asked, and watched him not answer.

It was remarkable, really, how long he managed it. Three seconds. Five. Long enough that she could see the machinery turning behind his eyes — weighing, calculating, arriving at some conclusion about her that she would have given a month's oil money to read. He looked at the packet. He looked at the window, where the gray sea ran on and on, offering no road out. He looked at her hands, of all things, as if they might tell him something about whether she could be trusted.

"Jude," he said finally. Just that. No family name, no port of origin, no story attached, like a coin worn smooth.

"And you're from?" she asked.

"Away," he said.

"And bound for?"

"Nowhere," he said, and then, hearing how it sounded, added, "for now."

Quentin nodded slowly and poured him more tea, and did not press, because she had buried a husband and learned something about the arithmetic of grief and secrecy: that everyone carrying a packet clutched to their chest has their reasons, and that reasons, like drowned men, will surface in their own time or not at all, and no amount of hauling on the line changes what comes up.

But that night, after he had slept again and she had banked the stove, she opened the ledger to log the day's weather — *Storm abating. Wind WSW, moderating. Sea still heavy* — and paused, pen above the page.

Three years ago she would have written more. *Stranger recovered from the surf, alive. Name uncertain.* Emeric had always teased her that her ledgers read like sermons, everything in them but the preacher. She had written herself into those pages once — her cold hands, her burnt suppers, the day the gulls stole an entire week's bread. Then Emeric's last letter had come, and then the official letter after it, and since then her entries had been pure instrument readings, weather without a witness.

Now she looked at the blank space below the barometer figures, where a person might write something true.

She wrote: *One man, alive.*

Then, after a moment, because the pen seemed to want it and because nobody was watching: *He would not let go of whatever he carries.*

She blotted the page, closed the ledger, and turned down the lamp. In the corner, Jude slept with the oilskin packet tucked beneath his cheek like a pillow, his fingers still curled around its edges even in sleep — and in the low light, as she paused at the stair, Quentin could have sworn he was smiling, as a man smiles at something he alone can read.
