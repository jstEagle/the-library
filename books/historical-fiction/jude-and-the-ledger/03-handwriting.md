## 3. Handwriting

Jude woke to the smell of burning oil and the sound of someone breathing deliberately.

Not the ragged, wet breathing of a man half-drowned—though he had been that man, he was fairly sure, some hours or days ago. This was controlled breathing, the kind a person produced when they wanted you to know they were awake and unimpressed by your being awake too. He opened his eyes a crack and saw an iron ceiling, blackened with soot, and hanging from it a lamp turned down so low it made more smell than light.

He was on a cot. A wool blanket had been tucked around him with a precision that suggested the tucking had been done out of duty rather than tenderness, the way one straightens a picture in a house one is merely inspecting. His head throbbed. His throat felt as though he had swallowed the sea twice and been made to apologize for it.

The deliberate breather sat at a table across the room—a small room, stone-walled, everything squared away like a ship's cabin or a widow's kitchen. She was writing. He knew from the dossier—he corrected himself; he knew *nothing*, he was a shipwrecked sailor, he knew only what any man knows—that she wrote constantly. The pen scratched and paused, scratched and paused, in a rhythm too regular to be thought and too impatient to be habit.

"You snore," she said, without looking up.

"I've never been told that before."

"Most men haven't done most of the things you did last night." She set down her pen with a click. "You fell into the surf at Gullwrack Point in the teeth of a gale, which is either bad luck or bad judgment, and then you fought me when I tried to haul you out of your coat, which I'd call ingratitude if I didn't suspect it was something worse."

Jude kept his face arranged into the mild confusion of a man genuinely trying to locate his own memories. It was easier than he expected. His body was doing most of the work for him; he genuinely could not account for the last hour before the water. "There was a packet," he said. "An oilskin packet. Was there—"

"There was."

"Then I'd like it back, please." He tried to sit up and discovered that his ribs had opinions. The room performed a slow quarter-turn around him, and he gripped the cot's edge until it stopped.

She watched him do this. Her eyes were gray and unhurried, the eyes of someone who had spent years watching weather she could not control and had stopped expecting apologies from anything. Quentin Okonkwo. Keeper of the light at Gullwrack Point. Widow of three years, though the word *widow* sat strangely on her; she wore her mourning the way she wore the enormous fisherman's sweater she had clearly put him in—the sleeves cuffed, the fit wrong, the wearing of it a practical matter rather than an emotional one.

"The packet," she said, "is on this table. Where it has stayed since I cut it out of your arms at two o'clock this morning. You held it through unconsciousness, vomiting, and my setting your dislocated finger, which for the record you also fought me on. Whatever's in it matters to you."

"It matters to other people. I'm carrying it for them."

"Ah." Something moved behind the gray eyes—not softening, exactly. Recalculating. "A courier."

"A sailor who takes odd work. Work's thin since the storms closed the northern runs."

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a woman filing a lie in a drawer to be examined later by better light. She rose, poured something from a kettle into a tin mug, and brought it to him. It was broth, weak and salted, and it was the best thing Jude had ever tasted, though he would have said so only under torture. "Drink that. Then sleep. When you can stand without the room turning, we'll discuss how you came ashore inside a mile of my light in the worst weather of the season, and why a courier fights so hard to keep hold of another man's letters."

She went back to her table. The pen resumed its scratching.

Jude drank the broth and thought, with a sensation not unlike seasickness, that he had prepared for many versions of this mission and none of them had included being nursed competently and seen through entirely in the first four hours.

* * *

Sleep took him despite himself, the way tide takes a bottle.

He dreamed of his brother. In the dream Emeric was standing on a dock in summer, laughing at something Jude couldn't hear, wearing the ridiculous yellow scarf their mother had sent him off to sea with twenty years ago, and Jude wanted to shout across the water—*they think you're dead, Em, they built you a grave, I stood beside it*—but in dreams one cannot shout, and Emeric's laugh went on, easy and alive, while the dock slowly darkened and rotted under his feet.

He woke to daylight, gray through a salt-fogged window, and the sound of paper unfolding.

The room was empty except for her. She had the oilskin packet open on her lap and was holding a letter with both hands, very still, the way one holds something that might be a bird or might be a live coal. Her stillness was total in a way her breathing had not been. Even the fog outside seemed to quieten.

"That's private correspondence," Jude said, from the cot.

She did not startle. That frightened him more than shouting would have. "It's addressed to no one," she said. Her voice was level. Level the way the sea is level right before it turns over a boat. "It's dated the ninth of March. This year."

"Then it's none of—"

"Quentin Okonkwo died three years ago," she said, and Jude heard the slip even as she heard it herself, the name offered instead of the title, the way a person says their own name at a graveside. She corrected nothing. She looked down at the letter again. "Emeric Okonkwo, keeper of the Gullwrack Point light, was lost with the *Cormorant's Pride* off the Skaiths in the autumn three years past. There were eleven widows in the harbor parish that winter. I was the twelfth. They rang the bell." Her thumb moved along the edge of the paper. "This letter is dated the ninth of March. Of this year."

"Dates can be miswritten," Jude said. "Grief-addled men write strange things. Storms scatter papers for hundreds of miles. Paper dries out. Ink runs—"

"Ink doesn't run backward into perfect letterforms." She was not arguing with him. That was the terrible thing about her; she wasn't even talking to him anymore, really. She was reading the letter again with her whole body, and Jude sat up slowly in the cot and understood that he was watching the exact catastrophe he had been paid to cause, happening in real time, six feet away, and that it looked nothing like success. It looked like watching a house settle on its foundations and realizing the ground beneath it was gone.

"Give me the letter," he said quietly. "Please."

"He wrote to me every week for nine years." She said it conversationally, as if continuing an argument they'd been having for days. "Every week, even in harbor, even the fortnight we spent together at my mother's when he complained there was nothing to write about and wrote me four pages anyway about the cat next door. I know his hand the way I know my own face. And this—" she lifted the page, and her hand, which had been steady, was now not steady at all, though her voice refused to follow it down— "this is his hand. The way he crosses his sevens. The way he makes his *k* like a little ship with two masts. Nobody else makes them like that. I used to tease him that he signed his letters like a fleet going out."

"Keeper Okonkwo—"

"Don't." The word came out cracked clean down the middle. She pressed her lips together, breathed once through her nose, and Jude watched a woman assemble herself piece by piece in front of him: shoulders, chin, hands folded flat on the letter. It was the most effortful composure he had ever witnessed, and therefore the most moving. "Don't call me that. My husband was Keeper Okonkwo. I'm just the woman who wound up keeping his lamp because nobody else wanted the rock."

"The letters aren't yours," Jude said, and hated himself even as the words came out, because they were true and useless, which is the worst thing truth can be. "I'll take them and go, today, whatever state I'm in. You'll never see me again. Whatever's in those pages has nothing to do with you."

"Nothing to do with me." She looked at him then, fully, for the first time since he'd woken, and Jude discovered what it was like to be looked at by someone who had spent three years alone with a ledger and a light and had gotten very good at seeing things as they were. "A man washes up on my rocks clutching letters in my dead husband's handwriting, dated after my dead husband's death, and tells me it has *nothing to do with me*. Sit down."

He was already sitting. He sat further down anyway.

* * *

She made tea. This struck Jude as the strangest detail of the whole morning, stranger than forged letters and faked deaths: that she measured out leaves and heated water and found two cups—one chipped, one not—and gave him the unchipped one, all while the color had not yet come back to her face. People in stories threw things. Real people made tea, because their hands needed employment or they would do something unrecoverable.

"How many are there?" she asked.

"I don't know. I carry the packet sealed."

"You carried it *in the sea*. Seals don't survive the sea; wax goes soft and paper goes to pulp. Whoever packed this knew that. They wrapped each letter in its own oiled paper, tied with thread, like a merchant's sample case." She unwrapped another one as she spoke, delicately, hating herself for it, unable to stop. "That's care. Someone cared about these letters arriving legible."

Or someone needed them legible to serve their purpose, Jude thought, and drank his tea.

She read. He watched her read, and it was the hardest work he had ever done, harder than swimming in the gale. Her face changed in small increments. A line appeared between her brows at something. At something else she exhaled through her nose—the ghost of a laugh, strangled at birth—and Jude understood with a lurch that the letters contained jokes. Someone had written jokes in Emeric's hand. Someone had known the shape of Emeric's humor well enough to pour it through a stolen pen, and she was receiving them like rations to a starving place, and every one of them was a nail in her.

"This one mentions the freckles," she said. Her voice had gone very quiet.

Jude's stomach dropped through the floor and the floor after it. *The freckles.* There was a line in the third letter—he had written it himself, or rewritten it, working from Emeric's old correspondence until two in the morning with his brother's words open beside him like anatomy charts—*you always claimed the freckles on your left wrist made the constellation Cassiopeia, and I never had the heart to tell you it was actually the Plough, because you navigated by it anyway.*

"Sheer invention," Jude said. "A guess. Half the women on this coast have freckles."

"On my left wrist," she said, "in the shape of Cassiopeia." She held up her arm. The sleeve of the enormous sweater had fallen back. The constellation sat there, faint against winter-pale skin, five stars of darker pigment, and above her wrist her pulse was beating hard enough that he could see it. "Nobody knows that. Nobody living knows that. He called it my compass. He said—" and here her voice finally failed, just for a breath, one plank giving way in a sound structure— "he said as long as I had them, I'd never be lost."

The fire ticked in the stove. Somewhere below them the sea worked at the rocks, patient, indifferent, doing its permanent arithmetic.

"So," she said at last, and set the letter down, and flattened it with both palms as though it might fly away. "Either my husband is alive, in which case he has been alive for three years while I kept his light and buried an empty coffin and learned to sleep on my own side of the bed. Or my husband is dead, and someone in this world can write his hand so perfectly that it fools his own wife, and chose to send those letters to this rock, in this season, inside this man." She looked up. Gray eyes, calm as the bottom of the sea. "And you're going to tell me which it is."

"I don't know," Jude said, which was true, and insufficient, and the only mercy he had left.

She nodded slowly, as if he had confirmed something. Then she gathered the letters—all of them, with a collector's economy of motion—rewrapped them in their oilskin, and set the packet on the table between them like a card laid face down.

"Well," she said. "Then you'll stay until you do remember. The storm's closed the harbor anyway. And Jude—"

He went cold. He had not given her his name.

"—if you're going to lie to a keeper," she said, picking up her pen and returning to her ledger as though the world still had edges, "at least don't talk in your sleep first."

Outside the window, the fog thickened over the rocks, and far out in the white, unseen, the sea began to rise again.
