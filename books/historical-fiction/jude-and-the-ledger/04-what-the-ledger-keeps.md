## 4. What the Ledger Keeps

Quentin did not sleep.

She told herself it was the storm still working itself out of the sky, the tail-end of it worrying at the shutters like a dog with an old bone. She told herself it was the stranger asleep in Emeric's old chair by the stove, breathing in the wet-burlap way of a man who had swallowed half the Atlantic and meant to give most of it back. She told herself several things, in the tidy voice she used for telling herself things, and none of them were the truth.

The truth was folded into an oilskin packet on the kitchen table, and it was dated three years after her husband drowned.

She lit the second lamp. Then, because one lamp seemed insufficient for what she intended, she lit the stub of candle she kept for power cuts and set it beside the lamps so that the table blazed like an altar. If she was going to be made a fool of, she would at least be a well-lit fool.

The ledger came first. That was habit, and habit was the only religion she had kept faithfully these three years. She opened it to the current page — *Wind SW by S, heavy swell, glass falling. Lamp trimmed at dusk and midnight. Oil consumed: as oil is consumed* — and looked at her own handwriting for a long moment. Neat. Economical. A hand that had stopped wasting ink on anything that wasn't weather.

There had been a time, Emeric used to tease her, when her ledgers read like confessionals. *Quentin burned the bread again and blamed the gulls.* *Quentin sang to the lamp and the lamp did not mind.* He'd found one once, early in their marriage, and read it aloud in bed until she threatened him with a pillow, and after that he called her entries *the true history of Gullwrack* and hers the only voice he trusted on the weather.

*You write yourself out of it,* he had said once, not long before the end, tapping the page where she'd recorded a supply run without recording that she'd been seasick the whole way home. *One day you'll look back and there'll be nothing left of you in here but barometers.*

He had been wrong about that. There was nothing left of her in the ledgers now but barometers, and she had done it on purpose, and she was not thinking about that tonight either.

She turned instead to the packet.

The oilskin was good stuff, sailor's stock, tied with the kind of double hitch Emeric used to tie and no one else tied quite the same way — but she made herself set that observation aside, because she was aware, even at midnight, even alone with two lamps and a candle, that a woman who wanted desperately to be deceived would find confirmation in a knot. She untied it with fingers that were steadier than she had any right to expect and spread the letters out on the scrubbed pine table like cards dealt by an unfriendly god.

Seven letters. All in the same hand. All in *his* hand.

She started with the earliest — dated fourteen months after the funeral she had not attended, because there had been nothing to bury but the sea's apology, which is to say nothing at all.

---

The forgery, if it was a forgery, was a masterpiece, and she hated it the way you hate anything excellent that has been aimed at you.

She knew Emeric's writing the way she knew the sound of his boots on the tower stairs — not as information but as geography. The particular forward lean of it, as if every word were walking into a headwind. The way his capital E's had a little serif like a wave cresting. The way he crossed his t's low and lazy unless he was angry, in which case they got slashed like sails in a squall. She had addressed forty of his envelopes in her life and received sixty of his letters, and she had read each of them the way you drink water when you're thirsty: entirely, without analysis.

Now she drank like a chemist.

She took a blank sheet from the drawer and made a column. On one side, the letters. On the other, her memory, which she distrusted almost as much as she trusted it.

*Item:* the letter-writer dropped his a's into ovals, open at the top, slightly narrow. Emeric's a's. She checked against the oldest letter she owned — the one from their first winter here, water-stained, kept in the ledger itself between the pages for March, where it had lived so long the paper had taken on the shape of its neighbors. Same oval. Same narrowness. But anyone who'd seen one of his letters could copy an oval. She wrote *inconclusive* and moved on.

*Item:* the spacing. This was subtler. Emeric wrote tight at the start of a line and looser toward the end, always, as though his thoughts ran ahead of his hand and his hand spent the whole line catching up. She measured — actually measured, with the brass rule she used for chart work — and the letters on the table did the same thing. Tight to loose, line after line, faithful to the flaw. Her skin prickled. Forgers copied letters. They rarely copied *rhythms*. She wrote *better than inconclusive* and underlined it twice and then scratched the underline out because it looked like fear.

*Item:* ink. She touched the tip of her finger to a word and smelled it. Iron-gall, homemade, the cheap way — vinegar rather than wine. She knew that smell. It was the smell of every letter she'd ever gotten from him, because Emeric bought ink exactly once in his life, decided it was extortionate, and thereafter made his own in a jam jar, and the whole house had smelled of vinegar for a decade, and she had complained, and he had said *it keeps the words honest, Quent, nothing sour ever lies* —

She put her finger down. She sat very still.

The stranger coughed in his sleep across the room, a deep wet sound, and turned his face to the chairback, and she was grateful for the interruption because it stopped her from doing something undignified with her hands.

*Item,* she wrote, and her pen hesitated over the word like a bird deciding whether to land. *Content.*

Because here was the thing she had been circling all night the way the wind circled the tower: the handwriting might be copied by any clever thief. But the letters knew things.

The first one — the earliest — spoke of the winter light on the water east of the point, how it came up through the morning fog like something being decided slowly. It mentioned that the third stair from the bottom still creaked in the same place, *left side, where I always meant to fix it and never did because I liked hearing you come down in the morning.* It asked after Mrs. Prynne at the harbor shop, whose hip was bad, and hoped the winter hadn't worsened her, and added — in parentheses, because Emeric's asides always lived in parentheses — *(tell her the marmalade was criminal and I think of it fondly).*

Mrs. Prynne's marmalade. Quentin had never written to anyone about the marmalade. It was not the sort of detail that traveled. It was the sort of detail that died in a kitchen, in a marriage, in a joke told twice.

Her eyes were stinging. She wiped them with the heel of her hand, crossly, and pressed on.

The second letter described a dream about the tower — *I dreamed the light went around and around and everyone it touched stood still, and you were in the gallery waving your arms trying to make them move, which is the most Quentin dream anyone has ever had, including Quentin* — and the third asked whether she still kept the ledger honestly, *or have you finally started lying to it about the state of your heart.*

She had, as it happened. She had started lying to it within the month of his death. She wrote *wind SW, glass falling* and called it a full accounting of herself, and some nights she could almost believe the ledger didn't know better.

But the fourth letter was the one that broke her discipline, because the fourth letter contained the freckles.

It was dated two years past. It was mostly news — such news as a dead man can send — talk of a port town unnamed, of work that paid, of *people I am careful around, which is a skill I learned too late and at too high a price.* And then near the end, in a smaller hand, the way he wrote things he wasn't sure he had the right to say:

*I saw a girl on the harbor steps yesterday with freckles like yours, all across the nose like someone shook a pepper pot at her, and I nearly made a fool of myself asking after strangers. You used to hate them. You used to stand at this window in summer cursing the sun for finding you. I hope you've made peace with the pepper pot. I hope somebody tells you the truth, which is that I counted them once, the summer we married, and got to two hundred and six, and lost my place, and started over, and you never caught me doing it. Two hundred and six, Quent. I'd know that arithmetic anywhere in the world.*

Quentin put the pen down.

She put both hands flat on the table, because the table was solid, and tables could be relied upon, and she needed one reliable thing while the ground did whatever it was doing beneath her chair.

Two hundred and six.

He had never told her. In eleven years of marriage he had never once let slip that he'd counted, and she had never once caught him counting, and she had *looked*, God help her, she had caught him watching her a hundred times in a hundred summers and assumed he was simply being Emeric, which was its own explanation for everything. The knowledge arrived in her chest like an intruder letting himself into a locked house — hope, that was, plain hope, the rudest guest imaginable, tracking seawater across the clean floors of her grief, opening cupboards she had nailed shut, whistling.

*No,* she said aloud, to the hope, or the freckles, or the empty chair. Her voice came out rusty. Across the room the stranger stirred and muttered and settled again, and she lowered her voice to the register she used for arguing with gulls. "No. It doesn't follow."

It didn't. That was the arithmetic she could trust. Even if the hand was true — even if every oval a and every tightening line was genuine — the letters proved only that Emeric Okonkwo had been alive fourteen months ago. They did not prove he had sent them. They did not explain why a man who knew about the marmalade and the freckles and the third stair had let three years go by without sending so much as a postcard. Letters could be stolen. Letters could be forged by someone who had known him well enough to steal his habits along with his hand. Letters could be *bait*, said a small cold voice that sounded annoyingly like her own common sense, and she told it sternly to wait its turn.

She gathered the letters, squared their edges, squared them again, gave up on squaring them. Her hands wanted occupation, so she gave them the teapot, and the act of making tea at three in the morning felt like the sanest rebellion available.

The stranger was awake. She noticed it the way you notice weather changing — not by seeing but by feeling, some shift in the pressure of the room. His eyes were open, fixed on the packet on the table with an expression she catalogued automatically, keeper-fashion: not curiosity exactly. *Recognition.* The look of a man seeing his own luggage come round on a carousel.

"You should be sleeping," she said. "You nearly drowned. It's considered poor form to survive a drowning and then skimp on rest."

"Couldn't help noticing," he said, voice gravelled, "you're up."

"I'm often up. The lamp requires company." She poured two cups anyway, because refusing tea to a half-drowned man felt like a sin even the ledgers would record, and carried his over. He took it with hands that were still rope-burned and shaking faintly, and he watched her walk back to the table and sit down between the lamps with the packet, and she watched him watch, and neither of them said one honest thing, and the silence between them grew politely enormous.

"What's your name?" she asked suddenly. "I never asked. Last night you were mostly fish."

A pause. A half-second too long, the length of a man choosing. "Jude," he said.

"Just Jude?"

"Just Jude."

"Well then, Just Jude." She sipped her tea. "When you're steady on your legs, the harbor road will be passable, and you can tell me everything you remember about where you found this packet. Every rock, every wave, every hour. I'm a great one for details."

Something crossed his face — quickly, badly hidden, the way a man hides a flinch when you touch a bruise he hasn't mentioned. "Details," he agreed. "I'm sure I'll remember plenty."

"Mm," said Quentin, and smiled at him with all the warmth of a lighthouse beam, which is to say: steadily, brilliantly, and with the specific intention of warning ships off rocks.

He drank his tea. She drank hers. Outside, the tail of the storm finally let go of the shutters and moved off down the coast, and in the sudden quieter air she heard the lamp turning overhead, patient as ever, sweeping its two hundred and six degrees of light across a sea that had, three years ago, given her up for widowed and was now apparently reconsidering.

When Jude slept again, she opened the ledger to the current day and dipped her pen.

*Wind moderating,* she wrote. *Stranger in the house says his name is Jude. Letters on the table are in Emeric's hand.*

Then, after a moment, in smaller script, in parentheses, because some asides could only ever live in parentheses:

*(two hundred and six)*

She closed the book on it before she could think better, and blew out one of the lamps, and went to see whether the storm had left anything else on her doorstep besides the impossible.
