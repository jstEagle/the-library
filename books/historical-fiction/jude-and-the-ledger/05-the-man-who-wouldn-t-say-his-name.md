## 5. The Man Who Wouldn't Say His Name

The first thing I learned about Quentin Okonkwo was that she interrogated the way other people made tea: patiently, with heat applied gradually, and with an expectation that everything in the pot would eventually come out.

She waited two days after I could stand without the room tilting. Two days of soup set down beside my cot without comment, of firewood appearing in the stove, of her moving through the tower on some private railway of routine—up the stairs at intervals, down again, always pausing at the lamp room window to look at the sea as though it owed her money. Then she pulled a chair to the middle of the kitchen, set it facing me, and sat down with her ledger under one arm like a magistrate's Bible.

"Name," she said.

I had prepared for this. I had prepared for it on the boat, in fact, composing lies between waves the way a drowning man composes prayers. But all my prepared lies evaporated under her gaze, and what came out instead was:

"Tomas."

She wrote it down. The scratch of her pencil was the most accusatory sound I had ever heard.

"Tomas what?"

"Just Tomas." I summoned a cough, weak and seamanlike. "The fever's still in me. Names come hard."

"Names," she said, "come hard to honest men too, so you're in good company either way." She turned the ledger around so I could see her handwriting, neat as stitching. *Stranger recovered from surf. Calls himself Tomas.* "Where's your boat?"

"Gone."

"I can see that. Where was it?"

"Out there." I gestured at the window, which was generous of the sea, since it covered everything.

Her expression did not change, but something behind it shifted, the way ice shifts on a pond before it breaks. "Mr. Tomas. I have kept this light for six years. In that time I have pulled four men out of the water. Three of them talked until I begged them to stop—about their ships, their cargoes, their wives, their sins, in ascending order of detail. One of them never spoke again, poor soul, but he wept at me comprehensively. You are the first to treat a simple question like a confession."

"I don't like talking about it."

"Most men don't. They do it anyway."

"The *Alcyone*," I said, because a lie needs a keel or it capsizes at once. "Coaster out of the north ports. Cargo of—" I searched frantically and found the most boring word in the language. "Barley."

"Barley."

"And general goods."

"General barley," she said. "And where were you bound?"

"South."

"That's not a port."

"It's a direction. I was going in it."

For a moment—and I would swear to this on any stack of Bibles you care to produce—I thought she was going to laugh. Her mouth moved in a way that suggested mutiny among the muscles, and then discipline was restored, and she looked down at the ledger and wrote something else. I have wondered since what she wrote. My guess is: *Liar. Bad at it.*

"The storm broke your ship," she said. "Of barley and general goods."

"On the rocks. I got into the water. I held a packet."

"You held a packet."

"Of papers. Personal." I put my hand where the oilskin had lived against my chest for so long that its absence felt like a missing tooth. She had taken it while I slept; of course she had. Any sensible woman would have. "Letters. From a friend."

"A friend whose letters you risk drowning for but won't name."

"He's dead," I said, which was true, if you counted three years of paperwork.

Something crossed her face then, quick as a gull. And I understood, with the sick lurch of a man who has stepped off a stair that isn't there, that she knew the hand inside that packet. That she had known it before I woke. That every question she asked me was being checked against something she already carried.

"All right, Tomas-who-was-going-south," she said, closing the ledger. "You'll stay until you can walk to the harbor without falling over. After that, the road is that way." She pointed, unnecessarily, at the entire horizon. "You'll earn your keep. There's nets in the boathouse need mending, and a shutter hanging by one nail, and you'll chop wood until the pile embarrasses itself. You'll eat at that table and sleep in that cot and give me no trouble."

"No trouble," I agreed.

"And when you remember who you really are," she said, rising, "you'll let me know. I'm told memory comes back gradual. Like fevers."

She went up the stairs. I sat in the chair and listened to her climb, and I thought: *Emeric, you wretched man, whatever you were to her, she is far too good for the whole family.*

* *

Cohabitation with a grieving lighthouse keeper, I discovered, is conducted mostly in silence, and the silence has grammar.

There was the silence of morning, which meant *the coffee is poured, do not speak until the second cup*. There was the silence she deployed when I mispronounced something in her kitchen—which meant *you may be a stranger and possibly a criminal, but you do not put the kettle on the wrong burner*. And there was the long evening silence, the one with weight in it, when she sat with her ledger open to pages she wasn't reading and I sat with my hands still and both of us listened to the wind test the shutters like a debt collector at a door.

I mended nets. This was no hardship; our mother taught all three of us to mend net before she taught us letters, on the grounds that letters wouldn't feed us and nets might. I sat in the boathouse with the mesh across my knees and worked the shuttle through the knots, and it was the closest thing to prayer I'd done in years, my hands remembering a rhythm older than my grief.

Quentin pretended not to notice the nets. This was a performance requiring considerable commitment. Each morning she walked past the boathouse to check the mooring lines, and each morning her eyes flicked to the growing pile of repaired mesh, and each morning she arranged her face into elaborate indifference, the way a cat arranges itself into a position suggesting it never wanted to be petted anyway.

On the fourth day she stopped mid-stride, examined a splice, and said, "Who taught you knots?"

"My mother."

"Hm." A pause. "It's good work."

From her, I understood, this was roughly equivalent to a knighthood.

"She'd have liked hearing that," I said, and it was even true.

That afternoon I fixed the shutter. This required holding it against the wall with one arm while driving nails with the other, in a wind that seemed personally offended by carpentry, and I swore with such fluency that she came out onto the step ostensibly to fetch peat and actually to watch, and I saw the corner of her mouth do the thing it did—the almost-laugh, the suppressed insurrection.

"You hold it like a man who's built things," she said.

"Ships' rails. Fences. Once a coffin." I didn't mean to say that last part. It came out the way truth does, sideways, wearing work clothes.

"Whose?" she asked, and her voice had changed—not softer exactly, but aimed differently.

"A friend's," I said. "Empty. We buried him with honors and nothing in the box but his boots and a bottle of rum he'd have hated to waste." All of it true, every word, about the wrong grave. That was the trick of my half-truths: they were all real stories, just borrowed, worn like another man's coat. Emeric's funeral had been real. The empty box had been real. I had simply been standing at the rail of it, fourteen years old in my heart, refusing to believe my brother fit in something so small.

Quentin looked at me for a long moment. Wind pulled at her collar.

"Emeric Okonkwo's coffin was also empty," she said quietly. "Did you know that? They gave me a box with his coat in it. His good coat. I used to think about the tailor who sold it to him, how he'd have been furious to hear where it ended up. Buried without him. Overcharged for the occasion."

"I'm sorry," I said. And here is the trouble with circling someone politely for days: your feet learn the shape of the circle, and sometimes you drift across the line into meaning it.

"So am I," she said, and went inside, and the shutter and I finished our argument with the wind alone.

* *

By the sixth day we had fallen into something so much like domesticity that it frightened me more than the sea had.

We ate together at the little table—salt fish, bread she baked in a manner that suggested the oven should be grateful, tea strong enough to trot a horse. She had opinions about everything and shared them only grudgingly, like a miser paying out coins. I learned that she took her bread with salt butter and considered jam an affectation. That she read in the evenings, one book at a time, straight through, including the prefaces. That she talked to the lamp when she trimmed it—not tenderly, but the way you talk to a colleague: *hold tonight, old girl, there's weather coming and neither of us gets a pension.*

In return she learned, whether she meant to or not, that I hummed when I mended—that same three-note phrase over and over, our mother's mending-song, though I changed the key so it wouldn't be recognized. That I flinched, slightly, at the sound of oars against a hull. That I always sat facing the window.

"You watch the water like it's going to apologize," she observed one evening.

"Or confess," I said.

"To what?"

"To whatever it's carrying."

She studied me across the table, lamplight catching the gray coming in at her temples, the freckles that Emeric had once written three whole paragraphs about in a letter our mother kept in the drawer with the good scissors. *Like a constellation,* he'd written. *She claims they make her look like a farm girl. I intend to spend fifty years mapping them.* I had read those paragraphs a hundred times. I had forged his hand well enough to fool her with them, God forgive me, and it was those freckles I'd stolen the joke from, and now the freckles themselves were three feet away, attached to a woman deciding whether to trust me, and I could not look at them without feeling like a burglar in a chapel.

"You're not a barley man," she said suddenly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Six years here. I know the coaster men. Barley runners are cheerful, stupid, and generous, usually all at once. You're none of those." She sipped her tea. "You're careful. Careful men carry cargo worth being careful about."

"Perhaps I'm just naturally somber."

"No one is naturally somber. Somberness is acquired." She rose, collected the plates, and paused at my shoulder—a deliberate stop, I realized, close enough that it was a kind of question. "Whatever you're carrying, Tomas. Whatever's in that packet. It will come ashore eventually. Everything does. This coast collects debts."

Then she was at the basin, sleeves rolled, working, and I sat looking at my own reflection warped in the dark window—my brother's chin, my mother's brow, a liar's convenient face—and I thought about the letters hidden in the floor of my cot, forged in a rented room by a man who had told himself he was protecting his brother, and about how the woman washing dishes in front of me had read them and gone white as the foam on the rocks.

Everything comes ashore eventually. I believed her. I was counting on a few more days of tide, that was all.

* *

On the eighth night the wind backed around to the northeast and began to build, and she worked the lamp early, and I carried oil up the stairs for her because my shoulder had healed enough to be useful and because the lamp room at lighting-time was the finest thing I had seen since—well. Since the last time I'd stood in a lamp room with my brother, thirty years ago, when he'd lifted me up to see the flame and said, *there, Jude, that's the job of jobs, keeping a fire against all that black water*, and I had wanted to be a keeper ever since, and he had become a smuggler instead, and life is funny if you let it be, which I did not recommend.

Quentin set the mantle glowing and adjusted the clockwork, and the great lens gathered the flame and threw it out over the water, five seconds bright, five seconds dark, and below us the sea ran black and silver to the edge of the world.

"There," she said, satisfied, and then, without looking at me: "You've been in a light before. You knew to stand clear of the lens. Knew the rotation. Don't tell me the *Alcyone* ran a light."

For a moment I considered lying, and then I was simply tired of it, in one specific direction.

"When I was a boy," I said, "my brother lifted me up to see a lamp like this one. Told me it was the best job in the world. Keeping a fire against all that dark."

She turned. The rotating beam passed across her face—lit, dark, lit—and in the flashes I watched her go very still.

"What was your brother's name?" she asked.

And there it was, the ground opening again, right on schedule. Every instinct I owned said *give her nothing*, and every hour of bread and nets and salt butter and almost-laughs said something else entirely.

"Funny thing about names," I said. "They come back gradual. Like fevers."

She held my eyes for a long five seconds of darkness, and in the next flash her expression had settled into something patient and terrible, the look of a woman who has decided to let a knot sit in the rope until morning.

"Finish the oil," she said, "and get down the stairs, Tomas-or-whatever. Because tomorrow I'm rowing to the harbor for supplies, and the harbormaster keeps files, and I have suddenly developed an interest in ship registries."

She turned back to the lamp. Below us, far out in the dark, the swell was beginning to carry the long groan of a sea getting ready for winter, and somewhere south of it, I knew, a relief ship was fitting out for one last run before the ice—and I stood in the glow of the best job in the world, holding oil I had no right to, and wondered how many days I had left before my name came home to roost.
