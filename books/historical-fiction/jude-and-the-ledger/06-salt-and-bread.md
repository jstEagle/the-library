## 6. Salt and Bread

The stranger mended nets the way some men prayed—slowly, silently, with his whole attention bent toward the task as though it might save him.

Quentin noticed this on the fourth morning after she'd hauled him out of the surf, and she resented noticing it. She had planned to dislike him thoroughly and without complication. Disliking a half-drowned stranger who would not give his name was a simple matter; he had made himself easy to dislike by lying badly about where he'd come from, by eating her bread, by occupying Emeric's chair at the table as though chairs were not haunted in this house. But then he had gone out to the drying shed unasked, found the tangle of net she'd been meaning to mend since October, and set to work with hands that clearly knew rope better than they knew lying.

"You'll want to double that mesh," she said from the doorway, mostly to be contrary. "The herring run small this year."

"The herring don't care what I want," he said, and doubled the mesh.

She left him to it. That was the arrangement they had arrived at, without ever discussing it: she left him to things, and he did them anyway. He split kindling until the woodbox overflowed. He scraped the crusted salt from the inside of the storm shutters and oiled the hinges until every one of them swung without complaint—a small miracle she refused to acknowledge aloud, though she caught herself testing them one by one that evening, lifting each shutter and letting it fall, listening to the clean click of well-tended iron like a woman checking another woman's arithmetic for errors and finding none.

He ate what she cooked and never once said it needed anything. This was how she learned he had been poor, or hungry, or both, for longer than was polite to ask about. Quentin had been poor herself once, before Emeric's wages as second mate had steadied them, and she remembered what bread meant to a person who had gone without. You could read a whole history in the way a man held his bowl—close to his chest, angled slightly inward, guarding it even when there was more in the pot. Jude—she still thought of him as Jude, the name stitched into his coat collar, whatever else he claimed—held his bowl like a secret.

* * *

On the fifth evening the wind came around to the northeast and pinned itself against Gullwrack Point with the particular grievance of a wind that intended to stay all winter, and Quentin decided, out of pure self-defense, to teach herself not to listen to it.

The problem was the evenings. Days were full—lamp trimming, log entries, oil measurements, the endless arithmetic of keeping a light fed. But after supper there came a stretch of hours in which the tower hummed, the sea muttered, and grief sat down across from her at the table like an expected guest. Three years of evenings she had filled with ledgers, rereading old entries until the handwriting blurred, going to bed early the way soldiers go over walls.

Tonight the ledgers were balanced, the wicks trimmed, and Jude sat across the table whittling a peg for the loose third stair, and the silence between them had grown so accustomed to itself that it no longer felt like solitude. It felt like company. Quentin distrusted it immediately.

"There's cards in the drawer," she said, before she had decided to say it. "Under the chart of the outer shoals. They were Emeric's."

Jude's knife stopped. She watched him decide not to react to the name—the smallest hitch in his shoulders, quickly mastered. Whoever he was, he was getting better at hiding it, which was its own sort of answer.

"Your husband played?" he asked carefully.

"Badly." She fetched the deck herself, worn soft as cloth, corners rounded by a thousand deals. "He cheated at everything except patience, and he lost at patience on purpose because he said winning solitaire meant you'd been alone too long." She heard herself say it and wished she hadn't—it was more of Emeric than she'd given anyone in three years—but Jude only nodded, solemn as a clerk, and waited for her to deal.

"He taught me a game," he offered. "Called Wreckers. You probably know it."

The name landed strangely in the room, given how he'd arrived, but she let it pass. "Coastal game?"

"Coastal game." He gathered the cards she'd dealt and began sorting them into suits with quick, practiced fingers. "You play in pairs against the table. One of you runs cargo, one runs the light. The runner tries to get three ships home safe; the keeper tries to warn them off the rocks. If the keeper plays a warning card—" he laid one down, a black queen, "—the ship has to turn back."

"And if she doesn't?"

"If she doesn't, and the runner's bluffing, the ship breaks." He said it lightly, but his thumb moved along the card's edge as if it remembered something. "Emeric always wanted to be the keeper. Said he liked knowing where everything was."

"That sounds like him." Quentin heard the tenderness in her own voice and hated it slightly less than she expected. "I never learned it. He tried to teach me once, on the floor of this very kitchen, and I threw a card at his head because he kept grinning."

"Why?"

"Because he was winning. He grinned hardest when he was winning, and second-hardest when I was angry about it." Her mouth was doing something. She made it stop. "Deal."

They played. The first hand she lost spectacularly, mostly because Jude explained the rules in the manner of a man reciting scripture from memory—accurately, and with no attention whatsoever to whether anyone was following. The second hand she lost more slowly, and by the third she had worked out that the black queens were worth hoarding and that Jude bluffed exactly the way he lied: competently, and with a tell in his left eyebrow that any wife of a cheerful cheat would have spotted across a harbor.

"You're raising that eyebrow," she informed him, playing her queen.

"I'm not."

"You are. Right now. There it goes again."

He clapped his other hand over his brow with such theatrical outrage that something happened in Quentin's chest—a sound, high and involuntary, escaping her before she could catch it.

She was laughing. Not the thin, careful exhalation she permitted herself over the harbormaster's dry notes, but real laughter, undignified and warm, sitting in her ribs like a bird that had flown in through a window and refused to leave.

The room went very quiet. Jude looked at her with an expression she couldn't read—startled, and beneath the startledness, something older and sadder—and Quentin pressed her knuckles to her mouth and felt her eyes sting and thought, with sudden violence: *no*. Because laughing belonged to a life she had closed the ledger on. Because the last person who had made this kitchen ring had done it with that same grin, that same shameless delight, and he was three years drowned, and every hour she spent enjoying the company of this liar at her table was an hour in which the drowning became less unbearable, and she did not want it to become less unbearable. The unbearable was hers. She had paid for it. She kept it trimmed and burning like the light.

"I'm tired," she said, standing, gathering the cards too quickly so that two spilled to the floor. "First watch is early."

"It's barely seven," Jude said gently.

"Then I'm tired early." She left the spilled cards where they lay, which was unlike her, and climbed the stairs to the lamp, and did not look back.

* * *

But the next morning there was bread to bake, and bread required flour from the bin Jude had refilled without being asked, and water from the pump whose handle he'd wrapped in tarred cord so it wouldn't bite her palms in the cold. She stood at the table kneading dough and watching him through the window repair the last stubborn shutter, the one on the north face that had defeated her for two winters, and she thought: *this is how it happens.* Not with fanfare. With flour and firewood and a man who fixes things quietly while your back is turned, until one morning the house works better than it did and you cannot find the seam where he entered it.

She punched the dough harder than necessary.

At noon they ate salt fish and the fresh loaf, and Jude tore his bread the way Emeric used to, from the middle outward, leaving a ruin of crumbs, and Quentin pushed the butter dish toward him without a word and hated herself for the gesture's ease.

"You play again tonight?" he asked.

"No."

"Because you lost?"

"Because I have a light to keep."

"You kept it fine yesterday. I checked. It's the brightest thing on this coast." He said it plainly, without flattery, the way you'd report the weather, and somehow that made it worse.

"The brightest thing on this coast," Quentin said, "was my husband, and the sea took him anyway. Brightness is no guarantee of anything."

Jude was quiet for a moment, turning his cup. Then, not looking up: "My brother was like that. Bright." A pause, delicate as a foot placed on thin ice. "The family joke was that he could talk his way out of a locked room."

Something shifted under Quentin's sternum—not pain exactly, but the memory of pain, the way a healed break aches before weather. "Was? Is he—"

"Drowned," Jude said, too smoothly. "Some years ago now."

And there they sat, the two of them, the widow and the liar, each mourning a man at the same table, and only one of them honest about it. Quentin looked at his bowed head, at the crumbs he was absently gathering into his palm the way a boy gathers shells, and felt the strangest impulse—not pity, quite. Recognition. As though they were both keeping lights over the same stretch of dark water, each convinced the other's beam was false.

"Tonight, then," she heard herself say. "Cards. But if you raise that eyebrow at me again, I'm putting you back in the surf."

"Fair terms," said Jude, and the corner of his mouth lifted, and it was so like Emeric's grin for one half-second that Quentin had to grip the table's edge.

* * *

That night they played until the candle guttered. She won two hands and accused him of letting her, and he denied it with such elaborate wounded dignity that she laughed again—twice, freely, before she could mount any defense—and somewhere past ten o'clock, shuffling the worn deck, she realized she had not once thought about the hour, or the ledger, or the particular weight of an empty side of the bed. The realization frightened her more than any storm. Grief had been her work, her discipline, the one duty she'd assigned herself that no one could take away, and here was this man dismantling it with a deck of marked cards and a talent for shutters.

She said good night first, as she always would, and climbed the tower stairs, and lit the lamp, and stood a long while watching the beam swing out over the black water.

From below came the small sounds of Jude banking the stove, the creak of him settling into the chair by the window—he slept there, or pretended to, and she had stopped pretending not to notice. The house held them both like a hull holds passengers who boarded separately and for different ports.

*Three ships home safe,* she thought. *One keeper at the light.* She wondered idly which card he'd play if their positions were reversed, and then she heard it: hooves on the cliff road, unlikely and urgent, and then a fist hammering on the tower door at midnight, and the harbormaster's boy shouting through the wind that there was news, urgent news, from the harbor—

Quentin was already moving down the stairs.
