## 7. The Harbormaster's Summons

The word came by way of Tam Broadbelt, who came by way of the cliff path, who came by way of no path at all in the sense that he slid the last twenty yards on his backside and arrived at the tower door looking like a man who had lost an argument with a hillside.

Quentin heard him before she saw him — a huffing, cursing progress up the rocks, punctuated by the occasional theological exclamation. She opened the door before he could knock, mostly to spare the door.

"Mrs. Okonkwo," Tam wheezed, removing his cap, which was at that point more mud than cap. "Thought you'd want word straight off."

"Tam. You've brought the harbor to my doorstep. How kind."

"Harbor's freezing," he said, as though reporting a death in the family, which in a manner of speaking he was. "Meridian's making one last run before the ice closes us in. One run, Mrs. Okonkwo. One."

Behind Quentin, inside the tower, a kettle began its slow climb toward a whistle. Jude looked up from the net he was mending — he had been mending the same net for three days, and doing it badly, and both of them pretended otherwise — and Quentin felt something turn over in her chest like a stone in a tide pool.

"One what?" she asked, though she knew.

"One berth." Tam wiped his nose on his sleeve with the frankness of a man who had never owned a handkerchief and saw no reason to start at his age. "Evacuation berth. Warm bunk, hot food, passage south past the freeze line. Harbormaster says whoever wants it has to be at the quay when she signals. Could be tomorrow. Could be three days. Weather decides."

"And there's only the one?"

"Only the one. Meridian's half-cargo of grain as it is. They're not running a ferry, they're running a mercy, and mercies come small this time of year." He jammed the cap back on his head. "I told them you'd not take it. Told them you'd sooner marry the lamp. But I said I'd carry word all the same, because your Emeric—" He stopped. Quentin watched him stop. It was like watching a man step into a hole he'd forgotten was there.

"My Emeric is dead, Tam," she said, gently enough. "You may finish sentences mentioning him. I won't break."

"Aye. Well." He shifted. His eyes flicked past her shoulder to the stranger sitting among the ruins of a fishing net, and Quentin saw the arithmetic happen behind Tam's face — the shipwrecked man, three weeks now, still here, still nameless to most of Gullwrack's gossips, which meant he was by now named a dozen different things to all of them. "There's two of you, then."

"There are two of us," Quentin agreed.

"Well." Tam looked at the sky, at the sea, at his boots, anywhere but at the problem he'd carried up the cliff. "One berth."

"I heard you the first time, Tam."

He nodded, touched his cap again, and began the long business of turning around on the ledge. Halfway through the maneuver he paused and said, without looking up, "You've kept that light through worse than ice, Mrs. Okonkwo. But nobody ever froze to death keeping it, neither. Think on that however you like." And then he was gone, sliding and huffing, down the path and out of her morning.

Quentin stood in the doorway a while, watching the gray water work itself against the rocks, and did not think about anything at all. It was a skill she had refined over three years. She could stand in a doorway, or row a boat, or trim a wick, and be perfectly empty, a room with the furniture draped in sheets. It was a comfort, that emptiness. It had never once lasted.

The kettle screamed behind her.

* * *

They ate bread and salt fish and the last of the pickled onions, because Jude had developed, in his weeks ashore, an enthusiasm for pickled onions that Quentin found both baffling and faintly endearing, the way one finds a dog endearing for barking at the moon.

"The Meridian," he said, in the careful tone of a man walking across ice he suspected was thin. "That's real, then. Not just harbor talk."

"It's real. Tam doesn't climb cliffs for rumors. The climb costs him too much dignity to spend on rumors."

Jude turned an onion fork over on his plate. "One berth."

"So I'm told."

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "You should have it."

"I didn't ask for your opinion on the matter."

"No," he agreed mildly. "But you were going to get it anyway, so we might as well skip the part where you glare at me and I pretend not to notice."

Despite herself — and she was very much despite herself — Quentin laughed. It escaped before she could catch it, a short, rusty sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled since before Emeric died. She hated it instantly and thoroughly. She hated how easily it came now, how the tower had developed habits around this man, how there were two mugs by the basin where there had been one, how she had started saying *we* in her head when she thought about firewood.

"You should take it," Jude said again, quieter. "The light can go dark for a season. Harbors close. Nobody's sailing in that ice anyway. But you — you've got a life somewhere south of this rock, presumably. People."

"People," Quentin repeated. She buttered her bread with great attention. "What people would those be?"

"Sisters. An aunt. Someone."

"I have a sister in Penzance who writes me twice a year to tell me her husband has been passed over for promotion again, and an aunt who died believing I lived in Cornwall, which I never have." She set the knife down. "My household consists of a lamp, a ledger, and an uninvited guest who mends nets the way a goat mends gardens. If I leave, the light goes dark until spring, and if a boat runs aground on Gullwrack in February because its keeper took the warm bunk south, I will know about it every night for the rest of my life."

"And if you stay," Jude said, "you'll know about it every night regardless, because you'll have frozen in the dark out of stubbornness."

"Stubbornness," said Quentin, "is a virtue that has never once required my approval to function."

It was meant as a joke. It landed as a joke; Jude smiled. But underneath it, both of them heard the other thing, the true thing: she was already staying. She had been staying since Tam slid away down the cliff. The decision was made and she was merely circling it, the way you circle a grave before you let yourself read the stone.

* * *

That afternoon they battened the shutters together, because the barometer had begun its slow sulk downward and because work was better than conversation, which had become dangerous. Quentin handed tools; Jude hammered; between them they got the east shutter seated properly for the first time since the first storm, and Quentin stood back and looked at it and thought, absurdly, *Emeric never could get that shutter to sit right.* Then she thought: *stop it,* and then she thought: *but he couldn't.*

"You're doing that thing," Jude observed, driving the last nail.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at something I've done and compare it unfavorably to someone. Your face goes very still, like a woman deciding whether to correct a vicar."

"My face does no such thing."

"It absolutely does. You did it over the card game Tuesday. I dealt, and you looked at my hands like they'd personally insulted you."

"They had. You shuffle like a man palming stolen silver."

"Emeric shuffled worse," Jude said, lightly, and Quentin went still all over, because he had not spoken the name before. Not once in three weeks. She had noticed its absence the way you notice a clock stopping — not at once, but with a slow accumulation of wrongness.

"You've never said his name," she said.

Jude's hands stilled on the hammer. Outside, the wind tested the new shutter, found it firm, and moved on, disappointed.

"Should I have?"

"You've avoided it the way you'd avoid a loose board. Deliberately. While pretending not to." She looked at him. He looked at the shutter. "You knew him. Before the wreck. Don't tell me — I'm not asking. I'm telling you I've noticed, and I notice everything on this rock, including you, especially you, and one of these days you are going to tell me the truth about how a man comes to be carrying himself onto my rocks in the middle of the worst storm in ten years, and I am going to listen, and it is going to change something. That's all. Pass me the tar brush."

Jude passed her the tar brush. Their fingers didn't touch, or almost didn't; there was a half-second where the brush hung between them, claimed by neither, and then Quentin took it and the moment went on its way like a gull riding the wind off the point.

"Whatever you decide about the berth," Jude said to the shutter, "decide it soon. Weather won't wait for you to be ready."

"Nothing waits for you to be ready. That's the whole trick of weather. Of everything."

* * *

Evening came down early, as it did now, the dark arriving like a debt collector. Quentin climbed the tower stairs and lit the lamp, and performed the small liturgies of the light — wick, glass, pressure, log — and wrote in the ledger:

*Wind SW freshening. Barometer falling. Shutter east repaired. Lamp satisfactory.*

Then she sat with the pen above the page for a long moment, and wrote, beneath it, in smaller letters, the first personal entry she had made in three years:

*Two of us. One berth.*

She looked at the words. They sat on the page with terrible simplicity, six words holding the whole shape of the winter. She read them again. Two of us. As though she and the stranger were a unit, a crew, a household — as though three weeks of shared bread and bad card games and a repaired shutter amounted to a *we*. When had that happened? Who had authorized it?

She thought of the oilskin packet, which she had returned to Jude after reading the letters within it, and which now lived wherever Jude kept things he did not discuss — under his coat, she supposed, against his chest, where he'd carried it half-drowned from the sea. Letters in Emeric's hand. Letters dated after her husband's funeral. A private joke about freckles written by a dead man. She had spent a week telling herself the packet explained nothing, and another week telling herself it explained everything, and she had settled nowhere, and the not-settling had begun to feel like standing on a floor that was very slowly tilting.

And now the harbormaster had sent a man up a cliff to say: *one berth.* As if the world had heard the question forming in her and answered it in the cruelest possible grammar — not *will you stay or go*, but *which of you*.

She closed the ledger. Below in the common room, she could hear Jude moving about, the clink of the lamp being trimmed down there, the ordinary sounds of a man making himself at home in a home that wasn't his. She found she could not begrudge him a single clink.

"Damn it all," Quentin said quietly, to the lamp, to the ledger, to the winter generally. The lamp, being a lamp, offered no opinion.

She went down the stairs. Jude was sitting at the table with the cards, dealing two hands out of habit, and when he saw her he paused, the deck still in his hands, a question on his face that he was too polite to ask and she was too honest to ignore.

"We need to talk about the berth," she said.

"We do," he agreed. And then, as she opened her mouth: "Before we do — there's something I should show you. Something in the packet I haven't shown you yet. I keep telling myself you don't need it, and I keep not believing myself, and I think the berth has made it urgent." He set the cards down, squared them neatly, unnecessarily, his hands needing occupation. "There's a letter in there addressed to you, Quentin. By name. And I should have given it to you weeks ago."

The lamp above them turned steady and bright, and the wind rose a note against the glass, and Quentin sat down slowly at the table and put her hands flat on the wood.

"Show me," she said.
