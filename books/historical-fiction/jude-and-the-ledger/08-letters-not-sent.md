## 8. Letters Not Sent

Jude Okonkwo had been many kinds of liar in his thirty-one years, but he prided himself on never lying badly. A bad lie was a kind of insult, he thought — it assumed the person you were deceiving couldn't count. He had given the woman at Gullwrack Point a name that was almost true, a story that was almost honest, and a manner so carefully bland that only a person looking for blandness would ever notice it.

Quentin Okonkwo was not looking for blandness. She was looking at him the way she looked at her ledgers, he suspected: checking the sums.

That was why, when she went down to the boathouse to see what the storm had shaken loose from her stores, Jude climbed the tower stairs instead of resting as he'd been told to do.

His ribs still ached where the sea had handled him like a debt collector. His hands had stopped shaking two days ago. The cut on his brow had scabbed over into a dark seam he could feel when he frowned, and he frowned often now, because frowning was honest work and he needed some of that in his life.

The lamp room smelled of oil and hot brass and, faintly, of a man's cologne that had soaked into the wood years ago and never quite left. Emeric's cologne. Jude knew it the way you know a childhood kitchen — not by naming it, but by the way your chest closes half an inch.

He hadn't been alone with his brother's handwriting since the packet washed ashore in his keeping. And he had not yet read all of it. That was the truth he'd been avoiding, dressed up as caution: there were letters in the bottom of the oilskin wrap he had not let himself open, because he knew what they would be.

He sat down on the keeper's stool, set the lantern between his feet, and unwrapped the packet with the care of a surgeon and the reluctance of a man opening a bill.

* * *

There were eleven letters. Nine were addressed to Quentin. Two were addressed to no one.

Jude had written none of them.

He read them again anyway, because reading them was the penance he'd assigned himself, and because each time he did, he found something new to feel terrible about. The ninth letter — dated fourteen months after the wreck that had killed nobody — described a market day in a town called Pell's Harbor, the price of mackerel, a dog that had stolen a whole wheel of cheese and defended it like a fortress. It was warm. It was funny. It was Emeric to the bone, every cadence of him, the jokes built the way Emeric built jokes, sideways, so you didn't see the punchline until you were standing on it.

And it was forged. Every word of it, forged by Jude himself, in a hand he had practiced against old letters until his wrist ached and his eyes burned and the forgery had become, briefly, more familiar than his own signature.

That was the part he never said aloud, even to himself, even in the privacy of his own skull: *it had felt good*. Sitting in a rented room in Pell's Harbor with a stolen stack of his brother's correspondence spread before him like scripture, writing letters from a dead man — it had felt like having him back. For three weeks, Emeric had been alive again at the end of Jude's pen, complaining about weather, asking after Quentin's freckles, signing himself *yours entirely* in that looping hand that had won arguments in three counties.

Then Jude would put down the pen and Emeric would be gone again, properly gone, and the room would be just a room, and the letters would be what they actually were: bait.

He had told himself it was necessary. The ring wanted proof. Old Corvane, who ran the operation out of Pell's Harbor the way a spider runs a web — patiently, and with an appetite — wanted to know whether Emeric Okonkwo was truly at the bottom of the sea or merely pretending to be, and if pretending, where his wife was, and how much leverage a wife might be worth. They had sent Jude north with an oilskin packet and a simple instruction: *get close to the widow. See what she knows. If the letters draw anyone out of hiding, follow them.*

They had not known Jude was Emeric's brother. That was the one mercy in the arrangement, and also the one cruelty, depending on the hour. Corvane's people recruited by need, not blood; they had found a man drowning in gambling debts and given him a rope made of rope's-end. Your brother's handwriting, they'd said. You're the only one alive who can do it justice.

They had been right about that, at least. Nobody else alive knew that Emeric crossed his sevens, or that he wrote *Quentin* with a flourish that looked careless but took him twice as long as the rest of the word, or that he ended love letters with *yours entirely* because *forever* frightened him and he'd admitted as much exactly once, drunk, on a pier in Pell's Harbor, the summer before everything.

Jude knew all of it. Jude had used all of it. Jude had sat in that rented room and thought, with the specific self-loathing available only to brothers, *he'll never know how well I can imitate him*, and then thought, immediately after, *because he's dead*, and then thought, in the small voice underneath both, *is he?*

Because that was the other thing. The question at the center of everything, the question he had been sent north to answer and had spent four months refusing to answer:

Did Jude believe his brother was dead?

* * *

He laid the letters out on the lamp room floor in the order of their dates, the way he had organized them in Pell's Harbor, a paper trail leading away from a grave that had no body in it. The first three were pre-wreck — real letters, Emeric's own, which the ring had taken from his lodgings after the "drowning" and which Jude was meant to study like a textbook. The next six were Jude's work. The last two were blank, held in reserve, in case the widow required further convincing.

The widow did not require convincing. That was the trouble.

Jude had imagined, on the boat north, a grieving woman who would take one look at the packet and weep and clutch it and ask nothing. What he had found instead was a lighthouse keeper who checked sums. She had held Emeric's last real letter up to the lamplight and turned it over twice before speaking, and Jude had watched her mind move behind her eyes like weather, and he had understood, with a lurch, that he had underestimated her by every measure a man can be underestimated.

She hadn't asked yet why a shipwrecked stranger carried letters in another man's hand. She was going to. She was building up to it the way storms built up off this coast — quietly, barometrically, with the gulls going silent first.

He should leave. He had told himself he would leave a dozen times since waking in her bed with his boots off and his head bandaged and the packet missing from under his coat. But the harbor was closed, and the roads were closed, and — these were the excuses, and he catalogued them honestly because a good liar audits his own inventory — the truth was simpler and worse. The truth was that he liked her.

He liked her bread, which was dense enough to ballast a skiff. He liked the way she talked to the lamp as though it were a slightly stupid horse. He liked that she had dragged him out of the surf at all, when leaving him there would have been tidier and cheaper and, God help him, safer for everyone involved. He liked that she had stopped writing herself into her own ledger — he'd seen the gap in the entries, pages of weather and wick-trimmings and not one word about the woman keeping them — and he recognized the trick, because it was his own trick, grief disguised as diligence, absence managed through paperwork.

Two liars circling politely, he had thought once, watching her mend a net with furious competence. Except she wasn't lying. That was the arithmetic that kept him awake. Whatever the sum came to at the end of this, she would have entered it honestly, and he would not.

* * *

He picked up the second-to-last letter — one of the blanks, unsigned, undated — and held it to the lantern light as though the paper might confess something the ink hadn't. Nothing. Just paper. Just a weapon waiting to be loaded.

It occurred to him, not for the first time, that he could simply tell her.

He rehearsed it sometimes, on the gallery walk, when the wind made confession feel anonymous. *Mrs. Okonkwo. My name is not Tam Wilde. My name is Jude Okonkwo, and I am your husband's younger brother, and I have come here carrying forged letters in his hand, sent by men who want either his corpse or his leverage, and I have not decided which side of that transaction I am on.* It sounded better in the wind. In the wind, it sounded almost noble — a brother infiltrating villains, protecting the innocent. On solid ground, in the flat honest light of her kitchen, it would sound like what it was: a man who had taught himself to write from a dead man's hand for money he owed to people who drowned informants.

Because that was the part he had learned too late, the detail that had curdled the whole enterprise: Tomas Ferrow. The informant. The man who had talked to the revenue officers and then gone out in a calm sea and not come back, and whose boat had been found scrubbed cleaner than any working boat has a right to be. Jude had grown up sharing a bed with Emeric in a house that leaked; Tomas Ferrow had shared their table for one entire hungry winter, telling stories about mermaids with the conviction of a man who believed them. And when Jude finally understood what had happened to him — understood it in the oblique, deniable way you understand things in Corvane's orbit, from silences rather than statements — he had been sitting in that same rented room, forging his brother's love.

He had finished the letters anyway. That was the fact he could not forgive, and so he carried it forward instead, the way you carry a stone in a pack — not because you've decided to keep it, but because putting it down would mean admitting you'd picked it up.

But he had also done something else, something he had told no one, not even the wind: he had left one thing out of the packet. A twelfth item. Not a letter at all — a page torn from his own notebook, in his own hand, addressed to Quentin Okonkwo by name, begun eleven times and finished zero times, currently folded into the false bottom of the oilskin wrap where it had been riding against his heart since Pell's Harbor.

*Dear Mrs. Okonkwo,* it began, in the eleventh attempt, and then nothing, because every continuation he tried was either a lie or a confession, and he had not yet determined which she deserved more.

*Trust no one who brings my hand to you.* He had drafted that line once, drunk, and torn it up, and regretted tearing it up, and here he was, regretting it still. If Emeric were alive — if he were out there somewhere behind this fiction of a drowned man, hiding from Corvane the way a hare hides from hounds — then the kindest thing Jude could do for him was exactly what he was doing: keep the widow settled, keep the ring satisfied, keep the lie warm. And if Emeric were dead — truly dead, at the bottom of his invented sea — then the kindest thing Jude could do was burn every letter and never let this woman learn that her grief had been farmed for profit by her husband's own blood.

Either way, the answer was silence. Either way, he lost her before she was ever his to lose. He almost admired the construction of it; Corvane would have.

* * *

Below him, the boathouse door banged. Quentin's voice came up the stairwell, distorted by the tower's acoustics into something formal and faraway: "Tam! There's a boat coming round the point — harbormaster's flag. Get down here."

Jude folded the letters quickly, wrapped them, weighed the packet in his hands. Eleven pieces of a dead man, plus one blank, plus one unfinished confession hidden in the lining.

"Aye," he called down, in the voice of a man named Tam Wilde, and heard how thin it sounded in the stone throat of the stairs.

He stood, tucked the packet inside his coat, and paused at the top of the spiral, one hand on the cold rail, looking back at the lamp — at Emeric's cologne soaked into the wood, at the stool worn smooth by a keeper's habits. Somewhere south of here, alive or otherwise, his brother was either waiting to be rescued or waiting to be avenged, and Jude had come north carrying the instrument of both and no idea which he intended it to be.

"Decide," he said aloud to the empty lamp room. The lamp did not answer. Lamps never did; that was why he liked them.

Then he went down the stairs to meet the harbormaster's boat, wearing his borrowed name, with a letter hidden against his ribs that began *Dear Mrs. Okonkwo* and did not know how to go on — and never noticed, until it was far too late, that he had left the packet's false bottom gaping open, and that Quentin Okonkwo checked sums.
