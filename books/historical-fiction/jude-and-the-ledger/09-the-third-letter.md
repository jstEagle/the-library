## 9. The Third Letter

The storm had a name, though nobody had given it one. Quentin thought of it as *the second visitor*, because it arrived the way unwelcome guests did—noisily, without apology, and with every intention of staying through supper and well past it.

She had battened what could be battened, trimmed the wicks, logged the pressure at a figure that made her mouth go dry, and then found herself with the particular restlessness that came when there was nothing left to do but wait for the weather to make up its mind. The tower hummed around her. Rain came at the glass in fistfuls.

The letters sat on the table where she had left them, in their oilskin packet, looking innocent as bread.

She had read them four times now. She knew them nearly by heart—the account of the fishing grounds off Corvo, the complaint about a bad tooth, the long paragraph about gulls that was really a paragraph about loneliness. She had checked each against her memory the way a woman counts silver after a party, and everything had matched. Everything had been Emeric's.

Which was precisely what troubled her at two in the morning, holding her cold hands around a cup of tea she would not drink.

Because if these letters were real—if he had written them in the years after the *Cormorant* went down—then why had none of them ever arrived?

Letters got lost. She knew this. She had consoled herself with this knowledge for three years, imagining mail sacks sliding into green water, sorting rooms in Lisbon flooded, clerks careless or dead. But four letters, all together, bundled in oilskin, carried inside the shirt of a half-drowned stranger who wouldn't say his own name? Lost letters did not travel in company. They did not arrive in matching dress.

Quentin set down the cup and picked up the packet.

It was heavier than it should have been. She had noticed this before and dismissed it—the weight of grief, she'd told herself, which was famously disproportionate. But now she turned it over under the lamp and saw what she should have seen days ago: a seam along the bottom edge, stitched twice, where the oilskin had been folded back on itself to make a false lining. Sailors' work. Emeric's kind of work. The kind of hidden pocket a man makes when he has something worth hiding and has thought, professionally, about hiding things.

Her heart was behaving badly. She told it so and slit the stitching with her sewing scissors.

Inside were three more sheets of paper.

Two were more of the same—Emeric's slanted hand, dated months apart, one describing weather and one describing nothing much at all, the way his letters always circled toward feeling sideways, never head-on. She read them quickly, hungrily, hating herself for the hunger.

The third sheet was folded smaller than the others, worn soft at the creases, as though it had been carried a long way in someone's pocket. And across it, in the same unmistakable hand, was her name.

*Quentin.*

Not *my dear*. Not *Q*, which is what he called her when they were being funny, or *Mrs. Okonkwo, Keeper*, which is what he called her when he was being funnier. Her full name, spelled out entire, like an address. Like a summons.

She unfolded it with fingers that did not entirely belong to her.

---

*Quentin,*

*If you are reading this, then my hand has found you, and I am sorry for it. I have no time to explain properly and no right to explain at all, so I will spend my few lines on the only thing that matters.*

*Trust no one who brings my hand to you.*

*Not the hand itself. Not the words. Anyone can carry a letter; few can carry it honestly. If a man comes to you with my writing in his pocket, know that he comes for a reason, and the reason is not yours.*

*I am sorry. You will understand someday, or you won't, and either way the light must be kept.*

*Burn this.*

*—E.*

---

She read it twice. Then a third time, because the second reading had not stuck, the words sliding off her mind like rain off oiled canvas.

*Trust no one who brings my hand to you.*

The lamp guttered in a draft and she flinched—actually flinched, a small animal movement she hadn't made since her first winter alone at Gullwrack, when every sound had been a stranger and every stranger a story with teeth.

Jude was asleep in the boathouse. Had been sleeping there, or pretending to, for five nights now. He mended her nets during the day with clever hands and ate her bread at her table and taught her a card game with a deck missing the queen of clubs, and last night she had laughed at something he said—a real laugh, ugly and loud, the first in longer than she cared to count—and had gone to bed feeling like a woman who had let a fox sleep in the henhouse because it had nice manners.

*He comes for a reason, and the reason is not yours.*

She stood very still in the middle of her kitchen while the storm leaned on the tower and the lamp above turned its slow patient circles, and she thought about the sequence of events with a clarity that felt almost like cruelty.

A stranger washes ashore clutching letters from her dead husband. Letters dated after his death. Letters that never came through any post. Letters delivered into exactly the right hands by exactly the wrong method—and the stranger cannot remember his own surname but can remember, in perfect detail, how Emeric used to complain about gulls.

"Well," Quentin said aloud, to the empty room, in the flat voice she used for counting supplies. "Well, then."

She took up the lantern. Then, after a moment's consideration, she took up the filleting knife as well—not the big one, the little sharp one she used for mackerel—and she put on her oilskins, and she went out into the wind.

* * *

The boathouse door groaned on its hinges; the storm had been working at it all night. Inside, the dark smelled of tar and old rope and, faintly, of another person, which was a smell she had unlearned and now recognized with a jolt.

Jude was awake. Of course he was awake. He was sitting up in the nest of blankets she'd given him, and even in the thin spill of lantern light she could see that he'd been awake for some time—that stillness of his, the watchfulness he wore like a borrowed coat.

"Keeper." His voice was rough with sleep he hadn't been having. "Storm wake you?"

"Storms don't wake me anymore." She set the lantern on an upturned crate between them, so that the light fell evenly on both their faces and neither held the advantage of shadow. It seemed only fair. "Something else did."

His eyes went to the knife. He was not subtle about it, but then, she supposed she wasn't either, standing in a doorway in a gale holding a filleting knife like a woman about to gut something.

"You found it," he said quietly.

The bottom dropped out of her stomach, though she kept her voice level, mostly. "You knew there was a letter in the lining."

"I hoped there was." He pushed the blanket back and swung his legs around, and she raised the knife perhaps six inches without meaning to, and he stopped moving at once, hands open on his knees. "I didn't know what it said. I swear that, at least."

"*At least.*" She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "That's a curious arithmetic, Mr.—what shall we use tonight? Still Mr. Cole? Or have we exhausted that one?"

"Quentin—" 

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that, hated him for hearing it. "Don't say my name like we're friends. I set your broken fingers. I fed you soup. I let you teach me a card game that cheats, by the way—I worked out Tuesday night that the dealer wins ties, and the dealer is always you—"

"That's just the rules of Vingt-Neuf," he said, with the ghost of his usual crooked humor, and then seemed to hear himself, and stopped.

"The rules," Quentin repeated. "Yes. There are always rules, aren't there? Rules for mending nets so the keeper doesn't notice how well you mend them. Rules for shipwrecks. Rules for liars." She held up the folded letter, and the lantern flame shone through the soft-worn paper. "*Trust no one who brings my hand to you.* Those are Emeric's words, Jude. Emeric's hand, Emeric's spelling of *harbor*, which he never once spelled correctly in his life, God rest him or don't. And those words say that whoever brought them to me—brought them to me *on purpose*—is not to be trusted."

The wind found a gap somewhere high on the boathouse wall and screamed through it, and neither of them moved.

"So here is what happens now," she said, and was surprised to find her voice had gone calm, the deep calm of deep water. "You are going to tell me who you are. Not the story with the cargo run to Corvo. Not the story about the surname you lost overboard. The true one. And you are going to tell me tonight, because tomorrow the tide turns and the harbor opens, and I will row you to the mainland myself and let the harbormaster ask his own questions, and he asks them with manacles."

Jude looked at her for a long moment. The lantern threw his shadow huge against the upturned dory behind him, and she thought—not for the first time—that whatever else he was, he was not a large man; his shadows simply lied better than most people did.

"If I tell you," he said slowly, "you may wish you hadn't asked."

"I have spent three years wishing I hadn't asked questions I never got to ask at all. My tolerance is considerable."

"And if I tell you," he went on, "you'll want to believe the worst of me. That would be simpler. I'd rather you understood it before you decided."

"I'm not deciding anything at lantern-point in a boathouse in a gale." She lowered the knife an inch—an inch, no more—but she did not lower it away. "Talk."

He rubbed a hand over his face, and for the first time since she'd dragged him out of the surf, he looked not merely tired but young. Younger than her, certainly. Younger than the lie he'd been living in her house.

"My name," he said, "is Jude Okonkwo."

The name landed in the boathouse like a dropped anchor. Quentin heard herself breathe. Once. Twice.

"Emeric was my brother," Jude went on, quietly, watching her face the way a man watches ice he means to cross. "My older brother. Eight years older. He taught me to swim off Blackpool Head and to read tide tables off the same page he learned from, and when our mother died he wrote to me every month for two years, and then the *Cormorant* sank, and I believed he was dead, because everyone believed it, because there was wreckage and there was a body and there was a widow keeping a light at the far end of the world."

"A brother." The word tasted foreign in her mouth. "He never spoke of a brother."

"He wouldn't have." Something flickered across Jude's face—old hurt, quickly shuttered. "We hadn't spoken in years by then. A falling-out. Mine, mostly." He paused. "That part's true and doesn't excuse anything, so you can file it however you like. But I'm his blood, Quentin. And those letters—"

"The letters." Her knuckles had gone white around the paper, and she forced them loose, laid the letter on the crate beside the lantern where she could see both it and him. "Your brother's handwriting. In your brother's pocket. Dated after your brother's death. Explain that, Mr. Okonkwo, and explain it well, because every other explanation I've invented tonight ended with you in the sea again."

Jude opened his mouth.

And closed it.

And looked, for one naked moment, so exactly like Emeric caught at something—he had that same habit, that same terrible honesty of the face arriving a heartbeat too late—that Quentin's whole body went cold.

"You can't," she whispered. "Can you."

"There are things I have to show you first," Jude said. "Or tell you. And you're not going to like the order I need to tell them in."

"The order," Quentin repeated, and her voice rose despite herself, sharp as the knife in her hand. "There's an *order*? There's a proper sequence for deceiving a grieving woman in her own home?"

"Yes," Jude said simply. "And it starts with why I was in that water at all—and ends with the fact that the men who put me there are going to come asking at this light for the same papers you're holding. Probably within the week."

Outside, the storm shifted its grip on the tower, and somewhere below, in the dark between the rocks, the sea drew back for a wave it meant to make count.

Quentin looked at the letter. At the knife. At the last piece of her husband's family alive and dripping in her boathouse, telling her the worst was still on its way.

"Then you'd best start at the beginning," she said. "And Jude—"

He looked up.

"—you'll tell it with that door open, where I can see the sky, and me standing closer to it than you are."

He nodded, and began.
