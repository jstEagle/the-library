## 10. Confession at Low Tide

Jude had been at Gullwrack Point for six days when he finally understood that the tide was going to make him a liar no longer.

It was not conscience that did it. He'd had six days of conscience and it had behaved like a cat with an injured paw—present, throbbing, easily ignored if he kept busy enough. What did it was the boathouse door, which had swollen in the damp so that it stuck halfway open, and which Quentin had fixed that morning without a word, planing the edge down with three strokes of a block plane while he watched from the doorway pretending to study the horizon like a man with nothing on his mind.

Three strokes. No wasted motion. She was a woman who solved problems instead of discussing them, and he was a man whose entire existence at her light depended on problems remaining undiscussed.

The tide went out that afternoon farther than any tide he'd seen since childhood, baring the ribs of the reef below the point, and the whole sea seemed to draw breath and hold it. Low water. Nothing hidden. It felt like a rebuke.

She was in the tower room when he climbed to find her, mending the second wick of the lamp with a pair of tiny scissors, and she looked up at him with those gray eyes that missed nothing except, by some mercy of God or geography, the one thing that mattered.

"Supper's on," he said.

"I know. I can smell it."

"It's the fish again."

"It's always the fish." She snipped a blackened thread of wick and let it fall into her palm. "You burn it, you know."

"The fish?"

"Every time. You get distracted. You stand at the window watching the water like you're expecting someone."

He had no answer prepared for that, which was itself an answer, and he saw her see it—the small stilling of her hands, the way she set the scissors down with exaggerated care, as though they were something more dangerous than scissors.

"Sit down, Mr. Ash," she said, using the false name he'd given her like she was handing him back a borrowed coat that didn't fit. "I think it's time we talked about the weather."

* * *

The weather. As if that were what they'd been circling for six days across two rooms and a hundred careful silences.

They sat at the little table under the lamp because neither of them suggested anywhere else and because the table had become theirs somehow—her chair pulled up on the left, his balanced on the right where the floor creaked, the teapot between them with its chipped spout pointing accusingly at him. The fish burned gently behind him and neither of them moved to save it.

"You've been very kind to me," Jude began.

"Don't start with kindness," Quentin said. "Start with the truth. Kindness is just what people say before the truth when they're frightened of it."

That stopped him. He looked at her—at the wind-scoured face, the freckles that Emeric had once counted in a letter and called *a constellation, my own private chart*, at the callused hands lying flat on the table like things she'd decided not to hide behind—and he understood that whatever he said next was going to hurt her worse than anything he could have done with silence.

"My name isn't Ash," he said.

"No," she said. "I didn't think it was. Ash is a tree. You're not tree-like. You're more of a—" she waved a hand, "—a gull. Something that shows up after wrecks."

"That's unfair to gulls."

"Probably. Go on."

He took a breath and found that his hands wanted to do something, so he turned his teacup in a slow circle, around and around, like a man steering a very small ship toward a very large rock.

"Emeric Okonkwo had a younger brother," he said. "Seven years younger. When Emeric left home—before you knew him—he used to write letters back. Long ones. Ridiculous ones. Half of them were about you, even then, before he'd worked up the courage to say anything to your face. Our mother saved every one."

Quentin had gone very still. Not white—not yet—but still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"He talked about you like weather," Jude went on, because once started he couldn't stop, and because the words had been sitting in his chest for six days doing damage. "'There's a keeper's daughter at Gullwrack,' he wrote, 'who looks at me like I'm a sum that won't balance, and I intend to devote my life to becoming arithmetic.' That's a direct quote. I was eleven. I thought all women were like that, because of you. It took me years to learn most of them aren't."

Something crossed her face—almost a smile, gone before it landed. "He never mentioned a brother."

"He would have. Eventually. He was—" Jude's voice caught on the ordinary pastness of it, "—he was a man who got round to things slowly. He'd have told you about me the way he told you everything, sideways, three years late."

"But he didn't."

"No. He didn't."

"And you are."

"Yes." He made himself look at her. "My name is Jude Okonkwo. Emeric was my brother. And the letters in that packet are in his hand, and I know it's his hand better than anyone alive, because I learned to write by copying it. Our schoolmaster gave us one slate between us. Everything I ever wrote, I wrote first as him."

The lamp hissed above them. Down the stairs and out the thick glass, the great lens turned its patient circles over the darkening water, sweeping its beam across a sea that held, at this moment, no ships at all—only emptiness, and the reef laid bare by the low tide like the bones of something that had once been alive.

Quentin stood up. She walked to the window, and she stood there with her back to him for a long time, and Jude sat at the table with his cold tea and his burning fish and waited for the blow to fall.

"How old are you?" she said at last.

"Twenty-six."

"So when he died—when we buried the empty box—you were twenty-three."

"Yes."

"Did you come to the funeral?"

"There wasn't anything to bury," he said, and heard the wrongness of his own sentence half a second too late. "I mean—I couldn't travel. I was at sea."

Her shoulders changed. Just slightly. A rigging taking a strain.

"Where were you at sea, Jude Okonkwo?"

And there it was. The second question, arriving exactly where he'd known it would, and the answer he could not give standing behind it with its hand out. *Why does a man carry forged letters through a storm? Why does a brother confirm a death he doesn't believe? Who sent you, and what do they want with the woman in this tower?*

"I can't tell you that," he said. "Not yet."

"Not yet." She turned around. Her face hadn't gone white now; it had gone the other way, into color, two bright flags of it high on her cheeks, and her eyes were terrible and dry. "Do you know what I did when the news came? Do you have any idea? Because I'll tell you, since we're confessing. I kept the log. That's what I did. The day they told me my husband was drowned, I wrote *wind northwest, moderate, visibility good.* Moderate. Good visibility. The worst day of my life and I logged it like a Tuesday, because if I wrote anything true in that book I would have had to live in it." She came toward him and he rose, because some animal part of him understood she might throw something. "And then you wash up on my rocks clutching his handwriting like it's scripture, and I spend six days hoping—hoping, like a fool, like a girl—that somehow, impossibly, against all arithmetic, he's alive. And now his brother sits at my table and tells me he's real, he has a name, and then stops. Stops *there*. Like a man stepping off a cliff and leaving his boots hanging in the air."

"If I tell you the rest—"

"Then tell me the rest!"

"—you will be in danger." His voice cracked on it, and he hated that, and pressed on. "Not hurt feelings, Quentin. Danger. Men in boats. The kind of danger that comes ashore asking questions." He spread his hands. "I swear to you on our mother's grave that what I'm keeping back isn't against you. But I need two days. Two days, and then you'll have everything, and if you want me off this rock I'll row myself off it in the tea chest if I have to."

"A man washed ashore half-drowned," she said, "and he's offering to leave in a tea chest."

"It's a sound vessel."

"It is a *tea chest*, Jude."

The absurdity of it hung there between them, and for one suspended moment they almost laughed together—and then she caught herself, jaw tightening, refusing the laugh the way she refused everything that threatened to make this bearable.

"Two days," she said. "You'll have your two days. But you'll sleep in the boathouse."

"Quentin—"

"Because I cannot," she said, and here at last her voice failed her, just once, just barely, "cannot sit across from a man wearing my husband's face in the lamplight and pretend I trust him. I'm sorry. I know you're not him. God help me, I know it every time you laugh, because you laugh in a completely different register, higher, and it startles me every single time, and I keep thinking *he never laughed like that,* and then I think *but who else would laugh at all in this house,* and I can't—" She stopped. Pressed both hands flat against her apron. "Boathouse. There's a blanket. Mind the hole in the roof; it drips on the port side."

"It's a boat shed," Jude said gently. "Everything's the port side."

"I know where the hole is," said Quentin Okonkwo, and turned back to the window, and did not look at him again.

* * *

The boathouse smelled of tar and old rope and, faintly, of the fish he had ruined, because she'd thrown it out for the gulls and the gulls had conducted their negotiations on the doorstep.

He lay awake on the workbench with the blanket pulled to his chin, listening to the tide come back in, covering inch by inch the bones of the reef it had exposed that afternoon. That was the thing about tides—they kept no secrets permanently. They only kept them on a schedule. And somewhere out in the dark, on water he could not see, men were coming who kept their secrets in ledgers written in blood, and every hour he stayed on this rock he was spending Quentin's safety like a drunkard spends wages.

He should go. He'd told her he would, and he meant it in the way he meant most things at midnight.

But then, near dawn, he heard the tower door open above him—heard her boots on the gallery, heard the small sounds of a keeper checking her charge in the black hours—and he got up and stood at the boathouse door in the wind, and watched her silhouette move against the lamp room glass, steady as the light itself.

She had thrown him out of her house. She had not thrown him off her rock. He turned that over the way he'd turned his teacup, around and around, looking for what it balanced out to, and decided it meant that some part of her—some part angrier at hope than at him—wanted him where she could hear the sea break against him in the night.

He stood watch until his feet went numb. Then he sat on an upturned dory and stood watch some more, and near first light he must have slept, because he woke with a start to the sound of boots on shingle and a voice calling up from the landing stage—a stranger's voice, carrying flat and clear across the morning water:

"Ahoy the light! Salvage men! We're looking for papers come ashore!"

And Jude, frozen in the boathouse doorway with the blanket still around his shoulders, watched Quentin step out onto the gallery above, calm as Sunday, to meet them—and understood that his two days had just run out early.
