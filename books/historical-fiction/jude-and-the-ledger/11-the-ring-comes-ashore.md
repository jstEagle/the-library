## 11. The Ring Comes Ashore

The morning after I put Jude out of the tower, the tide brought me two men instead of an apology.

I saw their boat first—a flat-bottomed thing with a patched sail, the kind of vessel that had no business crossing open water in a season like this and clearly knew it, hugging the coast like a coward hugs a wall. I was on the gallery with my glass, ostensibly checking the channel markers, actually checking on the boathouse, where a light had burned all night and a shape had paced all night and a door had opened exactly twice: once at moonset, once when the gulls drove him inside. I told myself I was checking for storm damage. I was a good liar even to myself by then; it seemed only fair to extend the courtesy to others.

The boat made for the landing below the point with the unhurried confidence of people who have already decided they are welcome. Two figures stepped out into the shallows and hauled it up themselves—no crew waiting, no second boat. Visitors who intended to leave under their own power, then, whenever they pleased. That detail lodged in me like a fishbone before I could even name why.

They climbed the path. I went down to meet them, because it is better to meet trouble on your own stairs than to let it knock.

* * *

They were brothers too, or might as well have been—the same way of standing, the same habit of finishing each other's silences. The tall one did the talking. He had a face like a shovel: broad, blunt, built for moving earth rather than being looked at. The short one hung back and did what I would later understand was the real work of the pair: he watched. He watched the tower, the boathouse, the fresh-mended shutters, and last of all me, with the patient attention of a man counting the spoons.

"Morning to you, Keeper." The tall one touched his cap without quite removing it, a gesture that managed to insult both the cap and me. "Faircross and Marsh, marine salvage. We're working the wreckage from the great blow." He said *the great blow* the way a clergyman says *the late lamented*, with relish dressed up as sorrow. "There's flotsam coming ashore along this whole reach. We've been authorized by the underwriters."

"Authorized," I said. It is a wonderful word. It means nothing and sounds like paperwork.

He produced a paper. I did not read it closely; forgery has a smell to it, like fresh ink over old intentions, and anyway I was watching his eyes while he watched mine, which is reading enough. "Mostly we're after cargo," he said. "Bales, casks, whatever the sea's given back. But—" and here his voice dropped half a register, into something almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing he'd done yet— "there's talk of papers. Letters. An oilskin packet, maybe. Salvage of that kind carries a reward, if it's handed over intact. More of a reward than bales."

"Letters," I repeated.

"Personal correspondence. Belonging to interested parties." He smiled. Shovel-face notwithstanding, he had practiced that smile; I could see the wear marks on it. "You'd be surprised what washes up. Bibles. Bills of lading. Love letters. A keeper out here must see a fair share of the sea's post."

Now, I want to be honest, because honesty arrived so late in my life that I feel obliged to honor it wherever I catch it: my first instinct was to lie badly. My heart was slamming against my ribs like a moth against a pane, and somewhere behind those ribs a voice was shouting *oilskin packet, oilskin packet* at a volume that would have cracked crockery. I felt the truth rise in my throat like nausea.

And then—this is the strange part, the part I have thought about since until it has worn smooth—some colder, quieter woman underneath me took hold of my tongue the way one takes hold of a rope in a squall, and said:

"The sea's post comes addressed to the rocks, mostly. Kelp. Dead gulls. Last month a piano, if you can credit it, though only the harp frame survived." I shook my head, rueful, a little dull. "No letters. And nothing worth a reward, or the gulls would have told you before you landed."

The watcher's eyes came off the boathouse and settled on me, and I understood that the interview had just begun and had also just ended, depending on how the next minute went.

"A piano," he said softly.

"Or part of one. I'm no judge of pianos."

"You live alone out here, Mrs.—?"

"Keeper," I said. "Keeper Okonkwo. The lamp doesn't care much about the rest."

"And there's nobody else? Nobody washed up after the storms?" His gaze drifted, casual as weather, toward the boathouse again. "We heard a man might've come ashore along this stretch. Big fellow. There'd be a finder's fee in it, if you'd seen him."

Here was the hinge, and I felt it turn. Because I could have said many things. I could have said *yes*, and sold Jude to them for the price of my solitude restored. I could have said *no* too quickly, with the bright particular denial of a woman protecting something, which is its own confession. Or I could do what I did, which was to laugh—short, genuine, tired, because some of it wasn't acting—and say:

"If a big fellow washed up here, madam, he'd have had to get past my nets, my gulls, and my opinion of trespassers. No. Nothing washed up but the usual. You're welcome to walk the tideline yourselves if you don't mind the smell."

I turned and gestured grandly at the entire gray ocean, an heiress giving away her estate.

* * *

They walked the tideline. Of course they did. They spent an hour at it, and I spent the hour in the doorway of the tower with mending in my lap I did not do, watching them poke at wrack and weed while the wind tried to take my needles. The tall one talked the whole time—to me across the distance, to his brother, to himself, a man who could not bear silence the way some men cannot bear water. The quiet one never talked at all, and every time I lost track of him I found him again looking at the boathouse.

At noon they gave it up with the good humor of men who can afford patience. The tall one tipped his cap a full inch this time, progress of a sort.

"We'll be along the coast some weeks yet," he said. "Working the reach. If anything should come ashore—papers, a man, a piano—we'll make it worth your while, Keeper. Interested parties pay well for their property." He paused on the path, and for a moment the practiced warmth drained out of him like tide out of a pool, and what remained was simply flat and cold and businesslike. "Sea gives things back eventually. Everything. It's the one debt that always gets collected."

"Then it will find you," I said pleasantly. "Everyone always does."

They rowed away south. I stood on the gallery with my glass and watched until the patched sail went below the horizon, and then I watched some more, because the horizon lies and I have never trusted it.

* * *

Jude came up from the boathouse an hour later, moving stiff as a man who had slept in a hull all night, which he had. He had heard the boat, he said. Had watched the whole visit through a gap in the boards with a fish-gaff in his hand and a prayer in what he insisted on calling his vocabulary.

"You lied well," he said. He meant it as a compliment. Coming from him, I suppose it was.

"I've had practice." I set bread and cold fish in front of him and did not sit. "They'll come back. Men like that don't leave, they circle. And they weren't salvagers."

"No," he said quietly. "They weren't."

"They asked about papers, Jude. Not cargo. Papers, specifically. An oilskin packet, specifically. They knew what they were looking for before your boat ever broke on my rocks." I kept my voice level, keeper-level, the voice I use in gales. "Which means somebody told them. Somebody who knew what was in that packet and where it was going to wash up. Somebody who wanted them to come asking."

He looked at his bread. He had not touched it. In three days I had learned that Jude not eating was the truest barometer in the house; the glass on the wall was a toy by comparison.

"They're not here for the letters," I said, and the understanding came down on me as I spoke it, each word a plank laid over dark water. "The letters are the excuse. They're here because someone paid them to ask, and they were told exactly what story to tell. And if Emeric wrote those letters—if some of them are real, if any of them are real—then he didn't just die, and he didn't just disappear." I heard my own breath go out of me. "He ran. He ran from *them*, Jude, and he used his own death to do it, and they have spent three years looking for whatever proof he carried that they couldn't afford for him to keep."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, the wind worried the shutters I had mended with a stranger's help, and the light above us turned and turned at its slow faithful work.

"He faked it," I said. "His death. He stood on some deck or some dock and let the world bury him, knowing what it would do to me, because the alternative was letting those men find him. And I—" My voice did something I did not authorize. I let it finish anyway. "I have spent three years keeping his light. Trimming wicks for a man who chose to be dead. And the worst of it, Jude, the part I cannot get around, is that sitting here now, knowing what I know—I think I would have done the same. If I'd seen what he saw. If running was the only way to be alive at all. I think I'd have chosen the lie too, and hated myself forever, and done it."

"That's the first kind thing anyone's said about my brother in a long time," Jude said, barely above the wind.

"It wasn't kind. It was arithmetic." I took the bread plate away untasted and set down, in its place, the oilskin packet, squared to the table's edge like a document of war. "But here is what kind looks like, since we're strangers to it. Those men will come back, and next time they won't tip their caps. You are going to stop lying to me by inches, starting now, and give me the whole ledger of it—who they are, what Emeric carried, why they sent *you* wearing his handwriting like a stolen coat. All of it. Because fear has been living in this house rent-free for three days, and I am done housing it."

"And if I still won't?"

"Then you can watch from the boathouse while I burn these letters in the lamp room, one page per rotation of the lens, and we'll see how interested parties enjoy the smoke."

It was an empty threat and he knew it and I knew it; the letters were the only currency either of us had. But Jude looked at the packet, and at me, and something in his face finally stopped circling and came to rest.

"There's a third letter," he said. "One you haven't found. And Quentin—it isn't in his hand."

* * *

Outside, far down the gray reach, a sail that had gone below the horizon came back above it, and stood there, motionless, watching the light.
