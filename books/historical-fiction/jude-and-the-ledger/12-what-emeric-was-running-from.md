## 12. What Emeric Was Running From

Jude had rehearsed the confession eleven times in the night, lying in the boathouse on a bed of folded sailcloth, listening to the wind worry the door latch like a dog with a bone. Eleven versions. In three of them Quentin forgave him before he finished the second sentence. In seven she threw something. In one—his favorite, the coward's version—he simply walked into the sea and let the current finish what the storm had started.

None of the versions survived contact with morning.

She came down to the boathouse at first light carrying two mugs of tea and no expression at all, which was worse than anger. Anger he could work with. She set one mug on the upturned dory beside him without a word, sat on the keel roller across from him, and waited.

"You didn't sleep," she observed.

"Neither did you."

"I kept a light burning all night. It's practically my profession." She blew on her tea. "You stood watch outside my door like a guilty spaniel. I want to know why."

He looked at his tea. The steam rose off it in a thin, honest line—the only honest thing in the boathouse.

"His name isn't really worth hiding anymore, is it," he said. "You already know. You've known since the lantern and the knife."

"Say it anyway."

"Emeric Okonkwo was my brother." His voice cracked on *brother*, which he hadn't planned, and he had to stop and start again. "Younger. By four years. He used to say I was born first so he'd have someone to blame things on."

Quentin didn't move. The wind found a gap somewhere in the eaves and made a low whistle of it, like an audience settling in.

"He's alive," she said. Not a question. A thing laid on the table between them, heavy as an anchor.

"Yes."

* * *

Jude told it in order, because that was the only way he could hold it together: start at the beginning, keep moving, don't look up.

"We grew up in Saltmarsh Reach, you know that much, probably. He never talked about home much—" he caught himself, corrected, "*I* never talked about home much. Emeric wrote. Emeric always wrote. Our mother said he came out of the womb holding a pen, complaining about the accommodations."

Something flickered across Quentin's face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way light ghosts across water. He pressed on before it could become something he couldn't survive.

"There's a man in Saltmarsh named Corvan Ashe. Owns half the fishing fleet on paper, owns most of the harbor in fact if not in law. Six years ago Emeric got into debt to him—a boat, gear, the usual. And when a man owes Corvan Ashe more than he can pay, Corvan doesn't send collectors. He sends an offer. You carry things for him. Small things at first. Then less small things."

"Contraband," Quentin said.

"Liquor, mostly. Untaxed tobacco. Once—only once that I know of—something in sealed crates he wasn't allowed to ask about. That was the night everything changed. Because the man who sailed with him that night was a customs informant named Tam Brellery, and three weeks later Tam Brellery was pulled out of the channel under the breakwater with his pockets full of gravel."

The tea had gone cold in Jude's hands. He set it down because he couldn't feel it anyway.

"Emeric didn't kill him. I need you to believe that, even though you have no reason left to believe anything I say. But he knew who did, and he knew that knowing it put a length of rope around his own neck. So he went to Corvan and told him he was out. And Corvan smiled—that smile of his, like a crack in a wall—and said, *of course you are.*"

"And then?"

"And then for two years nothing happened, which was worse than if something had. Emeric met you. He married you. He wrote me letters full of you—the keeper's daughter with the freckles and the ledgers, who logged weather like scripture and laughed exactly once per good joke, so you knew when you'd earned one." Jude looked at his hands. "I'm quoting. Badly. He was better at it than I am."

"Go on."

"The ring started using his boat again. Quietly. A run here, a run there. And Emeric understood what Corvan was doing—he was making Emeric complicit again, bit by bit, so that when the reckoning came, Emeric would be as guilty as the rest of them and couldn't testify against anyone. You can't inform on a man when your own ledger has the same ink in it."

"So he faked his death instead."

"So he faked his death instead." Jude heard how it sounded, said plainly, in this room, to this woman, and flinched. "There was a wreck. The *Kestrel*. They found debris, a burned hull section, his coat. His coat was the detail that sold it—Corvan wanted Emeric dead, and a dead man stops being interesting. A drowned smuggler is a closed file. Emeric gave the sea his name and his coat and vanished south, and for three years he's been living under another man's papers, working a mail boat out of Halloway, far enough away that the ring stopped looking."

Quentin sat very still. Outside, the gulls were arguing about something over the rocks, their voices thin and sharp as needles.

"For three years," she said, and her voice was level in the way ice is level, "he let me log him as lost. I wrote *widow* on forms, Jude. I signed papers. I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Why the letters? If he was safe, if the trick worked, why wash ashore here carrying his handwriting dated after his death? Why come to *me*?"

And there it was. The part he'd saved for last in every rehearsal, and now it arrived whether he was ready or not.

* * *

"Because the trick stopped working," Jude said. "Eight months ago, one of the old crew turned up in Halloway. Drunk, talkative, thought he recognized the mail boat clerk. Whether he did or not, word traveled back to Corvan. And Corvan Ashe is not a man who leaves a file open. He sent people to find where Emeric went."

"And they couldn't find him."

"They couldn't find him. So Corvan did something cleverer. He reasoned that a man who fakes his death has one weakness: everyone who believed it. Everyone who grieved him. You can't threaten a ghost, but you can threaten the people who mourn him—and better yet, you can make the ghost come to *you*." Jude's mouth tasted of salt. "Emeric wrote me last spring. Warned me. Told me if anyone ever brought news of him to Gullwrack Point, I should be suspicious, and then he told me who I was, in case I'd forgotten—because we hadn't spoken in years by then, and he thought I might be angry enough to be bought."

"Were you?"

"No." It came out faster and fiercer than he intended. "No. But I was frightened, and I was proud, and those make a poor crew. Because a month later, men came to *me*. Two of them, very polite, asking about my brother. Asking whether I loved him. Whether I'd like to help them find him—for family reasons, they said. They knew about you, Quentin. They knew the light, the harbor, everything. And one of them put an oilskin packet on my table and said, *when you're ready to help us find him, deliver these to his widow.*"

"The letters," she whispered.

"The letters. Forged. Every one of them—I studied his hand since I was six years old copying his schoolwork, and I forged them well enough that you believed, and God forgive me, that was the whole design. They wanted you to come looking. To leave the light, take ship, go chasing a husband alive somewhere south—and they'd be waiting wherever you landed. Because a grieving widow is leverage, but a grieving widow who *chose* to abandon her post for him?" He shook his head. "That's leverage Corvan could use on both of you at once. Emeric surrenders or you drown. Either way, the file closes."

Silence. The long kind. Quentin rose, walked to the boathouse door, and stood looking out at the gray water as though it might offer a second opinion.

"So the letters were bait," she said finally. "And I was the fish."

"The letters were bait," Jude agreed miserably. "But listen to me—listen, because this is the part I need you to hear. Somewhere between the harbor at Saltmarsh and your doorstep, the plan stopped being theirs and started being mine. Because I read them. I read what I'd written in his voice, and I realized I'd written him *home*. Every letter, every detail, all of it pointed here—to you, to this light, to the life he walked away from. And I thought: if my brother is alive, if he abandoned this woman to save his skin, then he should have to look at what that cost. And if he's dead, truly dead, then she deserves to know someone remembers him well enough to forge him." He laughed, short and bitter. "It felt noble at the time. Noble plans and I have a difficult history."

"And the third letter." Quentin turned from the doorway. "*Trust no one who brings my hand to you.* That's real. That's his."

"That's real. Emeric slipped it to me through a harbor agent in Halloway with instructions to include it however the packet reached you—whether I delivered it honestly or the ring forced me to. He guessed what they'd try. He built a warning into the trap itself." Jude spread his hands. "He couldn't tell me outright that I was being used, because he didn't know if I *wasn't*. So he wrote to you instead, and trusted the woman he married to be sharper than the brother who raised him."

She absorbed that. He watched her do it the way he'd once watched her check a ledger—line by line, no skipping, no rounding off.

"So he's alive," she said again, slower now, testing the weight of it. "He's alive, and he's hiding, and he let me grieve him because grieving me was the only way to keep me safe. And you came here as a lure, and stayed as—" she gestured vaguely at him, at the boathouse, at the mended shutters and the taught card game and the whole improbable week of it, "—as whatever you are."

"A liar," Jude offered. "With excellent penmanship."

And then—God help him—she laughed. One short, cracked, furious laugh, gone as fast as it came, but it crossed the room between them like a rope thrown over dark water, and he grabbed it.

"I should hate you," she said.

"You should. Most people manage it within the hour."

"But the ring knows where I am." Her voice shifted, dropped into something practical and cold, the keeper taking inventory. "They sent you. When you didn't report back—when the storms closed the harbor—they'll send someone else."

As if the sea had been waiting for its cue, a shape moved at the edge of his vision, out past the point. Jude stood up so fast the tea went over.

Two figures on the cliff path. Walking toward the tower. Unhurried, in oilskins that were too clean for fishermen, and between them they carried the particular silence of men who have been sent rather than sent themselves.

"Quentin," he said quietly. "Don't answer the door as yourself."
