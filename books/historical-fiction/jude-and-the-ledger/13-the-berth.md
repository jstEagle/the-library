## 13. The Berth

The flags went up at half past nine in the morning, and by ten the whole of Gullwrack Point knew it, because there was nothing on Gullwrack Point to know things *with* except Quentin, and Quentin had been standing at the gallery rail with her spyglass braced against her eye socket since the first smudge of smoke appeared on the horizon.

"Steamer," she reported to the tower behind her, where Jude was mending a shutter hinge with a flathead screwdriver and a great deal of profanity kept politely under his breath. "Two funnels. Paddle guards. That'll be the *Meridian*."

"The relief ship." He said it without looking up. They had both been saying it for three days the way one says a diagnosis.

"One berth," Quentin said. "Harbormaster's flag code confirms it. Red over white over red." She lowered the spyglass and found that her hands wanted to shake, so she folded them around the brass tube instead, which gave them something respectable to do. "He's signaled he'll send a boat when she anchors off the point. One seat, heated, all the way to Penzance."

"Heated," Jude repeated, with the reverence of a man who had spent two weeks watching Quentin feed a stove that ate coal like a starving cousin.

"Heated," she confirmed. "And a hot meal. And other people."

"Other people," Jude said again, softer, and Quentin caught the note in his voice—the one that said he had been a sociable man once, before the sea spat him onto her rocks and into her ledgers—and she looked away first, out at the approaching smoke, because she was getting alarmingly good at knowing what his face meant and that knowledge had consequences.

The consequence was this: she had begun, sometime in the last week without noticing it, to think of the tower as containing two people. *I should ask Jude*, she'd catch herself thinking about the good teacups. *Jude will want to see this*, about the sunrise doing something outrageous to the water. The tower had been built for one keeper and had sulked accordingly for three years, its rooms cold, its routines brittle, and now it had warmed and loosened as if someone had finally oiled the hinges of the whole establishment, and that someone mended nets badly and played cards worse and laughed at her jokes—her actual jokes, which Emeric had always claimed were an acquired taste he'd never managed to acquire.

She did not let herself think any of this out loud. Thinking it quietly was quite enough trouble.

* * *

The harbormaster's boat could not make the landing—nothing could make the landing, that was rather the point of Gullwrack—but the gig stood off in the lee of the point and a boy rowed the last stretch through the kelp beds to the little stone jetty, shouting up at the boathouse until Jude scrambled down to take the line. Quentin followed more slowly, because her knees held opinions about ladders now, and arrived in time to hear the boy deliver his news at full volume, as though the harbor's business could only be transacted at a shout.

"*Meridian*'s making one run only! Harbor freezes Thursday week, Mr. Tregowan says, and after that nobody in or out till spring thaw! One berth, mind—one! Master says you're to decide between yourselves who goes, and he's sorry, and there's marmalade in the parcel!"

"There's *what* in the parcel?"

"Marmalade. Mrs. Tregowan sent it. Said if you're going to be shipwrecked on somebody's rocks, better it be somebody who can cook."

Quentin accepted the parcel, the news, and the boy's salute, and watched him row away with the peculiar feeling of having been handed a verdict wrapped in breakfast preserves.

They carried the marmalade up to the kitchen and set it on the table between them, and for a while neither of them touched it, which was how Quentin knew they were both thinking about the same thing.

"One berth," Jude said at last.

"One berth."

"And two of us."

"Fancy arithmetic for a man who loses at cards to a woman taught by her dead husband."

That came out wrong. She heard it come out wrong even as she said it—Emeric's name still fit strangely in her mouth, three years worn smooth and then abruptly jagged again since the letters—and Jude, to his credit, did not flinch. He had gotten better, these past days, at not flinching. He simply pushed the jar toward her across the table, an offering, and said, "You should have the seat."

"Don't be absurd."

"I mean it. You've got people. A sister in Penzance, you mentioned. A life. I've got—" he gestured at himself, at the borrowed jersey, the mended nets, the whole improbable domesticity of him—"whatever this is."

"This is my kitchen," Quentin said, "and you've been eating out of it for a fortnight, which makes you either a guest or a freeloader, and I haven't decided which, but neither category gets to decide who leaves this rock. That decision is mine. I'm the keeper."

"You're the *hostage*," he said, too honestly, and then winced, because they had agreed—not in words, never in words—to leave the whole question of why he had washed ashore in the unfenced pasture where it grazed.

But he wasn't wrong, and they both knew it. The letters sat upstairs in the oilskin packet like a lit fuse. Somewhere beyond the horizon there were men who had sent those letters, men who believed—or hoped—that Emeric Okonkwo's widow could be drawn off her rock and into their reach. If she left, she would be walking into exactly the trap the letters implied. If she stayed—

If she stayed, and Jude left alone, then whoever came next would find her here, alone, undefended, with no one to lie for her.

So the arithmetic was not really about comfort, or sisters, or heated cabins. It was about who could protect whom, and from where. Quentin had spent three years learning that every choice at Gullwrack was a choice about light: what you showed, what you hid, what you burned yourself to keep burning.

* * *

She took the question up the tower that afternoon, the way she took everything up the tower, and tried to think it through with her usual methodical care, which was to say she trimmed wicks and argued with herself.

The case for giving Jude the berth was short and clean. He was younger. He had a brother—alive, apparently, impossibly, *Emeric*—who might need him near, or might need him far; Jude would know which. He had testimony, if testimony was what it sounded like he had, and a courtroom in some port town would be safer for delivering it than a lighthouse surrounded by freezing sea. And he was family.

There. She made herself think the word. *Family*. Not the one she'd married into and buried; a new one, assembled from wreckage, argument, and marmalade, and it would be a lie to call it anything else, and she was done, she told herself firmly, done with lying in her own head if nowhere else.

The case for taking the berth herself was shorter and dirtier: the light.

A light is not a building. Anyone can keep a building. A light is a practice, a marriage of hands and hours, and Quentin knew this lamp the way she knew her own signature—which wick drank oil fast, which gear tooth wore thin, how the lens liked to be cleaned, clockwise, never against. In spring the Board would send a relief keeper, but winter did not negotiate with boards. If she left, the light would go dark or go strange, and ships would pass Gullwrack Point in the dark weeks and trust their arithmetic to a dead flame, and Quentin Okonkwo had signed a ledger every day of her tenure swearing that would not happen while she breathed.

Also—and here was the dirty part, the part she almost didn't look at—if she left, she would be leaving *him* room. Room for Emeric to come back to a tower with no wife in it. Room for whatever reunion or reckoning the world seemed to be arranging. And she found she did not want to give him that room, not because she hated him, but because staying felt like the only letter she could write back. *You faked your death*, staying said. *Fine. Then live with what your death did to me. Come find me here, where you left me.*

It was not a noble reason. Nobility, in her experience, was mostly vanity wearing a uniform. But it was hers.

* * *

Supper was fish, bread, and the last of the cabbage, and afterward Jude dealt the cards, because dealing cards had become what they did instead of talking, and talking had become what they did instead of everything else, and the whole arrangement worked beautifully as long as nobody thought about it.

Naturally, Quentin thought about it.

"Emeric taught me this game," she said, discarding. "On our second courtship visit. I lost eleven hands running and he was insufferable about it, and then he told me the trick, which was that he'd been cheating, gently, the whole time, moving cards when I looked at the window. He said he wanted to lose to me eventually and couldn't manage it honestly."

Jude laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and then sobered. "That sounds like him."

"It does, doesn't it." She studied her hand. "You knew him as a boy, though. Which version of him did you know?"

"The one who convinced me the church bell rang differently when the sexton was angry, and made me sit in the tower for a whole Sunday listening for it." Jude laid down a card. "He was always building small machinery out of people. Kind machinery. Mostly."

"*Mostly*," Quentin repeated, and let it hang there, because *mostly* was the entire subject of the letters upstairs, and they both heard it, and neither reached for it, and the fire ticked on.

Later, when the dishes were done and the lamp in the kitchen turned low, Jude stood at the window looking out at the black water where the *Meridian* rode at anchor, a scatter of lights like a town gone to sea.

"I've been thinking," he said.

"Dangerous habit. It spreads."

"About the berth."

"I know." She dried the last cup and set it down. "So have I."

"You shouldn't give it to me."

"You don't know what I've decided."

"No," he agreed, turning, and his face in the low light was open in a way she hadn't seen before, unguarded, the liar's mask set aside like a coat hung on a peg. "But I know you, Quentin. Two weeks of losing at cards—I know you. You'll do the sums about the light, and the sums are right, and you'll stay, and you'll dress it up as duty, and underneath it will just be you deciding your life is worth less than everyone else's convenience. I've watched you do it with coal rations and candle ends. Don't do it with yourself."

The kitchen was very quiet. Rain tested itself against the glass, tentatively, a musician tuning up.

"That's a foul blow," she said at last, "and I'm not obliged to sit here and be seen through by a card cheat."

"I never cheated at cards."

"No. Only at being a stranger."

He took it without flinching, which was somehow worse. "Give me the berth," he said quietly. "Please. Let one person on this rock get out alive."

And there it was—the thing under the thing, the fear speaking plainly at last. He thought the ring would come for them. He thought the letters had painted a target on the tower, and he was right, probably, and he wanted her safe in Penzance behind other people's walls.

She wanted him safe too. That was the devil of it. Love, it turned out, when it finally arrived uninvited in a middle-aged widow's kitchen, looked remarkably like an argument about boat seats.

"I'll consider it," she said, which was a lie, because she had already considered it, thoroughly, all afternoon, and had arrived somewhere quiet and certain that she wasn't ready to show him yet. "Go to bed, Jude."

He went. At the door he paused. "For what it's worth," he said, "whoever stays—I hope it's the version of events where we're both wrong and everyone's fine."

"Everyone's rarely fine," Quentin said. "But thank you for the sentiment."

* * *

She climbed the tower one last time before bed, because the habit was the spine of her, and stood in the lantern room with the great lens turning its slow patient circles, throwing its spoke of light out over a sea that had settled into an unnatural, listening calm.

The barometer on the wall had been falling all day. She checked it now, as she had checked it forty times already, and frowned at it the way one frowns at a guest who has begun breaking things. It had dropped further. It was dropping still, steadily, the needle sliding down the scale like something pouring.

She had known storms her whole life. She had learned to read their approach in her joints and in the gulls and in the particular green of the water, and everything in her craft was telling her now, quietly and insistently, that this calm was not calm at all. It was the sea drawing breath.

Out past the anchorage, the *Meridian* rode her lights, waiting. One berth. Thursday week the harbor froze.

Quentin put her hand flat against the cold glass of the lantern room and looked south along the dark water, where the storm would come from, and thought: *not yet. Give me until I've finished deciding.*

Far off, at the very edge of seeing, the clouds had begun to pile themselves up against the sky like furniture stacked for moving—and the light swept round, and round, and found nothing there yet, and swept on.
