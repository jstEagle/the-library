## 14. Storm Glass Falling

The storm glass had been Quentin's mother-in-law's, a slim glass tube of camphor and water that Emeric had inherited along with his father's sea chest and his father's habit of tapping instruments meaningfully. For three years it had hung by the lantern room door doing nothing much, its crystals settling in lazy snowfalls that meant weather and more weather and occasionally, generously, no weather at all.

This morning the crystals had grown into a forest. Long, feathered spines stood on end throughout the fluid, dense as a winter hedgerow, and when Quentin lifted the glass to the gray window light they seemed to shiver.

"Well," she said to it. "That's rude."

She carried it down the spiral stair to the kitchen, where Jude was eating porridge with the concentration of a man who had learned not to trust meals to last. She set the glass on the table between them. He looked at it, and his spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Is it supposed to look like that?"

"No."

"Ah." He put the spoon down. "How long do we have?"

Quentin had spent the walk down the stairs doing sums—the barometer reading at dawn, the color of the eastern sky, which had been the yellow-green of a bruise going bad, the gulls who had all, to a bird, abandoned the point two days early. "If we're lucky," she said, "until nightfall. If we're not, it'll come up over the horizon like something with teeth before the noon bell."

Jude pushed back his chair and stood, and Quentin watched—still with a small private astonishment, after everything—him roll his sleeves as though he lived here. As though this were simply what they did now, the two of them: read the glass, and answer it.

"What do you need?" he said.

*Everything,* she thought. *Shutters, halyards, oil, the spare pane for the lantern room that I've been meaning to fetch from the boathouse since autumn, the driftwood gate, the boat turned and lashed, the cistern covered—*

"Buckets," she said aloud. "And your shoulders."

He grinned at her, and she pretended not to see it, and went to find the buckets.

* * *

By ten o'clock the barometer had fallen so far that Quentin marked the dial twice to be sure her own eyes weren't lying. The needle sat below anything inked on the instrument's face, out in unprinted territory, quivering like a small trapped animal. She had kept this light through eleven years of storms. She had a page in the ledger—a whole page, ruled and dated—for the worst of them, the Halloween gale that had taken the *Merry Alice* and nine men with her. That page recorded a barometer of 28.6 inches, and she had always believed, in the quiet superstitious way of keepers, that the number was a kind of floor, a promise the sky had made her.

The needle was now below it.

She wrote the new figure in the margin beside the old one, because the ledger deserved honesty even when the sky didn't, and then she went out into a wind that had begun to sing.

The sound was the first wrong thing. Gales roared; she was used to roaring, to the blunt animal voice of it. This wind sang—a high, rising note in the rigging of the world, in the gap under the tower door, in the seams of her own teeth—and underneath the note, far out past the point, the sea had gone a color she had only seen once before, a green so dark it was nearly black, moving in long swells like the backs of enormous animals turning over in their sleep.

Jude was on the roof of the storehouse, hauling the canvas cover over the coal hatch, and when she shouted his name he came down the ladder fast, hand over hand, the way Emeric never had—Emeric had descended all ladders with the ceremony of a man arriving at a party.

"It's coming early," she said.

He looked at the horizon, then at her face, and whatever he saw there made him nod without argument. "Tell me the order," he said. "You know the order. I'll just be the hands."

So she gave him the order, and they worked.

They worked the way she had once worked alone, except doubled, except halved in time. He carried the storm shutters up the outside stair while she greased their hinges; they hung them together, four hands to each iron frame, his strength where hers gave out and her knowledge where his failed. When the third shutter jammed in its track he didn't curse or force it—he held the whole weight of it off his forearms, arms shaking, and said, very calmly, "Now what," and she said, "Lift and left," and he lifted and left and it slid home with a boom like a coffin lid, and neither of them laughed until afterward, both of them, helplessly, at nothing.

That was the thing about shared labor, Quentin thought, driving the last peg into the boat's turned hull while Jude lashed it. It burned things off. All autumn they had circled each other with their secrets between them like a low wall—she with her letters and her knife-under-the-floorboards, he with his false name worn thin as old paint. But a storm did not care about anyone's walls, and there was no version of battening a light tower that could be done by a liar working alone. You needed a second pair of hands you could trust to hold the shutter while you drove the pin. There was no polite distance in it. There was only the work, and the person doing the other half of it.

"You're good at this," she said, surprising herself.

"I grew up on boats." He tied off the lashings, tested them, tied them again. "Emeric was the clever one. I was the strong one. Between us we almost made a whole competent sailor."

The name sat differently in her chest than it had a month ago. Not easier. Truer. "Was he really the clever one?"

Jude considered the question with the seriousness he brought to everything lately, his hair whipping across his face. "He was the one who could make you believe a thing was possible," he said. "That's rarer than clever."

Then the noon bell in the village should have rung, and instead the sky simply got lower, and they went inside.

* * *

The tower at two in the afternoon, with the storm still an hour out and the light already failing, was a strange amber place. The storm shutters turned day to dusk. Quentin lit the lamp early—not because it was needed yet but because she wanted the machinery warm and proven before the real blow arrived, and because, if she was honest, she wanted the sound of it, the great soft breathing of the flame, filling the tower with something other than the rising song of the wind.

Jude stood in the lantern room with his head tipped back, watching her work. He had been doing that more often—watching her at the mechanism with an expression she couldn't file anywhere, half guilt and half wonder.

"Say it," she said, trimming the wick, not turning around. "Whatever it is you keep not saying while I do this."

A pause. The wind took the opportunity to lean on the glass.

"My brother used to write about you doing this," Jude said. "In his letters. Before." He meant the true letters, the old ones, the ones from a living man to a living wife; they had spoken of these things in the long nights of the past week, carefully, the way you pick your way across wet rocks. "'She climbs the stairs at dusk,' he wrote once, 'and comes down someone else. Better. Like the light winds her too.'" His voice roughened slightly. "I didn't understand it until I watched you."

Quentin trimmed the second wick with great attention. Her freckles—she could feel them, absurdly, warming in the lamplight, the ones a dead man had once joked were the reason he'd proposed, navigation by stars being second nature to a man who'd courted a constellation.

"The glass is falling," she said, which was not gratitude, but which he correctly heard as such.

Down the stairs, then, to the business of endurance: filling every vessel in the tower with fresh water, laying in the lamp oil, setting the emergency stores of the storeroom in order against the possibility—which she would not say aloud, and saw in his eyes that he also would not—that the tower might be cut off, or worse, for days. They ate bread and cold ham standing up, like soldiers. Outside, the sea had started to be heard even through stone, a continuous deep percussion like a giant knocking politely and then less politely at the foundations.

It was while they were carrying the last of the oil up the stairs, Jude below her on the spiral steps with the heavy can, that the first gust arrived.

Not the singing wind. Something ahead of it, a scout—a single impact that struck the tower broadside and set the whole structure humming like a struck bell, dust sifting from the joints of the stairs, the lamp flame above them flattening and recovering. Quentin froze with her hand on the rail. Below her, Jude braced himself against the central column, and for one heartbeat they were perfectly still together, two people inside a bell waiting to hear how long the ringing lasted.

"That," Jude said quietly, "wasn't the storm."

"No," Quentin agreed. "That was the storm clearing its throat."

They climbed faster after that.

* * *

By four o'clock the world outside the lantern glass had stopped being scenery and become event. The swells came in now like siege engines, black-green, crested white, and broke against the outer rocks with impacts she felt in her boot soles. Rain arrived not as rain but as the sea itself flung upward, horizontal, hissing. And through it all, in the lee of the gallery rail, the two of them worked the final preparations: the auxiliary anchor chained, the signal flags bagged, the spare lamp checked, the logbook moved to the high shelf in its oilcloth.

Her hands and his had stopped needing instructions. That was the strangest part. Somewhere in the last two days of labor her body had learned his—to know where he was in her peripheral vision, to turn with the shutter at exactly the moment he lifted, to say "hold" and feel him hold. It was not like working with Emeric, which had been like dancing with someone who knew the same steps. It was like working with someone who had learned the steps by watching her dance alone.

"There," Jude said at last, tying off the final lashing on the gallery, shouting to be heard. "That's everything. What do we do now?"

"Now," Quentin called back, "we go inside, we eat something hot, and then we don't sleep, because the lamp will need tending every hour and the glass will need wiping and one of us will need to stand watch for ships."

"And the other one?"

"Sleeps badly and pretends otherwise."

He smiled through the streaming rain. Then his smile changed—slowed—and he was looking past her shoulder, out to sea, and Quentin turned to follow his gaze, and forgot the wind.

Out past the point, where the deep water began, the storm had torn a momentary seam in itself—one of those ragged holes the worst gales open and close like breathing—and through it, for perhaps five seconds, she could see clear across the gray chaos to the distant line of the harbor road. And on that line, small and distinct and impossibly bright against the dark, a lantern was moving. Moving fast. Coming toward Gullwrack Point through a storm that had closed every road, every path, and every sane reason to travel.

Beside her, Jude had gone very still.

"Quentin," he said. "Who walks to a lighthouse in this?"

She watched the little light bob and vanish behind a swell of rain, and felt the storm glass's feathered crystals in her memory, standing on end.

"No one," she said. "No one sane."
