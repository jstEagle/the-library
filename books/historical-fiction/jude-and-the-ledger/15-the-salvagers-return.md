## 15. The Salvagers Return

The morning after the storm glass began its long fall, Quentin woke with a thought fully formed in her head, the way some thoughts arrive—uninvited, dripping, insistent as a gull at a window: *They'll come back today.*

She didn't tell Jude this at breakfast. She buttered the bread and poured the tea and watched him across the table, this man who had lied to her for weeks and then stood all night in a boathouse rather than leave her alone with his lie, and she thought about how much easier it would be to hate him properly if he weren't so good at fixing things. He'd mended the hinge on the storeroom door that morning before she was even out of bed. He'd re-caulked the south window. He'd taught her Emeric's card game badly enough that she suspected he was letting her win, which was its own small treachery.

"You're doing the face," Jude said.

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided something and you're deciding whether to tell me."

"I don't have a face like that."

"Quentin." He set down his cup. "I grew up with your husband. I know the whole inventory of Thornbury faces. You're wearing number nine."

"There's an inventory?"

"There's a catalogue," he said solemnly. "Emeric kept it in his head. Number four is 'pleased but pretending not to be.' Number eleven is 'about to do something reckless involving boats.'"

"And what's nine?"

"'About to protect someone from themselves.'" He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "Which is rich, coming from me watching you do it."

She looked at him a long moment. The wind had changed in the night—not stronger yet, but wrong, coming around to the northeast with a wet iron smell that meant the barometer was right to be falling. Somewhere beyond the point, the sea was already organizing itself into something with intentions.

"The men from yesterday," she said. "The salvagers. They'll be back before the weather closes. And they won't ask nicely twice."

* * *

They came an hour past noon, when the light had gone the color of old pewter and the first real gusts were walking the grass flat against the cliff. Quentin saw them from the gallery—one large, one larger, moving up the path with the unhurried certainty of men who had stopped pretending to be anything other than what they were. The day before they had worn the manner of salvagers the way a coat is worn buttoned wrong: technically present, entirely convincing to no one.

"Jude," she called down the stairwell. "Company."

He came up two steps at a time, and when he reached the gallery and saw them, she watched everything easy go out of his face. "That's Colm," he said quietly. "The big one. The other one's called Finch. Colm broke a man's arm once over a disagreement about cargo weights. I watched him do it. He apologized afterward. That was the frightening part."

"How armed are we?"

"We have a knife. In the kitchen. For bread."

"Wonderful. We're the best-defended lighthouse on the coast."

The strangers did not knock, because there is a kind of man for whom knocking is a courtesy paid only to peers. They filled the doorway of the tower's lower room, sea spray still on their shoulders, and Colm looked around the space with the mild professional interest of a man pricing it for salvage.

"Mistress Okonkwo," Finch said. He was the smaller one and somehow the worse of the two; small men in that trade have usually had to be. "We met yesterday. You were less hospitable then, if you recall, though we'd put that down to feminine nerves."

"You could put the weather down to feminine nerves too," Quentin said. "It's about as accurate."

Finch smiled the way frost forms. "Our employer's patient, mistress. But his patience has an expense account, and we've drawn against it twice now. The papers that washed ashore here. The letters. We'll take them, and we'll be gone ahead of the blow, and everyone keeps their blood where nature intended it."

"They're mine," Quentin said. "They came out of the sea onto my rocks. That's salvage law, if we're being legal, and if we're not, it's simply true."

Colm moved then—faster than a man that size should move—and there was a pistol in his hand, not flourished but simply *present*, the way a hammer is present in a hand accustomed to nails. "The gentleman," he said, nodding toward Jude without taking his eyes off Quentin, "will come outside with us. The lady will fetch the letters. We'll conduct our business on the gallery where nobody does anything stupid behind a closed door."

"And if I don't fetch them?"

"Then we wait for the gentleman to become persuasive," Colm said, "and I'll say plainly that I'm better at persuasion than I am at patience."

What happened next happened with a terrible administrative smoothness. Colm walked Jude up the stairs ahead of him, pistol low against his spine, and out onto the gallery where the wind took Jude's hair and made him blink. Finch stayed below with Quentin, standing between her and the kitchen and its single inadequate knife, and gestured politely toward the stairs, a man inviting a guest to proceed him through a doorway.

Quentin climbed.

On the gallery, with the whole grey world heaving below—the swell already building in long organized lines, the gulls blown backward mid-wing—Jude stood with his back against the lantern housing and Colm's pistol steady on his chest. His hands were open at his sides. He was very pale, and he was looking at her, and what was in his face wasn't fear for himself. It was arithmetic. She could see him doing it. One pistol. Two men. One keeper, one liar, and a tower full of letters that could hang half a ring's worth of men if they ever reached a magistrate.

"Don't," Jude said. "Whatever you're thinking. Don't."

"She hasn't said anything," Finch observed.

"She's wearing number nine."

Quentin went to the lantern.

It was burning steady above them, the great lamp turning its patient circles, the flame that she had tended through three winters of grief because tending it was the only prayer she knew. It was sweeping its light out over the water now, over those building swells, over the hidden teeth of Gullwrack and the shallows where the sea kept its drowned things. Any ship out tonight—a fishing boat caught late, a coaster running for harbor, a lifeboat—would steer by her flame. Would trust it. That was the whole covenant of the service: that the light burned, that the keeper kept, that no storm and no threat and no reason ever extinguished it. She had sworn it. Every keeper swore it. The words were simple enough to memorize in an afternoon and heavy enough to break a life on.

*The light burns. Whatever comes.*

"Fetch the letters," Finch said again, from the stairwell mouth.

Quentin put her hand on the dousing lever.

She felt the whole shape of the moment then, the way you feel a wave under a boat before it lifts you. To douse the light in fair weather with ships abroad was the sin above all sins, the thing that ended careers and haunted keepers' graves. If any vessel foundered tonight on Gullwrack's rocks in darkness, it would be on her soul entire, and no magistrate and no explanation would ever lift it. She knew this. She had known it since she was nineteen years old and first learned the weight of the mechanism under her hands.

But she also knew what the light was doing at this moment: it was making the tower visible, making Jude visible against it, making a target of everything she had left. It was guiding these men's confidence as surely as it guided sailors home. And beyond that—farther out, in the building dark, in the eye of the weather that was even now closing its fist—there was a boat she couldn't see yet, and a man she'd mourned for three years, steering by her flame toward rocks that these two men were guarding.

*Trust no one who brings my hand to you.* Emeric had known they might come. Had perhaps counted on it.

"Quentin," Jude said softly. "The covenant. I know what it costs. Don't pay it for me."

"It isn't for you," she said, which was mostly true, and loved him—loved both of them, God help her—for the way his face broke at it.

"Last time, mistress," Finch said. "Letters."

"No," Quentin said.

And she pulled the lever.

* * *

The lamp did not go out gently. It went out like a held breath released—the flame dropping, the great lens going suddenly blind, and the darkness that followed was not the absence of light but a presence, a thing with weight, rushing in from all sides at once. For one heartbeat the gallery existed only as sound: wind, swell, the tick of cooling brass, and Colm's voice saying a word his mother would have boxed his ears for.

Then Finch lunged for the stairs to relight it, and Quentin stepped into his path, and everything happened at the speed of consequence.

He struck her—or tried to; she turned enough that his blow glanced off her shoulder and sent her hard against the housing, pain blooming white down her arm. Colm swung the pistol toward her, and Jude moved. Not bravely, not skillfully—he would have been the first to say so—but with the simple desperate physics of a man who had spent a season of lies earning exactly one honest act and meant to spend it well. He hit Colm at the waist, and the two of them went across the gallery in a tangle of limbs and cursing, and the pistol cracked into the air somewhere over the sea, a flat ugly sound swallowed instantly by the wind.

The ball went nowhere. The sea kept all its secrets.

They fought in darkness. That was the mercy and the terror of it—Quentin could hear more than see, the scuff of boots, Colm's breath like a bellows, Jude making a sound she'd remember afterward, small and effortful, nothing like the stories. Her shoulder screamed every time she moved but she got herself between Finch and the stairwell and stayed there, arms out, a keeper defending her light's corpse, and Finch—who was quick but not brave, who had chosen the trade precisely because most people surrendered—looked at this woman planted in front of a black lantern and found he lacked the appetite to push past her.

"Get below," she told him, and her voice came out steadier than any part of her felt. "Both of you. Off my rock. Now, while you can still find the path."

Colm got an elbow free and put it into Jude's face, and Jude went down along the rail, and Colm stood breathing hard in the dark with his empty hand flexing. He was reaching into his coat for something else—Quentin didn't let herself think about what—when the sky spoke.

Not thunder, though thunder was coming. Something else. Out past the point, through a ragged tear in the storm clouds, a light showed where no light should be. Small, low to the water, swinging with the motion of a small boat driven hard—and then lost again as the cloud closed.

A boat, running for the rocks. Running for shore. Running *here*.

Colm saw it too. She heard him stop moving. Heard him say, very quietly, to Finch, in the tone of a man whose expense-account patience has just run out in the other direction entirely: "That'll be the boss's problem now. We're done being paid to stand on a rock in this." And then, to Quentin, from somewhere below, already retreating: "You've killed your own light, mistress. Hope whatever it was worth."

Their boots went down the stairs. The outer door slammed. Through the gallery rail Quentin watched two shapes stumble along the cliff path and vanish into the driving dark, and then there was only the wind, and the swell booming on the rocks below like something clearing its throat, and Jude sitting slumped against the housing with his hand pressed to his face, blood showing between his fingers, laughing or sobbing or possibly both.

"You doused it," he said. His voice sounded punched. "Quentin. You actually—you doused the light. Do you understand what you just—"

"Yes," she said.

She knelt beside him in the blackness. Her shoulder was a live coal and her hands, when she checked them, were shaking hard enough that she could watch them do it, and none of it mattered even slightly. She got her good arm around him and helped him up, and together they turned toward the stairwell and the dark tower above them, where a cold lamp waited, and wicks, and matches, and work.

Because the light had to burn again. Tonight of all nights, with that impossible boat out there running the rocks—whatever it carried, whoever steered it—the light had to burn. She would relight it if it took her last match and her last working limb. She owed that to every sailor on the water.

She owed it, God forgive her, to whoever was out there steering toward her in the dark.

Below them, far down, the sea lifted its voice against Gullwrack Point, and through the storm's first true fury, Quentin thought she heard—over wind, over swell, over her own pounding heart—a sound no storm had any business making.

A bell? No.

A voice, calling up through the dark. Calling her name.
