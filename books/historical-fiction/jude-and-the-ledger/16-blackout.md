## 16. Blackout

The dark came down on Gullwrack Point like a lid.

Jude had imagined the moment of dousing a hundred times in the last hour—imagined it as Quentin must have meant it to be: the lamp simply gone, the great lens blind, the world outside the glass erased. What he had not imagined was the sound. The light had been roaring in its own quiet way for days, that low constant breath of flame and machinery he'd stopped hearing weeks ago. When Quentin threw the damping shutter and pulled the fuel line, the silence it left behind was enormous. It had weight. It pressed against his ears like deep water.

And beneath that silence, the storm.

"Down," Quentin said, and her voice in the blackness was the steadiest thing on the coast of anywhere. "Stairs. Now."

He went, one hand on the cold iron rail, the other pressed to the oilskin packet inside his coat—the letters, the whole rotten beautiful reason any of them were still alive. Behind him, above him, he heard the salvager's boots on the gallery grating, heard the man's confusion turn to fury as the world went out around him. A man who comes to a lighthouse with a gun does not expect the light to stop being a light.

A keeper's ultimate transgression, she had called it, before she did it. She'd said it the way Emeric used to say things he'd already decided—apologizing to no one, least of all himself.

*He's her husband,* Jude thought, absurdly, halfway down the spiral stairs, *and she just blinded her own tower for him.* Because Emeric was out there somewhere in that screaming dark, in a boat too small for the sea it was crossing, and if the light burned, the men with guns would use it to find their way to the letters. If the light burned, it would guide the ring straight up the rocks to everything Quentin loved.

So she had put it out. Jude thought he might love her for it, in the fierce and unromantic way you love people who do the necessary terrible thing while your hands are still deciding.

The gun went off above him.

* * *

He heard it twice before he understood it was aimed at anything. In this wind, a gunshot was just another insult the storm was shouting; the whole night sounded armed. But then sparks scattered across the stairwell above him—a lantern, dropped or struck—and by their light Jude saw the younger salvager coming down after him, coat snapping, pistol still raised, and saw Quentin reach the lamp room door at the bottom of the stairs and put her body between it and everything falling toward her.

"Not the lamp," she said. "You can have the letters when I'm dead, and not pleasantly even then."

"You've got it backward, missus." The man's voice was almost friendly, which was worse than shouting. He came down the stairs like a man descending a pub staircase, unhurried, sure-footed, the gun resting easy against his thigh. "Dead keepers tell no one where papers are hid. Alive ones, though—alive ones can be asked politely for years."

Jude did not decide to move. That was the thing he would carry afterward, the thing that woke him some nights in the years that followed: there was no decision, no brave accounting, nothing measured. His body simply understood, before his mind caught up, that the gun was aimed at Quentin and that Quentin was—there was no other word for it, however ridiculous it had become in six weeks of mended nets and bad card games and bread shared at a small table—Quentin was *his.*

He hit the man from the side, two steps' worth of staircase above the landing, and the two of them went down the rest together.

* * *

Falling down a lighthouse stairwell in a storm is less dramatic than it sounds and considerably more painful. There is no long graceful tumble. There is an explosion of elbows and iron and the particular bright agony of your skull meeting something that has stood in its place for eighty years and intends to go on standing in its place long after you are a story salvagers tell each other over drinks. They landed on the lower landing in a heap, and the lantern went out, and the dark closed over them again, thick as tar.

The gun skittered somewhere. Jude heard it spin away across iron and threw himself after the sound on pure animal faith.

The salvager caught his ankle.

They fought the way everyone fights really—in the dark, gracelessly, mostly by feel, all knees and breath and swearing. The man was bigger. He had the calm of someone who had done violence before and found it agreeable work. He got a forearm across Jude's throat and bore down, and the wind screamed through the gallery rail above them, and Jude thought—with a strange floating clarity, the kind grief gives you—that Emeric would never forgive him if he died here. Not because of sentiment. Because Emeric would find it unbearably funny, his little brother, the careful one, the one sent only to read ledgers and confirm deaths, bludgeoned on a lighthouse landing by a man named presumably something like Cyril.

Jude laughed. He felt it happen more than chose it—a wheezing, strangled sound under the man's arm—and the salvager hesitated, because a laughing victim is an unsettling thing, and in that half-second's hesitation Jude got his thumb into the soft flesh under the man's jaw and shoved, and twisted, and they were rolling again.

Something opened along Jude's side. He didn't know what until later—a gash from the stairwell's iron lip, laid open like an envelope. At the time it was just heat, sudden and alarming, the feeling of being abruptly much more interested in lying down than he had been a moment before.

The man's fist found his face. Then the dark took the rest.

* * *

Here is what Jude missed, unconscious or nearly so, for perhaps ten seconds that lasted a season:

He missed Quentin Okonkwo, widow, keeper of Gullwrack Point, come back up those stairs out of the dark with a fire iron in both hands and no expression whatsoever on her face.

He missed the sound the fire iron made.

He missed the salvager going over the low gate at the landing's edge, catching himself on the rail—not the outer rail, worse luck, but the inner—and scrambling, bleeding, cursing, up and out and gone into the wind like a man fleeing a house fire. He missed Quentin leaning on the fire iron afterward, breathing hard, saying to the empty stairwell, in the mild tone she used for correcting the logbook, "*Politely*, he said. For *years.*"

What he came back for was the cold, and then the pain, and then her hand on his face.

"Jude. Jude. Look at me, you great useless—open your eyes or so help me I'll write something unflattering about you in the ledger."

"That's," he managed, "that's supposed to be a threat?"

"It's worked on every man I've ever kept."

He opened his eyes. Her face swam above his, lit by the small storm-lantern she'd fetched from somewhere, and it was pale and set and wet with spray, and her hands—her strong, ink-stained, wick-trimming hands—were shaking. Not gently. Shaking like leaves, like bells.

She saw him see it and turned away, busying herself with his side, peeling back his coat with a tenderness she'd have denied under oath.

"It's a cut," she said, in her ledger voice. "Long but not deep. You'll want stitching. I've done horses."

"Horses," Jude said. "Charming. Do you talk to them this way?"

"The horses hold still." She tore a strip from her own petticoat without ceremony—he would learn, later, that she considered this the highest honor a garment could receive—and bound the wound with quick, competent turns, and all the while her hands went on shaking, and all the while her voice stayed level as a spirit gauge, and Jude lay on the iron landing of a blinded lighthouse with his blood soaking into a dead woman's petticoat and understood, with a fullness that hurt worse than the cut, that terror and tenderness were not two feelings at all. They were one feeling wearing two faces, and she was drowning in it, and so was he.

"Where's the other one?" he asked.

"Gone over the seaward rocks in the dark, or I'm a herring." She cinched the knot. "If the sea doesn't take him, he'll think twice about coming back without the light to climb by. Which," she added, and here her voice cracked, just once, hairline-thin, "was rather the idea."

* * *

Getting him down the remaining stairs was a humiliation Jude bore with what he felt was remarkable grace, given that it consisted largely of being carried by a woman four inches shorter than himself, complaining the whole way in a manner she probably believed was encouraging. ("You weigh more than you let on." "It's the letters." "The letters weigh four ounces." "They're very dense letters.")

She sat him in the keeper's chair at the foot of the tower, wrapped him in the good blanket, the one she pretended wasn't for guests, and then stood very still in the middle of the room.

He knew what she was doing, because he was doing it too. Listening. Weighing. The storm outside had lost whatever patience it had been pretending to have; the barometer had fallen so far that afternoon that she'd tapped the glass twice, as if it might reconsider. And somewhere out in all of it, in a boat the size of a large mistake, was Emeric.

"He'll run for the cove," Quentin said. "Dark or no dark. He knows these rocks better than the fish do." She stopped. Corrected herself, quietly, the way you correct an old habit: "Knew. Knew these rocks."

"And if he doesn't make the cove?"

"Then he'll make the north beach and curse the entire journey, which will at least confirm he's alive." She looked up the tower, into the dark above the dark. "But he'll be looking for the light, Jude. Coming in off that water, in that, a man looks for the light. It's how you find land. It's how you find *home.*" Her jaw worked. "I put it out to hide us, and I may have hidden the shore itself from him."

"Q—" He hadn't earned the shortening yet. She didn't seem to notice it had happened.

"There's no choice in it," she said, more to herself than to him. "That's the filthy joke of it. The ring's man could come back. The Meridian's master is watching this coast tonight, counting flashes. A dead light is a wrecked light—it signals distress, invites boarders, brings every scavenger between here and the mainland sniffing down our rocks. And my husband is at sea." She was already moving, pulling the lamp key from the hook at her belt, taking down the box of matches she kept dry against her breast like other women kept portraits. "There's no choice. There's only the order of things. Light first, fear after."

"Your hands," Jude said.

"I know about my hands."

She climbed the stairs ahead of him, refusing his arm, and he followed anyway—one hand pressed to his stitched-and-bound side, the other on the rail, climbing into the black throat of the tower behind a woman whose shaking he could hear in the rhythm of her steps.

At the top, in the great round room where he'd spent six weeks learning to haul fuel and trim wicks and stand watches he'd told himself were temporary, she knelt at the lamp's base and did what she had done every night for three years alone: opened the fuel line, primed the mantle, touched the match.

The first match broke in her fingers.

The second caught, and guttered, and died.

Jude crouched beside her, wound burning, and covered her shaking hands with his own.

"Together," he said. "Like the shutters. Like the cards. Like everything else we've survived by doing badly at first."

She made a sound that was either a laugh or the opposite of one. On the third match, his palm steady over her steady-over-nothing grip, the flame took—and ran—up the wick, and the mantle bloomed white, and the great lens gathered it and flung it out across the water in one slow, golden, resurrecting sweep, and Gullwrack Point was a lighthouse again.

Quentin stayed kneeling. The light turned above them, once, twice, painting her face gold and shadow, gold and shadow. She was crying, he realized. Without any of the noise of crying. Tears just running down a face otherwise entirely occupied with watching the beam go out and come home, out and come home, as if it might catch something on its return. As if it might bring something in.

"He'll see it now," she whispered.

"Yes."

"Whatever happens. Whatever he is now, whatever he's done, whatever he comes ashore to take from me—" The beam swung; the shadows turned. "He'll see the light. He'll always be able to say he saw it."

And then, because the world is not sentimental, that was exactly the moment the storm eased—not ended, nothing so kind, but split, cracked open down the middle the way storms sometimes do at their worst hour, offering a false and merciful eye—and through the gap, far out where no boat should have been, Jude saw the shape.

Small. Low. Running under a patched sail straight through the breaking water toward the point, handled with a skill that was itself a signature, a handwriting written in keel and wake.

Quentin saw it too. He watched the recognition go through her like the light going through the lens—all that scattered terror gathered, focused, thrown in one direction.

She rose slowly to her feet, one hand pressed flat against the warm brass of the lamp column as if steadying herself against the whole turning sky, and she did not say Emeric's name, because she was a woman who had learned not to say things she couldn't survive saying twice.

"Jude," she said instead, very quietly. "Someone's coming home."
