## 17. The Ship That Shouldn't Be There

The eye of a storm, Quentin had learned in her three years at Gullwrack Point, was not peace. It was held breath. The wind did not stop so much as kneel, and the sea beneath it went strange and glassy, heaving with the memory of all it had just done and all it intended to do again. The clouds tore open overhead into a ragged circle of pale sky, and the light that fell through it was the color of old pewter, and nothing—nothing—moved.

She had perhaps twenty minutes. Maybe fifteen. She had learned to read the eye the way other people read weather-glasses: by the shape of the hole, by how quickly the walls of cloud leaned inward. This one was wide and slow to close. She had time enough to do what needed doing and no more.

So she did it methodically, because method was the only prayer she trusted. She checked the lamp's feed and trimmed what wanted trimming. She hauled the spare oil up from the store room, grunting at the weight of it, thinking uncharitably that if the Light Board could see her hauling forty pounds of whale oil up a spiral stair one-handed while the worst storm in living memory gathered itself for a second course, they might finally approve her request for a second keeper. They would not, of course. The Light Board approved things the way rocks absorbed rain.

Jude was below, in the watch room, doing something to his shoulder that he clearly hoped she wouldn't notice him doing. She had noticed. She always noticed now; three weeks of shared bread and mended nets and card games played with a deck missing two sevens had taught her the geography of Jude's silences as well as any map. He moved like a man apologizing to his own body for the trouble he'd brought it.

"How is it?" she asked, not turning from the lamp.

"Improved," said Jude, in the tone of a man describing a fire as *contained*.

"Mm."

"You're going to look anyway, aren't you."

"I'm going to look anyway," she agreed, and looked, and made him sit still while she rewrapped the gash along his collarbone—the one he'd earned on her gallery rail two nights ago, holding off a man with a knife while the world went black around them both. It was knitting. It would scar. She found she didn't mind the scar. A scar was a record; she had come to believe in records.

Outside, through the lantern room's curved glazing, the sea lay flat and shivering under the impossible calm. Gullwrack's rocks showed dark and wet from the tideline up, every one of them exposed, every ledge and channel she knew by heart laid bare like the bones of an old argument. At low water in the eye, you could see the wreck of the *Merrow*, thirty years drowned, its ribs poking up black against the pewter light. Quentin had never seen it before. She thought, absurdly, that Emeric would have liked to see it—that she had spent seven years married to a man who collected shipwrecks the way other men collected stamps—and then stopped herself, because thinking of Emeric in the past tense had become a habit she could no longer trust.

The letters sat in their oilskin packet on the chart table. She had not burned them. She had considered burning them, in the black furious hours after Jude's confession, when the wind screamed like something cheated, and she had taken them out and held them over the stove and found that she could not. Not because they were precious. Because they were evidence—Jude's word for it, and the word was right—but also because they were, whatever else, written in her husband's hand. Forged or not, borrowed from memory or not, that hand had once written her freckles were the sky's own arithmetic, and she was not yet ready to make ashes of arithmetic.

A knock of boots on the stair—Jude coming up, favoring the left side. He joined her at the window, and for a while they simply watched the eye work, two people standing shoulder to shoulder against a silence louder than the storm had been.

"He'll have gone to ground," Jude said at last. "The other one. Corvan won't stay ashore with the harbor closing and half the ring already talking to the customs men. He'll cut his losses."

"And the letters?"

"He doesn't know we still have them. He came to take them, and he left without them, and men like Corvan don't forgive themselves a failure like that." Jude's jaw worked. "He'll be back. After the storm, if he can find a boat. Which means after the storm we need to be somewhere else, or have them somewhere else, or—" He stopped. Looked at her. "Quentin. You've gone white."

"I haven't gone white," said Quentin, who had gone white. "Look there."

She raised her hand—not quite pointing, keeper's habit, you pointed at nothing on the water until you'd confirmed it twice—and indicated the southern reach, where the sea met the torn skirt of cloud.

At first there was nothing. That was the trick of the eye, the way it flattened everything into a single held breath, so that your gaze slid across detail without catching. Then the detail caught.

A sail. No—a scrap of canvas, badly set, patched where patching had no business surviving. And beneath it, low in the water, hull-down in the long swell, the dark line of a small boat running the inner channel toward Gullwrack's rocks.

Running the inner channel. In the eye of the worst storm in living memory. Toward shore that would, within the hour, be a mile of white murder.

"Nobody runs the inner channel," Jude said slowly. "Nobody runs it in fair weather without a pilot. Nobody runs it in *that*—" he gestured at the closing walls of cloud "—unless they know it better than the pilots do."

"Someone does," Quentin said. Her voice came out very level, which surprised her, because inside her chest something had begun to move that she had not permitted herself to feel in three years, and it was moving with terrible confidence, the way a tide moves, indifferent to whether you are ready.

The boat grew as they watched. It was a gaff-rigged thing, twenty feet perhaps, salt-white and battered, and it handled the swell in a manner that stopped being luck the longer she looked. Whoever steered her did not fight the water. He *agreed* with it—gave when the sea gave, took when it took, used the back of each wave as a road and the face of the next as a wall to lean against. Quentin had grown up among boats. She had spent seven years watching a man handle boats. There was a way Emeric had of standing—not braced but *planted*, feet wide, knees soft, one hand lazy on the tiller as though the boat were a large dog that mostly meant well—

No. She shut the thought. She shut it hard, and locked it, and put the key in her apron pocket, because she had buried that particular madness once already and she did not intend to dig it up for a stranger with good seamanship.

"It's Corvan," she said. "It has to be. Coming back for the letters."

"Corvan doesn't sail," Jude said quietly. "Corvan gets sailed. And Corvan would send that boat in with six men in it, and I count—" he squinted "—one."

One. One figure at the tiller, dark-cloaked, hooded against the spray, standing planted, knees soft, one hand lazy on the—

*Stop.*

"We should signal the Meridian," she said, and heard how thin her own voice sounded. "If there's anyone aboard that boat, the longboat—"

"The Meridian's lying off past the point with two reefs down and her captain praying; nobody's launching a longboat into this. Whoever's on that boat isn't asking for rescue." Jude's voice had gone strange too—careful, the way his voice went when he was choosing between truths. "Quentin. Look at how she's steering. Look at the line she's taking."

She looked. The boat was making for the lee of the northern headland—the only water at Gullwrack where a landing was possible in anything above a dead calm, the slot between the Black Stones where the surge doubled back on itself and forgave exactly one mistake per lifetime. It was the landing Emeric had used a hundred times. It was the landing *she* used. It was not on any chart finer than a fingernail, and no salvager, no smuggler, no stranger to these shores would pick it out of a coastline in an hour, let alone in the dying minutes of a storm's eye.

The key in her apron pocket turned itself over.

"Emeric used to say," she heard herself begin, and her voice sounded very far away, as if someone else were speaking through her, "that the inner channel was honest. That it only killed people who lied about knowing it." She swallowed. "He used to run it with the main alone and the centerboard half-down and he'd sing while he did it, some dreadful thing about a mermaid and a tax collector, and I used to tell him the day the sea got tired of his singing I'd hear about it by post."

Jude said nothing. Beside her, very still, he was looking at the boat with an expression she could not read—except that she could, suddenly, entirely, because she had spent three weeks learning this man's face, and what was on it was recognition. Not surprise. Recognition. The face of a man seeing something he had been told was impossible and finding, in some cellar of himself, that he had known it all along.

"Jude." Her voice cracked on the second syllable. "Is that—"

"I don't know," he said, which was a lie, and then, because three weeks of honesty had gotten under his skin like salt: "I don't *know*. But I sent letters in his hand to draw the ring away from my brother, and my brother drew them from memory, and there were nights I wrote and wrote and could not remember which words were mine and which were his." His throat moved. "And I have not seen Emeric since I was fourteen years old. So I don't know. But Quentin—look at his hands."

She looked. The figure at the tiller had shifted, spilling a little canvas for the last of the channel, and even at this distance—half a mile of shivering water—she could see the hands: bare, working the sheet in short economical pulls, wrap and haul, wrap and haul, never gripping, always *agreeing*. Emeric's hands. She would know them anywhere. She had watched them write ledgers and peel apples and lie, once, magnificently, to a harbormaster about cargo manifests, and she had laughed at how easily he lied and kissed him after, and it had been three years, three years of ledger entries that said only *WIND SW FRESH, LAMP BURNED WELL*, because writing about herself meant admitting there was still a self to write about—

The boat entered the Black Stones' shadow and was gone.

* *

Then there was nothing to do but go down.

That was the truth of a lighthouse: you could stand in the lantern room and be a god of observation, and the moment something needed doing you became a woman on stairs. Quentin took them two at a time with Jude behind her protesting that she should wait, that it might be anyone, that she should at least take the knife—and she did take the knife, because she was grief-mad, not stupid, and hooked it through her belt without breaking stride.

The boathouse first. The eye would not hold forever; she could feel it beginning to fail, a pressure change in her teeth, the walls of cloud leaning in like a conspiracy. She got the door open and stood a moment in the flat strange light, breathing the bruised air, and out on the water the boat came sliding out of the Stones' shadow into the lee, close-hauled, beautiful, coming in on the last of its way like a sentence arriving at its final word.

The figure dropped the sail at the last possible moment and let momentum carry the hull toward the slip. He stepped forward to fend off—one hand out, palm open against the stone—and the boat kissed the slip gentle as a goodnight, and the figure turned and pushed back his hood, and Quentin Okonkwo, who had rehearsed this moment every night for three years and had never once gotten past the rehearsal, looked at her husband's face for the first time since they had buried an empty coffin in the rain.

He was thinner. There was grey at his temples that had not been there, and a scar through one eyebrow, and he was soaked to the skin and shaking with cold or exhaustion or fear, and he was looking up at her on the slipway the way a man looks at a jury.

"Quentin," Emeric said. His voice was roughened, half-ruined, but it was his voice, God help her, it was his voice saying her name the way he had said it ten thousand times, at breakfast, in the dark, across ledgers. "I expect you have questions."

She had, in that moment, precisely one coherent thought, and it was this: *the arithmetic.* The sky's own arithmetic. He had written it in a letter forged by his brother, and here he stood, and the numbers added up to something she could not yet bear to solve.

Behind her, on the boathouse step, Jude made a sound like a man being unmade and remade in the same breath, and said: "Em?"

Emeric's eyes went past her, found his brother, and filled—with something that was not quite joy and not quite apology but was some terrible compound of both—and he said, "Hello, little brother. I'm sorry about the handwriting."

The first drop of the returning storm struck the slipway between them, fat and cold as a coin, and Quentin reached down for the knife at her belt—
