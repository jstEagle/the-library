## 18. Emeric

He climbed the tower stairs the way he used to climb them on Sunday mornings after a night watch—slowly, one hand on the rail, as though apologizing to his own knees for what he'd asked of them. Quentin heard him before she saw him: boots wrung out with seawater, each step a small wet confession.

She had watched his boat come through the eye of the storm with the glass in her hand and her heart doing something unprofessional. She had watched it run the rocks at the only channel that would forgive it, and she had thought, *that stance, that lean into the tiller like a man arguing with an old friend*—and then she had put down the glass because her hands were shaking too badly to hold it steady.

Now she stood in the lantern room with the great lens turning behind her, throwing its slow wheel of light across the walls, across him, as he came up the last steps and stopped in the doorway. Soaked through. Hollow-cheeked. Older than three years had any right to make him, and yet so entirely, insultingly himself that she almost laughed.

His hair was longer. There was gray in it now that hadn't been gray before, or perhaps hadn't been honest before. His coat was ruined. His eyes went around the lantern room—the brass, the wicks, the log book open on its stand—and then found her and stopped, the way a compass needle stops.

"Quentin," he said.

"Don't," she said.

He closed his mouth. The light wheeled past his face: lit, dark, lit.

"You don't get to say my name first," she said. "You don't get to walk up those stairs looking like a drowned apology and open with my name as if it were a key you'd been keeping safe. Sit down."

"There's nowhere to sit."

"Then stand there and drip."

He stood there and dripped. Below them, faint under the wind, she could hear Jude moving about in the boathouse—he had gone down without being asked, without being told, taking his wounded arm and his terrible honesty with him, giving them this room the way you give someone a sickbed: quietly, and without expecting thanks. Her chest did something complicated about that. She set it aside. It would keep. Most of her feelings had learned to keep, these three years, like preserves in a cellar.

"I watched your boat," she said. "From up here. I thought I was seeing a ghost, and then I thought, no—a ghost wouldn't fight the tide like that. A ghost would let the sea have him. You fought it. You've always fought it. Even coming home to confess, you couldn't just arrive. You had to *sail*."

"The channel was there," Emeric said. "I've known it since I was nineteen."

"That's not an answer to anything. That's just sailing."

"It's the only thing I know how to do that doesn't involve lying."

The light swept past again. In its glow she saw him flinch at his own words, and she felt something twist that was not quite satisfaction. Satisfaction would come later, maybe. Or never. Right now it was more like the moment when a splinter finally sits close enough to the surface to take hold of with the tweezers.

"Jude told me some of it," she said. "The ledger. The ring. The informant they drowned—Tomas, Jude said his name was. Tomas who kept books for them and got careful. And you—what? You saw Tomas floating in the harbor and decided the wisest thing a man could do was become a widow-maker?"

"Yes," Emeric said simply. "That is nearly word for word the reasoning."

"Don't be glib."

"I'm not being glib. I'm being brief. If I'm not brief I'll start explaining instead of confessing, and explaining is how men like me survive our own consciences." He peeled off what remained of his coat and dropped it on the floor, and beneath it he was thinner than she remembered, the bones of him closer to the surface, a man who had been living carefully in unfriendly places. "Quentin. I need to say all of it, in order, and I need you to let me finish before you speak, because if you interrupt me I will lose my nerve, and I have rowed through the tail end of the worst storm in thirty years, and I would very much like not to have done that for nothing."

She folded her arms. Outside, the storm raged on either side of the eye's strange calm, like a beast circling to catch its breath.

"Say it, then," she said. "In order. And mind your tenses. I'll be listening to your tenses."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face and died there. He knew what she meant. Three years ago, tense trouble had been the tell that caught him out over a borrowed watch coat—he'd said *had* when he should have said *has*, and she'd known in four words that he was hiding something. It was, in fact, the last argument they'd ever had, the one before the *Gannet* sailed and never came home.

"On the twelfth of October," he began, "three years ago, the *Gannet* left harbor carrying cargo that was listed as salted herring and was not salted herring. I had been running that cargo for two years. I want you to understand—I knew from the first season. I told myself stories. Everybody in that trade tells themselves stories; the whole coast is upholstered with them. *It's only untaxed brandy. It's only silk. Nobody bleeds over silk.* Then Tomas Osei started asking questions of the wrong people, and Tomas floated up under the pier at Millhaven with his pockets full of stones, and I understood that the story I'd been telling myself had been written by other men, and that I was a line item in it."

"And your solution," Quentin said, "was to become a dead line item."

"My solution," he said, "was to sink the *Gannet*."

The lens turned. Light, dark, light. She gripped the rail of the lamp well and made herself keep breathing.

"She was mine," Emeric went on. "Free and clear, in my name, insured—which was its own small dishonesty, and I've paid that back twice over since, in coin, to the underwriters' society, anonymously, which I imagine confused them enormously. I loaded her with stone ballast where the cargo should have been, opened her seams off the Teeth, and took the dinghy ashore at a cove where nobody asks questions. The ring believed I was dead for exactly eleven days. On the twelfth day, one of their men found the dinghy. After that, it wasn't a question of whether they'd believe I was dead. It became a question of whether they could find anyone who mattered to me."

Quentin's mouth had gone dry. "So you cut me loose first."

"So I vanished," he said. "There's a difference, and it isn't a fine one, and I've had three years of nights to lie to myself about it, and I'm done lying. I cut everything. I cut the ledgers, the letters, the debts, the wife. I reasoned—and God, Quentin, I reasoned like a lawyer defending himself—that a widow was safer than a wife. That they don't interrogate widows. That grief would be armor." He looked down at his own hands, which were shaking. "I built you a grave and called it a shield."

"And then," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she expected, colder, which surprised her, because inside she was nothing but weather—"then you wrote to me anyway."

"No."

"*No?*"

"I never wrote another word after the wreck. Not one. What I sent you before—the letter from Millhaven, the one about the freckles—that was real, that was mine, that was written by a coward who still thought he had until spring to change his mind. Everything after. Every page in that packet. None of it came from my hand."

The light swung across the room and caught, for half a turn, the oilskin packet itself, sitting on the shelf by the log book where Quentin had left it after the confrontation with the salvagers, after the blackout, after the fight on the gallery that had left blood on the ironwork that she still hadn't scrubbed. Jude's packet. Jude's letters. In Emeric's hand.

"They're good," she said quietly. "They're very good. Whoever forged them knew—" and then she stopped, because she had already known this part, had assembled it in the small hours while relighting the lamp with her hands trembling, and saying it aloud was different. Saying it aloud made it true. "Jude knew the joke about the freckles. You told him that joke. You told him things you never told me you'd told anyone."

"He's my brother." Emeric said it the way you'd say *the sun rose*. "He was fourteen when I left home the first time. I wrote to him for years—proper letters, long ones, before the ring and the cargo and all of it, back when the worst thing I did was exaggerate my adventures. He has my hand better than I do now. Mine's deteriorated; his improved on practice. When he was a boy he used to copy my letters out for the sheer pleasure of it, the way other boys copy out poems." Something moved through his face then that was not guilt exactly, but the thing underneath guilt, the bedrock of it. "When the ring decided I might still be alive, they needed proof one way or the other, and they needed leverage, and the leverage they wanted was you. They knew about you. They'd read my letters—Tomas had copies of some, God help us all, seized before he died. They planned to send an agent to Gullwrack with a story and see what surfaced."

"So they sent Jude."

"They sent Jude," Emeric said, "*because* Jude volunteered. He'd been searching for me for two years—half convinced I was alive, half convinced he'd have to mourn properly eventually. When he heard the ring talking about the keeper's wife at Gullwrack Point, about the widow with the light, he understood two things at once. One: that if they reached you first, you'd be a hostage, and worse—you'd be a hostage who believed her husband had written her loving letters from beyond the grave, which is a cruelty I can't find words large enough for. Two: that if he reached you first, and if the letters drew attention—if the ring's hunters had to chase a phantom correspondence up and down the coast—then they'd be chasing *him*, and following *him*, and not following the one man they actually wanted, who was sitting in a room in Millhaven under a false name, slowly drinking himself toward a decision."

"What decision?"

"To stop running and come here," Emeric said. "To stop being clever. I am not a brave man, Quentin, but I am apparently a stubborn one, and it took me three years to be stubborn in the correct direction. Jude's letters bought me the time. Every forgery he sent was a flare lit on the opposite hill—*look here, look here*—while I sold the last of what I owned, bought a boat that could handle the Teeth, and waited for a storm bad enough that nobody would follow a madman crossing to Gullwrack."

"And the letters themselves." She picked up the packet now. It weighed nothing and everything. "The one addressed to me. The warning. *Trust no one who brings my hand to you.*"

"Jude wrote that one himself," Emeric said. "Without being asked. Without telling me. I found out afterward and I have never—" His voice cracked, actually cracked, and he pressed on through it. "I have never been so frightened in my life. Because that letter, if the ring had intercepted it, would have signed his death warrant alongside mine. He wrote it anyway. He looked at a woman he'd never met, a woman his brother had abandoned to a lie, and he chose to protect her at the cost of his own skin, and then he stood in your kitchen and let you hate him for it."

Below, faintly, the boathouse door creaked. Jude, pacing, or checking the lines, or simply unable to stay still while his fate was discussed overhead. Quentin thought of him mending nets in her kitchen with his sleeves rolled up. Thought of the card game, and her own laugh, sudden and shocking as a gull's cry. Thought of him standing watch in the boathouse all night in the wind rather than leave, rather than push, rather than take one step further into a life he had invaded by accident and kept out of decency.

"I did hate him," she said. "For about six hours. It was very efficient. Then he put himself between a gun and my light, and hatred found it didn't have the staff for the position."

Emeric laughed—a short, broken sound, startled out of him—and then pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "That sounds like him."

"It sounds like you, from what I can tell. Before."

"No." He lowered his hand. "Before, I would have schemed my way into being likable. Jude just *is*. I used to resent it. Now I mostly marvel at it, from a distance, like weather in another country." He straightened, and she saw him gather himself, saw him arrive at the part he'd climbed the stairs to say, the part beneath all the other parts. "Quentin. I'm going to ask for nothing. I want that understood before the storm starts again and makes everything louder. I did not come back for forgiveness. I came back because the letters exist, and the letters are evidence, and evidence in your house puts you in danger as long as I'm a rumor. And because—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "And because a man can only apologize to a lighthouse from offshore for so long before even he admits it's cowardice dressed as courtesy."

The lamp turned above them both, patient as it had been through every night of her solitude, every entry in the ledger that was about weather and nothing else, never about herself, never about him, because she had decided somewhere in the second winter that the record would stay clean even if her heart wouldn't.

"You're right that you get nothing," she said. "Not tonight. Tonight you get a towel, dry clothes from the chest in the storeroom—Emeric, don't look at me like that, they're yours, I never gave them away, I'm not made of principle—and bread, if there's any left after Jude discovered my baking. Tomorrow you get work. If you're staying under my roof, you'll trim wicks and haul oil and log the barometer, and you'll do it correctly, because I will not have my light mismanaged by a man who faked his own death *badly enough to be suspected*."

"It was suspected because the dinghy washed up. That was luck. Bad luck."

"It was suspected," Quentin said, "because you were always a little too pleased with your own plans, and the sea knows a smug sailor when it sees one." But her voice betrayed her at the end of it, catching on something, and she turned to the window fast, to the black water, to anything else.

Behind her she heard him move—hesitate—and not touch her. She heard him decide, deliberately, not to touch her, and it was somehow the most decent thing he had done in three years, and it undid her more than an embrace would have. She stood with her forehead nearly against the cold glass and breathed until she was certain of her voice.

"Emeric. Why did you really come? The evidence could have gone out with Jude on the Meridian. You know that. You could have stayed hidden. The truth is simpler than your reasons, so say the simple version or I'll assume the worst."

The silence behind her lasted four full turns of the lens. She counted them.

"The simple version," he said at last, "is that I died three years ago, and I would like, before the end of anything, to be remembered by the one person whose memory is worth having. Not forgiven. Remembered. I came home to be remembered."

Quentin turned around. The light passed over his face—wet, wrecked, hopeful in the way that hope looks on a man who has trained himself out of it—and she saw her husband, and she saw a stranger, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like the storm's eye itself, that both things were true and would remain true, and that the work ahead was not choosing between them but keeping a steady light over both.

"Then you'll be remembered," she said. "Accurately. Which is harder than fondly, and worth more." She picked up the towel from the hook and threw it to him, because her arms wanted very badly to do something else and a towel was the only honest option. "Now come down before the eye closes. And bring the packet. And knock on the boathouse door on your way—gently—with your *own* hand, and tell your brother that supper's at seven, and that if he's mended any more of my nets without asking I shall dock his wages."

"I don't get wages."

"Neither does family," Quentin said, and heard the word leave her mouth a half-second before she could inspect it, and decided—looking at Emeric's stunned face, hearing below the small halt of footsteps on the boathouse stair—that she would let it stand.

Outside, far out past the Teeth, the western sky was beginning to darken along its edge, the eye narrowing, the storm gathering its shoulders for the final blow. The barometer in the passage had fallen further in one hour than she had ever recorded it falling, and the Meridian was due at dawn with one berth, and three people stood in a tower built for one keeper.

"Quentin," Emeric said from the doorway, towel around his neck like a man wearing the past, "there's something you should know about the Meridian's berth, and about why the ring's hunters knew these waters so well, and I think—" he glanced at the falling glass, "—I think you should hear it before the wind comes back."
