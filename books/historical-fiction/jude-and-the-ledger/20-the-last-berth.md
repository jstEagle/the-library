## 20. The Last Berth

The longboat came ashore in the gray hour before dawn, when the storm had drawn one long breath and held it—a held breath being the sea's version of mercy, which is to say temporary and not to be trusted.

Quentin saw it first from the gallery, a dark shape bobbing in the surf line with four men pulling hard against the swell, and she thought, with the strange calm that had settled over her somewhere around the second hour of the worst night of her life: *Well. It's punctual.*

The Meridian waited beyond the point like a patient animal, riding at anchor with her lights swinging. The harbormaster had signaled at midnight—one berth, one seat, one window of perhaps two hours before the harbor closed for the season and did not open again until spring. One person could leave Gullwrack Point tonight.

Three people were standing on it.

She had known, in some chamber of herself that had been quietly working the problem all night while the rest of her hauled oil and trimmed wicks and bound up Jude's arm, how this would end. She had known it the way she knew the weight of the lamp mechanism, the way she knew how many turns of the crank brought the lens to full rotation—by heart, by long practice, by the simple arithmetic of who she was.

But first there was the matter of getting down the stairs without breaking her neck, which at her age and in her mood was a genuine competing concern.

* * *

The boathouse was chaos of the ordinary kind: Emeric hauling the skiff out of the waterline where they'd stowed it, Jude arguing with a lantern about his own capability to stand, the wind finding every gap in the boards and making its opinions known. Quentin stood in the doorway with the oilskin packet under her arm and let them argue for exactly as long as it took her to decide what she was going to say.

"—not going anywhere with a hole in me," Jude was saying. He had gone gray around the mouth since the fight on the gallery, though he'd insisted on walking down the tower stairs unassisted, which had cost him most of his color and one long pause on the fourth landing that none of them had acknowledged aloud.

"It's a stitch and a half," Emeric said. "I've seen you take worse from a kitchen drawer."

"I've seen you die," Jude shot back, and then went quiet, because that was the kind of thing that stopped an argument the way a stone stops a clock.

Quentin stepped into the light.

"Sit down, Jude," she said, "and listen, because I'm only going to say this once and I intend to enjoy it."

He sat. There was something about her voice—she'd heard it herself, that particular flat calm she used when the barometer fell faster than a man could fall after it—and it worked on him as it worked on everyone.

"The berth goes to you," she said. "You'll take the letters." She set the packet in his lap. "Every page. You'll give your testimony—the whole testimony, not the tidy half you gave me at low tide—to whoever has the authority to pull that ring up by its roots, and you'll keep giving it until they do. That's what those letters are for now. Not bait. Evidence."

Jude looked down at the packet as if it might bite him. "Quentin—"

"You spent three years learning to forge your brother's hand," she said. "It seems only right that the skill should finally do something honest. Consider it a career change."

Emeric made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"I can't take it," Jude said. "The berth. It should be you. You're the one who—" His hand moved vaguely at the tower, the light, the whole impossible edifice of the last three weeks. "You kept it. Through all of it. You're the one who deserves—"

"Deserves." Quentin pulled the second stool over and sat across from him, close enough that she could see the salt dried white in his eyebrows and the bruise coming up along his jaw like a bad sunset. "Let me tell you something about deserving, boy, because I've had three years to study the subject and I've become something of an expert."

She took a breath. The wind filled the silence behind her, obligingly.

"For three years I told myself I deserved the light. Deserved the solitude, deserved the ledgers, deserved the cold porridge and the cold bed and the cold company of my own grief, because grief was what I was owed and I meant to collect every penny of it. And do you know what grief paid? Nothing. It's a currency nobody honors. I was a rich woman in a country that doesn't exist."

"Quentin," Emeric said softly.

"Let me finish. I'm enjoying myself." She wasn't, quite, but she was doing something adjacent to enjoyment, something with the same sharp edges. "Then you washed up on my rocks clutching a packet of lies, and I hated you for it, and I fed you anyway. You mended my nets badly and taught me a card game and made me laugh twice against my will, and somewhere in there I stopped being a woman keeping a grave and started being a woman keeping a house. That's yours. Whatever else you came here to do—you did that. You found me, Jude Okonkwo. You and your brother between you, by way of forgery and shipwreck and the worst weather in living memory. Family arrives however it likes. Mine arrived half-drowned and lying about its name."

Jude's eyes were wet. He blinked hard and looked at the rafters like a man hoping they'd offer structural support to his dignity.

"And the light needs a keeper," she went on, briskly now, before the soft thing in her chest could get any ideas. "That's the plain truth under all the poetry. If I go, the light goes dark, and everything we did last night—everything *you* did, bleeding on my gallery rail—we did it so ships would have something to steer by. I am the only soul on this rock who can turn the crank and trim the wick and log the weather, and I will not trade a living family for a dead duty, but neither will I abandon the duty while the family sails away safe. Both things are true. I've made room for both. It took me three years and one forged letter to learn how, but I'm a quick study when sufficiently provoked."

Emeric crouched down beside her stool. Up close he smelled of seawater and lamp oil and three years of a life she hadn't been invited to, and his face was thinner than she remembered and older, and his eyes were the same, which seemed unbearably unfair.

"Say it plainly," he said. "Whatever judgment you're carrying. Say it so I know what I'm staying for."

"Staying?" She raised an eyebrow. "You haven't asked to stay."

"Have I earned the asking?"

"No," she said. "Not yet. Possibly not ever. But you may earn it, and I may permit it, and in the meantime there is work." She nodded upward, toward the tower, toward the lamp turning its slow circles above them even now, steady as a heartbeat in the dark. "The light stays lit. That's not forgiveness—that's the job. Forgiveness is a different ledger, and I haven't opened it. I may. I may not. You'll be here either way, hauling oil and saying nothing that can't be unsaid, until I decide whether the man who climbed my stairs at dawn is a husband or a ghost wearing his coat."

"That's fair," Emeric said, and his voice cracked on the second word, which was more honesty than he'd managed in three years, and Quentin chose—deliberately, with authorial intent—not to soften.

"Of course it's fair. I wrote it."

* * *

They got Jude into the longboat the way one gets a reluctant cat into a basket: with patience, mild deception, and eventually brute force applied gently.

The surf had dropped enough to launch from the shingle below the boathouse, and two of the Meridian's men waded out to help, shouting cheerful nonsense over the wind. Jude stood at the waterline with the packet clutched to his chest—the same way he'd held it half-drowned on her rocks, Quentin noted; some habits were load-bearing—and looked back at the two of them standing together on the stones.

"Write," she called. "A proper address this time. No more anonymous strangers washing onto my beach—I've had my fill."

"Who says you get to assign my correspondence?" Jude shouted back, grinning through whatever he was trying not to feel.

"Someone has to. You clearly lack supervision." Then, softer, stepping closer so he could hear her without the wind chewing the words: "You chose honesty when it would have been easier not to. Twice. Most men don't manage it once. Take care of yourself, brother-in-law."

He made a noise that was mostly a laugh and mostly not, grabbed her hand, and pressed it briefly against the oilskin packet over his heart, as if filing her there among the evidence. Then he let the sailors push him into the boat and sat down stiffly, holding his stitched side, looking smaller in the longboat than he ever had in her kitchen, and larger too, somehow—larger the way a man looks when he is finally pointed in the direction he means to go.

The oars bit. The boat swung out through the surf line. Quentin watched it rise and fall against the gray water, growing smaller, and beside her Emeric stood absolutely still, watching his brother leave for the second time in his life, and she reached over without looking and took his hand, because some gestures didn't require deciding whether the man was a husband or a ghost. Some gestures were just true.

"He'll make it," Emeric said.

"He'd better. I've invested significantly in him."

The boat cleared the point. They watched until it reached the Meridian, until a small figure was hauled up the side, until the ship's lights shifted and her anchor chain began to come home rattling like a struck chord. Then Quentin turned back to the tower.

"Come along," she said. "Storm's not done with us. Neither is the light."

* * *

The climb back up was slow. Emeric paused on the third landing, breathing hard, and Quentin realized with a small cold shock that she didn't know anymore what his body could endure—three years had rewritten him in ways she hadn't witnessed, and there would be no skipping the relearning.

"There's a kettle upstairs," she said. "And bread that's only slightly stale, and if you're very well behaved, honey."

"You're offering me breakfast?"

"I'm offering you bread. Breakfast implies a shared meal between people on speaking terms. We're on *working* terms. Work involves bread."

"That's the most reasonable terms anyone's offered me in years."

At the top, the lamproom was warm and bright and smelled of hot brass and burning oil, and the great lens turned its patient circles, throwing its blade of light out over the water where the Meridian was already dwindling southward, taking with her the letters, the testimony, the ring's whole rotten architecture headed for the light of day.

Quentin hung her oilskins on their peg. Emeric hung his beside hers, hesitating a moment—as if asking permission—before letting them touch.

She got out the logbook, opened it to the current day, and wrote, in her small square hand:

*Dawn. Wind moderating. Evacuation completed, one passenger, Gullwrack Point. Light maintained.*

Then she paused, pen hovering, and beneath it added:

*Staff: Q. Thornbury, keeper. E. Okonkwo, assistant. Provisionally.*

Emeric read it upside down, and something in his face came apart and reassembled itself, quieter than before.

"Provisionally," he repeated.

"Everything worth having is provisional," Quentin said. "That's how you know it's alive." She handed him a rag and pointed at the lens housing, spattered with spray from the gallery door. "Clean that. Then put the kettle on. And Emeric—"

He turned.

"When Jude writes—and he will write, he's constitutionally incapable of not writing—we answer together. Whatever comes next, whatever the world does with those letters, we meet it from this tower. That's not a promise. It's a schedule."

He smiled—the old crooked smile, worn thin at the edges but unmistakable—and picked up the rag.

Outside, the sea had begun to gather itself again for the storm's final act, and the light turned above them, steady, unhurried, sweeping the dark like a hand smoothing a bed, like a sentence written over and over until it says exactly what the writer means.

Quentin sat down at her desk, opened the ledger, and began to write.
