## 21. The Longest Night

*Quentin*

The storm did not end when Emeric came up the stairs. That was the first mercy of it—there was no pause in which to speak, no dreadful drawing-room silence in which two people might be expected to say something. The wind simply went on howling at the glass as though nothing at all had happened, and the lamp went on needing her, and so there was nowhere for the moment to go but into work.

He stood dripping on the lantern-room floor, seawater pooling around his boots in a dark map of everywhere he'd been, and she looked at him for exactly as long as it took to notice he was shivering, and then she handed him a cloth.

"Oil," she said. "The reservoir's low and I've been feeding it by halves since midnight. You'll remember how."

It was not a question. It was also not forgiveness. It was a task.

Emeric took the cloth the way a man takes communion from a priest who has every reason to refuse him, and he knelt at the reservoir and began, with hands still shaking from the sea, to do the oldest work of his life.

She had imagined this, in the three years since the letter came—the one with the admiralty stamp and the word *lost* in it. She had imagined his return maybe forty ways, and in most of them she wept, or struck him, or said something magnificent that had clearly been rehearsed by her imagination for weeks in advance. In none of them did she hand him an oil rag and check the wick height while he worked.

But the barometer was falling through the bottom of its glass, and the Meridian was somewhere out in that black water carrying Jude toward the mainland, and the light could not go out, and grief—she discovered—keeps excellently. Grief would keep until spring if it had to. There would be time, later, to fall apart in a heated room like a civilized woman.

"Quentin." His voice was rust and salt. "I—"

"If you apologize now," she said, trimming the char from a wick with small precise scissors, "you will do it badly, and I will have to listen to it, and then neither of us will be any good at this. The wind turns at four. We keep the light till then."

He closed his mouth. He was always a quick study; it was one of the things she had married.

They worked.

* * *

*Emeric*

There was a rhythm to it, and his hands found it before his heart did. Trim, wind, wipe, log. Quentin called the intervals without looking at the clock—he had forgotten she could do that, tell time by the feel of the mechanism under her palm—and he moved to meet each call half a beat before she finished speaking, so that the lamp room filled with a wordless choreography, two people passing in narrow space like dancers who had rehearsed in different cities and somehow arrived at the same steps.

Once, reaching across her for the burnishing tool, his sleeve caught hers, and they both went very still, the way one does when touching a stove one thought was cold.

"The third lens is clouding," she said finally. "Western face."

"I see it."

"Salt film. Vinegar's on the second shelf, if your arms still work."

"My arms work."

"Hm," said Quentin, in the tone of a woman filing a claim for future verification.

He cleaned the lens and watched his own reflection ghosted over the flame, thinner than the man in her memory must remember, grayer at the temples, and behind the reflection the great lamp threw its beam out over water that wanted to kill everything on it, and he understood—with the simplicity of a man who has stopped arguing with God—that this was what he deserved: not her fury, which would at least be a kind of address, but her competence. Her turning away from him toward the work. Being needed for his hands and not yet for anything else.

And it was enough. It was more than he had any right to expect. He polished glass.

At some hour past two she said, without preamble, "You're thin."

"You're keeping the light well."

"Don't change the subject with flattery."

"It wasn't flattery. It was observation." He wound the clockwork, counting the clicks. "You were always thin when you were worried. You've gone past thin into something the ledger should record."

"My weight," said Quentin, "is none of your business."

"No," he agreed quietly. "Not anymore."

She didn't answer that, but ten minutes later she set bread and cold fish on the little table by the log book, cut in two portions, and pushed the larger one across to him without comment, and he ate it standing up because sitting down felt like presuming.

* * *

*Quentin*

The trouble, she thought somewhere in the small hours, was that her body remembered him. Her hands passed him tools before her mind consented. Her ear turned toward his footsteps on the iron stair the way a compass needle turns north, uselessly, faithfully, to something that had no claim on it.

Three years of entries about weather and wicks and oil consumption. Three years of writing *I* less and less until she had edited herself out of her own record entirely, so that a stranger reading the ledgers would believe Gullwrack Point was kept by no one—that the light simply happened, that diligence was its own keeper. And now here was the man responsible for all that careful erasure, hauling oil like he'd never left, humming—actually *humming*, the idiot, some half-audible fragment of a shanty under his breath, the same four notes over and over the way he used to in the old harbor cottage, until she realized she had been listening for the fifth note and not finding it and that this was somehow worse than anything he could have said aloud.

"You've lost a note," she said.

He stopped. "What?"

"Your song. The one you hum when you think no one can hear. There were five notes."

Something moved through his face like weather. "Maybe the sea kept it," he said.

"Give it back, then. You owe me at least one note."

And Emeric—hollow, drowned-looking Emeric, three years dead and returned with his sins still wet—laughed. Just once, just a breath of a laugh, startled out of him, and the sound went through her like the beam goes through fog: illuminating everything whether she wanted it lit or not.

She turned back to the log and wrote, in a hand that only shook a little:

*Wind veering NNE. Sea mountainous. Lamp trimmed 2:40.*

Then, beneath, before she could stop herself, smaller:

*Two keepers tonight.*

* * *

*Emeric*

At three the storm tried the door.

It came as a sound first—a concussion through the whole tower like the sea slapping the rock with a flat of its whole hand—and then the door at the gallery level groaned in its frame, and they were both moving without discussion, he to the braces and she to the shutters, and for twenty minutes the work was pure animal necessity: hauling, wedging, bracing, water coming through a seam somewhere below and their feet splashing through it in tandem. At one point the tower leaned—or seemed to lean, the whole world tilting with the gusts—and Quentin grabbed his forearm to steady herself, and her fingers closed exactly where the scar was, the old rope-burn from their courting days, the one she used to trace absently while reading.

She looked down at her own hand as though it had committed a betrayal. She let go. But slowly.

"Below," she shouted over the wind. "The counterweights. If the clockwork fouls we'll need to crank by hand."

They cranked by hand. Side by side at the winch, taking the weight of the lens assembly in turns, and it was heavy work, honest work, the sort of work that leaves no room in a man for eloquence, and he was grateful for it. Every revolution of the winch was a sentence he couldn't say. He counted them instead. Four hundred revolutions between windings, and he gave each one the name of a thing he owed her: the year he spent afraid. The letters he never wrote. The grave he let her mourn at. The note of the song.

When the weights were seated and the mechanism ran true again, they stood a moment in the lamplight below, both of them heaving for breath, salt water and sweat indistinguishable, and Quentin pushed her hair out of her face with the back of her wrist and looked at him—really looked, the first full look she had given him since the cloth.

"If you die again," she said, "I will not attend the funeral. I will not observe a period of mourning. I will enter it in the ledger—*husband lost, presumed, again*—and I will go on trimming wicks. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," he said.

"Because I am very good at going on. You made me very good at it. It's the one gift you left."

"Quentin—"

"And I hate it," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle on the last word, one clean break like ice giving way, and then she pressed her lips together and picked up the log book and wrote the 3:40 entry with a perfectly steady hand, and Emeric stood in the flickering light and let her not-cry in peace, because it was the only kindness left that she would accept from him.

* * *

*Quentin*

Toward dawn the storm began spending itself the way storms do—not dying but dispersing, the vast fury breaking into squalls, the sea between the whitecaps going from black to merely gray-black. The barometer lifted a hair's breadth. Quentin felt the change in her teeth before she saw it in the glass.

Four o'clock. Wind turn. They had kept the light.

She sat on the keeper's stool, which was hers, having belonged to the station longer than either marriage or death, and Emeric sat on the oil crate, which was where he deserved to sit, and for a long while neither spoke. The lamp turned overhead, steady, indifferent, throwing its slow wheel of light across water that was calming by degrees, as if being talked down.

"He forgave me," Emeric said at last. "Jude. On the rail, before the boat came. He said—" and here his voice failed and restarted— "he said he'd carried those forged letters like stones, three years, waiting to learn whether I was worth the weight. And then he put them in my hand and said, *Bring them back to her yourself, or don't come.*"

"That sounds like Jude."

"You know him three days and you know him."

"Some people are legible," said Quentin. "Unlike their handwriting."

That was as close as they came, that night, to the matter of the letters. She saw him understand it—the flinch, the acknowledgment—and she saw him decide, wisely, to let it stand. There would be other nights. There would have to be. Forgiveness, she suspected, was not a door you walked through once but a corridor you walked down daily, and some mornings you would refuse, and some mornings the corridor would be short, and you took it as it came.

The eastern window went from charcoal to slate to a color that could not quite commit to being blue. Below them the rocks emerged from the retreating sea, glistening, littered with the storm's debris—spars, weed, the carcass of somebody's crate—and above them the gulls resumed ownership of the air, screaming their ancient complaints.

"We should log the relief ship's passage," Quentin said, "if she shows."

"She'll show. Jude's aboard." Emeric turned his wedding ring—her ring, the one she'd stopped wearing at year two, the one he still wore—around his finger once, twice, an old habit she had pretended for ten years not to find maddening and now found she had missed. "Quentin. When the storm's fully gone. When there's nothing left to keep the light against. Will you still want me at this table?"

She considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, and with the honesty that three years of ledger-keeping had beaten into her: write only what is true.

"No," she said. "Not want. Want is for weddings and catalogues." She rose, took up the fresh page, dated it in her careful hand. "But the light needs two keepers until the harbor opens, and the harbor won't open for weeks, and I find I don't intend to argue with arithmetic. Sit down, Emeric. There's tea, and porridge, and approximately nine hundred things you will explain to me, in order of how angry each one makes me. We'll start with the smallest."

He sat. Outside, the sun came up over a sea smooth as poured pewter, and far out on the horizon—a fleck, a smudge, growing—the shape of a ship making for harbor.

Quentin reached for the kettle, and paused, and added one line more to the open ledger, small and plain beneath the weather entries:

*Storm passed. Both keepers accounted for.*
