## 22. New Entries

Spring came to Gullwrack the way forgiveness did—slowly, and then all at once.

One morning in late April Quentin opened the tower door and found the world had changed its mind about her overnight. The thrift was blooming along the cliff path, pink and stubborn as ever; the gulls had resumed their ancient quarrels over nesting rights on the north ledges; and the sea, which for three winters had been a gray wall of grievance, lay glittering and blue as a child's drawing of the sea. She stood on the gallery with her tea going cold in her hand and felt something in her chest unclench by degrees, like a fist remembering it could also be a hand.

Behind her, inside the tower, Emeric was burning the porridge.

He burned the porridge every morning. He had been an adequate cook once, or perhaps she had romanticized it, or perhaps grief had seasoned everything he'd made with a flavor it no longer possessed now that he was alive and eating her out of house and larder. Either way, the smoke alarm of the age—the smell, and then Quentin appearing at the kitchen door with an expression she had learned from the weather—did nothing to improve him.

"It's blackened," he called through the doorway. "Blackened is a technique. They do it on purpose in the south of France."

"They do it *on purpose*," Quentin agreed, "with fish. And skill."

"You married me for my penmanship, not my cooking."

"I married you," she said, "under a serious misapprehension regarding your death, which turned out to be the best thing about you for three years running."

He laughed at that—actually laughed, the real one, the one that crumpled him sideways against the stove—and Quentin turned back to the rail before he could see what his laughter did to her face. Three months of this. Three months of the dead man puttering about her tower, mending things she hadn't known were broken, learning where she kept everything by watching her the way he used to before he'd learned to lie. It was not the marriage they'd had. It might, given time and honest labor, become a better one. She was not yet prepared to say so out loud. She wrote it, instead, in places he would never look—in the margins of the light journal, between the lines of the tide tables, in the small dry voice of her ledgers where she had once stopped recording herself altogether and had lately, cautiously, begun again.

*Keeper slept poorly,* said last night's entry. *Wind westerly, freshening toward evening. The keeper's husband made soup and talked too much while doing it. The keeper did not mind.*

That was the whole of it. That was enormous.

* * *

The letter came on a Thursday, carried up from the harbor by young Tam Broadbent, who now ran errands for the whole coast on a bicycle he loved more than most people love their children.

"Post for Keeper Okonkwo," Tam announced at the door, breathless from the hill. "Foreign post! Well—" he squinted at the stamp— "mainland, anyway. Proper postmark and all." He lowered his voice to the register reserved for gossip of national importance. "My mum says you've got family you didn't used to have."

"Your mum," said Quentin, "is correct, which will annoy her."

She waited until Tam had rattled back down the cliff path before she opened it, sitting on the doorstep in the sun with the envelope on her knee a moment first, because some letters deserve to be anticipated. She knew the hand before she read a word. She had spent a winter learning that handwriting could lie and still be true; this hand, though—it had settled. The letters stood up straighter.

*Dear Quentin,* it began, and then:

*I have started this letter eleven times. Jude Okonkwo is apparently a man who can forge his brother's soul with a pen and cannot write his own name to a friend without sweating on the paper. Forgive the blot. Forgive all blots henceforth.*

*I am writing from Harwich, where I have been given a room above a chandlery and a job testifying, which sounds strange until you know that the ring came apart in February like wet rope. Your letters—the ones you gave me—are with the Crown prosecutors now. Half the men are in custody and the other half are scattering so fast they're tripping over each other. The magistrate asked me how I came to hold evidence of this quality, and I told him a lighthouse keeper gave them to me, and he looked at me the way people look at lighthouse keepers, which is to say: as though we are slightly mythical and probably cold.*

*I am cold. Harwich in March is not Gullwrack, but it has ambitions.*

*I want to tell you what I have not managed to say properly, which is this: you pulled a liar out of the surf and fed him, taught him knots, lost at cards to him forty times when you could have won, and threw him into a gale rather than let him lie to you one day longer—and then you put your own berth in his hands anyway. I have thought about that last part every day since. You looked at everything I'd done wrong and chose to weigh it against the one night I told the truth, and the truth won. No one had ever done that arithmetic in my favor before. I intend to spend some years being worth it.*

*I am calling you my family. I know you hate fuss, so I am doing it in writing, at a safe distance, where you cannot throw anything at me. You are my family. Emeric is my brother, which is a fact; you are my family, which is a choice, and choices count for more—I have this on excellent authority from a woman who chose hers twice, once by pulling it from the sea.*

*Give my love to the light. Tell Emeric the porridge story when he next burns something; I can hear him doing it from here.*

*Your devoted,
Jude*

P.S. *There is talk of me taking over a chandlery of my own. If there is a wedding someday—and Miriam the sailmaker's daughter has opinions on the matter—you will both come, and you will not bring weather.*

Quentin read it twice, folded it along its existing creases, and sat for a long time looking at the harbor, which was open now, the ice gone, boats moving again, the whole coast resuming the business of going somewhere. Her eyes were wet, which she noted clinically, as a keeper notes fog. Then she went inside to find Emeric, and read it to him aloud, and had to stop halfway through the P.S. because he was laughing so hard at the porridge line that the words became unusable.

"He's right," Emeric gasped. "I burn it every time. Every single time. You've never once mentioned it."

"I was keeping it," said Quentin, "for an occasion."

* * *

The old ledger was full. It had been full since March, and she had been making do with loose pages tucked in the back, which offended her sense of order in a way she recognized, these days, as healthy. A person who cared whether her records were tidy was a person who believed there would be records, and a future in which someone might read them. For three years she had not believed either.

On a bright morning in May, with Emeric down at the harbor bargaining ferociously for a boat he did not need and could not afford, Quentin took the packet of new ledgers from the shelf in the storeroom—the spares the Board sent every autumn, whether wanted or no—and brought the freshest one upstairs to the lamp room, where the light was.

It seemed the right place to begin it.

She cleared the little table beneath the lens. She laid out her pen, her ink, her ruler for the column rules, all the small ceremonial instruments of a life conducted honestly. Through the glass the sea ran silver and green; a fishing fleet worked the far water, tiny and patient; the thrift on the cliffs had gone from pink to a low white foam of bloom. The lamp above her head sat dark in its housing, off duty until dusk, brass-bright, waiting. She had polished it that very morning. Some things you maintained whether or not anyone thanked you, and eventually you understood that the maintaining was the thank-you—you gave it to yourself, to the work, to whatever idea of faithfulness had gotten you out of bed in ten thousand storms.

Quentin opened the ledger to its first page.

For a while she only looked at it. Blank paper had frightened her once; blank paper was where Emeric's absence had lived, page after page where her own name should have been and wasn't, because writing *the keeper* in her own records had felt like admitting there was no one left to read them. She had been wrong about that. She had been wrong about nearly everything worth being wrong about: about the letters, about the stranger in her kitchen, about the man she'd mourned, about the difference between keeping a light and keeping a life. The mistakes had been enormous and the corrections had been too. She thought of Jude's line—*choices count for more*—and dipped her pen.

At the top of the first page, in the space she had left empty for three years in every book, she wrote:

*Gullwrack Point Light — Ledger of the Keeper.*
*Commenced the sixth of May.*
*Quentin Okonkwo, Keeper.*

Then, beside her name, unhurried, in the second line where no ledger had ever asked for a second name but no rule forbade one, she wrote:

*Emeric Okonkwo.*

She sat back and regarded the two names side by side. His hand had always been beautiful and had always lied; hers had always been plain and had mostly told the truth; there on the page they looked, for the first time, like a matched set. Below them she ruled her columns and entered the day: wind southwest, moderate; visibility good; lamp trimmed and ready; oil received from the *Mermaid*, four drums, no leaks. Ordinary entries. Glorious ordinary entries.

At the bottom of the page, after a moment's thought, she added one more line—not weather, not stores, just a record, because a ledger keeps what you trust it with:

*Family: two, accounted for. One ashore at Harwich, expected home at midsummer. One presently haggling at the harbor, expected home by supper.*

*All lights burning.*

She blotted it carefully, closed the book, and set it beneath the lamp where the evening would find it. Down the spiral stairs she could hear the tower door bang and Emeric's voice rising hopefully through the floors, announcing that he had bought a boat, that it was a bargain, that she was going to love it, that she should come and see it before she formed any opinions.

Quentin looked out one last time at the water—all that blue beginning, all those ships bound somewhere—and allowed herself a smile she would deny under oath.

"Coming," she called down. "And I'm already forming opinions."

But she was still smiling when she said it, and the light behind her stood ready for the dark, and the new ledger lay open to its first full page, waiting patiently—as ledgers do—for everything worth keeping.
