## 2. Jude Counts the Doors

There were two hundred and seventeen doors in Aldenmoor, and Jude could have named what stood behind every one of them.

Not the furniture — though that too, in a way, since furniture was collateral and collateral lived in ledgers — but the truth of each household: who owed whom, who wept at which hour of the night, whose chimney smoked thick in a month when it shouldn't, whose bread had gone dark with rye flour stretched beyond decency. A census was supposed to be a counting of heads. Jude had learned long ago that it was really a counting of debts, and that if you did it carefully enough, with a soft pencil and a softer heart, you could write down only what needed writing and leave the rest to memory, where it belonged.

The morning of the count began like all mornings in Aldenmoor began in the cold months: with frost on the inside of the window glass, ferned across the pane like something growing, and the smell of last night's fire gone sour in the grate. Jude rose before light, as clerks must, dressed by touch, and let themselves down the stairs of the customs house annex where the town kept its records — such as they were — past shelves of registers bound in cracked leather, past the great iron-bound chest that held the deeds, out into a street still blue with dark.

The town lay under the kind of silence that was not empty. Smoke stood up from forty chimneys straight as pillars in the windless air. Somewhere a dog was speaking to something it alone could see. And far off — so far it might have been imagined, might have been the sea heard through the bones of the earth rather than the ears — a sound like a door closing in another house.

Jude stopped on the customs house steps and listened.

Nothing. The sound did not come again, or if it did, it had folded itself into the ordinary sounds of a town waking: the pump handle's groan, a cart, the smith already at work with his small patient ringing. Jude stood a moment longer than was sensible, breath steaming, and then went in to fetch the ledger, the pencil, and the long day.

* * *

The census was not due. That was the first strange thing about it, and Jude noticed strange things the way other people noticed weather — automatically, without deciding to. The capital wanted a new enumeration every seven years, and the last had been taken only four years past, when Jude had walked these same streets with the same worn pencil and written down names that had not changed and would not change until they changed forever, which is a different thing from changing at all.

But the requisition had come in the autumn post, bearing new seals, seals Jude did not recognize, with instructions in an officious hand: *complete household inventory, all persons, all holdings, all obligations.* All obligations. Someone far away wanted to know not merely how many souls lived in Aldenmoor but what those souls owed, and to whom, and therefore — though the document did not say so, documents never say the true thing plainly — what could be taken from them.

So Jude counted doors.

The first door of the morning was the Widow Harsch's, at the crooked end of Weaver's Row, where the houses leaned together like old conspirators. She answered the knock in her shawl with her chin up, because the widow Harsch's chin had been up through a husband's bankruptcy, a son's disgrace, and a fire that took her bake oven eleven years ago, and it would be up when she died.

"You've grown," she said, which was what everyone said, and untrue for years now, and Jude wrote it down anyway, in the margin of the mind if not the ledger: *she has decided I am still young; let her have it.*

"New census, Mistress Harsch. Capital's orders."

"The capital." She said the word the way you'd name a distant relative who had once done the family harm. "And what does the capital want to know about my door?"

"Who sleeps behind it."

"Me," she said, "and my rheumatism, and God's opinion of us both." Then, because she was fair beneath her armor: "Come in out of the cold a minute. There's chicory."

There was always chicory at the Widow Harsch's, real coffee having left Aldenmoor's price range sometime before the war before this war, whatever the histories ended up calling the last one. Jude sat in her kitchen, which smelled of ash and dried mint, and asked the questions the form demanded — persons resident, ages, trades, livestock, acreage, debts — and wrote the answers in a hand that grew smaller and more careful as the list went on, because the widow's list was short and every entry was a wound dressed up as a statistic.

One person resident. No livestock. Trade: none, formerly baking. Debts —

"I owe nothing," said the widow, "except what can't be entered in a book."

Jude knew better. The widow owed four months' rent to Brenner the landlord, who was himself three payments behind on his own mortgage to a lender in Marstadt, and the whole sagging row of houses she lived among was mortgaged twice over to men who had never seen it and could not have found it on any map worth owning. But the form did not demand everything. Only what it demanded. Jude wrote: *debts: none recorded,* and felt the small warm treason of mercy by omission, which was the only kind available to a clerk, and which Jude practiced daily, the way other men practiced drinking or prayer.

* * *

By midmorning the fog had burned off and the town had its color back — brown and grey and the dull iron of the river, with here and there a shutter of faded blue that some stubborn soul repainted every spring against all evidence of fashion. Jude worked the east streets: Tannery Lane, where the smell announced the neighborhood before the turn did; the Mill Road, where the water turned the wheel with a sound like slow applause; Cordwainer Street, where the shoemakers' windows were full of lasts shaped like feet that would never be filled, because no one in Aldenmoor had bought new shoes in two years except to be buried in them.

This was the part of the work strangers never understood, the ones who thought a clerk pushed paper the way a miller pushes grain, mechanically, without tasting it. But a clerk in a small town tasted everything. Every door opened onto a story Jude had been keeping for years, the way the church kept relics — not displayed, not used, but known to be there.

Behind the third door of Cordwainer Street: the Pell family, six souls, and the eldest boy missing two fingers to the carding machine, and Mrs. Pell's sister visiting from over the hills for going on eight months now, sleeping in the loft, and everyone pretending not to know why she had really come, and Jude pretending not to know that everyone pretended. Behind the fifth: old Anders the cooper, sober nine years, whose ledger debt to the tavern keeper had been quietly forgiven and quietly recorded as paid, both men agreeing by silence that some accounts were better closed than settled. Behind the ninth: a locked door and a garden gone to seed and neighbors who lowered their voices passing it, because the man who had lived there had been taken by the gendarmes in the spring — not arrested, nothing so formal, just *taken*, the way weather takes a roof — and no one knew if the house should be counted occupied or empty, and neither did Jude, and the form had no column for it.

Jude stood a long time at that ninth door with the pencil poised above the paper, and finally wrote *one person resident, status uncertain,* which was the truest sentence in the whole census, and probably the most useless, and moved on.

That was the clerk's whole art, in the end. Moving on. Writing the world down small enough to carry and then carrying it, door after door, year after year, until the ledger weighed more than the grief and the grief got used to its new weight and stopped complaining.

* * *

It was past noon when Jude came back through the market square to the customs house to sharpen the pencil and eat the bread wrapped in a cloth in the coat pocket, and it was there — crossing the square with the sun high and thin and the fountain trickling its winter trickle — that Jude saw the courier properly for the first time.

Everyone in Aldenmoor knew of Beatrix Thornbury by now, in the way a town knows any new thing: quickly, incompletely, and with additions of its own. The tariff clerk from the capital, lodged in the room above the customs office these three weeks past. Quiet. Careful. Kept to the ledgers. The baker reported that Thornbury bought the same loaf every second day and never haggled, which in Aldenmoor was considered either madness or wealth, and opinion divided accordingly. The boy who swept the customs office said the clerk's desk was the tidiest he had ever swept around — not a loose sheet, not an inkstain out of place — and that the courier sometimes worked by candle long after the stove went cold, sitting perfectly still over the papers in a way that made the boy uneasy, as if the stillness were a kind of listening.

Jude had exchanged perhaps forty words with Thornbury in three weeks. *Good morning. The stamp ink is low. Yes, it always is.* The words of two people whose work touched at the edges, no more.

But today, crossing the square, Jude watched Beatrix Thornbury stop at the fountain — not to drink, only standing there, gloved hands folded, looking at the trickle of water with the expression of someone reading a letter — and Jude noticed the way the courier stood. Squarely. Both feet planted, weight even, head level, as though standing at attention were simply how the body rested. And the eyes: they moved the way a clerk's pen moved, quick precise passes, left to right — the square, the church steps, the mouth of the Mill Road, the sky for weather, back again. Not nervousness. Method. It was the walk of a person who had been taught to see a place the way a surveyor sees it, for exits and approaches, and had been taught it so long ago the lesson had become posture.

People do not learn that in tariff offices, Jude thought.

And then, because Thornbury had gone into the customs house ahead and left the outer door ajar, Jude — arriving minutes later, legitimately, keys in pocket, clerk's duty in hand — found the inner office door also ajar, and through the gap, the sight that rearranged the whole gray morning:

Beatrix Thornbury writing.

The courier sat at the desk with the afternoon light slanting across the paper, and was copying something — no, correcting something, a dispatch laid open beside the fair copy, and the hand moving across the page was the most careful hand Jude had ever seen on a living person. Not beautiful. Beautiful handwriting shows off; it wants to be looked at. This hand hid. Each letter formed exactly like its fellows, each line ruled invisible-straight without a rule, the whole page so uniform it looked printed, machined — the handwriting of someone who had been trained until personality bled out of the pen. Jude had seen that hand once before, years ago, on a diplomatic pass carried through the district by a government rider, and had admired it and thought nothing more of it, because there had been nothing more to think.

Now there was.

Because tariff reports did not need disguising. Grain weights and duty schedules and the price of hides in the provincial markets could be written in any hand at all, ugly or lovely, drunk or hurried — nobody forging tariffs, nobody burning them, nobody training clerks to write like machines. You wrote like a machine when the paper mattered. When it could hang someone. When it had to survive being read by enemies, compared against other pages, authenticated by ministers. The careful hand was not care. It was armor, and armor told you where the body expected to be struck.

Jude stood in the corridor exactly four seconds longer than innocence required — long enough to see Thornbury pause, dip the pen, blot the line with sand, and glance toward the door — and then knocked, and entered, and became entirely the town clerk.

"The Marstadt returns," Jude said. "The collector wants them copied into the quarterly book before the week's out. If your desk is occupied I can—"

"No." Thornbury covered the dispatch with a blotter in one smooth motion, unhurried, the gesture of a person who had rehearsed covering papers until covering papers was reflex. "Please. I'm finished here." A pause, and then, with a politeness so exact it circled around to being strange: "You're taking the census."

"Under orders. New seals from the capital. They want everything counted — persons, property, obligations." Jude laid the ledger on the counter between them, deliberately open to the morning's pages, deliberately casual. "You'll be in it, of course. Resident of the parish, occupation... what should I put? The form dislikes blank spaces."

The look Thornbury gave the question lasted half a heartbeat too long, and in that half-heartbeat something crossed the courier's face like a fish crossing under ice — there and gone, visible only by the shadow of it. Caution. Assessment. The arithmetic of how much truth a stranger could safely be sold.

"Civil service," Thornbury said at last. "Put civil service. It covers a great deal."

"It does," said Jude, and wrote it, in the careful small hand that hid nothing because Aldenmoor had never needed it to hide anything, and felt — distinctly, coldly, the way you feel the first drop of rain when you're halfway across a field — that the sentence cut both ways, and that the woman or man or whoever Beatrix Thornbury truly was had just told Jude a lie so polite and so practiced that it deserved, almost, to be respected.

* * *

The rest of the day's counting went on. Doors opened; doors closed. Jude wrote down souls and livestock and debts, mercies and omissions distributed according to an internal law no assessor would ever ratify. At the tanner's, at the miller's, at the two houses on Chapel Lane where the families shared a well and a feud in equal measures. The pencil wore down; the light went long and coppery; the fog came creeping back up the river valley like something returning to a den it liked.

But the census had developed a second ledger beneath the first, the invisible one, the one Jude kept in the place where the widow's chin-up defiance and the locked house on Cordwainer Street were stored. In it, entries accumulated all afternoon:

*Thornbury. Arrived six weeks ago on transfer papers signed by an office that does not sign transfers in autumn. Works late. Blots every line. Stands like a soldier at ease. Writes like a ministry. Bought the same loaf every second day and never haggled — not madness, not wealth: discipline. Asked the sweeping boy what roads led south out of the valley. Asked him casually. Nothing is casual in that voice.*

And underneath, in the space where conclusions went, Jude wrote nothing at all, because there was nothing yet to write. Only a question, taking shape slowly, taking warmth, taking weight: *What does the capital want with a complete inventory of a town this small, and why now, and what arrives in this valley that needs two hundred and seventeen doors counted before it gets here?*

Jude's mother had a saying, back when she was alive and running the marsh paths with other people's goods strapped under her skirts: *When the rich start asking poor towns questions, it isn't curiosity. Curiosity comes to visit; appetite moves in.* Jude had been raised on the tail end of that life — raised *out* of it, expensively, by the family debt, traded into the clerk's office the way other children were traded into apprenticeships, and had spent twenty years learning that paper was safer than smuggling. Paper stayed put. Paper obeyed. Paper could be filed.

But paper, Jude remembered suddenly, standing at the last door of the day in the failing light while somewhere behind the hills the artillery-ghost of a sound came once more and was denied — paper was also what her mother had carried, in the end. Bills of lading. Forged passes. The little documents that decided whether a cart at a checkpoint went forward or the people with it went to prison. Her mother had died old and warm and uncaught, which proved nothing except that luck ran out slower than sense.

The last door of the day was the customs house itself. Jude climbed the steps in the blue dusk, stamped the snow off, and hung the census ledger on its shelf beside the others — the birth rolls, the deed chest, the tax books going back ninety years, the whole paper skeleton of Aldenmoor, every name the town had ever been.

Through the inner office door, lamplight. The scratch of a pen. That machine-even hand, working late again, over papers Jude could not see and could not stop thinking about.

Two hundred and seventeen doors, and Jude had counted them all, and knew what waited behind every one.

Except the one directly ahead, glowing yellow at the seams, where the careful hand was writing something it had already corrected once, and the sand for blotting had been set out ready, and the room beyond smelled faintly — Jude noticed it as the door swung open, politely, in greeting — of sealing wax.

Not tariff wax. Diplomatic wax, black and bitter-smelling, the kind Jude had handled exactly once in a career of handling everything, on the single occasion a ministerial pouch had passed through Aldenmoor, eleven years ago, addressed to no one, held by no one, gone by morning.

The lamp inside was very bright. Beatrix Thornbury looked up from the page, and did not cover it fast enough.
