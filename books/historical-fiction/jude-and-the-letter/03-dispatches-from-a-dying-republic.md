## 3. Dispatches from a Dying Republic

The pouch arrived on a Tuesday, though Beatrix would remember it afterward as arriving on no day at all — arriving outside of time, the way catastrophes do, in the gray hour between the lamplighter's rounds and the baker's, when Aldenmoor still belonged to itself and to no one else.

They heard the horse before they saw it. That was unusual enough to bring them out of the customs house with a pen still behind their ear, ink drying on half a tariff reconciliation. Horses were rare here now; the war had eaten them the way it ate everything else — quietly at first, then all at once. The hoofbeats came down the mill road hard and uneven, a lamed animal being driven past its endurance, and Beatrix stood on the frozen step with their breath clouding and watched a rider resolve out of the fog that came off the river this time of year like breath from a dying mouth.

The courier wore the double-breasted coat of the Foreign Service, road-scorched and mud-hemmed, and under the coat's shadow at his chest he carried the pouch — the black oilskin with the twin seals, wax the color of dried blood, pressed with the signet of the Ministry that Beatrix had only ever seen on training documents in the capital, years ago, when they were young and the republic seemed like a thing that would outlast everyone's disappointment in it.

"Thornbury." The courier did not dismount so much as fall partway off, catching the post and hanging there. His face was the color of tallow. "Beatrix Thornbury, customs attaché, second grade."

"I'm Thornbury."

He fumbled at his collar and produced the order sheet one-handed, because his other hand never left the pouch. It was strapped across his body the way mothers carry infants through crowds — too tight, too close, with the constant small adjustments of someone who cannot stop feeling for it.

"You'll hold this until relieved. Relief convoy comes within the week — ten days, outside chance of more. You don't open it. You don't move it. You don't acknowledge it exists to anyone without ministry credentials." He recited it flatly, a catechism, and then his eyes did something strange — they went around the empty square, the shuttered weigh office, the frost-killed plane tree, the whole unremarkable geography of Aldenmoor, and something in them flickered that might have been doubt or might have been pity. "You have a cellar?"

"I have a cellar."

"Deep cellar?"

"It has a cellar."

He looked at the building again as if appraising a fortress and finding it made of paper. Then he pulled the counterfoil from his saddlebag, pressed it into Beatrix's gloved hands, and waited while they signed — their own name, which they had written perhaps forty thousand times in their life, suddenly requiring concentration, the *x* looping wrong.

"There's a convoy," Beatrix said. "There's actually a convoy coming here? To Aldenmoor?"

"There's a convoy going along the north road. This is on the north road now." The way he said *now* was the whole dispatch, the whole winter, the whole collapsing architecture of the republic compressed into a single syllable. He hauled himself back into the saddle, and the horse staggered, and Beatrix saw that its flanks were ribboned with whip-marks already stiffening into scabs. "Keep your lamp lit at night. They look for lit lamps. Means someone's home, means someone's official."

"Who looks—"

But he was already moving, already gone down the street at that same punishing drive, and the fog closed behind him the way water closes over a thrown stone, and Beatrix stood on the step holding a signature slip and listening to the hoofbeats fade until they could no longer be distinguished from the sound of the river working at the ice.

* * *

The cellar was not deep. It had been a coal store once, then a root cellar by some previous tenant's ambition, and now it was mostly a place where damp climbed the walls in pale maps and the customs house's overflow records went to slowly warp in their crates. Beatrix had lived above it for three years — assigned quarters, second grade, single occupancy — and had never thought of it as a room at all, merely as the underside of one.

They chose the corner behind the shelving where the foundation stone jutted, and they wrapped the pouch in the oilcloth meant for preserving harness leather, and they buried it under the warped crates of 1889 grain reconciliations, which no power on earth had ever wanted to read. Then they went back up the stairs, lit their lamp, set it in the window as instructed, and sat down at their desk.

It took them an hour to understand what they were feeling.

It was not fear. Not yet. What it was — and they examined it the way they examined a ledger line that refused to balance, turning it over, testing each element — was *importance*. A hot, humming, shameful importance, like fever or brandy. For eleven years Beatrix Thornbury had processed the freight of a town nobody bothered to conquer: tariffs on hemp and tallow, duty schedules, the endless arithmetic of sacks and bales passing through a market whose significance was purely local. History happened elsewhere. History happened in the capital, in the ministries where decisions were printed and sealed and dispatched outward like weather, and people like Beatrix were the rain gauges left out in the provinces to measure what fell.

Now the weather had landed on their step. Now the seals of the Ministry itself were buried beneath eleven crates of grain reconciliations in their shallow cellar, and somewhere to the east a relief convoy was either rolling toward this forgotten crossing point or failing to, and the difference between those two futures passed through Beatrix's small cold hands.

They caught themselves smiling at the ledger and wiped the smile away as if they'd been caught at something worse.

Because underneath the hum of it — and this was the thing they would not look at directly, not that night, not yet — underneath was the courier's face when he asked about the cellar. The depth of it. Men do not ask how deep your cellar is unless they know what is coming down roads you cannot see.

* * *

The next two days, Beatrix was unbearable to themselves. They filed everything. They filed early. They reconciled the hemp accounts to the third decimal and re-inked headers that needed no re-inking, because work was the only container they had ever trusted to hold them, and the work would not hold. Their eyes kept sliding to the cellar door in the floor whenever anyone came into the office — old Marta come to dispute her flour tax, the sweep-boy Tomas with his brushes and his whistling, the two carter brothers who always filed together and always argued. Every voice sounded like a question about the pouch. Every glance felt like an audit.

On the third evening Jude appeared.

Beatrix knew the clerk by sight, the way one knows a fixed feature of the landscape — the town clerk, quiet, watchful, with ink permanently shadowing the side of his right hand and a way of standing in doorways as though he'd measured the doorway and found it adequate. He came in with the census folios under his arm, which meant another round of the counting that had been going on all autumn, household by household, and Beatrix braced for the usual tedium.

"You've moved your lamp," Jude said.

Beatrix looked up. "What?"

"The window lamp. Three years it's been in the east pane. Tuesday night it was in the west." Jude set the folios on the counter without hurry. "I count things. Habit of the trade."

"The wick smoked in the east window. Draft."

"Mm," said Jude, in a tone that accepted the explanation the way a ledger accepts a figure — recorded, not necessarily believed — and opened his folio and proceeded to conduct the census of the customs house, which consisted of Beatrix alone, and which he nonetheless performed with grave thoroughness: name, age, occupation, dependents. None. None. Attaché, second grade.

"And goods held in storage?" Jude asked, pen poised.

"Tariff items only."

"Naturally." He wrote it, blotted it, closed the book. At the door he paused, half-turned, and Beatrix watched him decide whether to say the thing he'd come in with besides the census. He decided against it. The door closed. The snow began again sometime after, fat slow flakes falling past the lamplight like the ash of somewhere else burning.

That night Beatrix did not sleep. They lay above the cellar with the sounds of the town in their ears — the ice groaning on the river, a dog, a cart — and every ordinary sound rearranged itself into the sound of boots. Around two in the morning they gave up, dressed, took the lamp down the stairs, and dug the pouch out just to confirm it existed, that the world had not dissolved overnight into an ordinary province again.

The oilskin was intact. The seals were intact. But as they lifted it, the corner of the wrapping caught on the jutting foundation stone — the same stone the courier's horse must have shied from in its own way — and tore.

A small tear. Two inches of oiled paper peeling back from the inner wrapping, exposing the document beneath: heavy cream stock, folded in thirds, official crease.

And through that torn corner, in the firm slanted hand of a ministry secretary trained to write for strangers, Beatrix read one word before they snatched the fold shut, heart slamming, as though the word could be un-read, as though ignorance could be folded back along its crease:

*names.*

One word. Plural. Not cargo, not tariff, not treaty. Names.

* * *

They reburied it. They smoothed the tear flat and turned the wrapping so the damage faced the stone, which was the kind of solution that fools children and auditors, and they climbed the stairs and sat at their desk in the dark with their hands flat on the wood as if steadying themselves on a ship's deck.

The thrill was gone. That was the remarkable thing, how completely gone — the humming importance burned off in two seconds of exposure, and what remained underneath was cold and structural and clear, the way a building feels after the fire has taken the furnishings. Names. Someone had written down names, many names, sealed them with the blood-colored wax of the republic, and sent them hurtling through the dark toward a town that had never mattered, entrusting them to a clerk of the second grade with no dependents and a shallow cellar. Which meant the names mattered more than any single bearer of them. Which meant the convoy carrying relief might also be carrying requisitions. Which meant the republic was sending its paper ahead of its protection, the way you throw a rope to a drowning man who is already too far out — not to save him, but so that afterward, someone can say a rope was thrown.

Beatrix thought of the courier's whip-marked horse. Of the lit lamp in the window, a signal meaning *someone official lives here*, which was, they understood now, also a target painted on their own glass.

And they thought — with the peculiar honesty that comes only at four in the morning, when the self has no defenses left — of the word *names*, and of whose names those might be. Not theirs. Surely not theirs. Beatrix Thornbury was nobody's name; it was a name designed by the orphanage system precisely to belong to no one, a name issued like a uniform. Whatever list the republic was writing, Beatrix would be on the safe side of it. They had always been on the safe side of every list. That was what loyalty bought, wasn't it? That was the whole transaction?

Outside, far to the east, so faint it might have been the river settling, might have been thunder under the horizon, might have been nothing at all, the air carried a low sound and returned it.

Beatrix sat in the dark and told themselves it was the ice.

Downstairs, under eleven crates of grain reconciliations from a year no one would ever audit, the letter lay with its one exposed word facing the stone, and in the morning light it would still be there, patient as all dangerous paper is patient — waiting, as Beatrix now understood everything eventually waited to do, to be read.
