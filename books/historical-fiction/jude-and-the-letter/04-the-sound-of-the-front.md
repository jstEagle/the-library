## 4. The Sound of the Front

The first time the guns spoke, Jude Mistral was counting spoons.

Not spoons, precisely — pewter inventory for the Harrow household, entered in a hand so small and even that Mrs. Harrow had once accused him of writing in a whisper. He sat at the long table in the customs house annex where the census work was stacked like winter firewood: household rolls, tithe records, the great ownership ledgers bound in green calfskin that smelled of tallow and dust and forty years of other clerks' breath. Outside, October was doing its slow arithmetic on the lindens along the canal, subtracting leaves one wind at a time.

The sound came up from the south like a door closing in a very large house.

*Thoom.* A pause long enough to doubt it. *Thoom.*

Jude set down his pen. The ink pooled at the nib's tip, a small black eye.

He had heard artillery before — twice as a boy, carried on summer air from maneuvers far to the east, a sound his mother had called *thunder with manners*. This was not that. This was closer, and lower, and it did not come in drills or demonstrations. It came the way weather comes, without asking anyone's permission, and it kept coming: three reports, then five, then a rolling series of them that made the windowpanes shiver in their putty and sent a fine gray sifting down from the rafters onto the open ledger pages.

Jude looked at the dust settling between the ruled lines and thought, absurdly, *I will have to clean these before I write anything else.*

That was the clerk's curse, he would think later. When the world broke, the first instinct was to keep the record straight.

* * *

By evening the news had arrived ahead of itself, the way news does in a town like Aldenmoor — walking, limping, riding on cart beds among bundles and chickens and children too tired to cry. The southern road, which for all of Jude's life had delivered nothing more dramatic than tax assessors and traveling dentists, began to produce people.

They came in ones and twos at first, then in knots of ten and twelve, families folded into wagons built for hay, a man pushing a wheelbarrow with a grandmother seated in it like a queen reviewing her ruined kingdom. They stopped at the well in the market square because everyone stopped at the well; water was the one language every road understood. And there they told their stories, each one different, each one certain.

The front had broken at Kessel Ford. No — the front held at Kessel Ford, but the line to the west had folded like wet paper. No — there was no front anymore, only columns moving both directions and no one sure which flag pointed which way. The army was retreating in good order. The army was dissolving into the countryside. The army had been paid off and disbanded by officers who rode north with the pay chests. The republic had signed an armistice. The republic had fallen. The republic — said a woman with flour still white in the creases of her knuckles, as though she'd fled mid-bread — the republic had never existed at all except on paper, and paper, she said, burns.

Jude stood at the edge of the crowd with his notebook in his pocket and wrote none of it down, because none of it could be verified, and a clerk who recorded rumor was worse than a clerk who recorded nothing. But he listened. Listening was also a kind of filing.

What struck him was not the contradictions. Contradictions were the natural state of human testimony; he had spent two years untangling land disputes in which both parties swore before God to the same fence line. What struck him was the direction. Every story, however it disagreed about what had happened, agreed about where the tellers were going: north. Away. Through Aldenmoor, past Aldenmoor, Aldenmoor as a place you passed through on the way to somewhere that still existed.

He walked home through streets gone strange with unfamiliar carts. The lamplighter was working his rounds as if the world were ordinary, and Jude found himself almost grateful to the man — old Pemm, climbing his ladder with his pole and his little flame, lighting lamps no fleeing family would stop to see. There was a stubbornness in it that Jude recognized and, that night, would be forced to name.

* * *

His brother was packing when Jude came in.

Tomas worked fast and loud, the way he did everything — dragging the good blankets from the chest, stacking tinned things and smoked things and a sack of meal, arguing with the room as much as with Jude. He was twenty-six to Jude's thirty-one, broader, louder, a man who fixed wheels and hooves and pumps for half the town and believed, with religious fervor, that any problem could be solved by applying enough motion to it.

"I've got the cart," Tomas said without turning around. "We load tonight, we're over the north hills by tomorrow noon, we're at our cousin's in Vell by dark."

"Our cousin in Vell has two rooms and six children."

"Then we sleep on the floor like everyone else in this country is about to." He straightened, holding a lantern, and in its light Jude saw that his brother was afraid. Not performing fear, not borrowing it from the refugees — carrying it, deep in the face, around the eyes. "You heard those guns today. You heard them."

"I heard them."

"That's not maneuvers. That's not some exercise. That's the war, Jude. It's coming up this road the same way those carts are, and when it gets here—" He stopped, jaw working. "When it gets here, nobody asks whether you're a clerk or a wheelwright. They take your cart, your horses, your coat, and if you're young enough, they take you."

"They're not conscripting yet—"

"Yet." Tomas laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "There's the whole republic in one word. *Yet.* Everything's fine until it isn't, and then it was never fine, and everyone pretends they knew."

Jude hung his coat on its peg. It was a small domestic gesture, deliberate, and he watched it infuriate his brother, which was perhaps why he did it slowly.

"The ledgers," Jude said.

"What?"

"You want me to leave. Fine. Answer me one question first. If we go — if everyone goes — who owns the mill? Who holds the mortgage on the Kessler farm? What does Widow Anselm actually owe, and what has she already paid, and who can prove it?" He turned to face Tomas fully. "Every deed, every debt, every title in this district is written down in the customs house. Green books, four shelves, forty years. You burn those books or abandon them, and it doesn't matter how far north we run. Some man with a new flag and a fresh stamp will decide that everything belongs to whoever he says it belongs to, and there will be nothing — *nothing* — to say otherwise."

"So stay and guard paper while the soldiers come to take it. Wonderful plan. Very brave. Very useless."

"It's not useless." Jude heard his own voice go quiet, which it always did when he meant something most. "It's the only thing that isn't. People can run. People rebuild. But if the record of who they *are* disappears — their land, their names, what they paid, what they're owed — then they come back from running and find they were never anyone at all. I've seen it happen to families in probate disputes. Now imagine it for a whole town."

Tomas stared at him. Then he sat down heavily on the packed trunk, as though the argument had knocked something loose in his legs.

"You sound like Mother," he said finally.

"No," Jude said. "Mother would have taken the ledgers with her. She'd have floated down the canal on a raft of them."

And that — unexpectedly, horribly — made Tomas laugh, a real laugh, brief and cracked, and for a moment they were boys again in the smokehouse listening to their mother count her routes on her fingers: bridge, mill gate, marsh ford, never the same way twice.

But the moment passed, as moments do, and Tomas stood and unpacked the trunk with angry, careful hands, putting back the blankets one at a time.

"I'll wait three days," he said. "Three days. If the guns get closer, or if they start taking men off the street, I go. With you or without you."

"Fair," Jude said.

"And you'll write down whatever fool thing happens to you, I suppose. So someone knows."

"Someone should know," Jude said. "That's the whole point of me."

* * *

He could not sleep, and so near midnight he took the lamp back to the annex, let himself in with his own key, and walked the shelves.

The green books stood in their ranks, patient as gravestones and twice as honest. He pulled the oldest volume — his grandfather's era, nearly — and opened it to a random page, and read: *Aldenmoor district, holdings assessed, spring of the year —* and below it, in faded iron-gall brown, the names. The same names that filled the town now. Harrow. Kessler. Anselm. Brant. Families whose fields abutted one another, whose debts crossed like fence lines, whose quarrels and marriages were all here, reduced to figures in a fair hand.

Paper burns. The flour-dusted woman had been right about that. But paper also *keeps*, and there was the whole tragedy and glory of it — the same property, the keeping and the burning, and no way to have one without risking the other.

Somewhere south, muffled now by distance and darkness, the guns went on talking to each other. *Thoom. Thoom.* Patient. Unhurried. They sounded less like battle than like bookkeeping, Jude thought — some vast account being settled, entry by entry, and Aldenmoor was a line item that hadn't been reached yet.

He closed the ledger, squared it on the shelf, and ran his thumb along the spines until he found the current volume — the one with this year's census interleaved, the household rolls, the lists. His own neat handwriting everywhere, every door in town counted, every name entered.

Including, on the courier's page, a name he had copied out just days ago with particular care, because the hand beside it had been so precise, so controlled, so very much like his own: *B. Thornbury, diplomatic service, lodging at the Widow Anselm's.*

A courier. In a town that had never mattered to anyone. Carrying dispatches sealed against exactly the kind of collapse that was now rolling up the southern road like a tide.

Jude stood in the lamplight with his thumb resting on that page, and outside, faint under the guns, he heard the wheels of another refugee cart passing north, and north, and north.

Three days, Tomas had said.

Jude blew out the lamp and thought: *I wonder how many days the paper has.*
