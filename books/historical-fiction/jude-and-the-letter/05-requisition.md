## 5. Requisition

Beatrix woke to a sound like rain falling upward.

They lay for a moment in the narrow bed above the chandler's shop, in the room they rented by the month, and tried to fit that sound into the town's ordinary vocabulary. Rain did not fall upward. Carts did not roll at this hour. Aldenmoor's mornings began with the mill wheel, with the clatter of shutters, with the boy who swept the customs house steps whistling something tuneless and cheerful. This was none of those. This was boots — many boots, in rhythm, on frozen ground — and beneath it the low complaint of wagon iron, and under that, faint as a pulse, the guns again, closer than yesterday.

They dressed without lighting the lamp. Their hands knew the order of things: collar, coat, the dispatch satchel's strap across the chest, the way it had been strapped every morning for eleven years. The strap felt different today. Heavier, or perhaps only honest about its weight for the first time.

From the window they saw them coming up the customhouse square through the blue-grey light: soldiers in field grey that was nearly the color of the sky, maybe sixty of them, moving not like men arriving but like water finding its level. They did not run. They did not shout. Two squads peeled toward the bridge road, one toward the mill lane, and the rest flowed around the customs house the way a hand closes around an object it has been sent to pick up.

Beatrix watched a sergeant test the square's well, drawing water with the unhurried competence of a man who intended to be here a while, and understood, with a clarity that arrived whole and cold like ice in a glass, that no one had come to ask questions.

The republic they served had sent no orders. No warning. Only this.

* * *

By the time Beatrix reached the customs house the doors stood open and soldiers were already inside, and the strangest thing — the thing Beatrix would remember afterward, when memory became testimony — was how gentle it all seemed at first. No broken glass. No shouts. The soldiers moved through the front office with their caps off, as though entering a church, and one of them was reading the tariff schedule pinned to the wall with an expression of genuine scholarly interest.

A lieutenant with a clipboard met Beatrix at the threshold.

"Name and function," he said. Not unkindly. The way a man asks the time.

"Beatrix Thornbury. Courier of the fourth grade, attached to the provincial registry." The words came out in the practiced cadence of the service, rank and number following, and Beatrix heard their own voice as if from a distance — heard how much it sounded like paper being read aloud.

"Fourth grade," the lieutenant repeated, writing it down. "You'll want to speak with the colonel. He's expecting the staff."

*We are the staff*, Beatrix thought, absurdly. Three clerks, a boy who swept, an assayer with gout, and themselves. The staff of a building that had never been worth capturing before.

Inside, the ordinary morning had been inverted with surgical neatness. The soldiers were stacking ledgers.

Not throwing them. Not burning them. Stacking them — carrying the great volumes two-handed, with care, out of the record room and along the corridor to the counting hall, where they were being laid on the long tables in rows, spines aligned, as if for inspection by a schoolmaster. Beatrix watched a young soldier carry the 1831 trade register past them, cradled against his chest like an infant, and felt something turn over in their chest that had no name yet but would, before the day was done, have a very precise one.

Those pages held the names of every soul in Aldenmoor who had ever owed anything, sold anything, crossed anything, sworn anything. Beatrix knew because they had written entries in some of them. *Thornbury, B., lodging paid to the quarter.* A neighbor's name in a ledger column, that first crack in the complacency, weeks ago now — it seemed like another life. Now the entire town was being carried two-handed down a corridor by boys in field grey.

"The colonel will see you in the assay office," the lieutenant said, appearing again at Beatrix's elbow. "When you're ready. He said he's ready whenever you are."

Which was a strange sentence. Beatrix made themselves ready.

* * *

Colonel Vess occupied the small assay office the way a well-made piece of furniture occupies a room: exactly, without apology, fitting a space that had clearly not been built for him and making it seem as though it had. He was perhaps fifty, thin without being frail, with the kind of face that filing clerks trust on sight — orderly, lined, reasonable — and he was standing at the assay window drinking coffee from the assayer's own cup, and he set the cup down and said:

"Courier Thornbury. Thank you for coming so promptly. I hope we haven't inconvenienced your morning."

"We haven't received any notice of requisition," Beatrix said. "Under Article Twelve of the provincial codes, sealed records—"

"—are to remain sealed until relieved by proper authority, yes." Vess smiled. It was a good smile. It reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "I've read the codes. All of them, actually, before the war made them interesting rather than merely tedious. You may relax, Courier. I am proper authority. That is precisely the trouble."

"I don't understand, Colonel."

"No," Vess agreed pleasantly. "Very few people do, at the start." He turned from the window and looked at Beatrix fully, and Beatrix had been looked at by generals and border officials and once, memorably, by a man who later proved to be a spy, and none of them had looked like this. Vess looked the way an auditor looks at a ledger he already knows balances. "Let me tell you what I think you understand already, and then let me tell you what you don't. What you understand: the front is thirty kilometers south and moving this way faster than anyone in the capital will admit in writing. What you may not understand: that everything in this building is now more valuable than this building. More valuable than the town. Possibly more valuable than the bridge, and I assure you the bridge is worth a great deal."

"The ledgers," Beatrix said.

"The ledgers." Vess picked up the assayer's cup again, drank, set it down. "Names, Courier. Debts, titles, conscription ages, grain holdings, kinship claims, sworn statements. Every family in this district reduced to entries in your careful hand — and yours is careful; I've seen your folios, I compliment you — and every entry a lever. Armies can't govern towns. Paper governs towns. Whoever holds the paper holds the town, wherever the town physically happens to be." He gestured vaguely southward, toward the guns, toward the future. "In three weeks there will be new flags in this province. In three months, new laws. But the people will still be the same people, owing the same debts, hiding the same sons — unless someone holds the record of it. Then the record becomes the law. Do you follow?"

Beatrix followed. That was the horror of it. Eleven years of training in the sanctity of documents, and here was a man explaining, courteously, over stolen coffee, what their life's work actually weighed.

"You're taking the records south," Beatrix said. "To your command."

"I'm securing them," Vess corrected mildly. "History will decide whose command secures them best. History is very forgiving about pronouns." He studied Beatrix for a moment, head slightly tilted, and then did the thing that Beatrix would replay in memory for months afterward: he walked to the door, opened it, checked the corridor, and closed it again.

"Now," he said, turning back. "The diplomatic pouch."

The room did not change temperature. Beatrix's body simply stopped, briefly, the way a clock stops — the heart continuing out of habit while everything else hung.

"I don't know what you mean, Colonel."

"A sealed pouch, courier's custody, arrived in this town nine days ago with instructions to hold for a relief convoy." Vess recited it without notes, without effort, the way Beatrix might have recited a tariff table. "The relief convoy is not coming, Courier. I know this because my cavalry burned half of it yesterday at Marren Ford, and the other half turned back. Which means whatever you are holding for the republic, the republic is no longer in a position to collect." He spread his hands, an honest broker's gesture. "I'm not asking you to surrender it this morning. I'm telling you, as one professional to another, that the situation has changed, and that I prefer to do business with informed people. Think about it. We'll talk again."

"There is no pouch," Beatrix said. "There are routine dispatches. Tariff confirmations. Nothing—"

"Courier Thornbury." Vess's voice didn't rise. It settled, the way sediment settles. "I have taken this town's records, and I will read them, all of them, and among other things I will learn which rooms in this town have chimneys that draw and which families buy lamp oil in quantities inconsistent with their declared means. I will learn everything this town knows about itself, including, eventually, everything it knows about you. I mention this not as a threat but as a courtesy, because I find that courtesy produces better outcomes than threats, and I am a man who prefers good outcomes." He picked up the coffee cup a third time and raised it slightly, a toast. "You did nothing wrong. You held a seal faithfully. That used to count for something. It may still. Good morning."

Beatrix saluted — the arm moving on its own, trained into the marrow — and walked out of the assay office on legs that felt borrowed.

* * *

The rest of the day passed in a state Beatrix could only describe as watching.

They were permitted — instructed — to continue working, which meant sitting at their own desk in the front office while soldiers carried the town past them volume by volume, and the work itself had become a kind of theater of normalcy, forms stamped, receipts signed, all of it meaningless and all of it performed anyway, because the alternative was to stop moving and feel what was actually happening.

The boy who swept the steps — Piet, thirteen, whistled constantly, wanted to be a sailor on a river that ran nowhere near the sea — was made to carry ledgers too. Beatrix watched him struggle under the weight of the 1829 customs rolls, watched a soldier take the volume from him gently and say something that made the other soldiers laugh, kindly, and Piet laughed too, relieved, proud almost, included at last in something.

*He doesn't know what he's carrying*, Beatrix thought. *Neither did I, for eleven years.*

That was the shape of it, finally named. Not theft. Theft would have been simpler, would have admitted itself. This was transubstantiation — the ordinary work of ordinary hands becoming, overnight, an instrument. Every line Beatrix had ever written in fair copy was now ammunition. Every receipt they had stamped was now evidence. The neighbor's name in the ledger column, that first small unease — it had not been a premonition of danger. It had been a premonition of use. Paper was never dangerous lying still. Paper became dangerous the moment someone decided what it was for, and Beatrix had spent their whole life believing that deciding was someone else's job, that the courier's virtue lay in never asking, and now the never-asking sat across the square in the person of a polite colonel who read the provincial codes for pleasure and called his thefts *securing*.

In the late afternoon the light came long and amber through the front windows, and Beatrix watched the soldiers stack the last of the record room's shelves into a wagon under canvas, roped down, wheels greased. Somewhere in those crates was the conscription list of Aldenmoor. Somewhere in them was the baker's name, and the landlord's, and the assayer's gout recorded in triplicate, and the sweeping boy's dead father's fishing license. A town in crates. Roped down under canvas against weather that no longer mattered.

Jude was in the square. Beatrix saw him through the window — the clerk from the census office, quiet, watchful, the man whose handwriting in the margin of a folio had once struck Beatrix as merely elegant. He stood at the edge of the square with his hat off, not protesting, not begging, just watching the wagons with an expression Beatrix recognized because they had seen it in their own mirror that morning: a man doing arithmetic he did not want to finish.

Their eyes met through the glass. Neither nodded. Both understood.

As dusk fell, a runner came to the front office with a slip of paper for the lieutenant on duty, and Beatrix, stamping forms ten feet away, caught the lieutenant's murmur to his sergeant: *"Marren Ford's gone entirely. Convoy's scattered into the hills. Colonel says nobody in or out after dark until further orders."*

Nobody in or out.

The pouch was upstairs in the record room's old strongbox, behind a false panel Jude had shown them, wrapped in oilcloth, holding its single word — *names* — behind a torn corner. And now the building that held it also held sixty soldiers, a colonel who read codes for pleasure, and a standing order that no one should leave.

Beatrix stamped another form. Their hand, they noticed with distant professional approval, did not shake.

Across the square, in the darkening window of the census office, a lamplight appeared. One flame, steady, deliberate — lit, Beatrix was somehow certain, not for working but for signaling. For saying: *I am still here. I can see you. Come when you can.*

The last wagon pulled out of the square with the town's names under canvas, and the gates of the customs house were barred from the inside, and Beatrix sat in the amber dark doing the only work left to them, which was waiting, and watching the one lit window, and understanding — finally, completely, with the terrible clarity of a clerk who has always balanced the column — that the next thing they did with paper would be the first thing they had ever done with it that mattered.
