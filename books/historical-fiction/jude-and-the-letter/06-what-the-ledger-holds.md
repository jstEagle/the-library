## 6. What the Ledger Holds

Jude had always believed that a town could be read the way a ledger is read: column by column, debt against credit, until the sum of a place revealed itself. Aldenmoor's sum, Jude had long ago concluded, was neither grand nor terrible — a modest arithmetic of flour debts and timber rights, of widows owed small kindnesses and merchants owed small grievances. It was an honest sum. It balanced.

The soldiers did not read it that way.

From the window of the clerk's office — still Jude's office in name, though a corporal with chapped hands now sat at the desk with a rifle across his knees like a sleeping cat — Jude watched the customs house across the square. Two days since the requisition. The ledgers had gone into the building in the first hour, carried by soldiers who handled them without reverence, stacked under one arm like sacks of meal, and Jude had stood on the courthouse steps with his hands curled at his sides, counting doors to keep from screaming. Forty-one households. Three hundred and six souls. Every name in those books.

Now the windows of the customs house glowed all through the second night, lamplight moving behind frosted glass in slow, methodical passes, and Jude understood what he was watching: someone was reading his town.

* * *

He went at dusk, when the sentries changed and the square filled with the smell of the army's cook-fires, tallow and scorched barley, a smell that would cling to Aldenmoor for years afterward in the memory of everyone who lived through it. He wore his clerk's satchel and his clerk's face, the mild, apologetic expression of a man whose whole life had been spent apologizing for interruptions, and he told himself he was going only to ask about access — a town's records must remain accessible, there were tithe dates approaching, there were inheritance matters, there was always paperwork, paperwork outlives everything, even armies.

The corporal at the door examined the satchel more than the face.

"Colonel Vess has the archives," the corporal said. The word *archives* sat strangely in his mouth, a borrowed word, worn wrong.

"The ledgers are not archives," Jude heard himself say. "They are instruments. A ledger you don't keep current is worse than no ledger at all."

It was true, and truth, Jude knew even then, was the best disguise, because it required no rehearsal. The corporal squinted at him, decided he was too small a thing to be dangerous, and let him as far as the antechamber, where another soldier took the satchel and pointed him to a bench.

So Jude waited on a hard bench beneath a portrait of some dead magistrate, and through the half-open door of the reading room he saw the tables, and on the tables he saw his life's work spread open under military lamps, and he began — slowly, the way frost forms on glass — to understand how much there was to lose.

* * *

They had taken everything. Not only the tariff rolls and the property assessments, which any requisitioning force would want, but the parish copies, the burial registers, the militia musters of '09 that no one had opened in a generation, the apprenticeship bonds, the grain contracts, the water-rights disputes that had outlived every party to them. And there, in a stack set apart from the others, bound in grey cord and marked with a slip of violet paper — the conscription lists.

Jude knew those lists better than anyone alive. He had written most of them.

Not the decisions — the decisions came down from the provincial capital in the form of quotas, cold numbers like weather, *this district will yield forty men* — but the names. It had been Jude, two winters ago, sitting in this same office with the wind worrying the shutters, who had matched quota to household, who had weighed the Halversens' need for their eldest son against the Brandts' need for both of theirs, who had crossed off one name and written another and felt each stroke of the pen go through him like a needle. He had believed, then, that he was doing it gently. That careful handwriting was a kind of mercy. That if the lists had to exist, better they existed in the hand of someone who knew that Edda Marlowe's boy had a bad lung, that the Thiel twins could not both be taken or the farm would die.

Now the lists lay under a violet slip on a soldier's table, and Jude understood with a clarity that arrived not as thought but as temperature — cold, specific, spreading from the spine outward — that he had never been writing mercy at all. He had been writing inventory.

And the army read inventory faster than he ever had.

* * *

"Master Clerk." Colonel Vess stood in the doorway of the reading room, and though he had spoken to Jude only twice before — once at the seizure, once in passing — he used the title as though it were a fact he had verified. He was a neat man, grey at the temples, with the unhurried courtesy of someone who had never once needed to raise his voice to be obeyed. "You've come about your books."

"Instruments, Colonel. As I told your corporal. A ledger goes stale in a month. Debts shift. Deaths occur. If the records are to have value—"

"They have value," Vess said mildly, "precisely because they do not shift. That is their whole virtue, isn't it? A ledger says what was true. Men lie; ink doesn't." He stepped aside from the doorway — an invitation, or the appearance of one. "Come. Show me what I should look at first. You know this town far better than I do. Better, I suspect, than it knows itself."

That last sentence Jude would turn over for nights afterward, the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth. *Better than it knows itself.* It had sounded like a compliment. It was not a compliment.

He walked the tables beside the colonel and performed competence, pointing out the tax rolls, the granary schedules, the mill contracts, and all the while some colder part of him ran ahead like a scout, cataloguing dangers. Here was the muster roll — useless, outdated. Here the burial registers — harmless, unless one was calculating how many able-bodied men a town could hide behind its churchyard. Here the conscription lists, and Vess paused over them longer than over anything else, his finger tracing down a column of names, stopping at intervals as though plucking fruit.

"Thorough work," he said. "Cross-referenced by household. Occupations noted. Even exemptions — medical, hardship." His finger rested, lightly, beside a name. "Who made these determinations?"

"The provincial quota determined numbers. The local office assigned names. Standard practice."

"And the local office is you."

"The local office is the office," Jude said. "I am its clerk."

Vess smiled at that — a small, genuine-seeming smile, which was somehow worse than any other kind — and moved on. And Jude followed, and told himself the cold in his stomach was only fear for the town, for the three hundred and six, and almost believed it.

Until he reached the far table, and saw what else they had.

* * *

It should not have been there. That was the first thing — the impossible, structural wrongness of it. The diplomatic pouch had come to the customs house under seal, addressed to the courier Beatrix Thornbury, with orders to hold it for the relief convoy, and sealed pouches did not get opened by requisition clerks; they got passed along unopened or not at all, because the seals themselves meant something even to the men who ignored everything else. But the war was eating the republic's meaning faster than its territory, and here, on the far table, half-sheltered under a folded campaign map as though the soldier covering it had done so instinctively, out of some residual piety toward broken things — here was the pouch itself, slit along its seam with a knife, the wax seal cracked clean through like ice in March.

And protruding from it, one corner of heavy paper, cream-coloured, with a border of official engraving.

Jude had seen a thousand documents. He knew paper the way farmers know weather. This was treaty-weight stock, the kind used for instruments of state, and the engraving along its edge was the border pattern of the chancellery itself. Whatever that letter was, it was not a tariff circular. It had come from the capital at the moment the capital was still pretending to function, and it had been sitting in his customs house, two rooms from where he slept, while an occupying army settled into the town around it.

He did not touch it. He wanted to, with an ache that surprised him — wanted it the way a drowning man wants air, suddenly and totally — but Vess was three paces away and soldiers' eyes were everywhere, and so Jude did the thing his whole life had trained him for: he looked at it sideways, briefly, thoroughly, and memorized.

The torn corner of the outer wrapper — the wrapper beneath the slit pouch, the inner wrapping — had come loose, and through the gap, in the lamp's unsteady light, Jude caught a single line of the letter's opening. Formal salutation, then a phrase he read twice in the space of one heartbeat:

*...to all whom these presents concern, and in witness of the names herein...*

Names. In witness of the names therein.

His mind, that patient inventory-taker, laid the pieces side by side on its own quiet table and let the sum reveal itself. Conscription lists, seized. A letter from the chancellery, speaking of names under witness. An army that read ledgers the way wolves read snow — not for beauty, but for tracks. He did not know what the letter promised. He knew only what it was worth, and to whom, and that its worth and the town's fate had just become, in some way he could not yet calculate, the same number.

"Something of interest?" Vess had drifted back, hands clasped behind him, courteous as a host at dinner.

"The pouch came in before the occupation," Jude said. "Diplomatic seal. I only wondered whether it should be inventoried separately — sealed materials sometimes carry penalties for mishandling, even after—" He gestured vaguely at the world, at the collapsing front, at everything the word *after* now had to strain to contain.

"It has been inventoried," Vess said. "By me. Personally." He looked at the slit pouch without any change of expression at all, and Jude understood that the colonel had cut the seal himself, and that he was telling Jude this deliberately, and that the telling was a message whose full text Jude could not yet read. "You may go, Master Clerk. I'll send for you if the records require explanation. They will — you keep a very dense hand." A pause. "Your predecessor kept nothing half so useful."

There was no predecessor. There had been no other clerk in Aldenmoor in nineteen years. Vess knew that. The sentence was a hook dropped into dark water, and Jude felt it pass down through him, testing for weight.

"I'll be at the office," Jude said. "For when you send."

* * *

He walked home the long way, past the mill, past the frozen ruts of the cart road where the refugees' footprints had already been filled with new snow, and the cold clarity that had come to him in the reading room completed its work. This was the gift of his nature, he supposed — that panic in Jude Montrose-Clay never became noise. It became arithmetic.

Three hundred and six souls. Forty-one households. The conscription lists in Vess's hands meant transport, and transport in this winter meant the labour columns everyone had heard about from the fleeing families who passed through — the ones who spoke of roads east lined with people who no longer walked so much as continued. And the letter — whatever it said, whoever it named — was either a rope or a noose, and it lay within reach of a man who read inventories for a living, and every hour it stayed in that building its odds shifted.

He counted doors as he walked, out of habit. The Halversens'. The Brandts'. The Marlowes', where Edda's boy with the bad lung was chopping wood in the blue dusk, badly, pausing to cough. Jude had written that boy onto the exempt list with his own careful hand, and the list was now a weapon lying in another man's house, and careful handwriting, it turned out, protected no one from anything except ugliness on the page.

At his own gate he stopped. His brother Tam's boots were gone from the step — Tam, who wanted flight, who called the ledgers *kindling*, who had left anyway two days after the seizure, saying he'd rather owe his survival to his legs than to anybody's files. The house was dark. Jude stood in the snow a long moment, looking back at the customs house, where the lamps still moved behind the frost-glass in their slow, thorough passes.

One name, he thought. One name on one page, and the whole sum changes.

He needed to see that letter. Not its seal, not its provenance — its words. And there was exactly one person in Aldenmoor who might know what it held, the courier Thornbury, who filed dispatches in a hand so careful it had caught Jude's eye months ago, who had gone pale as milk when the soldiers came, who was at this hour somewhere in the occupied quarter carrying, Jude would have bet the office itself, a great deal more than tariffs.

Tomorrow, then. Or the day after. Soon.

Because Vess was reading the town by lamplight, and Jude had seen enough ledgers in his life to know the ending of every one: eventually, someone comes to collect.
