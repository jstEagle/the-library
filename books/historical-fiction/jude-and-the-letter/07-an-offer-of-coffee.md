## 7. An Offer of Coffee

The summons came at half past four in the afternoon, delivered by a boy in a uniform two sizes too large for him, who stood in the doorway of the customs house office with his heels together and his eyes fixed on a point somewhere above Beatrix's left shoulder.

"The Colonel requests your presence," the boy said. "At seven o'clock. The requisitioned residence on Tanner's Row."

*Requests.* Beatrix set down the pen they had been holding without writing anything for the better part of an hour and looked at the slip of paper the boy extended. It was written in a fine, upright hand — not the colonel's own, surely; a staff officer's — and it bore no seal, no signature, nothing but the time and the address and the single word *coffee* where one might have expected *tea*. Someone had thought about that, Beatrix realized. Someone had considered that this was a coffee town, not a tea town, that the garrison's courtesy would land more softly if it arrived in the local idiom. That was the kind of attention to detail that frightened Beatrix more than shouting would have. Shouting was honest. Attention was a net.

"I'll come," Beatrix said, and the boy saluted with a motion like a bird taking off, and was gone.

For a long while afterward Beatrix sat in the small office that had been theirs for eleven months — long enough to wear a groove in the floorboards between the desk and the filing cabinet, short enough that they still sometimes reached for a light switch that wasn't there — and listened to the sounds of the occupied building. Boots on the stairs. The scrape of the ledgers being carried out of the archive room, one stack at a time, each stack wrapped in sacking to protect the bindings, as though the army cared for the books themselves and not for what was written inside them. A soldier whistling something tuneless near the window. All of it horribly domestic. Occupation, Beatrix was learning, did not announce itself with trumpets. It arrived the way damp arrives in a wall: quietly, everywhere, changing the temperature of everything you touched.

They had watched their ledgers go. That was the thing Beatrix kept returning to, the image that surfaced when they closed their eyes: Sergeant Hollis — no, not Hollis, the other one, the one with the chapped hands — carrying the customs ledgers out under his arm the way a farmer carries a lamb, carefully, familiarly, as if he had done it all his life. Every name in Aldenmoor. Every debt, every shipment, every birth recorded at the border for tax purposes, every death. Eleven years of the town's ordinary life, bound in calfskin and canvas, now sleeping in a requisitioned strongroom under the guard of men who had been strangers nine days ago.

And somewhere in that same building, in a different drawer, the diplomatic pouch. Still sealed. Still waiting for the relief convoy that was — according to the last dispatch out of the capital, twelve days ago and already smelling of ash — *en route.*

Beatrix looked at their hands. They were steady. That surprised them. Inside, something was moving like water finding a crack in a foundation, slow and patient and impossible to stop.

* * *

Tanner's Row had belonged, until ten days ago, to the Aldenmoor Mercantile Association, whose chairman had done very well out of grain tariffs and had furnished his offices accordingly. Now a flag hung over the door that Beatrix did not recognize from any heraldry lesson — a plain field with a black bar, the kind of design that said *we are new and we intend to be permanent* — and the brass plate had been unscrewed, leaving four pale rectangles in the wood where the screws had held.

A sentry took Beatrix's name without writing it down, which was its own kind of message: *we know who you are; the knowing is not the problem.* He led them up a staircase of polished oak, past portraits of dead merchants who now seemed to watch the corridor with the mild embarrassment of the dispossessed, and opened a door into a room that smelled of woodsmoke and roasted beans.

Colonel Vess stood at the window with his back to the room, hands clasped behind him, looking out at the square. He was not what Beatrix had expected, though Beatrix could not have said what they had expected — something louder, perhaps, or coarser. Vess was a narrow man, grey at the temples, with the kind of face that would pass unremarked in any crowd in any city in the republic, which was precisely what made it memorable once you had seen it twice. He wore his uniform without decoration. His boots were polished but not gleaming. Everything about him suggested a man who had decided exactly how much effort each thing deserved and never spent a coin more.

"Courier Thornbury," he said, without turning. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. The chair by the fire — the other one wobbles, and I refuse to be responsible for spilling coffee on a merchant's carpet. He'd bill me for it. He'd probably bill my grandchildren."

There was a low table between two armchairs, and on it a silver pot — requisitioned, obviously, from some pantry — and two cups, and a plate of small dark biscuits that Beatrix did not recognize as anything sold in Aldenmoor. Army biscuits, then. Biscuits that had traveled with the army the way lice travel with soldiers. Vess poured without asking, and the smell rose up rich and bitter and foreign, and Beatrix understood suddenly that even the coffee was a demonstration: *we have supply lines. We have time. We can afford to be gracious.*

"You take it black," Vess said. It was not quite a question. "I asked young Ferro — the clerk who works the front desk — he says you drink it black, no sugar, standing up, usually before six." He handed the cup across. "I find I like knowing small things about people. It makes conversation easier. Don't you find?"

"I find," Beatrix said, "that most conversations are easier than this one appears to be."

Vess smiled. It was a good smile, economical, deployed rather than felt. "Good. Honesty to begin with. Let's keep it as long as we can." He settled into his own chair, crossing one ankle over his knee, cup balanced with the ease of a man who had drunk coffee in a hundred requisitioned rooms. "Do you know why I asked you here, Courier Thornbury?"

"To discuss the pouch."

"Mm. Partly." He sipped. Outside the window, across the square, the church bell began striking five, and Vess waited for it to finish with the patience of a man who had learned that bells could not be hurried and neither could anything else worth doing. "Partly to discuss the pouch. But mostly I wanted to look at you. You've been in this town eleven months. You file papers. You drink black coffee before dawn. And in nine days of occupation you have not once come to my office to ask after your diplomatic property — which is, I assume, the most important thing you have ever carried. Do you know what that tells me?"

"That I'm patient."

"It tells me you're frightened." Vess said it gently, the way a doctor delivers a diagnosis he has known for weeks. "Frightened people either run or freeze, and you haven't run, so you're freezing. Freezing is sensible. Freezing is what I would do in your position." He leaned forward slightly. "But frozen things thaw, Courier Thornbury, and I would much rather we were talking honestly before that happens than after."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, a door closed. Beatrix became aware of the cup in their hands, of its heat, of the way their fingers had arranged themselves around it with perfect composure while everything behind the composure rearranged itself into panic. *He knows nothing,* they told themselves. *He is guessing. Guessing is his profession.* But the words felt thin, and Vess's eyes were the color of wet slate, and they missed nothing.

"Tell me about yourself," Vess said. "Not the service record — I've read the service record. Tell me how a person comes to spend their life carrying paper through wars."

It was, Beatrix would think later, the most dangerous question anyone had ever asked them, because it was the only question they had never prepared an answer for. There was the official answer — orphaned in the border war, taken into the Diplomatic Service's foundling program, trained, tested, commissioned — and Beatrix gave it, in its proper order, in the flat recitative tone of a catechism. And Vess listened with his head tilted, and when it was finished he nodded slowly, as though Beatrix had confirmed something.

"They made you out of nothing," he said. "That's what the Service does. Takes children the war orphaned and makes them into instruments. Loyal to the document, not to the man. Not to the town. Not to anything that can die." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "It's efficient. I've worked alongside their couriers in three campaigns. They don't steal, they don't sell, they don't break. But they also don't *choose*, do they? Choice requires a self, and they burned yours out early. Am I being unfair?"

"You're being accurate," Beatrix heard themselves say, "in the way that men are accurate when they want something."

Vess laughed — a real laugh this time, short and surprised out of him. "Well. There's a self in there after all. Good." He set down his cup. "Then let me tell you what I want, plainly, because I respect plainness and I suspect you're starved of it."

He told them. He told it the way he did everything, economically, without decoration: the relief convoy was coming — he was confident of it, though he did not say how confident, and the confidence in his voice had a hairline fracture running through it that Beatrix heard only because they were listening for exactly that. When it came, the diplomatic pouch would be collected, signed for, and gone, and none of it need concern Beatrix further. In the meantime, the army required cooperation of a mundane kind. Confirmation of entries. Clarification of the customs codes. An explanation, perhaps, of certain marginal annotations in the ledgers — *whose handwriting, Courier Thornbury? Whose initials?* — because the ledgers were going to travel with the convoy too, all of them, east, where analysts would read eleven years of Aldenmoor's life the way one reads an enemy's diary.

"And in exchange?" Vess said. "In exchange, when the convoy comes, there will be seats on it. Seats are scarce. The officers' families have been promised most of them, and there is a list, and lists are terrible things, but I control this one." He let that sit in the smoky air between them. "You are not from here. I know that. You owe this town nothing. But you have lived among these people for a year, and I am offering you the chance to leave when leaving is still possible, with your papers intact and your name clean, instead of whatever the alternative turns out to be when the front arrives — and it will arrive, Courier Thornbury, sooner than the convoy does, I think, though I would thank you not to repeat that."

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Beatrix looked into their cup and saw their own face distorted in the black surface, and understood that they were standing on a ledge they had not known existed until this moment. Because here was the arithmetic Vess was laying out, neat as a ledger entry: give him the handwriting, the initials, the names behind them — Jude's careful clerical script, the annotations, the whole quiet network of a town that kept its own records — and receive a seat, a future, a clean name. Refuse, and become a line in someone else's ledger.

"Whose initials," Vess repeated, softly, "are in the margins of the 1831 conscription schedules?"

And Beatrix lied.

It did not feel the way they had imagined lying would feel. There was no heat, no rush, no dramatic internal thunderclap. It felt, in fact, almost exactly like telling the truth: the same breath drawn, the same level voice, the same steady return of the other man's gaze. Perhaps that was the horror of it. Perhaps that was what the Service had built into them after all, without ever teaching it — not honesty, but the *capacity* for either, indistinguishable from inside.

"The margins are mine," Beatrix said. "Cross-references. Tariff schedule amendments against the old conscription categories. Clerical housekeeping. If the annotations seem irregular, it's because I was never given a proper key to the filing system and I improvised one. I can reproduce the scheme from memory if it would help your analysts."

Every word true-shaped and false-cored. The margins were not theirs. The scheme they offered to reproduce was a fiction assembled in the space of four seconds, detailed enough to survive one inspection and collapse under two. And Vess listened, and nodded, and wrote nothing down, and Beatrix understood that he was doing the same thing they were: holding cards face-down, smiling, pouring coffee.

"I may take you up on that," he said. "Tomorrow, perhaps. These things are better fresh." He rose, and Beatrix rose with him, and for a moment they stood on either side of the dying fire like two men at a graveside. "Courier Thornbury. One more thing, and then you're free to go." His voice dropped into a register that was almost kind. "Whatever you're carrying in that pouch — and I don't know what it is, I want you to believe that, I genuinely don't know — whatever it is, it was written by people in a capital that is currently deciding, at this hour, whether this province is worth defending. Papers don't save towns. People save towns, occasionally, by accident. Remember that when the choice comes. It always comes."

He walked them to the door himself. On the landing, in the lamplight, he paused.

"The coffee," he said, "was genuine. The rest was negotiation. Sleep well."

* * *

Beatrix walked back through the town in the blue hour, past shuttered shops and the well where someone had left a bucket hanging, forgotten, and their body performed the walk perfectly — boots finding the cobbles, coat buttoned against the river wind — while inside, the vertigo opened beneath them like a shaft.

Because that was the thing no one warned you about lying. Not guilt. Guilt would have been simple, guilt would have been almost comfortable, a wound to bind and carry. What Beatrix felt was worse: *groundlessness.* They had lied to a colonel of an occupying army and the lie had held, and now the world had split into two versions — the one Vess believed, and the one that was true — and Beatrix alone stood on the seam between them, and the seam was very narrow, and the drop on either side went down a very long way.

They thought of the foundling house. Of Sister Marguerite, who had taught them penmanship and the principle that a courier's word, once given, was the only currency that never devalued. *Your word is the republic in miniature,* she used to say, rapping the knuckles of children who slanted their letters. *If it fails, everything fails.* Twenty years Beatrix had kept that word unbroken — through checkpoints and bribes offered and refused, through winters in border posts, through the slow rot of the government they served. Twenty years, and it had taken one cup of coffee and one hour of slate-grey eyes to end it.

And yet. And yet the lie had been the first thing they had ever *chosen.* Not assigned, not trained, not commissioned. Chosen — in the space of four seconds, for reasons they could not fully articulate even to themselves, in defense of a handwriting in a margin that belonged to a clerk who counted doors and knew everyone's debts.

At the corner of the customs house square, Beatrix stopped. A light still burned in the ground-floor window — the duty lamp, which regulation required be kept lit, though the regulations now governed an empty archive. Behind the glass, briefly, a shadow moved. Not a soldier's silhouette. Smaller. Quicker. Gone before Beatrix could fix it.

Someone was in the building. Someone who should not be.

Beatrix stood in the cold with their heart going like a drum and made the second decision of their life in a single day: they did not raise the alarm. They walked past the window without turning their head, climbed the outer stair to their rooms, and sat down in the dark to wait for morning — listening to the occupied building breathe around them, wondering which of them, now, was watching whom.
