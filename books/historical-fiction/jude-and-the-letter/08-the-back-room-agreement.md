## 8. The Back Room Agreement

Jude had learned long ago that a town speaks differently after dark.

By day Aldenmoor talked in the ordinary registers — the price of tallow, the wetness of the hay, whose pig had gotten into whose garden — and it was possible to believe, in the daylight, that the town was only what it said. But at night the other voice came out. The one under the words. Shutters closing a half-beat later than usual. A dog that barked at soldiers instead of strangers. The miller's daughter laughing too loudly at nothing, the way people laughed when they were afraid and did not want to be caught at it.

Jude walked home through that second voice with their collar up and counted, as they always counted, the lit windows. There were fewer tonight. Fear made people economical with candlelight; fear taught you to live smaller than your house. And one window burned where it should not have: the customs house annex, second floor, the courier's rooms.

Beatrix Thornbury was awake. Beatrix Thornbury had been awake for hours, if the lamp oil smell drifting from the yard drain was anything to judge by — and Jude had been standing by that drain longer than anyone would have thought decent, in the dark, doing what clerks do best. Watching. Adding up.

The sums were bad. They had been bad all day.

Jude let themselves into the clerk's cottage behind the customs house — the army had requisitioned the front rooms but left the back door alone, as armies always did, because armies thought in fronts — and stood in the cold kitchen listening to the stove tick. On the table lay the thing they had come home to think about, and had thought about instead through every waking hour since dawn.

It was a hymnal. Their mother's hymnal, actually — *Songs of the Valley*, spine cracked, half the pages soft as cloth from handling — and inside it, sewn flat against the boards of the cover with waxed thread, a shallow pocket of oilcloth just wide enough for a folded sheet of paper. Or four. Or six. Jude's mother had carried toll receipts in it, and once, memorably, a man's pardon, though whether the pardon was true no one in the family had ever been brave enough to ask. When she died the hymnal came to Jude along with the debts, and Jude had kept it on the shelf with the tax codes, which was either piety or irony, and Jude had never decided which.

Tonight it seemed like neither. Tonight it seemed like a tool.

Because Jude had gone back to the ledgers that afternoon — permitted to enter the records room now only under escort, a courtesy Colonel Vess extended to the town clerk the way a farmer extends a hand to a cow — and while the soldier at the door examined his fingernails, Jude had looked at the pile of seized volumes and seen, third from the top, a corner of paper that did not belong. Cream-colored. Foreign stock. Diplomatic rag, the kind that costs more per sheet than a laborer's day. It protruded from the diplomatic pouch the way a splinter protrudes from skin, and Jude knew — knew with the cold certainty of a person who has spent their life among papers the way fishermen know water — that the letter of safe conduct, or whatever it was, whatever it promised, was still in that building. Seized. Filed. One signature away from meaning nothing at all.

And beneath the conscription schedules, in Vess's tidy hand, the transport list was already drafted. Jude had seen it. Had read eleven names before the escort cleared his throat. The baker first — of course the baker first, armies learned early that hunger governs better than rifles — then the Halloran boys, then old Marta Fenn who could barely walk, because whoever wrote these lists had never once considered whether the people on them could survive them.

Eleven names. Out of a town of perhaps three hundred.

Jude set the hymnal on the table and put their hands flat on either side of it and let themselves feel, for exactly one minute, the full weight of what they were contemplating. Then they banked the stove, took up the lamp, and went next door to make a traitor of the courier.

* * *

The knock was three beats, evenly spaced, the knock of a person with nothing to hide. Jude heard movement inside — a chair pushed back, footsteps crossing to the window first rather than the door, which told Jude something about how Beatrix's week was going — and then the bolt slid and there stood the courier, lamplight behind them, looking like a person who had slept in their coat and not forgiven themselves for it.

"Master Clerk," Beatrix said. Not warm. Not cold. Measured, the way you'd measure flour when you suspected it might be short.

"Courier." Jude lifted the hymnal slightly, so its edge showed in the light. "You look like a man who hasn't eaten. I've bread that isn't poisoned and a stove the soldiers haven't found. Ten minutes."

Something moved behind Beatrix's eyes — suspicion, calculation, and beneath both, so faint it might have been imagined, relief. They stepped back from the doorway. Jude entered, closed the bolt, and hung their wet coat with the particular care of someone establishing, deliberately, that they intended to stay long enough to dry off.

The room was spare and very neat, except for one thing: the dispatch case on the table, empty, its straps undone, lying open like a mouth. Beside it a cold cup of coffee and a map of the province that Beatrix had evidently been studying upside down, judging by the wear marks on the wrong corners.

"You went to the yard drain tonight," Beatrix said, without preamble. "Behind the kitchen. You stood there eleven minutes."

"Ten and a half," Jude said. "I count faster than I stand." They set the hymnal down beside the open dispatch case. "I also went to the records room this afternoon. With my escort. Who is a boy named Piotr with chilblains and no imagination. And while he was admiring his own hands, I saw what the colonel has filed under *pending disposition.*"

Beatrix went very still. It was a soldier's stillness, or a gambler's — Jude couldn't tell yet which — and it confirmed everything the eleven minutes at the drain drain had suggested: the courier already knew how much danger they were in, and had known for some time, and had told no one.

"There's a page showing," Jude went on, quietly, into that stillness. "Foreign stock. Rag paper. Torn at the corner, top right, as if someone opened the seal in a hurry and repacked it worse. It's in the pouch, and the pouch is in the iron cabinet, and the iron cabinet is going wherever Colonel Vess goes. Which, if you've seen the wagon trains on the north road, is not toward any authority that honors safe conducts."

"You shouldn't have seen that."

"No," Jude agreed. "Nor should you have raised the alarm last night when I was in the records room after hours. But you didn't. So it seems we're both already guilty of something. The only question left is whether we're guilty of it together or separately, and separately has a much worse ending."

Beatrix sat down slowly, the way a person sits when their knees have opinions of their own. For a long moment neither spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, a patrol passed, boots striking cobbles in that unhurried rhythm of men who have all night and all the power.

Then Beatrix said, "Tell me the plan. All of it. Before I decide whether to report you."

"And after?"

"After, I'll decide whether I meant it."

So Jude told them.

They laid it out the way their mother had taught them to lay out a run — not as a story, because stories invite argument, but as an inventory, item by item, each thing resting on the thing beneath it. *First:* the conscription lists must be copied before Vess moves them, because a copy cannot be confiscated out of existence. *Second:* the copies travel in the hymnal's pocket, out through the marsh ford, to the partisan camp beyond the fens, where men who keep no ledgers will nonetheless remember three hundred names if those names are worth anything to them — and they will be, because every name on a transport list is a name the army wants, and the partisans want everything the army wants. *Third:* the letter itself — the real letter, the sealed promise — must leave the cabinet and cross the same ford, because a promise filed in a conquering officer's luggage is not a promise anymore, it is a trophy. *Fourth:* the routes. The bridge, guarded, watched, useless except for griefs and funerals. The mill gate, guarded, corruptible, held by a sergeant whose father owed Jude's family a debt of the old kind, the kind that predates flags. And the ford — the ford no one guards properly because no one who matters believes anything important comes out of a marsh at night.

"My family does," Jude said. "Did. For three generations before the customs house swallowed us. My grandmother ran salt through that marsh when the last war made salt worth more than silver. My mother ran everything else. I know which reed beds hold a man's weight in January frost and which ones eat him. I know the sentry rotation at the ford because I wrote half the garrison's duty rosters myself, before any of this happened, with my own careful hand."

Beatrix listened without interrupting, which Jude noted was rare — couriers, in Jude's experience, interrupted constantly, because their whole trade was compression and they could not bear a sentence carrying more weight than it needed. This one listened like a person auditing. When Jude finished, the silence lasted the length of ten heartbeats, and then Beatrix shook their head.

"No."

Just that. Flat, final, almost gentle.

"No," Beatrix repeated, standing, moving to the window, keeping the street in view out of habit. "You don't understand what you're proposing. That pouch is a diplomatic instrument under my commission. If it leaves my custody — if one page of it surfaces anywhere — I am not a courier who failed, I am a courier who turned. They don't court-martial you for losing dispatches. They hang you for delivering them to the wrong side. And you—" they turned, and for the first time Jude saw the exhaustion under the discipline, raw as torn skin— "you are a town clerk. Your whole defense is that you're nobody. You touch the ledgers because you've always touched the ledgers. The moment you carry a forged document across a line, you're a smuggler again, and your family's history stops being a story you tell and becomes evidence they use."

"It became evidence the moment Vess opened the census," Jude said. "He knows my name. He knows my mother's name — it's in the trade registries he confiscated, with annotations. I'm already a smuggler in his files. I'm simply one who hasn't been caught at anything yet. There is no version of this where I stay clean, courier. There's only a version where I'm useful, and a version where I'm not."

"And the town?" Beatrix's voice cracked upward. "Three hundred people. Eleven names on a list today — thirty tomorrow, when the convoy situation resolves however it resolves. You want to walk copied names through a frozen marsh based on a promise from men with rifles, and if it fails — if one sentry is awake, if one page is damp — Vess doesn't punish you and me. He punishes the town. Collective measures. He's said the word already, you know he has. Are you prepared to buy eleven lives with three hundred?"

"I'm prepared to buy eleven lives with two," Jude said, and heard their own voice drop into the register their mother used at the negotiating table, low and level and immovable. "That's the arithmetic nobody in a uniform ever does. Everyone counts the risk to the many. No one counts the risk to the two. Two people, one ford, one night. If we go and we drown, the town loses a clerk and a stranger, and Vess finds nothing, because the hymnal sinks and the copies are written in a hand he can't trace and the letter stays in his cabinet where he already thinks it is. The town doesn't pay. We do. That's the whole design of it."

Beatrix didn't answer immediately. They had turned back to the window, and Jude watched the courier watch the street, and understood that the refusal was failing — not being argued away, but failing on its own, the way a dam fails, quietly, from pressure accumulated long before.

"The baker," Jude said softly. "You buy your bread from the Ostrer woman. I've seen you in her queue, standing too straight, the way outsiders stand. Her sons are on the list. Both of them."

"I know whose names are on the list." Very quiet now.

"And the boy who sweeps your office. Tomas. He's fourth."

"I said I know." Beatrix pressed one hand flat against the window glass as if checking the world's temperature. "You think I don't lie awake doing exactly your arithmetic? You think I came to this profession because I never counted?" A breath. "I was raised by the service, Master Clerk. Raised the way you raise a tool. Every loyalty I have was assigned to me with a number attached. And I have done everything right. Everything. My seals have never lapsed. My routes have never leaked. I have carried things across borders that would have ended wars sooner or started them earlier, and I never once asked what was in them, because asking is the first disloyalty and I was very good at never asking."

The stove ticked. Down the street, the patrol turned a corner and its bootbeats faded.

"And now," Beatrix said, "there's a word showing through a torn corner, and it's *names,* and I can't stop seeing it. And there's a woman who sells me bread, and her sons' names are somewhere in that cabinet in a colonel's handwriting. And you come in here with your dead mother's hymnal and your family of criminals and you offer me the one thing I was built without." They turned around. Their face was terrible and calm. "A choice."

"Yes," Jude said.

"If I say yes, I need you to understand something. If it comes to it — if we're taken, if the ford goes wrong — I will say you coerced me. I will swear it, convincingly, with details, because I can lie when it matters and you possibly can't, and one of us hanging is better than two." Said flatly, without apology. "That is my condition. That is what my yes costs."

Jude considered it, and found — unexpectedly — that it didn't frighten them. It was, in its way, the most honest thing anyone had offered them in weeks.

"Accepted," they said. "Mine is simpler. We copy the names first. Before anything moves, before the letter, before the ford — every name gets copied twice, and one copy hides somewhere even we can't find if we're questioned. The letter is a promise. Names are proof. If we lose everything else, the names have to survive."

"Where?"

Jude allowed themselves the smallest smile of the night. "Churches are very honest about God and very dishonest about walls. There's a cavity behind the saint's shelf in Saint Brigid's that my grandmother used for brandy. It'll hold a hymnal."

For a moment the two of them stood in the lamplight — the clerk and the courier, the rooted and the routed, each holding a loyalty the other couldn't quite weigh — and the room felt less like a conspiracy than like a treaty signing between two small, exhausted nations.

"We start tomorrow night," Beatrix said. "Vess keeps the cabinet locked until the convoy resolves; he told me so himself over tea, watching my face the whole time. We'll have maybe two hours after the last patrol passes. I can open the pouch's compartment without disturbing the outer seal — it's why I was given the case." A pause, and then, drier: "Apparently I was good at something."

"You're good at more than that," Jude said, taking up their coat. "You didn't report me tonight."

"I may yet."

"No," Jude said, opening the door onto the cold, onto the dark town with its shuttered windows and its second voice. "You won't. Because you stood at that window for eleven minutes deciding, and I watched you decide, and people who take that long have already chosen. They just need the morning to admit it."

The cold followed them home. Sleep did not come, and Jude did not expect it to; instead they sat with the hymnal open on their knees, reading their mother's marginal notes in the margins of old psalms — *route good, ice thin past the willows, pay the ferryman double* — instructions from a dead woman to a future she had predicted without ever meeting it.

And it was there, in the smallest hour, with the ink-dry psalms and the frost climbing the window, that Jude heard it: through the wall, faint but unmistakable, the sound of the courier next door pulling a chair to their table, uncorking an ink bottle, and beginning, very carefully, to write.

Not a report. Reports were shorter.

Jude closed the hymnal and began to plan the route past the first checkpoint — the bridge at dusk, where grief would have to serve as their passport — and did not yet know that by the following evening, the town's first betrayal would already have a face.
