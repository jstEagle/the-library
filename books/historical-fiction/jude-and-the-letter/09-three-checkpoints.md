## 9. Three Checkpoints

Beatrix had learned to read maps the way other children learned to read faces — before they could name what they were doing, before anyone thought to teach them otherwise. The diplomatic service had found them at nine years old, an orphan with steady hands and a memory that held ink the way cloth holds dye, and the first thing they were taught was that a map was never a picture of the world. It was a picture of power. Rivers mattered because armies crossed them. Roads mattered because grain moved along them. Towns like Aldenmoor did not appear at all except as a smudge of type, a name in small letters that no one in the capital could have pronounced correctly if their lives had depended on it.

Now Beatrix stood at the window of the room above the chandler's shop — Jude had insisted on moving them there after the requisition, saying the customs house quarters were "no longer restful," which was the kind of understatement that Aldenmoor seemed to breed like frost on glass — and drew the town from memory on the back of a tariff schedule.

They used a burnt match for a stylus and lamp-black thinned with a drop of oil, and what emerged was not the official survey map hanging in Vess's office but something older and truer: the town as it lived rather than as it was administered. The river came down from the north in a long grey curve, patient as a debt. The bridge crossed it where the high road entered, stone-arched, wide enough for two carts, old enough that its parapets had been rebuilt twice and both times by men who cared more about tolls than beauty. To the east, where the mill race split from the main current and turned the great wheel that ground half the province's flour, there was the mill gate — a wooden barrier, a guard hut, a lane that ran between the granary and the tanner's yard and out into the orchard country. And to the south and west, where the town simply gave up and let the land have its way, the marsh began: a mile and a half of reed, black water, alder scrub, and paths that existed only in the feet of people who had walked them for generations.

Three ways out. Three ways in. Three places where a man with a rifle and a list could stop you and ask who you were.

Beatrix sat back and looked at what they had drawn, and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from arithmetic. Because that was what this had become, whether they liked it or not. Not courage, not conviction — those were luxuries that arrived later, in memoirs, in testimony given safely across a border. What it was now, tonight, three days after the soldiers came and two days after Jude laid the plan on the table between them like a hand of cards, was numbers. Three crossings. Two couriers. One letter. And a garrison whose colonel smiled the way ice smiles on a pond that has killed better swimmers than either of them.

* * *

The bridge first, because the bridge was honest, and honesty was the rarest thing left in the province.

Beatrix walked it that morning in the grey hour before the market opened, wrapped in a coat too heavy for the season, carrying a basket of nothing in particular — a heel of bread, a twist of paper containing salt, the props of ordinariness. There was a trick to walking past a checkpoint that the service had drilled into them until it lived in the spine: walk as though you are bored. Boredom is the one emotion no one interrogates. A frightened man is interesting; a hurried man is interesting; a bored woman with a basket of salt is furniture.

There were four soldiers at the bridge now. They had doubled the watch since the convoy was announced, since word came down the wire that something important would be moving through Aldenmoor within the week — though whether the something was the relief column or the ledgers or the letter itself, even Vess perhaps did not know. The soldiers had built a low barricade of ploughshares and sandbags across the near arch, and behind it a brazier burned with the greenish smoke of wet coal, and beside it a sergeant with a ledger of his own — because everyone kept ledgers now, Beatrix thought; the whole republic had become a single vast accounting exercise conducted at gunpoint — checking names against a list.

They watched the traffic go through. A farmer with a cart of turnips, waved past with a bored wave of his own. A woman with a child, asked one question — *where from* — and answered with a jerk of her thumb toward the market. A priest, saluted, actually saluted, which told Beatrix something about the garrison's politics. And then a young man, maybe nineteen, stopped. Papers demanded. Papers produced. The sergeant reading them slowly, the soldier at the brazier drifting over to stand at the young man's shoulder, close enough that their coats touched. Thirty seconds. Forty. Then the papers handed back and the young man waved through, and he walked on across the bridge with the careful, controlled stride of someone who has just remembered how legs work.

Beatrix counted. Four seconds to be noticed. Ten to be stopped. Thirty to be questioned. Sixty, if the papers were wrong, to be taken behind the barricade to the hut, and after the hut nobody came back out the same way they went in — that much the town already knew, from the cooper's son, taken eleven days ago for a forged transit pass, and not seen since.

So: the bridge required perfect papers, and perfect papers required time Beatrix did not have, or a grief that could not be forged. You could not carry a letter across the bridge. You could only carry yourself, and hope your self was boring enough.

They turned at the far end, where the milestone said ALDENMOOR in letters worn nearly smooth, and walked back, and the sergeant looked up as they passed and looked away again, and Beatrix felt the small cold satisfaction of having been classified as furniture — and beneath it, colder still, the knowledge that furniture can be moved aside in a moment when someone decides to take inventory.

* * *

The mill gate was different. The mill gate was not honest at all, and that made it, paradoxically, more navigable — because dishonesty, unlike the bridge, had rules, and rules could be learned.

Beatrix went at midday, when the wheel was turning and the air for a hundred yards around smelled of wet grain and cut wood and the sour sweetness of mash. The gate itself was a timber barrier across the mill lane, and the guard hut beside it was smaller than the bridge post, staffed by two soldiers instead of four — but these two were not garrison regulars. Beatrix marked the difference the way they had been trained to mark everything: the wrong buttons, the boots worn at the wrong angle, the way the taller one leaned against the hut doorframe with the boneless patience of a man guarding something he did not believe in. Second-line troops. Men scraped together from somewhere the war had already chewed through once.

And second-line troops, in Beatrix's experience, were governed by a different law than regulars. Regulars followed orders. Second-line men followed appetites. The tall one was watching the miller's daughter carry a pail across the yard with an attention that had nothing to do with security. The short one was eating. Both of them waved through a boy driving a handcart of bran without so much as glancing at the cart, and Beatrix understood: the mill gate was not a wall. It was a toll. It just hadn't yet decided what currency it charged.

That evening, over the lamp in the chandler's back room, Jude confirmed it without being told. "Corporal Henn," they said, tapping the map where Beatrix had drawn the gate. "He was a drover before the war. His brother married into the Ostrow family — the Ostrows who owe my mother's people money from before I was born." Jude said it flatly, the way they said everything about their family's history, as though reciting inventory. "He'll take a bribe. He'll also remember taking it, forever, and hold it over us, forever. That's the price of the mill gate. Not money. Leverage."

"Then we don't use it for the letter."

"No." Jude's finger moved south, across the blank space on the map where Beatrix had drawn nothing, because there was nothing to draw — the marsh did not appear on any map Beatrix had ever seen, official or otherwise. "We use it for the copies. Paper that can burn. People who can't."

Beatrix looked at the finger resting on the empty space and understood what was being offered and what was being withheld. The marsh path was Jude's inheritance, the smugglers' road their mother had run in another decade, in another collapse — and Jude was willing to spend it, but not to describe it, not yet, not on paper, not even in lamplight to a courier whose loyalty was still, by every fair measure, an open question. Beatrix did not resent it. Resentment requires the expectation of trust, and Beatrix had spent thirty years learning not to expect anything from anyone that could not be stamped and countersigned.

"The ford," Beatrix said. "Tell me about the ford."

Jude was quiet for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them the chandler was stacking tallow blocks, and the sound came up through the floorboards like a slow heartbeat.

"The ford isn't guarded," Jude said finally. "That's the problem."

* * *

It took Beatrix most of the night to understand what Jude meant, and when they did, they sat down on the edge of the bed and laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a person doing sums they hate.

Because Jude was right. The bridge was guarded heavily and honestly: papers, lists, procedure. The mill gate was guarded lightly and corruptly: bribes, favors, leverage. But the marsh ford was guarded by nothing at all — no barricade, no brazier, no sergeant with a list — and it was therefore the most dangerous crossing of the three, because its guardian was the land itself, and the land did not accept lies.

Beatrix reconstructed it from fragments gathered over two days of listening: the marsh began where the town's last gardens gave out, a mile of reed beds threaded with channels that froze in hard winters and drowned men in soft ones. There was a path — everyone agreed there was a path, and no two people agreed on where — that crossed by the old alder stand and came out on the far side at a drainage dike, and from there it was open country to the partisan camps in the hills. In summer it was a walk. In winter, with the channels skimmed with ice too thin to bear a man and too thick to wade through silently, it was a slow, screaming, numbing ordeal in water to the waist, in the dark, in weather that killed stragglers. And the army knew all this, which was why they didn't guard it. Why post sentries at a place where the terrain did the killing for free? Better to let the marsh audit every traveler itself. The ones who made it across were few enough to catch on the far side, where a patrol waited at the dike — Beatrix had heard the rumor from the baker's boy, who had heard it from a cousin who drove contraband brandy: a picket, unmarked, two hundred yards beyond the waterline, checking the mud on people's boots.

So the arithmetic resolved itself, and it was grim, and Beatrix wrote it out in lamp-black on the tariff schedule because writing made things true:

*The bridge: impossible for the letter. Possible for nothing we need.*
*The mill gate: possible for the copies, at the price of a debt that never closes.*
*The ford: possible for everything, at the price of possibly everything.*

Two couriers. Three crossings. One letter that could save a town or hang them all, and a deadline that shrank every day like ice on a channel — because Vess had announced at noon, in that courteous voice of his, that the evacuation schedules would be finalized within the week, and finalized meant sealed, and sealed meant the ledgers leaving in a wagon under armed escort, and every name in Aldenmoor traveling east in a locked box while the town itself was emptied behind them.

Beatrix blew out the lamp and lay in the dark and listened to the wind come off the marsh, a low wet sound like breathing, and did what the service had trained them to do in the worst hours, which was to strip the situation down to its skeleton and look at the bones.

One lie per crossing. That was the rule they settled on, the planner's rule, the only clean thing in the whole filthy business. At the mill gate, the lie would be commerce: grain moving, favors owed, the old currency of the town's tangled debts. At the ford, the lie would be absence itself — no lie spoken, no papers shown, just two shapes in the dark that the marsh and the night would have to conspire to hide. And at the bridge —

At the bridge, Beatrix realized, staring into the black, the lie had not yet been invented. The bridge was where the letter could not go and where, sooner or later, the letter might have to go anyway, because plans were not promises and the marsh could freeze harder and the mill gate could close, and a planner who prepared only the roads they preferred was not a planner but a gambler. The bridge needed a lie strong enough to survive a sergeant with a list, and there was only one commodity Beatrix knew of that moved across that checkpoint unquestioned, that the soldiers saluted, that opened the barricade like a word of command.

Grief. A coffin. A funeral.

They turned the thought over and felt its edges and hated it, because it was good, because it would work, because somewhere in this town someone was going to die in the next week — people always died in towns in winter, in collapsing republics, in stories like this one — and Beatrix was already, in the privacy of their own skull, spending that death like coin.

This was what history did to planners, they thought. It did not ask them to be brave. It asked them to be accurate. And accuracy, laid out honestly on the page, read like cruelty: *one death, purchased; one debt, incurred; one night of freezing water; odds improving if the wind is from the north.*

Somewhere below, the chandler's dog stirred and settled. Out toward the front, faint as a pulse, the artillery spoke again — two reports, then silence, then a third, closer than yesterday. The line was coming. In a week, perhaps less, none of these checkpoints would matter, because there would be new ones, with new flags and new lists, and the ledgers would be ash or booty and the letter would be a rumor.

Which meant the arithmetic had a final term, the one Beatrix had been avoiding since Jude first spread the map between them. Not *can we cross.* That was solvable. The unsolvable term was the one underneath: *what do we owe, and to whom, and in what order* — the town, or the mission; the names, or the letter; the living, or the truth. Every crossing was also a choice about which debt to pay, and the marsh ford paid only one.

Beatrix got up, relit the lamp, and added a last line to the map in small, precise letters, the handwriting Jude had once studied so carefully across a customs counter, looking for what it carried:

*All routes converge on one question. Answer it before the ice does.*

Then they heard, below the window, in the frozen street, footsteps stopping outside the chandler's door — unhurried, deliberate, the tread of a man who wanted to be heard — and a knock, soft and exact, that was not Jude's knock.
