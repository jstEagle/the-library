## 11. Names on a List

The ledgers had been gone for six days when Colonel Vess decided to show me one.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left. Six days since the dawn requisition — since the boots on the stairs and the canvas straps and the customs house smelling of other men's tobacco — and in those six days I had filed nothing, stamped nothing, entered nothing into any column of any book. I had swept the office, because the boy who usually swept it had stopped coming, and because my hands wanted something small and stupid to do. I had answered Vess's polite questions with politer answers. I had drunk his coffee and lied into it like a woman dropping coins down a well.

And then, on the seventh morning, a corporal I did not know appeared at my door with a single ledger under his arm, bound in the grey government cloth I had handled ten thousand times, and said, "The colonel requests you verify entries. He says you have the eye for it."

He said it without irony. That was the worst part, or I thought so then.

* * *

They had set up Vess's office in the customs house's back room — the one where the tariff appeals were heard, with the tall window and the stove that never warmed properly. He had made himself at home in the way that occupying officers do, which is to say he had changed almost nothing and everything: the same desk, but angled now to face the door; the same chairs, but arranged in a half-circle for interrogation; the map of the province unrolled across the wall where the portrait of the chancellor used to hang. The portrait was gone. I noticed its absence before I noticed anything else — that pale rectangle of unfaded plaster, the ghost-shape of a frame, the nail still holding a loop of picture wire. Even the republic's face had been taken down in Aldenmoor.

"Sit, please." Vess did not look up from his writing. He was a man who made you watch him finish things — sentences, meals, wars. His pen moved in small, economical strokes. On the desk beside him lay the ledger, closed, and on the ledger lay his hand, flat and patient, as if the book might otherwise walk away.

I sat.

"You'll wonder why I've asked you here," he said.

"I hadn't wondered yet, Colonel."

That earned me the smallest pause of his pen. He looked up then, and his eyes were the color of dishwater, mild and grey and utterly without cruelty in them, which frightened me more than cruelty would have. Cruel men are legible. Vess read like a ledger himself: columns, totals, no margin notes.

"You haven't wondered," he repeated. "You're a curious creature, Fräulein Thornbury. Courier-grade discipline. Most people wonder compulsively. You've been trained not to. It's remarkable." He turned the ledger around and pushed it across the desk. "Verify the entries for the month of Frostmonth. Initial each page. If the arithmetic is sound, we needn't trouble each other further."

It was an ordinary request wearing extraordinary clothes. Verification was my trade; he knew it; he was handing me my own leash and asking me to admire the leather. I opened the book with fingers I kept deliberately steady, and the smell rose up out of it — ink and dust and the faint sweetness of the sealing wax I'd burned through years of winters — and for one merciful moment it was only work, only the familiar grid of columns, only home.

Then I turned past the tariff pages.

* * *

You should understand what I expected to find, because what I found is only meaningful against it. A requisitioning army seizes records for inventory. That is the stated purpose, the legal purpose, the purpose printed on the requisition orders themselves in the beautiful bureaucratic language that raised me: *all commercial registers, tax rolls, and census documents to be secured for audit.* Audit. The word has a clean sound, like water over stones. I had spent six days telling myself that whatever Vess wanted, it was grain tallies and duty stamps, the dry bones of a provincial economy. I had told myself this the way one tells oneself the artillery is too far away to matter — not believing it, exactly, but letting the distance stand in for belief.

The conscription schedules began on page two hundred and eleven.

I know the number because I have not been able to stop knowing it since.

They were interleaved with the ordinary business of the town as if they belonged there — which, I suppose, in the republic's arithmetic, they always had. Someone in the provincial capital had sent them down months ago, folded into the routine dispatches, and someone in Aldenmoor — Jude, I thought, with a lurch I was not ready for — had done what clerks do: copied them into the master ledger in that careful hand, filed them between the wool duties and the bridge tolls, given them the same neat headings, the same ruled lines, the same obedient beauty. That was the horror of it. The lists looked exactly like every other page I had ever loved. History does not announce itself with different handwriting. It uses ours.

*TRANSPORT SCHEDULE — ABLE-BODIED PERSONS, AGES 16 TO 45.*

And beneath the heading, name after name after name, in columns I could feel my eye trying to refuse.

* * *

I will tell you about three of them, because three is all a person can carry honestly.

Kaspar Brandt, landlord. My landlord. The man whose stairs creak in the third-floor landing and who has never once fixed them because, he says, the creak tells him which of his tenants is coming home late, and he likes to know. He owned the building through his father, who owned it through his grandfather, back past the last war, back past the war before that, a chain of ownership and obligation going down through generations of Brandts like a river through country. In the ledger's cold column his occupation was rendered as *proprietor, lodging*, and his age as fifty-one, and beside both figures, in the margin, a clerk's mark — a small, precise cross — that meant, according to the schedule's key, *designated.*

Designated. For transport. The word sat there doing its terrible quiet work. I thought of Kaspar's creaking stairs. I thought of him hearing me come home.

Then Marta Feld, baker. Her ovens start before the lamps go out; her bread is the reason half the town gets up at all in winter. She had sold me rolls on credit during the hard spring two years ago and refused repayment with a wave of flour-white hands, saying, *you'll spend it here someday, girl, the town keeps its own books.* The town keeps its own books. And now here they were, kept by someone else entirely, and in them Marta Feld was thirty-eight years old, *baker (provisioner class)*, and designated. Her son's name followed hers — Emil, sixteen, apprentice — sixteen being the first number on the schedule's ladder, the first rung a boy could climb onto a list like that.

And then — I turned the page and there he was, and I felt the floor of the world tilt by one degree, the way a ship tilts, barely, before everyone starts running — Tam Weller. The boy who sweeps my office. Fourteen years old. The schedule did not care; the schedule had an asterisk for him, some provision about *juvenile laborers, conditional designation,* as if the condition softened anything, as if fourteen could be qualified. Tam, who whistles while he sweeps, badly, the same four bars of a song he can never finish. Tam, who once spent an entire afternoon showing me how to fold a paper boat and then set it sailing down the gutter outside the customs house, and we both watched it until the rain took it apart.

His name was in the ledger. His name was in a book I had touched every day of my working life, in columns adjacent to columns I had written myself, and I had never seen it, because I had never looked, because looking was not my function, because I was raised — I understood suddenly, wholly, with a pain like a stitch pulling shut — to believe that history happened elsewhere, in capitals, in chancelleries, in the sealed rooms where paper becomes fate, and that Aldenmoor was merely a place where paper rested between decisions.

History was not elsewhere. History was page two hundred and thirteen, line nine. History was a boy with a broom.

* * *

My hand was shaking. I made it stop, because Vess was watching, and because the shaking felt like the most dangerous thing in the room.

"Everything in order?" he asked. He said it mildly, pouring himself coffee from the pot on the side table, and steam curled up between us, and I understood that the entire exercise — the verification, the courtesy, the ledger delivered like a gift — was itself an entry in some private column of his. He was measuring me. He wanted to see whether the numbers meant anything to my face.

"The arithmetic is sound," I said.

"I'm sure it is. Your office kept immaculate records." He sipped. "Immaculate records are a form of civic virtue, don't you think? Everything accounted for. Everyone located. There's a kind of honesty in it that people don't appreciate until it's absent." He turned the ledger back toward himself with one finger, gently, the way one turns a wounded bird. "These lists distress people. They shouldn't. A list is only knowledge, and knowledge is only order. The alternative—" he gestured at the window, at the grey sky beyond it, at whatever was moving toward us along the western road, "—the alternative is what's coming, and I promise you it keeps no lists at all. Or rather—" and here he smiled, thin as a paper cut, "—it keeps only one list, and everyone is on it."

"Then your lists are the mercy, Colonel."

"They are the mercy," he agreed, without a flicker of irony, and that was the moment I finally understood what kind of man I was drinking coffee with: not a monster wearing a bureaucrat's face, but a bureaucrat who had followed the logic of his own forms all the way down into the dark, and found it warm there, and called the warmth mercy. He believed. That was the thing no one had warned me about. Monsters can be lied to easily because they expect lies. Believers check the arithmetic.

"Initial the pages you verified," he said, sliding the book back. "Pages two-eleven through two-thirty. Just your mark, in the corner. Procedure."

Procedure. I picked up the pen he offered. And here I must record what I did, because this book is nothing if not honest about the smallness of the choices that make large ones possible: I initialed the pages. All twenty of them. Including page two hundred and thirteen, including the margins where Tam Weller lived. My initials went down beside his name like a signature on a sentence, and Vess watched me do it, and thanked me, and I walked out of his office on legs that belonged to someone taller, down the corridor that smelled of boot polish and old rope, out into the street, into the cold, where the afternoon light lay on the snow like the lid of something closed.

I had touched the pages. Whatever happened to them now, my hands were in the record.

* * *

I did not go home. Home was Kaspar Brandt's creaking third stair, and I could not bear the stair. I walked instead — past the bakery, where I stood in the falling dark like a fool watching Marta Feld through the window as she scored loaves with quick white crosses, marking them, marking them, not knowing she was practicing on her own family's future; past the well, where a knot of women drew water in silence; past the church, where lamplight showed gold through the frost-furred glass and someone was singing inside, four bars of something, over and over.

At the corner of Miller's Row I stopped, because a figure was waiting there in the shadow of the grain merchant's awning, and I knew the shape of the stillness before I knew the person. Jude leaned against the post with their collar up, and their eyes — I saw this even in the bad light — had the flat shine of someone who had been crying recently and had dealt with it administratively.

"You've seen it," Jude said. Not a question.

"I verified it," I said, and heard my own voice crack on the word like ice under a boot.

"Then you saw the cross-marks. The designations." Jude stepped closer, voice dropping to almost nothing, breath smoking in the cold. "There are two hundred and forty-one names on those lists, Thornbury. I copied every one of them into that ledger with my own hand, eight months ago, when it was only filing. Only paper. I told myself it was only paper." A muscle moved in their jaw. "Tonight I counted them again from memory, walking here. Two hundred and forty-one. And tomorrow Vess loads them on a train with the rest of the town's records, and after that nobody knows what's true except the men who burned the truth's house down."

"What are you saying?"

Jude looked up and down Miller's Row, empty, snowed, silent, and then said the sentence that ended the person I had been for thirty-one years:

"I'm saying we make a copy before they take the original. Tonight. You and I. I know how, and I know where, and I need hands that can write fast and small."

Somewhere behind the frosted glass of the church, the singing stopped mid-phrase.

"Come inside," Jude said. "Before the patrol rounds the corner."
