## 12. The First Copy

The hymnal had come from the church of Saint Aldric, and it had come honestly, which mattered to me more than I could explain even to myself.

I had gone to Father Ilse that afternoon with a lie so small it was nearly a truth: that the old hymnals in the back pews were falling apart, that I would take the worst of them for repair, that the parish could trust its clerk with anything, couldn't it? He had smiled at me with his whole soft face and handed me three, and I chose the one with the broken spine and water-stained cover because a book that already looked ruined would never be missed, and because there was something in me — some smuggler's daughter instinct I had spent thirty years pretending not to inherit — that understood you hide a thing inside another thing that has already been damaged. No one searches a ruin twice.

Now the hymnal lay open on my kitchen table under the lamp, its psalms facing up like a held breath, and beside it, wrapped in oilcloth, the pages I had copied from the seized conscription lists in the two hours between Vess's soldiers changing watch and the lamplighter making his rounds. Two hours. My hand still remembered the cramp of them.

Beatrix sat across from me with their sleeves rolled to the elbow, and I noticed — the way I had been noticing everything about them since the night we made our agreement in the customs house back room — how carefully they did everything. How they unrolled the oilcloth as if unwrapping a wound. How they squared the pages against the table's edge before reading a single name.

"You counted them," Beatrix said.

"Four hundred and eleven." I said it flatly, because if I said it any other way my voice would break on it. "Households marked for transport. Four hundred and eleven families out of six hundred and twelve."

Beatrix was quiet. The lamp hissed. Outside, the winter wind came down off the hills and worried at the shutters the way it always did, indifferent, ordinary, as though the world had not changed its shape while we weren't looking.

"The colonel's men read those lists aloud at muster this morning," Beatrix said finally. "By company. Like inventory."

"I know. Marta Kessler's son stood in the square and heard his own household called. He's sixteen." I turned the hymnal toward me, found the page where the sewing would begin. "He came by the clerk's office afterward and asked me — asked *me*, Beatrix, as if I were still just the man who records births and deaths — whether the lists could be wrong. Whether clerks make mistakes."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That clerks make mistakes all the time." I looked down at my hands. They were my mother's hands, broad-knuckled, ink-stained at the side of the right one where the pen dragged. "It was the first kind thing I've ever said about my profession."

Something moved across Beatrix's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way light moves under ice. Then it was gone, and they picked up their pen, and we began.

* * *

You cannot hurry copying. That is the first thing, and the thing that nearly drove us both mad that night. Every name had to be written into the hymnal's margins in a hand small enough to escape notice, in ink pale enough to pass for watermark, in a script that mimicked the printer's ornaments so that a casual eye would see only decoration. My mother had taught me a version of this hand when I was nine years old, sitting on a flour sack in the back of her smuggling cart, practicing letters she told me were for keeping accounts. She never told me what accounts. I understood now. I understood everything now, too late and exactly on time.

Beatrix read; I wrote. We had tried it the other way first, but Beatrix's handwriting — that beautiful, careful, diplomatic hand I had once admired across a customs counter — was too recognizable. A courier's hand is trained into them the way a soldier's stance is trained into the body. Mine was a clerk's hand, which meant it was nobody's hand, which meant it was everyone's, which meant it was safe.

"Hollis Ferren," Beatrix read. "Miller. Household of five. Marked for transport east."

I wrote it in the margin beside Psalm 91, the one about the fowler's snare. I did not choose the psalms; the names fell where they fell. But God forgive me, I noticed. Hollis Ferren went down beside the promise of deliverance, and I wrote him small and pale and true.

"The Ansel family," Beatrix continued. "Seven. The grandmother is marked infirm."

The pen stopped in my fingers.

"Infirm," I repeated.

"That's what the list says."

"They're going to transport a woman who cannot walk."

Beatrix set down their own pen. For a moment neither of us said anything, and the lamp filled the silence with its small burning sound, and somewhere outside a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"We copy the names," Beatrix said at last, and their voice had the flatness I was learning to hear as their form of grief. "That's what we can do tonight. Tonight we copy names."

So I wrote the Ansel family, all seven, and put a small mark beside the grandmother's name that no one else would understand, and hated myself a little for the mark, because a mark is not mercy, a mark is just another kind of ledger entry, and I had sworn when I took the clerk's office that I would never again be only a man who wrote things down while other men did things.

But I wrote it anyway. You write it anyway. That is the second thing about copying: it teaches you the size of your own hands.

* * *

We worked past midnight. The candle burned down through its inches like a clock with a wick. Beatrix read names in a low, level voice — the baker Aldous Renn, whose bread I had eaten every morning for fifteen years; the Widow Sarn, who paid her taxes in eggs because it was all she had; Tam Wicke, the boy who swept the customs office floor, fourteen years old, listed under *laborers, able-bodied* — and each name went into the hymnal the way seeds go into frozen ground, without hope of quick return, in faith of spring or nothing.

Somewhere in the second hour Beatrix began to falter. Not the voice — the voice stayed level — but the pauses lengthened. I glanced up and saw them pressing the heel of their hand against their eye.

"Renn," they said. "Aldous Renn. He used to leave the burnt loaf on the counter for whoever came last. He said burnt bread builds character." A breath. "I bought the burnt loaf every day for a month when I first arrived here. I thought he didn't notice. He noticed. He knew my schedule before the customs service did."

"That's bakers," I said. "They know more than governments. It's the flour dust. Gets into everything."

Beatrix laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, as if laughter itself might be requisitioned. But it loosened something. They read the next ten names faster, steadier, and when they reached the end of the transport pages they sat back and flexed their fingers and looked at the hymnal the way a mason looks at a wall just completed.

"How many?" they asked.

I counted the margins. "All four hundred and eleven households. Plus the annotations — ages, infirmities, the special marks Vess's officers made on twenty-six names. Do you know what the special marks mean?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That frightens me more than the rest of it."

Beatrix nodded slowly. Then they did something I did not expect. They reached across the table and touched the hymnal's cover — just touched it, two fingers, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you hoped wouldn't startle.

"When I was trained," they said, "they taught us that paper is the most honest material in the world. Ink doesn't flatter. A record is a record. I believed it for twenty years." Their fingers curled away from the cover. "Then I watched Vess's clerks turn our ledgers into a weapon in an afternoon. Same paper. Same ink. Same honesty. And it cut four hundred ways by lunchtime."

"So paper isn't honest."

"Paper is obedient." Beatrix met my eyes across the lamp. "That's worse. That means what it becomes depends entirely on whose hand holds the pen."

I looked down at my own hand, ink-stained, trembling slightly, holding a pen over a psalm book full of stolen names. My mother's hand. The smuggler's hand. The clerk's hand. All of them mine, all of them obedient to something, and for the first time in my life I had chosen what.

"Well," I said. "Then let's be careful whose hand it is."

* * *

The knock came at half past two in the morning.

Three raps, spaced evenly, unhurried — and that unhurriedness was the worst part, because soldiers knock fast and neighbors knock soft, and this was neither. This was the knock of someone who knew the door would be answered regardless.

The pen jumped in my fingers and laid a blot across Psalm 119 like a wound. Beatrix went absolutely still — not frozen the way civilians freeze, but still the way I imagined a courier was trained to be still, every part of them suddenly assigned to silence. Their eyes went to the hymnal. To the oilcloth. To the window, where the shutter gaps showed nothing but dark.

"Back room," I breathed. "Under the floorboard. Go."

They gathered the hymnal against their chest and moved — fast, soundless, through the doorway into the back room where I kept the parish records, and I heard the single soft scrape of the loose board, and then nothing, and then it was just me and the knock and the blot of ink drying on the word *refuge*.

I blew out the lamp. The dark rushed in. I gave it the count of five — my mother's rule, five counts before answering any door after midnight, long enough to arrange your face — and opened the door with the chain still on.

Sergeant Brakke stood on my step with snow gathering on his shoulders.

Brakke. Of all of them, Brakke — the garrison sergeant with the bad knee and the habit of buying sausage from Frau Ott on credit he never settled, the one who had stood guard at the customs house looking bored rather than menacing, the one I had catalogued in my head weeks ago as *harmless* and now understood, seeing him framed in my doorway at two in the morning, that no man wearing that uniform was harmless, that harmlessness was itself a uniform some of them wore underneath the other one.

"Clerk," he said. His breath smoked. "Sorry for the hour."

"What is it, Sergeant?"

"Colonel Vess wants the census supplements. The household annotations. He says the ones from the eastern parishes are missing from the seizure." Brakke's eyes moved past my shoulder into the dark of the house, patient, professional. "He believes you have copies. Clerks keep copies. It's in your nature."

My heart was going so hard I could feel it in my teeth. Behind me, under a floorboard eight feet away, a hymnal held four hundred and eleven lives, and the man who could find it stood close enough that I could smell the woodsmoke on his coat.

"The eastern parishes," I said, and made my voice bored, made it clerkish, made it the voice of a tired man interrupted at his accounting. "Those went to the provincial archive in Marrow. Standard procedure — duplicate sets. I filed the transfer receipt in October. I can show you the entry tomorrow."

"He'd like them tonight."

"At two in the morning I'd like a great many things, Sergeant, none of which either of us will get." I let irritation do my lying for me; it was easier than fear, and harder to detect. "If Colonel Vess wants archive transfers verified, he can want it at business hours like everyone else in this town. I've served this district for nineteen years. My filings have survived three administrations. They'll survive his inspection."

Brakke studied me. Snow ticked softly onto the step between us. And then — I will write this plainly, because it matters — something shifted in his face, something almost like relief, as if he had been hoping to be refused.

"Business hours," he repeated. "Right. I'll tell him." He turned, then paused, his back to me, his voice dropping to something that wasn't quite a soldier's voice anymore. "Clerk. The boy who sweeps your office. Tam Wicke. His mother's been asking after the lists. Asking everyone." He didn't turn around. "People ask me things. I don't much like the answers I'd have to give."

And he walked away down the white street, favoring his bad knee, and I closed the door and stood with my forehead against the cold wood and shook — shook properly, the delayed shaking, the body settling its account after the mind has signed the receipt.

When I lifted the floorboard, Beatrix was looking up at me from the dark beneath it, the hymnal in their arms, their face pale as paper.

"He knows about copies," they whispered.

"He suspects. There's a difference." I helped them out. "In my trade we live on that difference."

Beatrix brushed the dust from their sleeves and looked at the hymnal, and then at me, and the terror in their face had braided itself with something else — purpose, resolve, whatever you call the thing that keeps a hand writing when the hand wants to run.

"Vess wants the annotations missing from the seizure," they said slowly. "Which means he's already auditing. Already counting what he took against what should exist." They swallowed. "Jude — if he's counting now, at two in the morning, then he's found a discrepancy somewhere. And discrepancies don't stay suspicions for long. Not with him."

Outside, far off toward the bridge checkpoint, a lantern swung in the dark — once, twice, steady as a pulse — and the town held its breath around us, six hundred households deep, four hundred and eleven of them already written into a psalm book, waiting for morning to find out what the colonel's arithmetic would cost.
