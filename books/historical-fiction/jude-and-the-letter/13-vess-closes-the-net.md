## 13. Vess Closes the Net

Morning came to Aldenmoor the color of dishwater, and with it came the drums.

Not the artillery drums of the front — those had a texture Beatrix had learned in their first week, a rolling bruise of sound that lived in the teeth and the breastbone. These were ceremonial: the long, deliberate rattle of a garrison announcing an assembly. They fell down the high street in measured waves, paused at the well, and began again, so that by the time Beatrix had laced their boots and looked once at the hymnal's hiding place beneath the loose hearthstone — looked, only looked, not touched, because touching was a habit that could be observed — every soul in the town knew that Colonel Vess wished to speak.

Beatrix walked to the customs house through a street gone strange with silence. Smoke curled from chimneys because smoke always did, but no one stood in doorways pretending to sweep. The baker's boy was not sweeping the steps of the bakery. Old Marta had not set out her winter turnips. The town had drawn itself inward like a hand closing, and Beatrix moved through the space where its fingers had been.

Two soldiers flanked the customs house door. Inside, the great hall had been rearranged overnight. The counting tables where clerks had bent over tariff ledgers for forty years were pushed against the walls, and in the center of the floor stood a single table bearing a single chair, and behind the table, upright and unhurried as a man waiting for a coach that would arrive precisely when he intended it to, stood Colonel Vess.

He had a voice built for this, Beatrix realized. Not loud. Carrying. The voice of a man who had never once needed to repeat himself.

"Householders of Aldenmoor," he said, when the last of them had pressed inside or found places at the windows, "and registered residents of the customs district." He let the phrase sit. *Registered.* As if the town had signed something. In a sense, Beatrix thought, it had — in ledgers with Vess's seals already drying on their spines. "You will have heard the guns to the east. You will have heard many things. I am here to tell you what is true."

The hall smelled of cold ink and lamp oil and, faintly, of the river mud that came up through the floorboards every winter and had never been conquered. Beatrix stood near the back, half-sheltered by the coat rack, hands folded like a person with nothing in their pockets but lint.

"The front is moving," Vess said. "It will pass through this region. That is arithmetic, not opinion." He did not gesture toward the east; he didn't need to. Everyone in the room could feel the direction of the guns the way one feels weather in an old scar. "The army has determined that Aldenmoor cannot be defended, and therefore will not be defended. What can be defended — what *will* be defended — is the orderly administration of this district until such time as the relocation is complete."

*Relocation.* The word went through the assembled faces like wind through wheat. Beatrix watched the miller's wife's mouth form it silently, testing its weight, finding it heavier than any word she owned.

"You will be evacuated," Vess continued, and now he did move, turning his head slowly so that his gaze crossed the crowd in a surveyor's line, "in an organized fashion, beginning in three days' time. Three days. You will take what you can carry. You will leave livestock, grain stores, and all written records in place. All documents — deeds, contracts, correspondence, parish registers, private letters — are subject to requisition under the emergency statutes, and have been since my arrival. Any document found concealed after this morning," and here his voice dropped half a register, not louder but denser, "will be treated as contraband, and its holder as a smuggler. The penalty for smuggling in a military zone is not a matter for civil courts. There are no civil courts."

Somewhere near the front of the hall a child began to cry, softly, the way children cry when they have learned already not to cry loudly, and Beatrix felt the sound go through them like a splinter working toward the bone.

Three days.

The number landed in Beatrix's chest and began, immediately, to do its work — to tick. That was the thing about deadlines that no one warned you about when your whole life had been made of paper and paper's comfortable illusions: a deadline was not information. It was a creature. Once it was spoken aloud it climbed inside you and started counting whether you consented or not, and it counted in Jude's handwriting, in four hundred and eleven names copied into a psalm book by lamplight, in the letter sealed in oilcloth whose route Beatrix alone among the people in this room knew.

Three days until evacuation. Which meant three days minus however long it took Vess to load wagons, which meant less than three days. And the ledgers —

"I will be direct with you," Vess said, "because I find directness saves everyone a great deal of wear." He placed both hands flat on the table, and the small motion silenced the hall more completely than shouting would have. "The district archives travel with me. Every ledger, every register, every record of who holds title, who owes debt, who was born, who married, and who died in this town, will be conveyed to regional command for consolidation. This is not confiscation. Your records will be preserved. Paper survives war better than people do, if it is kept properly." A thin pause, calibrated. "I keep paper properly."

*Every ledger.*

Beatrix's mind performed its treacherous, fluent arithmetic without being asked. The conscription lists were in the seized ledgers. The letter of safe conduct — the original, the republic's signature still damp after all these years of neglect — lay in the diplomatic pouch in the strongroom that Vess's men had inventoried down to the brass fittings. If the ledgers traveled with him and the pouch traveled with him, then everything that could save Aldenmoor's families or hang its courier would ride east in the same wagon, under the same guard, into whatever command structure emerged from the collapse — and commands in collapsing armies did not consolidate records out of sentiment. They consolidated them to know whom to question. Whom to transport. Whom to shoot at the roadside as an example to the rest of the column.

And the copies — the hymnal, four hundred and eleven names in a false psalm book beneath a hearthstone — would buy nothing if the originals left in Vess's baggage. Copies proved nothing without something to copy from. The partisans across the marsh wanted the original letter too, and their price was steep, and there were now exactly seventy-two hours in which steep prices could be paid.

Vess was still speaking. Beatrix dragged their attention back by force.

"— questions regarding property claims may be submitted in writing to the office of the district administrator, that is, to this building, second window, between the hours of nine and noon." A dry almost-smile crossed his face and was gone, a crack of ice forming and closing on a frozen pond. "I encourage you to submit them. Documentation, ladies and gentlemen, is the only form of immortality I have ever found to be reliable. People die. Names outlast them — provided someone keeps the books."

He stepped back from the table. The assembly, unsure whether dismissal required permission, stood in the terrible collective hesitation of people waiting to be told how to leave, and Beatrix used the moment to look — really look — at what stood behind Vess, along the eastern wall where the tariff maps used to hang.

Crates. New pine, iron-banded, stenciled with characters Beatrix recognized from a decade of dispatch work: *ARCHIVE, DISTRICT 9, PRIORITY CONSOLIDATION.* There were eleven of them. Eleven crates, each perhaps holding forty ledgers, and Beatrix had spent two years filing in this room and knew to the shelf how many volumes the town's memory amounted to. Four hundred and forty-odd books. Eleven crates. The arithmetic was exact, which meant the packing had already begun, which meant the three days Vess had granted the town's families had not been granted to the ledgers at all. The ledgers were already leaving. The three days were for the people — for the slow business of six hundred households deciding what a life weighed when reduced to what arms could carry.

Then the hall emptied, and the emptying took an eternity, and Beatrix let themselves be carried out with it, anonymous in the crowd, a courier with no commission anyone could name, walking home through streets where doors were already opening again because fear, like water, seeks its level and resumes its habits.

* * *

The strongroom request was a gamble, and Beatrix knew it, and made it anyway before noon.

"Personal effects," they told the sergeant at the requisition desk, laying their courier's credentials on the wood with studied boredom. "Dispatch case, seal ring, a change of linen. My relief convoy is expected within the week; I'll want my things ready."

The sergeant — young, chapped hands, the particular exhaustion of a boy given responsibility shaped like a rifle — turned the credentials over twice. Beatrix watched him read the numbers and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the numbers meant nothing to him and everything to whichever officer eventually checked them, and that between those two facts lay a corridor just wide enough for a lie.

"Courier materials stay in the strongroom," the sergeant said finally. "Colonel's order. Personal items — linen, that's fine, I can have Hessler fetch—" He stopped, frowned at a list pinned under his elbow, and Beatrix saw the moment the boy found the line item. "Your dispatch case was logged into priority consolidation yesterday. Crate seven."

"Crate seven," Beatrix repeated.

"It's all in the manifest." The sergeant said it defensively, as though Beatrix had accused him of theft, and perhaps in the grammar of soldiers he had reason to feel accused; a courier's case was not linen. "Everything's packed according to the manifest. Nothing comes out. Nothing goes in. The colonel signs the manifest personally when the wagons roll."

*Nothing goes in.*

So that door was closed, and closed deliberately — not against the town, Vess hardly needed to seal papers from farmers. Against couriers. Against the possibility that the man who had carried the pouch might want, in these final days, to add something to it or take something back. Vess had thought of it. Vess thought in manifests.

Beatrix thanked the sergeant with a nod that cost more effort than any performance of their career, collected nothing, and walked out past crate seven, which sat third from the wall with its stenciled lid nailed down and its rope handles waiting. From the outside it looked identical to its ten brothers. Beatrix committed its position to memory anyway — third from the wall, second row, the corner nearest the window with the broken latch — because a lifetime in the diplomatic service had taught them that when a legal door closed, you memorized the room. Not to force it. To know, later, exactly what had been taken and where it had gone. That was the creed they'd been raised on: paper outlasts people, and the witness outlasts the paper.

But the creed had a hole in it now, and the hole was shaped like three days.

* * *

They found Jude in the churchyard, ostensibly trimming the yew hedge, actually standing very still beside the south wall with secateurs hanging forgotten from one hand. The churchyard was the only public ground in Aldenmoor where two people could speak without a soldier in earshot; the army had no theology, and had posted no sentries among the graves.

"Don't trim that branch," Beatrix said quietly, coming to stand at the hedge. "Trim the one lower. It gives us a reason to face each other."

Jude moved the secateurs obediently. Their eyes were red-rimmed, whether from sleeplessness or something else Beatrix couldn't tell and didn't ask. "You heard him."

"The whole town heard him."

"Three days." Jude cut a sprig that didn't need cutting. "My mother used to say the worst lies come with numbers attached, because numbers make you feel like you've been given something. Three days. As if it were a gift."

"It's worse than a gift. I made inquiries at the requisition desk." Beatrix kept their voice level, their eyes on the hedge, their posture that of two neighbors discussing topiary. "The ledgers are already crated. Eleven crates, pine, iron bands, marked priority consolidation. The manifest is signed by Vess personally when the wagons roll. And my dispatch case — the pouch, Jude — is in crate seven."

The secateurs stopped.

"Which means," Beatrix continued, each word laid down like a stone across a stream, "that everything we're doing has a clock on it now, and the clock isn't three days. The wagons will be loaded the night before, because that's how it's done — you stage the transport ahead of the announcement, you don't announce and then pack. So subtract a day. And the marsh crossing takes a night, and the ford is guarded, and the partisans drive their bargains slowly. Subtract another. We are not working with three days. We are working with one, maybe two, and every hour Vess spends auditing brings him closer to the discrepancy he already suspects."

"And if we miss it." Jude's voice was flat, not a question, a plank being walked.

"If we miss it, the names ride east in crate after crate, and somewhere downstream a new authority reads them aloud and starts making lists of its own. Conscription lists become transport lists become — " Beatrix stopped. The end of the sentence had a shape they refused to give it in a churchyard. "And the letter rides with it. Unsigned-for, undelivered, dead paper in a dead republic's baggage train. The amnesty never spoken, the promise never kept even in the breach. Nothing."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere beyond the wall a cart rattled past loaded with furniture, a family leaving early on their own recognizance, defying nobody, saving nobody, simply going. The wheels cried on the frozen ruts. Beatrix listened to the sound diminish and thought: *that will be the whole town, soon. That sound, multiplied by six hundred.*

"There's something else," they said. "Something I didn't put into words until I was standing at that desk watching that boy read my credentials." They turned then, finally, and looked at Jude directly, because some things owed the dignity of a face. "Vess didn't close the strongroom against the town. He closed it against *me*. Nothing goes in, nothing comes out, manifest signed by his own hand. He is building a box, Jude, and he has been building it since the day he arrived, and I have begun to suspect the box has my name stenciled on it alongside the archive marks. He invited me to tea once and asked me questions that weren't questions. He audits at two in the morning. Now he packs my own case into his luggage. A man doesn't pack what he intends to leave behind."

Jude's jaw tightened. "Then the crossing can't wait for perfect conditions. My mother never waited for perfect conditions. She waited for *sufficiently* bad ones — bad enough that the guards were miserable and looking at their own feet." The old smuggler's arithmetic surfaced in their voice like a vein of ore. "The bridge first. Tomorrow at dusk there's a coffin going over — Widow Kessel's son, God rest him, died honest and needs burying in the parish across the water, and grief makes better cover than darkness because grief gets waved through. After that the mill gate, after that the ford. Three checkpoints, three nights, three different lies."

"And if one of the lies fails?"

"Then we learn which checkpoint is hardest," Jude said, "with whatever it costs to learn it."

Beatrix looked at them — at the red-rimmed eyes and the steady hands and the family inheritance of courage and shame braided together in one small stubborn clerk — and felt the deadline ticking in their chest like a second heart, and understood suddenly that time itself had changed sides. For years, time had been their colleague: time to file, time to wait for convoys, time for paper to make its slow patient way toward meaning. Now time was Vess's instrument, wound and ticking in eleven pine crates, and every hour it stole was stolen equally from the four hundred and eleven names in the psalm book and from whoever Beatrix Thornbury had believed themselves to be.

"One night, then," they said. "The bridge tomorrow. And Jude — " they hesitated, hating the hesitation, hearing in it the orphan's old reflex of bracing for loss, "— whatever that coffin carries across that bridge tomorrow, carry it as if it were heavy. Guards watch how you grieve. They always do."

Jude looked at the hedge, at the church wall, at some point beyond both where the marsh lay waiting under its skin of ice.

"It will be heavy," Jude said. "That's the part no one warns you about. Everything you smuggle weighs exactly as much as what it's worth."

Beyond the churchyard, far off toward the east where the guns lived, the afternoon light was already failing, and somewhere in the customs house a man with a manifest and a pen was crossing items off a list, one steady line at a time, and each line he struck was an hour off the world.
