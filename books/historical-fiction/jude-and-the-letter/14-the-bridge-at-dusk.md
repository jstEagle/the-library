## 14. The Bridge at Dusk

Old Marta Brenner had been dead for two days before anyone thought to make use of it.

Jude stood in the undertaker's back room while Halvick worked the screws of the coffin lid, and tried not to think of her as anything other than what she was: a widow of seventy-one years, mother of three, grandmother of none — the children gone east in the last famine year and never written back — a woman who had once thrown a boot at Jude's head for climbing through her plum tree, and missed on purpose, and told them so afterward with a wink that smelled of brandy. That was the woman. The thing under the shroud was only the house she had lived in.

"The soldiers stopped the Kessler funeral this morning," Halvick said. He did not look up from his work. His hands were steady; he was a man who had made his peace long ago with the fact that his trade served the living more often than the dead. "Turned the whole procession back. Made the family unload the box at the bridge and open it, right there on the planking. In front of God and the garrison both."

"And you still agreed to do this."

"I agreed," Halvick said, "because they'll be less likely to stop the next one. Armies are like that. They check the first box and then they trust their own thoroughness." He set the last screw and sat back on his heels. "It's a terrible thing I'm doing for you, clerk. I want you to know that I know it."

"It's a terrible thing I'm asking."

"Yes. But it's mine to do, not yours, which is how these things are always arranged." He wiped his hands on his apron, slow and deliberate, as though he could wipe away something more than sawdust. "She'd have done it, you know. Marta. She hated them since they took her hens in October. She'd have ridden across that bridge in her own coffin blowing raspberries at the sentries if the Lord had let her."

Jude almost laughed, and the laugh turned into something else in their throat, and for a moment the back room was very quiet — just the sound of the river under the floorboards, going about its business, carrying ice toward the sea.

* * *

The plan had been Beatrix's first, and Jude had reshaped it, because Beatrix's plans were made by someone who had crossed borders with sealed pouches and false bottoms in trunks, someone who thought in terms of routes and diversions, and the bridge at Aldenmoor did not answer to routes and diversions. It answered to grief. There were three crossings in the town and each one demanded its own currency: the mill gate ran on paper and old favors, the marsh ford on nerve and silence. The bridge ran on mourning. Nobody in the history of armies, Jude suspected, had ever found a way to search a funeral thoroughly without feeling that they had done something their mothers would have been ashamed of.

So: a genuine death, a genuine coffin, genuine mourners. Nothing forged, nothing invented. Only one lie, laid flat beneath the truth like a knife under a hymnbook.

The hymnal itself sat in Jude's satchel now, wrapped in oilcloth, and Jude could feel its weight against their hip the way one feels a bruise — not constantly, but whenever anything touched it. Forty-one names copied out in Beatrix's careful courier's hand and Jude's own clerk's hand, page after page lifted from the conscription lists two nights ago by lamplight while the wind worried the shutters and both of them flinched at every sound. The boy who swept the customs office. The baker's eldest daughter. Widow Brenner's own grand-nephew, which was a cruelty Jude had discovered only midway through copying and had carried ever since like a stone swallowed whole.

They had chosen a hymnal deliberately. Halvick's wife sang in the choir at Saint Aldhelm's; the book was hers, its spine already cracked from decades of use, its margins soft with thumbprints. If a soldier opened it at all, he would see what he expected to see: *Abide With Me*, *Now the Day Is Over*, the doleful standards of provincial funerals. The names were written between the lines of the hymns themselves, small and slanted, disguised as pen trials, as boredom, as the idle marks of a choir member waiting out a long sermon. It had taken Beatrix an hour to understand the trick of it when Jude showed them. "You've hidden a census inside a prayer," Beatrix had said, and there was something in their voice Jude couldn't name — awe or horror, or the place where those two become indistinguishable.

*It's not hidden,* Jude had answered. *It's where no one looks. There's a difference.*

* * *

By four o'clock the light had gone the color of weak tea, and the mourners began to gather outside Brenner house. There were nine of them, which was more than old Marta deserved on an honest day and fewer than the town would have sent if it weren't afraid to stand in the open. Jude knew every face. That was the strange arithmetic of Aldenmoor — you could count your conspirators the way you counted your neighbors, because they were the same list. Tam Ossler the wheelwright, who owed the army nothing and the Brenners everything. The Pell sisters, who wept easily and therefore genuinely. Young Corvin Brenner, the grand-nephew, sixteen and hollow-eyed, who had come down from the hills that morning when word reached him, and who was himself — though he did not know it — a name in the book riding above his great-aunt's knees.

Six pallbearers. Jude walked at the rear, in a borrowed black coat that smelled of camphor and another man's life, carrying nothing but a satchel and the weight of what was in it.

Dusk came early in winter, and dusk was the point. A body must be buried before full dark, by custom and by church law, and even Colonel Vess's men knew that funerals kept their own schedule, older than any requisition order. They carried her down the lane past the shuttered chandlery, past the well where last summer the women had gathered to trade complaints, and turned onto the causeway road, and there it was.

The bridge.

It had stood over the Alden for two hundred years, five arches of gray stone furred with moss at the waterline, and Jude had crossed it perhaps ten thousand times without once considering it as a thing that could refuse them. Now it had a barricade of planks and barrels at the near end, a lantern burning behind it, and two sentries with rifles slung, stamping their feet against the cold. Beyond them, at the far end, the third sentry stood apart, and Jude marked him and dismissed him in the same breath: far side, tired, half-facing upriver. It was the near pair that mattered.

"Funeral party," Halvick announced, before anyone asked. He held up the burial permit — genuine, signed by the town registrar before the occupation, stamped twice. "Brenner, Marta Elisabeth. Saint Aldhelm's yard. We're late already and the reverend will have our hides."

The nearer sentry was young — nineteen, twenty, cheeks raw with cold, the kind of face that hadn't finished deciding what it believed in. He looked at the permit. He looked at the coffin. And then, because he was young and thorough and had probably been yelled at that morning for letting the Kessler party through unsearched, he said the words Jude had spent all afternoon preparing not to hear.

"Open it."

* * *

Later — much later, lying awake in the dark of the clerk's office with the ledgers stacked around them like sleeping animals — Jude would try to remember what they had felt in that moment, and find only a white absence, a held breath of the soul. But in the moment itself there was no feeling at all. There was only training. Twenty years of standing in front of angry people with documents, of keeping one's voice level while a farmer explained what he'd do to the tax man's horse. The body knew how to do this. The body had been built for exactly this.

"You'll not open her," Jude said quietly.

The sentry's eyes came up.

"She died of the lung fever," Jude went on, in the same voice — the voice used for reading out assessments, gentle as a landslide. "Three days dying, and the last day it was bad. You'll have smelled such a thing before, or you will someday." A pause, measured to the heartbeat. "Her family is standing here. Her grand-nephew came down from the hills to bury her. So if your orders say you must look inside a coffin, then say so plain to Corvin Brenner's face, and we'll lift the lid together, and afterward every soul in this town will know what was done at the bridge, and whose name the colonel will give them for it."

Not a threat, formally. Not one raised voice, not one wrong word. Just the arithmetic laid on the table: one name, one shame, multiplied by every relative and gossip in a town of eleven hundred people. Jude watched the calculation move behind the boy's eyes like weather.

The second sentry — older, stubbled, a wedding band dull on his glove — shifted his weight. "Kessler this morning," he said to the young one, meaninglessly, the way men narrate their doubts.

"He's right," the young sentry said finally, to nobody, to the permit, to the dark coming down off the hills. "It's fever. You can smell it on a whole house, my mother said—" He stopped, caught himself, waved them through with a jerk of his chin that wanted badly to seem bored. "Go on, then. Quickly."

Halvick took the permit back with a nod of funereal thanks. The bearers lifted their burden. The wheels of the handcart creaked. And Jude walked out onto the bridge at dusk with forty-one names riding six feet behind, and felt the guilt arrive precisely on schedule, the way pain comes after the wound.

Because that was the thing about it, the thing no one warned you: it worked. Grief had opened the crossing the way a key opens a lock, and the key had been real, and the lock had been a boy's decency, and Jude had used both without spending a single coin of their own. Marta Brenner, who had missed on purpose with the boot and winked about it, had crossed the bridge one last time as a weapon. Somewhere ahead the coffin swayed gently on the shoulders of six neighbors, and the river ran dark beneath the arches, and Jude thought: *she would forgive me.* And thought, immediately and more honestly: *but I haven't asked her.*

* * *

Halfway across, the young sentry called out again.

Jude's heart performed a single enormous contraction and went still.

"Hey. Clerk."

They turned. The others kept walking — trained to, thank God, trained by funerals to keep moving — and Jude stood alone on the middle of the span with the water talking underneath.

"You're the town clerk," the boy said. "You were in the customs house. When they — before." He seemed to be struggling with why he had called out, having got whatever he wanted from calling out. "The names. In the ledgers. Are they — I mean, does everyone get listed? Even people who didn't do anything?"

The question hung over the black water. Jude looked at him — at the raw cheeks, the rifle held wrong, the terrible youth of him — and understood that this was not a test. It was worse. It was a confession looking for absolution from the nearest available official.

"Some lists are punishments," Jude said carefully. "Some are just... inventories. What happens to the people on them depends on who's holding the paper when the war ends."

"But if someone wasn't on it. If a name wasn't written down anywhere." He wasn't looking at Jude now; he was looking at his own boots. "Then it'd be like they weren't there at all. To the army, I mean. For transport."

The cold came up through the stones and into Jude's bones, and somewhere behind them, faint on the wind, the Pell sisters had begun to sing — low, ragged, *Abide With Me*, carrying Marta Brenner up the far bank toward consecrated ground.

"No," Jude heard themself say. "If a name isn't written down, no paper can take it."

The boy nodded, once, as if a debt had been settled in some direction neither of them would ever name aloud, and turned back to his post, and Jude walked on across the rest of the bridge with their blood ringing in their ears — because they had just lied to a soldier, or prophesied to him, or promised him something, and could not yet tell which.

* * *

The burying was quick and honest. Reverend Lassiter, who knew nothing and was better for it, spoke the words over the grave while the lanterns guttered, and the ground received Marta Elisabeth Brenner with all the ordinary ceremony the century allowed, and the mourners filed home in ones and twos along the dark lanes. Jude helped fill the grave — refused the shovel twice, accepted it the third time, and put their back into it, and found that the physical labor eased something that thinking could not reach. Earth on earth. That part at least was true all the way down.

Halvick lingered at the gate as the others drifted off.

"Well?" he said.

"Gone across. No one looked." Jude shouldered the empty satchel — the hymnal had traveled under the false bottom of the coffin's lining, wedged against the plank above Marta's shoulder, and now it waited in Halvick's cart among the spades, bound for the church wall come morning, thence to the partisan camp by means Jude preferred not to examine too closely in advance. "You did well."

"Don't." Halvick held up a hand. "Don't tell me I did well. I put a book above a dead woman's head and used her funeral as a toll ticket, and it worked, and I'll answer for it however I answer for it. You go on." He glanced back at the fresh mound, the leaning marker, the dark. Then, quieter, in a different voice entirely: "There's something else. Since you're here and it's dark and I'd rather say it once."

Jude waited.

"That fellow who's been hanging round the colonel's office. The one from the provisioner's — Wender, the one who translates the requisition orders for the outlying farms." Halvick's breath smoked in the cold. "My wife sings with his sister at Saint Aldhelm's. The sister says Wender's had money lately. New boots, money. And that he was seen coming out of the smokehouse lane last night at an hour no honest man keeps."

The name went into Jude like a splinter driven deep — Wender, Wender — turning over, fitting itself against other things. The checkpoints' uncanny readiness. Vess's questions arriving a half-day before they should have. Small discrepancies, none damning alone.

"Thank you," Jude said.

"Don't thank me. Just — whatever it is you and the courier are doing, do it faster." Halvick picked up his lantern. "Because if one man can sell you, another one can."
