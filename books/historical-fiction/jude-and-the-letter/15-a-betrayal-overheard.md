## 15. A Betrayal Overheard

The customs house had learned a new sound. For eleven years Beatrix had worked within its walls and known them as a kind of silence — the hush of paper, the tick of the long-case clock in the assay room, the scratch of nibs at dusk when the day's tariffs were tallied. Now the building breathed like a barracks. Boots on the stairs at all hours. The wet rattle of the stove pipes, stoked too hot by men who did not care what fuel they burned. And beneath it all, always, the murmur of Colonel Vess's office, where the door stayed shut and the shutters stayed drawn and the lamplight leaked through the slats like something the room was trying to keep.

Beatrix had been summoned there twice since the requisition, and both times had come away with the sensation of having been weighed — not their words but their hands, their eyes, the particular steadiness with which they accepted tea they did not want. Vess never asked twice. He asked once, beautifully, and then he watched you carry the question around for days to see where you set it down.

On the fourth evening after Jude's plan had been agreed to — four evenings of carrying the hymnal's existence inside them like a swallowed coal — Beatrix was sent back to the customs house after supper to fetch the duplicate tariff schedules from the reading room. A trivial errand. Captain Oster, who now commanded the ground floor, had waved them through without looking up from his cards. The soldiers had gone to their mess; the corridors stood empty and blue with dusk, smelling of boot blacking and cold stone and, faintly, of the river that ran behind the building like a held breath.

Beatrix meant only to take the schedules and go. But the reading room adjoined Vess's office through a partition — a thin one, put up decades ago by some frugal magistrate, plastered over lath and hair — and when Beatrix stepped inside and lit no lamp, preferring the last grey light from the river windows, they heard the voices through the wall as clearly as if the partition had been woven from reeds.

One voice was Vess's clerk. His name, Beatrix had learned, was Pell. A small man with ink-stained cuffs and a cough he kept folded in his sleeve like a letter he meant to send later. He had the fussy competence of a man who had survived three ministries by knowing where everything was filed, including other people's sins.

The second voice was not a soldier's.

* * *

Beatrix did not move. That was the first discipline of their training, older than any oath: when the paper begins to whisper, stand still and let it finish. They set the tariff schedules back on the shelf unopened and stood between the window and the wall with their hands loose at their sides, and listened.

"—the bridge list is fixed for now," Pell was saying, and his voice had the oiled ease of a man performing a transaction he had already forgiven himself for. "Two sentries by day, one by night after the tenth bell. The mill gate rotates on the even hours — that's army habit, nothing to do with me. The marsh ford they think is impassable. They're wrong, but I haven't told them so, and I don't intend to."

"And the courier?" The second voice was younger, unhurried, with the flattened vowels of the capital. Not local. Someone come up the road from the coast, perhaps, or down from the front. "Your colonel asks about the courier every day. You'd be worth more to me if you could answer *that*."

"I can tell you what I've seen in the files. The dispatch registry shows a diplomatic pouch logged into Aldenmoor on the ninth — incoming seal, regional command. It hasn't been logged out. Either it's still here or someone carried it past the registry, which means someone carried it past *me*, and I'd know." A pause; the sound of a drawer sliding, papers being squared. "There's also the matter of a letter of safe conduct that came through the conscription annex. Signed, sealed, addressed to no family in this town. It shouldn't exist. It exists."

Someone in town has already talked.

The thought arrived whole, cold, fully formed, the way ice forms on a bucket left out overnight — not gradually, but all at once, in the space between two checks. Because Pell was not guessing. Pell was reading from the seized ledgers, yes — that much was his office, his theft, his commerce. But the safe conduct letter was not in the ledgers. It had lived for one afternoon in the diplomatic pouch, and then in the false bottom of Jude's writing desk, and then wrapped in oilcloth inside the hymnal that sat, at this very hour, beneath the loose floorboard in the vestry where Old Marta let the choristers hide their sweets. No ledger recorded it. No file described it. Only three people alive knew it existed, and one of them was standing in the dark listening to a clerk sell its outline to a stranger.

Unless the stranger already knew. Unless he was fishing. Unless the whole conversation was bait laid for whoever else might be standing in the reading room at dusk, pretending to fetch tariff schedules.

Beatrix's heart performed its work with terrible loudness. They made themselves breathe through the counting their instructors had taught them — in for the length of a written line, hold for the length of an erasure, out for the length of a signature — and kept listening.

"Names," the younger voice said. "The colonel wants names before the convoy business is settled. Once the ledgers travel south, this town stops being worth anything to anyone, including you. Sell me something that keeps selling."

A laugh from Pell, dry as a moth. "You sound like him. He says things like that. *Everything is a ledger.* Well. Here's your entry." Papers again — Beatrix could hear the particular sound of the conscription annex, the heavier stock, the pages that smelled of iron-gall ink. "Garret Hallow, wheelwright, exempted twice, third listing stands. The Fenner household, five names, transport-eligible. The widow Sarelle — she's on no list, but her son is, and mothers will do anything for sons, so she's useful either way." A pause, longer. "And the clerk across the square. Jude Ferane. Family in the hauling trade going back — well, back. If anyone in this town knows the ways through the marsh, it's a Ferane. Your colonel should watch that one."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a boat tilts when someone shifts their weight at the far end. *Jude.* Spoken aloud in Vess's anteroom, priced and offered like a sack of grain. Beatrix found their own hands had closed and forced them open again, finger by finger, because a person discovered eavesdropping must have relaxed hands.

"That's town knowledge," the younger voice said. "I asked for courier knowledge."

"Courier knowledge costs extra."

"You haven't earned the right to joke with me." Something set down on wood — a cup, perhaps, placed with deliberate precision. "Here is what happens now. You give the colonel his rotation schedules, because you gave him your word and he values word-keeping in small men. You say nothing about the pouch, because the colonel's attention on the pouch is attention I don't need. And you forget the name Ferane. If the Ferane clerk is guilty of something, I'll find it myself. If the Ferane clerk is innocent, you've just cost me a week watching an innocent man, and I remember who costs me weeks. Are we understood?"

"Understood." Pell's voice had lost its oil. Underneath it was something older and more frightened, the voice of a man who had sold information his whole life and only now heard what the buyers sounded like.

"One more thing." Chairs; the meeting taking on its closing shape. "Someone has been asking about the hymnals at the church. Which ones went missing from the pew racks. That wasn't my question and it wasn't the colonel's. So either your town has a thief of psalms, or your town has a copier of lists. Find out which, quietly, before someone louder does. That's free advice. In my experience free advice is the only kind people take."

Boots crossed the office. The inner door opened — Beatrix heard the latch, felt the draft move along the corridor like a finger drawn across the skin — and then the outer door, and the younger man was gone into the blue evening, whistling something tuneless and unhurried, the way a man whistles when no one anywhere is looking for him.

Beatrix counted to two hundred. It was the longest arithmetic of their life.

* * *

They walked home the long way, past the shuttered bakery where Herr Dulac had sold them bread every morning for nine years and whose name, they now knew with awful specificity, sat on a transport list in an iron-gall hand; past the well where the water chain clinked in the wind like a slow clock; past the darkened schoolhouse whose windows had been chalked with troop counts by bored soldiers and scrubbed again by the schoolmistress, over and over, a palimpsest of occupation. The river fog was coming up the streets the way it did in late autumn, softening corners, making the town feel provisional — a sketch of itself, waiting to see if it would be inked in or erased.

Paranoia, Beatrix discovered, was not a fever. It was an arithmetic. Every face at a window became a calculation. Frau Fenner drawing her curtain — had she seen something, or was it only the cold? The boy who swept the customs office, Tobi, lingering at the corner with his broom — a child playing at delay, or a child paid in coins to note who walked the long way home? Even the fog itself seemed complicit, concealing exactly as much as an enemy would wish concealed.

Because the question had teeth now, and it would not stop biting: *who talked?*

Not Pell — Pell was a buyer's instrument, not the town's wound. Pell knew the files. But the hymnals. *Someone has been asking about the hymnals.* That question had not come from the files. That had come from a pew rack, from a neighbor's eye, from someone in Aldenmoor who had noticed a book missing and mentioned it to someone with a uniform and a notebook. And once a town has produced one such mention, it can produce others. The hymnal was safe under its floorboard, but the hymnal's safety now rested on the discretion of an unknown mouth, and Beatrix lay awake that night cataloguing mouths. The sexton, who tidied the pews and would count books the way careful men count tools. Old Marta, who noticed everything about her church and forgave little of it. Any of the choristers. Any of their parents. Any frightened person, and winter was manufacturing frightened people by the day.

And beneath that fear, colder, lay the other thing, the thing they would have to decide whether to tell Jude: *Ferane.* Said aloud in the colonel's anteroom. Withdrawn by the stranger's order — but withdrawn, not erased. An order could change. A name once spoken into that room did not leave it; Beatrix knew how offices worked, how a name entered into a marginal note survived the death of the note itself, migrated, resurfaced. Somewhere in Vess's paperwork, perhaps already, Jude Ferane existed as a pencil mark in a margin, patient as mold.

To tell Jude or not to tell. That was the night's true ledger. To tell was to hand fear to the one person carrying the heaviest load across the marsh; fear made feet loud, and loud feet drowned in fords. To withhold was to become, in some small private way, what Pell was — someone trading in another person's danger without their consent. Beatrix turned the problem over and over, and every resolution had a margin note in it, and every margin note had their name.

Near morning they thought of the stranger's whistle, and that settled something. A man who whistles leaving a town full of informants is a man who has already been paid. Paid by whom? Not by Vess — he moved around Vess, not through him. Then by the capital, or by the front, or by whichever power intended to inherit the ledgers when Vess was done polishing them. There were more buyers in the world than sellers, Beatrix realized, and Aldenmoor, poor irrelevant Aldenmoor, had suddenly become a market. Their letter — *their* letter, though they had no right to the possessive — was now one commodity among several, and commodities attract appraisers.

Which meant the timetable had changed. Whatever Jude believed about patience, about old favors and quiet paths, the quiet was already sold. Somewhere between the ninth bell and the dawn, Beatrix stopped thinking of the plan as a secret and began thinking of it as a race.

* * *

They found Jude at the records annex just after eight, ostensibly auditing the fuel allotments, actually doing nothing of the kind. The room smelled of coal dust and cold paper. Frost ferned the lower panes of the window; beyond it, the square lay white-rimmed and empty except for a sentry stamping warmth into his feet by the pump.

"Pell met someone last night," Beatrix said, without preamble, because preamble had become a luxury. "In Vess's office. Selling the checkpoint rotations. Selling names." They watched Jude's face perform its careful stillness — the clerk's face, the face that had counted doors and debts for a decade and learned to hold numbers without flinching. "He named the Fenners. He named Garret Hallow. And he named you."

Jude's stillness held for three seconds, then a single blink, like a lamp guttering and recovering. "Named me how?"

"As a Ferane. As someone who'd know the marsh." Beatrix hesitated, and hated the hesitation, and pushed through it anyway. "The buyer told him to forget it. That's not comfort — that's just a debt deferred. And there's more. Someone in town has been asking about the missing hymnals. Not Pell. Not Vess. Someone else. Which means someone here noticed, and spoke, and we don't know to whom or for what."

The frost ticked against the glass. Out in the square, the sentry coughed.

"Then we were never as alone as we thought," Jude said at last, quietly, and there was something in the voice beneath the words that Beatrix recognized from their own chest — not despair, exactly, but the recalibration of a person discovering that the map they trusted has a second map printed invisibly beneath it. Jude looked toward the window, toward the church spire rising above the rooflines, where under a floorboard a borrowed God kept watch over a stolen census.

"The ford," Jude said. "We move up the crossing. Not in three days. Tomorrow night."
