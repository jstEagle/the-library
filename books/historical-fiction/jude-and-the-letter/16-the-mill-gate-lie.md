## 16. The Mill Gate Lie

The forgery took four hours, and Jude spent three of them not writing.

That was the trick no one taught clerks: the pen is the easy part. Anyone with a steady hand and a stolen sheet of requisition stock can make ink look official. What cannot be rushed is the thinking that surrounds it — the way a real requisition breathes, the small honest ugliness of bureaucratic habit. Colonel Vess's quartermasters stamped their forms slightly off-center; Jude had noticed it on the second day of the occupation, because noticing was the whole of a clerk's life. Their dates were written in the northern style, month before day, though the province had used the old order for two hundred years. They abbreviated *quintals* as *ql.*, never as *q.* And they always, always wrote the receiving officer's rank above his name, in a hand half again as large, as if the rank were the more important fact.

Jude sat at the kitchen table with a single tallow candle burning low, and practiced.

*Capt. R. Ostrand, 3rd Requisition Coy.* Again. Again. The rank swelling proudly over the smaller name. The *q* with its little tail. The date turned backward like a superstition. On the seventh attempt the hand stopped being Jude's hand and became something else entirely — a bored captain's scrawl, written on horseback, in a hurry to be done with paper and get to dinner. That was the final ingredient. No forger survives perfection. Every true document in the world is slightly lazy.

The paper itself had come from Tam Weller, which was to say from the mill, which was to say from the army. Three blank sheets of requisition stock, wrapped in oilcloth and carried under a sack of cracked wheat, traded across the back fence of the Weller yard two nights past in the oldest currency Aldenmoor possessed: a favor owed.

Jude's mother had called favors *the other ledger*. She kept it the way she kept everything else — in her head, unwritten, uncopiable. The Weller entry went back twenty-two years, to the winter of the flood, when the river took the mill's lower wheel and the granary flooded to the rafters, and it was Jude's grandmother who had known which smuggler's path ran dry-ground above the marsh and moved the Weller seed-corn out by night before the rot could take it. Without seed-corn there is no next year's flour. Without flour there is no mill. The Wellers had been paying that winter back in installments ever since — a sack here, a blind eye there, and once, memorably, a marriage witnessed. Now Jude was calling in the principal.

It felt less like spending than like spending something living. Favors, Jude thought, blowing out the candle as grey light came up over the rooftops, are the only money that loses value when you look at it too long.

* * *

The hymnal lay wrapped in the false bottom of a flour pannier, beneath forty pounds of genuinely honest meal. Beside it, folded into quarters and worn soft at the creases, the forged requisition. Two hundred and eleven names rode in that pannier — every household marked for transport, every boy over fourteen, every father on the conscription lists — copied out in Beatrix's careful courier's hand during three nights of lamplight and held breath. The bridge had already taken its toll of terror; Old Marta Hessel lay in her coffin under the alders now, and the hymnal's twin had crossed the water tucked against her shroud, and Jude still woke some mornings with the taste of that guilt in the back of the throat, sweet and rotten at once, grief spent like coin.

The mill gate was different work. The bridge demanded you be invisible. The mill demanded you be ordinary.

Aldenmoor's gristmill sat at the town's eastern edge where the river bent stupidly around a shelf of rock, and the army had seized it in the first week — naturally, inevitably, the way water finds the low ground. Grain was the war's true god, and whoever held the mill held the town's throat. They had posted a guard at the cart gate, a corporal's billet in the office, and a schedule of grinding that ignored entirely what the millstone had been made to grind. The townspeople brought their wheat in and watched it vanish into army sacks, and received in exchange an army receipt that everyone understood was a eulogy for their own flour.

But mills need tending, and armies do not know mills. So the Wellers had been kept on — Tam and his father and two of the cousins — because a soldier who forces a millstone wrong ruins it, and even Colonel Vess's immaculate requisitions could not conjure a new stone out of occupied countryside. Which meant that every morning, six carts came to the side gate hauling millwright's supplies, dressed stone, tallow, leather belting, and the endless specialized nothing that keeps machinery alive. And every morning a guard checked papers he did not understand, against goods he did not recognize, and waved them through because the alternative was admitting he did not know his business.

Jude put on the oldest coat in the house, rubbed flour into the cuffs and hem until the smell of the place lived in the wool, and loaded the pannier onto the mule.

* * *

Tam Weller was waiting at the side gate with his cap in his hands, which was wrong, and Jude saw it from thirty paces and began recalculating everything.

He should have been at the stones. Instead he stood beside the sentry — a young private, cheek still carrying the bruise of new shaving, rifle held the way one holds a broom — and he was talking to him. Too much. Talking the way people talk when they are filling a silence so it cannot fill itself, and his eyes kept cutting to the lane, to the road, to Jude coming.

*Someone has talked.* The thought arrived fully formed and cold, wearing the shape of the thing Jude had overheard Beatrix describe: a clerk in Vess's office selling information, someone in town already turned. Jude had held that knowledge for two days like a stone under the tongue, telling no one, not even Beatrix — no, especially not Beatrix, who would have begun rearranging the whole plan around a fear Jude couldn't yet prove. Now the stone spoke.

Jude did not turn around. Turning around is how you tell a watcher that you know. Jude came up the lane exactly as Jude had come up that lane a thousand times since childhood — slow, bored, the mule's lead looped loose, the other hand digging at an ear. Ordinary as bread. The private straightened. Tam's face did something complicated and landed on a smile that convinced no one.

"This is the millwright's run," Jude said to the private, before anyone else could speak, in the flat tone of a person who has said it six hundred times. "Side gate, not the cart gate. You're new."

"Corporal had me switched on yesterday." The private fumbled a slate from his belt. "I'm to check—"

"You're to check that nothing comes through but mill goods, because if the corporal finds chaff in a stone shipment again it's his neck, not yours." Jude let a beat pass, then softened it the way one does — the smallest shrug, a shared weariness. "You'll learn the loads by Friday and hate all of us by Sunday."

That earned half a grin, and half a grin is a door opening. While the private looked down at his slate, Jude's eyes found Tam's, and asked the question without words, and Tam answered it worse — a flick away, a swallow. Not *betrayal*, then. Something. Something Tam knew and didn't want known.

"Papers," the private said.

Jude handed over the requisition and watched a stranger hold three hours of candlelight in his hands. The private read it slowly, moving his lips — month before day, rank above name, the lazily honest *ql.* — and Jude stood in the cold morning feeling each of those small details like rungs underfoot, and hated how much of survival came down to another man's stamp drifting half a degree off-center.

"Says here flour," the private said. "Forty pound."

"Grinding sample for the new belt tension," Tam said, too fast, and Jude wanted to close his eyes. "Standard."

"It doesn't say standard."

"It doesn't have to," Jude said, mildly, wearily, the voice of every tired tradesman since the invention of paperwork. "Son, open the pannier if you want. Put your hand in forty pounds of flour and explain to your corporal why the sample's got your fingerprints in it."

The private looked at the pannier. Looked at the paper. Performed, visibly, the arithmetic of a young man deciding which superior's anger he least wished to meet, and found that neither anger had a face he knew. He waved the mule through.

"Unload at the third bay," Tam called after them, voice cracking only a little. "Father wants the belt leather first."

They passed the gate. The wheel turned behind the wall with its old patient thunder, and the smell of the mill closed over Jude like water — stone dust and oil and warm grain, the smell of every working morning of a childhood spent sweeping this floor while Grandmother argued tolerances with Tam's grandfather in the doorway. For ten steps Jude allowed it. Then the third bay swallowed them into shadow between stacked timbers, and Tam's hands were shaking so badly he dropped the pannier strap twice getting it off.

"You knew," Jude said quietly. "At the gate. You know something."

"Don't." Tam's voice was barely above the wheel's noise. "Don't ask me that today."

"When, then? Tomorrow, when they've marched us onto the transport lists? My brother's name is on those lists, Tam. Half the names in that—" Jude stopped himself, redirected. "Half the town is in those records. If you've heard something—"

"I haven't *heard* anything." Tam gripped the timber stack, knuckles white. He was thirty-one years old and looked fifteen, the way frightened men do. "There's a sergeant came round Tuesday. Asking my father about the flood winter. About who helped us move the seed corn. Writing it down in a little book." His eyes came up, and they were wet with something worse than fear — shame, the specific shame of a debtor watching his creditor's house burn. "Twenty-two years, Jude. Your grandmother's favor. I know what it weighs. I'm telling you what I heard, aren't I? Standing here telling you?"

"Yes," Jude said. "You are. And I won't forget it."

"That's what I'm afraid of." Tam laughed, a broken sound. "Don't put me in your ledger, Jude. Please. I'm not doing this to be owed."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the great wheel turned its patient circles, indifferent, older than both their families' debts.

"No," Jude said finally. "No ledger. Just — thank you. As one man to another, thank you." And then, because sentiment is a luxury paid for in minutes: "The belt leather. Walk me out with it. Same as any Tuesday."

* * *

Loading the mule for the return trip was theater, and Jude performed it with a craftsman's contempt for the audience. Belt leather went on top, loudly, badly, dropped once. The flour pannier went on last, cinched under Tam's hands while the private watched from the gate with the boredom of a man whose shift had already died. The sergeant Tam had mentioned was visible across the yard, at the office window, writing in a small book. Jude did not look at him directly. Looking directly at a man who writes things down is a courtesy clerks extend only to friends.

Then the side gate, the slate, the wave-through, the lane.

The copies left Jude's body at the moment the gate passed out of sight behind the hedge — left in the sense that Jude felt the pannier grow lighter by exactly the weight of two hundred and eleven names, though nothing physical had changed. Somewhere ahead, past the hedgerows and the drover's track and the long bend of the river, the partisan camp waited across the marsh, and the hymnal's twin already rested among them, and these pages would join it. After that the names belonged to other people: partisans with their own ledgers, their own prices. Jude had surrendered control of them the way one surrenders a letter to the post — with faith, and no recourse.

The plan had been to go straight home. The plan had been to deliver word to Beatrix that the second crossing was done, that the net now held two knots instead of one, and then to sleep for a week.

Instead, at the bend where the drover's track met the river path, Jude stopped the mule and stood a long time looking at the water, and let himself think the thought that had been waiting all morning for a private moment.

*Tam's father knows about the flood winter.* A sergeant asking questions. A little book. The chain of custody of an old favor, laid out in army ink: if the Wellers gave up the smugglers' path, the path gave up Jude's family, and Jude's family gave up Beatrix, and Beatrix carried the letter, and the letter was everything. One thread, pulled gently, and the whole weave came away in the occupier's fist.

And yet Tam had stood at the gate and lied badly and bravely, and asked only not to be entered in the ledger. That was love, wasn't it — the unrecorded kind? Jude's mother had believed so. She'd have said the Weller account was closed clean this morning, settled in full, no entries either way.

Jude's eyes stung, and it might have been the flour dust.

The mule shifted, wanting forward. Downstream, beyond the willows, smoke rose from the town in thin domestic columns, and among all those chimneys one belonged to a room where Beatrix was waiting with a lamp trimmed low and a lie held together with spit and courage, having told Colonel Vess nothing over tea while their neighbors' names cooled in the colonel's satchel. Jude turned the mule toward the smoke and walked, and did not hurry, because hurrying is a signature too.

It was nearly noon when the house came in view, and Jude saw immediately that the lamp was not trimmed low.

It was burning in broad daylight, behind the shutter's crack — the signal they had agreed meant *come now, come carefully, something has changed* — and standing in the lane outside, talking to a neighbor about weather with perfect innocence, was a soldier Jude had never seen before, taking notes in a small book.
