## 17. What Beatrix Carries

There was a game the children played in the foundling yard at Corvenne, in the years after the last war, when the snow came early and stayed like an unwelcome relative. They called it *Who Are You Today*. One child would stand at the gate and demand papers — a slate, a scrap of cloth, anything that could be held out and inspected — and the other children would invent themselves on the spot: a tanner's daughter, a soldier's son, a merchant's ward fleeing the shelled coast. The invented names were always better than the real ones. The real ones were mostly the same name anyway, or no name at all, just the number chalked on the dormitory door and the number stitched into the collar of every coat.

Beatrix had been good at the game. That was the first thing anyone ever said about them, afterward, when it stopped being a game: *the child has a talent for documents.*

They had been six when the war ended and seven when the wagons came for the orphans of the eastern provinces, sorting them the way grain is sorted — the ones with living relatives to one pile, the ones with documented births to another, and the rest, the unclaimed remainder, to the long gray building at Corvenne with its chapel and its ledgers and its smell of lye and boiled linen. Beatrix belonged to no pile. Their mother's name was written once, in a parish register that burned with the church in the second winter of the war, and their father's name was written nowhere at all, so far as anyone knew. A clerk at the wagon had looked at them for a long moment — a thin child with ash-colored eyes and mud frozen in their hair — and then wrote, in a hand as neat as print: *provenance unknown; assign per service need.*

That was how Beatrix Thornbury acquired a name. Not born to it. Assigned.

* * *

The diplomatic service maintained the orphanage the way a great house maintains its kennels — generously, without sentiment, and always with an eye to what might be bred there. The children of Corvenne were fed better than most orphans, taught languages by tutors who arrived in black coats and never smiled, and examined twice a year by men from the capital who carried leather cases and asked questions that were not questions. *If a man tells you the river runs north, and you have seen it run south, what do you write in your report?* Seven-year-old Beatrix had answered, *I write what I saw,* and the man had made a small mark in his book, and Beatrix learned only years later that the correct answer — the answer that got a child moved up the list — was *I write what serves the mission.* They had gotten the mark anyway, though. There was something in the flatness of the reply, the absence of fear, that the examiners liked. A courier who wept over a lie was a courier who would weep over a truth. Better to find the ones who felt nothing at all about paper.

And Beatrix felt nothing about paper. That was the whole of their education, distilled. People lied, people fled, people died in ditches with their faces turned toward home, but a document — a document was incorruptible. A seal did not change its mind. A signature did not grow afraid in the night. The tutors said it plainly enough, though they dressed it in Latin: *the word endures where the flesh fails.* Beatrix took it not as doctrine but as description, the way a child takes the fact of weather. It was simply true. The world was chaos and grief, and paper was the only thing anyone had ever built that held its shape.

By twelve they could copy three hands of script and forge four seals well enough to pass a customs inspection. By fourteen they had memorized the tariff schedules of two neighboring states and the personal cipher of a minister whose face they had never seen. By sixteen they had made their first run — a satchel of treaty amendments across a mountain pass in April, through snowmelt and bandit country, delivered intact to a consul who never asked their name because couriers were not supposed to have names, only numbers and routes. The consul had signed the receipt, pressed the wax, and said, *Well done, Courier Nine-Seven,* and Beatrix had walked back down the mountain feeling — what? Not pride. Something colder and cleaner than pride. *Confirmed.* Like a stamp on a passport. Like ink drying.

The other children of Corvenne had wanted to be soldiers, sailors, saints. Beatrix had wanted, with a ferocity that frightened even them, to be *reliable*. To be the thing that does not fail. Because a thing that does not fail cannot be thrown away, and everything at Corvenne was thrown away eventually — the weak children first, then the slow ones, then, when the budget tightened, the tutors themselves, dismissed overnight with their black coats and their unsmiling mouths. Only the system remained. Only the paper endured. Beatrix had decided very early, standing in the yard with the snow coming down and the game of *Who Are You Today* dying on the wind, that they would rather be a page in someone else's ledger than a name in no ledger at all.

It had not occurred to them until Aldenmoor that this was not the same as wanting to be alive.

* * *

Now they sat in the narrow room above the customs house with the shutter half closed against the afternoon, and the letter lay in the false bottom of the dispatch case, and Beatrix found themselves doing something they had never done in nineteen years of service: thinking about the weight of a thing that had no weight.

Paper weighed nothing. Everyone knew that. And yet the pouch had grown heavier every day since Vess's soldiers came, since the ledgers went out under the soldiers' arms like prisoners being marched, since the boy who swept the office — Tomas, his name was Tomas, he whistled while he worked and swept the corners no one else bothered with — had appeared on a transport list between a baker and a widow. Paper weighed nothing, and Beatrix could feel it in their lap like a stone.

*You are tired,* they told themselves. *Tiredness makes metaphors of everything.*

But it was not tiredness. It was the crack in the wall of their whole life, widening. They had been raised to believe that history happened in capitals, in chancelleries, in the hands of men whose signatures moved armies — and that everyone else was scenery, population, the gray mass that history flowed *through* on its way somewhere important. Beatrix had been content to be part of the flow itself, neither stone nor water but the current, the carrying. Couriers did not ask what they carried. Asking was a form of disloyalty, and disloyalty was the only sin the service recognized.

And yet.

And yet there was Jude, who had stood in the back room three nights ago with lamplight on their face and said, *Those aren't entries. Those are people. My neighbors. I christened half of them* — and Beatrix had felt the sentence go through them like a splinter working toward the heart. Because it was true, and because Beatrix had known it was true for years and had built their entire self on not knowing it. Every dispatch they had ever carried had been about people. Every tariff schedule was hunger measured and priced. Every troop movement was some village's sons counted like cordwood. The paper was never innocent. The paper was people, flattened and dried and filed, and Beatrix had spent their life carrying the flattened dead of other towns and calling it duty.

They got up and crossed to the window. Below, the street lay under curfew-gray light, and a patrol went by with the loose, bored rhythm of men who had been told they would be heroes by Friday. Somewhere a dog barked and was silenced. The mountains to the east had cloud on them, and the artillery had gone quiet in a way that felt less like peace than like a held breath.

*Who are you today?*

The old game rose in them unbidden, and Beatrix realized, with a sensation like stepping off a stair that isn't there, that they did not know the answer. Courier Nine-Seven was a number, and numbers could be reassigned. Beatrix Thornbury was an assigned name, given by a clerk at a wagon, chosen from nothing, meaning nothing — a coat stitched onto a child who had been cold. The loyal servant of the service was a role, and the service was dissolving around them like sugar in rain; Vess's polite, thorough occupation had seen to that. Strip away the number, the assigned name, the role — strip it all away, as the war had stripped it from everyone in this town — and what remained?

A person who had watched a baker's name get entered on a transport list and felt something break open in their chest. A person who had lied to Colonel Vess over coffee — their first lie, their first real one, told not for a mission but for a neighbor's secret — and had discovered, in the vertigo of it, that lying for people felt entirely different from lying for paper. Lighter. Warmer. Terrible and alive.

A person, maybe, who had spent thirty years being a document and had only just noticed that documents don't feel the cold.

* * *

There was one more thing Beatrix carried, and it was not in the dispatch case.

It was a memory, small and worn smooth by handling, of the only act of disobedience in their childhood. They were nine. A girl from the next dormitory — a slow girl, a sweet girl, one of the ones marked for dismissal when the budget inspectors came — had hidden a sparrow she'd found with a broken wing, kept it in a box under her cot, fed it crumbs stolen from breakfast. When it was discovered, the matron made the girl drown the bird herself, in the wash basin, in front of the dormitory, as instruction. And Beatrix, nine years old, who had already learned that the correct answer to every question was *what serves the mission*, had stepped forward and said: *It was my bird too. I'll do it.*

They hadn't done it. They had taken the bird out to the yard in the dark and broken its neck quickly, kindly, and buried it under the frost, and come back inside with wet hands and a face like a closed door, and the matron had noted their obedience, and the girl had looked at Beatrix with an expression Beatrix did not understand for twenty more years.

They understood it now. It was the look Jude gave them across the back room table. It was gratitude braided with grief — the gratitude of someone watching another person pay a price they couldn't afford, and the grief of knowing the price existed at all. *Thank you for taking the drowning from my hands. God forgive us that there is drowning.*

Beatrix had told no one that story in their entire adult life. There had never been anyone to tell it to. The service did not want its couriers to have histories; histories were attachments, and attachments were leverage, and leverage was vulnerability. So Beatrix had carried the memory the way they carried everything — sealed, dry, undamaged — and had told themselves that carrying was enough, that things preserved in transit were as good as things lived.

Jude had changed the arithmetic. Not with argument — Jude rarely argued — but with the simple, unbearable fact of their presence: a person who knew every family in town by its debts and its dead, who had refused to flee because the ledgers proved *who owned what*, who treated a name as a sacred trust rather than an entry. Beside Jude, Beatrix's lifelong creed — *loyal to paper, not people* — suddenly sounded not like wisdom but like a wound that had been dressed so long it had been mistaken for skin.

And now the letter. Now the choice that was coming for them whether they walked toward it or not. Deliver the amnesty as duty demanded — hand it up the chain, let the ministries decide, let history happen elsewhere as it always had — or put it into the hands of the people it was written about, and become, for the first time in their existence, not a carrier but a chooser.

The service would call it treason. Beatrix had finally worked out, sitting alone in the narrowing light, that the service would also call it treason if they did nothing — because the service no longer existed, and a loyalty with no object is just a habit, and habits are how people sleepwalk into graves.

Below in the street, a lantern swung past the shutter crack. Boots on cobbles. Vess's men, walking their rounds, thorough as ever. Beatrix stood motionless until the light passed, and in that stillness they made no decision at all, and understood that the decision had already been making itself for weeks — in the ledger margins, in the lie over coffee, in the hymnal full of copied names sleeping in Jude's back room.

Tomorrow, or the day after, Jude would come to them again. Beatrix could feel it the way one feels weather behind a wall. And this time they would not refuse. This time they would ask the question that had been forming in them since the yard at Corvenne, the question the game had always been secretly asking:

*Who are you, when no one assigns you?*

They took off their boots, lay down fully clothed beneath the thin blanket, and did not sleep. In the dark, the dispatch case sat on the chair where it always sat, and the letter inside it seemed almost to breathe — a promise of amnesty, signed and sealed, still traveling, still waiting for the hands that would finally open it.

Beatrix fell asleep at last near dawn, and dreamed of the foundling yard, and of a girl holding out a slate and demanding papers, and woke with the answer on their lips before they remembered there was no one left in the dream to give it to.

Downstairs, the office bell rang — the signal Jude used, two short pulls and a long — and Beatrix was on their feet before consciousness fully returned, reaching for the case, knowing with a certainty that felt older than their assigned name:

Whatever Jude had found in the seized records, whatever truth slept in that sealed letter, the time for carrying it in silence was over.
