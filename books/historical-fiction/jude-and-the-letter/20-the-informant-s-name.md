## 20. The Informant's Name

The name came to Jude the way sickness comes to a household — not all at once, but in small signs that only look like signs in retrospect.

It was the flour, first. Jude had gone to Marta Kessler's mill on the Tuesday after the hymnal left through the bridge, and Marta had leaned across her counter with white hands and a whiter face and said, quietly, the way people say things in occupied towns, "Your brother was asking after you. Not where you were. Where you *went*." She had said it while bagging rye she did not have, and she had not looked up, and Jude had thanked her for the flour as though she'd spoken about the weather.

Then there was the matter of the smokehouse key. It hung on two nails inside the back door of the family house, one nail for use and one nail for show, because their mother had always kept a spare and no one had ever remembered why. On Wednesday evening the key was on the wrong nail. Jude noticed it the way one notices a portrait hung crooked — a small wrongness that rearranges the whole room around itself.

And then there was Tomas himself, who had stopped urging flight.

That was the strangest part. For weeks he had been a broken record of leaving — *the front is coming, Jude, the front is a flood and we are a fence post* — and now he had gone silent on the subject entirely, which meant either that he had given up on his stubborn sibling or that he no longer needed to leave to feel safe. A man who has bought his own pardon stops shouting about the exits.

Jude sat with these three facts the way one sits with a diagnosis. Turning them over. Refusing them. Turning them over again.

On Thursday, Colonel Vess's clerks re-marked the mill gate — fresh paint on the checkpoint sign, a new roster of guards at hours when no grain moved. Someone had told the garrison that the mill mattered. No one in Aldenmoor knew what had gone out through the mill gate except Beatrix, and Jude, and the guard Osten who owed their mother a debt older than either of them could name.

And someone had told the garrison that the marsh paths existed.

That was the wound beneath all of it, the one Jude kept probing like a loose tooth. The soldiers at the bridge checkpoint had searched a coffin poorly, carelessly, as if searching were theater — but the soldier at the far end of the bridge, the young one with the chapped hands, had asked a question no outsider would know to ask. He had asked whether the Havel family still buried their dead facing east. That was a town question. That was a *graveyard* question, learned from someone who walked past those graves every Sunday of their life.

Jude stood at the window of the clerk's office that Thursday afternoon, watching snow come down over the rooftops of Aldenmoor with the terrible gentleness of snow that falls on towns about to be burned, and let the last fact assemble itself.

Three people knew the marsh paths well enough to speak of them. Old Wrenna, who was half-blind and hated the army more than she hated God. Jude. And Tomas — who had run the routes twice as a boy before the debt to the Harrow guild pulled him off them forever, who had spent his whole adult life resenting that the smuggling money had paid for Jude's clerk's schooling instead of his own freedom, who had begged and shouted and finally fallen silent on the subject of getting out.

Tomas, who had been seen drinking at the Crooked Lantern with a lieutenant from the requisition office, twice, and who had laughed when Marta mentioned it, and said brothers drink with anyone who pays.

The name arrived, then, not as a revelation but as a settling. The way silt settles in still water. Jude had known, probably, since the flour. Knowing was just the moment the water stopped moving.

The smokehouse stood at the end of the family property, a low stone building with a sod roof, older than the republic and most of its predecessors. Their mother had cured meat there every autumn of Jude's childhood, and the smell of hickory smoke had soaked so deep into the stones that even empty, even cold, it exhaled the past. In winter it held nothing but hanging hooks and darkness and the memory of abundance.

Tomas went there on Friday evenings. He always had. He said it was to check the foundation stones before the freeze, which was true in the way that most of his excuses were true — technically, transparently, beside the point. It was really the only place in Aldenmoor where he could sit alone with whatever it was he carried, and Jude had never once followed him there in thirty years of shared blood.

Jude followed him there now.

The snow squeaked underfoot. The sky was the color of unpolished pewter, and the light was already failing at four in the afternoon, the winter solstice dragging its long shadow across everything. Jude crossed the yard without going into the house, because going into the house would mean warmth and delay and the possibility of turning back, and turned instead along the fence line where the drifts rose waist-high and made walking a negotiation.

The smokehouse door opened inward. Tomas was inside with a lantern, crouched at the north wall with a trowel in one hand, actually checking the foundation stones, and for one absurd moment Jude simply watched him — this gray-bearded man kneeling in the dirt of their childhood, doing exactly the pointless thing he always claimed to be doing — and felt the grief arrive ahead of the anger, the way pain arrives ahead of the knowledge of what's been cut.

"Tomas."

He didn't startle. That was its own answer; guilty men startle. He set down the trowel with great deliberation and turned, and his face in the lantern light was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"I wondered when you'd come," he said. "You always were the patient one."

"Was I." Jude stepped inside and closed the door. The dark closed in around the lantern's circle of light, and the old smoke breathed out of the stones, hickory and autumn and their mother's strong hands. "Tell me about east-facing graves, Tomas."

Something moved behind his eyes — not flinch, exactly. Recognition. The expression of a man who hears the verdict read and realizes he'd been hoping, right up until the words, for acquittal.

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Sit down, Jude."

"There's nowhere to sit. There hasn't been anything in here but hooks since I was nine years old."

"Then stand." He rose anyway, brushing the dirt from his knees, and the two of them faced each other in the little stone room like figures in a painting neither would have chosen to sit for. "I want you to understand something before you say what you came to say."

"Say it for me, then. You've had practice saying things to people who weren't me."

The barb landed. He took it standing. "The transport lists are real," Tomas said. "You've seen them, you've copied them into a hymnal, don't look at me like that, I've known about the hymnal since before the coffin went over the bridge — yes, all of it. And do you know what happens to towns on those lists when the paperwork goes missing? They go on the *other* lists. The ones nobody copies out by lamplight. The ones that get settled with rope instead of stamps."

"So you told them."

"So I *asked questions*," he said, and his voice rose and cracked against the low ceiling. "At first. At the Lantern, over drinks, playing the drunken fool brother because the drunk fool is invisible, everyone talks over him and around him and sometimes *at* him. And then Lieutenant Brandt started answering my questions with questions of his own, and then there was coffee at the garrison, and then—" He gestured vaguely, helplessly, at himself, at the whole ruined architecture of it. "Then I was in. Whatever 'in' means when the wall you're leaning on is going to fall on top of you."

"What did you give them?"

Tomas looked at the lantern. He looked a long time at the lantern.

"The graveyard custom," he said. "The mill schedule. That your friend the courier keeps irregular hours and burns paper in the office stove after midnight." His jaw worked. "That the Havel family still has rights-of-way through the marsh that aren't written anywhere."

The last one landed hardest, and he saw it land. The marsh paths were the oldest thing they owned — older than the house, older than the name, a inheritance of mud and reed-banks and moonless nights passed mother to child with no deed and no record, which was precisely what made them precious. What made them survivable. And Tomas had handed them to a colonel the way you hand a match to a child: without thinking what would burn.

"They can't use them," Tomas said quickly. "I made sure of that. I told Brandt the paths shift with the season, that half of them drowned three years ago, that anyone who tries them without Wrenna's eye ends up in the bog. Which is all true. I gave them enough to make me useful and not enough to make you catchable—"

"You gave them enough to make *me* catchable, Tomas. You just didn't know it yet." Jude's voice came out very quiet, and quiet was worse than loud; loud was grief, but quiet was arithmetic. "The mill gate has new paint on its sign. New faces on its roster. Do you know why? Because you told them the mill mattered, and now the route you think you protected is being strangled inch by inch, politely, with requisition orders. There's one way out of this town left, and it's the one you sold them directions to."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Outside, the snow kept falling on the yard, on the fence, on the whole forgiving-looking world.

"I wanted us out," Tomas said finally, and his voice had changed — smaller, rawer, the voice from underneath all his certainties. "That's all it ever was. Every word I said to you since the artillery started, every fight, every — I wasn't trying to win an argument, Jude. I was trying to keep you alive. And then Vess's man offered me papers. Two sets. Passage west, out of all of it, before the front arrives and makes the question moot." He laughed, and it was an awful sound in the little stone room. "Do you know how long I've wanted passage anywhere? Since I was sixteen and Mother put the marsh routes in your hands instead of mine. One night's work for her, one lifetime of staying for me. And now some colonel offers me the thing I've wanted for thirty years in exchange for a few names, and I tell myself the names are safe, the people are safe, I'm careful, I'm *careful*—"

"You're careful," Jude repeated.

"Yes."

"And the boy at the bridge who knew about the east-facing graves — was that your care?"

"I couldn't stop everything." His voice broke open on the last word. "You can't steer a flood, Jude. You can only decide which parts of the house get wet."

"But it's not your house getting wet, is it. It's the Keslers. It's Osten, whose debt to Mother you just converted into a death sentence if they trace the grain order. It's Beatrix, burning paper at midnight while your lieutenant takes notes." Jude stepped closer, close enough to see the pores and the exhaustion and the thirty years of resentment that had never once been spoken aloud between them until now, when it was too late for speaking to fix. "You wanted us out. I know that. I believe that. But somewhere along the way *out* became worth more than *us*, and you didn't notice, because treason doesn't announce itself. It comes in through the smokehouse door one cup of coffee at a time."

Tomas's hand came up — Jude genuinely could not have said whether to strike or to plead — and stopped, trembling slightly, in the air between them. Then it dropped.

"Give me a reason not to report this," he whispered.

"To whom? Report it to whom, Tomas?" Jude heard their own voice go strange, almost gentle, which was the worst cruelty available. "There's no one left above this. That's what you never understood about paper. You thought ledgers were chains. They're the only witness we have. Burn them and there's just us, standing in rooms, telling each other stories."

He sat down then, abruptly, on the cold earth floor against the north wall he'd been pretending to inspect, and put his head in his hands, and looked suddenly ten years older than the morning. "What happens now?"

"Now?" Jude looked down at their brother — the boy who had taught them to swim in the mill pond, the man who had checked their boots for ice every winter morning without ever being asked, the informant, the coward, the brother, all of it true in the same body the way it is true in every body. "Now you tell me whether Brandt expects anything from you this week. Now you feed him exactly what I give you to feed him and nothing else. And now you live with it. That's the sentence. Not rope, not exile. Just this room, and what you did in it, for as long as any of us last."

Tomas nodded without lifting his head. Snow ticked softly against the sod roof. Somewhere beyond the walls the garrison bugle sounded the early-evening hour, thin and brass and absurd over the falling town.

Jude turned to go, hand on the door latch, and had gotten it half-raised when Tomas spoke again from the floor, very quietly, in the voice of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"There's one more thing you should know. Before you decide how much of me to forgive." He raised his head. His face in the lantern light was terrible with honesty. "Brandt didn't ask me about the courier's midnight fires. I offered that. Because they were already watching the office, Jude — they knew about the burning before I ever said a word. Which means someone else in this town is talking too. Someone closer to the customs house than I ever got."

The latch froze under Jude's hand. Outside, the snow went on falling, gentle and indifferent, covering every path — the honest roads and the marsh ways alike — with the same white, featureless, unreadable page.
